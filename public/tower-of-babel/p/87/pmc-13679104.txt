C-Reactive protein and the CALLY index for predicting complicated appendicitis: a retrospective cohort study of 5,428 patients
Authors: Mahmut Ozan Aydin, Erkan Somuncu, Cenk Ozkan, Zeynep Betul Yildiz, Kubilay Ozgur Oztutuncu, Ozgur Gangal, Beyhan Kilic, Serhan Yilmaz, Emre Bozdag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistinguishing complicated from uncomplicated appendicitis before surgery remains a critical yet unresolved clinical challenge. Delayed recognition of complicated appendicitis, particularly in cases of perforation or abscess formation, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Although various laboratory markers and inflammatory indices have been proposed as markers associated with disease severity, their relative diagnostic performance remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the discriminatory performance of conventional laboratory parameters, leukocyte-derived indices, and composite biomarkers for complicated appendicitis.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center cohort study, 5,428 patients who underwent emergency appendectomy between 2015 and 2025 were included. Patients were classified as uncomplicated or complicated appendicitis based on intraoperative findings. Preoperative laboratory parameters, including C-reactive protein, white blood cell count, total bilirubin, leukocyte-derived indices, and composite biomarkers such as the C-reactive protein–albumin–lymphocyte (CALLY) index and the hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) index, were analyzed. Receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to assess diagnostic performance, and optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify variables independently associated with complicated appendicitis.ResultsOf the 5,428 patients, 707 (13.1%) had complicated appendicitis. C-reactive protein demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.781) and demonstrated the strongest independent association (OR: 5.29). Total bilirubin was also independently associated with complicated appendicitis (OR: 1.21). Leukocyte-derived indices showed limited discriminatory ability, with only the systemic immune-inflammation index remaining significant in multivariate analysis. The CALLY index demonstrated relatively strong discriminatory performance (AUC: 0.747), with lower values associated with increased disease severity.ConclusionsC-reactive protein remains the most robust laboratory marker for identifying complicated appendicitis. Composite biomarkers such as the CALLY index may provide additional value in risk stratification. These findings support the use of routinely available laboratory parameters in clinical risk assessment, although prospective validation is warranted.Keywords: Acute appendicitis, Complicated appendicitis, Prediction, C-reactive protein, CALLY index, HALP index, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio

Background

Acute appendicitis is the most common cause of emergency abdominal surgery worldwide and remains one of the most common surgical emergencies. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, approximately 17 million new cases occur annually, accounting for nearly 29,300 deaths worldwide and highlighting the substantial clinical and economic burden imposed on healthcare systems [1].

Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and clinical evaluation, distinguishing uncomplicated from complicated appendicitis before surgery remains challenging. Complicated appendicitis, typically characterized by perforation or periappendiceal abscess formation, is often associated with delayed presentation or diagnostic delay and is linked to increased postoperative morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and higher healthcare costs. Mortality has been reported to be approximately twofold higher in patients with perforated compared with non-perforated appendicitis [2].

Accurate preoperative identification of patients at risk for complicated appendicitis is therefore clinically important. Among the laboratory parameters evaluated in this context, white blood cell count (WBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP) have been most extensively studied. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated the superior discriminatory performance of CRP over WBC in predicting complicated appendicitis, with pooled AUC values approaching 0.78 in large-scale analyses [3, 4]. In recent years, leukocyte-derived inflammatory indices — including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) — have attracted increasing interest as potential markers of appendicitis severity, given their accessibility and low cost. However, existing studies have reported only modest discriminatory performance for these indices, and their independent discriminatory value alongside established markers such as CRP remains insufficiently clarified [5–7]. Furthermore, composite indices incorporating multiple inflammatory and nutritional parameters have been proposed as potentially superior alternatives, though their utility in acute appendicitis has not been systematically evaluated, particularly in large patient cohorts.

The aim of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic performance of routinely available laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices, including composite indices, in predicting complicated appendicitis in a large cohort of patients.

Materials and methods

A retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent appendectomy between January 2015 and December 2025. All cases were identified from the hospital electronic medical record system. Patients whose preoperative laboratory data were unavailable in the hospital electronic system due to external testing were excluded. In addition, patients who underwent concomitant appendectomy during cytoreductive or oncologic surgery, those with appendiceal mucocele, and patients who underwent interval appendectomy were excluded. The remaining patients represented those who underwent emergency appendectomy for acute appendicitis. Based on intraoperative findings documented in the operative reports, which were independently reviewed by a surgeon not involved in the surgical procedures, patients were subsequently classified into two groups: uncomplicated appendicitis (Group 1) and complicated appendicitis (Group 2). Complicated appendicitis was defined as the presence of appendiceal perforation, gangrenous appendicitis, or periappendiceal abscess detected intraoperatively. Phlegmonous appendicitis was categorized as uncomplicated, consistent with previously published clinically oriented classification systems [8, 9]. This study was approved by the local ethics committee (approval number: KAEK/2026.01.38) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Demographic characteristics (age and sex) and preoperative laboratory parameters of the patients were recorded and analyzed. Laboratory values obtained within 24 h before surgery were defined as preoperative values. Absolute cell counts obtained from the complete blood count, rather than differential percentage values, were used to calculate inflammatory indices. The evaluated laboratory parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total bilirubin, and hematological cell counts derived from the complete blood count. Based on these parameters, several inflammatory indices were calculated, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), hemoglobin–albumin–lymphocyte–platelet (HALP) index, and the C-reactive protein–albumin–lymphocyte (CALLY) index. SII was calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count, HALP as hemoglobin × albumin × lymphocyte count / platelet count, and CALLY as albumin × lymphocyte count / C-reactive protein.

Inflammatory indices were calculated only in patients with complete data required for each index; patients with missing parameters were excluded from the corresponding analyses. The number of missing values for these parameters is presented in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices for complicated appendicitis. The area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated for each parameter. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV) were reported. Discriminative performance was interpreted based on AUC values.

Continuous variables were subsequently dichotomized according to these optimal cut-off values and entered into the multivariate logistic regression model to identify variables independently associated with complicated appendicitis. Age, WBC, CRP, total bilirubin, SII, NLR, CALLY, and HALP were included in the model. The results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Because several inflammatory indices were derived from overlapping hematologic parameters, only the NLR was included among leukocyte-derived indices, while PLR and MLR were excluded to reduce potential multicollinearity and avoid redundancy in the model. To formally assess multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated for all variables included in the multivariable model. VIF values for all variables remained within acceptable limits, indicating no clinically meaningful multicollinearity among the included variables. Dichotomization was performed to facilitate clinical interpretability and to allow direct comparison with published literature reporting binary thresholds for these markers. This approach, while enhancing practical applicability, may result in some loss of statistical information inherent to the categorization of continuous variables. As cut-off values were derived from the same dataset used for analysis, these thresholds should be considered exploratory and require validation in independent cohorts [10].

Results

A total of 5,563 patients who underwent appendectomy during the study period were identified. After applying the predefined exclusion criteria, 5,428 patients who underwent emergency appendectomy for acute appendicitis constituted the final study cohort. Of these, 4,721 (86.9%) were classified as uncomplicated appendicitis and 707 (13.1%) as complicated appendicitis (Fig. 1). The mean age of the patients was 33.0 ± 12.6 years. The mean age was 32.3 ± 11.8 years in Group 1 and 37.8 ± 15.9 years in Group 2. Patients with complicated appendicitis were significantly older than those with uncomplicated appendicitis (p < 0.001). Overall, 64.8% of patients were male. The rate of complicated appendicitis did not differ significantly between males and females (12.8% vs. 13.4%, p = 0.509). Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Flow diagram of patient selection

CRP levels were significantly higher in patients with complicated appendicitis, with median values of 18.78 (5.09–52.68) in Group 1 and 100.60 (41.76-197.09) in Group 2 (p < 0.001). The median WBC level was 13.85 (10.89–16.79) in Group 1 and 14.61 (11.40-17.93) in Group 2 (p < 0.001). Total bilirubin levels were also significantly higher in Group 2 (0.57 [0.38–0.86] vs. 0.74 [0.48–1.12], p < 0.001). Inflammatory indices including NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII were significantly higher in Group 2 (all p < 0.001), whereas CALLY and HALP indices were significantly lower (both p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in LDH levels between the groups (p = 0.603). Detailed laboratory parameters are presented in Table 1.

Because albumin measurements were not routinely available for all patients, albumin-based indices were calculated only in patients with complete data. For the CALLY index, 2,223 patients (41.0%) were excluded due to missing values, leaving 2,710 of 4,721 patients with uncomplicated appendicitis and 495 of 707 patients with complicated appendicitis available for analysis. Similarly, for the HALP index, 2,060 patients (38.0%) were excluded, resulting in 2,859 of 4,721 patients in the uncomplicated group and 509 of 707 patients in the complicated group included in the analysis. These analyses were therefore conducted on complete-case subsets for each index. This approach may have introduced selection bias, which should be considered when interpreting the results of albumin-based indices. In contrast, the SII index was calculated for the entire study cohort.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of laboratory parameters and inflammatory indices for complicated appendicitis. CRP demonstrated the best discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI 0.761–0.801). The optimal cut-off value for CRP was 46.8 mg/L, yielding a sensitivity of 71% and a specificity of 71%. The CALLY index also showed relatively strong discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.747 (95% CI 0.723–0.770) and an optimal cut-off value of 0.11 (sensitivity 70%, specificity 70%). As both the CALLY and HALP indices are inversely associated with disease severity, AUC values for these indices were calculated using the inverse test direction, whereby lower values were classified as positive for complicated appendicitis. Other inflammatory indices, including NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII, demonstrated only modest discriminative ability (AUC range: 0.607–0.626). Statistical comparison of ROC curves using the DeLong method confirmed that CRP achieved significantly higher discriminatory performance than all leukocyte-derived indices (all p < 0.001). Direct statistical comparison between CRP and the CALLY or HALP indices was not performed due to differences in available sample size arising from missing albumin data. WBC, age, and the HALP index showed limited discriminatory performance. When the biomarkers were ranked according to their AUC values, CRP showed the highest diagnostic accuracy, followed by the CALLY index, whereas leukocyte-derived indices demonstrated only modest discriminatory performance. Among all evaluated markers, CRP demonstrated the highest negative predictive value (94.3%), followed by the CALLY index (92.8%), indicating that normal values of these markers may be useful in ruling out complicated appendicitis. The NPV values of leukocyte-derived indices ranged from 89.9% to 90.6%. Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study, we systematically compared the diagnostic performance of conventional laboratory markers and composite inflammatory indices for identifying complicated appendicitis. Among the evaluated biomarkers, CRP demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy for identifying complicated appendicitis. Notably, the CALLY index also demonstrated relatively strong discriminatory performance, highlighting its potential as a novel composite biomarker in this clinical setting. In contrast, leukocyte-derived indices such as NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII exhibited only modest discriminatory performance. In addition, total bilirubin levels were significantly associated with complicated appendicitis.

In the present study, phlegmonous appendicitis was classified as uncomplicated appendicitis despite its inclusion within the complicated category in current WSES guidelines [11]. This approach was adopted to better reflect clinical decision-making, as phlegmonous appendicitis typically follows a more benign course and does not usually require urgent intervention compared with perforated or abscess-forming disease. This classification is consistent with previously published approaches: Bom et al. defined uncomplicated appendicitis as phlegmonous inflammation without necrosis or perforation, reserving the complicated category for cases with necrosis or perforation [8], while Mori et al. similarly classified catarrhal and phlegmonous appendicitis as uncomplicated and gangrenous or perforated forms as complicated [9]. Given that the primary aim of this study was to identify laboratory markers associated with clinically significant complications, specifically perforation and intra-abdominal abscess formation, classifying phlegmonous appendicitis as complicated may have introduced clinical heterogeneity and attenuated the discriminatory performance of the evaluated biomarkers.

Among these parameters, CRP achieved an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI: 0.761–0.801) at an optimal cut-off of 46.8 mg/L and retained the strongest independent association with complicated appendicitis in multivariate analysis (OR: 5.29; 95% CI: 4.244–6.595; p < 0.001). These findings are consistent with a large body of evidence supporting the diagnostic superiority of CRP over other conventional inflammatory markers. This is further supported by findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Yu et al. [3] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing seven studies from seven countries and demonstrated that CRP achieved the highest discriminatory performance among the evaluated biomarkers, outperforming both WBC count and procalcitonin. Acharya et al. [4] synthesized data from 62 studies in a comprehensive systematic review. Among these, 37 studies provided perforation-specific discriminatory data, and the pooled AUC for CRP across this subset was 0.78. This finding is closely aligned with our own AUC of 0.781 derived from a cohort of 5,428 patients. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted in light of methodological considerations. Both analyses included heterogeneous patient populations and demonstrated considerable statistical heterogeneity (I² = 70% and 98%, respectively), which may limit the generalizability of their findings [3, 4]. In this context, the present study—encompassing 5,428 patients over a ten-year period—represents one of the largest single-center cohorts in the literature and may provide more stable and clinically applicable cut-off estimates.

Contemporary cohort studies have consistently confirmed the leading role of CRP in identifying complicated appendicitis. In a prospective multicenter study conducted across 11 hospitals in the Netherlands, including 1,360 patients, CRP and age were independently associated with complicated appendicitis in multivariate analysis (OR: 2.33; 95% CI: 1.83–2.96 and OR: 2.00; 95% CI: 1.44–2.77, respectively), while WBC count failed to reach statistical significance and was excluded from the final model—a pattern consistent with the findings of the present study [12]. In that study, a clinical prediction model incorporating biochemical and imaging findings was developed, and omission of CRP reduced the C-statistic from 0.87 to 0.83, underscoring its independent and incremental predictive contribution. Similar findings were reported by Dölling et al. [13], who analyzed a retrospective cohort of 559 patients and demonstrated superior discriminatory performance of CRP compared with leucocyte count (AUC 0.76 vs. 0.57). These findings suggest that CRP may serve not only as a diagnostic marker for acute appendicitis but also as an indicator of disease severity. However, that study classified phlegmonous appendicitis as complicated according to EAES 2015 criteria, resulting in a high rate of complicated appendicitis (62.5%), which may have influenced the reported cut-off values. In addition, the absence of multivariable analysis limits conclusions regarding the independent discriminatory value of CRP.

Comparison of CRP cut-off values across the literature reveals considerable variability that warrants cautious interpretation. In the present cohort, the optimal cut-off was 46.8 mg/L. Patmano et al. [14] reported a substantially lower threshold of 25.5 mg/L in a single-center retrospective cohort of 530 patients, a difference that may reflect the inherent instability of cut-off estimation in smaller samples, as well as the known sensitivity of Youden index–derived thresholds to cohort composition. Conversely, higher thresholds have been reported in other settings. Lezama et al. [15] identified CRP > 74 mg/L as an independently associated marker in a prospective cohort of 2,507 patients; however, their analysis included only laparoscopic appendectomies. This methodological choice introduces an important selection bias, as patients presenting with septic shock, diffuse peritonitis, or advanced perforation—who typically undergo emergency laparotomy or open appendectomy rather than laparoscopy—were systematically excluded. Although this would be expected to reduce the representation of the most severe cases, the higher cut-off reported by Lezama et al. suggests that factors beyond selection bias may have contributed to the observed threshold. The most extreme cut-off reported in the literature was described by Brombacher et al. [16], who identified a CRP threshold of 110 mg/L in a retrospective cohort of 358 patients from a rural South African hospital. This outlier value likely reflects the well-documented phenomenon of delayed presentation in low-resource settings, where barriers to healthcare access frequently result in patients presenting at more advanced stages of disease. As CRP rises progressively with both the duration and severity of inflammation, such delayed presentations are expected to shift the distribution of CRP values upward and consequently produce higher diagnostic cut-off thresholds.

Taken together, the variability in CRP cut-off values reported across the literature should be interpreted with caution and likely reflects differences in patient populations, healthcare access, inclusion criteria, and disease classification systems that may influence threshold optimization. In this context, the cut-off of 46.8 mg/L identified in the present study—derived from a large appendectomy cohort comprising 5,428 patients over a ten-year period—may provide a relatively stable estimate compared with thresholds reported in smaller single-center series. Furthermore, in the present study, CRP remained strongly and independently associated with complicated appendicitis in multivariate analysis (OR 5.29), supporting its clinical utility in the risk stratification of appendicitis severity.

The CALLY index is a composite immunonutritive scoring system incorporating serum CRP, albumin, and lymphocyte count, originally developed by Iida et al. [17] as a prognostic biomarker in patients undergoing hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma, and subsequently evaluated across a broad spectrum of digestive system malignancies in multiple meta-analyses [18, 19]. To our knowledge, the CALLY index has not previously been investigated in the context of acute appendicitis, either as a diagnostic marker or as a predictor of complicated appendicitis. Its constituent components individually reflect key pathophysiological pathways implicated in appendicitis severity: CRP represents the systemic inflammatory response, lymphocyte count reflects immune activation and stress-related lymphocyte depletion, and albumin serves as a marker of systemic inflammatory burden and nutritional status. Taken together, this composite structure provides a plausible biological rationale for the application of the CALLY index in predicting appendicitis severity. In the present study, lower CALLY index values were significantly associated with complicated appendicitis, demonstrating an inverse relationship with disease severity. Accordingly, our findings suggest that the CALLY index may have potential clinical utility in discriminating disease severity in this setting. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to determine whether the CALLY index could be incorporated into clinical prediction models for appendicitis severity.

Although serum albumin was not included in the routine preoperative laboratory panel in our institution, it is a widely available, low-cost, and rapidly measurable parameter that can be obtained at the bedside. Given the promising discriminatory performance of the CALLY index observed in this study, its incorporation into routine preoperative evaluation warrants consideration. We encourage future prospective studies to include routine albumin measurement, which would enable real-time CALLY calculation and allow formal validation of its clinical utility in this setting.

In the present study, patients aged 31 years and older demonstrated a 1.39-fold increased risk of complicated appendicitis, identifying age as independently associated with disease severity. Several biological mechanisms may underlie this association. With advancing age, progressive arteriosclerotic changes in appendiceal microcirculation, involution of lymphoid tissue, and attenuation of the systemic inflammatory response may contribute to a more rapid progression from luminal obstruction to gangrene or perforation. In addition, older patients often present with less typical clinical symptoms and a blunted inflammatory response, which may contribute to delayed diagnosis and treatment. Previous studies have similarly reported an age-dependent increase in appendicitis severity. Weinandt et al. demonstrated that appendicitis in elderly patients is associated with significantly higher rates of complicated disease and worse clinical outcomes compared with younger populations [20]. Likewise, a nationwide analysis including 538,400 patients showed that individuals older than 60 years had significantly higher rates of complicated appendicitis and mortality than younger patients [21]. These findings support the role of increasing age as a relevant factor in the preoperative risk stratification of appendicitis severity.

Leukocyte-derived inflammatory indices, including NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII, were significantly elevated in patients with complicated appendicitis; however, their discriminatory performance remained modest, with AUC values ranging from 0.607 to 0.626. Contemporary cohort studies have reported similarly limited discriminatory accuracy for these parameters. Saridas et al. [5] reported an AUC of 0.634 for SII in a cohort of 334 appendectomy patients, and Feier et al. [6] likewise concluded that multiple leukocyte-derived indices were insufficient as independent discriminators of appendicitis severity despite significant univariate associations. WBC counts followed a similar pattern, showing significant elevation in complicated appendicitis in univariate analysis but losing significance after multivariable adjustment. This limited performance is mechanistically plausible, as leukocyte-derived ratios reflect relative shifts within circulating immune cell populations that occur across a broad spectrum of inflammatory conditions and therefore lack sufficient specificity for discriminating disease severity. In the present study, NLR did not retain independent significance in the multivariate model when evaluated alongside CRP, consistent with the meta-analytic findings of Hajibandeh et al. [7], who reported that NLR may provide complementary —but not superior—discriminatory value compared with acute-phase reactants. Notably, SII was the only leukocyte-derived index that remained independently associated with complicated appendicitis (OR 1.60; p = 0.002). As SII integrates neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts into a single metric, it may better reflect the complex inflammatory response associated with disease severity compared with single-ratio leukocyte parameters.

Total bilirubin has also been proposed as a potential marker of appendicitis severity. In the present study, total bilirubin levels were significantly higher in patients with complicated appendicitis and remained independently associated with disease severity in the multivariate model (OR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.004–1.464; p = 0.046). This association is thought to result from bacterial translocation and endotoxin-mediated impairment of hepatocellular bile excretion. As appendiceal wall integrity becomes compromised, gram-negative bacteria and their endotoxins may enter the portal circulation and interfere with canalicular bilirubin secretion, leading to transient cholestasis. This mechanism has been described in previous studies evaluating hyperbilirubinemia in acute appendicitis, including the work of Halaseh et al. [22]. In addition, Giordano et al. [23] reported that hyperbilirubinemia was significantly associated with appendiceal perforation, with an overall AUC of 0.73. Taken together, these findings suggest that although total bilirubin may reflect advanced inflammatory and infectious burden, its discriminatory performance is lower than that of CRP and should therefore be interpreted as an adjunctive rather than primary marker of appendicitis severity. Although statistically significant, the modest odds ratio of 1.21 indicates a limited independent effect size, and total bilirubin should therefore be regarded as a supplementary rather than a decisive marker in clinical practice.

Taken together, these findings suggest that preoperative laboratory parameters may support clinical decision-making by facilitating risk stratification of patients with acute appendicitis. Markedly elevated CRP, bilirubin, or leukocyte-derived indices — or a notably low CALLY index — may prompt earlier surgical intervention, prioritization of cross-sectional imaging, and appropriate preparation for a potentially complex operative procedure.

The present study has several important strengths. Most notably, the large cohort of 5,428 patients represents one of the largest single-center appendectomy datasets evaluating laboratory markers of complicated appendicitis, providing substantial statistical power and improving the stability of the derived estimates. In addition, a broad panel of inflammatory biomarkers—including conventional markers, leukocyte-derived indices, and composite indices such as the CALLY index—was evaluated within a unified analytical framework. Notably, although the CALLY index has been extensively studied in oncological settings, its application in acute appendicitis has not previously been explored to our knowledge, offering a novel perspective on composite immunonutritive biomarkers in acute inflammatory disease. Operative findings were independently evaluated by a separate reviewer to minimize potential assessment bias. Finally, multivariate modelling enabled assessment of the independent discriminatory performance of these markers beyond simple univariate associations.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Due to the retrospective design of the study, certain laboratory parameters were not available for all patients. In particular, serum albumin was not included in the routine preoperative laboratory panel for patients with suspected acute appendicitis in our institution. However, albumin measurements obtained as part of individualized clinical assessment in a subset of patients allowed for the calculation of albumin-based indices in this subgroup. Accordingly, the findings related to the CALLY and HALP indices should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as this subset may not fully represent the overall cohort. Of note, patients in whom albumin was measured may differ systematically from those without albumin data, and the possibility of selection bias affecting the CALLY and HALP analyses cannot be excluded. The number of missing values for each parameter was explicitly reported in Table 1. Imaging findings were not included in the analysis, as the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of preoperative laboratory parameters. Incorporating imaging results could have introduced bias. These laboratory markers were evaluated as adjunctive tools for preoperative risk stratification and were never intended to replace imaging in clinical decision-making. Therefore, the analysis was intentionally limited to blood-based markers. Finally, the cut-off values reported in this study were derived from the same dataset used for diagnostic performance estimation and multivariable analysis, which carries an inherent risk of threshold overfitting. Accordingly, the proposed thresholds may not generalize to other patient populations and should be interpreted with caution. Prospective validation in independent cohorts is warranted before these values are applied in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CRP demonstrated the strongest diagnostic performance for predicting complicated appendicitis and the strongest independent association in multivariate analysis. Leukocyte-derived inflammatory indices showed statistically significant but limited discriminatory ability, likely reflecting their nonspecific nature. The CALLY index demonstrated promising diagnostic potential despite not previously being evaluated in acute appendicitis, suggesting a potential role for composite immunonutritive markers in assessing disease severity. Given their moderate discriminatory performance, these biomarkers should be interpreted as adjunctive risk stratification tools to support — rather than replace — clinical judgment and imaging-based evaluation. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the role of such indices in clinical decision-making.
