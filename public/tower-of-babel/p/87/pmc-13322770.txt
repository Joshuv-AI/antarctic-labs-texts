A rat muscle contusion model created by a modified drop-mass method using ultrasound
Authors: Ryo Miyazaki, Takashi Kanamoto, Yujie Ren, Shunya Otani, Seira Sato, Tomoki Ohori, Akira Tsujii, Kosuke Ebina, Hiroyuki Tanaka, Seiji Okada, Ken Nakata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsMuscle contusion healing remains challenging because incomplete regeneration can lead to excessive scar formation, which may impair functional recovery. Therefore, reproducible animal models are important tools for investigating the healing process and evaluating potential therapeutic strategies. This study aimed to establish a reproducible muscle contusion model using a modified drop-mass method with ultrasound. Furthermore, the feasibility of using this model for molecular screening of muscle contusion healing was investigated.MethodsA total of 70 male Wistar Hannover rats were initially enrolled in this study. Two rats were excluded because of tibial fractures after injury induction; therefore, 68 rats were included in the final assessments. In this model, the placement of the rat tibialis anterior muscle and the impactor tip was confirmed by ultrasound prior to dropping the weight. To evaluate the reproducibility of the injury, ultrasound measurements of muscle size, histological evaluation of muscle tissue structure, gene expression analysis of inflammation-related factors and macrophage markers, and muscle strength assessment were performed. Moreover, based on a comprehensive genetic analysis of samples collected from this model, molecules involved in the healing process of muscle contusions were screened.ResultsAbnormal ultrasound images, including hypoechoic areas, were observed in all the injured muscles. A peak increase in the maximum muscle cross-sectional area was observed 24 hours after injury, which subsequently decreased. Histological analysis revealed fibrous tissue at seven days post-injury, becoming more prominent at day 14. Muscle strength was reduced at three and seven days after the injury. Inflammation-related genes were markedly upregulated on the day of injury, while the expression of macrophage marker genes increased three days post-injury. Ribonucleic acid sequencing revealed gene clusters, including tissue kallikrein (KLK1), with higher expression on day 7 than on day 3 post-injury. High expression of KLK1 was confirmed in the injured muscle tissue of model animals on day 7 after injury using quantitative polymerase chain reaction and immunohistochemical staining.ConclusionA reproducible model of skeletal muscle contusion can be established using a modified drop-mass method guided by ultrasound. This model demonstrated potential utility for exploring the molecules involved in the healing process of muscle contusions.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(7):767–774.

Introduction

Muscle contusion, a direct physical injury to skeletal muscle tissue caused by an external force, is among the most common injuries observed in sports.1 Surgical haematoma removal may be performed in the case of severe pain; however, the standard treatment involves the rest of the affected area, along with external fixation and icing, with most cases exhibiting a favourable clinical course.2,3 However, in severe cases, muscle tears, residual pain, muscle weakness, and restricted joint range of motion can impair daily activities and delay return to sports.4,5 The underlying causes are acute swelling and inflammation, as well as scar formation due to fibrosis during the healing process.1,6 Therefore, an urgent need exists to establish the pathophysiology and develop therapies for the acute and fibrosis/scar formation phases of muscle contusions.

Animal models serve as potent tools that form the foundation for understanding disease mechanisms and developing therapeutic strategies. Various animal models have been developed to study skeletal muscle injury.7,8 Animal experimental models developed using the drop-mass method have been commonly employed in the past.9,10 Muscle injury is typically induced by applying a heavy weight to the limbs. However, previous studies have been inconsistent in the weight and drop height used, and there is considerable variation in the calculated energy delivered to the muscle, even when considering different animal species and skeletal muscles (Supplementary Table i).11-16 Furthermore, many studies lack detailed descriptions of the location and severity of muscle injury. The use of highly reproducible animal models is essential for elucidating pathophysiology and evaluating the effectiveness of new treatments. As mentioned above, current research models of muscle contusion leave room for improvement.

Ultrasound diagnostic devices allow highly sensitive detection of changes in skeletal muscle echogenicity, as well as identification of muscle tears and haematomas, and as such are widely used for diagnosing skeletal muscle injuries.17,18 Studies using cardiotoxin-induced skeletal muscle injury models have demonstrated the feasibility of quantitatively evaluating injured muscle areas and performing ultrasound-guided injection of therapeutic agents or cells into the injured site, indicating potential applications not only in diagnosis but also in evaluating treatment efficacy.19 To the best of our knowledge, no reports have documented the use of ultrasound in animal models of muscle contusions.

The study tested the hypothesis that a stable muscle contusion model can be constructed with a modified drop-mass method using ultrasound guidance, and that this model is useful for elucidating the mechanism of muscle contusion. The created models were evaluated using ultrasonography, histological analysis, gene expression analysis, and muscle strength tests. Furthermore, based on a comprehensive gene expression analysis of samples collected from the same model, an attempt was made to identify molecules associated with the healing process of muscle contusions.

Methods

Animals

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the authors’ affiliated institutions. All experiments in this study adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and an ARRIVE checklist is included in the Supplementary Material. Overall, 70 male Wistar Hannover rats (CLEA Japan, Japan) aged eight to 13 weeks were used in this study. The rats were housed (two per cage) in a temperature-controlled room with a 12-hour day/night cycle, and adequate amounts of food and water were provided ad libitum. A mixture of three agents was administered intraperitoneally to achieve analgesia and sedation: medetomidine (Domitor; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo, Japan) 0.3 mg/kg; midazolam (Dormicum; Maruishi Pharmaceutical, Japan) 4.0 mg/kg; and butorphanol (Vetorphale; Meiji Seika Pharma, Japan) 5.0 mg/kg.

Study design and sample size

In this study, muscle injury was induced in one lower limb. A total of 70 rats were initially enrolled in this study, of which two were excluded from the analysis due to the occurrence of tibial fractures. For the ultrasound evaluation, six rats were subjected to injury and underwent longitudinal assessments under anaesthesia at multiple timepoints: pre-injury, ten minutes post-injury, and on days 1, 3, 7, and 14, followed by euthanasia on day 14. For the gene expression analysis, 34 rats were used in total, with samples collected and euthanasia performed at pre-injury (n = 6) and at six hours (n = 6), 24 hours (n = 6), three days (n = 6), seven days (n = 6), and 14 days (n = 4) post-injury. Histological evaluation was performed on 30 animals, and tissue samples from 20 of these were also used for gene expression analysis. Thin sections were prepared from the entire tibialis anterior (TA) muscle using a cryostat. A small number of these sections were used for histological evaluation, while the remaining sections were used for total RNA extraction. This minimized the variability of the tissue source of total RNA between samples. For the muscle strength measurements, 24 rats were used. The measurements were performed at pre-injury for control data (n = 3), and at days 3 (n = 6), 7 (n = 6), 14 (n = 6), and 42 (n = 3) post-injury, with each animal being euthanized immediately following the respective assessment.

Development of a muscle contusion model by a modified drop-mass method using ultrasound

A custom-made device was designed based on the conventional drop-mass method, with modifications aimed at improving the reproducibility of muscle injury (Figure 1a). The contact area of the impactor tip was reduced, and the structure was modified to allow the mass to fall onto the impactor. The long axis of the impactor cross-section measured 18 mm, corresponding to the inner diameter of the cylinder, whereas the short axis measured 3 mm. The impactor tip was positioned on the skin above the TA muscle, perpendicular to its longitudinal axis. Ultrasound monitoring confirmed that the TA muscle was located directly between the impactor and the tibia when the hip joint was in maximum internal rotation, so the limb was fixed to maintain this position when creating muscle injury. The falling weight used in this model was 960 g, dropped from a height of 3 cm. The impactor was manually caught immediately after impact with the lower limbs to prevent sustained contact with the lower limb. This ensured that a single, brief impact was delivered per drop (Figures 1b and 1c).

Ultrasound examination

Ultrasound evaluation was performed according to previously described methods for studies involving the rat tibialis anterior muscle, including a rat tibialis anterior contusion model and ultrasound-based assessment of the tibialis anterior muscle.14,19 Briefly, the rats were evaluated using the SONIMAGE HS1 (Konica Minolta, Japan) ultrasound system and a linear probe HL18-4 (10 MHz) in B (brightness) mode. Short-axis ultrasound images of the TA muscle were acquired with the probe orientated as perpendicularly as possible to the muscle fibres and saved at 15 fps. Subsequently, each frame of the saved short-axis video images was reviewed, and the still image with the largest cross-sectional area of the TA muscle was selected. The region of interest was determined using the hand tool function of the Lenaraf 220b software (Vector Japan, Japan) and quantitatively assessed. This method demonstrated high intra- and interobserver reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Single comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-test, while multiple comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance and a post hoc Tukey–Kramer test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) or Ekuseru Toukei (Social Survey Research Information, Japan). Data are presented as mean (SD), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Ultrasound confirmation and temporal assessment of muscle injury

Ultrasound imaging revealed that the limb position with maximum internal hip rotation allowed the TA muscle to be positioned between the impactor and the tibia (Figure 1c). Macroscopic muscle tears were visually confirmed in 74% (32/43) of the animals during post-injury dissection (Figure 1e); however, abnormal ultrasound images, such as hypoechoic areas, were observed in all animals (Figure 1f). Temporal evaluation of the maximum muscle cross-sectional area using ultrasound demonstrated that the area was significantly increased compared with pre-injury levels at ten minutes and one and three days post-injury, but no significant difference was observed compared with pre-injury levels at seven days post-injury (Figure 1d). Muscle tears were confirmed histologically seven days post-injury in all six rats evaluated by ultrasound.

Verification of the healing process after muscle injury

Histological evaluation of the injured muscle tissue revealed that the fibrotic tissue stained blue with Masson’s trichrome staining on day 7 post-injury became significantly pronounced on day 14 post-injury, and coincided with areas strongly stained red with picrosirius red (PSR) (Figure 2a). Both the tetanic and twitch forces were significantly lower on the injured side than on the non-injured side at days 3 and 7 post-injury, with no significant differences observed at days 14 and 42 post-injury (Figure 2b). Gene expression analysis of muscle tissue on the day of injury and days 3, 7, and 14 demonstrated that interleukin (IL)-1β, tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-6, and IL-10 peaked on the day of muscle injury, while macrophage markers such as adhesion G protein-coupled receptor E1 (ADGRE1), cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68), and CD206 exhibited the highest expression on day 3 post-injury (Figure 2c).

Gene expression analysis

Principal component analysis of RNA sequencing data, comparing normal samples of the TA muscle with those collected at three and seven days post-injury, revealed similarities within each group and differences between the groups (Figure 3a). Additionally, differentially expressed genes were identified using criteria of fold change > 2.0 and false discovery rate < 0.05, revealing multiple genes with distinct expression patterns between three and seven days post-injury (Figure 3b). The most variable 100 genes were classified into six clusters using k-means clustering. Cluster A demonstrated relatively high expression at seven days post-injury compared with that at three days post-injury, while cluster B exhibited increased expression at three days post-injury (Figure 3c). The genes in cluster A included MyHC-embryonic (Myh3) and MyHC-neonatal (Myh8), juvenile isoforms of MyHC, and periostin (POSTN), a gene implicated in fibrosis (Figures 4a and 4b). Other factors included stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1/Scd1), delta-like non-canonical Notch ligand 1 (DLK1), and kallikrein-1 (KLK1), which have few or no reported associations with skeletal muscle injury. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis of temporal gene expression changes in the muscle contusion model confirmed that all these genes were highly expressed on day 7 post-injury (Figure 4c). Localization of the KLK1 protein in muscle tissue seven days post-injury was confirmed in F-actin-positive muscle fibre–like tissue adjacent to the scar and vascular tissues (Figure 4d).

Discussion

The key finding of this study is the establishment of a reproducible model of skeletal muscle contusion using a modified drop-mass method with ultrasound, enabling the exploration of novel factors involved in the healing of muscle contusions.

Muscle injury models include drug-induced models using myotoxins such as cardiotoxins, as well as laceration, freezing, ischaemia, and volumetric muscle loss models.7,8 These models were selected based on specific objectives, such as investigating pathophysiology or developing treatments, including for skeletal muscle regeneration research. Muscle contusion models using the drop-mass method, where heavy weights are dropped from a height onto the lower limbs, are widely used in academic research, with many studies11-16 citing adherence to the protocol established in two previous key studies.9,10 However, variations exist across studies in animal species, target muscles, and the drop-mass parameters – including shape, weight, and fall height – depending on the research group and experimental design.14,20-22 Furthermore, several studies have not provided detailed descriptions of the location and extent of muscle damage. In this study, we aimed to improve the reproducibility of injury creation by optimizing the limb position of the target animals using ultrasound evaluation, confirming the impact site, and reducing the height from which the weight was dropped. Temporal ultrasound evaluations, muscle strength tests, and acute-phase gene expression analyses confirmed that the muscle damage was stably induced. Ultrasound assessment also enabled quantitative assessment of hyperacute changes after injury, suggesting its usefulness as a model for acute treatment of muscle injury. Additionally, ultrasound visualization not only confirmed the creation of muscle injury but also allowed us to exclude animals with concomitant fractures, which is believed to be a significant contribution to the standardization of the model. The use of ultrasound in animal models of traumatic skeletal muscle injury has not been previously reported, and this study is expected to contribute to the understanding of the pathophysiology and the development of treatment methods.

To evaluate the reproducibility and validity of the present model, the injury and healing process was assessed from molecular, histological, and functional perspectives. The temporal changes in inflammatory and macrophage-related gene expression, the development of fibrotic tissue, and the transient reduction and subsequent recovery of muscle strength collectively support the reliability of this model as a traumatic muscle injury model. Several reports have described the promotion of the transcription of acute inflammatory factors following muscle contusion and the subsequent induction of macrophages.16,23,24 In the timing examined in the present model, the peak expression levels of each gene were six hours after injury for IL-1β and IL-6, 24 hours after injury for IL-10 and Arg1, and three days after injury for ADGRE1, CD68, and CD206. The anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 has been reported to play an important role in regulating the M1-to-M2 switch of macrophages in injured skeletal muscle. The expression pattern of IL-10 in this model is consistent with that in previous studies using a mouse muscle contusion model.23 Furthermore, in this model, collagen fibre regions stained blue with trichrome staining and observed as fibres with PSR, which were absent on day 3 after injury, were identified on day 7 and were observed bridging the muscle injury by day 14 after injury. These histological changes occurred during the period of progressive muscle recovery, demonstrating the validity of this model as a model of traumatic muscle injury. Additionally, in the present model, muscle strength deficits persisted on days 3 and 7 post-injury, with the formation of fibrotic tissue in the injured area. A comprehensive gene expression analysis aimed at identifying the factors involved in the healing process during this period recognized several genes associated with muscle regeneration and scar formation,25,26 as well as multiple genes whose functions in skeletal muscle remain unclear. One such gene was KLK1, which encodes a serine protease involved in physiological processes including vasoconstriction and vasodilation;27 however, the function of the gene in skeletal muscle injury and its healing process remain unknown. Based on its temporal and spatial expression patterns, the gene is expected to play a role in the healing of skeletal muscle injuries.

This study had several limitations. First, although the injury procedure was standardized using ultrasound guidance, minor inter-animal variability in the precise location and extent of muscle damage may have remained in the present study. However, the results of longitudinal ultrasound evaluation and gene expression analysis indicated that a certain degree of uniformity in the model was maintained, suggesting its potential utility for explaining the pathophysiology and developing therapeutic strategies. Nevertheless, the consideration of spatial differences in muscle injury sites is essential for their application in experiments. Second, it should be taken into consideration when using this model that muscle tears identified by ultrasound may vary depending on the attributes of the animals. Finally, the functional analysis of molecules, including KLK1, demonstrating transient expression promotion during the healing process of injured skeletal muscles in this model, has not been conducted. KLK1 is known for its diverse functions in other tissues.28 New findings are expected in the future through functional analysis based on temporal and spatial evaluation of protein expression.

In conclusion, it is possible to develop a reproducible model of skeletal muscle contusion using a modified drop-mass method with ultrasound. This model also exhibits potential for exploring the molecules involved in the healing process of muscle contusions.
