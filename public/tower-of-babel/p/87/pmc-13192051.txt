Evaluation of joint range of motion in Anatolian shepherd dogs: inter-observer reliability and radiographic validation
Authors: Kamil Serdar İnal, Elif Bağatır Kurban, Can Nacar, Taylan Önyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of the study was to determine reference values for joint range of motion (ROM) in Anatolian Shepherd dogs, assess the validity of manual goniometric measurements and validate these findings against radiographic measurements.MethodsSeventeen healthy adult Anatolian Shepherd dogs were included. Goniometric measurements of the shoulder, elbow, carpus, hip, stifle, and tarsus were performed in triplicate by three blinded observers. Standing joint angles were measured with minimal restraint, while full flexion and extension were assessed under sedation. Radiographic measurements of the same joints served as the reference method for validation.ResultsInter-observer reliability was moderate to excellent (ICC = 0.56–0.98) for the elbow, carpus, stifle, hip, and tarsus. However, the shoulder joint showed poor reliability (ICC = 0.465) for flexion. Comparison with radiography revealed no statistically significant differences for the shoulder, elbow, carpus, stifle, and tarsal joints (p > 0.05). Conversely, significant differences were found in the hip joint for both flexion and extension (p < 0.001), with goniometry underestimating flexion and overestimating extension.ConclusionPlastic goniometry is a valid and reliable tool for assessing distal joints in Anatolian Shepherd dogs. However, measurements of proximal joints require caution. While the mean goniometric values for the shoulder were accurate, the low inter-observer reliability indicates operator dependency. For the hip joint, the large gluteal muscle mass possibly creates a systematic error, rendering goniometry less accurate. Therefore, radiographic validation is recommended for precise ROM assessment of proximal joints in giant breeds. Deep sedation or anaesthesia appears essential for obtaining valid data by using the full ROM and ensuring the reliability of measurements in this breed, especially in aggressive animals.Keywords: Anatolian Shepherd Dogs, Goniometric, Radiographic, Joint, Measurements, ROM angles

Introduction

Goniometric joint angle measurements are a frequently used method in small animal clinical practice. Veterinary physiotherapists routinely use goniometry measurements, which are an inexpensive, convenient and accessible tool for assessing joint range of motion (ROM) [1, 2]. In previous studies, goniometry was used to monitor problems related to the carpal [3], elbow [4], stifle [5], and hip joints in dogs [6, 7]. Reference values are needed to assess ROM in orthopaedic examinations in dogs. Previous studies have reported the need to determine breed-specific ROM in dogs [8, 9]. The veterinary literature reports ROM in Labrador Retrievers [1], German Shepherds [10] and French Bulldogs [9]. Goniometric measurements in Anatolian Shepherd dogs were reported in one study [11]. A significant limitation in the current veterinary literature is the absence of reliable and valid data on goniometric joint angle measurements in Anatolian Shepherd dogs [11]. Specifically, no study has assessed the inter-observer reliability of this clinical method or compared its findings with radiographic measurements in this breed. However, the reliability of goniometric measurements is often challenged by inter- and intra-observer variability, necessitating rigorous methodological evaluation, particularly when applied to clinical decision-making. Therefore, this study aims to perform goniometric measurements of the shoulder, elbow, carpal, hip, stifle and tarsal joints in Anatolian Shepherd dogs and to compare the goniometric measurements with radiographic measurements. Moreover, the agreement among researchers regarding the goniometric measurements is assessed using intraclass correlation (ICC).

Materials and methods

This prospective study was conducted with the approval of the Ondokuz Mayıs University Animal Experiments Ethics Committee (Decision no: 2023/13). The inclusion criteria included being an Anatolian Shepherd dog aged 2–5 years, having no orthopaedic problems, no history of orthopaedic problems or trauma, not having a radiographically detectable joint disease and being brought in to our animal hospital for reasons other than orthopaedic symptoms. Dogs that met the inclusion criteria and were brought to the Veterinary Teaching Hospital of Ondokuz Mayıs University between 2023 and 2024 were included in the study. The signalment information (age, sex, body weight) of the dogs was recorded. Complete patient histories were obtained to ensure the absence of orthopaedic trauma. All cases underwent physical and orthopaedic examinations prior to inclusion in this study.

Goniometric measurements

The goniometric measurement of the joint in full flexion and full extension angles was performed under sedation, whereas measurements in neutral standing position of the joints were performed without sedation. Goniometric assessments were performed with a transparent plastic goniometer marked with 1° incremental gradations. The anatomical reference points and general measurement protocol utilised were conceptually derived from a previous study [1]. Goniometric measurements were performed independently by three researchers. To ensure blinding, the observers were unaware of one another’s recordings; the instrument’s angle scale was visually obscured during positioning, and the final values were read and documented by a separate fourth observer to eliminate potential reading bias (Fig. 1).

Each goniometric measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was recorded. Prior to initiating any measurement, the targeted joint was manually explored throughout its full ROM to establish the anatomical centre of rotation; the goniometer’s central pivot was placed over this determined axis. For the forelimb, carpal flexion and extension angles were determined using the long axis of the third and fourth metacarpal bones and the antebrachial longitudinal axis. This antebrachial axis was established by a line connecting the lateral humeral condyle to the antebrachium’s midpoint at the ulnar styloid process level. Carpal valgus and varus angles were measured as previously described [1]. Elbow and shoulder angles were determined relative to the longitudinal axes of the humerus, antebrachium and scapular spine. Similarly, hind limb angles (tarsus, stifle and hip) were assessed by aligning the arms with the long axes of the metatarsal bones, tibia and femur, referencing key landmarks, such as the lateral femoral epicondyle and the sacral tubercle and ischial tuberosity

Radiographic measurements

Propofol (4 mg/kg, intravenous) was used for the premedication and sedation of the dogs. The dogs were intubated. Mediolateral radiographic views were taken under sedation by placing the shoulder, elbow, carpal, hip, stifle and tarsal joints in full flexion and full extension. To ensure that the true physiological limits of each joint were reached, full flexion and extension were achieved manually by a trained assistant wearing protective lead apparel. The assistant applied steady pressure to the distal segment until the definitive tissue resistance (end-feel) was felt, at which point the radiograph was obtained. Radiographic image measurements were performed using Horos software, which utilises DICOM images. Measurements were based on the anatomical landmarks used as references in the goniometric measurements.

Statistical analysis

A power analysis (G*Power 3.1) was performed to calculate the minimum sample size (17 dogs) (p < 0.05, power 93% and Cohen’s d = 0.80) to reliably detect potential discrepancies between goniometric and radiographic measurements. Elbow joint ROM measurement data from a previous study were used to calculate this effect size [11]. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the means for body weight and age. Goniometric and radiographic measurements of the full flexion and full extension of the joints were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The ICC was examined to assess the agreement among researchers in the goniometric measurements of joint angles. The ICC values were interpreted according to the scale proposed by Portney, L.G [12]., where values less than 0.50 indicate poor reliability, between 0.50 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.90 indicate good reliability, and values greater than 0.90 indicate excellent reliability. A Repeated Measures ANOVA was used to compare the mean measurements obtained by the three different researchers. Furthermore, a dependent samples t-test was used to compare the mean values of the three researchers’ goniometric measurements and radiographic measurements of joint angles. The SPSS (IBM, Version 21 for Mac) package was used for the statistical analysis of the data. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Seventeen Anatolian Shepherd dogs (male: 10, female: 7) met the inclusion criteria and were included in this study. Although five dogs partially met the inclusion criteria, they were excluded from the study because radiographic examination revealed hip dysplasia in four dogs and elbow dysplasia in one dog. The mean age was 46 ± 22 months, and the mean body weight was 51.2 ± 9.9 kg. The dogs included in the study had no history of trauma, lameness, clinical or radiographic orthopaedic problems, such as degenerative joint disease. The normal standing joint angle was measured without sedation. Due to the dogs’ anxious behaviour in the examination room, they were held by their owners during the measurements. Goniometric measurements of full flexion and full extension could not be performed without sedation, as the dogs would not allow it. Repeated measures ANOVA showed no statistically significant differences among the three observers for any of the goniometric measurements (p > 0.05), indicating high inter-observer reliability. The inter-observer agreement is shown in Table 1. Comparison of goniometric and radiographic measurements revealed no statistically significant differences for the shoulder, elbow, carpal, stifle, and tarsal joints (p > 0.05). Significant differences were observed only in the hip joint, both in full flexion (p < 0.001) and full extension (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, joint angles in Anatolian shepherd dogs were measured using goniometry by three researchers in a blind fashion at full flexion, full extension, and a standing position. Joint angles were measured radiographically from radiographs taken at full flexion and full extension. We selected the Anatolian Shepherd Dog for this study because it is a very popular breed of sheepdog used in our region as a livestock guardian dog against predatory animals. Although it originates from Anatolia, the breed is popular worldwide, particularly in the USA, as well as in Anatolia and the Near East [11].

In a similar study conducted on Labrador Retriever dogs, no statistically significant differences were reported between goniometric and radiographic measurements [1]. A similar situation was observed in the Anatolian Shepherd dog, a giant breed, where no statistically significant differences were found between the two methods. Measurements taken with plastic goniometry are simpler, more practical, involve no radiation exposure, and are less expensive than radiographic techniques [1]. Furthermore, they can be performed without sedation in cooperative dogs. No statistically significant differences were reported in Labrador Retrievers when goniometric measurements were taken with and without sedation [1]. Although sedated and non-sedated measurements were not directly compared in this study, non-sedated measurements were considered unfeasible in these dogs due to their potential anxiety in the clinic. Therefore, joint angles were measured under sedation to ensure complete muscle relaxation and to establish reliable reference values.

The statistical differences observed between the goniometric and radiographic assessments in this study can largely be attributed to the substantial soft tissue envelope characterising giant breed dogs. In contrast to the direct osseous visualisation provided by radiographs, clinical goniometry relies heavily on the tactile identification of anatomical landmarks. Previous research on breeds with lighter distinct muscle mass, such as Labradors, has already noted that soft tissue around proximal joints interferes with landmark palpation [1]. In giant breeds, this issue is more pronounced. The heavy muscle groups surrounding the humerus, femur and hip create a barrier that makes the correct placement of a plastic goniometer difficult. Radiography offers a clear advantage by directly revealing osseous landmarks independent of the surrounding muscle mass. This allows for a more definitive measurement in these patients, effectively eliminating the soft tissue impediments that restrict external goniometry.

The goniometric measurements of Anatolian Shepherd dogs were reported in one study in which the standing joint and ROM angles of the dogs were measured. A single researcher performed the measurements [11]. To validate the data measured in our study and increase its reliability, three researchers measured the joint angles, and an ICC assessment was performed. Our results revealed that the ICC coefficient values ranged in the distal joints between 0.56 and 0.98, indicating moderate to excellent inter-observer reliability [12]. The relatively lower inter-observer reliability (ICC = 0.567, p = 0.018) observed for carpal varus can be primarily attributed to the very narrow range of these angular values. In our study, the mean valgus 11 ± 2° and varus angles 11 ± 2° were extremely narrow. When measuring such restricted ranges of motion, even a minimal, expected manual goniometric error (e.g., 2–3 degrees) can yield a disproportionately large variance. Because the ICC is highly sensitive to the true variance of the sample relative to measurement error, the naturally small biological variation in carpal varus/valgus mathematically limits the achievable reliability. A discrepancy was also identified in the measurements of the shoulder joint. Despite the absence of a statistically significant disparity between the mean goniometric flexion angle (48 ± 5°) and the radiographic flexion angle (50 ± 5°), the inter-observer reliability of the goniometric measurements was found to be poor (ICC = 0.465). This finding suggests that the measurement of shoulder flexion in Anatolian Shepherd Dogs is mainly dependent on the operator. The observed variations in measurements may be attributed to the challenges associated with identifying the scapular spine and the shoulder joint within substantial muscle tissue. However, the researchers’ combined average was consistent with radiography, indicating that measurement errors were random rather than systematic. Therefore, while goniometry can provide an accurate estimate when the mean of multiple measurements is taken, practitioners should be aware of the variability in the shoulder joint. In contrast, the lower reliability and accuracy in the hip joint appears to be systematic due to the massive gluteal musculature which obscures the palpable landmarks of the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity. Unlike the random error seen in the shoulder, the hip measurements showed a statistically significant deviation from the radiographic measurements in both flexion and extension (p < 0.001). Specifically, the thick soft tissue envelope likely caused an artificial elevation of the goniometer arms, resulting in an underestimation of flexion and an overestimation of extension. Consequently, while goniometry is adequate for the forelimb and distal hind limb, we suggest radiographic confirmation for the precise assessment of the hip joint in these giant breeds.

ROM and standing joint angles in dogs are valuable information used in corrective surgery for joint arthrodesis and angular deformities in the extremities [13, 14]. According to the veterinary literature, it has been reported in Greyhounds [15], Labrador Retrievers [1], chondrodystrophic versus non-chondrodystrophic breeds [16], French Bulldogs [9] and Anatolian Shepherd dogs [11]. However, studies have shown minor differences in ROM between breeds [16]. Therefore, the data obtained from the ROM measurements must be verified. In our study, joint angle measurements were performed by multiple researchers, and agreement between researchers was evaluated, which increased the accuracy of the measurement data. Furthermore, joint angle measurements performed using correctly identified radiographic landmarks were mainly consistent with those obtained using goniometric measurements.

A limitation of this study is that the ranges of motion measured under sedation may not reflect the actual range of motion when the dog is conscious, possessing normal muscle tone and proprioception. Another limitation regarding manual goniometry is the difficulty in perfectly aligning the goniometer’s rotational axis with the joint’s center of rotation, which may have introduced variability between observers.

In conclusion, the present study indicates that plastic goniometry is a practical and reliable tool for evaluating joint ROM in Anatolian Shepherd dogs, particularly for the distal extremities (elbow, carpus, stifle, and tarsus). However, given the operator-dependency observed in the shoulder and the soft-tissue interference in the hip, radiographic confirmation is recommended for the precise assessment of proximal joints. In addition, given the physical magnitude and reserved temperament of this breed, the administration of sedation appears essential, as it ensures the necessary muscle relaxation for valid ROM determination. The breed-specific reference data established in this study provide a valuable foundation for orthopaedic examinations, the diagnosis of joint pathologies, and the planning of corrective surgery.
