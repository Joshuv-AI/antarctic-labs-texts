Partial Hydrogenation of Aryl-Substituted Benzothiophenes, Benzofurans and Indoles by Photocatalytic Water Activation
Authors: Robin Mayska, Armido Studer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transition-metal-catalyzed hydrogenation of heteroarenes provides direct access to three-dimensional, semisaturated scaffolds but remains particularly challenging for sulfur-containing compounds due to catalyst poisoning and undesired desulfurization. As a result, general methods for the direct catalytic hydrogenation of aryl-substituted benzothiophenes to the corresponding 2,3-dihydrobenzothiophenes (DHBTs) are lacking. Herein, we report a photocatalytic phosphine-mediated radical hydrogenation using water as the hydrogen source, enabling direct access to DHBTs. The generality of this method was demonstrated by a broad substrate scope, including diverse aryl- and heteroaryl-substituted substrates as well as related benzofurans, indoles, and other N-heterocycles. Gram-scale and deuteration experiments further highlight the practicality of this approach.

Semisaturated heterocycles are common motifs in pharmaceuticals and bioactive molecules. − These compounds have attracted increasing interest, as higher degrees of saturation and three-dimensionality are associated with enhanced solubility, potency and metabolic stability, thereby increasing the probability of clinical success. − Since drug candidates are often assembled from (hetero)­aromatic building blocks via cross-coupling reactions, , they frequently exhibit low degrees of saturation and limited three-dimensionality. − In this context, hydrogenation provides a powerful strategy to access the corresponding semisaturated structures from readily available (hetero)­arenes, and is therefore of significant interest. ,

Benzothiophenes (BT) frequently serve as key structural motifs in medicinal chemistry and materials science (Figure ). − Despite their prevalence, the direct catalytic hydrogenation of 2- or 3-substituted BTs into the corresponding semisaturated 2,3-dihydrobenzothiophenes (DHBT) remains a significant challenge in organic synthesis. This can be attributed to the strong coordinating ability of both substrate and product, which can lead to catalyst poisoning. − In addition, the relatively weak C–S bond in DHBTs can promote undesired C–S bond cleavage, resulting in hydrogenolysis side reactions. , Because of these challenges, the first catalytic hydrogenation of substituted BTs was not reported until 2012 by Glorius and co-workers (Scheme A). , Although this enantioselective method represents a breakthrough, its application is limited to alkyl-substituted BTs. Thereafter, a few alternative catalytic strategies have been developed that are capable of reducing substituted BTs, including photoredox- and electrocatalytic approaches. ,− However, examples of 2- or 3-aryl-substituted BTs were not reported in these studies. Considering that heterobiaryl scaffolds are prevalent in pharmaceuticals, − the development of general methods for their partial hydrogenation is highly desirable. To the best of our knowledge, the only closely related example was reported recently by Wu and co-workers, who achieved dearomatizing deuteration of two aryl-substituted BTs using electrophotocatalysis. Pfaltz and co-workers reported an indirect approach based on benzothiophene 1,1-dioxides for asymmetric hydrogenation, taking advantage of their reduced aromaticity and coordination ability, followed by reduction to afford DHBTs (Scheme B). The hydrogenation step was subsequently further optimized by other groups. , However, the feasibility of the final deoxygenation step was demonstrated only for alkyl-substituted substrates. Therefore, catalytic methods for the direct hydrogenation of aryl-substituted BTs remain underexplored, limiting access to the corresponding chemical space.

Semihydrogenation of Benzothiophenes and Derivatives

Our group has recently developed a photocatalytic, phosphine-mediated radical hydrogenation system that enables the reduction of alkenes, naphthalenes and quinolines, employing water as the hydrogen source (Scheme C). , The key intermediate of these transformations is the phosphoranyl radical Ar3P•–OH, operating as a hydrogen atom donor due to its exceptionally low H–O bond dissociation energy (<10 kcal/mol). The phosphoranyl radical Ar3P•–OH is generated by oxidation of the corresponding phosphine, followed by nucleophilic attack of water with deprotonation. Encouraged by these studies, we questioned whether this strategy could be extended to more challenging substrates. Herein, we report that this approach enables the partial hydrogenation of aryl-substituted BTs, providing direct access to the corresponding DHBTs under mild catalytic conditions (Scheme D).

The generality of the method is further demonstrated by extending its scope to related heterocycles including thienopyridines, benzofurans, and indoles. Gram-scale and deuteration experiments using inexpensive D2O additionally highlight the practicality of our method.

We initiated our studies using 2-phenylbenzothiophene 1a as a model substrate and our previously reported reaction conditions, employing the Ir-photocatalyst PC1 ([Ir­(dF­(CF3)­ppy)2(dtbbpy)]­PF6), phosphine P1 (P­(p-MeOC6H4)3), HAT-catalyst HAT1 (2,4,6-triisopropylbenzenethiol), and water in acetonitrile under irradiation at 456 nm for 24 h. Under these conditions, DHBT 3a was formed in 37% NMR yield (Table S1). Control experiments confirmed the necessity of all reaction components. A screening of various phosphines did not reveal a superior alternative to P1 (Table S2). Subsequent studies identified the reaction time as a critical parameter. Prolonged irradiation led to decomposition of the product, likely due to oxidative side reaction associated with the high oxidation potential of PC1. Decreasing the reaction time to 16 h improved the NMR yield to 50% (Table S3). Further optimization of the reagent loadings revealed that slight reductions in the amount of P1, HAT1, and water were beneficial, increasing the NMR yield to 64% (see Tables S4–S6, entry 2). Finally, adjusting the photocatalyst loading, in combination with an 18 h reaction time, afforded 3a in an isolated yield of 71% (Table S7). Additional variations of solvent, photocatalyst, HAT catalyst, and light source did not result in any further improvement of the yield (see Tables S8–S10).

After optimization of the reaction conditions, we investigated the scope of 2-aryl-substituted BTs (Scheme , Conditions A). BTs with meta- or ortho-methyl substitution on the aryl moiety afforded the corresponding DHBTs 3b and 3c in moderate to very good yields. Notably, the meta-substituted derivative 3b required a shortened reaction time (14 h), as prolonged irradiation led to decomposition, whereas the ortho-substituted analogue 3c showed no degradation and required extended reaction times (72 h) to achieve full conversion. The longer reaction time required for 3c is likely due to reduced stabilization of the radical intermediate generated after hydrogen atom transfer (see mechanistic discussion below), resulting from steric hindrance associated with the ortho substituent. Fluorinated aryl substituents were well-tolerated, affording 3d and 3e in moderate yields. A meta-trimethylsilylphenyl-substituted BT delivered 3f in 53% yield. Substitution on the benzothiophene core was also feasible, as demonstrated by the successful preparation of 6-methoxy-substituted DHBT 3g in 40% yield.

Next, we investigated the scope of 3-aryl-substituted benzothiophenes (Scheme ). To achieve full conversion, a slightly increased photocatalyst loading and extended reaction times were required (Conditions B). Interestingly, in contrast to the 2-aryl-substituted substrates, no product decomposition was observed, even upon prolonged irradiation. We first examined 3-phenylbenzothiophene 2a and a series of para-substituted derivatives bearing electronically diverse substituents (2b–2f). The corresponding DHBTs 4a–4f were obtained in good to excellent yields (76%–94%), indicating minimal electronic effects. For substrate 2c, an extended reaction time (5 days) was necessary to reach full conversion, which may be attributed to competing degenerative dehydrogenation of 4c initiated through the amine oxidation by the photocatalyst. Meta-substituted aryl-BTs were similarly well-tolerated, affording 4g and 4h in yields of 82% and 76%. Consistent with the 2-aryl series, ortho-methyl substitution required prolonged reaction times, delivering 4i in 86% yield after 72 h. The method proved to be broadly applicable to heteroaryl-substituted substrates, with 4j–4l isolated in very good to excellent yields (85%–96%) without competing hydrogenation of the heteroaryl moieties, highlighting the excellent chemoselectivity of the transformation. Substitution of the benzothiophene core was also tolerated, as shown by the isolation of 4m and 4n in very good yields. Furthermore, disubstituted BT 2o bearing both an alkyl and an aryl substituent was hydrogenated in excellent yield (98%), demonstrating that disubstituted BTs are also well-tolerated. The applicability of the methodology to late-stage modification of bioactive compounds was demonstrated by the successful hydrogenation of arginine vasopressin receptor antagonist 2p and estrone-derived BT 2q. The low yield of 4q is likely attributed to the poor solubility of 2q in MeCN, as most of the unreacted material could be recovered.

Finally, selected nonaryl-substituted BTs bearing phosphonate (1h), amide (2r), or ether substituents (2s) were successfully converted to their corresponding DHBTs (3h, 4r, 4s) in moderate to good yield (30%–79%), showing that the method is not limited to the hydrogenation of aryl-substituted BTs.

Motivated by these findings, we explored even more challenging thienopyridine substrates. Thienopyridines are frequently encountered in bioactive molecules. − However, selective hydrogenation of this scaffold remains largely underexplored, with only a single report limited to alkyl-substituted substrates. As a result, general methods enabling access to partially saturated thienopyridine derivatives are lacking, motivating further investigation. Under the optimized conditions, dihydrothienopyridines 4t–4w were obtained in good to excellent yield (73%–91%). Notably, 4t was formed in excellent yield, while [2,3-b]-thienopyridines have been reported to be challenging substrates in metal-catalyzed hydrogenation due to strong bidentate coordination. In addition, the successful preparation of 4u and 4v further demonstrates the applicability of the method to structurally diverse heteroaryl systems. The more nitrogen-rich dihydrothieno­[2,3-b]­pyrazine 4w was similarly obtained in very good yield. Importantly, competing hydrogenation of the N-heteroarene moieties in these compounds was not observed, further highlighting the high chemoselectivity achieved for these radical hydrogenations.

To evaluate the generality of the radical hydrogenation strategy across different heterocyclic classes, we also examined structurally related benzofurans and N-protected indoles, as well as selected pyridine containing analogues, using adjusted conditions C and D in Scheme . 2,3-Dihydrobenzofurans 7a–7d and 2,3-dihydrofuro­[3,2-b]­pyridine 7e were obtained in 71%–82% yield, demonstrating the compatibility of both 2- and 3-aryl-substituted benzofuran scaffolds. Substrate 7d highlights tolerance toward aryl bromides, which are frequently prone to hydrodehalogenation under hydrogenation conditions. Boc- and Cbz-protected 2-phenyl-2,3-dihydroindoles 8a and 8b were obtained in excellent yields, while Cbz-deprotection is commonly observed under Pd/C–H2 hydrogenation conditions. Additionally, alkyl- and aryl-disubstituted indole 6c was successfully reduced in good yield (76%). The transformation was also extended to pyrrolo­[2,3-b]­pyridine 8d, which was isolated in good yield. 3-Aryl-substituted indoles were similarly well-tolerated, as demonstrated by the preparation of 8e and 8f, with O-benzyl-protected 8f further highlighting the compatibility of hydrogenolysis-sensitive protecting groups. In addition, ester-substituted 2,3-dihydroindole 8g was obtained in excellent yield, suggesting that selected nonaryl substituents are also tolerated. Thus, radical hydrogenation via water activation provides a H2-gas-free and chemoselective alternative for the reduction of benzofurans, indoles, and related N-heterocycles.

To assess the practicality of the method, a gram-scale hydrogenation was performed using 5.0 mmol of 3-aryl-substituted BT 2d (Scheme A; see the SI for details). The corresponding DHBT 4d was isolated in a 97% yield, demonstrating the excellent scalability of the reaction.

Deuteration experiments of 2d were conducted to evaluate the use of D2O as an inexpensive deuterium source and to gain mechanistic insights (see Scheme B). The expected 4d-D 2 was obtained in 51% yield along with 41% of trideuterated 4d-D 3 and 5% of monodeuteration (4d-D, not shown in the scheme, see the SI). The 2,3-dideuterated product 4d-D 2 was formed as a 1:1 diastereomeric mixture. A control experiment using 4d as the substrate with D2O under otherwise standard conditions revealed that the hydrogenation product did not engage in any detectable H/D exchange. Additionally, the deuteration of 2d was repeated and terminated after 6 h, and the remaining starting material was analyzed, revealing the formation of 2d-D (49% D; see the SI).

Based on these experimental results, the following mechanism is proposed, exemplified for the hydrogenation of 3-aryl-substituted 2d (Scheme C; for the analogous mechanism of 2-aryl-substituted BTs, see the SI (Scheme S2)). , The excited Ir­(III)-complex oxidizes the triaryl phosphine P1 to the corresponding radical cation. Trapping with water with concomitant deprotonation leads to the phosphoranyl radical Ar3P•–OH, which engages in a hydrogen atom transfer to the less-substituted carbon of the thiophene moiety in 2d, generating the more-stabilized tertiary C-radical intermediate A and triaryl phosphine oxide (P1=O). As deduced by the isolation of the trideuteration product 4d-D 3 , radical A can likely be oxidized through single electron transfer and subsequent deprotonation leads to the reformation of starting material 2d in a degenerative process. Upon running the reaction in D2O, this degenerative process will lead to the formation of 2d and also its monodeuterated analogue 2d-D. However, in the productive path, radical A gets reduced by the thiol cocatalyst to afford the final product 4d along with an aryl thiyl radical. The latter gets reduced by the Ir­(II) complex, closing the photoredox catalysis cycle. The thus formed thiolate anion is eventually protonated to close the thiol catalysis cycle.

In conclusion, we have developed a photocatalytic phosphine-mediated radical hydrogenation method that enables the partial hydrogenation of aryl-substituted BTs using water as the hydrogen source, providing direct access to the corresponding DHBTs. The generality of this approach was demonstrated by a broad substrate scope, including various aryl- and heteroaryl-substituted substrates and related benzofurans, indoles, and N-heterocycles. A gram-scale experiment and deuteration labeling using inexpensive D2O further emphasize the practicality and synthetic utility of this method. Taken together, this work provides a complementary strategy to established hydrogenation methods, enabling access to aryl-substituted DHBTs that were previously difficult to obtain.
