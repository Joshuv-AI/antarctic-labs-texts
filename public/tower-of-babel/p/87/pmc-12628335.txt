Mesoporous Silica Nanoparticles for Quercetin-Controlled Delivery to Protect Cisplatin-Induced Ototoxicity
Authors: Muyao Shen, Handong Wang, Junfeng Liu, Zixin Zhou, Xingguang Wu, Hanqi Fan, Haoliang Du, Yue Qiu, Dingding Liu, Dengbin Ma, Yong Wang, Zhao Tang, Shu Yang, Yuan Zhang, Tianhong Zhang, Xia Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cisplatin (CDDP), a widely used chemotherapeutic agent, is limited by severe ototoxicity side effects. Local drug delivery via the middle ear represents the most effective approach for treating inner ear disease. However, therapeutic efficacy is constrained by poor middle ear retention and limited permeability across the round window membrane (RWM). Quercetin (QU) exhibits potent activity against CDDP-induced cytotoxicity but suffers from delivery challenges. To address this, we developed amino-functionalized mesoporous silica nanoparticles (NH2-MSNs) loaded with QU (QU-N-MSNs), leveraging the permselective properties of the RWM. This system was noninvasively administered to the cochlea via trans-tympanic delivery. The synthesized QU-N-MSNs demonstrated a uniform particle size of approximately 116–124 nm, positive charge, and sustained drug release properties. Compared to free QU, QU-N-MSNs demonstrated significantly enhanced antiapoptotic and cytoprotective activities in vitro. In vivo studies confirmed nanoparticle retention within the inner ear for ≥14 days post administration and efficient RWM penetration. Pretreatment with QU-N-MSNs prior to CDDP exposure in murine models substantially mitigated ototoxicity, as evidenced by reduced hearing threshold shifts across multiple frequencies, preservation of hair cells (HCs) and spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs), and attenuation of mitochondrial-mediated SGN apoptosis. These findings establish QU-N-MSNs as an effective RWM-penetrating delivery platform, offering a promising strategy to enhance hydrophobic drug bioavailability in the inner ear and prevent CDDP-induced ototoxicity.

Introduction

Cisplatin (CDDP) is widely used in the chemotherapy of human cancers, with severe dose-limiting toxicities. It causes permanent hearing loss in 80% of patients by inducing oxidative stress in cochlear structures, which triggers inflammatory and apoptotic damage. Effective clinical interventions remain elusive. , Quercetin (QU), a dietary flavonoid, shows promise in mitigating CDDP-induced ototoxicity through its antioxidant and antiapoptotic properties. − In contrast to many other otoprotective agents, preclinical studies demonstrate that QU does not compromise the antitumor efficacy of CDDP. − On the contrary, it exhibits low toxicity and may reduce tumor growth independently. ,− However, its clinical translation is hindered by poor solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid degradation. Nanoparticle delivery systems have emerged as promising solutions to this challenge. Various drug delivery methods of QU have been explored, including polymers, liposomes, chitosan, and others. ,

The silica nanoparticles (SiNPs), approved by the American Food and Drug Administration, are widely used in the food industry, clinical imaging diagnosis, and drug delivery , Among them, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) stand out for controlled drug release, offering tunable structures, versatile surface functionalization, high surface area, and excellent biocompatibility. Particularly promising for inner ear drug delivery, , MSNs can safely degrade into nontoxic silicic acid in the body and have demonstrated human safety at doses up to 9 g/day. , The safety of MSNs in combination with the round window injection technique has also been demonstrated. Moreover, MSNs show great potential as QU carriers.

The blood-labyrinth barrier (BLB) limits inner ear drug concentrations achieved through systemic administration. While dose escalation could potentially overcome this barrier, risks of systemic toxicity. , The round window membrane (RWM) presents an attractive alternative route, offering superior drug absorption compared to oral or intravenous delivery. However, direct RWM puncture can lead to further hearing damage, , highlighting the need for less invasive approaches. Our study employs an optimized drug delivery strategy that utilizes middle ear administration for subsequent RWM infiltration, enabling inner ear access while preserving membrane integrity.

The ability of nanoparticle delivery systems to penetrate the RWM is critical for noninvasive drug delivery via RWM infiltration. The success of nanoparticle-mediated drug delivery across RWM depends on three key physicochemical parameters: size, shape, and surface charge. Nanoparticle size critically determines RWM permeability and biodistribution, where sub-200 nm particles exhibit optimal tissue penetration, colloidal stability, and cellular uptake. − However, excessively small particles (<100 nm) may induce inflammation. Spherical nanoparticles exhibit superior membrane interaction. The surface charge of nanoparticles is also an important factor for delivery efficiency, as positive charges promote cellular uptake. , Both QU and unmodified MSNs exhibit negative surface charges, potentially limiting their uptake and necessitating surface modification. The incorporation of amino groups can not only reverse surface charge to promote binding to cell membranes but also enhance the hydrophobic drug loading capacity of nanoparticles. Nevertheless, excessive positive charges induce cytotoxicity, while neutral/slightly positive surfaces maximize blood circulation time and target accumulation. , In summary, the amino group modification of MSNs presents a promising strategy for delivering hydrophobic drugs to the inner ear. Suitable spherical nanoparticles (near-zero or slightly positively charged, between 100 and 200 nm in size) have the capability to penetrate the inner ear via RWM, potentially offering prevention and treatment for inner ear disorders.

In our research, we developed an amino group-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticle system (NH2-MSNs) encapsulating QU (QU-N-MSNs) with sustained release and applied a more advanced noninvasive method of RWM infiltration to deliver QU-N-MSNs to the inner ear in murine models. To investigate the potential clinical application of QU-N-MSNs, we first validated that QU had superior cytoprotective properties against CDDP-induced damage. Motivated by a better cytoprotective and antiapoptotic performance of QU-N-MSNs compared with free QU, we verified the ability of QU-N-MSNs to improve uptake efficiency in vitro. In vivo, we confirmed the penetrating capacity of microspheres through the RWM into the inner ear at multiple scales and verified the safety of the noninvasive RWM route. The QU-N-MSNs combined the RWM penetration ability of microspheres and the cytoprotective and antiapoptotic efficacy of QU. Afterward, we move our evaluation of QU-N-MSNs forward in a CDDP-induced hearing loss (CIHL) murine model. We investigated QU-N-MSNs’ protective effects against CDDP-induced ototoxicity and underlying mechanisms both in vitro and in vivo. Our findings provide insights into a novel, practical therapeutic approach that combines the optimization of drug administration and the advancement of drug delivery systems for mitigating CIHL.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

The mouse cochlear hair cell line (HEI-OC1) was obtained from Prof. Renjie Chai (Institute of Life Sciences, Southeast University, Nanjing, China) and incubated in DMEM (Gibco, USA) enriched with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) in an incubator at a temperature of 37 °C.

Preparation of QU-N-MSNs

The MSNs were synthesized using a modified sol–gel method as previously described. The cationic surfactant CTAC (H105309, Aladdin) was used as a template, TEOS was used as a silica source, TEA (T431270, Aladdin) was used as a catalyst, and organic solvent 1-octadecene (O109488, Aladdin) was used as an emulsion agent. Briefly, CTAC solution (10 wt %) and TEA (0.3 wt %) were added to water, followed by a gentle stir at 60 °C for 60 min. Next, the TEOS (20 v/v %) in 1-octadecene was added to the mixture and stirred for 12 h to guide the formation of mesoporous structures. After that, the products were harvested via centrifugation, then washed and repeated three times, and the residual organic material was removed through calcination (550 °C for 6 h). The final powder was stored at room temperature for further study.

One-step synthesis of NH2-MSNs using a modified sol–gel method. Briefly, the procedure for the formation of the aqueous phase is the same as that above, with the addition of 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (A107147, Aladdin) after the mixture is added to the upper layer of the aqueous phase. Followed by centrifugation, washing, and calcination, the precipitates of NH2-MSNs were collected.

For the QU-N-MSNs, QU and NH2-MSNs were added in ethanol and then underwent ultrasonic dispersion and magnetic stirring at 37 °C for 24 h. After ethanol washing, the product was recovered by centrifugation and lyophilization. QU-MSNs were prepared using MSNs in the same way.

Characterization of QU-N-MSNs

N2 adsorption–desorption analysis was conducted to determine the specific surface area and pore volume of microspheres using an automated specific surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics 3Flex, USA). The specific surface area and pore size distribution were calculated with the BET and BJH formulas.

The morphology of QU-N-MSNs was examined by SEM (Hitachi Regulus 8100, Japan). TEM (FEI Talos F200X G2, USA) was used to analyze the inner channel. An Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS, Oxford X-MAX) was applied to the elemental analysis of microspheres. The composition was verified by FTIR spectrometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA). Electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) was used to evaluate the zeta potential of the QU-N-MSNs. The dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Nano ZS90, UK) method was used for measuring the particle sizes of the QU-N-MSNs. The ImageJ software was used to count the microsphere particle size under the SEM microscope as well.

Measurement of Drug Loading

An ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer was used to determine the absorption spectrum of QU solution at different concentrations, and the standard curve was generated based on the absorbance value at peak absorbance.

The QU and NH2-MSNs solution (1:1, 2:1, 4:1 ratios) was prepared, and the mixture was centrifuged to collect the supernatant (3000 rpm, 10 min). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of QU-N-MSNs and QU-MSNs were calculated using the following equations: EE=mdrug,total−mdrug,supernatantmdrug,total×100% DL=mdrug,total−mdrug,supernatantmNPs+mdrug,total−mdrug,supernatant×100% where m drug,total and m drug,supernatant are the total amount of drug and the amount of drug in the supernatant, respectively, and m NPs is the total mass of NPs.

In Vitro Release of QU-N-MSNs

An equal amount of QU-N-MSNs and QU-MSNs was added into a 1 × PBS buffer containing 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and mixed well. The solution was divided into equal aliquots, and the supernatant was collected after rapid centrifugation at different time points. Absorption levels were gauged at 256 nm wavelength, and a fitting curve was established using OriginPro 2024 software to assess the drug release behavior.

Biological Effects of a Drug

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of drug QU (Q794164, Macklin) in vitro, HEI-OC1 cells were incubated with either QU (2–800 μM) or DMEM (Control) for 12 h. Cell viability was detected using a CCK-8 assay kit (C0037, Beyotime), and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was derived.

The cytoprotective efficacy of QU was assessed by pretreating cells with graded QU concentrations (0.000005–200 μM) for 12 h prior to CDDP (30 μM diluted in DMEM, 24 h). In parallel, the CDDP group was pretreated with DMEM (12 h) and followed by CDDP (30 μM) treatment for 24 h. The Control group was maintained in DMEM throughout the entire period. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) was evaluated according to the cell viability data.

Biocompatibility Assay and Dose of QU-N-MSNs In Vitro

Considering the cytoprotective effect of QU, HEI-OC1 cells were treated with either NH2-MSNs or PBS (Control) for 1, 2, and 3 days to investigate the biocompatibility of QU-N-MSNs. The biocompatibility of NH2-MSNs was evaluated through a Live/Dead cell staining kit (CA1630, Solarbio), followed by imaging with a fluorescence microscope. To further explore the optimal dose of QU-N-MSNs, HEI-OC1 cells were pretreated with QU or QU-N-MSNs (6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 360 μM) for 12 h and then exposed to CDDP (30 μM) for 24 h. The culture method for the Control group and CDDP group was the same as that used in the EC50 assay for the efficacy of QU, using CCK-8 reagent and evaluating the OD value at 450 nm.

Cell Apoptosis Analysis of QU-N-MSNs In Vitro

We further investigated the protective ability of QU-N-MSNs against CDDP-induced apoptosis in vitro to reveal its deeper protective mechanism. HEI-OC1 cells were pretreated with NH2-MSNs, QU (18 μM), and QU-N-MSNs for 12 h and exposed to CDDP (30 μM, 24 h). The CDDP group was pretreated with DMEM (12 h) and followed by CDDP (30 μM) treatment for 24 h. The Control group was maintained in DMEM throughout the entire period. The apoptosis of HEI-OC1 cells was determined via flow cytometry analysis with an Annexin V Fluor647/PI double staining kit (FMSAM647-100, Fcmacs) and CytoflexS flow cytometer (Beckman, USA).

Cellular Uptake Assay In Vitro

The use of FITC-labeled microspheres represents a well-established and reliable technique. FITC and QU share similar molecular (389 and 302 g/mol) weights and charge characteristics. − Since these fluorescent markers share overlapping emission spectra with QU, we directly labeled the nanospheres to track their cellular uptake and intracellular distribution to reflect the behavior of QU-N-MSNs. , Cells were incubated with FITC@N-MSNs or FITC (Control) for 2, 12, or 24 hours and stained with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium (ZLI-9556, ZSGB-BIO). Fluorescent signals were captured using an APX100 microscope.

Evaluation of RWM Penetration In Vivo

To assess the penetrating-RWM-capacity of microspheres at multiple scales, the distribution of microspheres in the inner ear was examined using a combined approach that integrated tissue sectioning and in vivo imaging. Building on previous findings demonstrating that fluorescent dye loading does not significantly alter microsphere particle size. We strategically selected two fluorescent tracers, IR780 and rhodamine B (RhoB) (with comparable molecular weights as QU , but opposite charges , ) as fluorescent probes for tracer studies to ensure the reliability of our experimental results.

Briefly, the NH2-MSNs were labeled with IR780 (IR780@N-MSNs) and RhoB (RhoB@N-MSNs) via magnetic stirring-assisted adsorption. The adsorption process was conducted at room temperature under light-protected conditions for 24 h. Subsequently, the two types of labeled nanoparticles were separately injected into the left ear of the mice. For evaluating the long retention time of NH2-MSNs in mice in the injected local area, the fluorescence signals of IR780@N-MSNs were captured using an IVIS spectroscopy system (AniView600, Guangzhou, China) at hours 1, 6, 24, and days 3, 5, 7, 9, and 14 after injection (Control group received no untreated). Then we opened the bulla and detected the fluorescence signal emitted from the mouse cochlea on day 14 and used the IVIS spectroscopy system to investigate the trans-RWM penetration ability of microspheres. Finally, the mouse cochlea was excised, and CLSM SP8 confocal microscopy (Leica, USA) was used to observe the distribution of NH2-MSNs in the inner ear.

Animals

Wild-type adult C57BL/6 J mice, aged 8 weeks and weighing 20 g, were examined by ABR, and those with normal hearing were selected. The procedures of the animal study were approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital.

Auditory Brainstem Responses Tests

First of all, the safety of noninvasive RWM surgical procedures and the appropriate surgical dose were investigated. For the safety of injection, mice in the Control group underwent an operation on the left ear without injection. The auditory function of the left ear using a closed-field microphone on days before and on day 5 postoperation (right ear without operation as control).

To determine the optimal injection dose of QU-N-MSNs in vivo, mice were injected with 10 or 20 mg/mL of QU-N-MSNs in the inner ear via a postauricular approach. Auditory brainstem response (ABR) tests using an open-field microphone before (D0) and on days 40 (D40), 60 (D60) after injection.

We subsequently investigated the protective effects of QU-N-MSNs. Mice were divided into 5 groups, including the Control group, CDDP group, CDDP+QU group, CDDP+NH2-MSNs group, and CDDP+QU-N-MSNs group (3 mice in each group). The procedures in the treatment of each group were as follows: administration of NH2-MSNs (20 mg/mL, 2 μL), QU (12.6 mg/mL (calculated based on LC of QU-N-MSNs), 2 μL), or QU-N-MSNs (20 mg/mL, 2 μL) and then intraperitoneal injection of CDDP after 4 days (5 mg/kg/day, consecutive 4 days). Each group received a direct injection of a 2.0 μL solution above the RWM. The CDDP group was only injected with CDDP via intraperitoneal injection, and the Control group was not treated during the whole period.

To evaluate the hearing function of mice in each group, an open-field ABR test was conducted on days 3, 7, and 14 (D3, D7, and D14) after CDDP treatment. The baseline ABR (-D9) was determined before injection. Briefly, ABRs were recorded in response to pure tone stimuli (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, and 32 kHz) from 90 dB SPL in 5 dB decrements with an open-field microphone. We collected and produced the acoustic signals using an RZ6 workstation and BioSig software (Tucker-Davis Technologies, USA). The lowest stimulus level when a response was observed was regarded as a threshold.

Hematoxylin–Eosin Staining

After the post-final ABR test (D14), cochlear tissues were collected for histological analysis. The decalcified cochlear nerve was snap-frozen in anodized oxalate (OCT), sectioned into 5–7 μm slices, and subjected to HE staining. R2, R3, and R4 refer to the apical, middle, and base cochlear turns. Images were captured using an Olympus SideView VS200 microscope. For SGNs analysis, the SGNs region was outlined along the cochlear duct contour, and SGNs density (number of SGNs per unit area) was quantified using ImageJ software. Stria vascularis (SV) pathology was evaluated in the middle turn (R3) across experimental groups.

Immunofluorescence and Immunohistochemistry Staining

For the detection of HCs loss in mouse cochlear samples, the basilar membrane was carefully dissected into three parts: the basal turn (Base), middle turn (Middle), and apical turn (Apex) after 3 days of decalcification, and immunofluorescence staining with MYO7A and DAPI was conducted. The images were captured by CLSM SP8 confocal microscopy.

For mitochondrial function in SGNs, the cochlear sections were immunostained using protocols consistent with those for HCs. Briefly, SGNs were labeled with Anti-Tuj1 antibody (ab78078, Abcam,1:1000) and the mitochondria-marker Anti-TOMM20 antibody (11802-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:500), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody and DAPI. Images were captured using an Olympus SideView VS200 microscope and ImageJ software. Mitochondrial expression levels (TOMM20 IOD/Area) were assessed by quantifying the integrated optical density (IOD) of TOMM20 immunostaining in SGNs (R3).

To evaluate the degree of oxidative stress in SGNs, we stained mouse SGNs with an anti-4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) antibody (ab48506, Abcam). Oxidative stress levels were assessed by calculating the IOD of 4-HNE normalized to the SGNs area (4-HNE IOD/Area).

Western Blot

The expression levels of apoptosis-related proteins were analyzed by using Western blotting. After lysing using RIPA buffer, the resulting protein samples were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Antibodies to the following proteins were used for Western Blot: P53 (10442-1-AP, Proteintech) and GAPDH (60004-1-IG, Proteintech). The relative intensity was quantified by ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

Student’s t-test was used to evaluate the statistical difference between the two groups, and multiple comparisons were assessed using one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction (GraphPad Prism 9.0). EC50 and IC50 values were derived by nonlinear regression analysis using four-parameter logistic modeling in GraphPad Prism 9.0. Results were presented as the mean ± SD. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with the following specific notations: *P < 0.05 for the CDDP group, NH2-MSNs group, QU group, and QU-N-MSNs versus control group; # P < 0.05 for the NH2-MSNs group, QU group, and QU-N-MSNs versus CDDP group; & P < 0.05 for the QU group versus QU-N-MSNs group.

Cytoprotective Ability of the Drug In Vitro

The biological activities of QU were first explored. The EC50 of QU is 1.276 nM, suggesting that it can protect from CDDP-induced ototoxicity even at relatively low concentrations (Figure A). Only QU treatment even increased the HEI-OC1 cell viability (#1–#6). Meanwhile, the result of the toxicity assay showed that QU can kill 50% of HEI-OC1 cells at the concentration of 367.8 μM (IC50) (Figure B), which was 109-fold higher than its EC50 (1.276 nM), suggesting that QU at high concentrations is not harmful, indicating the safety in the application. These results validated that QU has a broad therapeutic window, providing a potential theoretical basis for the clinical application of QU-N-MSNs.

Optimal Dose of QU-N-MSNs In Vitro

The Live/Dead staining results showed that the cell density increased over 3 days in both groups, with few dead cells (Figure C). As we already confirmed that QU within a wide range of concentrations can protect HEI-OC1 cells from CDDP damage, we narrowed the effective concentration range to further investigate the optimum concentrations of QU and QU-N-MSNs. Compared to the free QU, QU-N-MSNs promoted higher protective viability of the HEI-OC1 cells at the same concentrations, with a substantially wider therapeutic window (6–36 μM) (Figure E) than that of free QU (12–24 μM) (Figure D). In addition, comparing the lowest effective concentrations of QU-N-MSNs and QU for the treatment of CDDP-exposed cell damage (Figure D,E), QU-N-MSNs (6 μM onward) were half the lowest effective concentration of free QU (12 μM onward). This implies the improved protective effects of QU-N-MSNs against CDDP compared with QU treatment. The most protective concentration of 18 μM was selected for subsequent investigations.

Antiapoptotic Activity of QU-N-MSNs In Vitro

Apoptosis is an important mechanism of CDDP injury, and CDDP can induce cell apoptosis through multiple pathways. Based on the protective effects of QU-N-MSNs against CDDP-induced damage in HEI-OC1 cells, we further investigated the protective ability of QU-N-MSNs on CDDP-induced apoptosis to reveal its deeper protective mechanism. Consistently, as revealed by flow cytometry analysis, the apoptosis of HEI-OC1 cells increased by CDDP exposure was restored by the pretreatment with QU and QU-N-MSNs. The QU-N-MSNs + CDDP group showed better antiapoptotic ability relative to the QU + CDDP group (Figure F,G).

Enhancement of Cellular Uptake

To investigate why QU-N-MSNs exhibit efficacy at lower concentrations for cytoprotective and showed a better antiapoptotic activity, we considered the possibility that the carrier may have enhanced the uptake efficiency of the drug by the cells. We detected the cellular uptake of QU-N-MSNs in vitro by fabricating FITC@N-MSNs and free QU. The results showed that the fabricated microspheres enhanced the efficiency of cellular uptake and had long-term stability (Figure A).

Ability of RWM-Penetration In Vivo

For a multiscale analysis of microsphere distribution and metabolic behavior, we employed a combined approach utilizing tissue sectioning and in vivo imaging to assess the inner ear tissue localization of microspheres following the injection of fluorescently tagged NH2-MSNs.

IVIS showed fluorescence imaging after the injection of IR780@N-MSNs in the inner ears of mice at the indicated time. We found the fluorescence signal of IR780@N-MSNs 1h after injection. Meanwhile, we observed that the strong fluorescence signal of IR780@N-MSNs existed for 9 days, which then showed a slow decrease from 9 to 14 days. Throughout the whole 2 weeks, we found no fluorescence signal of IR780@N-MSNs in the other part of the body. The results indicated the long retention time of NH2-MSNs in mice in the injected local area and very low systemic drug concentrations, indicating the blockage of bulla during the surgery and the possibility of increasing the drug concentration in the inner ear (Figure B). We then opened the bulla and detected a fluorescence signal emitted from the excised mouse cochlea on day 14 post-unilateral injection (Figure C). The signal was found to be successfully absorbed into the cochlea through RWM (Figure C­(a)), supporting the RWM penetration ability of NH2-MSNs into the mouse cochlea with long retention. Furthermore, the location of the fluorescence signal is mostly in the cochlea rather than the semicircular canals (Figure C­(b)).

To explore the cochlear distributions, the mouse cochlear section of the fluorescent signal (RohB@N-MSNs) was detected on day 14 after microsphere injection. The results not only further visually verified the ability of NH2-MSNs to penetrate RWM into the cochlear but also showed that microspheres were distributed in the inner ear tissues of mice, mainly including the SGNs (Figure D,F,G), SV (Figure D), and HCs (Figure D–F).

In summary, the results verified the ability of NH2-MSNs to penetrate-RWM and suggested that NH2-MSNs mainly acted on SGNs, HCs, and SV. The subsequent studies will focus on these tissues.

First, the safety of the surgical techniques employed for RWM administration was supported by the lack of any notable rise in ABR thresholds (Figure S2). We validated the in vivo safety of QU-N-MSNs and used a concentration of 20 mg/mL, 2 μL as the dosing regimen in vivo (Figure A). An earlier investigation demonstrated that CDDP-triggered apoptosis in inner ear cells occurred via the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade. Discussion

CDDP is a widely used antitumor agent; however, severe side effects, including ototoxicity, limit its application. The well-known traditional Chinese medicinal formulation, Liu Wei Di Huang Decoction (LWDWD), which is used for replenishing the Yin of the kidney, and is also reported to protect against CDDP-induced toxicity in mice. The FDA has approved QU for dietary supplement applications. QU is considered to play a dual role in enhancing the therapeutic effects of CDDP and also alleviating its side effects. However, limited research has been conducted on the effects of QU on the inner ear.

In our study, QU is selected based on network pharmacology analysis as the main active component in the LWDWD (Figure S4). QU exhibited an IC50 of 367.8 μM and an EC50 of 1.276 nM in HEI-OC1 cells, indicating its broad-cytoprotective efficacy against CDDP-induced ototoxicity, providing a strong rationale for advancing its evaluation in a CIHL in vivo model.

The RWM route demonstrates superior pharmacokinetic advantages over conventional oral or intravenous administration. , MSNs represent an ideal nanocarrier for application as an inner ear drug carrier. In this study, MSNs were selected as the drug delivery platform based on their established clinical safety profile and superior material properties. , Multiple studies have proposed MSNs as promising carriers for QU in inner ear delivery. , Through amino-functionalization, we engineered NH2-MSNs specifically optimized for QU delivery and RWM penetration. The designed nanoparticles featured: a narrow size distribution (100–200 nm) for RWM penetration, a near-neutral surface charge to enhance cellular uptake while maintaining biocompatibility, and an enhanced drug loading capacity through a mesoporous architecture.

The QU-N-MSNs prepared via a modified sol–gel method, exhibited outstanding performance: high loading efficiency (EE:86.48%, DL:63.36%) and sustained release kinetics (up to 500 h), and ideal physicochemical properties (116 nm (SEM) −124 nm (DLS), +0.8 mV, and spherical morphology (SEM/TEM). These physicochemical parameters closely matched our design specifications and corroborated previous findings on nanoparticle-based inner ear drug delivery systems. , Following comprehensive physicochemical validation, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of QU-N-MSNs through systematic and subsequent studies.

The therapeutic potential of QU-N-MSNs was systematically evaluated through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies. Initial biocompatibility assessments revealed excellent safety profiles and significant protection against the CDDP-induced ototoxicity of QU-N-MSNs in HEI-OC1 cells. More importantly, mechanistic investigations demonstrated superior antiapoptotic effects of QU-N-MSNs compared with free QU, suggesting the QU-N-MSNs enhance both delivery and pharmacological efficacy of free QU. The observed efficacy at reduced concentrations strongly implies improved cellular internalization, a hypothesis subsequently validated through uptake studies. Our hypothesis was confirmed in subsequent in vitro studies.

In vivo study of biodistribution revealed the trans-RWM efficiency of the QU-N-MSNs through a combination approach of integrating tissue sectioning and in vivo imaging. We facilitated a multiscale analysis of whole-body distribution and metabolic behavior over a 14-day period. IVIS imaging demonstrated that the material can be trained in the inner ear for up to 14 days and efficiently penetrates the RWM, which indicates the prolonged time window and opportunities for sustained release of compounds in the prevention of CDDP-induced ototoxicity. Furthermore, the location of the fluorescence signal is mostly in the cochlea rather than the semicircular canals, potentially attributed to charge interactions between the internal fluids and QU-N-MSNs. Additionally, the tissue sectioning not only visually verified the ability of microspheres to penetrate-RWM into the cochlear, but also showed that the microspheres were distributed in the inner ear tissues of mice, mainly including the SGNs, SV, and HCs, highlighting these structures as the main focus for further mechanistic analysis.

After confirming the safety of the surgical procedure and identifying the appropriate surgical dose in vivo, we subsequently investigated the protective effect of QU-N-MSNs in vivo. Given that the death of HCs induced by cisplatin is irreversible, a preprotection strategy is essential. For nanoparticles with sustained-release characteristics, administration 3–5 days prior to cisplatin exposure is a well-established and effective strategy. This intermediate pretreatment window provides sufficient time for drug penetration through the RWM while ensuring prolonged drug retention within the cochlea, striking an optimal balance between loading and duration. Based on this rationale, we selected a 4-day pretreatment regimen to ensure robust drug enrichment in the cochlea before cisplatin insult.

In CDDP-induced ototoxicity mouse models, the pretreatment of QU-N-MSNs exhibited superior protective effects compared with free QU, while no significant hearing preservation was observed in the NH2-MSNs treatment group. QU-N-MSNs provided significantly better efficacy in mid-to-low frequency ranges compared with free QU. It may be because the drug concentration gradient exists within the cochlea. Drugs administered via RWM (located near the cochlear base) gradually distribute toward the apex through perilymph circulation, resulting in insufficient QU concentration at low-frequency regions, which may explain the limited protection observed in these areas. In contrast, the enhanced RWM permeability of QU-N-MSNs enables more efficient drug delivery to mid-to-low frequency regions, achieving adequate therapeutic concentrations through improved accumulation and sustained release. Furthermore, while both QU and QU-N-MSNs groups showed comparable hearing protection within the first 7 days, only the QU-N-MSNs group maintained stable hearing thresholds thereafter, which may also be associated with the reason that QU-N-MSNs function as an effective drug delivery system capable of sustained QU release as well.

Therefore, we conclude that QU-N-MSNs exhibit hearing protection superior to QU by slow release as well as by increasing the bioavailability of QU, which mainly originates from the QU itself and not from the NH2-MSNs material.

Previous studies have demonstrated that CDDP-induced ototoxicity primarily targets HCs, SGNs, connective tissues, and the spiral ligament. ,, In this study, we observed that the microspheres were predominantly localized in the inner cochlear tissues, including SGNs, SV, and HCs. This suggests that the protective mechanism of QU-N-MSNs against CIHL may involve the preservation of SGNs, SV, and HCs, which are directly affected by CDDP. Consequently, we focused our investigation on evaluating SGNs, SV, and HCs as a key site of injury, utilizing histological and immunostaining analyses of cochlear sections.

The HCs' observations showed that the pattern of HCs' damage aligns with the well-established pattern of CIHL, which primarily initiates in the basal region and predominantly affects the outer hair cells. The statistical results revealed that the reduced density of HCs in the CDDP group was most significantly alleviated by the pretreatment of QU-N-MSNs. The targeted protection of QU-N-MSNs against HCs loss contributes to their hearing preservation. ,,

SV is one of the main targets of CDDP ototoxicity. Severe SV damage can both directly lead to CIHL and increase the sensitivity of the cochlea to CDDP damage. , The observation results showed that QU and QU-N-MSNs could effectively prevent structural abnormalities in the SV induced by CDDP, while NH2-MSNs alone did not show significant protective effects.

Quantitative analysis of SGNs density confirmed the superior protection of QU-N-MSNs against CDDP compared to free QU, likely attributable to the nanoparticle formulation’s enhanced cochlear delivery and sustained drug release properties. This finding is particularly significant, given the critical role of SGNs in auditory function and their vulnerability to cisplatin toxicity.

Our experimental results demonstrate that QU-N-MSNs can protect against CDDP ototoxicity through several key mechanisms. The QU-N-MSNs effectively inhibit p53-mediated apoptosis, which represents a principal pathway of CDDP-induced SGNs damage. This protective effect is particularly crucial since SGNs represent one of the most direct and earliest targets of CDDP-induced toxicity, and apoptosis is the predominant mechanism underlying CDDP-mediated SGN damage. − The protective ability of QU-N-MSNs can preserve mitochondrial integrity. This finding aligns with established evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction occurs rapidly following CDDP exposure. Our study confirmed that QU-N-MSNs significantly reduced oxidative stress markers while maintaining mitochondrial membrane potential. These results are consistent with the mitochondrial protective effects of QU against CDDP-induced mitochondrial damage and the strong oxidation resistance ability of QU, while clearly demonstrating the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of QU achieved through QU-N-MSNs-mediated delivery.

The partial protective effect of NH2-MSNs alone on SGN density, despite lacking ABR threshold protection, presents an interesting finding that warrants further investigation. This phenomenon could be attributed to several potential mechanisms. First, the nanoparticles may adsorb a fraction of cisplatin, thereby locally reducing its ototoxic concentration. Second, as suggested by a previous study, the structure of MSNs might modulate the local mechanical microenvironment, potentially activating mechanosensitive signaling pathways that promote axonal growth and neuronal preservation. Furthermore, the amino-functionalized surface could provide a more favorable substrate for neuronal attachment and stability. While the exact mechanism requires further elucidation, these intrinsic bioactivities of nanoparticles offer a promising avenue for enhancing therapeutic outcomes. For instance, future strategies could employ advanced delivery methods to co-deliver neurotrophic factors with QU-N-MSNs, potentially yielding synergistic effects for hearing protection.

While this study demonstrates the potential of QU-N-MSNs for otoprotection, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, direct measurement of drug release kinetics in the murine perilymph was not feasible due to the technical challenges associated with the minimal volume of perilymph and the risk of cerebrospinal fluid contamination during sampling. To circumvent this, we employed a combination of fluorescent-dye-labeled nanoparticles, in vitro release studies, and in vivo imaging to infer the distribution and release behavior of the nanocarriers. However, it is important to note that this approach provides an indirect assessment, and the in vitro release conditions may not fully recapitulate the complex inner ear microenvironment.

Despite these limitations, the clinical translation potential of QU-N-MSNs is supported by the following key advantages: First, their core components have a reliable safety profileQU has been confirmed to have good human tolerance as a natural product, while the MSNs have demonstrated excellent biocompatibility in numerous preclinical and clinical studies as drug carriers. Second, from an industrial perspective, the sol–gel synthesis process for MSNs is mature and stable, offering strong prospects for large-scale production. Subsequent drug loading and surface functionalization processes can also be easily standardized, complying with the good manufacturing practice (GMP) requirements. However, prior to clinical application, it remains essential to thoroughly evaluate the long-term biosafety of this formulation, including a systematic investigation of its in vivo distribution, clearance pathways, and potential tissue accumulation risks.

Conclusions

In summary, our research demonstrates promising therapeutic drug carrier QU-N-MSNs combined with advanced delivery system technology for effective relief of CIHL. This QU-N-MSNs platform effectively prevents CIHL by enhancing QU delivery efficiency, thereby outperforming free QU. Mechanistically, QU-N-MSNs exert their protective effects by reducing mitochondrial reactive ROS levels and suppressing mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Importantly, our multitissue validation confirms that the therapeutic benefit stems from QU rather than the NH2-MSNs carrier. However, whether NH2-MSNs contribute additional otoprotective effects through their intrinsic physicochemical properties remains unclear. Future studies should explore the precise molecular pathways of QU-N-MSNs to determine whether their mechanism is solely inherited from QU or involves novel nanocarrier-mediated regulatory pathways.
