Cinobufotalin reduces glioblastoma resistance to temozolomide by inhibiting the CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway
Authors: Jiwei Sun, Xianyuan Ma, Jian Song, Yesen Zhang, Ning Li, Yulong Li, Xiang Zhang, Yi Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTemozolomide (TMZ) resistance is a key factor that restricts the therapeutic effects of glioblastoma (GBM). The pharmacological properties of cinobufotalin (CB) indicated that CB promotes the cell death of GBM cells, while the underlying mechanism remains not fully elucidated. This study aims to elucidate the mechanism by which CB reduces TMZ resistance during GBM progression.MethodsBALB/c nude mice were used to construct in situ models, and were intraperitoneally injected with CB and TMZ to evaluate their effects on tumor growth. Live imaging, hematoxylin-eosin (HE), and immunohistochemical (IHC) were employed to detect tumor growth in BALB/c nude mice. T98G cells were treated with CB, TMZ and the phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway inhibitor or activator, or transfected with C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) up/down-regulation plasmids to evaluate whether CB could reverse TMZ resistance and further explore whether the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway was involved in this mechanism. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EDU) staining, transwell assay, flow cytometry, and Western blot were performed to detect cell proliferation after different treatments.ResultsIn BALB/c nude mice, the combination therapy of CB and TMZ reduced TMZ resistance and inhibited tumor growth, as evidenced by suppressing tumor proliferation, decreasing MGMT expression levels, and increasing tumor cell death. In T98G cells, CB also reduced TMZ resistance, as indicated by decreased cell proliferation and invasion capacity, reduced MGMT expression, and increased cell death. The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is essential for CB’s function, as its activation not only promotes cell growth but also enhances TMZ resistance. Further investigation demonstrated that CB inhibits CCL5 transcription, thereby blocking the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and ultimately inhibiting cell growth.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that CB enhanced the sensitivity of GBM to TMZ by blocking the CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. It provides a promising therapeutic strategy to reduce TMZ resistance in GBM treatment.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Glioblastoma (GBM) multiforme poses a significant threat to patient health due to its poor prognosis caused by increased temozolomide (TMZ) resistance. This study focuses on the clinical challenges of TMZ resistance in GBM and innovatively introduces cinobufotalin (CB). It was found that the combination of CB and TMZ can synergistically inhibit tumour growth in vitro and in vivo.

What is known and what is new?

• As an anticancer drug, CB can effectively reduce TMZ resistance by inhibiting the phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway.

• C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) is highly expressed in TMZ-resistant GBM cells, and its expression is regulated by CB, thereby inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This study indicates that CB exerts its function in reducing TMZ resistance by targeting the CCL5-mdiated PI3K/Akt/mTOR signalling pathway.

• This study provides a novel biomarker (CCL5) and combined theapeutic target for GBM drug resistance. The initiation of clinical testing for CCL5 and the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway will further enhance the clinical applicability of this research.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive malignant tumor with a high recurrence rate (1-3). GBM is characterized by low differentiation, strong invasion, and easy recurrence (4,5). The currently available conventional treatment methods mainly include surgical resection of GBM tumors, followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy (6,7).

Temozolomide (TMZ) is an oral alkylating agent used to treat GBM. In 2005, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved TMZ for the treatment of GBM patients. Compared with patients receiving radiotherapy alone, the combination of TMZ and radiotherapy extended the median survival of GBM patients from 12.1 to 14.6 months (8,9). TMZ functions by alkylating the O6 position of guanine in tumor cells, resulting in DNA damage and inducing tumor cell apoptosis (10). This mechanism is considered the primary pathway through which TMZ exerts its anti-tumor effects. Although TMZ shows some therapeutic potential for GBM, over 40% of GBM patients gradually develop resistance to TMZ (11-13). The resistance mechanisms of TMZ mainly involve the phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, the C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5)-CCR5 pathway, and enhanced DNA methyltransferase activity, such as O6-methylguanine-DNA-methyltransferase (MGMT) (10,14-16). Therefore, overcoming TMZ resistance remains a significant challenge in the treatment of GBM.

Cinobufotalin (CB) is a bioactive ingredient extracted from the dry epidermis or glandular secretions of the toad, which has anti-tumor, inhibition of endothelial cell formation, and immune enhancement effects. Emerging studies have demonstrated that CB, as a potent anti-tumor drug, exhibits remarkable efficacy against lung cancer, gastric cancer, and liver cancer (17-22). In U87 cells, CB inhibits cell proliferation by inducing the expression of cleaved caspase 3 and caspase 9 (23). The results of enrichment analysis indicated that the mechanism of CB on GBM mainly includes inhibiting cell cycle, promoting cell apoptosis, and regulating immunity (24). Furthermore, in colon cancer, CB reverses the 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) resistance by promoting tumor cell apoptosis and inhibiting cell migration and invasion (25). In lung adenocarcinoma, CB reverses cisplatin resistance by regulating the PI3K/Akt pathway (26). Therefore, in our study, we aimed to investigate whether CB could reverse the resistance to TMZ in GBM cells, and if so, to identify the underlying mechanisms involved in this process.

In our present study, we were curious to investigate whether the combination of CB and TMZ could enhance the effect of inhibiting GBM. Here, we proved that CB reduced the resistance of GBM to TMZ both in T98G cells and in BALB/c nude mice. Furthermore, we showed that the mechanism for CB enhancing TMZ sensitivity in GBM involves inhibiting the CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2546/rc).

Methods

Xenograft model

BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, male, College of Veterinary Medicine Yangzhou University) were used for xenograft experiments to evaluate the efficacy of different treatment regimens on tumor cell growth. Luciferase-transduced T98G cells (5×105) were injected into the basal ganglia of each mouse. One week after injection, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (each group included at least six mice to avoid experimental results being affected by mouse mortality, while ensuring biological replicability of the experimental outcomes). T98G group: mice received an equal amount of physiological saline via intraperitoneal injection. T98G + CB group: mice received CB (5 mg/kg/d) via intraperitoneal injection. T98G + TMZ group: mice received TMZ (10 mg/kg/d) via intraperitoneal injection. T98G + CB + TMZ group: mice received CB (5 mg/kg/d) and TMZ (10 mg/kg/d) via intraperitoneal injection. The treatment lasted for a total of 20 days. The activity of luciferase was measured at 14 and 28 d after injection of T98G cells. The tumor size was measured by live imaging (Perkin Elmor, IVIS®Lumina III, SCR_017597) after 15–20 min of injection of 100 µL luciferase substrate (GOLDBIO, 115144-35-9) into the tail vein. Animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. Lun ke dong 2022 [464]) granted by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University, in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals. A protocol was prepared before the study, but it was not registered.

Construction of T98G cells for stable expression of luciferase

T98G cells were seeded at a density of 2 mL per well in a 6-well cell culture plate (1×105). The cells were infected with the lentivirus (pLV-luciferase-IRES-puro) at a molecular oxygen index (MOI) of 20. After 16 h of viral infection, the medium was replaced with complete medium and the cells were cultured for an additional 32 h. Following 48 h of cell infection, 1 µg/mL of puromycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; ST551) was added, and the medium was changed every 2–3 days with antibiotic supplementation. After 3–4 weeks of culture, a stable Luc-transfected cell line was constructed.

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

The tumor tissues were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde (PFA) and then sliced into 4 µm sections for subsequent experiments. For Ki67 staining: the sections were incubated with Rabbit anti-Ki67 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab15580) at 4 ℃ overnight. The following day, using goat anti-rabbit IgG to incubate the sections for 20 min at room temperature. For terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL): the sections were incubated with biotin labeling solution at 37 ℃ for 60 min. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the sections were incubated with streptavidin-HRP at 37 ℃ for 30 min. Finally, using the microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan; BX53, SCR_017482) and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; SCR_003070) to analyze the results.

Cell culture and treatment

GBM cells including SVGp12 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA; CRL-8621, RRID: CVCL_3797), U251 (Procell, Wuhan, China; CL-0237, RRID: CVCL_5H95), and U138 (ATCC, HTB-16, RRID: CVCL_0020) were all purchased from company and grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), while the T98G (Procell, CL-0583, RRID: CVCL_0556) cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. The cells were cultured in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator.

The cells in the control group were treated with nothing. The cells in the CB group were treated with CB at varying concentrations: 5, 10, 25, and 50 µg/mL. The cells in the TMZ group were treated with TMZ at varying concentrations: 0, 5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µM. The cells in the CB + TMZ group were treated with CB (25 µg/mL) and TMZ (100 µM). The cells in the TMZ + IN-2 group were treated with TMZ (100 µM) and IN-2 (5 µM). The cells in the CB + TMZ + 740Y-P group were treated with CB (25 µg/mL), TMZ (100 µM) and 740Y-P (20 µM).

The T98G cells were transfected with plasmids containing sh-NC, sh-CCL5, OE-NC and OE-CCL5 using Lipofectamine 2000. Following transfection, the cells were treated with CB (25 µg/mL) and TMZ (100 µM) for 48 h under standard culture conditions.

Cell Counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assay

CCK-8 assay was used to assess cell viability. T98G cells (5×104 cells/mL) were seeded in a 96-well plate and treated with CB (25 µg/mL) and TMZ (100 µM) for 48 h. After treatment, the cells were incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Biosharp, Hefei, China; BS350A) at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Then, used the DR-3518GL reader (Diatek, Wuxi, China) to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Transwell assay

Cell invasion was performed using 24-well transwell chambers coated with Matrigel (BD Bioscience, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA; 354234). T98G cells (1×104) were suspended in 200 µL serum-free MEM and seeded into a matrigel pre-coated upper chamber. 800 µL MEM with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. The non-invading cells in the upper chambers were removed gently with cotton tips after 48 h of incubation. The invading cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min and stained with crystal violet for 10 min. The cells were counted in six randomly selected areas under an inverted microscope (Leica, Shanghai, China; DMi1, SCR_017132).

5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EDU) staining

EDU staining was performed to assess cell proliferation. The treated T98G cells were incubated with EDU working solution (Beyotime, C0071S) at 37 ℃ for 2 h, and stained with Hoechst (Beyotime, C1017) to label the nucleus. The EDU-positive cells were counted using ImageJ software.

Western blot

Western blotting was employed to detect the expression levels of proteins. Total proteins were obtained from T98G cells or tumor tissues using the radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protein inhibitor. The western blot was conducted according to the previously published protocols (27). The antibodies used in the figures are listed in Table 1.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

After transfecting T98G cells with pGL3-CCL5 (WT) and pRL-TK plasmids using Lipo 2000 for 6 h, the cells were treated with CB (10, 25, and 50 µg/mL) for another 48 h. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, to detect the transcriptional activity of CCL5 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; E1910). Briefly, after washing the T98G cells with PBS, add lysis buffer and incubate at room temperature for 30 min. Add firefly luciferase substrate to the lysate and measure the luminescence value (RLU1), then add marine luciferase substrate to measure the luminescence value (RLU2). The relative luciferase activity is calculated as RLU1/RLU2.

Statistical analysis

All experiment data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (SCR_002798) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experimental results were obtained from three independent biological replicates, with data collected by two investigators. The groups’ comparison and multiple groups’ comparison were analyzed by Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) following Turkey’s test, respectively. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CB decreases the resistance of TMZ in GBM cells

To select a TMZ-resistant GBM cell line, we evaluated the expression levels of MGMT in U138, T98G, U251, and SVGp12 cell lines. Western blot analysis indicated that T98G cells exhibited the highest MGMT expression levels compared with other cell lines (Figure 1A). CCK-8 assay results showed that cell viability was gradually decreased with increasing TMZ doses (Figure 1B). Among these cell lines, T98G cells exhibited the highest cell viability and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), which was consistent with their highest MGMT expression. Taken together, we selected T98G cells for our subsequent experiments.

Subsequently, we evaluated the IC50 of CB (5, 10, 25, and 50 mg/mL) in T98G cells. CCK-8 assay results indicated that the IC50 of CB was 52.31 mg/mL, while TMZ’s IC50 was 183.07 mM (Figure 1B,1C). We next evaluated the anti-proliferative effect of CB (25 mg/mL) and TMZ (5–200 mM) combination by conducting CCK-8 assay at 48 h. The results showed that the combination of CB and TMZ reduced the IC50 of TMZ from 183.07 to 48.02 mM (Figure 1D). Taken together, our findings revealed that CB enhanced the sensitivity of T98G cells to TMZ.

To further confirm the regulatory effect of CB and TMZ on T98G cells, we selected the concentration of 25 mg/mL CB and 100 mM TMZ for our subsequent experiments. EDU and Transwell assay results showed that both cell proliferation (Figure 1E) and invasion (Figure 1F) were reduced in the CB and TMZ groups compared with the control group. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a marked increase in cell death in the CB and TMZ groups compared with the control group (Figure 1G). Corresponding to the increased apoptosis, we observed elevated expression levels of pro-apoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2)-associated X protein (Bax) and cleaved caspase 3, while the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was decreased in the CB and TMZ groups compared with the control group (Figure 1H). Additionally, the expression levels of MGMT were reduced in the CB and TMZ groups compared with the control group (Figure 1I). These findings demonstrate that both CB and TMZ treatment can inhibit T98G cell growth.

We further explored whether the combination of CB and TMZ showed enhanced inhibitory effects on T98G cells. Compared with the CB or TMZ treatment groups, the CB + TMZ group presented a more pronounced inhibition of cell growth, as indicated by decreased cell proliferation (Figure 1E) and invasion (Figure 1F), along with increased cell apoptosis (Figure 1G,1H). Furthermore, the CB + TMZ group exhibited the lowest expression levels of MGMT (Figure 1I). Taken together, our findings demonstrate that CB attenuates the resistance of T98G cells to TMZ through inhibiting cell growth and promoting cell apoptosis.

Combination therapy of CB and TMZ inhibits tumor growth in mice

To further explore the regulatory effects of combination therapy on tumor growth in vivo, we implanted luciferase-stable T98G cells into the basal ganglia of BALB/c nude mice. Tumor growth was evaluated using luciferase bioluminescence at 14 and 28 d after injection of T98G cells. We found that the tumors in the combination group were smaller than those in the CB or TMZ monotherapy groups (Figure 2A). The results of HE staining indicated that the combination treatment groups exhibited smaller tumor tissue, reduced cell infiltration, and a reduction in nucleus division compared with the CB or TMZ monotherapy groups (Figure 2B). The results of Ki67 and TUNEL staining showed that compared with the monotherapy groups, the combination therapy group presented reduced tumor growth (Figure 2C), while increased apoptosis (Figure 2D). Notably, the expression levels of MGMT were obviously reduced compared with the monotherapy groups (Figure 2E). In summary, our findings collectively demonstrate that CB effectively reduces TMZ resistance in GBM both in vitro and in vivo.

CB inhibits the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in TMZ-resistant T98G cells

To explore the underlying mechanism by which CB reduces TMZ resistance in T98G cells, we focused on the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. This pathway is not only crucial for cell survival (28-31), but also closely associated with TMZ resistance in GBM cells (32-34). For this purpose, we first evaluated the expression levels of p-PI3K, PI3K, p-Akt, Akt, p-mTOR and mTOR using western blot. The results showed that both CB and TMZ groups exhibited decreased expression levels of p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR compared with the control group. Notably, compared with the CB or TMZ groups, the CB + TMZ group showed further reduced expression levels of these proteins (Figure 3A). These data suggest that the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway may play a crucial role in CB function.

To further confirm the role of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in TMZ-mediated resistance, we treated T98G cells with the PI3K/Akt/mTOR inhibitor IN-2 (5 mM) (35). We observed a significant decrease in p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR expression levels in TMZ + IN-2 group compared with the control group (Figure 3B). In addition, the cell proliferation (Figure 3C) and invasion (Figure 3D) were reduced in the TMZ + IN-2 group compared with the control group. Furthermore, compared with the control group, the TMZ + IN-2 group exhibited enhanced cell apoptosis, as indicated by elevated Bax and cleaved caspase 3 expression, while Bcl-2 expression was decreased (Figure 3E,3F). Notably, we also observed decreased MGMT expression in TMZ + IN-2 group compared with the control group (Figure 3G). Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the inhibition of PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway effectively suppresses the growth of TMZ-resistant T98G cells.

Next, we continued to investigate whether the activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway would reverse the TMZ resistance reduction and cell growth inhibition induced by CB. T98G cells were treated with 740Y-P, a specific activator of PI3K (36). We found that compared with the CB + TMZ group, the CB + TMZ + 740Y-P group exhibited a significant increase in p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR expression levels (Figure 3B). Correspondingly, compared with the CB + TMZ group, this group showed a significant increase in cell proliferation (Figure 3C) and invasion (Figure 3D), along with increased TMZ resistance (Figure 3G), while cell apoptosis was decreased (Figure 3E,3F). Taken together, our findings collectively demonstrate that the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway plays a crucial role in CB-mediated functions.

CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is necessary for CB to inhibit cell growth

Next, we continued to explore the underlying mechanism by which CB inhibits the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. For this purpose, we screened several potential targets from the GSE151680 database (37), including CCL5, CCL10, CCL11, IFNL1, MGMT, RSAD2, and SERPINA1 (Figure 4A,4B). Previous research has indicated that the CCL5-mediated Akt pathway is a contributor of TMZ resistance in cancers (16,38,39). We therefore selected CCL5 for subsequent experiments.

We first evaluated the expression levels of CCL5 in GBM cells to verify whether CCL5 is involved in TMZ resistance. Western blot analysis results showed that the T98G cells with the highest TMZ resistance also exhibited the highest CCL5 expression (Figure 4C), suggesting there is a relationship between TMZ resistance and CCL5 expression. We next investigated whether the expression levels of CCL5 were affected by CB or TMZ treatment. RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis results showed that CCL5 expression in T98G cells treated with CB or TMZ was reduced compared with the control group (Figure 4D,4E). Notably, the CB + TMZ group presented further reduced CCL5 expression compared to CB or TMZ groups (Figure 4D,4E). Similar CCL5 expression reduction was observed in tumor tissues (Figures 4F and G). We next performed dual-luciferase reporter experiments to assess whether CB affects the transcription of CCL5. The results showed that the transcription of CCL5 was reduced after treatment with CB (Figure 4H). Our findings indicate that CB inhibits cell proliferation and tumor growth through suppressing CCL5 expression at the transcriptional level.

To further verify the regulatory effects of CCL5 down-regulation on T98G cells, we used shRNAs to down-regulate CCL5 expression. RT-qPCR results showed the sh-CCL5-2 exhibited the lowest expression of CCL5 mRNA (Figure 4I). Correspondingly, western blot analysis showed a significant reduction in CCL5 expression (Figure 4J). Thus, we selected this cell line for our subsequent experiments. The results demonstrated that compared with the sh-NC group, the sh-CCL5 group not only exhibited T98G cell growth inhibition, as evidenced by decreased cell proliferation (Figure 4K) and invasion (Figure 4L), along with increased cell apoptosis (Figure 4M), but also showed reduced PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway activity, as indicated by decreased p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR expression levels (Figure 4N). More importantly, the TMZ + sh-CCL5 group presented stronger inhibition on T98G cell growth compared with the TMZ + sh-NC group (Figure 4K-4N). These data collectively demonstrate that the CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway plays a crucial role in TMZ-induced T98G cell growth.

As the CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is located downstream of CB and alleviates TMZ resistance, we subsequently up-regulated CCL5 expression to verify whether its overexpression would reverse the effects induced by CB and TMZ. The results showed that the CB + TMZ + OE-CCL5 group exhibited enhanced CCL5 expression (Figure 4J) and PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway activity compared with the CB + TMZ + OE-NC group (Figure 4N). Meanwhile, this group also showed increased cell proliferation (Figure 4K) and invasion (Figure 4L), while decreased cell death (Figure 4M). In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that the upregulation of CCL5 not only enhances cell survival but also activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that CB effectively inhibits the proliferation of TMZ-resistant T98G cells and suppresses tumor growth in BALB/c nude mice. Moreover, CCL5-mediated PI3K/Akt/mTOR signal pathway plays a pivotal role in this regulatory process. In conclusion, our study not only provides an effective strategy to overcome TMZ resistance but also sheds light on the crucial role of CCL5 in modulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.

Emerging studies indicated that TMZ resistance is the main reason for the failure of GBM treatment (40,41). Therefore, our aim is to identify the key molecules or potential pathways involved in TMZ resistance and determine whether targeting these pathways could enhance GBM sensitivity to TMZ. CB was extracted from toad and has anti-tumor activity. It has been reported that CB could enhance the anti-tumor drugs’ sensitivity, like cisplatin (18,26). In our study, we demonstrated that CB reverses TMZ resistance in GBM cells by inhibiting cell proliferation and invasion while promoting cell apoptosis. Consistent with these effects, the combination treatment of CB and TMZ also inhibited tumor growth in BALB/c nude mice, as indicated by decreased tumor cell proliferation, while increased tumor cell death.

The chemokine CCL5 has multiple biological functions, including chemotactic effects on cells, regulation of inflammatory responses, regulation of cell growth and differentiation, and promotion of tumor occurrence and development. It is reported that CCL5 was highly expressed in GBM cells (42-44). In our study, we observed elevated CCL5 expression in GBM cells, which is consistent with previous studies. Meanwhile, CCL5 knockdown led to a reduction in cell proliferation, and these effects were further exacerbated by TMZ treatment.

Previous data have proved that CCL5 plays a key role in regulating PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. In chondrosarcoma cells, CCL5-induced PI3K/Akt promotes angiogenesis (39,45,46). In lung cancer, CCL5 promotes tumor cell migration via the PI3K/Akt pathway (47). In GBM cells, the CCL5/CCR5 activated Akt is responsible for DNA repair (16). Here, our study demonstrated that the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is necessary for CB to enhance TMZ sensitivity, as the inhibition or activation of the PI3K pathway exhibited opposite effects on cell growth and cell apoptosis. Additionally, our study also demonstrated that CCL5 overexpression not only promotes cell growth but also activates PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.

At the same time, there are limitations in our study. The underlying mechanism by which CCL5 activates PI3K requires further investigation. CCL5 primarily exerts its biological functions through binding to its specific receptor (e.g., CCR5). Previous literature has demonstrated that CCR5 is a key receptor mediating the activation of the CCL5-induced PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway (38,47,48). Based on this, we hypothesize that the observed effects of CCL5 in this study may also depend on CCR5. To validate this hypothesis, future studies could further investigate the expression changes of p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR by knocking down or inhibiting CCR5 in a CCL5-overexpressed background, thereby elucidating the necessity of CCR5 in this pathway. Additionally, exploring other potential regulatory factors or feedback mechanisms in this pathway will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the specific regulatory network of CCL5 in the combined therapy of CB and TMZ. In addition to MGMT, recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of pentraxin 3 (PTX3) in TMZ resistance (7,49). PTX3 can enhance the repair ability of tumor cell DNA damage and further promote the formation of TMZ resistance by activating the NF-kB signaling pathway (50,51). In future research, we will investigate whether CB regulates PTX3 expression and elucidates the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that the CCL5/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway is involved in CB-mediated reduction of TMZ resistance, suggesting that targeting this pathway may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for GBM treatment.
