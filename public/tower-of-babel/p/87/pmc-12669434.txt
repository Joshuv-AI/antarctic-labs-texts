Role of Local Factors on the Occurrence of Peri‐Implantitis: A Cross‐Sectional Analysis
Authors: Alberto Monje, Ramon Pons, Maria Costanza Soldini, Paul S. Rosen, José Nart, Daniel Buser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveA cross‐sectional study was made to evaluate the role of local factors, including surgical, implant, and prosthesis‐related parameters, in the presence of peri‐implantitis.MethodsConsecutive partially edentulous patients with ≥ 1 implant presenting peri‐implantitis were included. Clinical and radiographic data were collected to characterize local factors. Overall, 28 site‐specific variables were analyzed for peri‐implantitis and non‐peri‐implantitis implants. Simple and multiple logistic regression analyses were performed to explore potential associations. The diagnostic accuracy of those variables that demonstrated statistical significance in the multiple logistic regression analysis was further tested.ResultsIn total, 100 patients with 452 implants met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 227 implants (50.2%) were diagnosed with peri‐implantitis, while 225 (49.8%) did not present peri‐implantitis. The multiple logistic regression analysis demonstrated statistical significance for the following factors: (1) medial implant position within the prosthesis (OR = 3.9) was more prone to develop peri‐implantitis; (2) internal Morse taper implant connection was the least likely to develop peri‐implantitis (OR = 0.43); (3) mesio‐distally centered implants were less prone to exhibit peri‐implantitis (OR = 0.46); (4) bucco‐lingual centered implants were less prone to exhibit peri‐implantitis (OR = 0.29); (5) implants positioned inside (≥ 2 mm) the bony housing were less prone to exhibit peri‐implantitis (OR = 0.13); and (6) implants tilted 10°–30° (OR = 5.84) and ≥ 30° (OR = 7.43) were more prone to exhibit peri‐implantitis. Moreover, a prediction model based on the aforementioned factors yielded a high level of accuracy.ConclusionsImplant malpositioning is a critical factor associated with peri‐implantitis.

Introduction

Peri‐implantitis is regarded as a plaque‐associated chronic inflammatory disorder that manifests with peri‐implant soft tissue inflammation and progressive bone loss. The onset and progression of the disease are associated with qualitative and quantitative differences in the microbiome (Monje, Insua, and Wang 2019). Data from reports indicate that peri‐implantitis is a heterogeneous mixed infection including periodontopathic microorganisms (Monje, Insua, and Wang 2019; Monje, Kan, and Borgnakke 2023; Monje et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025). However, despite the infectious nature of the disorder, deep‐sequencing analysis has not suggested a specific microbial composition at diseased sites that differs significantly from healthy peri‐implant sites (Dabdoub et al. 2013). Moreover, it has been demonstrated that less than 50% of all species were not held in common between the biofilms of healthy teeth and their implants, and that 85% shared less than 8% of the abundant species (Monje et al. 2025). This is not surprising, since the peri‐implant bacterial community following implant placement and implant restoration shows the recruitment of new microorganisms, and maturation is accompanied by a progressive increase in anaerobic bacteria (Kumar et al. 2024). These data suggest that the exposure of dental implants to disease occurrence/recurrence following therapy is rather higher when compared to teeth. This may be attributed in part to the morphology and composition of the peri‐implant soft tissue and its interface with the implant surface, characterized by suboptimal hemidesmosomal adhesion rather than in part by connective tissue attachment (Aellos et al. 2024).

Interestingly, epidemiological reports published from different settings in a variety of geographical locations have shown the prevalence of peri‐implantitis at the patient level to be substantially higher than the prevalence at the site level (Rodrigo et al. 2018; Mauland et al. 2024). (Papathanasiou et al. 2016; Obreja et al. 2021) The importance of this consistent finding is based on the fact that some implants might be more prone to peri‐implantitis within susceptible patients than others. It is thus speculated that local factors may shift the condition between disease and health. These factors have been categorized as predisposing, precipitating factors, and acceleratory factors (Monje, Insua, and Wang 2019), and subsequently subcategorized into four major predisposing groups: surgery‐related, hard tissue‐related, soft tissue‐related, and prosthesis‐related (Monje, Kan, and Borgnakke 2023). This scenario arose based on data derived from a survey about a decade ago that suggested implant position (too buccal), soft tissue thickness (thin), and the buccal band of keratinized mucosa (deficient) to be major contributors to peri‐implantitis (Adell et al. 1981). Recently, the American Academy of Periodontology and the Academy of Osseointegration held a consensus meeting to shed light on the implications of these local factors on the onset/progression of peri‐implantitis (Monje et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025). Modest clinical evidence appears to be present for surgical factors, in particular suboptimal implant position and, to a lesser extent, implants placed in regenerated bone, as being more prone to exhibit peri‐implantitis (Monje et al. 2025). Moreover, some of the evidence would suggest that a shortened distance from the prosthetic margin to the crestal bone during implant placement may predispose to peri‐implantitis (Monje et al. 2025).

A factor that was regarded as key in the onset/progression of peri‐implantitis is the implant surface with its characteristics (Derks et al. 2016). In fact, evidence suggested that early bone loss during healing, leading to an exposed roughened implant surface in the trans‐crestal area, is predictive of the development of peri‐implantitis (Windael et al. 2021; Ravida et al. 2023). It is speculated that the link between physiological and pathological bone loss relies on the exposure of the roughened implant surface to the peri‐implant sulcus, which may facilitate bacterial contamination. It is interesting to note that biological complications caused by peri‐implant infections were not reported for the original Brånemark implants used in the 1970s with a machined implant surface (Adell et al. 1981). The term first appeared in a paper in 1987 by Mombelli et al. (1987) related to peri‐implant infections at implants with a rough, micro‐porous TPS coating, even in the transmucosal part of one‐piece implants. This highlights the role of the implant surface in predisposing individuals to peri‐implantitis. Accordingly, Tarnow (1993) proposed the concept of hybrid design implants with a roughened implant surface in the apical portion to improve the quality of osseointegration, with a machined implant surface in the coronal portion of the implant to reduce the risk of the development of peri‐implantitis.

There are only limited data on the association between local factors and peri‐implantitis. In this regard, the present cross‐sectional study was designed to evaluate the role of local factors in influencing the occurrence of peri‐implantitis.

Materials and Methods

The protocol of this cross‐sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Extremadura (Badajoz, Spain) (Ref. SES‐CEI: 1207‐2024), and followed the Declaration of Helsinki on human studies. Patients were recruited at the CICOM‐MONJE Institute (Badajoz, Spain), and gave written informed consent to participate. All data was kept anonymized. The study was reported according to the STROBE guidelines, and the protocol was registered in www.clinicaltrials.gov.

Sample Size Calculation

The statistical analysis included the estimation of unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) via simple logistic regression for each independent factor and under the approach of generalized estimation equations (GEEs) or mixed models to take into account the multiplicity of implants per patient. The sample size was calculated based on the findings derived from a study exploring the same subject and published elsewhere (Kumar et al. 2018). Considering that the total sample was aimed at being represented by peri‐implantitis and non‐peri‐implantitis within the same subjects, a total of 79 patients and 238 implants were required to achieve a statistical power of 80% in identifying OR = 3, equivalent to a peri‐implantitis rate of 25%–50%. The authors, however, opted to expand the sample size by approximately 20% at the patient and implantlevels in order to increase the power to approximately 90%.

Study Sample

Consecutively treated partially edentulous patients who had previously received roughened surface implants placed in edentulous ridges for oral rehabilitation were eligible. Only patients presenting one or more implants with peri‐implantitis were included. Moreover, the included patients were required to have a cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan taken during diagnosis. CBCT is routinely performed in our center following the confirmation of peri‐implantitis, based on the clinical characteristics and periapical radiographic assessment, to assist in deciding the therapeutic modality, due to its accuracy in identifying marginal bone level, defect morphology, and implant position (Insua et al. 2021). Implants were required to be in function for ≥ 36 months following delivery of the final prosthesis to exclude cases of excessive early bone loss that may prove misleading in the diagnosis of peri‐implantitis. Subjects were excluded from the study if they presented any of the following conditions: pregnancy or breastfeeding, zygomatic or pterygoid implants, or the use of medications at the time of diagnosis known to modify bone metabolism.

Case Definition of Peri‐Implantitis

Peri‐implantitis was defined according to the 2017 World Workshop of Periodontal and Peri‐implant Diseases (Berglundh et al. 2018). The case definition applied was thus as follows: Presence of bleeding and/or suppuration on gentle probing.Probing depth ≥ 6 mm.Bone level ≥ 3 mm apical to the most coronal portion of bone level implants or at the rough‐smooth interface of tissue level implants, based on periapical radiographs.

Radiographic Analysis

CBCT scans were performed by an experienced radiologist (V.C.). Images of the included patients were obtained with the CBCT‐ICAT Model 17–19 system (Imaging Sciences International LLC, Hatfield, PA, USA). The settings were 16 × 13 mm in width and depth, 120 kVp, 20.27 mAs, scan time 14.7 s, resolution at 0.25 voxels, and field of view (FOV) varying according to the region scanned. Files were exported in DICOM format and entered in the coDiagnostiX software (Implant planning Software, Dental Wings GmbH, Chemnitz, Germany). The radiographic evaluation included an analysis of the implants and their relationship with adjacent anatomical structures. These assessments were made by a previously calibrated examiner (R.P). For calibration, 10% of the total sample of implants was randomly selected from patients not included in the study, and measurements were assessed spaced 24 h apart. The examiner yielded an intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) of > 0.85 in the assessment of bucco‐lingual implant position.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed for both quantitative (mean and standard deviation [SD]) and qualitative variables (absolute and relative frequencies). Inferential analysis involved estimation using GEEs of simple logistic regression models to test the odds of an implant exhibiting peri‐implantitis in the presence/absence of local factors. Non‐adjusted ORs were determined, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the coefficients were obtained from the Wald Chi (Kumar et al. 2024) statistic equation. Multiple logistic regression models were further calculated with the variables that yielded significance (p < 0.05) and with those that showed a tendency without reaching significance (p < 0.1). GEEs were used due to the intra‐individual correlation between implants since few patients contributed with more than one implant. Estimations were obtained using the robust Huber‐White sandwich‐type method in order to obtain a more accurate variance of regression coefficients. Moreover, the diagnostic accuracy was calculated based on the variables that demonstrated significance in the multiple regression model. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and the ROC curve were calculated according to the Youden criterion.

Results

A total of 100 partially edentulous patients who met the eligibility criteria were included in the study (Figure S1). Of these, 30 were men and 70 women, with a mean age of 62.5 ± 9.7 years. Only 6% were smokers, while 3% were former smokers. Most patients (38.4%) were diagnosed with stage III periodontitis, grade C (43.4%). Regarding compliance, 54% were regular compliers and 21% erratic compliers. Concerning plaque control, 42% presented < 20%, while 38% (range 1–13) presented 20%–50%. The mean number of implants per patient was 4.5 ± 2.4 (range 1–13) (Table S1). In total, 452 implants were evaluated, with 227 (50.2%) being diagnosed with peri‐implantitis and 225 (49.8%) without peri‐implantitis. Implants with peri‐implantitis represented 55.6% ± 27.2% of the overall sample size (Table S2). A negative correlation (r = −0.38; p < 0.001) was found between the total number of implants and the number of implants diagnosed with peri‐implantitis. In other words, the greater the number of implants, the fewer the number of implants that exhibited peri‐implantitis in the same individual. This reflects the site‐specificity pattern of peri‐implantitis.

Discussion

From the dogmatic perspective, the site‐specific prevalence of peri‐implantitis is intriguing (Rakic et al. 2018). In an inflammatory disease mediated by pathogenic bacteria, it would be assumed that all dental implants placed in the same individual are all under the same degree of susceptibility. Nevertheless, the higher prevalence demonstrated at patient level when compared to implant level challenges this concept. The rationale for understanding this is based on the implication of local factors associated with the occurrence of the disease (Figures 4, 5, 6) (Monje, Kan, and Borgnakke 2023). However, clinical evidence linking modifiable local factors, whether anatomical or “man‐made” and peri‐implantitis is sparse (Albrektsson et al. 2016). The present cross‐sectional study shed light on the detrimental role of different local factors on the onset/progression of peri‐implantitis. Thus, the findings of the present study suggest that peri‐implantitis can be preventable if treatment planning is comprehensively outlined and if surgical and restorative care are meticulously executed. These findings do not rule out the etiological role of the bacterial biofilm in the occurrence of peri‐implantitis, but rather stress the importance of identifying and modifying those local factors that may cause exposure of the roughened implant surface to the peri‐implant sulcus, and hence to the oral environment that can serve as a reservoir for bacterial colonization. The rationale underlying this hypothesis is based on clinical and nonclinical trials that have demonstrated that either dehiscence‐type bone defects during implant placement (Song et al. 2024), following guided bone regeneration (Schwarz et al. 2012), or interproximal bone loss (Windael et al. 2021; Ravida et al. 2023; Galindo‐Moreno et al. 2015) during early healing, may compromise peri‐implant health over the long term. Moreover, local factors may contribute to predisposing the peri‐implant tissues and prosthetic components to plaque accumulation. However, cautiousness must be exercised when interpreting these findings, considering that only patients diagnosed with peri‐implantitis and with multiple implants supported prosthesis in function for ≥ 36 months were included in the multiple logistic regression analysis.

Findings from this study suggest that implant positioning in an inadequate mesio‐distal perspective is crucial in the prevention of peri‐implantitis. The plausibility behind this finding is that the emergence profile is primarily dictated by implant position. A wide angle could contribute to food impaction (Chanthasan et al. 2022) or to impairment of the integrity of the junctional epithelium of the implant supracrestal complex (Strauss et al. 2024). Additionally, it is not surprising that medial implants in multiple implant‐supported prostheses are more commonly exposed to peri‐implantitis (OR = 3.90, p = 0.006). This is in agreement with data published elsewhere (Ravida et al. 2019). Although the reason for this finding is not yet clear, it is speculated that: (1) medial implants are less accessible for self‐performed oral hygiene; (2) connections may oftentimes be ill‐fitting; and (3) implants placed in close proximity to mesial and/or distal implants may also increase susceptibility. In contrast, biomechanical aspects such as bending moment do not seem to deteriorate the prognosis of medial implants (de Souza Batista et al. 2017). Likewise, implants outside of the bony housing seem to be associated with greater odds for developing peri‐implantitis. This suggests that the lack of integrity of buccal bone during implant placement, or excessive bone resorption due to a thin residual buccal bone during implant surgery, may predispose to the disease (Monje, Roccuzzo, et al. 2023). Nonclinical data have demonstrated the role of buccal bone thickness in preventing major physiological and pathological bone loss (Monje, Chappuis, et al. 2019). It was noticed that when the buccal bone thickness was < 1.5 mm, there was an average of approximately 4 mm of buccal bone loss, which might facilitate surface contamination and subsequent inflammation/disease establishment (Monje, Chappuis, et al. 2019). This is also in line with clinical findings that have suggested higher rates of peri‐implantitis at sites with deficient alveolar bone dimensions when compared to wider recipient implant sites (Ortiz‐Echeverri et al. 2024).

While robust data have identified certain risk factors for peri‐implantitis, such as a history of or active periodontitis, a lack of compliance with supportive peri‐implant care, and systemic drivers (Monje, Kan, and Borgnakke 2023), the role of local factors has received little attention to date. Regarding implant malpositioning from a three‐dimensional perspective within the alveolus, Canullo et al. (2016) have demonstrated a very strong association between implant malpositioning and peri‐implantitis (OR = 48.2, p = 0.0001). In relation to adjacent implants/teeth, Schuldt Filho et al. (2014) showed that implants placed very close to one another (< 3 mm) were more likely to develop peri‐implantitis when compared to implants placed ≥ 3 mm apart (OR = 2.98, p = 0.006). The present study further validates that implant malpositioning in bucco‐lingual and mesio‐distal perspectives increases the likelihood of peri‐implantitis. Furthermore, the ideal apico‐coronal implant position has been the subject of controversy. Obreja et al. (2021) demonstrated that the highest incidence of peri‐implant mucositis corresponded to those dental implants placed in a range from1 to 3 mm below the bony crest. Kumar et al. (2018) in turn noticed that single‐unit implants placed ≥ 6 mm from the adjacent CEJ were more likely to exhibit peri‐implantitis (OR = 8.5, p < 0.05). In line with this, the simple logistic regression analysis showed a statistically significant association between deeper implant positioning (≥ 6 mm from the adjacent CEJ) and peri‐implantitis (OR = 3.33, p = 0.030). This observation might be attributed to the impact of the sub‐crestal implant‐abutment interface that dictates the intensity and location of peri‐implant inflammatory cell accumulation and a greater invasion of neutrophils compared to supra‐crestal interfaces (10,512 vs. 2398 neutrophils/mm2) (Broggini et al. 2006). The multiple logistic regression model did not validate this finding, however. Likewise, two other publications failed to identify any such association (Corbella et al. 2023; Paolantoni et al. 2023). Interestingly, other factors widely associated in the literature with the onset/progression of peri‐implantitis (Monje and Blasi 2019; Halperin‐Sternfeld et al. 2016), such as the buccal band of keratinized mucosa (OR = 0.61, p = 0.04) and vestibular depth (OR = 0.49, p = 0.005), were found to be significant. However, in the present study, they only reached significance in the simple logistic regression analysis. This may suggest that while the keratinized mucosa contributes to protecting the peri‐implant environment, as suggested by a large body of evidence (Sanz et al. 2022), it appears to have an overarching dominance over surgery‐related factors associated with peri‐implantitis. Since such surgical factors have received little attention in the literature, the over‐weighing role of the keratinized mucosa over these factors has not been adequately explored. Along these lines, it has been suggested that implant malpositioning may entail a buccal deficiency of keratinized mucosa (Hammerle and Tarnow 2018). This might be attributed to the biological phenomena occurring subsequent to tooth extraction, where the buccal bone suffers more notable lateral ridge resorption that promotes coronal migration of the mucogingival margin (Cardaropoli et al. 2003; Araujo et al. 2006). In scenarios characterized by limited ridge width, where inadequate implant placement might occur more often, the buccal band of keratinized mucosa may also be absent or narrow. Thus, the clinical implication of our finding is that despite the outweighed association of implant positioning with peri‐implantitis, the missing band of keratinized mucosa may enhance the likelihood of disease occurrence. Moreover, it must be taken into account that the keratinized mucosa was assessed during the diagnosis of peri‐implantitis and not at baseline.

The implant connection also appeared to contribute to peri‐implantitis. The multiple logistic regression analysis showed a Morse taper connection to be less prone to exhibit peri‐implantitis (OR = 0.43, 0.03) when compared to an external connection. It is worth noting that implants with an internal hex demonstrated the greatest tendency to exhibit peri‐implantitis (OR = 10.8, p = 0.004). However, this anecdotal finding might be attributed to the small sample of implants that incorporated this feature in the present study (11 implants) and which coincidentally presented with peri‐implantitis. The superiority of the Morse taper connection is due to the greater stability it provides to the abutment (Castro et al. 2014) and to the enhanced sealing effect that prevents microleakage, which could result in bacteria migrating into the implant interface (Larrucea et al. 2018). This in turn could lead to an inflammatory infiltrate that would eventually result in bone loss (Broggini et al. 2006).

The statistical power is one of the major strengths of this study, especially considering the thorough analysis of variables potentially involved in the onset/progression of peri‐implantitis. The prediction model demonstrated a high level of accuracy in diagnosing peri‐implantitis. Canullo et al. (2016) reported an accuracy of 82.35% in a prediction model assembling profiles for plaque‐, prosthesis‐, and surgery‐induced peri‐implantitis. The diagnostic accuracy achieved in the present study was nearly 80%. In contrast, our study was founded on objective and measurable data based on CBCT scans, clinical assessment, and periapical radiographic analyses. It is advisable to incorporate these local factors into disease risk assessment tools for the prevention of peri‐implantitis (Heitz‐Mayfield et al. 2020). Shortcomings to any study must always be acknowledged. For instance, the vast majority of the implants assessed, regardless of their diagnosis, had one concrete implant connection (external hex) and a specific surface feature (anodized surface). Given that these characteristics were suggested as predisposing and acceleratory factors (Monje, Insua, and Wang 2019; Laleman and Lambert 2023), data from this study might not be completely representative of other clinical scenarios or situations involving other implant systems. On the other hand, an interesting finding from this study was a negative correlation between the total number of implants and the number of implants diagnosed with peri‐implantitis. This may indicate that cluster scenarios of multiple peri‐implantitis were infrequent. Such data may partially conflict with results published elsewhere (Derks et al. 2016). Although this finding may support the role of local factors in the prevention/development of peri‐implantitis, it can be attributed to the fact that many patients carried multiple implants with different function times. Although this variable was not found to be significant (see Table S2), it could bias the assessment.

Limitations must be acknowledged. Non‐peri‐implantitis implants were not further categorized as to whether they were healthy or had mucositis. Despite the overwhelming data suggesting peri‐implant mucositis to be a precursor to peri‐implantitis (Salvi et al. 2023), not all mucositis lesions progress to peri‐implantitis (Heitz‐Mayfield 2024). In fact, the prevalence of mucositis is rather higher when compared to peri‐implant health over the long term (20 years) (Roccuzzo et al. 2023). For this reason, we opted to pool the categories of mucositis with health for this comparison. Moreover, if mucositis were present, it could not be determined whether it was transient or long‐standing. It is also relevant to acknowledge limitations related to patient selection, considering that only individuals exhibiting peri‐implantitis were included. It was deemed suitable to compare implants with and without a diagnosis of peri‐implantitis in diseased patients in order to fairly explore the role of local factors. Otherwise, the patient's risk profile, including genetic predisposition and/or the presence of habits, might have interfered with the outcomes. Moreover, limitations derived from the assessment must be commented on. For instance, local factors were evaluated during the diagnosis of peri‐implantitis. For ethical reasons linked to the ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) principle of radiation safety, it might not be advisable to obtain CBCT scans on a regular basis following implant placement. In order to compensate for this shortcoming and to validate these factors, two evaluators (RP, CS) retrospectively appraised baseline clinical photos saved in coded digital folders with the patient records. In addition, due to the cross‐sectional nature of this study, establishing a direct link between the local factor and peri‐implantitis predisposition/protection is not possible. Caution is thus recommended when interpreting these findings.

In summary, the findings from this study highlight the role that the surgeon may have in relation to peri‐implantitis. Renouard et al. defined “human factors” as the primary cause for complications in implant dentistry, and suggested the application of specific protocols to reduce the impact of human error (Renouard et al. 2017). In fact, implants placed and restored by specialists tend to exhibit lower rates of peri‐implantitis (Derks et al. 2016; Buser et al. 2012). Therefore, advanced training is suggested to approach implant therapy, encompassing didactic and practical aspects related to anatomy, surgery, and restoration. Nowadays, with the implementation of computer‐assisted strategies, implant treatments must improve in terms of predictability and effectiveness in order to reduce the risk of complications over the long term. Practitioners should not obviate these resources in order to optimize the treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

Implant malpositioning is an important local factor associated with peri‐implantitis. Moreover, implant location within the implant‐supported fixed prosthesis and the implant‐abutment connection may further play a role in the onset/progression of peri‐implantitis. Two considerations are postulated to lie behind these findings. In effect, local factors may either (a) promote plaque accumulation at the supra‐crestal component, or (b) lead to excessive physiological bone loss during early healing, thus resulting in exposure of the roughened implant surface to the peri‐implant sulcus. This, in turn, may increase the risk of bacterial contamination during function. Cautiousness must be exercised, though, when interpreting these findings, considering that only patients diagnosed with peri‐implantitis and with multiple implants supported prosthesis in function for ≥ 36 months were included in the multiple logistic regression analysis.
