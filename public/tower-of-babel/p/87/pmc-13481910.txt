Meta-Analysis of the Incidence and Influencing Factors of Malignant Tumors in Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Yingkai Gao, Guancheng Ye, Luyuan Gao, Cunxiang Xie, Jian Huang, Hailong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to systematically evaluate the incidence of malignant tumors in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients and identify influencing factors, providing an evidence-based foundation for early identification of high-risk groups and individualized management.MethodsCohort studies on tumor incidence and ifluencing factors in RA patients were searched in both Chinese and English databases from inception to July 31, 2025. Two researchers independently screened literature, extracted data, and assessed quality. Meta-analysis was performed using Stata 12.0 to calculate pooled incidence and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The study is registered with Prospero (CRD420251141184).ResultsA total of 44 cohort studies involving 901,941 RA patients were included. The pooled incidence of malignant tumors was 4.1% (95%CI 3.3%-4.8%). Significant influencing factors were: age (HR 1.17, 95%CI 1.12–1.22), male sex (HR 1.58, 95%CI 1.23–2.04), smoking (HR 1.33, 95%CI 1.07–1.66), tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) use (HR 0.80, 95%CI 0.66–0.97), abatacept use (HR 1.19, 95%CI 1.10–1.29), and comorbidities (HR 1.13, 95%CI 1.02–1.26), particularly lung disease (HR 2.17, 95%CI 1.48–3.17) and diabetes (HR 1.10, 95%CI 1.03–1.17). The following influencing factors without statistical significance are: body mass index(BMI), Non steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs(NSAIDs), methotrexate(MTX), biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs/targeted synthesis disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs(bDMARDs/tsDMARDs), interleukin-6 inhibitor(IL-6i), Janus kinase inhibitor(JAKi), glucocorticoid, serum-positive, diease duration.ConclusionAmong RA patients, older age, male sex, smoking, abatacept use, and comorbidities may be associated with increased tumor risk, while TNFi use may be associated with lower risk. Clinicians should consider these factors to identify high-risk populations for enhanced monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00574-3.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Malignant tumor, Influencing factors, Meta, Incidence

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, symmetrical polyarthritis characterized as an autoimmune disease, with its primary clinical manifestations involving joint inflammation. The underlying pathological mechanism includes aberrant activation of the immune system, leading to the release of numerous inflammatory mediators and the progressive degradation of joint cartilage and bone. Globally, the prevalence of RA ranges from approximately 0.24% -1% [1], with a higher incidence observed in females compared to males [2] In recent years, the incidence of RA in China has been increasing annually. During the progression of RA, nearly 80% of patients experience comorbidities [3], which include cardiovascular diseases, respiratory disorders, infections, and malignancies. Among these, malignant tumors are a common comorbidity associated with RA [4].This association may be attributed to the chronic inflammatory state and dysregulated immune system observed in RA patients. Persistent inflammatory responses can facilitate tumor formation and progression, a phenomenon particularly evident in certain RA patients [5]. Furthermore, the presence of certain biomarkers in patients with RA, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, has been associated with the development of cancer [6]. These biomarkers are not only integral to the pathophysiology of RA but may also play a crucial role in cancer development. A study conducted in South Korea identified malignant tumors as the leading cause of mortality among individuals with RA [7]. Therefore, it is imperative to consider the potential for malignancies in RA patients within clinical settings.

Previous research has indicated that the incidence of certain types of malignant tumors is significantly higher in RA patients compared to the general population [8, 9]. However, there is a paucity of large-scale studies examining the incidence of tumors in RA patients, and research on the associated risk factors remains inadequate. Consequently, this study aims to perform a meta-analysis of the incidence and risk factors of malignant tumors in RA patients. The goal is to provide an evidence-based foundation for identifying high-risk groups at an early clinical stage and for developing tailored monitoring and intervention strategies.

Search Strategy

The literature retrieval process utilized several databases, including the PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Embase, China Knowledge Network (CNKI), VIP, Wanfang Data and the Centre National Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), These databases were searched for studies published up to July 31, 2025, focusing on patients with RA who have developed malignant tumors. The scope of the literature was restricted to publications in Chinese and English. The Chinese search terms included “rheumatoid arthritis,” “malignant tumor,” “cancer,” “incidence,” and “risk factors.” Correspondingly, the English search terms comprised “rheumatoid arthritis,” “neoplasm,” “cancer,” “tumor,” “incidence,” and “prevalence,” among others. Detailed retrieval strategies are provided in the supplementary materials, eAppendix 1.

Materials and Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: [1] The study population consisted of adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with confirmed RA, diagnosed according to the 1987 ACR or the 2009/2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria; [2] The study design was a cohort study; [3] The sample size included more than 200 patients; [4] Outcome measures included the incidence of malignant tumors in RA patients or reported relevant risk factors. The following criteria will be used to exclude studies from consideration: [1] The research subjects are integrated with other systemic autoimmune diseases; [2] The outcome is a malignant tumor in a specific location; [3] The literature reviewed includes types such as reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, theses, or studies that cannot be extracted from data.

Literature Screening and Data Extraction

EndNote X9 software is utilized for literature management and weighting. Two researchers(Gao YK and Ye GC) independently screen the literature and extract data. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher (Wang HL) acts as an arbitrator. The extracted information comprises the first author, year of publication, country/region, sample size, number of malignant tumor cases, risk factors, and their effect values (such as OR, HR, RR, etc.).

Literature Quality Assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) is employed for evaluation, which consists of three components: selection of study population, comparability, and outcome measurement. Each study can receive a maximum of 9 points: 4 for selection, 3 for outcomes, and 2 for comparability. Detailed scoring criteria are provided in the supplementary material, eAppendix 2. Each affirmative response is scored, and based on the total score, studies are categorized as high quality (scores of 7–9), medium quality (scores of 4–6), or low quality (scores below 4). Two researchers (Gao YK and Ye GC) independently conducted a literature quality assessment using the NOS scale. In case of disagreement, it was decided by a third researcher.

Identification and Handling of Repetitive Groups

If multiple articles meet any of the following conditions, it is determined that there is a duplicate population: [1] Clearly declare the use of the same database or queue; [2] The research institutions, regions, time periods, and sample sizes are highly consistent and cannot prove sample independence; [3] Subsequent publications cite previous research and indicate data overlap.

For identified duplicate groups, the following priority rules are used for processing: [1] For identical samples, only the one with the most complete information, longest follow-up time, or largest sample size is retained; [2] Mother subset relationship, including mother set research and excluding subset research; [3] The study that retains the longest follow-up time for different follow-up times within the same population; [4] When reporting different combinations of influencing factors within the same population, they can be separately included in the analysis of different influencing factors. However, when merging effects, it should be noted that they come from the same population and sensitivity analysis should be conducted.

Statistical Analysis

Use Stata 12.0 software conducts Meta analysis. When pooling the incidence of malignant tumors, the proportion and the 95%CI are calculated. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on publication year and country. For influencing factors, we pooled the natural logarithm of the HR using the inverse-variance weighting method. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q-test and I²statistics. if I²>50% or P < 0.05, use the random effect model; we considered I²> 75% as high heterogeneity, and in such cases, the pooled effect sizes were interpreted with caution. Otherwise, use the fixed effect model. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies and change the merge model. Use Egger’s test and funnel chart to evaluate the publication bias. The difference of P < 0.05 is statistically significant.

Results

Following the initial database retrieval, a total of 12,403 documents were identified. After the removal of duplicate entries, 8,223 articles remained. Subsequent screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts resulted in the inclusion of 44 documents, all of which are cohort studies [10–53], and all reports HR. Therefore, no conversion of effect measures was required, and all meta-analyses for influencing factors were performed using HR. The literature screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1, while the primary characteristics of the selected studies are detailed in Table 1. The quality of the literature was assessed using the NOS. The assessment revealed that 35 studies were of high quality, 9 were of medium quality, and none were of low quality. The consistency of the scores was good (Kappa = 0.84, 95% CI 0.76–0.92). For detailed information on specific items and their scores, please refer to Table S1 in the supplementary materials. It is worth noting that the NOS scale focuses more on evaluating the completeness of the report rather than the rigor of the methodology, and there is some variability among raters. Although the consistency of ratings in this study is acceptable, bias introduced by this cannot be ruled out.

Analysis of Pooled Proportion Incidence of Malignant Tumors in RA Patients

This study encompassed a total of 44 studies, comprising 901941 patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). There was substantial heterogeneity among the studies (I²=99.9%, P<0.01), necessitating the use of a random effects model for analysis. The findings indicated that the pooled cumulative incidence of malignant tumors in RA patients was 4.1% (95%CI 3.3%-4.8%, P<0.01). The 95% prediction interval (PI) for the true incidence was 0.0% to 9.4%, reflecting the substantial heterogeneity across settings. Given the considerable heterogeneity of the outcome measure, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify its sources. The exclusion of studies such as that by Raheel et al. resulted in a significant reduction in heterogeneity. This study had a low NOS score. This suggests that the quality of the literature may be the primary source of heterogeneity. Further examination revealed publication bias in the literature (Egger's test, P=0.002). A series of subgroup analyses regarding the pooled cumulative incidence was performed, with the summary presented in Table 2. We performed a subgroup analysis based on the publication year, country, follow-up duratio and literature quality of the included studies to assess the pooled proportion incidence of malignant tumors in patients with RA . The findings indicated that the publication year and literature did not significantly influence the incidence rate, suggesting that the results demonstrated robustness. As for the subgroup analysis by country. The analysis revealed that Singapore exhibited the highest pooled cumulative incidence at 11.8% (95%CI 10.0%-13.9%, P<0.01), a difference that was statistically significant. Overall, the pooled proporion incidence of malignant tumors in RA patients appears to be slightly lower in North America compared to Eurasia. However, the limited number of studies from certain countries and regions necessitates cautious interpretation of these findings, which should be further validated through large-scale research in the future. In the subgroup analysis of follow-up time, there was a significant gradient in the amount of effect between groups (the lowest in the<5 year group and the highest in the>10 year group), and there was less overlap in the confidence intervals between groups, suggesting that the duration of follow-up may be one of the important factors affecting the cumulative incidence rate of malignant tumors in RA patients. As the follow-up time prolongs, the cumulative risk of tumor occurrence significantly increases.

Analysis of Influencing Factors for Malignant Tumors in RA Patients

Sociodemographic Factors

Age

9 studies [12, 16, 18, 23, 32, 41, 46, 50, 51] evaluated the association between age and RA combined with tumors. with a total of 178516 subjects. Due to substantial heterogeneity among the studies (I2=96.3%, P<0.01), a random effects model was employed. The findings indicate that age may be associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients(HR 1.17, 95%CI 1.12-1.22, P<0.01), as illustrated in Figure 2a. Given the high level of heterogeneity in the outcome measure, a sensitivity analysis was performed to identify its source. The heterogeneity was notably reduced upon the exclusion of the study by Ahn et al., which had previously excluded patients with cardiovascular diseases, suggesting this exclusion as a primary source of heterogeneity. Detailed results of the sensitivity analysis are provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S1). The distribution of scattered points in the funnel plot is asymmetric(Figure 6a), indicating the presence of publication bias.

Sex

8 studies(12, 18, 23, 32, 41, 46, 50, 51) evaluated the association between sex and malignancy in patients with RA, with a total of 178089 subjects. Due to substantial heterogeneity among the studies (I2=95.6%, P<0.01), a random effects model was employed. Research indicates that compared with female patients, male RA patients have a 58% increased risk of developing malignant tumors (HR 1.58, 95%CI 1.23-2.04, P<0.01), as illustrated in Figure 2b. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using the one-by-one exclusion method. The results showed that after excluding any one study, the remaining pooled HR values did not exhibit statistically significant deviations compared to the overall pooled HR values, indicating that the meta-analysis results of this study possess good robustness.The results of the sensitivity analysis are detailed in the supplementary materials (Figure S2). The distribution of scattered points in the funnel plot is asymmetric(Figure 6b), indicating the presence of publication bias.

Smoking

4 studies [18, 41, 42, 51] investigated the association between smoking and malignancy in patients with RA, encompassing a total of 13103 participants. The heterogeneity among these studies was relatively low (I2=39.6%, P=0.174), and a fixed-effect model was employed for analysis. The findings indicate that smoking may be associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients (HR 1.33, 95%CI 1.07-1.66, P=0.009), as illustrated in Figure 2c. The distribution of scattered points in the funnel plot is asymmetric(Figure 6c), indicating the presence of publication bias.

BMI

2 studies [18, 41] examined the relationship between BMI and malignancy in patients with RA, encompassing a total of 12112 cases. The heterogeneity among these studies was relatively low (I2=47.4%, P=0.168), and a fixed-effect model was employed for analysis. The findings indicate that BMI does not have a statistically significant effect for malignancy in patients with RA(HR=1.00, 95%CI 0.97-1.03, P=0.919), as illustrated in Figure 2d. The distribution of scattered points in the funnel plot is asymmetric(Figure 6d), indicating the presence of publication bias. However, due to the limited number of studies included, the funnel plot is only used as a reference.

Medication-Related Factors

NSAIDs

4 studies [12, 41, 46, 54] examined the relationship between NSAIDs and malignancy in patients with RA, involving a total of 56,688 participants. The heterogeneity among these studies was relatively high (I2 = 55.6%, P = 0.08), prompting the use of a random effects model. The findings indicate that the impact of NSAIDs on malignancy in patients with RA is not statistically significant (HR 1.11, 95%CI 0.87–1.43, P = 0.387), as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Given the substantial heterogeneity of the outcome measure, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify its source. The stepwise elimination method revealed a significant reduction in heterogeneity upon excluding the study by Raheel et al., which had a low NOS score. This suggests that the quality of the literature may be the primary source of heterogeneity. Detailed results of the sensitivity analysis are available in the supplementary material (Figure S3). The distribution of scattered points in the funnel plot is asymmetric(Fig. 6e), indicating the presence of publication bias.

5 studies [32, 41, 42, 46, 51] examined the relationship between MTX and malignancy in patients with RA, involving a total of 45633 participants. There was no heterogeneity between the studies (I2 = 9.3%, P = 0.353). The results show that the use of MTX had no statistically significant effect for malignancy in patients with RA (HR 0.96, 95%CI 0.85–1.07, P = 0.448), as shown in Fig. 3b. bDMARDs/tsDMARDs

Further details on the sensitivity analysis are provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S4). Disease Duration

4 studies [18, 41, 46, 51] examined the correlation between disease duration and malignancy in patients with RA. The combined sample size across these studies was 35031 participants, and the heterogeneity among the studies was moderate (I²=55.6%, P = 0.08), warranting the use of a random effects model. The findings indicate that the disease duration of RA is not statistically significantly associated with the development of malignant tumors (HR 1.07, 95%CI 0.90–1.27, P = 0.424), as illustrated in Fig. 4b. Due to the substantial heterogeneity of the outcome measure, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify its source. The exclusion of the study by Sugimoto et al. resulted in a marked reduction in heterogeneity. This study was conducted based on a single-center cohort, which may be the reason for the heterogeneity. Detailed results of the sensitivity analysis are provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S8). Furthermore, the scatter distribution of the funnel plot is roughly symmetrical, indicating no significant publication bias.

Comorbidity

The association between comorbidities and malignancy in patients with RA was evaluated across 5 studies [12, 23, 42, 46, 50]. A total of 143691 cases were analyzed, revealing heterogeneity among the studies (I²=75.5%, P < 0.01), which necessitated the use of a random effects model. The findings indicate that comorbidities may be associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients (HR 1.13, 95%CI 1.02–1.26, P = 0.024). The scatter distribution of the funnel plot is roughly symmetrical, indicating no significant publication bias. Given the substantial heterogeneity of the outcome indicator, sensitivity analysis was employed to identify the source of heterogeneity, examining each study individually. The division method revealed a significant reduction in heterogeneity upon the exclusion of the study by Ahn et al., which previously included patients with cardiovascular diseases, identified as a primary source of heterogeneity. Detailed results of the sensitivity analysis are provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S9). Subgroup analysis demonstrated that within the comorbid subgroup, lung disease (n = 19543, HR 2.17, 95%CI 1.48–3.17, P < 0.01) and diabetes (n = 22130, HR 1.10, 95%CI 1.03–1.17, P = 0.007) may be associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients, while hypertension(n = 124148), hyperlipidemia(n = 101816) and other diseases(n = 14545, Including fractures and severe infections) does not have a statistically significant association with malignancy in patients with RA, as illustrated in Fig. 4c.

Sensitivity Analysis and Publication bias

Sensitivity analysis revealed that, the primary sources of heterogeneity include exclusion of patients with cardiovascular diseases, variability in literature quality, single-center cohort, and follow-up time. When the number of studies included in the combined analysis of a influencing factor is ≥ 10, we use the Egger's test to assess publication bias. For outcomes with a small number of included studies (<10), the Egger's test has low efficacy, and its P-value is only for reference. We use a funnel plot for intuitive interpretation (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Summarize the main Research Results

This study encompasses 44 cohort studies examining the incidence of malignant tumors in patients with RA. The the pooled cumulative incidence of malignant tumors in RA patients is 4.1%(95%CI 3.3%-4.8%，P<0.01). Of these studies, 16 studies only reported the incidence rate of malignant tumors in RA patients, and 28 studies reported the incidence rate and influencing factors of malignant tumors in RA patients. The meta-analysis indicates that advanced age, male, smoking, use of abatacept, and the presence of comorbidities may be associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients, and use of TNFi may be associated with an decrease risk for malignant tumors in RA patients. However, due to significant heterogeneity and publication bias in some results, cautious interpretation is warranted. Further details are provided in Table 3.

Strengths

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the inaugural meta-analysis examining the influencing factors associated with tumors in RA patients. The influecing factors considered encompass a broad spectrum, including the sociological characteristics of patients, rheumatic treatment medications, laboratory indicators, comorbidities, disease progression, and other relevant aspects, thereby demonstrating extensive coverage and a high degree of innovation. Additionally, this study conducts a meta-analysis on the incidence of malignancy in patients with RA. Prior to this, researchers such as Smitten et al. [8] and Simon et al. [9] conducted correlation studies that explored the incidence of various types of malignant tumors in distinct groups. Our study, however, incorporates a greater number of literary sources, resulting in a larger sample size and enhanced research quality.

Influencing Factor Analysis of Malignancy in Patients with RA

The findings indicate that age is associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients. In light of the global trend of population aging, the incidence of numerous diseases, including malignant tumors, is closely linked to advancing age [55]. Research indicates that the aging process is accompanied by increased genomic instability [56], enhanced immune inhibition [57], prolonged exposure to carcinogenic factors, and other changes, all of which contribute to an elevated incidence of tumors. However, the high heterogeneity observed in the age warrants careful interpretation. The heterogeneity may stem from different age categorizations (e.g., continuous vs. categorical) and varying baseline age distributions across studies. Compared with female patients, male RA patients have a increased risk of developing malignant tumors. This may influenced by inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6, which are more pronounced in male RA patients. This may be attributed to differences in the endocrine system and immune response between genders [58, 59].

Smoking is associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients.Research has shown that smoking is not only a known risk factor for RA, but also one of the triggers for various malignant tumors. The study by Tue et al. found that smoking is associated with an increased premature mortality rate in RA patients, mainly due to a higher incidence of malignant tumors [60]. The impact of smoking on RA patients is not limited to an increased risk of cancer. Studies have shown that smoking may exacerbate the progression of RA and affect treatment outcomes. For example, in RA patients treated with JAKi, smoking is considered one of the factors that increases the risk of and malignant tumors [61].

Regarding the use of bDMARDs/tsDMARDs, we first analyzed all bDMARDs/tsDMARDs as a single category to assess the overall risk associated with biologic/targeted synthetic therapies. Subsequently, we performed separate analyses by drug class (TNFi, IL-6i, abatacept, and JAKi) to evaluate potential differences in cancer risk according to mechanism of action. The use of abatacept may be associated with an increase risk for malignant tumors in RA patients. The findings of a meta-analysis by Wenhui Xie et al. [62] support this, suggesting that abatacept may increase the risk of cancer in RA patients, thereby corroborating our research and indicating a degree of robustness in the results. Abatacept exerts its immunomodulatory effects by regulating the co stimulatory signaling pathways of T cells, namely the CD28 and CD80/CD86 signaling pathways. This mechanism plays a key role in inhibiting the pathological process of rheumatoid arthritis [63]. However, it is precisely this immune regulatory effect that may lead to a weakening of immune surveillance function, thereby increasing the risk of malignant tumor occurrence. However, the observational data cannot establish causality and that unmeasured confounding (e.g., disease severity, prior treatments) is a major threat to validity. The use of TNFi seems to be a protective factor against malignant tumors in RA patients, which is consistent with the research results of Choi et al. [29], Lan et al. [40].

However, this also requires consideration of confounding factors, for instance, may reflect channeling bias where patients with lower baseline cancer risk are preferentially prescribed these agents.

In examining comorbidities, this study identified that concurrent lung disease and diabetes serve as risk factors for malignancy in patients with RA. There exists a complex interplay between diabetes and RA, where metabolic abnormalities in diabetic patients may exacerbate the inflammatory response of RA, thereby heightening the risk of malignancies. Research indicates that metabolic abnormalities, such as elevated blood pressure and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, are more pronounced in patients with concurrent RA and diabetes [64]. These metabolic disturbances may elevate the risk of malignancies by impairing immune system function. The relationship between lung diseases and RA is particularly significant. RA patients are susceptible to pulmonary interstitial lesions and exhibit a bidirectional relationship with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [65]. The presence of lung diseases not only aggravates the condition of RA, but also increases the incidence of malignant tumors through complex immune mechanisms.

Clinical Significance and Recommendations

Malignant tumors represent a significant comorbidity of RA and are a major cause of mortality among affected patients. A large-scale cohort study in Korea showed that the mortality rate of RA patients is significantly higher than that of the general population, with malignant tumors being one of the most common causes of death, accounting for 17.8% of total deaths [66]. A comprehensive understanding of the incidence and influencing factors associated with malignant tumors in RA patients is crucial for early detection and prevention strategies. Clinically, heightened vigilance is warranted for male patients, the elderly, and those with concurrent lung disease or diabetes, necessitating more rigorous screening and monitoring protocols. For example, low-dose spiral CT can be performed once a year to screen for lung cancer, gastrointestinal endoscopy can be performed every 1-3 years to screen for gastrointestinal tumors, female patients can be performed molybdenum target examination every year to screen for breast cancer, and male patients can be performed prostate specific antigen testing every year to screen for prostate cancer. Additionally, regular assessment of inflammatory markers in RA patients is essential to mitigate inflammation levels. When selecting bDMARDs/tsDMARDs for RA treatment, careful selection of treatment drugs should be based on the specific situation of the patient.

Limitations

(1)Only publicly available Chinese and English literature has been included in this study, which may introduce publication bias; (2)Some influencing factors (such as BMI, IL-6, serum positivity) are included in a relatively small number of literature, and their statistical power is limited; (3)The studies included in this analysis are predominantly from Europe, the United States, Japan, and South Korea, potentially leading to inaccurate pooled proportion incidence. There is a need for more research and data from other regions; (4)Due to the exclusion of studies with site-specific cancer outcomes and the focus on overall malignancy in the included studies, subgroup analyses by tumor type were not performed in this meta-analysis, which may mask important differences. Future research should investigate site-specific cancer risk when sufficient studies become available; (5)Most studies have not adjusted for confounding factors, which may lead to bias; (6)Although 35 studies were of high quality, 9 were of moderate quality. Potential biases inherent in these moderate-quality studies, such as selection or detection bias, could have influenced our pooled estimates.

Conclusion

In summary, patients with RA have a higher likelihood of developing malignant tumors compared to the general population. Specifically, the incidence of malignant tumors is higher among male and elderly RA patients, as well as those with elevated levels of Abatacept and CRP, and those with concurrent lung disease and diabetes. In the future, it is important to consider these risk factors in the diagnosis and treatment of RA. These findings can serve as a reference for developing targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for RA.
