Advances in Efforts to Enhance Care Coordination and Wellbeing Among Family Caregivers of Children With Medical Complexity
Authors: Jaxon J. Vallely, Alicia Haupt, Kelly W. Harris, Justin Yu
Published: 2026

As the population of children with medical complexity (CMC) continues to grow, services and resources for their family caregivers, who manage most of their medical and logistical needs, have not kept pace. In “Family Caregivers of Children with Medical Complexity: Changes in Health-Related Quality of Life and Experiences of Care Coordination,” we examined the relationship between changes in family experiences of care coordination and changes in family caregivers’ health-related quality of life.1 In a longitudinal study following CMC family caregivers for one year after their child’s enrollment in a pediatric complex care center, CMC family caregivers reported significant improvements in their experiences of care coordination, yet no meaningful changes in any dimension of health-related quality of life. Our findings suggested that while dedicated care coordination services can improve family experiences of care coordination, multimodal interventions attending to mental health and social determinants of health are likely needed to improve family caregiver well-being. Here we review findings published since our study, highlighting selected research focused on CMC care coordination, care plans, and family caregiver well-being.

Care Coordination

We highlight two randomized controlled trials that evaluated enhanced care coordination models for CMC with mixed results. Cohen et al. randomized CMC families to either enhanced care coordination services from a nurse practitioner-physician dyad or a 12-month waitlist control.2 At one year, compared to waitlist controls, family caregivers of CMC receiving the intervention reported significantly higher utility of care planning tools; however, no significant differences were observed in family experiences of care coordination, child or family caregiver health outcomes, or child health care utilization or costs. Interestingly, once both groups were receiving the intervention, significant improvements in family caregiver physical and sleep-related health and reductions in overall health care costs emerged, suggesting benefits of care coordination interventions may accrue over longer periods. Nageswaran et al. assessed the efficacy of a structured collaboration between complex care clinicians and CMC’s home health nurses and primary care providers.3 Compared to CMC families receiving usual care, intervention recipients reported significantly less time spent coordinating care, however experienced no reductions in emergency department visits or hospitalizations. Together, these trials demonstrate the potentially intractable nature of high health care utilization among CMC, inherent latency of measurable clinical benefit in care coordination research, and the need to reconsider and reprioritize outcome measure selection. For example, improving family caregiver administrative burden may represent a more meaningful intervention target, particularly in the context of documented disparities in the administrative workload faced by CMC family caregivers. Alternatively, more intensive and resource-heavy models of care coordination and/or longer study timelines may be required to achieve significant improvements in child health and care utilization outcomes.

Shared Care Plans

We also reviewed recent literature examining the impact of shared care plans. Bugnon et al. used a qualitative co-design approach to explore the potential benefits of enabling patients, family caregivers, and clinicians to co-create and co-manage shared medication plans within the electronic health record.4 Participants believed shared medication plans had the potential to promote collaborative multidisciplinary medication co-management, improve medication safety and efficacy, and empower patients/family caregivers and foster shared accountability. In a study of emergency information forms, Lyons et al. evaluated how form implementation impacted CMC care in the emergency department.5 Although use of emergency information forms was not associated with shorter disposition decision times, they were viewed favorably by emergency department clinicians who reported that the forms were accurate and expedited care delivery. The authors also observed a small reduction in CMC hospitalization rates. These studies highlight potential strategies for using the electronic health record to facilitate information sharing and improve the accessibility of concise, accurate medical information. In addition to evaluating the efficacy of shared care plans on child health and care efficiency outcomes, future research must evaluate approaches for broader dissemination and implementation across health systems.

Family Caregiver Quality of Life

Recent work builds on our findings regarding the relationship between enhanced care coordination and CMC family caregiver well-being. In a systemic review and meta-synthesis of qualitative studies exploring CMC family caregivers’ experiences with health services, Page et al. identified ongoing fragmentation and poor communication within pediatric health systems as a central source of family caregiver stress.6 Family caregivers also emphasized their desire for clinicians to recognize the impact of a child’s needs on the entire family unit and health systems to provide “holistic” family-centered care specifically addressing social drivers of health and family caregiver well-being. Similarly, in a secondary analysis of the National Survey of Children’s Health 2016–2021, Huynh et al. found that the two main predictors of poorer mental health, reduced coping, and increased aggravation among CMC family caregivers were 1) lack of emotional support, and 2) higher frustration and effort in care coordination demands.7 The authors demonstrated that CMC family caregivers often rely on external, non-family sources (eg, health care providers, peer support groups) for emotional support, underscoring the importance of health system-based psychosocial resources.8 Lastly, we highlight Esser et al.’s retrospective case series describing a virtual psychiatry service embedded within a pediatric complex care program in Canada.9 In contrast to the aforementioned trials,2,3 which yielded no improvement in family caregiver well-being, the authors observed new mental health diagnoses and recommendations for psychotherapy and/or medication treatment among the majority of CMC family caregiver participants – demonstrating the high prevalence of unmet mental health needs and feasibility of dedicated mental health services for CMC family caregivers. Overall, these findings are further evidence that while enhanced care coordination is an essential service for CMC families, it is unlikely to improve family caregiver well-being in isolation. Future work must also focus on the development, evaluation, and dissemination of enhanced psychosocial services.

Future Work

To further improve the health and well-being of CMC families, our field must prioritize multimodal family-centered approaches that enhance care coordination support and address the broader social and emotional drivers of health. Importantly, the perspectives of CMC family caregivers must be integrated into the design, testing, and dissemination of such interventions to ensure we are measuring outcomes that truly matter to families.10
