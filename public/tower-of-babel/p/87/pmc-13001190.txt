Effectiveness of IV cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate mofetil in the treatment of childhood-onset lupus nephritis in a resource-limited setting
Authors: Adrienne Katrin M Guiang-Valerio, Ma Theresa M Collante, Christine B Bernal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLupus nephritis (LN) is a major cause of morbidity in childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE). Cyclophosphamide (CYC) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) are recommended first-line induction therapies, yet comparative pediatric real-world data, particularly from Asian populations, remain limited. This study compared the effectiveness of CYC and MMF for induction of renal remission and evaluated patterns and predictors of treatment switching among Filipino children with LN.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of children with LN treated at a tertiary hospital. Patients received CYC using the NIH intravenous protocol or standard-dose MMF. Complete renal response (CRR) was defined as proteinuria < 0.5 g/day (UPCR < 500 mg/g) with stable renal function. Incidence rates were calculated using person-time methods. Time-to-CRR was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank testing. Logistic regression identified predictors of treatment switching.ResultsA total of 231 patients were included. Baseline disease severity was greater in the CYC group, with higher proteinuria and more frequent Class IV nephritis. Overall CRR incidence was 4.86 per 100 person-months (95% CI 4.18–5.62), with similar rates between CYC and MMF (4.91 vs. 4.71; p = 0.818). CRR occurred in 77.0% and 84.2% of patients, respectively, and median time-to-CRR did not differ significantly (13 vs. 24 months; p = 0.431). Treatment switching was common, mainly due to inadequate response, and occurred more frequently with MMF. Rituximab was used in refractory cases. Female sex and tuberculosis increased the likelihood of switching, whereas higher prednisone dose and rituximab use were protective. No patients progressed to end-stage kidney disease.ConclusionsCYC and MMF showed comparable effectiveness for induction of childhood-onset LN. Frequent treatment modification reflects individualized, real-world management. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem involvement, with lupus nephritis (LN) representing one of its most serious and prognostically significant complications. In children, the incidence of childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) is estimated at 0.3–0.9 per 100,000 child-years, with a prevalence of 3.3–8.8 per 100,000 [1]. Approximately 55% of affected children develop LN during the disease course. Compared with adults, pediatric patients exhibit higher rates of renal involvement (50–82% vs. 20–40%) [2], with particularly increased prevalence among Asian populations [3].

Management of pediatric LN typically involves immunosuppressive induction therapy combined with corticosteroids and supportive care. Cyclophosphamide (CYC) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) are recommended first-line agents in current European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [4]. However, optimal induction strategies in children remain uncertain, as most evidence is extrapolated from adult trials and pediatric-specific comparative real-world data are limited.

International pediatric cohorts have reported complete renal response (CRR) rates ranging from 50% to 78.8% at 24 months [5], while long-term follow-up studies demonstrate CRR rates of 65.2% and 78.3% at 6 and 12 months, respectively, with corresponding partial remission rates of 19.6% and 7.6% [6]. These findings highlight heterogeneity in treatment response and long-term outcomes, underscoring the need for population-specific real-world evidence to guide clinical decision-making.

Despite the high burden of LN in Asian populations, no published studies from the Philippines have directly compared CYC and MMF in children. Furthermore, patterns of treatment modification—including switching between agents or escalation to biologic therapies—remain poorly characterized in routine practice.

This study therefore aimed to compare the effectiveness of CYC and MMF for induction of renal remission in Filipino children with LN using KDIGO criteria and to describe patterns, reasons, and predictors of treatment switching in a real-world setting.

Methodology

Study design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary referral hospital in the Philippines.

Study sample

All children diagnosed with cSLE and LN who were evaluated at the Pediatric Rheumatology department between January 2008 and September 2024 were eligible. Consecutive sampling was employed.

Patients met the 2019 EULAR/ACR SLE classification criteria [7] and had LN [8] confirmed either by renal biopsy demonstrating Class III, IV, or V disease or by clinical and laboratory evidence of active renal involvement as defined: (1) renal biopsy documenting LN according to the classification of the World Health Organization as proliferative glomerulonephritis class III (focal), IV (diffuse), or V (membranous); and/or (2) clinical activity as defined by one or more of the following [8]:

Incident decrease in renal function (serum creatinine, > 1.0 mg per deciliter [88.4 µmol per liter])

Incident decrease in renal function (serum creatinine, > 1.0 mg per deciliter [88.4 µmol per liter])

Proteinuria (> 500 mg of protein in a 24-hour urine specimen, random urine protein creatinine ratio (UPCR) of > 500 mg/g, dipstick protein of 2 + with any level of specific gravity, or dipstick protein of 1 + if with low specific gravity).

Proteinuria (> 500 mg of protein in a 24-hour urine specimen, random urine protein creatinine ratio (UPCR) of > 500 mg/g, dipstick protein of 2 + with any level of specific gravity, or dipstick protein of 1 + if with low specific gravity).

Microscopic hematuria (> 5 red cells per high power-field) or the presence of cellular casts, increasing proteinuria with rising levels of serum creatinine.

Microscopic hematuria (> 5 red cells per high power-field) or the presence of cellular casts, increasing proteinuria with rising levels of serum creatinine.

Active urine sediment (hematuria or cellular casts).

Active urine sediment (hematuria or cellular casts).

Serologic abnormality (anti-DNA antibodies or hypocomplementemia).

Serologic abnormality (anti-DNA antibodies or hypocomplementemia).

Genetic testing for monogenic lupus was not routinely available; therefore, such cases could not be systematically excluded and this limitation was acknowledged.

Data collection

Medical records were reviewed using a standardized electronic data collection form. Extracted variables included demographic characteristics, disease duration, clinical manifestations, renal histopathology, laboratory parameters, medication exposure, adjunctive therapies, infections (including tuberculosis), and outcomes.

Treatment protocols

Patients were grouped according to induction regimen:

Cyclophosphamide (CYC): NIH high-dose intravenous protocol (0.5–1.0 g/m² monthly for 6 months), followed by quarterly maintenance pulses for ≥ 12 months.

Cyclophosphamide (CYC): NIH high-dose intravenous protocol (0.5–1.0 g/m² monthly for 6 months), followed by quarterly maintenance pulses for ≥ 12 months.

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF): 600–1,200 mg/m²/day in divided doses.

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF): 600–1,200 mg/m²/day in divided doses.

Adjunctive therapies, including methylprednisolone pulse therapy (MPPT) and biologic agents such as rituximab, were administered at the discretion of the treating physician for severe or refractory disease.

Definition of treatment switching

Treatment switching was defined as any modification of the induction regimen, including:

transition between CYC and MMF.

transition between CYC and MMF.

escalation to biologic therapy (e.g., rituximab).

escalation to biologic therapy (e.g., rituximab).

addition or substitution of another immunosuppressant.

addition or substitution of another immunosuppressant.

Switching decisions were based on inadequate clinical response, persistent or recurrent disease activity, adverse effects, infection, or physician judgment.

Outcome definitions

Complete renal response (CRR):

proteinuria < 0.5 g/day (UPCR < 500 mg/g).

proteinuria < 0.5 g/day (UPCR < 500 mg/g).

stable or improved kidney function (serum creatinine within ± 10–15% of baseline).

stable or improved kidney function (serum creatinine within ± 10–15% of baseline).

Partial renal response (PRR):

≥ 50% reduction in proteinuria to subnephrotic range.

≥ 50% reduction in proteinuria to subnephrotic range.

stable renal function.

stable renal function.

No response: failure to achieve CRR or PRR within 6–12 months.

Proteinuria was assessed using UPCR due to availability in the retrospective dataset. Body surface area–adjusted pediatric thresholds were not consistently available and represent a limitation.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using STATA MP version 18. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage.

Group comparisons used chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Time-to-event outcomes (time-to-CRR) were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank testing. Cox proportional hazards regression identified predictors of renal response. Logistic regression models evaluated predictors of treatment switching.

Hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. A two-sided p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the hospital (Protocol No. REC-2024-10-142-TF-FR-AP). Given the retrospective design and use of de-identified medical records, the requirement for written informed consent from participants and/or their parents or legal guardians was waived by the ethics committee. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. The researcher did not receive any monetary or other forms of compensation for the study. No potential conflicts of interest were identified.

Time-to-complete renal response

Time-to-event analyses evaluated time-to-complete renal response using Kaplan–Meier methods (Fig. 1). Median time-to-CRR was 13 months for the CYC group and 24 months for the MMF group. The difference was not statistically significant (log-rank χ² = 0.62, p = 0.431).

Kaplan-Meier survival curves of time to complete renal response

Kaplan–Meier estimates indicated an approximate probability of CRR at 6 months of 80% for CYC and 70% for MMF.

Patients who underwent treatment switching demonstrated a lower CRR incidence rate (2.68 per 100 person-months; 95% CI 2.06–3.43) and longer median time-to-response (24 months) compared with those who remained on their initial regimen (8.54 per 100 person-months; 95% CI 7.08–10.22; median 9 months). Time-to-response was significantly delayed among patients who switched (log-rank χ² = 86.48, p = 0.001).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of Filipino children with childhood-onset lupus nephritis, cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate mofetil demonstrated comparable renal response rates. However, treatment switching was common and reflected real-world clinical decision-making rather than protocolized escalation.

Patients treated with CYC had more severe disease at baseline, including higher proteinuria and a greater prevalence of Class IV nephritis, suggesting preferential use of CYC in aggressive presentations. These baseline differences should be considered when interpreting comparative outcomes. This mirrors previous pediatric LN cohorts, in which more aggressive cases often prompt clinicians to favor CYC. For example, a French retrospective study of 33 children with Class IV LN found similar efficacy between MMF and CYC induction but used CYC preferentially in more clinically aggressive cases [9]. Despite this imbalance, both induction strategies achieved similar remission rates and time-to-response. Kaplan–Meier analyses showed numerically earlier responses with CYC, but differences were not statistically significant based on log-rank testing, indicating no clear superiority of one regimen.

Our findings echo those from larger, multi-center pediatric cohorts. In an international cohort of 382 children with LN, 57% achieved complete remission by 24 months and 34% partial remission, with no significant difference in remission rates between induction with CYC versus MMF [10]. This supports that the choice of induction agent alone may not determine long-term remission stability and highlights the importance of close monitoring and timely therapy adjustment.

Treatment switching was common in our cohort, particularly among MMF-treated patients. This likely reflects early inadequate response despite MMF’s favorable safety profile, prompting clinicians to escalate or modify therapy. Importantly, switching was not limited to transitions between CYC and MMF. Biologic therapy was also employed in refractory cases, with rituximab administered to 30 patients in the CYC group and 5 patients in the MMF group as adjunct or rescue treatment. These findings reflect real-world practice in which biologics are considered for persistent or severe disease rather than reliance on conventional immunosuppressive agents alone. The phenomenon of early switching is supported by pediatric-onset LN studies showing that failure to reach early remission targets predicts relapse and deterioration [6, 11]. In fact, longer-term data from a Chinese cohort of 92 childhood-onset LN patients demonstrated that non-remission at 6 or 12 months was associated with worse renal outcomes, including chronic kidney disease and relapse [6].

Interestingly, we observed that female sex was significantly associated with treatment switching. While most pediatric and adult studies focus on sex as a risk factor for disease severity or renal survival [12], our finding suggests that treatment retention may also differ by sex; hormonal, immunologic, or pharmacokinetic mechanisms may underlie this difference, but further research is needed to elucidate these factors.

Adjunctive therapies influenced outcomes. Higher corticosteroid dosing and rituximab use were associated with lower switching rates, although these findings may reflect confounding by indication. Tuberculosis significantly increased the likelihood of switching, underscoring infection-related challenges in high-burden settings.

Children who received MPPT at induction were less likely to require a regimen switch, suggesting that early aggressive corticosteroid intervention can enhance disease control and limit the need for later modifications. This aligns with pediatric LN cohorts where early reduction of proteinuria and disease activity predicts more sustained remission [6]. Moreover, RTX use in our cohort appeared to reduce the likelihood of switching among CYC-treated patients, supporting prior observational data that biologics may serve as effective salvage or adjunctive therapies in resistant LN, although randomized pediatric data remain limited.

Infection risk posed a substantial challenge across both treatment arms. Notably, the presence of tuberculosis significantly increased the likelihood of changing therapy. This finding is especially relevant in high TB-burden regions, underscoring the need for vigilant infection screening and tailored immunosuppressive management. Broadly, the overall infection rates we observed are consistent with those reported in pediatric LN cohorts, which often document a high burden of infection during induction therapy [13].

Beyond CYC and MMF, newer strategies are emerging in pediatric LN. Multi-target therapy combining glucocorticoids, MMF, and calcineurin inhibitors (such as tacrolimus) has shown promising efficacy. In one retrospective pediatric study of 36 children who failed conventional therapy, over 90% achieved partial or complete remission within 6 months with a favorable safety profile [14]. Such regimens may offer viable alternatives for children who do not tolerate or respond adequately to first-line agents.

Overall, our study underscores that treatment switching is not merely a reflection of drug failure but a pragmatic and necessary component of pediatric LN management. In real-world practice, early non-response, baseline disease severity, comorbid infection, and adjunctive or biologic therapies all contribute to treatment modification decisions. Recognizing these patterns can inform more proactive and individualized treatment algorithms rather than a fixed “one-size-fits-all” induction strategy.

Limitations include the retrospective single-center design, non-random treatment allocation, baseline severity imbalance, treatment heterogeneity including biologics and pulse steroids, absence of standardized adverse-event reporting, reliance on adult-based proteinuria thresholds rather than body surface area–adjusted pediatric definitions, lack of biopsy activity and chronicity indices, and inability to evaluate monogenic lupus due to resource constraints. These factors may limit causal inference.

Conclusion

CYC and MMF are both effective induction therapies for pediatric lupus nephritis. Early non-response frequently necessitates treatment modification, including switching between conventional agents or the addition of biologic therapies. Factors influencing switching included baseline disease severity, adjunctive steroid use, infection risk, and patient characteristics, underscoring the multifactorial nature of treatment decisions in real-world pediatric LN management. Multi-center prospective studies are needed to guide optimal, evidence-based strategies in this population.
