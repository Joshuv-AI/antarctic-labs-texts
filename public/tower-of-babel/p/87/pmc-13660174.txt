Descriptive comparison of comprehensive HIV knowledge and condom non-use at last sexual intercourse among unmarried adolescent girls and young women in Nigeria
Authors: Michael O. Tomori, Hassan Ogunwemimo, Sunday A. Adedini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAcross sub-Saharan Africa, and Nigeria in particular, young women and adolescent girls aged 15 to 24 continue to face a disproportionate risk of HIV — and a large part of that risk comes down to unprotected sex. Knowing about HIV is widely assumed to lead to safer behaviour, yet the evidence that knowledge actually translates into condom use is far from straightforward. This study takes a closer look at that relationship, comparing comprehensive HIV knowledge and condom non-use among unmarried adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in Nigeria across three points in time.MethodsWe drew on data from the Nigeria Demographic and Health Surveys conducted in 2008, 2013, and 2018. Condom non-use at last sexual intercourse — a standard DHS indicator — served as our measure of risky sexual behaviour. The analysis focused on sexually active unmarried women in two age groups: adolescent girls aged 15 to 19, and young women aged 20 to 24. We excluded participants with missing or unclear responses for the outcome variable or key covariates. Weighted descriptive statistics and survey-weighted binary logistic regression models were run separately for each age group and survey year, with all analyses accounting for the clustered, stratified sampling design of the NDHS. Stata 16.1 was used throughout.ResultsHIV knowledge improved over the study period, particularly among young women in the older age group, while trends in condom use followed a less predictable pattern. Among adolescent girls, comprehensive HIV knowledge was not meaningfully linked to condom use in 2008, but that association became statistically significant by 2013 and held through 2018. Importantly, though, condom non-use actually rose in 2018 even as knowledge levels reached their highest point — a clear sign that knowing is not the same as doing. Socioeconomic status and region of residence were both significantly associated with condom use.ConclusionComprehensive HIV knowledge among unmarried AGYW in Nigeria has grown, but it has not reliably translated into condom use. This gap between what young women know and what they are able to do points to barriers that go well beyond information — structural, social, and economic forces that shape the choices available to them. Closing that gap will require interventions that take those realities seriously.

Introduction

Risky sexual behaviour is a recognised public health concern for young people in every part of the world. In Europe and North America, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has documented its prevalence among high school students, noting links to unprotected sex, drug use during sexual encounters, and elevated risk of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections [1]. In Asia, roughly one in five girls and one in eight boys aged 15 to 19 report having multiple sexual partners — often without protection [2]. The picture in sub-Saharan Africa is equally concerning: around 22% of adolescent girls and 15% of boys in that age group have multiple partners, frequently without condom use [2]. These are not abstract statistics — they represent young lives placed at unnecessary risk, and they make a compelling case for coordinated action.

In Nigeria, the burden falls most heavily on adolescent girls and young women, who account for a disproportionate share of new HIV infections — a pattern repeated across sub-Saharan Africa [3, 4]. Several factors help explain this vulnerability: early sexual debut, multiple concurrent partners, and inconsistent condom use all contribute to elevated risk [5, 6]. More recent research has also highlighted the role of sociocultural pressures and low self-esteem in shaping HIV risk among young Nigerian women [7].

Improving HIV knowledge is a natural starting point for addressing these risks. When young people understand how HIV is transmitted and can separate fact from persistent myth, they are better positioned to protect themselves [8]. Yet the evidence reveals a troubling gap: fewer than 40% of young people aged 15 to 24 in sub-Saharan Africa have comprehensive HIV knowledge, and Nigeria is no exception [9]. That gap matters — because limited knowledge has been consistently linked to higher rates of infection [10]. Research also shows that age, education, household socioeconomic status, the sex of the household head, and access to media all shape how much young people know about HIV [8, 11]. Targeted awareness campaigns aimed specifically at AGYW have shown promise in improving both HIV knowledge and sexual health outcomes.

And yet, knowledge alone has not been enough. Women and girls made up 44% of new HIV infections globally in 2023, and that figure climbed to nearly 62% in sub-Saharan Africa — a stark reminder that biological vulnerability, combined with deep-rooted social and structural inequalities, continues to place young women at the sharp end of the epidemic [4]. In many settings, societal norms and gender dynamics actively undermine young women’s ability to negotiate condom use or insist on safer sex — meaning that even those with good HIV knowledge may find themselves without the power to act on it [12, 13].

Despite all of this, there is remarkably little research on the specific relationship between HIV knowledge and sexual risk behaviour among unmarried AGYW in Nigeria. This is a significant gap, given how distinctly this group is positioned — shaped by particular vulnerabilities, navigating particular social pressures, and experiencing particular consequences.

In this study, we use condom non-use at last sexual intercourse as our working measure of risky sexual behaviour, consistent with its application in the Demographic and Health Surveys. We recognise that risky sexual behaviour can be measured in other ways, and our findings should be understood within this specific operational definition.

Our aim is to shed light on how HIV knowledge relates to sexual risk behaviour in this underserved group — and in doing so, to offer evidence that can help shape better policies, more effective programmes, and more honest conversations about what it will actually take to protect the health and wellbeing of young women in Nigeria.

Data and methods

Study designs and participants

This study draws on repeated cross-sectional data from the Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS), using three survey rounds conducted in 2008, 2013, and 2018. The NDHS is a nationally representative survey that captures a wide range of population, health, and socioeconomic indicators — from fertility and maternal health to contraceptive use and HIV-related knowledge and behaviour. Surveys follow a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design and collect data from women, men, children, and households. For this study, we worked with the individual women’s recode (IR) files.

We restricted our analytic sample to sexually active unmarried adolescent girls aged 15 to 19 and young women aged 20 to 24. A complete-case approach was used, meaning we excluded anyone with missing or “don’t know” responses for the condom use outcome, as well as anyone with missing data on any of the covariates included in the multivariable models. Across the three survey waves, these exclusions removed 5,829 observations — just over half (50.1%) of those initially eligible — leaving a final weighted analytic sample of 5,808 respondents.

Of these, 2,429 were adolescent girls and 3,379 were young women. Breaking this down by survey year: 2,048 respondents were from the 2008 wave (833 adolescent girls and 1,215 young women), 1,932 from 2013 (836 adolescent girls and 1,096 young women), and 1,828 from 2018 (760 adolescent girls and 1,068 young women). Measures

Outcome variables

The outcome variable in our study was condom non-use as at last sexual intercourse. Respondents were asked whether they used a condom during last sex with most recent partner, with response options of “yes”, “no”, or “don’t know”. Responses were coded as 1 for condom non-use (“no”) and 0 for use (“yes”). Respondents who selected “don’t know” or had missing responses were excluded from the analysis.

Key independent variables

Comprehensive HIV knowledge was measured using five DHS-standard items assessing correct knowledge of HIV prevention and rejection of common misconceptions. Respondents were asked three questions on HIV prevention and transmission and two questions addressing common misconceptions about HIV transmission. Specifically, these items assessed whether respondents knew that: (1) consistent condom use during sexual intercourse can reduce the risk of HIV infection; (2) having one uninfected, faithful partner can reduce the chance of contracting HIV; (3) a healthy-looking person can have HIV; (4) HIV cannot be transmitted through mosquito bites; and (5) HIV cannot be transmitted by sharing food with a person living with HIV. Responses were recorded as “yes,” “no,” or “don’t know.” Respondents who answered all five items correctly were coded as having comprehensive HIV knowledge (1), while those who answered any or all items incorrectly or responded “don’t know” were coded as not having comprehensive HIV knowledge (0).

Covariates

Covariates in our study included education, religion, employment status, region of residence, household wealth, place of residence, ethnicity, age at sexual debut, and media exposure. These variables were selected based on prior literature and represent key demographic and socioeconomic characteristics relevant to sexual behaviour. Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Respondents who responded “don’t know” or whose response was missing in the outcome variable were removed from the analysis. Furthermore, respondents who were married, above 24 years old and inactive sexually were excluded from the study sample. “Sexually active” was defined as having a sexual relationship in the past 12 months.

Statistical analysis

Binary logistic regression models were used to examine the association between comprehensive HIV knowledge and condom non-use among unmarried adolescent girls and young women. We restricted our analysis to complete cases. Crude odds ratios (COR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated separately for each survey wave and age group.

Analysis proceeded in three stages. First, descriptive statistics were generated to summarize respondent characteristics, the outcome variable, and the key explanatory variable across survey years and age groups. Descriptive results are presented as weighted percentages and sample sizes. Second, univariable logistic regression models estimated crude associations between comprehensive HIV knowledge and condom use across survey waves. Third, multivariable logistic regression models included relevant sociodemographic covariates to estimate adjusted associations and assess whether the relationship between HIV knowledge and condom use persisted after controlling for potential confounders.

All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design of the Demographic and Health Surveys. Sampling weights were applied to adjust for unequal probabilities of selection and non-response. Survey design parameters were specified using the DHS primary sampling unit variable (v021), stratification variable (v022), and sampling weight variable (v005). Analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.1 with the survey (svy) command suite to obtain design-corrected standard errors and confidence intervals. Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level.

Results

All results presented below reflect observed associations and temporal patterns and do not imply causal relationships or programme effects.

The proportions of non-users of condom initially decreased but later increased over time in both the 15–19 years and the 20–24 years age groups. Among adolescents (15–19 years) who dropped to 62% in 2013 from 71% in 2008 to 65% in 2018, condom non-use decreased to 51% in 2013 from 59% in 2008 but later increased to 60% in 2018 among young women (20–24 years). Among young women aged 20 to 24, knowledge levels held fairly steady in the early years — 41.2% in 2008 and 42.5% in 2013 — before climbing noticeably to 56% by 2018. The sharpest gain, in other words, came in the most recent period, suggesting that whatever combination of education, outreach, and awareness-building was happening between 2013 and 2018 was beginning to reach this group in a more meaningful way.

Among adolescent girls aged 15 to 19, the picture was one of steady, if slower, progress. Comprehensive HIV knowledge rose from 34% in 2008 to 36% in 2013, then to 46% in 2018 — a gain of 12% points over the full ten-year period. Encouragingly, the pace of improvement also picked up in the later period, mirroring the trend seen in older women.

Taken together, these figures tell a story of genuine progress. More young women and adolescent girls in Nigeria are entering adulthood with a solid understanding of HIV than was the case a decade ago. Yet even by 2018, fewer than six in ten young women and fewer than half of adolescent girls had reached the threshold of comprehensive knowledge — a reminder of how much ground remains to be covered.

Trends in HIV knowledge and condom use

Looking at the trends across 2008, 2013, and 2018, one pattern stands out immediately: HIV knowledge and condom use were moving in very different directions by the end of the study period (Fig. 1). Comprehensive HIV knowledge rose steadily — from 37.4% in 2008 to 39.2% in 2013, and then more sharply to 50.9% in 2018. Condom use, however, told a more complicated story. It climbed encouragingly from 34.9% in 2008 to 43.2% in 2013, but then fell back to 37.6% in 2018. The fact that condom use declined in the very year that HIV knowledge reached its highest point is striking, and it points to something important: knowing about HIV, on its own, is clearly not enough to change behaviour.

Education was relatively high across both age groups throughout the study period. The large majority of participants in both the 15–19 and 20–24 age groups had completed at least secondary education, though older women were consistently more likely to have done so — ranging from 90.1% to 91.8% among 20–24-year-olds, compared to 83.9% to 87.0% among adolescent girls.

Religion followed a similar pattern. Christianity was the dominant faith in both groups, with higher proportions among older women. Islam and other religions together accounted for between 14.8% and 22.9% depending on the group and survey year.

Employment changed notably over time. Among young women aged 20 to 24, the proportion currently working rose from 45.1% in 2008 to 64.7% in 2018 — a substantial shift that likely reflects both economic change and shifting norms around women’s participation in the workforce.

Regional distribution shifted as well. The South-South dominated in 2008, accounting for 45.1% of adolescent girls in that year. By 2018, the picture had become more evenly spread, with the North Central region gaining ground and accounting for 20.4% of the 15–19 age group.

Household wealth showed a clear gradient between age groups. Wealthier households were better represented among 20–24-year-olds, with the richest quintile making up between 35.1% and 38.8% of that group across survey years. Among adolescent girls, the richest quintile accounted for a notably smaller share, ranging from 22.0% to 28.9%.

Urban residence increased over time, particularly among young women in the older age group, rising from 53.6% in 2008 to 58.9% in 2018 — a gradual but steady shift toward city living.

In Ethnicity, Hausa and other ethnic groups together made up the largest share of participants across all three survey years.

Age at sexual debut differed sharply between the two age groups, as would be expected. Among adolescent girls aged 15 to 19, between 85.1% and 87.3% had their sexual debut before the age of 18. Among 20–24-year-olds, the proportion was considerably lower — ranging from 31.7% to 42.2% — reflecting the simple fact that many in this group had not yet reached 18 during the earlier survey years.

Media exposure was high across the board, and consistently higher among the older age group. One concerning trend, however, was a decline in media exposure among adolescent girls — falling from 87.4% in 2008 to 77.9% in 2018 — at a time when access to health information is arguably more important than ever.

The headline finding is fairly consistent across most groups and years: young women who had comprehensive HIV knowledge were meaningfully more likely to have used a condom at last sex than those who did not. But the strength of that relationship shifted over time, and by 2018 it was beginning to weaken in the older age group.

In 2008, the association was clear in both age groups. Adolescent girls aged 15 to 19 with comprehensive HIV knowledge had 36% lower odds of condom non-use compared to their peers with no or partial knowledge (COR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.46–0.89). Among young women aged 20 to 24, the gap was even wider — a 41% reduction in the odds of condom non-use (COR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.45–0.77).

By 2013, a similar pattern was held. Adolescent girls with full HIV knowledge were 43% less likely to have foregone condom use (COR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.42–0.78), and young women in the 20–24 age group showed a 31% reduction in those odds (COR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.53–0.91). The association remained statistically significant for both groups.

2018 is where the picture becomes more nuanced. Among adolescent girls, the link between HIV knowledge and condom use held firm — those with comprehensive knowledge still had 43% lower odds of condom non-use (COR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.39–0.85). Among young women aged 20 to 24, however, the association was no longer statistically significant, with a borderline result that just crossed the threshold of uncertainty (COR = 0.75; 95% CI: 0.55–1.01). In practical terms, this means that by 2018, having comprehensive HIV knowledge was no longer reliably associated with condom use among the older age group — a shift that, read alongside the wider trend of rising knowledge but declining condom use, reinforces the case that something beyond information was driving behaviour in that period.

HIV knowledge held up as a meaningful predictor of condom use in most — but not all — groups and years, even after adjustment. Among adolescent girls aged 15 to 19, the relationship was not quite statistically significant in 2008 (AOR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.48–1.00), sitting right at the boundary. It became clearly significant in 2013 (AOR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.37–0.77) and remained so in 2018 (AOR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.41–0.92). For young women aged 20 to 24, the pattern was reversed: the association was significant in both 2008 (AOR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.43–0.76) and 2013 (AOR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.52–0.90), but by 2018 it had faded to non-significance (AOR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.59–1.13). In other words, among older women, comprehensive HIV knowledge was no longer a reliable predictor of condom use by the most recent survey wave — a finding that adds another layer to the knowledge-behaviour gap story.

Education has a protective effect at certain points in time. Having secondary or higher education was associated with lower odds of condom non-use among 20–24-year-olds in 2008 (AOR = 0.40; 95% CI: 0.20–0.79) and in both age groups in 2013, but this effect had disappeared by 2018. The fading influence of education over time is worth noting, and may reflect broader shifts in the social and economic environment in which young women are making decisions about sex.

Religion emerged as a consistent factor among older women. Those identifying as Muslim or belonging to other non-Christian faiths had significantly higher odds of condom non-use among 20–24-year-olds in both 2008 (AOR = 1.80; 95% CI: 1.15–2.82) and 2018 (AOR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.18–3.02). The consistency of this finding across a decade suggests that religious and cultural norms around sexual behaviour and contraception continue to shape condom use in this group.

Household wealth was one of the most stable predictors across the study period. Wealthier young women aged 20 to 24 were consistently less likely to report condom non-use, with the effect strongest in the richest quintile — in 2008 (AOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.11–0.75) and again in 2018 (AOR = 0.33; 95% CI: 0.15–0.74). This pattern speaks to the structural dimension of sexual risk: where a woman stands economically has a direct bearing on her ability to negotiate and access protection.

Place of residence mattered early in the study period but less so later. In 2008, living in a rural area was associated with significantly higher odds of condom non-use for both adolescent girls (AOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.21–3.03) and young women (AOR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.03–2.19). By 2018, however, this urban-rural divide had narrowed to the point of non-significance — possibly reflecting the spread of health services and information into more rural settings over that decade, though the reasons deserve further exploration.

Region of residence grew more important over time. By 2013, young women aged 20 to 24 in the South-South had nearly twice the odds of condom non-use compared to those in the North-Central region (AOR = 1.90; 95% CI: 1.18–3.08). By 2018, this regional disparity had widened and extended to adolescent girls as well — with South-South adolescents showing more than twice the odds of condom non-use (AOR = 2.62; 95% CI: 1.51–4.53) and young women in the same region showing 76% higher odds (AOR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.13–2.75). These growing regional gaps suggest that the factors shaping condom use are not playing out evenly across Nigeria, and that geography matters in ways that deserve targeted attention.

Age at sexual debut and media exposure, despite their intuitive relevance, did not emerge as statistically significant predictors of condom non-use in the adjusted models. This does not necessarily mean they are unimportant in young women’s lives, but it does suggest that once other sociodemographic factors are accounted for, their independent effect on this particular outcome becomes difficult to detect.

Discussion

This study set out to track how comprehensive HIV knowledge and condom use have moved together — or failed to — among unmarried adolescent girls and young women in Nigeria over a decade of nationally representative survey data. What emerged was a relationship that is real but inconsistent, and that challenges the assumption that better awareness reliably leads to safer behaviour [14, 15].

One of the most telling findings concerns adolescent girls aged 15 to 19. In 2008, comprehensive HIV knowledge showed no statistically significant connection to condom use in this group. By 2013 and 2018, that connection had become meaningful. This is not simply a story of knowledge catching up with behaviour — it suggests that the relationship between what young women know and what they do may take time to develop, and may depend on the right conditions being in place, such as greater access to youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services [16]. Knowledge, in other words, may need somewhere to land.

But 2018 also brought a striking contradiction. That year saw the highest levels of comprehensive HIV knowledge recorded across both age groups — 45.9% among adolescents and 65.0% among young women — and yet condom non-use rose at the same time. This divergence, illustrated clearly in Fig. 1, is not entirely surprising given what the broader literature tells us: information alone struggles to dislodge entrenched social norms, overcome stigma, or compensate for the limited negotiating power that many young women face in their sexual relationships [17, 18]. The gap between knowing and doing is not a failure of education — it is a reflection of the structural and social forces that education cannot reach on its own.

Evidence from intervention studies in Nigeria supports this reading. Research by Arije and colleagues [19] suggests that HIV knowledge translates more consistently into protective behaviour when it is embedded within comprehensive programmes, rather than delivered in isolation. The stronger knowledge-behaviour associations seen in the later survey years may therefore reflect cumulative gains from years of programmatic investment, rather than the effect of knowledge alone.

Socioeconomic status emerged as one of the most consistent predictors of condom use throughout the study. Wealthier young women aged 20 to 24 were repeatedly less likely to report condom non-use, a pattern that aligns with prior evidence showing that financial independence and higher social standing give women more power to insist on condom use, regardless of a partner’s preferences [20]. At the other end of the spectrum, women of Islamic faith showed significantly higher odds of condom non-use in certain survey years — a finding that points to the continued influence of religious and cultural frameworks on sexual health decision-making, consistent with previous research in the Nigerian context [21].

The geographic picture shifted considerably over the decade. In 2008, living in a rural area was a significant risk factor for condom non-use. By 2018, that association had largely dissolved — possibly reflecting the gradual expansion of health services and information into more rural communities. In its place, a different geographic story emerged: by 2018, young women in the South-South region were significantly more likely to report condom non-use than those in the North-Central region, and this disparity affected both adolescent girls and older young women. These growing regional inequalities mirror broader national patterns in HIV knowledge and behaviour [22, 23], and they suggest that geography continues to shape young women’s sexual health in ways that national-level programmes alone are unlikely to address.

Conclusions

The central message of this study is both clear and uncomfortable: improving what young women know about HIV has not been enough, on its own, to change what they do. Across all three survey waves, the gap between rising knowledge levels and persistent condom non-use tells a story about the limits of information as a driver of behaviour change. HIV knowledge matters — it remains a meaningful factor in predicting protective sexual behaviour — but it is not sufficient. Socioeconomic status, cultural norms, relationship power dynamics, and where a woman happens to live all shape her ability to act on what she knows.

What this means for policy and practice is straightforward, even if implementing it is not. HIV prevention programmes need to go beyond knowledge transmission and engage seriously with the structural and social conditions that govern young women’s choices. That means investing in youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services that young women can actually reach and trust. It means tackling the gender and power inequalities that leave many young women unable to negotiate condom use even when they know its importance. And it means addressing the socioeconomic barriers — poverty, dependence, limited options — that quietly but consistently undermine protective behaviour. Knowledge is a necessary starting point, but it is not, and has never been, the finishing line.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that are worth stating clearly. Because the DHS uses a cross-sectional design, we cannot establish causation — exposure and outcome were measured at the same point in time. Condom use and sexual behaviour were both self-reported, which means the data may be affected by recall errors or by participants responding in ways they felt were socially expected. Risky sexual behaviour was measured solely as condom non-use at last sex, which leaves out other important dimensions of sexual risk such as having concurrent partners or engaging in transactional sex. The decision to use complete-case analysis — excluding those with missing data — may have introduced some selection bias if the excluded respondents were systematically different from those who remained. Finally, because the analysis focused on sexually active unmarried young women, the findings cannot be straightforwardly applied to married adolescents or those who were not sexually active during the study period.
