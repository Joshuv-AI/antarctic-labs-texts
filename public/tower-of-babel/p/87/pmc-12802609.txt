Renal artery stenosis (RAS), commonly caused by atherosclerosis or fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), is a well-recognized etiology of secondary hypertension. Its treatment via percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty, with or without stenting, is generally considered safe, with a low risk of serious complications and a mortality rate of <0.2%.1

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a rare, potentially reversible clinical and radiological entity characterized by a constellation of symptoms including visual disturbances, altered mental status, seizures, and headache. Radiographically, PRES typically involves vasogenic edema in the parietal and occipital lobes.2,3 Although its pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, cerebral autoregulatory dysfunction, endothelial injury, and blood-brain barrier disruption are considered central mechanisms.4,5

We present a unique case of transient cortical blindness secondary to PRES after successful renal artery stenting in a middle-aged patient with treatment-resistant hypertension.

History of Presentation

A 41-year-old man presented for evaluation of refractory hypertension. Despite taking 4 antihypertensive medications, his blood pressure remained uncontrolled. He had no headache, chest pain, visual symptoms, or neurologic deficits at presentation. On physical examination, the patient was alert, oriented, and neurologically intact. His blood pressure was elevated at 168/102 mm Hg.

Past Medical History

The patient had no known history of diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or cardiovascular disease. His only relevant history was longstanding hypertension unresponsive to multiple antihypertensives, including a diuretic, beta-blocker, calcium channel blocker, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for treatment-resistant hypertension in this patient included RAS (atherosclerotic or FMD-related), primary hyperaldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, coarctation of the aorta, and noncompliance with medication. Given his age and resistance to therapy, renovascular hypertension due to RAS was highly suspected.

Investigations

Doppler ultrasound and magnetic resonance angiography revealed >90% stenosis of the left renal artery. Laboratory tests showed normal serum creatinine, electrolytes, and cortisol levels. Aldosterone/renin ratio was unremarkable. Electrocardiogram and echocardiogram showed left ventricular hypertrophy, consistent with chronic hypertension.

Management

The patient underwent elective renal artery stenting via left radial artery access, which was chosen given the cranial position of the left renal artery. A 6-F Judkins-right guiding catheter was used. A 0.014-inch wire, with a buddy wire for support, crossed the lesion, which was treated with a 6.0 × 18 mm balloon-expandable stent. Postdilation was performed using a 7.0 × 15 mm balloon with an ostial flare technique. The immediate angiographic results were excellent, with no complications (Figures 1 and 2).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Approximately 30 minutes postprocedure, the patient developed complete bilateral blindness while remaining alert. His vital signs were stable, with no hypertensive crisis.

Brain computed tomography and initial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no infarct or hemorrhage. Ophthalmologic examination showed normal ocular structures, suggesting a central cause. Over the next few hours, the patient developed altered sensorium (Glasgow Coma Scale: 12). Repeat MRI (diffusion-weighted imaging and FLAIR [fluid-attenuated inversion recovery]) showed bilateral occipital and parietal subcortical hyperintensities with apparent diffusion coefficient hypointensities—features consistent with cytotoxic edema and PRES (Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5). Magnetic resonance angiography and venography excluded other vascular events.

On day 3, transient gaze deviation occurred. Electroencephalogram showed diffuse slowing (theta waves: 5-6 Hz) without epileptiform discharges. The patient was treated with intravenous levetiracetam. By day 9, the patient's vision began improving, with full restoration of vision and mental status by day 10. Follow-up MRI showed complete resolution of prior abnormalities.

At the 4-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring showed an average blood pressure of 124/73 mm Hg without any antihypertensives.

Discussion

PRES is a clinical-radiological syndrome often associated with hypertensive emergencies, renal dysfunction, and cytotoxic therapies. It is exceedingly rare after endovascular renal interventions. This case illustrates a rare complication after renal artery stenting of PRES with transient cortical blindness in a patient with uncontrolled hypertension.

PRES predominantly affects the posterior cerebral regions, possibly because of lower sympathetic innervation, which impairs autoregulatory capacity during hypertensive states.5,6 Our patient developed PRES after renal artery angioplasty—a procedure not previously linked to this complication in the literature. The presumed mechanism is acute cerebral autoregulatory dysfunction triggered by hemodynamic shifts poststenting in a hypertensive patient.

The pathophysiology of PRES remains incompletely understood, but several mechanisms have been proposed. The vasogenic theory posits that severe hypertension overwhelms cerebral autoregulation, leading to hyperperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, and breakdown of the blood-brain barrier, resulting in vasogenic edema.3 The cytotoxic theory suggests that direct injury to the cerebral vascular endothelium from endogenous or exogenous toxins (eg, cytotoxic drugs and dyes) leads to cerebral edema, independent of blood pressure changes.7 The neuropeptide/vasoconstriction theory proposes that circulating vasoactive substances such as endothelin-1 and thromboxane A2 trigger widespread cerebral vasoconstriction, ischemia, and subsequent vasogenic edema.4 This theory is supported by the frequent observation of vasospasm and reduced cerebral perfusion in patients with PRES.

In the present case, the sudden hemodynamic shift after renal angioplasty in a patient with longstanding uncontrolled hypertension may have precipitated cerebral autoregulatory failure, leading to PRES. Despite normal immediate postprocedural hemodynamics and no metabolic abnormalities, the development of cortical blindness and subsequent encephalopathy, along with imaging findings, were diagnostic of PRES.

Although current hypertension and renal artery stenting guidelines (eg, American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology) do not list PRES as a recognized complication, our case suggests that careful hemodynamic monitoring is warranted after stenting, especially in patients with longstanding uncontrolled hypertension.

Management of PRES is largely supportive. There is no specific pharmacological treatment. The primary approach involves addressing the underlying trigger, which is often hypertension or exposure to a neurotoxic agent. In hypertensive PRES, a cautious reduction in blood pressure is advised. Rapid or excessive reduction can lead to cerebral hypoperfusion and ischemia. The target is generally a 20% to 25% reduction from baseline over the first 6 hours, with a goal blood pressure of 130-150/80-100 mm Hg.8 The use of corticosteroids has been postulated given their antiedematous effects, but there is no conclusive evidence supporting their efficacy in PRES. In fact, corticosteroids have been implicated as a potential precipitating factor in some cases.9

This case underscores the importance of recognizing PRES as a potential, though rare, complication after renal angioplasty. Prompt neuroimaging, supportive care, and blood pressure management remain the cornerstone of treatment. Although PRES usually resolves within days, our case was notable for prolonged symptoms lasting 10 days, indicating that complete recovery is possible even with delayed resolution, thus avoiding unnecessary interventions.

To date, only a few cases have documented PRES after renal artery intervention, particularly in the setting of fibromuscular dysplasia or uncontrolled renovascular hypertension. In our patient, although no hypertensive spike was recorded, a relative shift in cerebral perfusion pressure postangioplasty could have induced the syndrome even in the absence of absolute hypertensive crisis.

Importantly, this case highlights a rare but serious complication of renal angioplasty that should be considered when neurological symptoms occur postprocedure. Early neuroimaging, particularly MRI with FLAIR and diffusion-weighted imaging sequences, is key to prompt diagnosis and management.

Conclusions

PRES is a rare but important differential diagnosis in patients presenting with sudden visual loss and encephalopathy after renal angioplasty—a procedure generally considered minimally invasive and safe. This case highlights the need for clinical vigilance, as transient cortical blindness may be the initial and sole symptom. Notably, radiographic findings may lag behind clinical onset, making early recognition critical. Awareness of this potential complication can help avoid unnecessary alarm and ensure timely, appropriate conservative management.
