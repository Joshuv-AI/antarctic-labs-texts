Glycated nisin enhances nisin's cytotoxic effects on breast cancer cells
Authors: Ali Mohammadi, Nasrin Kazemipour, Gholam Abbas Ghorbankhani, Solmaz Morovati, Mahdi Hashempour Sadeghian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial peptides, such as nisin, are proposed as promising agents for cancer treatment. While glycation has been recognized as an effective method for enhancing various physicochemical properties of nisin, its anticancer effects remain unexplored. Therefore, we aimed to assess the anticancer potential of glycated nisin against MDA-MB-231 cells. The MDA-MB cells were treated with increasing concentrations of nisin and glycated nisin for 24, 48, and 72 h. The IC50 values for nisin were higher than those for glycated nisin. Glycated nisin at concentrations of 20 and 40 µg/mL decreased cell viability more than nisin at the same concentrations. The rate of apoptosis in the group treated with 20 µg/mL of nisin was lower compared to other treatment groups, and no significant difference in apoptosis rates was observed at different time points (p > 0.05). However, in the glycated nisin groups with concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL, the level of apoptosis was very high after 24 h (73–81% of cells undergoing apoptosis). Overall, our study suggests that glycated nisin exhibits stronger cytotoxic effects on MDA-MB-231 cells, primarily involving the induction of apoptosis. This indicates its potential utilization as an alternative approach to address the issue of drug resistance in cancer cells.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide1. Although there have been advancements in treatments for breast cancer, the overall prognosis for individuals with advanced-stage or recurrent types of the disease remains challenging2. Surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, and hormonal therapy are the primary therapeutic approaches for treating breast cancer3. However, these methods have been reported to cause normal cell damage, require long-term administration and yield limited therapeutic outcomes4. Therefore, the development of novel anticancer agents is needed. MDA-MB-231 cells are classified as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), indicating the absence of estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2)5. These cells are known for their aggressive behavior, demonstrated by high proliferation rates, invasion, and metastasis potential. This makes them a model for studying metastatic processes and drug resistance6.

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), such as nisin, are suggested as promising agents for cancer treatment7,8. Due to their cationic and amphipathic characteristics, may attach to cancer cells and form pores in the cell membrane, resulting in the cytotoxicity of these cells9,10. Nisin, a polycyclic bacteriocin composed of 34 amino acids, is generated by Lactococcus lactis, a Gram-positive bacterium, in the process of fermentation11. This AMP is the only approved one by the FAO and WHO for human use. It has been utilized in the food industry and there have been no reports of it causing widespread resistance12.

Several studies have verified the cytotoxic effects of nisin through the induction of selective apoptosis in tumor cells including human cervical, hematological, colorectal, skin and hepatocellular cancer cells5,7,13–24. However, the anticancer effect of nisin on certain cancer cells such as human promyelocytic leukemia may not be sufficient25. On the other hand, the synergistic anticancer effect of nisin has been demonstrated when combined with other therapeutic modalities13,26–28. In our previous research, we investigated the effectiveness of nisin A and Newcastle disease virus (NDV) in fighting cancer cells, both individually and in combination. The analysis showed that combining nisin A with NDV was more effective at inhibiting the growth and survival of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells than using NDV or nisin alone26. Kamarajan et al.21 showed that HNSCC cells, which exhibit high resistance to cisplatin and ionizing radiation, can undergo apoptosis when high-purity forms of nisin A and Z are used in combination with cisplatin. Yousefi et al.29 produced a recombinant protein consisting of three bacteriocins: nisin, enterocin, and epidermisin. This protein fusion resulted in the highest level of apoptosis in gastric AGS cells. Preet et al. studied the combined effect of nisin and doxorubicin on skin carcinoma in mice. They found that using nisin together with doxorubicin can significantly enhance the drug's ability to reduce tumor size, potentially making the treatment more effective against cancer. This reduction was greater than what either nisin or doxorubicin could achieve on their own. The researchers hypothesized that nisin could increase the uptake of doxorubicin by cancer cells by disrupting or perforating the cell membrane. This would facilitate the drug's entry into the cells more readily30.

Glycation is an effective method for improving the functional properties of proteins, and several studies have investigated the glycation of various proteins to enhance their functionality31,32. Some functional properties improved by glycation33 include solubility, heat stability34, emulsifying35, foaming36, antioxidant37, textural38 and antibacterial properties39. Although glycation has been identified as a successful technique for improving various physicochemical properties of nisin, its anticancer effects have not yet been investigated.

In our previous study, we found evidence suggesting that nisin A could serve as a novel therapy for breast cancer. Its underlying mechanism involves triggering selective apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cell lines5. In the present study, our aim was to enhance the apoptotic activities of this bacteriocin through glycation and investigate its potential for clinical use.

Trypan blue staining

The trypan blue assay is used to distinguish between viable and non-viable cells in a cell culture. It works based on the premise that viable cells with intact cell membranes will exclude trypan blue dye. In contrast, non-viable or damaged cells will allow the dye to enter, causing them to appear blue when observed under a microscope. Therefore, the trypan blue assay is commonly used to assess cell viability and integrity in biological samples41.

In the nisin group with a concentration of 20 µg/mL, no significant difference in cell viability was observed in the three time intervals studied compared to the control group (p > 0.05). Nisin with a concentration of 40 µg/mL showed a significant difference in cell viability compared to the control group after 48 h of treatment (p ≤ 0.001). However, nisin at a concentration of 80 µg/mL significantly reduced cell viability 24 (p ≤ 0.05), 48 (p ≤ 0.0001), and 72 h (p ≤ 0.0001) after treatment compared to the control group. Glycated nisin at all four concentrations (5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL) caused a significant decrease in cell viability (p ≤ 0.05). Discussion

In this study, we compared the cytotoxic effects of different concentrations of nisin and glycated nisin on MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. Our data clearly indicated that glycated nisin decreased the proliferation of MDA-MB-231 cells more effectively than regular nisin. This was evidenced by the decreased viability of the cells in MTT, trypan blue, and colony assays. These findings were strongly supported by the results of AO/EB staining and flow cytometry, which showed an increased percentage of apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells after incubation with glycated nisin.

It is commonly observed that probiotic microorganisms, such as Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis), are often influence cellular responses against cancerous cells either through direct contact or by releasing substances, including bacteriocins43,44. Nisin as an FDA-approved bacteriocin has been shown to induce selective apoptosis in different types of tumor cells5,7,12–24. It reduces cell proliferation by increasing intracellular calcium levels, triggering of cell cycle arrest, and activation of CHAC1 in cancer cells7,14. Thanjavur et al. found that normal cells retained 74% viability when treated with nisin, whereas HepG2, HeLa, and A549 cancer cells showed significantly higher cytotoxicity, with only about 13% viability remaining after treatment. This cytotoxic effect was evident even at a low concentration of nisin (5 µg/mL)45. According to the study by Hosseini et al., nisin was found to be non-toxic to the mouse fetal fibroblast cell line (NIH3T3) at concentrations up to 800 µg/mL. This suggests that nisin may not be lethal to normal cells at the tested concentration16. Nisin has also been found to cause selective toxicity toward melanoma cells, indicating that it could potentially be used to treat or prevent the spread of melanoma18. Kamarajan et al. have used nisin ZP against HNSCC and HUVEC cell lines21. They proposed that therapeutic dose of nisin for humans (66.7 mg/kg) is higher than what is naturally present in some foods, but it remains within safe limits based on toxicity studies conducted in rodents and regulatory standards set by the FDA. Moreover, cancerous tissues exhibit heightened glucose consumption and accelerated aerobic glycolysis compared to normal tissues46. Targeting glycolytic enzymes and glucose transporters which are overexpressed in cancer cells has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer therapy47. One such strategy involves glycoconjugation, where drugs are bound to glucose or other sugars. This approach has gained considerable attention in recent years. Many sugar conjugates have been specifically designed as cancer-targeting cytotoxic compounds, with the aim of reducing toxicity and increasing selectivity for cancer cells. Glucose conjugates of DNA alkylators48–52, adriamycin53, paclitaxel54, benzylguanine inhibitors55, and metal-binding compounds56,57 are among the agents investigated for facilitating cancer treatment. In this line, glycation has been recognized as an effective method for enhancing various physicochemical properties of nisin31,32, yet its anticancer effects remain unexplored.

Fluorescent substances are produced during the advanced stages of the Maillard reaction, which is common in food processing and cooking. This reaction involves the dehydration and fission of Amadori rearrangement products. These products are intermediate compounds that form early in the Maillard reaction. Nisin, a peptide, is sensitive to high temperatures. Therefore, the glycation process was carried out at a lower temperature of 60 °C to prevent degradation of nisin over a longer period of 7 days. A study by Delgado et al. found significant correlations between the presence of fluorescent substances and other markers produced by heating, such as hydroxymethylfurfural, furfural, glucosylisomaltol, and furosine58. These correlations suggest that fluorescence can be used as an indicator of the extent of the Maillard reaction and the formation of related compounds. In the current study, unlike glycated nisin, the incubation of nisin alone did not change fluorescent emission over the 7 days. This observation indicates that glycation occurred during the Maillard reaction and was responsible for the fluorescent emission changes.

FT-IR spectroscopy is particularly useful for studying protein-carbohydrate systems because there are distinct regions in the mid-infrared spectrum where the chemical fingerprints of carbohydrates and proteins do not significantly overlap, making it easier to differentiate between them40,59. Proteins have strong and identifiable spectral features, particularly the amide I and II bands, which are centered approximately between 1650 and 1540 cm−1. These bands are associated with the protein's structure and were observed to change in the study. The study's results confirmed the formation of amide bonds between nisin and glucose or dextran, evidenced by a decrease in transmittance in the region between 1650 cm and 1540 cm−1, indicating the presence of these bonds. Carbohydrates showed a series of overlapping peaks in the region of 1180–953 cm−1. These peaks result from various vibration modes such as the stretching of C–C and C–O bonds and the bending mode of C–H bonds. These peaks are collectively referred to as 'saccharide' bands and are the most intense in the mid-infrared spectrum60.

Generally, our study suggested that glycated nisin exhibited stronger cytotoxic effects on MDA-MB-231 cell lines primarily involving apoptosis induction. The cytotoxic effects were found to be dependent on both time and concentration. Higher concentrations of both nisin and glycated nisin resulted in more significant cell death in a shorter amount of time, which is consistent with other studies regarding the anti-cancer effects of nisin and its combinations16,21,26,30,61–64. This effect was more pronounced in the glycated nisin groups. The maximum tolerable concentration (MTC) for nisin is considered to be 25 µg/mL, which means the cell lines can tolerate up to 25 µg/mL of nisin without significant damage65. Consequently, nisin at the lower concentration of 20 µg/mL had minimal deleterious effects on the cell lines compared to the control and other treatment groups. In nisin 80 and glycated nisin with a concentration of 40 µg/mL, the level of cell viability was very low in all three time intervals. However, glycated nisin at a concentration of 40 µg/mL reduced cell viability to a greater extent compared to nisin 40 and even nisin 80. Indeed, glycated nisin with a concentration of 20 µg/mL decreased cell viability more than nisin with the same concentration.

The results obtained from different studies show variations in cell viability observed with nisin treatment, which are influenced by several factors including the type and concentration of nisin used, the specific cell lines being tested, and the duration of incubation. These factors collectively play a role in determining the outcomes of nisin's effects on cell viability. Zainodini et al. showed that the most significant level of cell toxicity was observed when cells were exposed to the highest concentration of nisin for a duration of 72 h14. Kamarajan et al. investigated the impact of various types of nisin, specifically low-content nisin (2.5%), nisin AP (95%), and nisin ZP (95%) on HNSCC cells. They found that the rate of apoptosis in HNSCC cells was significantly higher when treated with both nisin ZP (95%) and nisin AP (95%) compared to treatment with low-content nisin (2.5%)21. Ahmadi et al. reported that the concentrations of 2000, 2500, 3000, and 4000 µg/mL of nisin had a significant effect on inhibiting the growth of colorectal cancer cells, with their therapeutic dose being greater than that used in our study17. Likewise, Hosseini et al. showed that administering nisin at concentrations of 800, 1000, 1200, and 1500 µg/mL remarkably decreased cell viability by triggering mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis16. Paiva et al. discovered that when MCF-7 and HepG2 cells were exposed to a high concentration of nisin (140 µM), the percentage of living cells dropped to below 20%66.

However, some studies have reported that nisin does not have a sufficient effect on cancer cells. For example, research by Begde et al. found no signs of apoptosis in both normal and cancerous Jurkat cell lines at concentrations that were cytotoxic67. On the other hand, combined use of nisin with other treatment methods has been shown to enhance its anticancer effects. We previously investigated the cytotoxic effects of nisin A and Newcastle disease virus (NDV) on the MDA-MB-231 cell line. The cells were treated with various concentrations of the virus and nisin, both individually and in combination. Nisin at doses exceeding 20.00 μg/mL demonstrated antiviral effects, interfering with NDV's oncolytic function. Furthermore, the results showed that the combination therapy exhibited stronger anti-proliferative and cytotoxic properties compared to the NDV treatment alone26. In a study conducted by Rana et al., the researchers synthesized chitosan-coated silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) that were co-loaded with two anticancer agents, nisin and 5-FU. It was found that the anticancer properties of nisin and 5-FU were enhanced when they were combined on the single composite nanoconstruct, as evidenced by the in vivo experiments on the murine skin cancer model13. Preet et al. revealed that the co-delivery of nisin, doxorubicin, and their combination using gold nanoparticles showed significant therapeutic efficacy against DMBA-induced skin cancer in mice. This effectiveness is likely due to the induction of apoptosis through ROS-mediated effects and immunomodulation27. The cytotoxicity of a combined therapy using Rituximab and nisin on human Burkitt’s lymphoma cells have also been considered. This combination therapy significantly decreased the survival of Burkitt’s lymphoma cells and increased apoptosis more effectively than either treatment alone. This highlights the potential use of nisin as a supplementary therapy to boost the effectiveness of Rituximab in treating lymphoma28. Monfared et al. determined that encapsulating nisin in CD-NSs (Cyclodextrin Nanosponges) enhances its effectiveness in killing melanoma cancer cells, improves its cellular uptake, and increases the induction of apoptosis62. Moreover, they confirmed that both free and encapsulated nisin have a stronger ability to kill colon cancer cells (HT-29) than breast cancer cells (MCF-7)63.

In the current study, the IC50 values for MDA-MB-231 cells at all three time points (24, 48, and 72 h) after treatment with nisin were higher than those for the glycated nisin group (Table 2). Specifically, the IC50 values 72 h post-treatment were 11.64 µM for nisin and 0.05 µM for glycated nisin. This indicates that glycated nisin is much more effective than nisin alone in inhibiting the growth of these cancer cells. Different IC50 values for various cell lines are reported in other studies, as shown in the Table 3. The IC50 values for melanoma, HepG2 (liver cancer), and SW480 (colorectal cancer) cell lines are reported to be 188.5 µM, 112.25 µM, and 600 µM, respectively, after 24 h of exposure to nisin17,18,66. These values are higher than the IC50 value for MDA-MB-231 cells (105.6 µM) in the current study after 24 h of exposure to nisin. However, the IC50 value for MCF7 cells (another type of breast cancer cell) was reported to be 105.46 µM after 24 h66 and 11.68 μg/mL after 72 h64, which are similar to the value found in our study. This suggests that nisin may be less effective in inhibiting melanoma, liver, and colorectal cancer cells compared to breast cancer cells within the same timeframe. The number of live cells obtained from the AO/EB assay was similar to the results obtained from other experiments conducted in the study. However, the number of dead cells was higher when compared to the results obtained from flow cytometry (Figs. 5 and 6). It may be due to inaccurately counting certain populations of cells. Specifically, late-stage apoptotic cells, which have compromised cell membranes, may be mistakenly counted as necrotic cells in the AO/EB assay, leading to an overestimation of cell death.

The significance of time was more prominent in the MTT assay than in trypan blue staining (Figs. 3 and 4). This discrepancy arises from the distinct mechanisms underlying these two cytotoxic assays. The MTT assay measures cellular metabolic activity, while trypan blue staining assesses cell membrane integrity68. If cells lose their integrity early on, trypan blue dye enters the cells, resulting in non-viable cells displaying a blue cytoplasm. This can occur before the cells' enzymatic activity is fully compromised. As a result, the decline in cell viability is more noticeable over time in the MTT assay compared to trypan blue staining. Moreover, the MTT assay may detect subtle changes in metabolic activity or proliferation that are not reflected in immediate cell death assessments by trypan blue staining. Essentially, the MTT assay provides a clearer indication of decreasing cell viability over time due to its focus on metabolic activity.

Assessing the safety profile of the glucose-nisin conjugate on normal cell lines is essential to ensure that it does not adversely affect healthy cells. Addressing this aspect in future studies will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the therapeutic potential and safety of the glucose-nisin conjugate. Moreover, glycoconjugation has been shown to enhance the solubility and stability of compounds, which can lead to improved bioavailability33. This is particularly relevant for nisin, whose therapeutic efficacy can be limited by poor bioavailability. Hence, direct measurement of bioavailability is proposed for future studies to investigate the pharmacokinetic profile of glycated nisin in vivo.

In conclusion, the current data on glycated nisin support the concept that this conjugate peptide may enhance the cytotoxic effects of nisin against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells by promoting cancer cell apoptosis. Therefore, conjugating nisin with glucose may reduce the required treatment doses and potential side effects of nisin on healthy cells. However, it is recommended that future research include additional cancer cell lines and thoroughly investigate the underlying mechanism of glycated nisin against cancer cells, both independently and in combination with other therapeutic approaches.

Methods

Glycation of nisin

Ultra-pure nisin, gifted from Handary SA (Brussels, Belgium), was dissolved in 10 mM phosphate buffer at pH 6 and centrifuged at 4000×g (eppendorf AG, Germany) for 15 min to separate any undissolved nisin. The final protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method69.After determining the nisin concentration in the solution, a five-fold concentration of glucose (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in the nisin solution. The mixtures were then lyophilized using a Christ Alpha 2–4 LD plus lyophilizer (Germany) and subjected to 60 °C and 70% humidity induced by a saturated KBr (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) solution in a desiccator for 3 days70.

Determination of glycation degree

The degree of glycation was determined using the OPA (ortho-phthalaldehyde) method71. The OPA reagent was prepared by combining 2 mL of tetrahydridoborate (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at 100 mM, 2.5 mL of SDS (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 100 μL of 2-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA) at 20%, and 40 mg of OPA (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) previously dissolved in 1 mL of absolute methanol (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Distilled water was added to the mixture to reach a final volume of 50 mL. Twenty μl of the conjugated nisin, containing 72.8 molar nisin/conjugated nisin, was added to 100 μL of the OPA reagent. The light absorbance was measured after three minutes at 340 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek's PowerWave XS2, USA). Fluorescent spectroscopy

Advanced glycation end products were assessed through fluorescent spectroscopy employing a microplate reader (BioTek's PowerWave XS2, USA). The samples (45 nm/mL) were prepared by dispensing them into wells of microplates, with a volume of 100 µL/well. Excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 370 nm and 440 nm, respectively72. To ensure accuracy, each sample underwent three analyses. Phosphate buffer served as the blank control. The data obtained were computed by subtracting the average absorption of the blank from the average absorption of the samples.

FT-IR

The powder samples were blended with potassium bromide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA) at a ratio of 1:10 (W/W) and transformed into tablets. Infrared spectra were captured using the Spectrum RXI spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA). Each sample underwent five scans by the device, and the mean transmittance was computed60.

Cell culture

The MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell line was supplied by the Pasture institute, Iran. Cells were cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Bio-Idea, Iran), 100 unit/mL gentamicin and 100 µg/mL amphotericin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA). These cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until they reached 80–90% confluency73.

Experimental design

Following a 24-h incubation period, the culture media of MDA-MB-cells were removed and replaced with 20, 40, and 80 µg/mL of ultra-pure nisin A (Handary SA., Brussels, Belgium), and 5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL of glycated nisin at different time intervals (24, 48, and 72 h). The cell viability and percentage of apoptosis of treated cells were investigated using different methods as described below.

Trypan blue dye exclusion assay

The seeded cells (105 cells/well) were treated by different concentrations of nisin A or glycated nisin as described earlier. Using PBS containing 1 mM EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA), the cells were separated from the bottom of plates and subsequently centrifuged at 200g for 10 min. At the end, 100 μL of cells suspension combined with 300 μL of trypan blue dye (Merck KGaA, Darmstad, Germany), and changes were observed under light microscopy (Leitz, Germany), distinguishing between live cells (appearing clear) and dead cells (appearing dark). All treatments were examined in triplicate.

The cell viability (MTT) assay

The cellular toxicity of nisin and glycated nisin on MDA-MB-231 cells was determined using the MTT assay, as previously reported74. In summary, MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in a 96-well cell culture plate containing 1 × 104 cells. After an overnight incubation in RPMI complete medium, the cells were exposed to various concentrations of nisin A or glycated nisin for 24, 48, and 72 h. Cells treated with DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA) were considered as the negative control. After treatment, the complete medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh serum-free RPMI, and cells were incubated in the presence of 50 µL of 4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (2 mg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA) at 37 °C for a further 4 h. The formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the optical density of each well was measured using a Biorad microplate reader at 570 nm. This experiment was conducted with the same conditions repeated three times. Cell viability (%) was measured as described previously75.

GraphPad Prism 10 was used to calculate the IC50 values for 24, 48, and 72-h exposure periods. The IC50 was determined using regression analysis, specifically by fitting a dose–response inhibition curve to the normalized data from the MTT assays. The positive control and negative control were assumed to represent 0% and 100% viability, respectively.

AO/EB assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in 6-well plates with RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS (105 cells/well). After 24 h, the medium was changed to the treatment solutions. Subsequently, the numbers of live, apoptotic, and necrotic cells were determined by the AO/EB staining method after 24, 48, and 72 h, as described previously76. Specifically, the floating cells and medium were transferred to 1.5 mL tubes. The adherent cells were detached with 0.5 mM EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA). Next, the detached cells and supernatant from the same treatment were mixed together. Then, the cells were centrifuged at 200g for 10 min and gently rinsed with 1 mL of cold PBS. The cells were immediately stained with 100 μg/mL acridine orange (AO) and ethidium bromide (EB). The stained cells were counted using a hemocytometer slide and observed under an epifluorescence microscope at a wavelength of 455 nm. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate. The live and necrotic cells exhibited a normal green and orange nucleus, respectively, while cells in the early apoptotic stage showed fragmented and condensed green chromatin. Cells in the late apoptotic stage displayed more extensive fragmentation of green chromatin.

Flow cytometry

The FITC Annexin-V apoptosis kit (Padza Padtan Pajhooh; Iran) was employed, somewhat modified from prior reports, to assess the apoptosis rate of cancer cells after treatments63. In more detail, MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 12.5 cm2 cell culture flasks and treated for 24, 48, and 72 h with different concentrations of nisin A and glycated nisin. After the incubation periods, the cells were harvested, washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and suspended in 1X binding buffer. Approximately, 0.25 × 107 cells were then slowly mixed with 5 μL of FITC Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) and remained incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Following staining, 400 μL of binding buffer was added to each sample, and the results were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10) with a FACS-Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Each experiment was carried out separately and in triplicate.

Colony assay

The proliferation rate of the MDA-MB-231 cells was evaluated through colony assays following the described treatments. The cells were cultured onto 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. After a 24-h incubation period, the cells were subjected to the designated treatments for 48 h. Subsequently, the cultures were allowed to incubate for an additional 10 days, with the culture medium replaced every 2 days with fresh RPMI medium. After the incubation period, colonies were identified using 0.5 mM crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Mo, USA), and the proportion of colonies containing fewer than 50 cells to the total number of colonies was calculated21.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA). The dataset underwent a two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Results were presented as mean ± SD from three separate experiments. A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied for statistical interpretation.
