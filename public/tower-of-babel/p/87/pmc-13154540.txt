A comparison of methods for calculating expected body mass indices among youth with eating disorders
Authors: Jessica A Lin, Carly E Milliren, Monique Santoso, Elizabeth R Woods, Sara F Forman, Tracy K Richmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercentage of median body mass index (mBMI) is an established method to calculate malnutrition severity for youth with eating disorders (ED) but does not account for individual variability in premorbid growth trajectories. We aimed to develop an individualized expected BMI (inBMI) measure and compare estimates of malnutrition severity derived from inBMI versus mBMI.MethodsUsing growth chart data for participants in a longitudinal ED registry (RECOVERY), we calculated percent of expected BMI at initial treatment presentation using two methods: (1) mBMI, the 50th percentile for age-and-sex, and (2) inBMI, defined as the 10% trimmed mean of each participant’s premorbid BMI percentiles. We compared estimates of malnutrition severity between methods overall and by premorbid growth category.ResultsAmong 97 participants (mean age 15.7 years; 85% female), mean BMI percentile at presentation was 31.8 (SD = 26.6). A mean of 12.8 premorbid BMI measurements per participant were available to estimate inBMI. Mean %mBMI was 94.2 (SD = 17.7) vs. 86.8 (SD = 14.0) %inBMI, with modest correlation between measures (r = 0.29; p = 0.004), and mean difference (mBMI–inBMI) of 7.3 (SD = 19.2). Concordance between methods was high for those with premorbid growth trajectories in the ‘normal’ range (5th–84th). However, %mBMI was consistently lower than %inBMI for those with < 5th percentile trajectories (i.e., overestimating malnutrition) and higher for ≥ 85th trajectories (i.e., underestimating malnutrition).ConclusionsEstimates of malnutrition severity differed meaningfully when using population median vs. individual growth trajectories. Individualized expected BMIs may offer a patient-centered alternative for assessing malnutrition severity, particularly among youth with premorbid growth in under or overweight ranges.Keywords: Eating disorders, Malnutrition, Adolescence, Body mass index, Median body mass index (mBMI)

Introduction

Percentage of median body mass index for age and sex (mBMI) is an established method for estimating malnutrition severity among patients with eating disorders (ED) and is often used to inform weight goals during ED treatment [1–3]. Because mBMI is easy to calculate and reflects a healthy weight goal for many, it has been widely adopted in both clinical care and research as a marker of nutritional status and ED recovery. However, as the epidemiology of EDs has evolved, particularly with improved recognition of the prevalence across the weight spectrum [4, 5], there has been growing concern that reliance on population median-based measures may not provide a complete or patient-centered assessment of weight status or treatment targets [1, 3, 6].

In 2013, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) was published, updating the diagnostic criteria for various restrictive EDs to be more inclusive of weight and sex and emphasizing the importance of the altered cognitions leading to restrictive behaviors [7]. The DSM-5 eliminated a specific weight cutoff to meet criteria for anorexia nervosa (AN), and introduced the diagnosis of atypical anorexia nervosa (AAN) defined by the presence of AN symptoms in individuals whose weight would be categorized as ‘normal’ or higher despite significant weight loss [7]. Furthermore, amenorrhea was removed as a diagnostic criterion, allowing for inclusivity of males and potentially facilitating earlier diagnosis in pre-pubertal females [7]. Lastly, the diagnosis of avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) was added, a diagnosis characterized by persistent disturbances in eating that result in failure to meet nutritional needs in the absence of body image disturbance [7, 8]. Notably, prior studies have demonstrated that many youth with ARFID have longstanding low weight or attenuated growth trajectories prior to eating disorder onset or diagnosis [9–13].

Despite the revised DSM-5 criteria acknowledging the diversity in body sizes among individuals with EDs and clinical guidance emphasizing consideration of individual growth trajectories [3, 7], a standardized, individualized approach to determining expected BMI has not been established. Evidence shows that EDs occur in individuals along the entire weight spectrum; for example, some patients may have grown consistently along a BMI less than the 10th percentile before onset of an ED and others may have grown consistently along a BMI greater than the 85th percentile [13]. Particularly among individuals at the lower or upper extremes of premorbid weight, reliance on population median-based reference values may obscure clinically meaningful deviations from expected growth patterns [13, 14]. As a result, the absence of individualized methods for estimating expected BMI may hinder timely recognition of medical complications and treatment needs for youth with EDs [13, 14].

The aim of this study was to compare methods for calculating expected BMI, using the traditional mBMI versus an individualized BMI (inBMI) based on average premorbid growth trajectory through childhood. We hypothesized that inBMI would better account for the growth diversity among youth with EDs, and that mBMI may misestimate malnutrition severity, particularly among individuals with premorbid growth patterns well above or below the median for their age and gender.

Methods

This study used data from the Registry of Eating Disorders and their Co-morbidities OVER time in Youth (RECOVERY), a longitudinal, web-based cohort study of adolescents and young adults with EDs who received outpatient treatment at Boston Children’s Hospital, which has been described in detail previously [15]. Baseline data were used for the 160 participants recruited between 2017 and 2020 [15]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Boston Children’s Hospital.

From each participant’s baseline RECOVERY survey, we collected sex at birth, gender identity, race and ethnicity, and self-reported ED diagnosis. Date of initial ED-focused evaluation visit was extracted from our electronic medical record (EMR) and used to calculate age at treatment presentation. Self-reported approximate date of ED onset was extracted from clinical documentation at initial presentation and used to calculate age at ED onset. These data were extracted by two independent reviewers (an Adolescent Medicine physician and a research assistant familiar with EDs), and any discrepancies were resolved by a third physician reviewer.

We extracted all BMI measurements available in our EMR, including all institutional outpatient visits, emergency department visits, and/or inpatient admissions, and any outside BMI data received during the initial presentation for an ED-focused evaluation. Some participants also provided consent for us to extract growth curve images from the EMRs of the network of primary care providers affiliated with our institution, if available. We applied a digital numerical extraction tool (WebPlotDigitizer) to extract age and BMI from these growth curve images [16].

Using available BMI data, we calculated percent of expected BMI at initial ED-focused evaluation using U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) growth curves [17]. For each participant, we first extracted BMI at the 50th percentile for age and sex at initial presentation to capture median BMI (mBMI). Second, to estimate our individualized measure of expected BMI (inBMI), we calculated each participant’s average premorbid BMI percentile for age and sex from BMI measurements as early as age 2 through 1 year prior to self-reported ED onset. We used a 10% trimmed mean to calculate each participant’s average premorbid growth trajectory. A trimmed mean removes a percentage of the highest and lowest observations to account for large variation in BMI measurements through childhood and the potential for extreme outliers or measurement errors which may not reflect an individual’s ‘average’ growth. The inBMI corresponding to each participant’s average premorbid growth percentile was extracted from CDC growth curves for their age at initial presentation. Percent of expected BMI for each measure (mBMI and inBMI) was calculated by dividing BMI at initial presentation by the corresponding expected BMI value. We then examined differences in the estimated severity of malnutrition between the two measures and any association with demographic or clinical factors. Percent expected was classified into severity of malnutrition based on the following cutpoints: < 70%, severe; 70–79%, moderate; 80–90%, mild; and none, > 90% [1].

We made a number of exclusions of BMI measurements and/or participants. When estimating premorbid growth, measurements within 1 year of ED onset were excluded due to the potential for alteration in BMI trajectory prior to recognition of disordered eating behaviors. Participants without growth data prior to 1 year before ED onset were excluded (n = 45). We also excluded participants with fewer than 3 years of premorbid growth data (n = 16). Lastly, we excluded participants older than 20 years at initial presentation (n = 2) as CDC growth curves are only estimated up to 20 years of age. The final number of participants included in our analysis was n = 97.

Statistical analysis

We report descriptive statistics using frequency (percent) for categorical variables and mean (standard deviation; SD) or median (interquartile range; IQR) for continuous variables. We examined the concordance between percent expected BMI between methods using Pearson correlation (mBMI vs. inBMI) and report the correlation coefficient (r), 95% confidence interval (CI) and p-value. Using the calculated difference between percent of expected BMI between methods (mBMI–inBMI), we examined the association between this difference and sociodemographic and clinical factors using correlations for continuous variables and t-tests or ANOVA for categorical variables. Agreement between malnutrition severity classifications between methods was examined using a chi-square test of symmetry, and weighted kappa (95% CI) was used to quantify agreement beyond chance. All analyses were performed in SAS (v9.4; Cary, NC), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings to key analytic decisions. First, to examine the impact of how premorbid growth was summarized, we evaluated alternative approaches for calculating each participant’s average premorbid BMI percentile and inBMI, including a 5% trimmed mean, 15% trimmed mean, untrimmed mean, and median. Second, we applied a more stringent definition of the premorbid window by excluding BMI measurements from the 2 years prior to self-reported ED symptom onset, such that only measurements obtained more than 2 years prior to onset were included to calculate premorbid growth. Finally, to assess whether findings differed by diagnostic subtype, we performed a sensitivity analysis limited to participants with restrictive eating disorders (AN or AAN).

Sensitivity analyses

Results were robust to alternative approaches for summarizing premorbid growth trajectories, including use of a 5% trimmed mean, 15% trimmed mean, untrimmed mean, and median to calculate average premorbid BMI percentile. Overall patterns of discordance between mBMI and inBMI were similar across approaches, with mean differences between methods ranging from 7.1 to 7.4, correlations ranging from 0.28 to 0.37, and slight agreement between malnutrition severity classification with weighted kappa ranging from 0.05 to 0.12.

In sensitivity analyses excluding BMI measurements from the 2 years prior to ED symptom onset (n = 87; mean 11.1 premorbid BMI measurements per participant), results were similar to the primary analysis. Mean %mBMI and %inBMI were 93.9 (SD 18.6) and 86.2 (SD 14.1), respectively, with a mean difference of 7.7 (SD 19.8), modest correlation (r = 0.30, p = 0.005), and slight agreement in malnutrition severity classification (weighted kappa = 0.08). Patterns of differences between methods across premorbid growth categories were consistent with the primary analysis.

Among participants with restrictive eating disorders (AN or AAN; n = 72), mean percent of mBMI was 92.0 (SD = 11.0) and mean percent of inBMI was 86.0 (SD = 13.5). The correlation between measures was lower and did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.15; p = 0.20), while the mean difference was slightly attenuated (mean = 6.0; SD = 16.1), and agreement in malnutrition severity classification remained modest (weighted kappa = 0.14). Patterns of discordance across premorbid growth trajectory categories were consistent with those observed in the full cohort.

Discussion

In this study comparing two methods for estimating expected BMI for youth with eating disorders, we observed meaningful differences in estimated severity of malnutrition and only a moderate correlation between methods in percent of expected BMI. Agreement in malnutrition severity classification was slight, although most discrepancies reflected shifts of a single severity category, suggesting clinically adjacent rather than extreme discordance overall. Notably, discordance between methods was most pronounced among youth at the lower or upper extremes of premorbid growth trajectories. This pattern likely reflects the inherent properties of comparing individualized growth trajectories with a population median reference, as individuals whose premorbid growth lies far from the 50th percentile will naturally demonstrate larger deviations when expected BMI is defined using the median. Consequently, when using mBMI, malnutrition may be overestimated for those who were already underweight prior to illness and underestimated for those with elevated premorbid BMI trajectories. Clinical guidance has similarly emphasized the importance of considering historical growth trajectories when determining expected or treatment goal weights in youth with eating disorders, although multiple approaches have been proposed and no standardized quantitative method has been established [3].

Underestimating expected BMI, and thereby underestimating the severity of malnutrition, may have an especially significant impact on those who started with and/or still present with weights that would be considered ‘normal’ or above. These individuals oftentimes receive a diagnosis of AAN since they present at a normal weight [5]. However, the ‘normal’ or higher presenting weight may lead to a false sense of security, despite equal risk of complications in those with AAN relative to those with AN or who appear underweight [14, 18]. These complications include, and are not limited to, bradycardia, electrolyte abnormalities, amenorrhea, and psychological comorbidities (e.g., suicidal ideation, depression) [4, 5, 19, 20]. One prior study reported that despite significant weight loss and complications of malnutrition, patients with higher percent of mBMI were less likely to receive inpatient care due to presenting at a higher weight [21]. Males are also more likely than females to present with AAN, as are those with a history of overweight or obesity [21, 22]. Both male sex and having a history of overweight/obesity are known risk factors for delayed recognition and ED care [14, 23, 24], so underestimating expected BMI may be further detrimental to the treatment and ultimate recovery for these unique subpopulations.

There are also challenges associated with overestimating expected BMI or setting expected BMI unrealistically high. Many individuals follow premorbid growth trajectories below the median BMI percentile prior to ED onset, a pattern commonly observed among youth with ARFID [9–13]. In these cases, using population median-based expected BMI may overestimate malnutrition severity, contributing to overly aggressive medical evaluation or hospitalization, or to distress related to reaching an unrealistic target weight that may be well above their historical growth trajectory. At the same time, some youth with longstanding ARFID may have experienced chronic undernutrition and attenuated growth, complicating determination of an appropriate expected BMI [9–13]. Even in such cases, incorporating individualized growth patterns may support more realistic, clinically appropriate treatment goals.

Our study has a number of limitations. First, our inBMI approach requires availability of pediatric premorbid growth data and therefore cannot be applied to individuals lacking such data or readily generalized to adults with eating disorders. Second, calculation of inBMI is more complex than use of median BMI, which can be directly obtained from CDC growth charts, potentially limiting immediate clinical feasibility without automated tools. Third, our methodology for calculating inBMI is novel and thus, has not been widely validated. Although findings were consistent across several approaches for summarizing premorbid growth in this study, future work is needed to compare the performance of inBMI with other methods for estimating individualized expected BMI or weight [3]. Additional validation in larger, independent cohorts and more diverse populations with respect to sociodemographic characteristics and eating disorder diagnoses is also needed. Weight-based metrics like expected BMI are most relevant for EDs characterized by weight loss or nutritional compromise and may be less informative for other diagnoses (e.g., bulimia nervosa or binge eating disorder). Although ED diagnoses were self-reported in this study, the majority of participants reported AN, AAN, or ARFID, for which assessment of malnutrition severity is a core component of clinical evaluation. Finally, our analysis focused on estimating severity of malnutrition at initial presentation for ED-focused evaluation and did not evaluate inBMI as a longitudinal metric of weight restoration or ED recovery. If applied longitudinally, additional considerations may arise because normal pubertal development is often associated with substantial shifts in BMI percentile independent of eating disorder pathology [25]. Although averaging premorbid BMI percentiles may partially account for such variability, it remains unclear whether measurements from different developmental stages (e.g., pre- versus post-pubertal) should be weighted differently when calculating inBMI. Future studies should evaluate the performance of inBMI to assess weight restoration and examine associations with physiologic markers of recovery (e.g., vital signs, menstrual status, hormone levels, body composition), eating disorder-related cognitions and behaviors, and broader mental health outcomes (e.g., anxiety and depressive symptoms).

Our study’s strengths include use of objective historical growth data derived from medical records, thereby limiting variability introduced by patient-reported weights. We also applied conservative cutoff criteria by excluding BMI measurements in the year prior to ED onset, thereby reducing the likelihood of capturing early weight changes related to disordered eating behaviors and to more accurately estimate healthy periods of premorbid childhood growth. Notably, findings were consistent across alternative approaches for summarizing premorbid growth trajectories, and among the subgroup of participants with restrictive eating disorder diagnoses. Findings were also robust when using a more stringent definition of the premorbid window that excluded BMI measurements from the 2 years prior to symptom onset, supporting the conclusion that results were not driven by early weight changes related to emerging eating disorder symptoms or behaviors. Although individualized expected BMI may be more complex to calculate manually in clinical settings, future work could explore development of automated EMR-based tools to facilitate scalable implementation of premorbid growth metrics. If clinicians choose to approximate this approach in practice, a pragmatic strategy would be to calculate the average of available BMI measurements from age 2 years until 1 year prior to illness onset while excluding extreme high and low values to approximate a trimmed mean and reduce the influence of outliers.

Our methodology and use of individualized expected BMI reflects a patient-centered approach to clinical assessment at initial presentation. Incorporating premorbid growth trajectories may offer a more nuanced alternative to population-based measures for assessing malnutrition severity in youth with eating disorders, with potential implications for clinical care and future research.
