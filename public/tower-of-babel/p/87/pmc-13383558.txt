In reply to; beyond shear stress: septic microvascular failure remains a multifactorial phenomenon
Authors: Ivor Popovich
Published: 2026

Dear editor.

I was pleased to read the response to my article by Damiani et al. [1]. They are correct to point out that septic microvascular dysfunction is a complex phenomenon, and leucocyte adhesion, coagulation, and endothelial shedding are observed. As they also note, observation does not imply dominance or necessity. Shear is known to alter adhesion and thrombosis.

Biological models attempt to elucidate the full complexity of a process. Physical models attempt to isolate a confined set of variables within that process and answer the question of what happens when they are altered. The two are complementary. For example, the Guyton model is a famous and well established model in critical care that ignores most aspects of venous physiology, yet allows us to understand how it can be manipulated [2]. In the same way, the shear model is still internally constructed with cellular physiology in its frames, just not externally visible.

It is correct to remind the reader that the shear model remains experimentally untested, and that the “shear target” is a free parameter. This is the point. The alteration of shear targeting and its relevance to sepsis is the hypothesis that requires testing. If experimental work shows that altering shear signalling abates microvascular shutdown, then the model becomes consistent with experimental observations. If not, then it is disproven.

The anatomical structure of arterioles is indeed hierarchical and branching, but again our goal is not to recreate the anatomy perfectly. At the level of the terminal arterioles the network does behave as multiple parallel circuits from the perspective of flow competition and resistance. More broadly, it is crucial to not let the biological complexity of reality discourage us from utilising models to advance our understanding. Sepsis in particular requires fresh approaches in order to develop new therapies. The field clings on to the dominant theory of “cytokine storm”, yet decades of attempts to alter or remove cytokines have failed to alter outcomes for patients [3, 4].

In summary, Damiani & Colleagues and I are largely in agreement. I hope my model will continue to stimulate discussion between scientists approaching the complex issue of sepsis from different lenses.
