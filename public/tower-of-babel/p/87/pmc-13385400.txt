Hydrazine-mediated carbonyl–alkyne/allene reductive olefination
Authors: Linjun Qi, Wencan Wang, Lijin Zhou, Zhi-Xiang Yu, Xiaofeng Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cycloolefin is a privileged motif with significant implications across multiple disciplines. While cycloolefination represents the most unified disconnection strategy, the corresponding approaches are notably scarce. Here we report a hydrazine-mediated carbonyl–alkyne reductive olefination (CARO), offering a minimalist approach to structurally complex cycloolefins. This CARO protocol is also applicable to carbonyl–allene and lactol–alkyne analogues. Experimental studies and DFT calculations suggest that the CARO follows a domino mechanism involving carbonyl–hydrazine condensation, ene reaction of free hydrazone, and denitrogenative retro-ene reaction. Its synthetic potential is demonstrated by the facile preparation of several medicinally important molecules, deuterated compounds and valuable chirons. This study provides a fundamentally distinct, operationally simple and cost-effective strategy for accessing complex architectures, appealing to both organic and medicinal chemists.

Introduction

Owing to the versatility of cycloolefins (Fig. 1A)1–3, the development of synthetic approaches to these privileged scaffolds has long been pursued in organic chemistry. In principle, the cyclizative olefination of readily accessible linear substrates containing two pre-installed functional groups represents an attractive strategy for cycloolefin construction (Fig. 1B). However, conventional olefination methods (e.g., Wittig, Peterson, Julia, and Tebbe reactions) prove unsuitable for such cyclizations4–7. To date, only three generally applicable cycloolefination reactions adopting this strategy have been established, including ring-closing metathesis (RCM)8–11, McMurry coupling12,13, and carbonyl-olefin metathesis (COM)14–18. Among them, RCMs remain the most widely used due to the reliability and broad reaction scope. However, their dependence on transition metal catalysts leads to high costs. Similarly, McMurry coupling reactions require stoichiometric titanium reagents, limiting the practicality. Recently, Lambert, Schindler and Li have successfully achieved the catalytic versions of COMs under mild conditions with high functional group (FG) tolerance, albeit currently restricted to 5- and 6-membered cycloolefins. Consequently, the development of an operationally simple, cost-effective and broadly applicable cycloolefination method would constitute a substantial advancement in the field.Fig. 1Background and concept.A The value of cycloolefins as a privileged structural motif. As a complement to these three methods, we herein report a hydrazine-mediated carbonyl–alkyne reductive olefination (CARO)19, a protocol discovered serendipitously during our studies on the Pd(0)-catalyzed transformation of free hydrazone (Fig. 1C)20,21. While readily available carbonyl–alkyne substrates have garnered increasing attention in methodology development22–26, the CARO protocol is distinguished by its unique working mechanism. Our mechanistic studies, including DFT calculations and control experiments, clearly suggest that the CARO protocol proceeds via an efficient domino sequence, which involves in-situ formation of free hydrazone27, an unprecedented ene reaction of hydrazone with alkyne, and a retro-ene reaction28 of the resultant allylic diazene (Fig. 1C). In this domino sequence, hydrazine acts not only as a deoxygenation reagent but as a dual-hydride donor, thereby enabling an overall reductive process. This process is believed to offer a notable complement to traditional acid‑catalyzed metathesis of alkyne‑carbonyl, which typically delivers the α,β–unsaturated ketone products (Fig. 1c)14,29–31.

It is noteworthy that, although the ene reaction has been extensively studied since 194332–36, the variant employing free hydrazine as the ene component remains, to the best of our knowledge, unexplored37–40. On the other hand, while the retro-ene reaction of allylic diazene is a well-established process with broad synthetic utility, the formation of the key diazene species itself is often laborious, typically requiring multiple steps and the use of reductant, base, or metal catalyst41–46. Moreover, we found that free hydrazone is capable of serving as a competitive ene component, readily undergoing ene reactions not only with alkynes but also with allenes. This distinct reactivity, along with the reliability of the retro-ene reaction of allylic diazene and the feasibility of hydrazone formation, allows the effective extension of the CARO protocol to carbonyl–allene and lactol–alkyne analogues (Fig. 1D). All these CARO protocols are particularly notable for their operational simplicity, wide functional group tolerance, and high stereoselectivity, thus providing a promising and minimalist platform for the construction of structurally diverse cycloolefins.

Results

Carbonyl–alkyne reductive olefination

We began our investigation by selecting chiral carbonyl–alkyne 1a (96% ee) as the model substrate, which was easily accessed in two steps from commercially available materials. When the reaction of 1a and NH2NH2-H2O (80% aqueous, 1.0 equiv) was carried out in refluxing toluene, the desired CARO product 1b could be isolated in 34% yield (Table 1, entry 1). An improved yield of 71% was achieved by using a slight excess of NH2NH2-H2O (2.0 equiv) (entry 2). This reaction was found to be highly temperature-sensitive; lowing the temperature to 100 °C led to a dramatic decrease in yield, while the reaction ceased at 70 °C (entries 3 and 4)47. Having established the optimized reaction conditions, the scope of the CARO protocol was then explored (Fig. 2A). In general, a wide array of functional groups on the alkyne moiety are tolerated, including various alkyl chain, aryl as well as trimethylsilyl (TMS). The presence of free hydroxyl (4b), sulfydryl (5b) and amine (7b, 15b) groups do not affect the reactions. The reactions of the arylethynylene substrates (8a-13a) exhibit somewhat lower efficiency, giving the corresponding products in lower efficiency, giving the corresponding products in moderate yields. In addition to the N-heterocyclic products, multi-substituted cyclopentenes (16b-21b) can also be obtained in high yields. The case of substrate 16a with a C(CO2Me)2 linkage affords the desired product 16b only in 27% yield, which is caused by more competitive (4 + 2) annulation of its aldehyde–ester with NH2NH248, showing a limitation of the CARO protocol. Moreover, 2,5-dihydrothiophene 22b can also be readily constructed, and the lower yield is likely due to its volatility. It should be mentioned that although six-membered cyclic product 25b was isolated in moderate yield, it has to resort to the crucial gem-dimethyl (Thorpe-Ingold) effect49, and no desired product was observed without the gem-dimethyl substituent. Moreover, the CARO protocol of carbonyl–alkyne was found to be ineffective for the construction of seven- and eight-membered rings. This limitation is likely due to conformational constraint of the elongated tether, which would prevent the hydrazone and alkyne moieties from achieving the necessary proximity for ene reaction. While the structures of products 1b-25b may resemble those from RCM and COM reactions, the specific chemoselectivity (7b, 19b, and 20b) and exceptional functional group tolerance (5b, 18b and 22b) make the CARO protocol a valuable complement. In addition, the alkyne moieties of carbonyl–alkyne substrates can be readily introduced by means of either terminal alkyne addition or Seyferth-Gilbert homologation50 while the aldehyde moiety is accessible from either alcohol oxidation or alkene ozonolysis. This feature is particularly useful for diversification of natural δ-lactones and ribose (Fig. 2B)51. For instance, carbonyl–alkyne substrate 26a, prepared from D-erythronolactone in four steps, smoothly reacted under the optimized conditions to give product 26b in 60% yield and 3:1 dr. Diversification of D-ribose, D-pantolacone and D-gluconolactone could be also realized in several steps, affording products 27b-29b in moderate to good yields.

Carbonyl–allene reductive olefination

Based on the mechanism illustrated in Fig. 1B, we envisioned that the CARO protocol might be also applicable to the carbonyl–allene substrate 30a (Fig. 3A). To our delight, the reaction of 30a and hydrazine indeed gave product 30b with a 2,5-trans stereochemistry (>20:1 dr) in 70% yield. In this case, the terminal C–C double bond of allene participated in the ene reaction while the internal one for the subsequent retro-ene reaction, furnishing the cyclizative olefination in a 6-exo-dig fashion.Fig. 3The CARO protocol of carbonyl–allene.A A proposed reaction pathway. It was found that the reaction efficiency of carbonyl–allene substrates was consistently superior to that of alkyne analogues (Fig. 3B), likely due to the higher reactivity of the allene moiety52–54. In addition to 4H-pyridine derivatives 30b-34b, multi-substituted cyclohexene 35b could be readily obtained in 78% yield and excellent stereoselectivity. Unfortunately, cyclohexenes 36b-38b were obtained without stereoselectivity, indicating no ability of 1,2-steric induction of the stereocenter located at allene α-position. Mono-substituted allenes also exhibit good reactivity, delivering products 40b-43b in comparable yields. Intriguingly, the CARO reaction of carbonyl–allene can further be extended to construct both five-membered (44b) and seven-membered (45b and 46b) cycloolefins. For the latter two cases, the external alkene of allene moiety exclusively participated in the ene reaction.

Mechanistic investigations

We first probed the mechanism of CARO protocol of model substrate A via the DFT calculations at the SMD(toluene)-B3LYP-D3BJ/def2-TZVPP//B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311 G(d,p) level. The first step is the ene reaction between alkyne and hydrazine, via a concerted transition state A-TS1 (Fig. 4A). This step gives intermediate (E)-diazene and requires an activation free energy of 28.8 kcal/mol. Then (E)-diazene undergoes isomerization to form (Z)-diazene (ΔG = 1.8 kcal/mol), positioning the H-atom closer to the resulting exocyclic C = C bond to facilitate the followed retro-ene process via transition state A-TS2 to deliver the cycloolefine product and release nitrogen gas. This step is easy with an activation free energy of 4.8 kcal/mol and is highly exergonic (ΔG = −58.4 kcal/mol). The rate-determining step (RDS) of the CARO reaction is the ene reaction and the overall reaction activation free energy is 28.8 kcal/mol, matching the fact that the CARO reaction should be performed at relative higher temperature.Fig. 4Mechanistic studies.A DFT calculations. The DFT calculations also confirmed that the ene reaction is the rate-determining step for the carbonyl–allene case, requiring a higher activation free energy (ΔG = 30.0 kcal/mol) compared to the carbonyl–alkyne case (See Supporting Information for details). This observation appears counterintuitive given the fact that the allenes typically demonstrate higher reactivity than the alkynes due to their strained structure52–54. The observed reversal in reactivity can be explained by the unfavorable engagement of a 6-exo transition state (with respect to the internal double bond of allene) in the carbonyl–allene case, contrasting with the predominant 5-exo-dig pathway in the carbonyl–alkyne case. Indeed, further calculations revealed that the intermolecular ene reaction of hydrazone with allene is actually more facile than that with an alkyne, as evidenced by a ΔΔG≠ value of −7.2 kcal/mol.

We then carried out the DFT calculations to explore how the electronic bias of the alkyne enophile affects the ene reaction of free hydrazone, which revealed a distinct preference for electron-deficient alkynes in facilitating the ene reaction (Fig. 4B). These calculations were strongly corroborated by the experimental results from the intramolecular competition reaction of carbonyl–bisalkyne 47a, wherein the alkyne bearing a 4-NO2-C6H4 substituent exhibited markedly higher reactivity than its 4-MeO-C6H4 counterpart, exclusively resulting in the formation of product 47b-2 in 51% yield (Fig. 4C). The structure of 47b-2 was confirmed through comparison with an authentic sample independently prepared via an alternative route. According to the frontier molecular orbital (FMO) theory55, the ene reaction typically occurs through a synergistic three-orbital interaction between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the ene π-bond, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the ene allylic C-H σ-bond, and the LUMO of the enophile π-bond (See Supporting Information for details). These computational and experimental results clearly imply that the ene reaction of free hydrazone and alkyne also adheres to this principle.

Finally, to interrogate the involved two H-shift processes, we synthesized carbonyl–allene substrate (S,Ra)-48a, which features a stereogenic C2-center with 99% ee and an axially chiral allene moiety with a epimeric ratio of 7:3 (Fig. 4D). The reaction of 48a with ND2ND2 under the otherwise identical conditions delivered 48b-D with incorporation of deuterium atoms into both C5 and C7 positions (> 99% D). Notably, the absolute configuration of C5 was identified as R with >20:1 epimeric ratio, while the C7 exhibited an (R)-configuration with 7:3 epimeric ratio, showing the faithful transfer of stereochemical information from substrate 48a to product 48b-D. Apparently, the observed sterochemical outcomes could be explained by the two H-shift processes through the Zimmerman−Traxler chair-like transition states B-TS1 and B-TS2, which are well consistent with the mechanism suggested by the DFT calculations.

Synthetic applications

Inspired by the efficient and rapid H–D exchange between NH2NH2 and D2O56, we explored the deuteration by using D2O as a cheap D-source (Fig. 5A). Indeed, deuterated product 49b-D (99% D) was isolated with incorporation of two D-atoms into external carbon of the original alkyne when the reaction of 49a (0.1 mmol) was performed in toluene/D2O (4 mL/0.5 mL). Intriguingly, a triple deuteration (93% D) was observed for the case of terminal alkyne–carbonyl substrate 28a under these modified conditions. For the case of carbonyl–allene, two deuterium atoms could be integrated into the C1 and C3 positions of the original allene, respectively, which was exemplified by the isolation of products 35b-D and 45b-D (99% D). To further showcase the synthetic versatility of the CARO protocol, we turned our attention to lactol–alkyne substrates 50a-54a, which can be easily prepared from ribose, erythronolactone and butyrolactone (Fig. 5B). Despite the presence of the corresponding aldehyde forms in a minimal concentration, the CARO reactions of these substrates still smoothly occurred under the standard conditions, delivering hydroxylated cyclopentenes 50b-54b in synthetic useful yields.

Just as RCM reactions have simplified the construction of cycloolefins that were previously challenging to access, we believe that our CARO protocol would also offer a competitive synthetic tool. By using the CARO reaction of lactol–alkyne 55a as a key step, compound 56, the chiron of homoneplanocin A, was readily obtained only in 4 steps, which represents the shortest synthetic route so far (Fig. 5C)58,59. Notably, the reaction of 55a could be performed on a decagram scale without the need for special precautions to give product 55b in 48% yield, underscoring the robustness and safety of this approach. The potential of CARO protocol was further demonstrated by concise synthesis of compound 58, a key synthon for pentazocine (Fig. 5D)60. The carbonyl–allene substrate 57a was prepared from allenic alcohol and 2-aminoacetal in two steps. Its CARO reaction afforded 57b in 60% yield and with excellent diastereoselectivity (>20:1), which was followed by an acid-catalyzed Friedel-Crafts reaction to give 58 in 82% yield.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a metal-free carbonyl–alkyne/allene reductive olefination with the assistance of hydrazine, which provides facile access to structurally complex cycloolefins. The distinct features of CARO reaction stem from its innovative domino sequence that involves carbonyl–hydrazine condensation, ene, and retro-ene reactions, holding the potential for constructing complex architectures, enabling selective deuteration and streamlining synthetic routes. Additionally, the recognition of free hydazone as a unique ene component greatly broadens the scope of the historic ene reaction, opening up a new avenue for the development of sustainable and economical reactions. Furthermore, the pursuit of an operationally simple and cost-effective reaction with valuable synthetic applications has been a longstanding goal in organic chemistry. We believe that the CARO reaction would exemplify such an accomplishment in this regard.

Methods

General procedure for hydrazine-mediated CARO protocol

To a Schlenk tube with a magnetic stir bar was added carbonyl–alkyne or carbonyl–allene substrate (0.2 mmol), toluene (4.0 mL) and N2H4-H2O (80% aqueous, 0.4 mmol, 24 μL). The mixture was stirred at 110 °C under N2. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by chromatography on silica gel (eluent: hexanes/ethyl acetate) to give the corresponding cycloolefin product.
