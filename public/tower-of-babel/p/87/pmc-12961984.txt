Phage Display Technology: Design, Library Construction, and Panning Strategies for Antibody Development
Authors: Hobin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phage display technology serves as a powerful in vitro platform for discovering antigen-specific antibodies, enabling the rapid identification and engineering of monoclonal antibodies for therapeutic applications. This review discusses key factors such as phagemid vector, antibody fragment formats, in-frame selection strategies, and effective library size in the context of maximizing functional diversity. Based on these factors, a detailed comparison of phage display antibody libraries is provided, including naïve, synthetic, and semi-synthetic formats, with emphasis on their distinctive features and design strategies. Furthermore, major panning strategies—solid-phase, liquid-phase, and cell-based panning—are compared, highlighting their respective strengths and limitations for isolating functional binders with regard to antigen presentation, epitope accessibility, and selection fidelity. Collectively, the refinement of library design and selection methodologies has led to the development of numerous approved antibodies, establishing phage display as a foundational technology in antibody drug discovery. Ultimately, this review aims to facilitate the effective application of phage display technology in developing antibody-based therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

Antibodies are secreted by B cells that specifically bind to antigens, thereby neutralizing and eliminating pathogens or foreign substances. As a key component of adaptive immunity, antibodies play a pivotal role in immune defense (Hoffman et al., 2016). With their diverse functions, including specific antigen recognition, neutralization, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), and opsonization, antibodies have broad applications as both diagnostic and therapeutic agents (Paul et al., 2024; Rhiel and Becker, 2021; Singh et al., 2018).

The type of antibodies being developed is primarily immunoglobulin G (IgG), characterized by a Y-shaped structure composed of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains (Porter, 1963). The light chain of a human antibody is classified into the kappa (κ) and lambda (λ) families (Barbié and Lefranc, 1998). Each antibody chain consists of a variable (V) region responsible for antigen recognition and a constant (C) region characterized by conserved sequences within the same antibody class or isotype. The V region of each chain consists of three hypervariable sites, called complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), interspersed with four framework regions (FRs) (Sela-Culang et al., 2013). The diverse sequences within the V domains of the heavy chain (VH) and light chain (VL) combine to form the fragment variable region (Fv), which is essential for antigen binding (Chiu et al., 2019). Each heavy chain and light chain are connected through a disulfide bond between the light chain constant region (CL) and the first heavy chain constant domain (CH1), together forming the fragment antigen-binding (Fab) region along with the Fv.

The heavy chains are linked by two disulfide bonds within the flexible hinge region, and the heavy chain constant domains 2 and 3 (CH2 and CH3) below the hinge region form the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region. The Fc region binds to Fc receptors (FcRs) on immune cells, mediating ADCC and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), and interacts with C1q to activate CDC (Gogesch et al., 2021; Hanson and Barb, 2015). It also binds to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), extending its half-life in circulation (Patel and Bussel, 2020). With advances in recombinant technology, various antibody fragments are being developed in diverse formats (Fig. 1), primarily focusing on Fab and single-chain variable fragments (scFv), to meet specific needs such as enhanced tissue penetration, shorter half-life, and bispecific functionality (Pirkalkhoran et al., 2023; Xenaki et al., 2017).

The advent of hybridoma technology enabled the development of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), marking the beginning of the production of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies. In 1975, César Milstein and George Köhler developed hybridoma technology, a method of producing large quantities of monoclonal antibodies targeting a specific antigen by fusing myeloma cells with antigen-specific B lymphocytes (Köhler and Milstein, 1975).

Despite its success, the use of mouse-derived antibodies in humans has been hampered by high immunogenicity and impaired effector function due to weak Fc binding to human receptors. To address this, chimeric antibodies were created, combining the V-regions of a mouse antibody with the C-regions of a human antibody. These chimeric antibodies, comprising approximately 75% of human components, were less immunogenic than fully mouse antibodies (Clark, 2000; Rodgers and Chou, 2016). Rituximab (Rituxan®) and infliximab (Remicade®) are two chimeric antibodies that have achieved blockbuster status due to their effectiveness in treating autoimmune diseases and B-cell lymphomas, respectively, demonstrating the remarkable success of this therapeutic approach.

Humanized antibodies have been developed by retaining only the CDRs of mouse antibody and replacing all other regions with human antibody sequences. These antibodies, which contain approximately 95% of human components, have significantly reduced immunogenicity and have enabled long-term therapeutic applications (Clark, 2000; Rodgers and Chou, 2016). Pembrolizumab (Keytruda®), trastuzumab (Herceptin®) and bevacizumab (Avastin®) are humanized antibodies that have achieved blockbuster status for their success in treating various cancers.

Despite containing over 95% human sequence, certain patients demonstrate a human anti-human(-ized) antibody (HAHA) response (Harding et al., 2010), highlighting the ongoing necessity for fully human antibodies. To address this, two primary approaches are utilized: transgenic mice (Lonberg, 2005) and in vitro display technologies (Bradbury et al., 2011). The transgenic mouse method benefits from in vivo affinity maturation, which generally results in higher binding affinities compared to in vitro display technologies. However, somatic hypermutations during this process may potentially induce immunogenicity, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, developing antibodies using transgenic mice necessitates substantial time and financial resources. This technology has been applied to develop therapeutic antibodies, such as Ipilimumab (Yervoy®) and Nivolumab (Opdivo®), which are immune checkpoint inhibitors used in cancer immunotherapy. The other approach to monoclonal antibody development, in vitro display technologies, includes phage display, mRNA display (Kamalinia et al., 2021), ribosome display (Chance and Kang, 2024), bacterial surface display (Jose, 2006), yeast surface display (Teymennet-Ramírez et al., 2021), and mammalian cell display (Zhang et al., 2014). This review will primarily focus on phage display technology.

The technique of displaying peptides using bacteriophages was first developed by George P. Smith in 1985 (Smith, 1985). Building on this innovation, John McCafferty and Gregory Winter extended the application of phage display technology to the development of antibody libraries in the 1990s (McCafferty et al., 1990). Most phage display systems utilize M13 filamentous bacteriophage, which initiates infection by binding N2 domain of its pIII (minor coat protein), to the F pilus of E. coli (Karlsson et al., 2003). Although the growth of the host is slowed, the non-lytic nature of the M13 bacteriophage prevents host cell death (Rakonjac et al., 2017), thereby reducing contamination from host lysis proteins and allowing for efficient phage propagation and purification.

Phage display libraries are typically constructed by expressing antibody fragments (e.g., scFv) fused to the N-terminus of pIII, enabling binding to specific antigens (Fig. 2A). As phagemids lack the components necessary for complete phage particle formation, the display of the antibody fragment on the phage surface is accomplished through infection with a helper phage, which provides all the essential elements for phage particle assembly.

Phage display panning is a robust in vitro method used to isolate clones with high specificity and affinity for a target antigen. This process typically involves multiple rounds (typically 2-4 cycles) of binding, washing, and elution, allowing for the enrichment of phage clones that exhibit strong binding affinity to the target antigen (Fig. 2B). After panning, individual clones are subjected to screening using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to identify the most specific binders. Selected clones are then sequenced and subsequently converted into monoclonal antibodies for further characterization and validation. This straightforward and efficient approach enables the identification of antigen-specific clones within approximately two weeks.

Phage display is particularly advantageous for producing human monoclonal antibodies against antigens unsuitable for animal immunization due to low immunogenicity or high toxicity. It also enables the isolation of antibodies targeting self-antigens, supporting the development of therapeutic antibodies for autoimmune diseases. Notably, adalimumab (Humira®), atezolizumab (Tecentriq®), and ranibizumab (Lucentis®) have achieved blockbuster status in the treatment of autoimmune diseases, cancer and ophthalmologic diseases, respectively, underscoring the clinical and commercial success of phage display technology.

Phage display technology, as a powerful tool for antibody generation, will continue to evolve and play a central role in advancing diagnostic and therapeutic antibody development, contributing to the progress of biomedical research and pharmaceutical industry. This review explores key considerations in library construction, the types and characteristics of phage display libraries, and various panning strategies to provide a comprehensive understanding of this robust technology.

KEY CONSIDERATIONS FOR CONSTRUCTING PHAGE DISPLAY LIBRARIES

Phagemid vector

Phagemid vector is a plasmid designed for phage display that incorporate several essential components (Fig. 3): E. coli origin of replication, f1 origin for single-stranded DNA packaging, antibiotic resistance gene, inducible promoter (e.g., LacZ), signal sequence (e.g., PelB, OmpA), multiple cloning site for antibody fragments, and phage coat protein encoding gene, typically gene III (gIII).

Tagging peptides (e.g., His, Myc, FLAG, or HA) are frequently inserted between the antibody fragment and pIII. These tags enable monitoring of display levels on the phage surface and facilitate detection of phage-bound antibody fragments during panning via ELISA. They are also useful for verifying in-frame insertion during library construction (Bai et al., 2015) and for the affinity purification of soluble antibody fragments.

Furthermore, inclusion of an amber stop codon (UAG) upstream of the coat protein gene allows switching between display and soluble formats: suppressor strains such as XL1-Blue, TG1, ER2537, or ER2738 permit full-length antibody-pIII fusion expression, while non-suppressor strains like TOP10F′ terminate translation at the amber codon, yielding only soluble antibody fragments. As summarized in Table 1, most antibody phagemid vectors incorporate both epitope tags and an amber codon to maximize versatility in selection and downstream applications.

Types of antibody fragments: scFv and Fab

Among the various antibody fragment formats used in phage display (Fig. 1), scFv and Fab are the most commonly employed. Each antibody fragment presents distinct advantages and limitations that must be carefully considered when constructing phage display libraries.

The scFv, with a molecular weight of approximately 25 kDa, is relatively small in size, which facilitates superior tissue penetration compared to larger antibody fragments (Gezehagn Kussia and Tessema, 2024). This property is particularly advantageous for targeting densely packed tissues or conformationally complex folded proteins. Additionally, the simplified structure of scFv comprising only a single polypeptide chain facilitates straightforward vector cloning and gene assembly, thereby enabling the generation of libraries with high diversity (Pansri et al., 2009). Furthermore, scFv demonstrates efficient display on phage surfaces and high-yield bacterial expression, resulting in cost-effective production (Sheets et al., 1998). However, the artificial construct of scFv often leads to reduced stability, making it susceptible to aggregation and degradation (Ma et al., 2020). Moreover, upon conversion to full-length IgG, the binding affinity observed in the original scFv may be compromised (Steinwand et al., 2014). Nevertheless, in the case of immune checkpoint therapies such as CAR (chimeric antigen receptor)-T, selected scFv can be directly applied to CAR construct design (Ghaderi et al., 2022).

In contrast, Fab fragments maintain natural pairing between heavy and light chains, conferring greater structural stability and improved solubility (Ma et al., 2020). They also exhibit high compatibility with full-length IgG conversion, preserving their original binding affinity (Chan et al., 2011), thus offering superior developability characteristics. Nevertheless, the requirement for co-expression of two separate polypeptide chains complicates vector design and cloning, and Fab fragments generally demonstrate lower expression efficiency in bacterial systems (Skerra et al., 1991). Moreover, due to their larger size (approximately 50 kDa) and requirement for dual-chain assembly, they exhibit lower display efficiency on the M13 phage pIII protein compared to scFv (Hust et al., 2007).

In-frame selection design

To ensure the functional quality of a phage display library, implementation of an in-frame selection strategy should be considered. Out-of-frame insertions or premature stop codons may arise due to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) errors, degenerate primer usage, or misligation events during library construction. These aberrations can result in non-functional or truncated single-chain variable fragment-pIII fusion proteins, significantly reducing the effective diversity and compromising the efficacy of subsequent selection processes. To address these challenges, various in-frame selection strategies have been applied (Table 2).

β-lactamase as a genetic selection marker enables efficient enrichment of in-frame antibody clones during phage display library construction by eliminating frameshifted variants through antibiotic selection. In the HuCAL GOLD and PLATINUM Fab libraries (Prassler et al., 2011; Rothe et al., 2008), in-frame selection was achieved by fusing the β-lactamase gene with antibody constructs, followed by clone selection in carbenicillin-containing medium to eliminate frame-disrupted variants introduced during sequential CDR cloning. This strategy facilitated the development of large, high-quality libraries with enhanced functional diversity and consistent isolation of high-affinity antibodies across diverse targets. Similarly, Yang et al. (2009) employed a proofreading step in their synthetic human scFv library by fusing assembled scFv genes to the 5′-end of the β-lactamase gene, enabling enrichment of in-frame variants through antibiotic selection prior to CDR-H3 diversification. This approach achieved over 70% functional diversity and facilitated the isolation of nanomolar-affinity clones against multiple antigens.

Properly folded antibody fragments can be enriched through the application of domain-specific binding proteins, such as Protein L, which recognizes correctly folded κ light chain variable domains (Vκ) (Nilson et al., 1992; Paloni and Cavallotti, 2017), and Protein A, which selectively binds to conformationally intact VH domains, particularly those from the VH3 family (Hillson et al., 1993; Roben et al., 1995). Løset et al. (2005) employed Protein L purification to extract properly folded Vκ expressing clones in scFv libraries. Although this purification process reduced the library size by approximately 95%, it significantly enhanced the proportion of functional clones, achieving up to 80% soluble scFv expression. This optimized library improved the selection efficiency of antigen-specific antibodies, including those recognizing challenging or self-like antigens. In the ALTHEA Gold Libraries™, in-frame selection was performed using Protein A affinity capture to enrich clones expressing properly folded VH3 domains (Valadon et al., 2019). This approach effectively eliminated non-functional variants, increasing the proportion of in-frame clones from 61-84% to 95-97%, thereby improving the overall quality of the library for antibody discovery applications.

Tagging peptides encoded within phagemid vectors can facilitate in-frame selection by enabling detection of properly expressed antibody fragments. Bai et al. (2015) fused an HA tag to the C-terminus of scFv constructs and subjected phage-displayed single-CDR libraries to proofreading panning against an anti-HA antibody. This strategy effectively enriched in-frame CDR variants, significantly increasing the proportion of functional sequences from 31-86% to 90-93%. Teixeira et al. (Azevedo Reis Teixeira et al., 2021) employed an SV5 epitope tag fused to the C-terminus of scFv constructs expressed on the yeast surface to enable selective detection of properly expressed antibody fragments. Yeast cells displaying scFvs were stained with anti-SV5 antibody and subsequently sorted using flow cytometry. This strategy effectively enriched in-frame and well-expressed CDR variants. The filtered CDR pools were then assembled into full scFv constructs and transferred into a phage display system for antigen-specific selection.

Effective size of phage display library

The human antibody repertoire is generated through V(D)J recombination, junctional diversity, and somatic hypermutation, resulting in a theoretical diversity of approximately 1015 to 1018 distinct antibody sequences. However, experimental observations indicate that the human body contains approximately 1011 B cells, of which around 109 are circulating peripheral naïve mature B cells, suggesting that the number of distinct antibodies actually expressed in vivo is substantially lower than the theoretical maximum diversity (Rees, 2020). Therefore, an effective antibody selection process requires a library size sufficient to encompass the diversity of the human antibody repertoire.

The relationship between library size and the probability of successful antibody isolation can be understood through statistical sampling theory (Hoogenboom, 2002; Perelson and Oster, 1979). The probability (P) of capturing a specific clone within an antibody repertoire of diversity (D) given a library size (N) can be estimated using the Poisson approximation: P=1-e-N/D. For instance, when the repertoire diversity is approximately 109 unique clones, a library size of 109 provides ~63% probability of sampling any given clone at least once, while a library of 1010 achieves >99.99% coverage probability. Although phage display libraries with a relatively small size of approximately 108 independent clones can yield binders against specific antigens (Bai et al., 2015; Pansri et al., 2009), the probability of isolating clones with the desired affinity and functional properties increases as the library size expands. In general, it is recommended to construct libraries containing at least 109 independent clones to ensure the successful isolation of antibodies against a broad range of antigens. High-performance, large-scale libraries typically comprise ~1011 unique clones, thereby maximizing the likelihood of identifying potent and specific binders (Dong et al., 2021; Lloyd et al., 2009; Schwimmer et al., 2013; Tiller et al., 2013). The effective library size may be reduced due to the presence of non-functional, out-of-frame clones and clones with low expression efficiency in E. coli. Therefore, constructing larger libraries is advantageous to compensate for such losses in functional diversity and to enhance the probability of isolating high-quality binders with desired characteristics.

To achieve high phage display library sizes, electroporation is preferentially employed over chemical transformation due to its superior transformation efficiency (Chai et al., 2020). However, a single electroporation procedure typically yields approximately 109 to 1010 transformants, indicating that dozens or even hundreds of transformation reactions are required to generate libraries exceeding 1011 in diversity. For adequate amplification and diversity preservation of a 1011-scale phage library, the number of infected bacterial cells must substantially exceed the library’s independent clone count to ensure sufficient representation of each unique clone. Moreover, when considering phage infection efficiencies (Ly-Chatain, 2014), culture volumes of several liters are required for implementation of such large-scale library amplification procedures. Due to these technical and physical constraints, the practical upper limit for library size has been reported to be approximately 1011 clones (Table 2).

TYPES OF PHAGE DISPLAY ANTIBODY LIBRARIES

Phage display antibody libraries are generally classified into four types (Fig. 4). Naïve libraries are constructed from B cells that have not been exposed to specific antigens, while immune libraries are derived from B cells of immunized individuals. Synthetic libraries utilize artificially synthesized genes to diversify the antibody CDR regions. Semi-synthetic libraries combine naturally derived sequences with synthetic diversification to achieve both stability and diversity. Notably, both synthetic and semi-synthetic libraries are designed with consideration of antibody developability during library construction. In the context of antibody therapeutics, developability refers to a comprehensive set of physicochemical and biological attributes that extend well beyond binding affinity alone, encompassing stability, manufacturability, solubility, viscosity, aggregation propensity, and immunogenicity (Bailly et al., 2020; Jain et al., 2017; Jarasch et al., 2015). These properties collectively serve as critical determinants of whether early-stage candidates can successfully progress through development. Neglecting developability can create a major bottleneck where antibody candidates with excellent binding affinity fail in downstream stages due to stability issues or manufacturing challenges. More than 90% of drug candidates ultimately fail during clinical development, and developability issues account for a substantial proportion of these failures (Bailly et al., 2020). This review examines the characteristics of naïve, synthetic, and semi-synthetic libraries in chronological order of their development over the past two decades.

Naïve libraries

Naïve phage display libraries are constructed from immunoglobulin genes isolated from B cells that have not been exposed to specific antigens (Fig. 4). These libraries offer the advantage of generating antibodies against a broad range of antigens due to their unbiased nature. For fully human library construction, B cells can be obtained from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), bone marrow, lymph nodes, and/or spleen of healthy donors (Table 2).

To encompass the entire antibody repertoire from B cell-derived cDNA, multiple forward and reverse primers are required to amplify the variable regions (VH, Vκ, and Vλ). Given the high sequence similarity among framework regions within each V gene family, degenerate primers can be designed to broadly capture V(D)J-recombined sequences. Approximately 20 forward primers and 6 reverse primers are typically sufficient to cover most variable region sequences (Lai and Lim, 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Ruschig et al., 2023; Schladetsch and Wiemer, 2021).

Løset et al. (2005) utilized peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) from 6 donors, employing IgD and IgM VH genes to obtain the most extensive and naïve V gene repertoire possible. For antibody fragments, they selected scFv format, which typically demonstrates higher expression levels in E. coli compared to Fab fragments. In the library, scFvs using lambda light chains exhibited 16-69% higher expression levels on average compared to those using kappa light chains. Nevertheless, after Protein L purification as an in-frame selection strategy, the pL-NBLκ library demonstrated better quality by yielding more unique antigen-specific clones with fewer panning rounds.

Pansri et al. (2009) developed a compact human scFv phage display library comprising 1.5×108 individual clones derived from 140 healthy donors. The library incorporated both predominant V gene families (VH1, VH3, VH4, Vκ1, Vκ3, Vλ1, and Vλ6) and less frequently observed ones (VH2, VH5, VH6, Vκ2, Vκ4, Vλ2, Vλ3, and Vλ5), thereby demonstrating genetic diversity. Efficient antibody selection was achieved against challenging targets, including aflatoxin B1, snake venom, cancer cell surfaces, and rabies virus, even following just one or two rounds of panning. Selected clones predominantly utilized VH3 and Vκ1 gene segments, reflecting the distribution observed in natural antibody repertoires. Despite its moderate size, the library exhibited broad functionality and diversity without significant epitope bias.

The CAT2.0 human scFv phage display library (Lloyd et al., 2009) was constructed using B cells isolated from 160 donors across multiple tissues (spleen, fetal liver, and bone marrow), establishing a diverse repertoire of 1.29×1011 clones through more than 100 electroporations. Screening against 28 antigens identified over 5,000 antibodies, with 3,340 demonstrating functional activity. Analysis confirmed the library’s broad functional germline V gene diversity (VH: 45/49, Vλ: 28/30, Vκ: 30/35), which closely mirrored in vivo usage patterns. Following selection, VH1 frequency increased from 32% to 51% (particularly VH1-69) due to superior expression and folding characteristics. Conversely, VH4 representation decreased from 20% to 10%, consistent with its known association with autoreactivity and typical underrepresentation in both in vivo and in vitro selections (Pugh-Bernard et al., 2001; Ray and Rothstein, 2023). Post-selection light chain analysis revealed Vλ predominance (90%) with Vκ diminishing (9%), despite Vκ being more prevalent in human serum (60-70%). This bias is attributed to superior folding efficiency and expression stability of Vλ-containing scFvs in E. coli.

The HAL4 (κ), HAL7 (λ), and HAL8 (κ) libraries (Hust et al., 2011) were constructed from lymphocytes of 44 ethnically diverse donors, utilizing 250 electroporations that yielded 2.2×109, 2.8×109, and 2.4×109 independent clones, respectively. V gene analysis showed VH1 and VH3 were overrepresented, whereas VH5 and VH6 were underrepresented. VH2 appeared only in kappa libraries. Rare subfamilies such as Vλ6, despite being infrequent in vivo, were included. Although the distribution did not fully mirror natural human repertoires, these discrepancies enhanced the structural diversity of the libraries. Screening against 110 antigens revealed more binders from the lambda library than the kappa library, likely due to higher expression efficiency of lambda scFvs in E. coli. Selected clones predominantly utilized VH1 and VH3, consistent with in vivo patterns, while no binders from VH2 were isolated. Vκ1 and Vλ1 were the most frequent light chains among selected clones. These observations collectively suggest that the E. coli expression and folding system imposes a selection bias that favors specific antibody gene subfamilies.

The XFab1 and XscFv2 libraries (Schwimmer et al., 2013) were constructed from multiple tissues of 30 donors resulting in large-scale repertoires of 2.6×1011 and 3.6×1011 clones, respectively. High-affinity antibodies were isolated, with dissociation constant (KD) values as low as 20 pM (XFab1) and 10 pM (XscFv2). This study enables a direct comparison between Fab and scFv libraries generated under identical conditions. In the selected libraries, despite certain disparities in VH-VL family pairing, shared pairing preferences such as VH1-Vλ3/Vκ1 and VH3-Vλ3/Vκ1/Vκ2/Vκ3 were consistently detected. Furthermore, VH1 and VH3 were dominantly used, while VH2, VH7, Vλ9, Vλ4, and Vκ5 were under-represented in both libraries. Regarding antigen-specific patterns, VH1 was dominant in XscFv2 and VH3 in XFab1 for β-gal, whereas this pattern was reversed for insulin receptor (InsR). Notably, VH5 was over-represented in InsR-binding clones from both libraries, highlighting format- and target-dependent V gene selection. Additionally, Vλ5, comprising <5% of the naïve pool, was enriched to ~20% in selected XFab1 clones but not in XscFv2, likely reflecting the stabilizing effect of the CH-CL interface in the Fab format (Röthlisberger et al., 2005).

The HAL9 (λ) and HAL10 (κ) scFv phage display libraries (Kugler et al., 2015) were constructed using peripheral blood from 98 healthy donors, containing approximately 1.04×1010 and 4.45×109 independent clones, respectively. These libraries employed the pHAL30 vector, modified from pHAL14 by rearranging the His-Myc tag sequence to Myc-His to enhance scFv expression efficiency. To address low kappa scFv expression (Hust et al., 2011), the bulky hydrophobic phenylalanine residue at the CL kappa C-terminus was removed. This modification increased the proportion of selected kappa antibodies from 4% in previous HAL4/7/8 libraries to 15.6% in HAL9/10. The CDR3 length, amino acid distribution, and V gene usage patterns generally resembled those of the natural human antibody repertoire. Interestingly, VH4 and Vκ4 were overrepresented in the initial library but showed decreased frequency after selection, suggesting disadvantages in antibody expression, folding stability, or auto-reactivity during selection. Conversely, Vλ6, though rarely observed in vivo, appeared at meaningful frequency in selected antibodies, demonstrating potential for experimental validation of expression and antigen-binding capabilities of rare V genes.

The IORISS1 scFv phage display library (Pasello et al., 2016) was constructed using lymphocytes from 15 healthy donors, yielding a total library size of 1.26×109 independent clones. To enhance expression efficiency, the phagemid vector was modified by replacing the PelB leader peptide sequence with the E. coli PhoA leader peptide sequence, and display efficiency was improved by substituting the full-length pIII protein with a C-terminally truncated version (amino acids 198-406). For each of the 5 sub-libraries derived from three donors each, 20 electroporations were performed, yielding approximately 108 clones per sub-library. The final library exhibited over 95% unique antibody sequences. The V gene usage closely resembled the natural human antibody repertoire, with VH3 being the most dominant family. Consistent with findings from other studies (Hust et al., 2011; Lloyd et al., 2009; Løset et al., 2005), VH-Vλ pairings were more frequent than VH-Vκ pairings. Specific binders were successfully isolated against six different antigens, demonstrating that a diverse and functional antibody library can be effectively generated from a relatively limited number of donors.

Kim et al. (2017) constructed a naïve human Fab phage display library comprising 3×1010 independent clones derived from 809 healthy donors. Due to the structural instability of Fab fragments containing lambda light chains, the library was constructed exclusively with kappa light chains. Notably, while natural human antibody repertoires are typically Vκ3-dominant, this library exhibited a higher proportion of Vκ1 (65%) compared to Vκ3 (15%). The heavy chain variable region families VH1 (33%) and VH3 (31%) were most frequently represented, demonstrating relatively balanced usage. At the subfamily level, VH1-69, VH3-23, VH3-30, Vκ1-39, and Vκ3-20 gene segments were predominantly utilized. Somatic hypermutation resulted in non-germline residues primarily within CDR1 to 3 regions of both VH and Vκ domains, contributing significantly to antibody diversity and binding affinity while reflecting functional selection pressures. The library’s efficacy was validated through the isolation of antigen-specific antibodies against 10 diverse targets, with the most effective clone demonstrating a KD of 1.7 nM.

Dong et al. (2021) constructed a naïve human scFv phage display library using overlap PCR on lymphocytes isolated from 400 peripheral blood, 30 bone marrow, and 10 cord blood samples. To augment VH-VL recombinational diversity, the primary library (~1×108) was transformed into the Cre+ E. coli BS1365 strain, where Cre-LoxP-mediated recombination yielded a final library of 1×1011 clones. This approach substantially increased clonal diversity, and sequencing of 60 random clones revealed that over 75% were unique sequences. The functional utility of the library was validated by selecting antibodies against PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9), among which clone 3D2 demonstrated binding with a KD of 19.6 nM and effectively reduced serum cholesterol levels both in vitro and in vivo.

While naïve phage display libraries can be successfully constructed, they typically yield antibodies with only moderate binding affinities. In contrast, immune phage display libraries are constructed from B cells of individuals previously exposed to specific antigens (Fig. 4), facilitating the isolation of high-affinity, functionally matured antibodies (Dong et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2007; Xu et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2004). However, immune library construction raises ethical concerns regarding donor recruitment and specimen collection, and their application is limited to specific pathogenic targets or disease contexts.

Furthermore, naïve libraries often harbor antibody-encoding sequences that exhibit cytotoxicity toward E. coli host cells, thereby compromising the effective functional library size. To overcome these inherent limitations, synthetic phage display libraries have been increasingly developed, wherein antibody sequences are systematically designed and assembled under stringent experimental conditions to achieve optimized diversity, enhanced expression levels, and superior functional performance.

Synthetic libraries

Synthetic approaches offer an alternative strategy for antibody library generation (Fig. 4). Early synthetic methods predominantly utilized degenerate oligonucleotides to introduce random diversity into CDR sequences. Although this approach successfully produced functional libraries, it had significant limitations, particularly the generation of undesirable sequences such as unnatural codons and stop codons due to random codon arrangement. The advent of sophisticated synthetic technologies, notably trinucleotide mutagenesis (TRIM) and Slonomics®, has substantially enhanced the quality and precision of synthetic library construction. TRIM represents a chemical synthesis-based approach that employs trinucleotide phosphoramidites to achieve precise control over amino acid incorporation at predetermined positions (Sondek and Shortle, 1992). Slonomics® is a platform technology that utilizes double-stranded trinucleotide building blocks to facilitate automated, high-throughput synthesis of genes and libraries (Van den Brulle et al., 2008). Both methodologies enable stringent control over amino acid composition within protein libraries, effectively eliminating undesired amino acids, stop codons, and frameshift mutations. Through these advanced synthetic technologies, researchers can precisely design and diversify antigen-binding CDR regions and combine them with a curated set of framework regions, thereby generating functionally diverse synthetic antibody libraries (Table 2).

The HuCAL scFv phage display library (Knappik et al., 2000) was constructed through comprehensive analysis of human antibody gene sequences commonly found in immune responses. Seven consensus frameworks for VH (VH1A, VH1B, VH1, VH2, VH3, VH4, VH5, and VH6) and seven for VL (Vκ1, Vκ2, Vκ3, Vκ4, Vλ1, Vλ2, and Vλ3) were selected to encompass over 95% of the natural antibody repertoire. These frameworks were combined to generate 49 unique VH-VL pairings. To minimize antigen-driven bias, CDR1 and CDR2 regions were derived from representative germline sequences of their respective subfamilies. In contrast, CDR-H3 and CDR-L3 regions were diversified using TRIM, incorporating natural amino acid frequencies and length distributions. This strategy minimized stop codons, frameshifts, and undesirable residues, thereby enabling construction of a high-quality synthetic antibody library containing 2.1×109 independent clones. The library successfully generated antigen-specific binders exhibiting sub-nanomolar affinities without requiring affinity maturation. Additionally, a modular gene design was implemented by incorporating unique restriction sites at each CDR and framework boundary, facilitating versatile molecular manipulations such as CDR swapping, framework replacement, and chain shuffling.

The ETH-2-Gold scFv phage display library (Silacci et al., 2005) was constructed utilizing three commonly found human antibody germline segments: DP47 (VH3-23), DPK22 (Vκ3-20), and DPL16 (Vλ3-19). To maintain structural integrity while maximizing functional diversity, sequence variation was strategically confined to the CDR3, whereas CDR1 and CDR2 regions were preserved in their germline configurations. Given the pivotal role of CDR3 regions in antigen recognition, diversification was implemented at critical positions: residues 95-100 within the DP47 CDR3, residues 91, 92, 93, 94, and 96 within the DPK22 CDR3, and residues 91-96 within the DPL16 CDR3. This targeted mutagenesis was accomplished through PCR amplification using partially degenerate oligonucleotide primers, resulting in a comprehensive library of 3×109 independent clones. The exclusive use of the DP47 germline segment provided additional experimental advantages, including enhanced thermodynamic stability and compatibility with Protein A-based purification and detection, thereby facilitating downstream characterization and selection processes.

The HuCAL GOLD (Rothe et al., 2008) was constructed based on the same consensus frameworks as the original HuCAL, utilizing seven VH and seven VL consensus frameworks derived from human antibody genes. However, HuCAL GOLD is a Fab phage display library in which all six CDRs were diversified using TRIM technology, thereby precisely mimicking the sequence and length diversity observed in natural antibodies. The CDR libraries were cloned into the pMORPH18 vector, and a β-lactamase selection system was applied at each step to efficiently eliminate frameshift mutations, resulting in the generation of 3.6×1010 independent clones with high functional quality. This library was subsequently transferred to the pMORPH23 vector implementing CysDisplay technology, ultimately yielding a final library comprising 1.6×1010 independent clones. CysDisplay technology facilitates display through disulfide bond formation between Fab fragments and the phage protein pIII. The addition of reducing agents (such as DTT) enables effective elution of phages that bind to antigens with high affinity. Through this approach, Fab fragments with stable binding properties and exceptionally high affinity at the picomolar (pM) level, reaching up to 40 pM, could be successfully selected against various antigens.

Yang et al. (2009) employed a single-scaffold approach using human antibody genes VH3-23 and Vλ1g, which are known for their high expression levels and stability. This approach simplifies library construction and standardizes subsequent development and engineering processes of selected antibodies. Partially degenerate codons mimicking natural diversity were incorporated into the CDR-H1, H2, L1, L2, and L3 regions, while the CDR-H3 region was fully randomized with variable lengths to maximize diversity. These scFv genes were fused with the β-lactamase gene in the pFDV vector to select only in-frame clones, yielding approximately 107 transformants. To further maximize the diversity of the final library, diverse CDR-H3 sequences were reintroduced into these validated gene fragments, and the completed scFv genes were cloned into the pComb3X phagemid vector to construct a scFv phage display library comprising 7.6×109 clones. This library successfully generated multiple unique scFv clones with high binding affinities in the nanomolar range against various types of antigens, including human and non-human proteins, as well as small molecule haptens.

The HuCAL PLATINUM (Prassler et al., 2011) is a synthetic Fab antibody library that represents an improvement over the HuCAL GOLD. Instead of including the VH4 and Vκ4 frameworks that exhibited poor selection efficiency in the HuCAL GOLD (Rothe et al., 2008), the library incorporates two master genes from the VH3 family (VH3-23 and VH3-15), which are highly represented in natural repertoires. In total, seven VH frameworks and six VL frameworks were utilized. All six CDRs were diversified using TRIM technology. Specifically, a length-dependent design was implemented for CDR-H3 to reflect the observation that amino acid composition varies with loop length in human antibodies. To improve antibody developability, the library design also considered factors such as codon and expression optimization, removal of N-glycosylation sites (Asn-X-Ser/Thr motifs) (Dutta et al., 2017; Mellquist et al., 1998; Shakin-Eshleman et al., 1996) that may lead to aberrant post-translational modifications (PTMs) in mammalian cells, and minimization of potentially immunogenic motifs. After constructing the heavy chain and light chain libraries separately, β-lactamase counter-selection was applied prior to their combination to eliminate defective clones, ultimately securing 4.5×1010 independent clones. This refined design enabled the discovery of approximately four times more unique antibody sequences against the same antigen compared to the HuCAL GOLD. Moreover, the proportion of antibodies with high affinity (KD <10 nM) increased significantly, and IgG expression levels in mammalian cells improved by 1.7-fold to 4.6-fold, demonstrating enhanced functional properties.

The Synthetic Fitness Fab (SF-Fab) phage display library (Zhai et al., 2011) was constructed using four VH frameworks (VH1-69, VH3-23, VH3-30, and VH4-34) and two kappa VL frameworks (Vκ1-39 and Vκ3-20), which were selected based on criteria of thermostability, natural germline frequency, and heterodimer compatibility. Each CDR region was synthesized using Slonomics® technology to precisely mimic the positional amino acid frequencies of functionally validated sequences from natural antibody repertoires, ultimately yielding a library of 3.6×1010 transformants. Importantly, this design approach overcomes the inherent limitations in theoretical diversity imposed by amino acid linkage biases that arise from natural V(D)J recombination. The resulting CDR-H3 regions exhibited 64% average sequence identity to the natural human repertoire, representing greater similarity to native sequences than the HuCAL GOLD library (54%) (Rothe et al., 2008), while demonstrating a high average Fab expression rate of 93.4%. Most significantly, over 69% of high-affinity antibodies (nanomolar-level) isolated against diverse antigens were derived from rare clones with theoretical frequencies below 10–9, confirming that this library successfully maintains the favorable characteristics inherent to natural antibodies while achieving exceptional genetic diversity.

The Ylanthia is a fully synthetic human Fab antibody library (Tiller et al., 2013) constructed by selecting frameworks with favorable biophysical properties for development and production. Framework selection was based on comprehensive evaluation of isoelectric point (pI) optimization for enhanced solubility, minimization of PTM sites for quality control, high expression levels, and thermal stability. This process yielded 36 fixed VH/VL pairs comprising 12 VH, 9 Vκ, and 6 Vλ genes with superior characteristics. The framework distribution is dominated by the VH3 family (including VH3-23), accounting for over half the collection, with even distribution of Vκ1 and Vκ3 families, and Vλ1-51 representing ~50% of Vλ genes. VH2, VH4, and Vκ4 genes were intentionally excluded due to poor phage display efficiency (Hust et al., 2011; Kugler et al., 2015; Lloyd et al., 2009; Rothe et al., 2008). CDRs were designed to mimic natural antibody diversity, with CDR-L3 synthesized using TRIM technology and CDR-H3 generated using Slonomics® technology. The latter excluded PTM-prone residues (Asn, Asp, Met) and varied from 6 to 17 amino acids in length. The final library contains 1.3×1011 independent clones and successfully generated high-affinity binders with picomolar KD values against diverse antigens through CysDisplay selection. Selected Fabs retained their superior properties upon conversion to the IgG1 format, demonstrating significant potential for reducing time and costs in drug development.

The PHILODiamond is a large-scale synthetic scFv phage display library comprising 4.1×1010 independent clones (Weber et al., 2014). Similar to the ETH-2-Gold (Silacci et al., 2005), it is constructed using three human antibody frameworks: DP47 (VH3-23), DPK22 (Vκ3-20), and DPL16 (Vλ3-19). By using the DP47 framework, all clones in scFv format exhibit high thermodynamic stability and can be conveniently purified via Protein A affinity chromatography. While sharing fundamental design principles with the ETH-2-Gold library, such as focusing combinatorial diversity on the CDR3 regions using partially degenerate oligonucleotide primers, PHILODiamond incorporates several key enhancements. Notably, CDR-H3 diversity has been expanded to encompass 4-7 amino acids, compared to the 4-6 amino acid range of the ETH-2-Gold, thereby generating clones with predominantly longer CDR-H3 loops. Additionally, a distinctive S52N mutation was introduced at VH position 52, a residue commonly modified during somatic hypermutation, to enhance hydrogen bonding capacity and recapitulate characteristics of natural antibody maturation. Consequently, the PHILODiamond demonstrated superior performance relative to the ETH-2-Gold, yielding a greater number of antibody clones against the majority of tested antigens. Functionally, more than 90% of randomly selected clones successfully expressed scFv fragments, and numerous antigen-specific clones exhibited binding affinities within the nanomolar range across diverse target antigens.

Across all platforms, the use of stable and frequently occurring human antibody frameworks (e.g., VH3-23) is prioritized to ensure high expression, structural integrity, and compatibility with downstream applications. CDR diversification, particularly of CDR-H3, is a central focus, with TRIM and Slonomics® technologies widely employed to precisely control amino acid composition, eliminate undesirable codons, and closely mimic natural antibody repertoires. Additionally, developability considerations such as removal of N-glycosylation motifs and avoidance of PTM-prone residues are commonly integrated. Frameworks with poor display or folding efficiency (e.g., VH4, Vκ4) are often excluded. Collectively, these strategies enhance the functional output, affinity range, and manufacturability of antibodies derived from synthetic libraries.

Semi-synthetic libraries

Semi-synthetic phage display libraries are constructed by strategically combining natural antibody sequences with synthetic sequences (Fig. 4). This approach provides the advantage of simultaneously leveraging the biological properties of natural antibodies while maintaining the controllability of artificial design. Notably, CDR-H3 contributes most significantly to antibody diversity and antigen-binding specificity (Fernández-Quintero et al., 2018, 2019); however, fully recapitulating its high diversity and complex 3D structure through synthetic means remains challenging. Consequently, semi-synthetic libraries directly extract and incorporate CDR-H3 sequences derived from human B cells, thereby securing functional diversity (Weitzner et al., 2015) and structural stability (Passon et al., 2025) while minimizing clinical immunogenicity (Hu et al., 2023). Alternatively, synthetic sequences can be computationally designed based on natural antibody repertoire databases to closely resemble native sequences, thus enabling the construction of highly functional libraries. The features of semi-synthetic libraries are summarized in Table 2.

Bai et al. constructed a scFv phage display library using a non-combinatorial approach with pre-designed CDR sequences that are highly nature-like while minimizing PTMs (Bai et al., 2015) to overcome the limitations of conventional random combinatorial approaches for CDR sequences. They analyzed human antibody sequence data to incorporate the patterns and frequencies of somatic hypermutation, while simultaneously excluding sequences containing undesirable PTM-inducing motifs such as N-glycosylation (Asn-X-Ser/Thr) (Dutta et al., 2017; Mellquist et al., 1998; Shakin-Eshleman et al., 1996), deamidation (Asp-Gly) (Liu et al., 2008; Stephenson and Clarke, 1989; Sydow et al., 2014), isomerization (Asn-Gly) (Liu et al., 2008; Stephenson and Clarke, 1989; Sydow et al., 2014), oxidation (Cys, Met) (Dashivets et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2008), and non-enzymatic cleavage (Asp-Pro) (Hinterholzer et al., 2021; Vlasak and Ionescu, 2011). Each single-CDR library was then subjected to one round of panning against an anti-HA antibody to enrich for in-frame sequences. Subsequently, the validated CDRs were assembled into a final scFv library in which all six CDRs were diversified using frameworks DP47 (VH3-23), DPK22 (Vκ3-20), and DPL3 (Vλ1-47). The resulting library comprised approximately 8×108 unique clones. However, a notable bias was the overrepresentation of shorter CDR-H3 sequences, which was attributed to the higher incidence of synthesis errors in longer CDRs and the preferential selection of shorter CDRs during anti-HA-based in-frame selection. Nevertheless, the library yielded numerous scFv clones with nanomolar affinities against a variety of antigens, demonstrating that a functional antibody library can be established using a non-combinatorial, pre-designed synthetic diversity strategy even with limited CDR diversity.

The ALTHEA Gold LibrariesTM are semi-synthetic scFv phage display libraries (Valadon et al., 2019) specifically designed for therapeutic antibody discovery, featuring a strategic combination of synthetic and natural sequences. The library is based on a single VH framework (VH3-23) and two distinct VL frameworks (Vκ3-20 and Vκ4-01). The CDR-H1, H2, L1, L2, and L3 regions were diversified using TRIM technology at positions identified through structural analysis of antigen-antibody complexes as sites of direct antigen contact. To maximize developability, sequence motifs associated with undesirable PTMs were strictly excluded from the design, including those responsible for N-glycosylation (Asn-X-Ser/Thr) (Dutta et al., 2017; Mellquist et al., 1998; Shakin-Eshleman et al., 1996), isomerization (Asp-Gly) (Liu et al., 2008; Stephenson and Clarke, 1989; Sydow et al., 2014), deamidation (Asn-Gly/Ser) (Liu et al., 2008; Stephenson and Clarke, 1989; Sydow et al., 2014), oxidation (solvent-exposed Met) (Dashivets et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2008), and aggregation (solvent-exposed Trp) (Alam et al., 2020; Hageman et al., 2018). Library construction followed a three-step process: (1) generation of primary library (PL) by assembling synthetic scaffolds with a set of neutral CDR-H3/JH (H3J) fragments; (2) filtration of the PL by heat treatment at 70°C followed by Protein A selection to enrich for in-frame, thermostable antibody variants (filtered library, FL); and (3) assembly of the final secondary library (SL) by inserting natural H3J fragments derived from 200 healthy donors into the FL. The final library comprises 2.5×1010 independent clones with an in-frame rate of 85%. Antibodies selected against various antigens exhibited single-digit or sub-nanomolar KD values and high thermal stability above 75°C. This approach validates the ALTHEA Gold LibrariesTM as an efficient platform for discovering antibodies with optimal biophysical and developability profiles.

Teixeira et al. constructed a semi-synthetic scFv phage display library (Azevedo Reis Teixeira et al., 2021) designed to directly derive drug-like antibodies with high affinity and excellent developability from early screening stages. The library was engineered using four clinically validated therapeutic antibody frameworks: VH1-24, VH3-7, VH4-30-4, VH1-18, and Vκ1-12, Vκ2D-29, Vκ3-11, Vλ2-14. CDR-H1, H2, L1, L2, and L3 were synthesized via oligonucleotide array-based synthesis after removing sequences susceptible to PTMs (N-glycosylation, deamidation, isomerization, oxidation) and biophysical liabilities (unpaired cysteines, aggregation-prone motifs, hydrophobic patches). In contrast, CDR-H3 diversity was directly sourced from healthy human B cells to capture natural diversity unachievable through synthetic methods. Individual CDR libraries were sorted using flow cytometry in a yeast surface display system targeting the C-terminal SV5 tag. High-expressing CDRs were combined to construct the final scFv phage display library comprising approximately 9×109 transformants. This methodology leveraged yeast endoplasmic reticulum quality control as a biological filter to eliminate poorly expressed sequences. Derived antibodies exhibited sub-nanomolar to low nanomolar antigen-binding affinities and demonstrated superior developability characteristics compared to existing clinical antibodies, including expression yield, thermal stability, solubility, and aggregation resistance. This approach streamlines antibody drug discovery and reduces late-stage developmental failure risks due to biophysical liabilities.

In summary, semi-synthetic libraries are strategically designed with comprehensive consideration for developability from the earliest stages of development, extending beyond conventional focus on library size and antigen-binding affinity alone. These libraries leverage natural antibody sequences while systematically eliminating PTM-inducing sequence motifs and optimizing key biophysical parameters such as thermal stability and aggregation propensity. This comprehensive design facilitates early-stage selection of developable antibody candidates, thereby reducing development timelines and costs while minimizing late-stage attrition risks.

PHAGE DISPLAY PANNING STRATEGY

Up to 2022, antibody phage display has led to the approval of 20 therapeutic antibodies across diverse disease indications, highlighting its pivotal role and potential in antibody therapeutics development (Table 3). In phage display technology, panning is a critical step that enables the selective enrichment of antigen-specific phage clones. The choice of panning strategy can significantly influence screening efficiency, antibody characteristics, and target specificity. Panning methods can be broadly categorized into solid-phase, liquid-phase, and cell-based approaches, depending on target properties, experimental objectives, and assay conditions. This review provides a comparative analysis of these three major approaches (Fig. 5). Additionally, various specialized panning techniques have been developed, including competitive panning, which selects neutralizing antibodies by incorporating competing ligands or antibodies along with the antigen (Zeng et al., 2020; Zhang and Dimitrov, 2009), and internalizing panning, which selectively enriches antibodies capable of undergoing cellular internalization following cell surface binding (Poul et al., 2000; Wang and Cao, 2009).

Solid-phase panning

Solid-phase panning represents a widely employed and highly reproducible method in phage display technology. This approach involves the immobilization of antigens on plastic surfaces, such as immunotubes or microtiter plates, followed by incubation with phage antibody libraries to selectively enrich phages exhibiting specific antigen-binding properties. The method is favored due to its procedural simplicity, well-established standardized protocols, and consistent performance across diverse laboratory environments. These advantages render this approach amenable to automation and high-throughput screening applications, thereby facilitating industrial-scale antibody development. Moreover, this method is readily adaptable to various target types, including recombinant proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids.

The effectiveness of this approach is demonstrated by the fact that many functional antibodies described in this review were isolated using solid-phase panning, and numerous approved therapeutic antibodies have been developed through this technique, employing recombinant proteins or specific domain constructs as antigens (Table 3). The target antigens of approved therapeutic antibodies developed via solid-phase panning include cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-23) (Benson et al., 2006; Chen et al., 1999; Jespers et al., 1994; Liu et al., 2016), bacterial toxins (e.g., anthrax toxin) (Mazumdar, 2009), and single-pass transmembrane proteins (e.g., VEGFR2) (Lu et al., 2002).

Despite its advantages, solid-phase panning exhibits inherent limitations associated with the antigen immobilization process. The conformational integrity of antigens may be compromised during surface coating, potentially resulting in the loss of native binding properties. Additionally, critical epitopes may be masked through direct contact with the solid surface. These challenges are particularly pronounced when targeting membrane proteins, including G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and tetraspanins, or other multi-pass transmembrane proteins, where preservation of native conformational states is crucial for effective functional antibody selection. To mitigate these limitations, phage display can be conducted using recombinant proteins designed to mimic extracellular loop structures, such as cyclic peptides (Wu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2004). However, for the precise recognition of antigens in their native physiological state, alternative panning strategies should be considered.

Liquid-phase panning

Liquid-phase panning is a technique in which antigens in solution are reacted with phages to selectively isolate antigen-phage complexes, thereby enriching for specific binders. Typically, antigens are biotinylated and subsequently recovered using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads or similar capture methods. This approach offers several advantages over solid-phase panning, as it enables the selection of binders to antigens in their more native conformations and enhanced epitope accessibility. This feature is particularly advantageous when targeting structurally sensitive molecules, such as cytokines or membrane protein fragments (Ferlin et al., 2010; Kenniston et al., 2014; Monk et al., 2004; Nastri et al., 2012).

A combined approach is also frequently employed, wherein solid-phase panning is initially used to rapidly and efficiently enrich for a diverse pool of antigen-binding candidates, followed by liquid-phase panning in subsequent rounds to select binders with high affinity and optimal compatibility with the antigen’s native structure (Baker et al., 2003; Benson et al., 2006). This strategy leverages the rapid screening efficiency of solid-phase panning while exploiting the structural precision of liquid-phase panning for refined selection.

However, liquid-phase panning also presents several limitations. The process requires additional procedural steps, including antigen biotinylation and magnetic bead handling, rendering it more complex than solid-phase methods. Furthermore, there is an inherent risk of losing potentially valuable binders during pull-down and washing steps due to incomplete capture of antigen-phage complexes. Liquid-phase panning is also less compatible with high-throughput screening formats and, similar to solid-phase panning, faces challenges in accurately reproducing the native structure of multi-pass transmembrane proteins.

Cell-based panning

Cell-based panning represents a phage display screening strategy that utilizes target antigen-expressing cells to isolate antibodies capable of recognizing antigens within their native cell surface context. This approach proves particularly advantageous for complex targets, including membrane proteins, whose conformational integrity is frequently compromised during the purification processes required for conventional solid-phase or liquid-phase panning methodologies. In cell-based panning, the target antigen is presented in its native conformation on the plasma membrane, thereby enabling the selection of antibodies with enhanced likelihood of binding to functional epitopes present in vivo. Furthermore, this method preserves critical PTMs, including glycosylation and phosphorylation, which are essential for maintaining the biological relevance and binding specificity of the selected antibodies.

Owing to these advantages, cell-based panning has been successfully employed in the development of therapeutic antibodies against membrane targets such as EGFR and CD22 (Li et al., 2008; Salvatore et al., 2002). Notably, even for secreted proteins such as IFN-γ, the therapeutic antibody Emapalumab was developed through an initial liquid-phase panning step, followed by cell-based panning using CHO cells engineered to overexpress IFN-γ on their surface via a pDisplay vector (Ferlin et al., 2010). This sequential approach facilitated the selection of antibodies exhibiting high specificity and functionality within a physiologically relevant context.

Despite its advantages in preserving the physiological structure of target antigens, cell-based panning presents several experimental challenges. The abundance of non-specific proteins on the cell surface contributes to elevated background noise, necessitating stringent negative selection protocols and multiple washing steps to minimize non-specific binding. Additionally, the experimental workflow is considerably more complex and time-consuming due to requirements for cell maintenance, transfection procedures, and cell line establishment. Quantitative control over antigen expression levels remains limited, thereby complicating direct comparisons of binding affinities between selected antibodies. Given that experimental outcomes can vary substantially depending on cell type, antigen expression levels, washing conditions, and phage recovery efficiency, meticulous optimization of experimental parameters is essential for successful implementation.

Recent technological advances have incorporated flow cytometry-based approaches, which enable quantitative analysis and sorting of phage particles bound to antigen-overexpressing cells based on fluorescent signal intensities (Czarnecka et al., 2025; Glaser et al., 2023). This enhancement facilitates more efficient antibody selection processes compared to traditional methods. Although cell-based panning remains technically more demanding than conventional panning approaches utilizing purified antigens, it continues to serve as a powerful platform for discovering functionally relevant antibodies, particularly within the context of therapeutic antibody development.

PHAGE DISPLAY WITH ADVANCED TECHNOLOGY

NGS-based screening of phage display library clones

Conventional phage display screening relies on isolating individual clones, validating their functional activity through ELISA, and performing Sanger sequencing on positive hits. This manual colony-picking approach is inherently limited in scope, typically analyzing only 96-384 clones at a time. Moreover, E. coli propagation bias often leads to preferential detection of relatively abundant clones in the final panning rounds, while rare variants with superior binding affinity, enhanced specificity, or favorable developability characteristics may remain undetected.

In contrast, next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based screening enables simultaneous profiling of millions of sequences from pooled samples, facilitating both detection and quantification of low-frequency clones that may exhibit superior affinity and specificity compared to more abundant variants (Barreto et al., 2019; Ljungars et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2017). Beyond throughput, NGS analysis across biopanning rounds enables quantitative monitoring of library enrichment (Vaisman-Mentesh and Wine, 2018; Yang et al., 2017). This approach enables early detection of aberrant selection patterns, such as premature diversity loss, thereby validating the panning process. By analyzing the amplification and depletion kinetics of individual sequences, this method also provides deeper insights into target-binding sequence motifs and potential antibody candidates (Braun et al., 2020).

Since NGS provides sequence information as pooled data, individual clones must be reconstructed, necessitating robust methods to identify functionally valid candidates from vast sequence repertoires. For example, AutomaTed Tool For Immunoglobulin Analysis (ATTILA) represents an automated bioinformatics workflow that processes round-by-round NGS data to identify valid antibody sequences by detecting canonical structural motifs within quality-filtered VH/VL sequences. Candidate prioritization is achieved through enrichment scoring based on frequency changes across rounds. This full-length sequence-based approach outperforms CDR3-focused methods in accurately identifying antibodies with improved binding affinity (Maranhão et al., 2020). Similarly, a statistical framework called the Position-Specific Enrichment Ratio Matrix (PSERM) showed stronger reproducibility and correlation with experimentally measured binding data than traditional frequency- or enrichment-based analyses (Smith et al., 2023). This approach quantitatively links antibody sequence variations to functional properties such as antigen-binding affinity and non-specific interactions by analyzing NGS data from both pre- and post-selection antibody libraries. By calculating enrichment ratios for each amino acid position within the CDRs and combining them into composite scores, PSERM captures how specific residues and their positional combinations contribute to functional outcomes.

Through NGS analysis, phage display screening has evolved from a low-throughput, clone-by-clone validation process into a quantitative and comprehensive exploration of sequence–function relationships. This advancement enables the identification of rare, high-affinity, and developable antibody variants that would have been missed by conventional approaches, while also providing deeper insights into how sequence diversity governs binding specificity and functional performance.

AI-driven phage display library design and developability assessment

In recent years, considerable efforts have been devoted to designing antibody libraries in silico through artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning-based methodologies. Traditional Position-Specific Scoring Matrix (PSSM)-based design approaches are inherently limited by their treatment of each amino acid position as independent. However, the integration of language models with Bayesian optimization enables more sophisticated sequence space exploration by capturing inter-residue correlations and dependencies, facilitating the generation of scFv libraries that exhibit both improved binding affinity and greater sequence diversity (Li et al., 2023).

Extending beyond binding optimization, a recent model integrates developability considerations directly into antibody library design. Trained on large-scale, high-quality antibody sequence datasets curated based on the physicochemical properties of marketed therapeutic antibodies, this model can generate novel and diverse sequences with medicine-like characteristics (Rajagopal et al., 2024). Antibodies produced through this AI-driven approach have demonstrated critical drug development properties, including high expression yields, enhanced thermal stability, minimal non-specific binding, and low propensity for self-aggregation. This represents a paradigm shift from random library exploration toward rational design, where candidates with favorable clinical developability profiles can be identified at the design stage itself.

AI is also increasingly applied to evaluate and prioritize the developability of candidate sequences after they are generated. These early-stage assessments—covering attributes such as aggregation, solubility, and immunogenicity—represent a critical strategy for shortening development timelines and minimizing failure rates in downstream clinical trials. Given that traditional experimental approaches are costly and time-consuming, sequence-based AI prediction technologies have emerged as complementary tools.

Protein aggregation is a major factor that compromises the stability and safety of biopharmaceuticals. To predict aggregation propensity, a framework has been proposed that utilizes AlphaFold-predicted structures from protein sequences combined with molecular dynamics simulations (Knez et al., 2025). This methodology integrates local surface curvature with hydrophobicity mapping, enabling highly accurate predictions (r = 0.91) of protein aggregation rates at high concentrations using only simple linear machine learning models. Complementing this structural approach, the CamSol approach enables quantitative prediction of protein solubility across pH conditions based solely on sequence information (Oeller et al., 2023). This allows for the in silico filtering of sequences prone to low solubility and high aggregation, while providing practical guidance for CDR replacement and site-directed mutagenesis design. The method can also predict solubility changes in response to formulation pH variations.

Beyond single-property predictions, several studies have demonstrated the value of integrating multiple AI-based assessments into antibody discovery workflows. Musnier et al. (2025) introduced a pipeline that incorporates AI-based characterization into the early phases of phage display screening. Functional ligand-binding sites were identified using MAbTope, a docking-based epitope mapping AI tool, while sequence- and structure-based AI analyses quantified key developability parameters such as PTM liability, humanness, and hydrophobic patch profiles. This integrated approach enabled early-stage elimination of candidates with elevated aggregation propensity, demonstrating that simultaneous consideration of target functionality and developability from the initial screening phase can substantially mitigate late-stage development failures.

Similarly, Sweet-Jones and Martin (2025) developed a protein language model (PLM)-based triage pipeline for large-scale antibody candidates from phage display screening. Using AntiBERTy, an antibody-specific language model, candidate sequences were embedded and compared against distributions of clinically approved versus failed antibodies for early developability assessment. A linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier, a machine learning classifier that separates data into distinct groups, was trained on these datasets to determine whether new candidates resembled the approved or failed population, overcoming affinity-focused selection biases.

Collectively, these AI-driven strategies combined with phage display represent a significant advancement in antibody development, enabling earlier identification of promising candidates with improved developability profiles and reduced late-stage attrition risk.

CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES

To date, most FDA-approved antibody therapeutics have been developed through animal immunization-based approaches, including hybridoma technology and transgenic animal systems. Animal-derived antibodies offer distinct advantages: they undergo natural affinity maturation in vivo, yielding antibodies with high binding affinity and specificity, while providing variable region sequences with pre-validated expression and stability profiles. Notably, the advent of humanization technologies and transgenic mouse platforms (e.g., XenoMouse, VelocImmune) has enabled the generation of fully human antibodies, establishing a robust track record of successful clinical translation and drug development.

In contrast, phage display technology enables direct in vitro selection of antibodies without animal immunization, offering rapid screening within several weeks at relatively lower costs. Importantly, this platform provides access to challenging targets that are unsuitable for or refractory to animal immunization, including toxic antigens, highly conserved proteins, and self-antigens. Building upon these advantages, phage display technology has emerged as a powerful in vitro platform for the efficient selection of antigen-specific antibodies, undergoing continuous advancement over the past three decades since its inception. Extensive efforts have been directed toward improving library design by increasing diversity and expression efficiency while minimizing undesirable PTMs and sequence liabilities. These advances have contributed to the successful development and regulatory approval of 20 therapeutic antibodies to date.

Nevertheless, as an in vitro platform, phage display harbors several inherent limitations. First, the absence of in vivo affinity maturation may result in initially selected antibodies exhibiting relatively lower binding affinity or specificity. Second, antibodies selected under simplified in vitro conditions may display unpredictable developability characteristics—including solubility, aggregation propensity, viscosity, nonspecific binding, and immunogenicity—when exposed to the complex physiological environment. Additional challenges include library size constraints, discrepancies between prokaryotic and eukaryotic expression systems, and limitations in antigen presentation and structural integrity during panning. Of particular concern is E. coli propagation bias, which can lead to the exclusion of high-affinity clones during selection. Finally, the bacteriophage-based nature of the platform necessitates rigorous control of trace contaminants, including residual phage proteins, bacterial endotoxins, and DNA fragments.

To address these limitations, various strategies have been actively pursued, including high-throughput screening, cell-based panning, structure-guided antibody engineering, and rational library design that balances sequence diversity with developability. Furthermore, NGS has enabled quantitative tracking of sequence evolution across panning rounds, facilitating the identification of rare, high-value clones that might otherwise be overlooked due to E. coli propagation bias, thereby yielding antibody candidates with superior binding and stability profiles.

Recently, AI and machine learning technologies have been increasingly integrated throughout the antibody discovery pipeline. When combined with NGS analysis, these computational approaches are being applied across multiple stages, including library design, biopanning process monitoring, and developability assessment of antibody candidates. Notably, AI-driven analysis enables precise selection of high-quality antibody candidates at early stages, thereby significantly reducing failure rates in clinical development and substantially enhancing the likelihood of regulatory approval.

Taken together, these technological advancements are progressively addressing the intrinsic limitations of phage display technology, positioning it as a pivotal platform for the development of next-generation, high-performance antibodies in the era of precision medicine.
