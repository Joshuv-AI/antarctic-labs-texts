Differential Diagnostic Value of Neutrophil Gelatinase-Associated Lipocalin and Cystatin C in Ischemic Stroke Patients with or without Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Ying Jin, Xiaohong Chen, Xiao Xiao, Xiaoyan He, Jie Tang, Yuwei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveThe similarities in organizational structure and microenvironment between the brain and kidneys suggest the potential utility of kidney biomarkers in the detection of cerebrovascular diseases. Cystatin C (CysC) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), well-established sensitive biomarkers of kidney injury, may also recognize as indicators of neuroinflammation. However, their diagnostic capabilities for ischemic stroke (IS) attacks under different kidney function states remain unclear. This case-control study aims to evaluate the diagnostic value of serum kidney biomarkers for ischemic stroke (IS) attack, with focus on NGAL and CysC.MethodsA total of 498 patients with first IS attack, 173 patients with risk-related diseases (designated as the disease control [DC] group), and 293 healthy subjects (serving as the healthy control [HC] group) were enrolled. A comprehensive comparative analysis was performed to examine the associations between common kidney biomarkers (Specifically, NGAL and CysC) and IS.ResultsSerum NGAL levels were significantly elevated in patients with first IS compared with both the HC group (z=5.964, P<0.001) and the DC group (z=12.191, P<0.001). In contrast, serum CysC levels were significantly higher in these patients relative to the HC group (z=5.762, P<0.001), but no statistically significant difference was observed when compared with the DC group (z=1.663, P=0.289). Partial correlation analysis revealed: 1) among IS patients with normal kidney function, NGAL exhibited the strongest partial correlation with IS (rpartial=0.341, P<0.001), whereas the other four kidney markers showed no statistically significant association (all P>0.05); 2) among IS patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), CysC showed the highest partial correlation (rpartial=0.460, P<0.001), followed by estimated glomerular filtration rate (rpartial=−0.373, P<0.001), creatinine (rpartial=0.279, P<0.001), NGAL (rpartial=0.233, P<0.001), and urea (rpartial=0.182, P<0.001). Stratified multiple linear regression analysis based on kidney impairment demonstrated: 1) in patients with preserved kidney function, only NGAL was correlated with IS risk (OR=6.54, P<0.001), with moderate diagnostic effect (AUC=0.734, P<0.001); and 2) for CKD patients, CysC outperformed NGAL in diagnosing IS attack, demonstrating a stronger correlation with IS risk (OR=5.97, P<0.001) and a higher discriminatory ability (AUC=0.835, P<0.001).ConclusionIS is intricately linked to both kidney injury and neuroinflammation. NGAL and CysC serve as appropriate biomarkers for diagnosing IS attack in patients with normal kidney function and those with CKD, respectively. Respective monitoring of CysC and NGAL in individuals with and without CKD could facilitate early diagnosis, prevention and targeted management of stroke in high-risk populations.Keywords: ischemic stroke, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, cystatin C, kidney injury, neuroinflammation

Introduction

Stroke is an acute cerebrovascular event characterized by sudden disruption of cerebral blood flow. According to the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guidelines, strokes are classified into hemorrhagic (HS) and ischemic (IS) subtypes, with IS being accounted for approximately 87% of cases. The World Stroke Organization report identifies stroke as the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of death and disability,1 severely affecting health, survival, and long-term quality of life even among survivors. The brain and kidney share similar microvascular structures and susceptibility to ischemia-reperfusion injury, enabling bidirectional pathophysiological crosstalk. Shared risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and hyperuricemia,2–4 link stroke with both chronic kidney disease (CKD) and acute kidney injury (AKI). Consequently, stroke and kidney injury frequently co-occur and exacerbate each other’s risk. Emerging evidence indicates that impaired kidney function is strongly associated with increased stroke incidence, recurrence, and adverse outcomes.5,6 Some clinicians have begun incorporating kidney markers into predicting stroke risk.7 However, conventional markers such as serum creatinine (Cre) have notable limitations: they rely on renal compensatory capacity and typically only elecate after more than 50% of kidney function is compromised, thereby reducing sensitivity for early detection. As a result, Cre levels may remain/appear normal despite underlying stroke pathology. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) offers greater specificity and reliability as an indicator of renal function. However, its role as a diagnostic biomarker for stroke remains debated,7,8 with conflicting evidence regarding its prognostic utility.9 Therefore, the diagnostic value of these traditional kidney biomarkers for IS attack requires confirmation through more rigorous research designs.

In recent years, as awareness of kidney impairment has grown, new biomarkers have become increasingly important in diagnosing nephropathy. Among them, cystatin C (CysC) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) have received significant attention. CysC is considered an ideal endogenous marker for assessing glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and has gained global recognition since it was recommended by the FDA in 2002. Several studies have reported that CysC may predict ischemic stroke (IS),10,11 potentially mediated through mechanisms involving inflammation, chemotaxis of cells and cytokines, activation of autophagy, and lipid oxidation and deposition.12 However, current clinical guidelines and consensus statements do not endorse the routine measurement of CysC in clinical practice.

NGAL is a recently discovered biomarker that can predict AKI onset, reflect its severity, and forecast disease prognosis. As a higher sensitivity and specificity than Cre in early AKI diagnosis, it has been recommended as a potential AKI marker by the tenth Acute Dialysis Quality Initiative Consensus Conference.13 Recent clinical utilities show that NGAL not only enables early prediction of AKI but also reflects CKD progression, with serum levels rising as CKD advances.14 Meanwhile, studies have also identified NGAL as a marker of neuroinflammation and related behavioral dysfunction.15–17 In the brain, NGAL is predominantly expressed by neurogliocytes and critically regulates their functional phenotypic specification.18 Increased NGAL can disrupt the blood-brain barrier and cause white matter injury via activating microglia and reactive astrocytes, promoting neutrophil chemotactic infiltration, thrombin-/erythrocyte lysate–induced brain injury and microvascular thrombosis, thereby amplify post-stroke inflammation, even neuronal death.19 During the IS recovery period, increased NGAL in brain tissue can induce astrogliosis and post-stroke emotional disorders,20 and exacerbate amyloid-β-induced toxicity, contributing to neurodegenerative changes in the brainstem nucleus.21 Iron chelators may help mitigate such changes and treat CNS diseases, though their neuroprotective effects might depend on NGAL levels.22 Clinically, some patients exhibit mild neuroinflammation and behavioral dysfunction before stroke onset, with symptoms worsening as stroke progresses.

Given that the accumulating evidence suggests a potential bidirectional correlation between kidney injury and IS, along with their shared underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, NGAL and CysC can reflect both kidney function and neuroinflammatory processes, and emerge as independent predictors of both early kidney dysfunction and IS attack. However, it remains unclear whether the clinical utilities of NGAL and CysC is consistent among IS patients with or without chronic kidney disease. To address this gap, this study aims to investigate the clinical utilities of NGAL and CysC in diagnosing the occurrence of IS, particularly in the context of the presence or absence of chronic kidney function impairment.

Materials and Methods

Participants

IS patients: From March 2021 to October 2024, a total of 498 patients firstly diagnosed with IS were envolled in this study at the Stroke Center of Mianyang Central Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) first occurrence of IS symptoms such as limb weakness or numbness, facial numbness or drooping, slurred speech or language difficulty, gaze deviation, vision loss or blurring, dizziness with vomiting, sudden severe headache without prior history, vomiting, altered consciousness, or seizures; 2) diagnosis consistent with the Chinese Stroke Association guidelines for clinical management of ischaemic cerebrovascular diseases,23 cranial CT excluded hemorrhage but showed low-density cortical infarcts, wedge-shaped lacunar lesions, blurred boundaries between cortex and white matter, or involvement of the lenticular nucleus or insular lobe, without intracranial space-occupying lesions, or diffusion-weighted MRI revealed cortical punctate or flaky hyperintensities (acute lesions); and 3) availability of ACR and eGFR records from within 3 to 6 months prior to admission. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) HS patients who met the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guidelines for the Management of Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage;24 2) patients with recurrent IS; 3) IS patients who developed secondary AKI within 48 hours of admission, as defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 criteria,25 including an increase in serum creatinine of ≥26.5 μmol/L within 48 hours or urine output of <0.5 mL/kg/h for more than 6 hours; 4) patients with a history of pre-existing chronic kidney disease; and 5) patients without complete records of ACR and eGFR within three months prior to admission.

Disease Controls (DC): A total of 173 patients with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and/or hyperuricemia were enrolled during the same period as the IS patients. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients with a history of brain trauma within one month prior to enrollment; 2) patients with primary kidney disease; 3) patients with secondary AKI; and 4) incomplete records of ACR and eGFR within 3 to 6 months before admission.

Healthy controls (HC): During the same period, 293 volunteers with normal liver and kidney function and negative hematuria were randomly recruited to serve as healthy controls. Their serum levels of alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase were within the normal range, defined as <40 U/L.

Sample Collection and Kidney Function Assay

Blood samples from IS patients were collected immediately upon admission, while HC and DC subjects were sampled in the morning. Approximately 5 mL of venous blood was drawn into BD Vacutainer® SST II Advance tubes, gently mixed, and allowed to stand for approximately 30 minutes. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 1017 × g for 15 minutes to separate the serum. The obtained serum was used for the measurement of urea (Ure), Cre, CysC, and NGAL within 2 hours.

Ure was adopted the urease method, Cre was adopted the sarcosine oxidase method, and CysC and NGAL were adopted the transmission turbidimetric method, and were measured on the LAbOSPECT 008AS automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, Japan). The assay kits were provided by the Maccura Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The eGFR of subjects was calculated using the CKD-EPI CysC-Cr equation applicable to the Chinese population.26

CKD Diagnosis

All selected IS and DC patients had ACR and eGFR records available within 3 to 6 months prior to hospital admission. CKD was diagnosed when at least two records of ACR>30 mg/g and/or eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m2 were documented, based on measurements conducted every 1 to 2 months. The Cre and CysC used for eGFR calculation were measured using the same methodology and instrumentation as previously described. ACR was calculated as the ratio of urinary albumin to urinary creatinine. Urinary albumin was quantified using an improved immunoturbidimetric assay, and urinary creatinine was determined via the sarcosine oxidase method, both performed on an A25 automated specific protein analyzer (Biosystems, Spain). The two assay kits were supplied by Chongqing Biostec Biological Technology Co., Ltd.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS version 22.0 and MedCalc version 22.3 were employed for data analysis. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range, while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. The χ2-test was used to compare differences in medical history between IS and DC patients. Differences in kidney biomarker levels across groups, as well as pairwise comparisons, were assessed using the independent-samples Kruskal–Wallis test and post-hoc multiple comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Using the HC and DC groups as controls (designated as the no-IS group), and considering all IS patients and the stratified subgroups with or without chronic kidney disease (designated respectively as the NKF-IS group or CKD-IS group) as the subjects, partial correlation analysis based on multiple linear regression was conducted to assess the partial correlations between kidney biomarkers/risk diseases and IS attack. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the performance of kidney biomarkers for diagnosing IS, with the area under the curve (AUC) serving as a measure of accuracy. Using DC patients as the reference group, stratified cross-tabulation analysis was applied to estimate the risk correlated with kidney function markers and IS, with odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Kidney Marker Levels

The Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to evaluate differences in five kidney biomarkers among the three groups. At the overall level, significant intergroup differences were identified for Cre (χ2=36.695, P<0.001), CysC (χ2=46.030, P<0.001), eGFR (χ2=34.072, P<0.001), and NGAL (χ2=153.953, P<0.001), while Ure levels did not differ significantly across groups (χ2=3.862, P=0.145) (Figure 1). The Post-Hoc pairwise analyses revealed that Cre (z=4.482 and 5.734, both P<0.001), CysC (z=5.955 and 5.762, both P<0.001), and NGAL (z=3.872 and 12.191, both P<0.001) levels were significantly elevated in both the DC and IS groups, respectively, relative to the HC group. Conversely, eGFR values were significantly reduced in the DC and IS groups compared to the HC group (z=−4.180 and −5.587, both P<0.001). Furthermore, NGAL levels in the IS group were significantly higher than those in the DC group (z=5.964, P<0.001). No statistically significant differences were detected between the DC and IS groups for Cre (z=0.085, P=0.932), CysC (z=1.663, P=0.289), or eGFR (z=0.120, P=0.905). Among the five kidney biomarkers, only NGAL levels exhibited a consistent elevation across the process from healthy status to disease-related risk and ultimately to ischemic stroke, highlighting its potential as a dynamic marker of pathological progression.

Diagnostic Performance of Kidney Biomarkers on the IS Attack

To observe the impact of two types of control groups on diagnostic performance analysis, the HC, DC, and no-IS (ie, HC+DC) groups were used as reference groups, respectively. For the overall IS patients, NGAL demonstrated the strongest but modest performance in diagnosing IS, with AUC of 0.749, 0.761, and 0.711 (z=10.010~15.347, all P<0.001) when the no-IS, healthy control (HC), and disease control (DC) groups were used as reference groups, respectively (Figure 2A–C). These AUC values were significantly higher than those of other biomarkers (z=5.243~8.271, all P<0.001), with no significant differences observed among the three NGAL AUCs themselves (z=0.543~1.860, all P>0.05). In addition, CysC, Cr, and eFDR also demonstrated poor performance in diagnosing IS only when HC and no-IS groups were used as reference groups (AUC=0.529~0.621, all P<0.001). These findings suggested that NGAL outperformed the other four kidney biomarkers in diagnosing IS in individuals both with and without established risk factors.

Kidney Dysfunction and IS Risk

Using the aforementioned cutoff values for CysC and NGAL, along with the medical decision thresholds for Ure, Cre, and eGFR, we assessed the correlation between their detection abnormality rates and IS attack risk. Among all patients, increased NGAL (OR=4.08, P<0.001), increased CysC (OR=1.98, P=0.006), and decreased eGFR (OR=2.41, P=0.034) were significantly correlated with an increased risk of IS attack (Table 4). Following stratification by kidney function status, elevated NGAL (OR=6.54, P<0.001) emerged as the only significant risk factor for IS attack among patients with normal kidney function. In contrast, among patients with chronic kidney disease, elevated CysC (OR=5.97, P<0.001) and reduced eGFR (OR=3.28, P=0.016) were correlated with higher IS risk. The statistical power of these significant findings ranged from 0.865 to 1.000. These findings suggest that monitoring NGAL may be particularly informative in individuals with normal kidney function, whereas CysC and eGFR should be prioritized in those with impaired kidney function.

Discussion

Numerous studies investigate the association between kidney injury and IS attack.27–29 Evidence has established a bidirectional relationship between these conditions: IS can lead to kidney injury, and preexisting kidney injury may increase IS risk.29,30 Given the structural and functional similarities in the microvascular system between brain and kidney, pathological factors affecting the kidney may also lead to damage in brain tissue.31 Therefore, some clinicians propose using conventional kidney biomarkers to predict the IS attack in patients with chronic kidney disease. Our study demonstrates that conventional serum kidney biomarkers (such as Ure, Cre, and eGFR) exhibit limited or no diagnostic capability and are ineffective in differentiating ischemic stroke patients from healthy individuals and/or those with predisposing conditions. In contrast, CysC may emerge as a valuable serum biomarker for diagnosing IS attack in patients with chronic kidney disease, whereas NGAL shows promise as a diagnostic biomarker in individuals with normal kidney function.

However, our review of the previous research on the role of serum kidney biomarkers in diagnosing IS reveals that the findings remain inconclusive and unsatisfactory. Several studies have confirmed that reduced eGFR and elevated ACR are risk factors for non-fatal IS,32 with eGFR demonstrating a comparatively weaker diagnostic value for IS than ACR.33 Among emerging markers in recent years, increased CysC levels have been comfired to be associated with higher IS risk,34,35 while also demonstrating potential protective effects against neurodegenerative processes.36 In contrast, NGAL has not demonstrated significant diagnostic role for either the onset or recurrence of IS during the recovery phase.37 Consequently, it remains uncertain which specific aspects of kidney dysfunction contribute most significantly to IS risk, or which serum markers offer the most reliable prediction of IS occurrence.

Unlike most previous studies, this integrative analysis includes two control groups: disease and healthy controls. The disease controls cover four conditions associated with IS risk, specifically hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and hyperuricemia. The matched prevalence between the disease controls and IS group helps minimize bias arising from underlying chronic diseases. Healthy controls are included to account for asymptomatic conditions predisposing individuals to IS without overt symptoms, such as atrial fibrillation and patent foramen ovale,12 which often go undiagnosed and may lead to misclassification as healthy. This comprehensive control design enhances the robustness of the analysis and facilitates a clearer understanding of the correlation between kidney dysfunction and IS events.

After incorporating age, gender, and four IS-related chronic comorbidities as confounding factors, our correlation analysis between five serum kidney biomarkers (Ure, Cr, CysC, NGAL, and eGFR) and IS events reveals: Among IS patients with chronic kidney disease, these kidney biomarkers demonstrate more robust correlations with IS events compared to the aforementioned chronic conditions. Although most kidney biomarkers do not show statistically significant correlations in IS patients with normal kidney function, NGAL remains the biomarker most robustly linked to IS events. These findings suggest a more substantial correlation between kidney dysfunction and IS occurrence compared to the other chronic comorbidities, indicating a potential contributory role of impaired kidney function in the development of IS.

However, further ROC analysis reveals limited or negligible diagnostic performance of Ure, Cre, and eGFR for IS events in patients with undetermined kidney function. These biomarkers demonstrate relatively high diagnostic accuracy only among IS patients with chronic kidney disease. Meanwhile, the cut-off values of these biomarkers lie within the reference range and are proximal to the midpoint, which limits their clinical utility for diagnosing IS events. In contrast, CysC demonstrates the strongest diagnostic capability in IS patients with chronic kidney disease, and only NGAL demonstrates moderate diagnostic value in IS patients with normal kidney function. Interestingly, the cut-off values of NGAL and CysC were extremely close to their respective reference upper limits both in IS patients with or without chronic kidney disease. Thus, both NGAL and CysC may serve as effective biomarkers for diagnosing IS events, although their clinical applicability varies according to kidney function status.

NGAL is newly recognized as an early biomarker for acute and chronic kidney injury, and emerging evidence suggests its potential role in neuroinflammation.38 Our study shows a progressive increase in NGAL levels from healthy individuals to high-risk populations and further to IS patients. This finding suggests that NGAL may reflect systemic or neuroinflammatory processes. Subsequent ROC analysis stratified by kidney function shows that NGAL can predict IS events in patients with and without CKD. Notably, in those with normal kidney function, NGAL is the only biomarker with significant diagnostic performance, highlighting its potential as a neuroinflammatory indicator. However, since NGAL is more sensitive to kidney injury than the other four biomarkers, it remains unclear whether subclinical kidney dysfunction contributes to IS onset in these patients. Fortunately, recent animal provide supporting evidence for our understanding of neuroinflammation, indicating that in early-stage or transient ischemic stroke, inflammatory or stress-induced alterations in brain tissue may lead to elevated expression of NGAL.39 Additionally, relevant evidence of NGAL in the context of neuroinflammation has been discovered in studies involving human patients with Alzheimer’s disease and schizophrenia.15,40 A few research reports that NGAL can be used to predict the recurrence of transient ischemia or stroke.37,41

Our study also indicates that CysC may serve as a particularly valuable predictor of IS in patients with chronic kidney disease. CysC is recognized by the Food and Drug Administration in 2002 as a reliable biomarker for glomerular filtration rate, and has been demonstrated to be more sensitive in detecting early kidney impairment. Several animal studies has comfired its ability to reflect neuroinflammation. During cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, elevated levels of endogenous CysC contribute to the preservation of lysosomal membrane integrity, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects.36 Meanwhile, our research reveals that the other four kidney biomarkers also exhibit high diagnostic performance in patients with chronic kidney disease, indicating a potential role of kidney injury in IS events. Therefore, CysC demonstrates the strongest diagnostic capacity among IS patients with chronic kidney disease, attributable not only to its correlation with neuroinflammation but also likely to its high sensitivity in detecting early kidney impairment.

Limitations

First, without baseline creatinine levels, we could not exclude patients with acute kidney injury whose creatinine increased by more than 1.5 times within 7 days. Second, administering medication to patients with high-risk diseases and inaccurate recall of 7-day medication use before admission from patients with altered consciousness may introduce potential measured bias. Third, it must be confirmed whether the investigation conclusion for initial IS attack applies to other situations. Finally, due to the suddenness of the IS attack, the timing of blood sampling varied, which may potentially introduce bias into the analysis. Additionally, this study did not account for subclinical kidney dysfunction; well-designed investigations are required to evaluate the clinical significance of NGAL and CysC in this context.

Conclusions

There remains considerable controversy in the current reports regarding the diagnostic value of serum CysC and NGAL for IS attack. This study, which carefully stratified patients according to kidney function status, demonstrated that CysC exhibits a significant diagnostic advantage for IS events in individuals with chronic kidney disease. In contrast, among populations with normal kidney function, including apparently healthy individuals, NGAL shows measurable diagnostic capacity. These findings offer valuable biomarker insights for the early diagnosis of IS and may contribute to enhanced prevention and monitoring strategies.
