Effectiveness of a virtual reality simulation based on experiential learning theory for enhancing diabetic ketoacidosis management competencies: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Gyuli Baek, Eunju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a life-threatening complication requiring rapid assessment and specialized nursing. This study evaluated a virtual reality (VR) simulation program grounded in Kolb’s experiential learning theory to enhance nursing students’ DKA care competencies.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 44 nursing students (experimental: n = 22, control: n = 22) recruited via convenience sampling at a university in Korea. Participants were assigned using simple randomization. The primary outcomes were problem-solving ability and performance confidence; secondary outcomes included class evaluation, simulation design evaluation, and practice immersion. The experimental group received a VR-based program (lecture, VR, PBL, debriefing), while the control group engaged in e-book-based case simulation. The intervention was explicitly designed based on the four stages of Kolb’s experiential learning theory.ResultsThere were significant interaction effects between group and time for primary outcomes, indicating the experimental group had significantly greater improvements in problem-solving ability (F = 11.51, p = .002) and performance confidence (F = 4.87, p = .033) compared to the control group. For secondary outcomes, the experimental group scored significantly higher in class evaluation (t = 5.60, p < .001), simulation design evaluation (t = 5.88, p < .001), and practice immersion (t = 3.612, p = .001).ConclusionThe VR simulation program based on experiential learning theory was superior to e-book-based case simulation in enhancing students’ problem-solving skills and clinical confidence in DKA care. These findings support integrating VR simulation into nursing curricula as a superior pedagogical strategy to improve clinical readiness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08970-w.Keywords: Virtual reality simulation, Experiential learning theory, Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), Nursing education

Introduction

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a life-threatening metabolic emergency necessitating rapid clinical judgment and precise intervention [1]. Nurses play a critical role in managing the complex dehydration and hyperglycemia associated with DKA [2–4]. However, insufficient clinical experience and training gaps remain significant barriers to patient safety [5]. While simulation-based education is common, traditional human patient simulators often struggle to replicate the subtle, rapidly evolving physiological shifts characteristic of DKA [5]. Virtual reality (VR) simulation addresses these limitations by providing high-fidelity, immersive environments for practicing high-risk scenarios without clinical risk [6].

Recent work underscores both the complexity of DKA management and the central role of well-prepared nurses. Implementation of evidence-based clinical guidelines has been shown to improve pediatric DKA outcomes [7], and high-fidelity simulation is known to enhance students’ self-awareness and confidence [8–10]. However, recent findings reveal critical gaps in nurses’ knowledge and attitudes toward DKA, suggesting an urgent need for innovative, immersive educational strategies specifically targeting this condition [11].

Despite the potential of VR, improving problem-solving in DKA care requires more than immersion; it necessitates a structured pedagogical framework to convert experience into sound clinical judgment. **** Kolb’s experiential learning theory provides this structure by aligning DKA’s clinical complexity with a four-stage cognitive cycle [12]. In this study, VR facilitates ‘Concrete Experience’ of an acute crisis, while subsequent debriefing promotes ‘Reflective Observation’ of the patient’s metabolic data. This enables students to form ‘Abstract Conceptualizations’ of DKA protocols, which they then apply through ‘Active Experimentation’ in repeat scenarios. While VR has been utilized for general skills, research applying this theory-driven cycle specifically to VR-based DKA management through a randomized design remains scarce.

This study aimed to evaluate a Kolb-based VR simulation program to fill this gap. The following hypotheses were tested:

Study design and participants

This study employed a single-blind, randomized controlled trial design to evaluate the effects of a virtual reality simulation program, based on Kolb’s experiential learning theory, on nursing students’ problem-solving ability and performance confidence in managing diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). To minimize assessment bias, the study was single-blinded; the data analyst was kept unaware of the group assignments throughout the data processing and statistical analysis stages. Participants were third- and fourth-year nursing students from K University in D metropolitan city who had no visual or hearing impairments and voluntarily consented to participate after being informed of the study’s purpose and procedures. Data collection was conducted from May 20 to June 30, 2025. The required sample size, calculated using G*Power 3.1.9 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) for a two-group repeated measures ANOVA (effect size = 0.25, power = 0.80, α = 0.05), was 34 students. Considering dropout rates of 10–30% reported in prior VR simulation studies [13, 14], the sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in 44 participants (22 per group). Recruitment was conducted through announcements on school bulletin boards and social networks, and applicants were accepted on a first-come, first-served basis. Although this recruitment method may introduce selection bias, it was utilized to ensure efficient recruitment of the target population; this potential bias is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Participants were randomly assigned to the experimental or control group using the Social Psychology Network’s Research Randomizer (www.randomizer.org). Allocation concealment was strictly maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. These envelopes were prepared by a research assistant not involved in data collection and were opened only after the participant had completed the informed consent process and the pre-test. No dropouts occurred during the intervention or follow-up periods; consequently, data from all 44 participants (22 in each group) were included in the final analysis. The study flow, including recruitment, allocation, and analysis, is detailed in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1). Cooperation was requested to prevent information exchange between groups. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Problem-solving ability

Problem-solving ability was assessed using the Korean version of the tool originally developed by Lee [15] for high school students and later modified and validated for college students by Park and Woo [16]. The scale consists of 25 items, with total scores ranging from 25 to 125. Scores were calculated as the mean across all items, where higher scores indicate greater problem-solving ability. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = “very rarely”; 5 = “very often”). In Park and Woo’s [16] study, Cronbach’s α was 0.90, and it was 0.92 in the present study.

Performance confidence

Performance confidence was measured using an instrument originally developed by Yoo [17] and subsequently revised by Song [18] to evaluate nursing care for patients with endocrine disorders. This study utilized the validated Korean version. The tool comprises 15 items across three subdomains: preparatory knowledge (4 items), patient assessment (5 items), and nursing performance (6 items). The total score ranges from 15 to 75, and the mean score was used for analysis. Higher mean scores reflect greater overall confidence in providing nursing care across all three subdomains. Cronbach’s α was 0.96 at the time of development, 0.93 in Song [18], and 0.90 in the present study.

Class evaluation

To evaluate the educational experience, class evaluation was assessed post-intervention for both groups. For the control group, the items were adapted to evaluate their e-book-based case simulation experience. The instrument used was originally developed by Ko et al. [19] and later revised for simulation learning by Yoo [20]. The instrument consists of 12 items across four subdomains: class management (2 items), teaching methods and materials (5 items), objectivity of evaluation (3 items), and class satisfaction (2 items). Scores range from 12 to 60, calculated as a mean score. Cronbach’s α was 0.89 in Yoo [20] and 0.86 in the present study.

Simulation design evaluation

The simulation design was assessed for both groups post-intervention to compare the quality of the high-fidelity VR simulation and the low-fidelity e-book-based case simulation. We used the Korean version of the Simulation Design Scale, originally developed by the National League for Nursing and validated in Korean by Yoo [20]. To ensure face validity for the control group, the instructions for the scale were slightly adapted (e.g., replacing “VR simulation” with “e-book-based case simulation session”). The tool comprises 21 items across five subdomains: learning objectives and content (6 items), support (4 items), problem-solving (5 items), feedback (4 items), and fidelity/realism (2 items). The total score ranges from 21 to 105, with the mean score used for analysis. Cronbach’s α was 0.92 in the original study, 0.90 in Yoo [20], and 0.78 in the present study.

Practice immersion

Practice immersion was measured using the Simulation Learning Immersion Scale developed and validated by Ko et al. [21]. This scale was administered to both groups post-intervention to compare psychological engagement between the different simulation modalities. The instrument comprises 16 items across four subdomains: cognitive absorption (7 items), presence (3 items), concentration (3 items), and autotelic experience (3 items). Total scores range from 16 to 80, and scores were calculated as the mean across items. Cronbach’s $\alpha$ was 0.89 at the time of development and 0.88 in the present study.

Intervention

Development of a VR simulation nursing education program for DKA care based on experiential learning theory

The nursing education program in this study was developed using Kolb’s [12] experiential learning model as the instructional design framework. In experiential learning theory, “experience” refers to the knowledge and actions used to evaluate and resolve a given situation, and “learning” is the process through which experience is trans-formed to create knowledge [22, 23]. This emphasizes that learning is facilitated when learners actively experience situations and independently consider solutions, highlighting in-dividual differences in how they manipulate information and the role of experience in learning. According to Kolb [12], learning occurs through a cyclical process of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation, in which all four stages must be experienced. Moreover, this cyclical process fosters improvement across affective, perceptual, cognitive, and behavioral domains when learners reflect critically on a given situation [22]. Accordingly, the program in this study was de-signed to incorporate all four stages of the experiential learning cycle to enhance nursing students’ problem-solving abilities.

The first stage is concrete experience, which stimulates the senses and provides direct experience [22, 23]. In this study, the program comprised a 90-minute lecture and 30-minute three-dimensional VR simulation. The VR simulation was delivered via a head-mounted display (HMD; Meta Quest 2) to ensure high-fidelity immersion, and the software was custom-developed using the Unity 3D engine, specifically focusing on DKA clinical scenarios. A panel of experts, comprising one endocrinologist and three nurses, provided input to confirm the content validity of the VR simulation scenarios and ensure realism.

The second stage is reflective observation, which involves linking sensory experiences and understanding to facilitate the formation of long-term memory. During the VR simulation, students served as practicing nurses and performed assigned missions related to patient problems, thereby reinforcing knowledge through application. Successful mission completion allowed progression to the next stage, whereas in the case of failure, students were required to repeat the video and mission to encourage repeated practice and memory consolidation.

The third stage is abstract conceptualization, through which learners analyze new information, modify existing knowledge, make decisions, and plan actions [22]. This stage requires concentration and cognitive engagement [22]. In this study, students engaged in small-group problem-based learning (PBL) discussions to analyze new information, refine existing knowledge, and deliberate on nursing problems presented in the scenarios. The educational objectives included advancing from knowledge acquisition to comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation, such as identifying symptoms of DKA; establishing appropriate and prioritized nursing diagnoses; assessing vital signs, laboratory results, and clinical findings in patients with hypo/hyperglycemia; and discussing safe medication administration and nursing interventions. Students also explored insulin education for patients with hypoglycemia, applied nursing processes, and practiced therapeutic communication with team members.

The final stage is active experimentation, in which learners apply the conclusions drawn from the learning process to new experiences. In this study, students engaged in reflective journaling, instructor feedback, and structured debriefing based on Ko and Choi’s [24] SENSE model, which consists of five steps: Share, Explore, Notice, Support, and Extend. Example questions included, “What did you feel during the game?” (Share), “What was challenging or emotionally difficult during the game?” (Explore), “What interfered with your engagement or immersion, and why?” (Notice), “If you felt stressed, how about taking a deep breath and relaxing for a moment? What aspects of your performance would you like to praise yourself for?” (Support), and “How do you think what you learned in this program will help in clinical practice? What specific aspects would you like to apply in which situations?” (Extend). The students then reviewed the nursing process required for DKA care and planned how they could apply the learned knowledge and skills in real clinical settings.

Intervention protocol

The intervention group participated in a four-session nursing education program conducted in a single day to ensure learning continuity and prevent experimental decay. Session I consisted of a 90-minute theoretical lecture. Session II included a 30-minute VR simulation. Before Session II, a 10-minute orientation was provided to familiarize students with the HMD hardware, as none of the participants had prior professional VR simulation experience. Session III involved 30-minute small-group problem-based learning (PBL) discussions. Session IV provided a 30-minute structured debriefing based on the SENSE model.

The control group received the same 90-minute theoretical lecture as the intervention group. Subsequently, they participated in a 30-minute e-book-based case simulation, which served as a low-fidelity simulation activity. This e-book-based case simulation included DKA patient scenarios that required learners to select and apply appropriate nursing processes. To ensure structural and educational equivalence with the intervention group, the control group then completed an individual reflective journal for 30 min and participated in a 30-minute summary and review session led by the instructor, which functioned as a paper-based PBL process. Consequently, both groups were provided with a total of 180 min of structured learning time, with identical instructor contact time (120 min total, including the lecture and feedback/summary sessions) to ensure consistency in study intensity.

Immediately after the intervention, both groups completed questionnaires to assess their problem-solving abilities, performance confidence, and practice immersion. They also evaluated the instructional design through the class evaluation and simulation design evaluation scales. To ensure face validity for the control group, the instructions for the simulation-related scales were slightly adapted (e.g., replacing “VR simulation” with “e-book-based case simulation session”).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to the main analysis, the normality of the data distribution was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Homogeneity between the two groups was examined using chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. The baseline homogeneity of the dependent variables was verified using independent $t$-tests. The effects of the intervention on the dependent variables were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. The assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test. Regarding missing data, as no dropouts occurred during the intervention and data collection periods, all 44 participants were included in the final analysis without the need for imputation. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the researcher’s affiliated institution (IRB No. 40525-202501-HR-087-03). All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits before enrollment and provided written informed consent. Participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Additionally, the trial was retrospectively registered at the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS) of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (Registration No. KCT0011670; https://cris.nih.go.kr).

No statistically significant differences were found between the experimental and control groups in terms of general characteristics. In addition, the pre-test homogeneity assessment for the dependent variables—problem-solving ability and performance confidence—showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups, confirming homogeneity (Table 1).

Differences in problem-solving ability and performance confidence

The problem-solving ability of the experimental group increased from 3.25 ± 0.31 before the intervention to 4.34 ± 0.47 after the intervention, which was higher than that of the control group (3.39 ± 0.45 before and 3.84 ± 0.40 after). Statistically significant differences were found by group (F = 4.96, p = .031), by time point (F = 64.81, p < .001), and for the interaction between group and time point (F = 11.51, p = .002).

The performance confidence of the experimental group increased from 3.55 ± 0.42 before the intervention to 3.99 ± 0.63 after, whereas the control group decreased from 4.08 ± 0.46 to 3.46 ± 0.61. Statistically significant differences were observed by group (F = 15.85, p < .001), by time point (F = 23.22, p < .001), and for the interaction between group and time point (F = 4.87, p = .033) (Table 2).

Post-intervention secondary outcomes

As shown in Table 3, the experimental group reported significantly higher scores across all secondary variables compared to the control group. Specifically, the class evaluation score of the experimental group (4.57 ± 0.56) was significantly higher than that of the control group (3.68 ± 0.49; t = 5.60, p < .001). Similarly, the simulation design evaluation score was significantly higher in the experimental group (4.42 ± 0.56 vs. 3.55 ± 0.40; t = 5.88, p < .001). The practice immersion score was also significantly higher in the experimental group (4.54 ± 0.50) compared to the control group (4.33 ± 0.35; t = 3.612, p = .001). These results indicate that the high-fidelity VR simulation provided a more engaging and effectively designed instructional environment than the e-book-based case simulation.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the effectiveness of a VR simulation nursing education program based on Kolb’s experiential learning model for nursing students caring for patients with DKA. The main findings demonstrated significant quantitative improvements in the experimental group compared to the control group. Specifically, problem-solving ability scores in the experimental group increased by 1.09 points (from 3.25 ± 0.31 to 4.34 ± 0.47), whereas the control group showed only a 0.45-point increase (t = 3.814, p < .001). Performance confidence also showed a marked difference; while the experimental group improved by 0.44 points (3.55 ± 0.42 to 3.99 ± 0.63), the control group experienced a decrease of 0.62 points (4.08 ± 0.46 to 3.46 ± 0.61), resulting in a significant interaction effect (F = 4.87, p = .033).

The program’s success in enhancing problem-solving abilities is consistent with previous studies reporting that VR simulations enhance skills by enabling repetitive practice and increasing presence [25, 26]. While VR-based education may exacerbate learning gaps depending on prior knowledge [27], this study addressed the issue by integrating the first two stages of Kolb’s [12] experiential learning model—concrete experience and reflective observation [22, 23]. Furthermore, collaboration with clinical and computer science experts ensured high fidelity, which likely contributed to these improvements [28].

The improvement in performance confidence aligns with studies showing that VR-based training for nursing skills can enhance confidence more effectively than traditional methods [29–31]. To address potential anxiety or negative experiences associated with VR [32], this study ensured adequate orientation and structured debriefing. Specifically, small-group PBL discussions facilitated abstract conceptualization, while structured debriefing based on the SENSE model [24] allowed learners to reflect critically and consolidate learning, promoting metacognition and confidence [33].

The program also had positive effects on class evaluation, simulation design evaluation, and practice immersion. These findings are supported by previous studies indicating that experiential learning-based VR simulations increase engagement and satisfaction [34–36]. High evaluation scores may also reflect the novelty and motivational aspects of VR as an innovative educational medium [37, 38].

Regarding implications for curriculum design, these results suggest that VR simulation should be integrated strategically within nursing programs, particularly for high-risk, low-frequency clinical scenarios like DKA care. Given its effectiveness, VR simulation could be utilized as a capstone activity following core theoretical modules. Furthermore, VR could potentially serve as a viable supplement to or partial replacement for clinical practicum hours, especially in situations where access to specific clinical placements is restricted.

Despite these promising results, this study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, the participants were recruited from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although random assignment was performed, the lack of allocation concealment might have introduced selection bias. Third, all outcomes were measured using self-report questionnaires, which are susceptible to social desirability bias and may not fully reflect actual clinical competence. Fourth, while the control group participated in a matched 30-minute review session, the structured debriefing based on the SENSE model was provided only to the intervention group. This difference in the nature of post-learning activities may act as a confounding variable, making it difficult to isolate the effect of the VR simulation itself from the specific debriefing model. Fifth, this study focused only on immediate post-intervention effects; therefore, the long-term retention and transfer of learning to actual clinical practice remain unknown. Finally, the lack of prospective trial registration remains a limitation for transparency. Future research should incorporate multi-center designs, objective performance assessments, and longitudinal follow-ups to strengthen the evidence for VR-based nursing education.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a VR simulation nursing education program based on Kolb’s experiential learning model significantly improves problem-solving abilities, performance confidence, and practice immersion among nursing students. These results support the feasibility of integrating structured VR-based learning strategies as a valuable pedagogical tool for managing complex clinical cases such as DKA. However, as these findings are derived from a specific student population at a single institution, caution is required when generalizing the results to broader nursing contexts. Future research should incorporate multi-center designs and objective performance assessments to further strengthen the evidence for VR-based instructional design in nursing education.
