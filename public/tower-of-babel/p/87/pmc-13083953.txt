Mitigating obstructions to attain successful application and implementation of building information modeling (BIM) in residential construction projects’ lifecycle
Authors: Abdullah Alsehaimi, Muhammad Usman Ghani, Abdullah O Baarimah, Aawag Moshen Alawag, Madhusudhan Bangalore Ramu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effective integration of Building Information Modeling has become essential for improving coordination, efficiency, and lifecycle performance in construction projects. However, despite its global diffusion, BIM adoption in residential construction within developing countries remains constrained by multiple implementation challenges. This paper investigates the critical barriers influencing BIM integration and examines how overcoming these barriers enhances lifecycle success across the design, construction, and operation phases. A conceptual framework grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model and socio technical systems theory was developed and empirically tested using data collected from 166 construction professionals. Structural Equation Modeling based on partial least squares was employed to analyze the hypothesized relationships. The results identify four primary categories of BIM implementation barriers, namely behavioral, managerial, technical, and operational factors, with behavioral and managerial dimensions exerting the strongest influence. The structural model confirms that mitigating these barriers significantly improves overall project lifecycle performance. Beyond its regional context, the paper provides a transferable framework for understanding digital innovation adoption in project based environments and offers strategic guidance for policymakers and industry stakeholders in developing countries seeking to accelerate digital transformation in residential construction. The findings contribute to both BIM adoption literature and broader digital transformation research by demonstrating that successful implementation requires alignment across technological capability, organizational readiness, and human behavioral factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43261-w.Keywords: Residential projects, BIM, Barriers, Modelling, ConstructionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In developing countries, construction projects typically confront a wide range of barriers like delays in completion time, overruns in the budget, below average quality, and a higher probability of incompetency to achieve the desired objectives. Many developing countries have experienced rapid urban and population growth, driving a significant increase in the demand for residential buildings to meet basic housing needs. In this context, the implementation of Building Information Modelling (BIM) is increasingly influenced by stakeholder expectations and national legislative initiatives that promote digital transformation and sustainable construction practices1.

Building Information Modeling (BIM) can potentially improve the design, execution, and management of buildings. It continually undergoes a radical shift in response to demand from sector stakeholders for technology to address systemic and recurrent problems, such as capital, production, and schedule2. BIM includes the capacity for management, information, and processes to interact. Moreover, technical expansions in BIM have led to the employ of multiple advanced technologies to enhance the realization of building construction. Thus, BIM has been drawing attention as an essential project lifecycle framework supporting design, construction, and operation phases that significantly impacts and manages a construction project’s lifespan.

Starting from 2D drawings representing basic geometry, BIM evolves to 3D modeling that enhances spatial understanding and coordination3. The 4D dimension integrates time, enabling effective scheduling and project sequencing, while 5D adds cost estimation and budget control capabilities. The 6D dimension incorporates facility management and lifecycle data to support operation and maintenance activities4. Finally, 7D BIM embeds sustainability and energy performance analysis, enabling designers and engineers to assess environmental impact, carbon emissions, and resource efficiency throughout the building’s lifecycle5.

Numerous research has tried to untangle the BIM application’s Gordian Knot level difficulties that worked on establishing the present degree of adoption, characterizing, outlining the barriers, and identifying the motivations. The construction industry of underdeveloped countries is lacking in adopting BIM and there are no systematic initiatives to probe the confronting challenges in BIM adoption. Although researchers around the globe have concentrated on the BIM activities and method efficacy but minute effort has been made to discover the upfront challenges in BIM deployment and how to maximize the BIM usage in overall project lifecycle of developing nation’s construction sector.

In addition, conquering the BIM obstacles relating to BIM overall success in the construction sector is receiving small academic attention, even though knowing the BIM application hurdles is raising the adoption of BIM in the construction sector. Subsequently, the current paper anticipates the effects of the elimination of BIM adoption barriers for overall BIM success in the residential building project lifecycle. The need for more research and knowledge management adversely impacts the work lifecycle in the construction business6.

The evolution of BIM can be understood through its maturity levels, ranging from Level 0 to Level 4. Level 0 involves simple 2D CAD drawings with minimal collaboration, while Level 1 introduces basic 3D modeling with limited data integration. Level 2 advances to multi-disciplinary collaboration where project participants share and coordinate models across platforms, and Level 3 enables full interoperability through a shared common data environment (CDE), enhancing real-time communication and integration across the project lifecycle7,8.

In the construction industry, residential construction zones are deprived of digital development and innovations, and this sector needs digital technologies, like BIM, to achieve sustainability in processes. BIM has many portfolios mainly comprised of efficient construction management and fast project delivery for residential construction professionals. BIM has advanced as a multidisciplinary working platform to develop, merge, and maintain the existing databases comprised of construction information (portfolio of facilities) to facilitate operations and maintenance. The construction industry in developing countries is characterized by a highly diverse workforce, consisting of professionals, contractors, and laborers from different cultural, educational, and linguistic backgrounds. This diversity while beneficial for knowledge exchange often creates communication and coordination challenges that can affect BIM implementation efficiency9. BIM may assist multidisciplinary cultural team members and promote data exchange. BIM helps in decision making process during the project conception and execution and also improves the performance of residential projects mainly10.

Similarly, BIM enhances the relationship between project participants. Retrospective research has indicated the benefits of BIM tools in cost estimation and management methods. BIM provides cost savings by identifying potential design conflicts before the project execution11. The adoption benefits of this BIM allow the design team to modify specific architectural attributes in response to customer input. As a result, tools of the visual interface of the BIM have been regarded as an indispensable technology for the residential design of construction, not just primary design relevant phase but additionally for optimized design12.

International evidence further demonstrates that BIM adoption barriers vary across regulatory and organizational contexts. In the Italian construction sector, BIM challenges have been categorized into people related, managerial, technological, project specific, and external institutional factors, highlighting the multidimensional nature of implementation constraints13,14. Similarly, research in the United Kingdom classifies BIM adoption determinants into social influence, enabling conditions, technological readiness, performance expectations, and effort expectations, reflecting the influence of organizational culture and institutional support mechanisms15,16. In the Korean construction industry, technological capability, managerial commitment, technical support, and subjective norms have been identified as key drivers influencing BIM acceptance and organizational adoption behavior17. These cross country findings reinforce the argument that BIM implementation is shaped by both socio organizational dynamics and technological readiness, supporting the theoretical foundation of the present study and justifying the classification of barriers into behavioral, managerial, technical, and operational dimensions.

In addition, used BIM to facilitate issue identification and management employing a web-based application Programming Interface (API). It exemplifies BIM’s proficiency to see obstacles and project progress18. BIM has modified the conventional construction project management techniques that influence the project’s lifecycle. BIM uses various approaches during this lifecycle, which are often disrupted by the comments of other specialists throughout various forms of development, denoted for the design, construction, and operation stages19. It might be assumed that overcoming BIM hurdles influences the success of BIM in residential Building Lifecycles. Numerous investigations and analyses of the obstacles in the BIM implementation have been done. These studies categorizations of BIM barriers in construction are based on each country’s specific characteristics. Consequently, it varies from nation to nation. Similarly, the properties of the components differ considerably across countries.

Using BIM, this research will aid Partners in attaining glory in their construction projects by minimizing losses and boosting quality. In developing countries where comparable construction projects are carried out, current research would help to provide several enticing benefits to construction professionals, such as policy developers, construction work bidders, and architects. To provide a clearer direction for the investigation, this paper addresses the following specific research questions:

RQ1: What are the critical barriers that hinder the successful implementation of BIM in residential construction projects in developing countries?

RQ2: How do these barriers influence the overall success of residential construction projects across the design, construction, and operation phases of the project lifecycle?

RQ3: To what extent does overcoming these barriers contribute to achieving successful BIM integration and lifecycle performance?

Based on the research questions, the primary objectives of this paper are threefold:

Obj1: To identify and categorize the critical barriers affecting BIM implementation in residential construction projects within developing countries.

Obj2: To examine the extent to which these barriers influence project lifecycle success across the design, construction, and operation phases.

Obj3: To develop and validate a predictive model that explains how overcoming BIM implementation barriers contributes to improved project outcomes.

These objectives provide a structured foundation for analyzing the relationship between BIM adoption challenges and lifecycle performance, ensuring that the paper offers both empirical evidence and theoretical insights relevant to digital transformation in construction.

Although this paper focuses on BIM implementation in residential construction projects, its findings offer broader theoretical implications for digital innovation adoption across complex organizational systems. The identified barriers align closely with principles of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which emphasizes perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and behavioral resistance as central determinants of technology adoption. Similarly, socio-technical systems theory highlights the interdependence between human behavior, organizational structures, and technological infrastructures, suggesting that successful digital transformation requires alignment across these domains. The paper’s results reinforce these theoretical perspectives by demonstrating how behavioral attitudes, managerial support, and technical integration collectively influence implementation success. Therefore, while the empirical setting is regionally grounded, the conceptual insights contribute to a wider understanding of how socio-organizational dynamics shape technology adoption in multidisciplinary and global contexts. Accordingly, the paper hypothesizes that overcoming BIM implementation barriers has a significant positive impact on the success of residential construction projects throughout their lifecycle. Methodology

This paper adopted a structured multi-phase research design to achieve the stated objectives. First, a systematic review of recent literature was conducted to identify and compile potential barriers affecting BIM implementation in residential construction projects, which directly addressed Objective 1. Second, a pilot survey was undertaken to validate the relevance and clarity of the identified barriers and to determine their underlying factor structure, enabling refinement of the measurement instrument and strengthening Objective 1. Third, the refined questionnaire was administered in the main survey to collect quantitative data from residential construction professionals, supporting Objective 2 by capturing perceptions of how barriers influence lifecycle performance across design, construction, and operation phases. Fourth, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling was applied to assess the measurement model reliability and validity and to test the hypothesized relationships between BIM implementation barriers and project lifecycle success, thereby addressing Objective 2 and Objective 3. Finally, model evaluation procedures including bootstrapping, confidence intervals, effect size assessment, and predictive relevance analysis were used to ensure robustness of the findings and to confirm the explanatory and predictive capability of the proposed model, which supports Objective 3. This study involved human participants in the form of a voluntary questionnaire survey. All participants were informed about the purpose of the research prior to participation and provided informed consent. Participation was anonymous and no personally identifiable information was collected. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations and complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Identification of factors

This paper’s data was collected by examining a wide range of sources, including published publications, research articles, official documents, and green building regulations to get a thorough understanding and knowledge. Existing literature was reviewed extensively to find the most critical building information modeling implementation barriers that have an impact on achieving a successful construction project life cycle. The relevant literature was collected using Web of Science (WoS) database, using designed keyword combination, i.e., (building information model* OR BIM) AND (barrier* OR implementation OR adoption) AND (small construction OR residential project* OR construction) for last six years data, i.e., from 2017 till to date. Only relevant studies were identified, i.e., studies focusing on small residential construction projects. After in detail screening 43 studies were selected for in detail review and further identification of BIM implementation barriers. The Web of Science database was selected as the primary source due to its strong indexing standards, quality-controlled journal coverage, and compatibility with structured keyword filtering. Web of Science ensures inclusion of peer reviewed and high impact publications, which supports methodological rigor. Although Scopus also provides extensive coverage, the search strategy was limited to one database to maintain consistency in indexing structure and to avoid duplication of records. The use of Web of Science alone was considered sufficient for identifying relevant peer reviewed literature aligned with the scope of this paper as indicated in previous research20,21. The identification was performed in terms of matching the overall objectives of the paper with existing literature. Briefly it was a short-listing process which ultimately given a total of 21 barriers corresponding with building information modelling implementation to achieve successful projects. Finally, identified barriers were effectively reviewed in terms of their relativity with research objectives and a final set of 21 barriers was prepared for further analysis. Pilot survey and main questionnaire survey

For the purpose of this research, a pilot survey was completed by 156 participants using a random selection method. This pilot survey was a key component in the data collection process as it validated each item in the survey and assisted in identifying the most important items. The reason to select 156 participants to complete the pilot survey was to provide high levels of statistical reliability and representation of all professionals in the residential construction sector. While typically pilot studies will have smaller sample sizes, past research has shown that larger pilot sizes (50–200 participants) can greatly improve both the validity and reliability of the instrument, especially when multivariate or SEM analyses are used45.

Based on the pilot surveys feedback, the main questionnaire was finalized. The questionnaire survey feedback and emails were sent to more than 500 respondents. In the main survey questionnaire, the 5-point Likert scale was applied as the measurement method for getting quantitative information. The survey questionnaires were distributed electronically in Saudi Arabian construction industry. Respondents also included facility management professionals working in small construction projects. Professionals in large populations, more innovative areas, and elevated construction needs were surveyed in this fashion since it was deemed the most effective approach to acquire a representative sample of their perspectives. Because of this, respondents were selected on the presumption that they had prior experience with small-scale building projects, either as contractors, consultants, or clients. These companies represented the three key sectors of the construction sector. Because BIM is currently seldom utilized for smaller projects, stratified sampling is a viable strategy for determining target audiences. Due to the narrow scope of this survey’s investigation on BIM, however, this sample strategy should provide more reliable and accurate results for the academic community. Stratified sampling is preferred because it removes the possibility of a biased sample being selected, produces a statistically indistinguishable sample from the population of interest, and allows for extrapolation from the sample to the population. Applying pre-approach methods such as phone calls, emails, and social media, we could ascertain that our potential participants were already acquainted with small construction projects and were eager to participate in our research. Smaller construction businesses were given preference since big construction companies could only bid on projects with higher contract values.

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)

The measurement model was constructed with the help of PLS-SEM. It was the main element in the development of the quantification model to analyze the convergent rationality, which further indicates the coherence degree of the data obtained from the survey. The tests that were performed on the measurement model were the composite reliability scores, Cronbach’s alpha, and every extracted variance. For all the constructs involved in the measurement model, the internal loadings were indicated, which were relative to the significant relationship between factors. The minimum acceptable value for external loading is 0.5, but the required value is 0.6. The analysis helped effectively to modify the loadings and to delete the factors that are non-significant with respect to relationships in the measurement model.

Analyses and discussion

Pilot survey results

The pilot survey included responses from 156 construction professionals and was conducted to assess the reliability and structure of identified BIM implementation barriers. Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.80 confirmed strong internal consistency across all variables. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was then applied to categorize the 21 identified barriers into meaningful groups based on their shared characteristics. The analysis resulted in five barrier categories: management, technical adoption, operational, behavioral, and digital education barriers. Among these, management barriers ranked highest in importance (mean = 3.31), followed by technical adoption barriers (mean = 3.29), operational barriers (mean = 3.27), behavioral barriers (mean = 3.24), and digital education barriers (mean = 3.23). These findings provided a structured foundation for the subsequent main survey and SEM analysis by clarifying the relative influence of different BIM implementation challenges in residential construction projects. Discussion

The findings provide deeper insight into the multidimensional dynamics shaping digital innovation adoption within project-based organizational environments. While the empirical focus centers on BIM implementation in residential construction, the results reveal broader patterns applicable to technology adoption across multidisciplinary domains. The prominence of behavioral, managerial, technical, and operational barriers demonstrates that successful digital transformation is not solely driven by technological availability but depends heavily on human attitudes, organizational readiness, and process integration. These outcomes align with the principles of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which emphasizes perceived usefulness, ease of use, and behavioral resistance as key determinants of adoption, as well as socio-technical systems theory, which highlights the interdependence between social structures and technological systems47,48. By illustrating how these factors collectively shape implementation success, the paper offers transferable theoretical insights that extend beyond the construction sector, contributing to a broader understanding of how organizations navigate digital change in complex environments.

The first BIM barrier component relates to (behavioral barrier β = 0.456, P < 0.001) construction employees’ attitudes about adopting BIM and the behavioral challenges they encounter while adopting BIM in residential building projects. This subgroup explains 5.665% of the variance (pilot survey stage), with items including B15 “Lack of subcontractor support”, B14 “Workers do not want to adopt BIM”, B6 “Reluctance to transition to BIM”, B3 “Lack of BIM experts” and B12 “Lack of universal method to implement BIM”. Small construction project implementation deviations significantly impact subcontractor support and eventually establish a highly improper tone amongst project stakeholders49. The unwillingness of construction workers to alter the current environment directly affects the behavioral barrier. The majority of workers need extra training to understand the constraints of BIM utilization in construction projects, which might cause issues if just one approach is made available50. With a path coefficient of 0.456, the PLS-SEM model reveals the factor’s greatest effect on the BIM application hurdles. It is essential for the BIM exercise that good behavioral support embraces BIM deployment. During the maintenance and operation phase, BIM deployment is inhibited by a lack of interest, organizational issues, and a mismatch between people, procedures, technologies, and processes51.

The second primary component is associated with Management Obstacles (β = 0.436, P < 0.001): The second-ranked subgroup comprises management-related obstacles that construction employees encounter while applying BIM in residential building projects. The variation explained by this subgroup is 28.015% (pilot survey stage). This subgroup contains the following items: B18, “Impact of Covid-19 on small construction projects,” B20 “, High variety of workers in projects,” and B16 “, Time-consuming implementation method.” The cumulative impact of management barriers is sizeable when discussing the utilization of BIM in a residential construction projects52,53. One of the administrative barriers to implementing BIM was the precarious situation residential construction firms found themselves in after the COVID-19 pandemic. Workplaces are diverse because many small businesses use construction workers from other nations, mainly Bangladesh and India54. The heterogeneity of the construction industry’s personnel is a major hindrance to implement BIM adoption is also quite time-consuming, according to the most recent studies55. The paper’s findings provide direct answers to the proposed research questions by clarifying how BIM implementation barriers influence project lifecycle stages. In response to RQ1, the analysis identified behavioral, managerial, technical, and operational barriers as the most critical factors hindering BIM adoption in residential construction projects. These barriers manifest differently across project phases. Regarding RQ2, the results show that during the design stage, technical and knowledge-related barriers limit effective model coordination and visualization capabilities. In the construction phase, managerial and operational barriers, such as insufficient leadership support and workflow disruptions, significantly affect project execution and collaboration. During the operation phase, behavioral resistance and lack of digital skills reduce the long-term effectiveness of BIM-based asset management. Addressing RQ3, the structural model confirms that overcoming these barriers has a strong positive impact on overall project lifecycle success, demonstrating that improved stakeholder alignment, training initiatives, and process standardization can significantly enhance BIM integration and performance outcomes across all project stages.

Conclusion

This paper investigated the critical barriers influencing Building Information Modeling (BIM) implementation in residential construction projects and examined how mitigating these barriers enhances lifecycle performance. Guided by three research objectives, the paper first identified and categorized major BIM implementation obstacles, then assessed their impact across design, construction, and operation stages, and finally developed a predictive model explaining the relationship between barrier mitigation and project success.

The findings reveal that BIM implementation challenges are multidimensional, with behavioral and managerial barriers exerting the strongest influence on adoption outcomes, followed by technical and operational factors. These barriers affect each project stage differently, where technical challenges limit design coordination, managerial and operational constraints disrupt construction workflows, and behavioral resistance reduces effectiveness during the operation phase. The structural model further confirms that overcoming these barriers significantly enhances lifecycle performance, demonstrating that BIM success depends on socio-organizational readiness as much as technological capability.

From a theoretical perspective, this research contributes to broader digital innovation literature by reinforcing the relevance of socio-technical systems theory and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) in explaining technology adoption within complex project environments. The paper highlights how interactions between human behavior, organizational structures, and technical systems shape implementation success, offering transferable insights applicable beyond the construction sector. These findings extend existing BIM adoption models by integrating lifecycle performance considerations, thus enriching the conceptual understanding of digital transformation in project-based industries.

Practically, the paper provides actionable guidance for policymakers, project managers, and industry stakeholders by emphasizing the importance of leadership commitment, workforce training, and standardized implementation frameworks. Encouraging early stakeholder involvement, investing in digital education, and promoting phased BIM adoption strategies can significantly improve implementation outcomes and long-term project sustainability. These insights are particularly valuable for developing economies where digital transformation remains uneven across sectors.

Future research could expand this work by examining BIM adoption in different cultural and regulatory environments, enabling cross-country comparisons that enhance the generalizability of findings. Longitudinal studies may also explore how BIM implementation evolves over time, particularly as digital maturity increases. Additionally, integrating emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, digital twins, and blockchain with BIM systems could provide new perspectives on managing project complexity and improving lifecycle performance.

Overall, this paper advances scientific understanding by demonstrating that BIM implementation success is not merely a technological issue but a multidimensional transformation process requiring alignment across behavioral, managerial, and technical domains. By offering empirically grounded insights and a validated predictive framework, the research contributes to both academic scholarship and industry practice, supporting more resilient, efficient, and sustainable construction project delivery worldwide.
