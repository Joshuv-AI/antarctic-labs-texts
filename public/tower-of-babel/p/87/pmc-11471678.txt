Septic arthritis as breakthrough invasive fusariosis after cord blood transplantation
Authors: Shinichi Katsuoka, Hidehiro Itonaga, Yasushi Sawayama, Masahiko Chiwata, Haruka Watanabe, Yuichi Yamada, Machiko Fujioka, Takeharu Kato, Shinya Sato, Koji Ando, Masato Tashiro, Takahiro Takazono, Yoshitaka Imaizumi, Koichi Izumikawa, Katsunori Yanagihara, Hiroshi Mukae, Yasushi Miyazaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The present case developed invasive fusariosis without typical skin lesions during voriconazole prophylaxis after unrelated cord blood transplantation.•Fusarium solani species in the present case was resistant to voriconazole in an in vitro assay.•The clinical course of the present case indicates the concern about breakthrough infections of invasive fusariosis resistant to azoles.

Introduction

Fusariosis is a non-Aspergillus invasive mold infection in patients with hematological malignancies and its incidence ranges between 0.26 and 3.4 % [1,2]. Invasive fusariosis (IF) is likely to develop in patients with risk factors such as neutropenia and steroid treatment. Frequently involved sites at the diagnosis of IF are skin lesions, lung infiltration, sinusitis, and blood (i.e., fungemia). A common clinical manifestation of IF is skin lesions with black eschars (a necrotic hard palate ulcer); skin lesions are detected in approximately 80 % of IF cases. Septic arthritis due to Fusarium species was previously reported in 1 out of 15 patients (7 %) [3], indicating that it is a rare clinical manifestation of IF.

Fusarium species often show susceptibility to voriconazole (VRCZ), posaconazole (PSCZ), and amphotericin B (AMB). Guidelines recommend the immediate utilization of VRCZ followed by the surgical debridement of infected tissue in localized infections [4,5]. Recent case reports described azole (VRCZ and PSCZ)-resistant IF in patients with hematological malignancies [[6], [7], [8]]. Although there have been concerns regarding breakthrough infections of IF resistant to VRCZ, azole-resistant IF was not identified in several large-scale studies on patients who received VRCZ prophylaxis. Therefore, it is not widely known when physicians need to consider breakthrough infections by azole-resistant Fusarium species in patients receiving VRCZ prophylaxis.

We herein report a case of septic arthritis due to VRCZ-resistant Fusarium solani species complex (FSSC). This case report provides important information that will contribute to improvements in the diagnostic approach for IF in patients with hematological malignancies.

Case report

A 51-year-old male was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia with myelodysplasia-related changes (AML-MRC) and achieved complete remission (CR) after induction chemotherapy. He did not undergo allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) during the first CR of AML-MRC because of the absence of HLA-matched sibling donors and unrelated donors in the Japan Marrow Donor Program. Twelve years after CR1, AML-MRC recurred and the patient underwent his first transplantation using unrelated cord blood with VRCZ as anti-fungal prophylaxis after chemotherapy. He achieved second CR; however, AML-MRC recurred 29 months after the first transplantation. He underwent a second transplantation using unrelated cord blood with reduced intensity conditioning therapy. On day +24 after the second transplantation, the patient did not achieve neutrophil engraftment. He subsequently underwent a third transplantation on day +34 after the second transplantation and continued anti-fungal prophylaxis with VRCZ.

The third transplantation using unrelated cord blood (total nucleated cell dose 2.3 × 107 cells/kg; CD34-positive cell dose, 0.7 × 105 cells /kg; and HLA 3/6 antigen matched, from a female donor) was performed after conditioning therapy (fludarabine 30 mg/m2 and cyclophosphamide 200 mg/m2 for 1 day). On day +6 after the third transplantation, he presented with febrile neutropenia that responded poorly to antibiotics and received l-AMB at 3 mg/kg. However, he was unable to continue treatment with l-AMB due to the development of grade 4 anaphylaxis (CTCAE v5.0) and, thus, restarted treatment with VRCZ. Methyl-prednisolone (mPSL) at 60 mg was initiated for pre-engraftment syndrome from day +14, and the patient achieved neutrophil engraftment with the third-donor complete chimerism and remission of AML. On day +21 after the third transplantation, fungi were detected in urine of the patient. It was difficult to identify the species of fungi based solely on morphological findings. Combination therapy of micafungin (MCFG) and VRCZ was started. The serum level of β-D glucan and the galactomannan index were 58.3 pg/mL (normal range, <20 pg/mL) and 1.0 C.O.I. (normal range, <0.5), respectively. Fungi were persistently detected in urine after neutrophil engraftment on day +25. The patient developed grade II GVHD (skin stage 3 and gut stage 1) with skin erosions on his neck, trunk, limbs, and genital area. Computed tomography showed a small nodule in the upper lobe of the right lung. He presented with painful swelling of the right knee with the serum level of a β-D glucan level and galactomannan index were 435.8 pg/mL and 0.4 C.O.I. on day +42. An abscess in the right bursa (Fig. 1A, B) was identified on magnetic resonance imaging and the fungus was detected by the culture examinations of urine and synovial fluid samples but not blood sample; however, we failed to identify the species of fungi based solely on morphological findings. Since fungi were regarded as the cause of septic arthritis, empiric anti-fungal therapy using l-AMB 3.0 mg/kg was initiated with mPSL as prophylaxis for an allergic reaction on day +63. He died of progressive fungal infection on day +75 after the third transplantation. To identify the species of fungi, we performed ribosomal DNA sequencing for the ITS1/ITS4 region. The fungal species was identified as FSSC according to the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST, a database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information). We then examined the drug susceptibility of FSSC, which was detected in urine and synovial fluid samples. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of anti-fungal drugs were assessed using the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute M38-Ed3 method. Discussion

We herein described the development of septic arthritis due to FSSC in a post-transplant patient who received VRCZ prophylaxis. Multiple localized skin nodules are frequently detected when patients develop disseminated IF. In contrast, a previous study reported that septic arthritis due to Fusarium species developed without typical skin lesions [3]. The most frequent molds causing septic arthritis are Aspergillus species, and, thus, septic arthritis caused by Fusarium species is rare. Due to potential concerns regarding increases in azole-resistant mold infections in the post-VRCZ era, physicians need to consider not only Aspergillus infections, but also azole-resistant IF when patients with hematological malignancies develop septic arthritis without skin lesions during VRCZ prophylaxis.

In the present case, systemic skin involvement with the erosion of acute GVHD was observed after the third transplantation. Although it was difficult to establish whether the portal entry of FSCC was the skin or urinary tract, damage in these surface barriers to infection may result in the development of IF, as previously reported [9]. Therefore, there appear to be some risk factors for IF, including neutropenia, steroid treatment, and the skin involvement of acute GVHD. A large-scale study is required to identify clinical risk factors after allo-SCT.

We failed to identify Fusarium species based solely on morphological findings, but confirmed the diagnosis of fusariosis by ribosomal DNA sequencing. This confirmation depended on the ribosomal DNA sequencing of fungi cultured from urine and synovial fluid samples, which suggests that the early diagnosis of IF is difficult in present clinical practice. The clinical course of our case indicates the importance of developing diagnostic modalities for IF. Quantitative PCR was previously reported to be a promising tool for the identification of Fusarium species [10], and such an approach will enable us to diagnose IF in the early phase in the future. Further experimental and clinical investigations are warranted for the early diagnosis of IF.

It was difficult to start l-AMB treatment immediately after the diagnosis of septic arthritis, because of concerns regarding a severe allergic reaction to l-AMB. Based on the results of MIC tests, it would be of interest whether the early initiation of l-AMB treatment with steroid prophylaxis alters the outcome of septic arthritis due to FSSC. In addition, to improve the prognosis of septic arthritis due to FSSC, including breakthrough infections, the development of a therapeutic algorithm, including novel agents (e.g., olorofim and manogepix), is required for infections by azole-resistant Fusarium species.

In summary, the clinical course of this case suggested that careful attention is needed for the development of azole-resistant IF during VRCZ prophylaxis. Furthermore, the diagnosis of Fusarium species-induced septic arthritis in the early phase is required to initiate the optimal treatment when patients do not have any typical skin lesions.
