Pharmacological targeting of the mycothiol cysteine ligase MshC in Mycobacteriumabscessus
Authors: Thanh Quang Nguyen, Claire Hamela, Luping Pang, Yi Liu, Greta Carola Giannini, Lucas Boeck, Gerald Larrouy-Maumus, Laurent Kremer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of pulmonary diseases caused by Mycobacterium abscessus is on the rise and surpasses tuberculosis in many countries. However, intrinsic resistance of this pathogen to antibiotics profoundly limits our therapeutic arsenal, and new chemotherapeutic agents and targets are urgently needed. Mycothiol (MSH), the major thiol in mycobacteria, plays an important role in resistance to oxidative stress. Previous work in Mycobacterium smegmatis and Mycobacterium tuberculosis indicates that the ATP-dependent cysteine ligase MshC catalyzes the penultimate step in MSH biogenesis. Here, we investigated whether MshC represents a potent drug target in M. abscessus. CRISPRi was used to demonstrate that mshC is essential in vitro and during infection in zebrafish larvae. Subsequently, in silico drug screening identified three potential MshC-binding molecules, namely rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289, significantly inhibiting M. abscessus growth and MSH production. Spontaneous missense mutations in MshC generated cross-resistance to all three compounds. Mapping the mutations conferring resistance on a MshC three-dimensional homology model defined the enzyme catalytic site as the potential drug binding cavity. Additionally, overexpression of mshC in a susceptible M. abscessus strain increased resistance levels to rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289, but not to rifabutin. Conversely, reduced mshC expression levels in the CRISPRi mutant increased susceptibility to the inhibitors. Systematic assessment of olverembatinib and SB-224289 in combination with 18 additional drugs revealed distinct synergistic and antagonistic interactions. These results emphasize a yet unexploited chemical structure class against M. abscessus and highlight the potential of targeting the MSH pathway for future translational developments against M. abscessus lung diseases.Keywords: antibiotic resistance, CRISPRi, drug screening, inhibition, MshC, Mycobacterium abscessus, mycothiol

Infections with non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) represent an increasing health problem (1). NTM are environmental mycobacteria, displaying various degrees of virulence and associated with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations. Alongside the Mycobacterium avium complex, the Mycobacterium abscessus (Mab) complex represents one of the most commonly identified pathogens responsible for NTM pulmonary diseases (2, 3). Approximately 50% of infections caused by the Mab complex involve the lungs and occur predominantly in immunocompromised individuals or in patients with pre-existing pulmonary conditions, such as bronchiectasis or cystic fibrosis (CF) (1, 4, 5). In these patients, Mab accelerates inflammatory lung damage, leading to increased morbidity and mortality (6, 7). Infections caused by Mab are known to be extremely difficult to treat, due to the intrinsic or induced drug resistance mechanisms developed by these microorganisms, making them highly resistant to most available antimicrobials (8, 9, 10, 11, 12). In addition, various patients experience relapse after medical treatment or surgical removal of the pathogen. Currently, the treatment requires long-term multidrug therapy including a macrolide (clarithromycin or azithromycin), an aminoglycoside (amikacin) and an intravenous β-lactam (cefoxitin or imipenem) for at least 12 months (13), resulting in a cure rate of only 25 to 40% in the case of macrolide resistance, which occurs in 40 to 60% of the isolates (14). Thus, novel mycobacterial targets and pharmacophores need to be explored to relieve the burden of drug resistance and effectively reduce the bacterial loads in the infected patient. Therefore, inhibiting enzymes involved in essential cellular processes of Mab, such as detoxification processes, may be of interest for future therapeutic developments.

Mycobacteria and most Actinobacteria produce mycothiol (MSH; 1-D-myo-inosityl-2-(N-acetyl-L-cysteinyl)amido-2-deoxy-α-D-glucopyranoside), the functional equivalent of glutathione in eukaryotes as the major low-molecular-weight thiol to protect against oxidative stress, electrophilic toxins and antibiotics (15, 16). MSH biosynthesis is carried out in five steps catalyzed by a glycosyltransferase (MshA), a phosphatase (MshA2), a deacylase (MshB), a cysteine ligase (MshC), and an acetyltransferase (MshD). MshC is a cysteine ligase catalyzing the ATP-dependent condensation of cysteine and the GlcN-Ins intermediate (Fig. 1A) (17). MshC is essential for the maintenance of the redox balance and metabolic homeostasis of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (18, 19). Targeted genetic disruption studies have shown that mshA and mshC are essential while mshB and mshD are dispensable for in vitro growth of M. tuberculosis (20, 21, 22), suggesting that the MSH pathway, particularly MshC, may represent an attractive drug target for the development of new anti-tuberculosis drugs (23). In contrast, MSH is not essential for the growth of Mycobacterium smegmatis, since disruption of mshA, mshB, mshC, or mshD resulted in viable mutant strains (24). In Mab, however, the importance of MSH has not been addressed yet, although a recent genome-wide analysis by saturated transposon mutagenesis suggested that mshC is essential (25).

In this study, we investigated the essential character of mshC and the potential of MshC as an attractive drug target in Mab by identifying new inhibitors. CRISPR interference assay was employed to demonstrate that mshC is indispensable in vitro and in vivo, while in silico drug screening identified three molecules targeting MshC and inhibiting Mab growth. Collectively, our results validate MshC as an attractive drug target in Mab and provide a starting point for further drug design and optimization developments.

Results

In vitro essentiality of mshC in M. abscessus

The msh genes are scattered throughout the genome in Mab: mshA (MAB_4057c), mshB (MAB_1316), mshC (MAB_2116) and mshD (MAB_0745). To evaluate the importance of mshC in Mab growth, the CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) assay was applied. The pLJR962 (Table S1), containing an anhydrotetracycline (ATc)-inducible nuclease-deactivated Cas9 (from Streptococcus thermophilus) as well as an ATc-inducible single guide RNA (sgRNA) was employed as the vector for CRISPRi in Mab, as described earlier (26, 27). Protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences were identified in mshC, and several sequences complementary to the non-template target sequence were cloned into pLJR962, upstream of dCas9 (Fig. 1B) (28). Four pLJR962_mshC_sgRNA constructs were generated, with mshC_sgRNA3 and mshC_sgRNA4 differing by only one nucleotide (Table S2). After transformation, the different Mab CRISPRi mutants were cultivated in 7H9 broth in the presence of increasing concentrations of ATc and tested for growth. Figure 1C shows that not all mutants were equally responsive to ATc. While the strains harboring mshC_sgRNA1, mshC_sgRNA2, or mshC_sgRNA3 displayed impaired growth (OD600 measurements) at ATc concentrations >250 ng/ml, growth of the strain carrying mshC_sgRNA4 was stopped at 250 ng/ml ATc. The IC50 of ATc against MAB_mshC-sgRNA4 was 220 ng/ml (Fig. S1). Given the highest susceptibility of the mshC_sgRNA4 strain to ATc, this CRISPRi mutant was further studied. Increasing ATc concentrations were added to mshC_sgRNA4 cultures, RNA was extracted, and quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed. There was a strong correlation (R2= 0.76) between the concentration of ATc and the level of mshC transcriptional repression, with a 22.4% and 47.6% decrease in the presence of 110 and 220 ng/ml of ATc, respectively, in MAB_mshC-sgRNA4 relative to the strain carrying the empty pLJR962 (Fig. S2).

While ATc (up to 1000 ng/ml) did not affect the growth of the strain carrying the empty vector, CFU determination of the strain containing mshC_sgRNA4 showed that 250 ng/ml of ATc was associated with a strong decrease in the CFU (3-log reduction after 3 days of incubation as compared to the condition without ATc), validating the OD measurements. This effect was also confirmed by visual inspection of the culture in the presence or absence of ATc (Fig. 1D). Next, diluted cultures were spotted onto 7H10 agar supplemented with ATc concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 ng/ml. Growth of the CRISPRi strains was inhibited with increasing ATc concentrations, showing a notable effect with the mshC_sgRNA4 strain (Fig. 1E, upper panels). Similar results were obtained when spotting the cultures on Cation-Adjusted Mueller-Hinton (MH) agar medium (Fig. 1E, middle panels) or when replacing ATc by doxycycline, although with a less pronounced effect at low concentrations (Fig. 1E, lower panels).

Together, these results demonstrate that the CRISPRi produces a robust impairment of mshC gene expression associated with a striking mycobacterial growth defect, strongly suggesting that mshC is indispensable for Mab in vitro growth. All subsequent studies were based on the Mab mshC_sgRNA4 strain, hereafter referred to as Mab_pJLR962:mshC.

CRISPRi-mediated repression of mshC impairs growth and pathogenicity of M. abscessus in zebrafish

We used the zebrafish model to address the effect of mshC silencing on the infection outcome by treating intravenously infected embryos with ATc, following a previously established protocol (29). We injected 30 h post-fertilization (hpf) embryos in the caudal vein with ≈200 CFU of Mab R (parental strain), Mab_pLJR962 or Mab_pLJR962:mshC, transformed with pTEC15 expressing TdTomato. At 1 day post-infection (dpi), larvae were treated with 10 ng of ATc to repress the expression of mshC, a concentration that failed to show toxicity signs in ATc-treated larvae (29). Daily monitoring of larval survival demonstrated increased mortality over time in the non-treated groups (≈80% mortality at 11 dpi) as expected, while only ≈30% of embryos infected with Mab_pLJR962:mshC and treated with ATc succumbed to the infection (Fig. 1F, left). Imaging whole infected larvae showed lower bacterial foci in Mab_pLJR962:mshC treated with ATc as compared to the ATc-untreated larvae (Fig. 1F, right panel), in agreement with the survival results. Overall, this underscores that temporal control of mshC repression is working in vivo.

These results confirm the CRISPRi system to reduce mshC expression in Mab in the infected host and further strengthen the essentiality of MshC as a valuable drug target for the treatment of Mab diseases.

Homology modeling and in silico molecular docking of MshC inhibitors

Since the crystal structure of MshC from Mab is not available yet, a structural homology model was built using MshC from M. smegmatis (PDB ID: 8HFN), sharing 75.7% sequence identity with MshC from Mab with a GMQE score of 0.94 and a QMEAN score of 0.9, indicating good model quality and reliability (Fig. 2A). SWISS-MODEL evaluates the quality of the predicted structure using the QMEAN scoring function both locally for each residue and globally for the entire model. To assess the local quality of the predicted protein model, a graph was generated showing the similarity scores of individual residues plotted against their position in the sequence. The majority of residues displayed scores near 1, indicating strong local reliability (not shown).

Next, in silico molecular docking was performed to identify the most favorable binding modes of ligands interacting with Mab-MshC. The quality of docking results was evaluated by binding affinity. More negative binding energy indicates stronger and more stable ligand-protein interactions. The homology modeled MshC structure was used as the receptor for docking 15,236 compounds from the DrugBank database with binding affinities ranging from −0.6 to −12.6 kcal/mol. Of note, dequalinium and 5′-O-[N-(L-cysteinyl)-sulfamonyl] adenosine (CysSA) are 2 M. tuberculosis MshC inhibitors discovered previously (30, 31) displaying binding affinities of −6.9 and −8.5, respectively, thus validating our screen (not shown). Potential inhibitors with a binding affinity above −10 kcal/mol and market availability were prioritized, leading to a subset of a few potential ligands. Among these, rebastinib (Fig. 2B) showed a binding affinity of −10.8 kcal/mol and exhibits Pi-Anion interactions with the side chains of the D87 and E91 and Pi-Alkyl binding with P88 (Figs. 2C and S3A). Rebastinib is a member of the class of ureas and used in trials for the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia. It is an orally bioavailable inhibitor of multiple tyrosine kinases with potential antineoplastic activity, offering new therapeutic possibilities for hematologic malignancies (32, 33). Additionally, anti-cancer effects of rebastinib have been observed in multiple types of tumors, including breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer, underscoring its potential value in anti-cancer drug development (34). The second potential inhibitor, olverembatinib (Fig. 2B) showed a binding affinity of −10.9 kcal/mol with the modeled MshC structure (Fig. 2C). While the trifluoromethyl group of olverembatinib forms a hydrogen bond with the main chain of W227 (2.3 Å), the compound also establishes two carbon hydrogen bonds with G226 and G189 and forms additional interactions with surrounding residues including D86, G189, W227 and L204 (Fig. S3B). Similar to rebastinib, olverembatinib is also a BCR-ABL1 tyrosine kinase inhibitor having been approved in China for the treatment of some Chronic Myeloid Leukemia patients (35, 36). One other top-list compound, SB-224289 (Fig. 2B), with a binding affinity of −12.1 kcal/mol, is a small molecule initially discovered as a human serotonin receptor antagonist (37) and found to antagonize the antifungal mechanisms of Papuamide A (38). In MshC, SB-224289 (Fig. 2C) is anchored mainly through a strong π-anion interaction with D87 and a π–alkyl/π–π stacking interaction with W227. Additional stabilization is provided by stacking hydrophobic interactions with the backbone of R188 and G189 on one side and P88 on the other side (Fig. S3C).

MshC inhibitors disrupt mycothiol biosynthesis in M. abscessus

To investigate the effect of rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224289 on MSH production, Mab was treated with each inhibitor and the MSH concentrations were measured using LC-MS. While a clear peak representing MSH could be visualized at retention time of 4.2 min on the extracted ion chromatogram at [M + H]+= 487.1592 m/z in the untreated samples, the ion counts were strongly reduced in bacteria treated with 0.1× MIC50 of any of the inhibitor (Fig. 2D), and the MSH peak disappeared completely when cultures were treated with 0.5× MIC50 of each inhibitor (Fig. S4A), suggesting a dose-dependent effect of the inhibitors on MSH production. To exclude the effects of altered overall metabolism, glucosamine inositol (1-D-myo-inosityl-2-amino-2-deoxy-α-D-glucopyranoside)—the substrate of MshC characterized by [M-H]−= 340.1249 m/z—was extracted and normalized to protein concentrations. No significant difference was found between untreated or treated cultures (Fig. S4B).

Activity of rebastinib, olverembatinb, and SB-224289 against mycobacteria

Exposure of exponentially-growing Mab to increasing concentrations of rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224289 shows that the three compounds inhibit growth, with the latter one being the most active compound sharing a comparable effect to rifabutin, included as a control drug (39) (Fig. 3, A–C). The MIC50 values of rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224298 were 20.8, 36.9, and 2.4 μM, respectively. While both rebastinib and olverembatinib showed a bacteriostatic effect, SB-224298 exhibited a bactericidal effect, like rifabutin (Table 1). The activity of all three compounds was further confirmed following determination of the CFUs when treating the cultures with 1×, 5×, and 10× MIC50 of rebastinib (Fig. 3D), olverembatinib (Fig. 3E) and SB-244289 (Fig. 3F). The CFU numbers at 72 h of treatment were comparable to those of the inoculum at all concentrations tested for rebastinib and olverembatinib. In contrast, exposure to SB-244289 was associated with a more pronounced killing effect, similar to rifabutin (39).

The activity of all three compounds was next tested using a wide panel of clinical isolates belonging to the Mab complex, as well as against other mycobacterial species. All showed activity against the different clinical isolates, with rebastinib outperforming the efficacy of olverembatinib (Table 2). Among the three inhibitors, SB-244289showed the highest inhibitory activity against most strains, but the MIC50 values of SB-244289 were higher than those of clarithromycin. Interestingly, the three inhibitors were active against M. tuberculosis (10.1–25.5 μM range) but inactive against M. smegmatis (Table 2). The potent activity of SB-224289 prompted to assess the ability of three closely related structural analogs, designated GR-127935, SB-216641 and SB-616234-A (Fig. S5A), to inhibit Mab growth. With MIC50 values of 24, 108 and 9.5 μM, respectively, these compounds appear less active than SB-224289, albeit targeting MshC as can be inferred from their very low activity (MIC >200 μM) against the Mab strain overexpressing mshC (Fig. S5, B and C).

Mutations in MshC confer resistance to rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224289

To address whether mutation in MshC may be responsible for increased levels of resistance to rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224289, spontaneous resistant mutants were selected by plating Mab CIP104536 (S) in the presence of 1×, 5× or 10× MIC50 of each inhibitor. The frequency of resistance for each compound is presented in Table S3. While this frequency was relatively high for rebastinib and olverembatinib (varying between 1.1 × 10−6 and 4 × 10−6, depending on the drug concentration used for selection), it was much lower in the case of SB-224289 (varying between 5.5 × 10−6 at 1× MIC50 to 5 × 10−8 at 10× MIC50). Three rebastinib resistors (designated R1 to R3) and three olverembatinib resistors (designated O1 to O3) were isolated, exhibiting increased resistance levels to rebastinib and olverembatinib, respectively, as compared to the parental strain, while all mutants remained susceptible to rifabutin (Fig. 4A). MIC determination pointed to a >10-fold increased resistance level for R1-R3 (>200 μM) and a >5-fold increased resistance level for O1-O3 (>200 μM). Four selected SB-224289 resistors (designated SB1-SB4) displayed >17-fold increased resistance levels (>50 μM) as compared the parental strain (Table 3). Interestingly, all three mutants selected for resistance to olverembatinib (O1 to O3) showed cross-resistance to rebastinib (5-fold increased resistance as compared to the parental strain) and conversely, all three mutants selected for resistance to rebastinib (R1 to R3) showed cross-resistance to olverembatinib (4 to 5-fold increased resistance as compared to the parental strain) (Fig. 4B). Similarly, all four SB-224289 resistors (SB1-SB4) were highly resistant to the two other compounds (>200 μM) (Table 3). Together, these results suggest that the three inhibitors are very likely sharing the same target and mode of action in Mab.

Comparative analysis of the mshC sequences from R1-R3 with the parental strain identified a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in mshC in all three resistors at position D87 (D87A replacements in R1 and R3 and D87G replacement in R2) (Table 3). In O1 to O3, the SNP in mshC was found at position W227 (W227R replacement in O1 and O2 and W227G replacement in O3). Mutants selected on SB-224289 harbored either a D87A replacement and/or a W227R mutation (Table 3). To further confirm that these sole mutations are responsible for resistance to the inhibitors, recombineering was used to restore a wild-type mshC gene into the O1 and R1 resistant mutants (Fig. S6A). Bacteria in which the R227 and A87 were replaced by W227 and D87 residues, respectively, became fully susceptible to all three inhibitors (Fig. S6B).

Overall, these results highlight the presence of common missense mutations in MshC conferring cross-resistance to rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289 in Mab.

Modulating mshC transcriptional levels affects the activity of rebastinib and olverembatinib

We next investigated whether low mshC transcriptional levels sensitize Mab to the three MshC inhibitors. This was achieved by treating the Mab_pJLR962:mshC with a sub-MIC concentration of ATc (110 ng/ml) (Fig. S1). ATc did not influence the activity of rebastinib, olverembatinib, SB-224289 or rifabutin in Mab_pLJR962 (Fig. 5A, upper panels). In contrast, Mab_pJLR962:mshC showed a 2-fold increased susceptibility to rebastinib, olverembatinib, and a 4-fold increased susceptibility to SB-224289, but not to rifabutin, when increasing the ATc concentration from 0 to 110 ng/ml (Fig. 5A, lower panels and Table S4). These effects could be reproduced when spotting the cultures onto CaMH agar and were particularly pronounced with SB-224289 (Fig. 5B).

In contrast, overexpression of a wild-type copy of mshC under the control of the strong constitutive hsp60 promoter in wild-type Mab (validated by Western blotting using anti-HA antibodies) (Fig. 5C) resulted in high levels of resistance to all three MshC inhibitors, but not to rifabutin (Fig. 5D). This indicates that the mshC expression levels influence the susceptibility profile of Mab to rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289. Overall, these data further support the hypothesis that rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224289 inhibit specifically the MSH pathway.

Systematic assessment of olverembatinib and SB-224289 drug combinations

To evaluate how MshC inhibitors could be integrated into future multidrug regimens, we systematically assessed pairwise combinations of olverembatinib and SB-224289 with 19 additional Mab-active drugs using a diagonal checkerboard approach (DiaMOND; (40)). Most interactions were antagonistic, particularly when MshC inhibitors were combined with aminoglycosides, β-lactams, or quinolones (Fig. 6, A–C and S7, A and B). Despite this overall trend, several potent drug-drug synergies emerged, including multiple combinations with oral agents. SB-224289 synergized strongly with bedaquiline and clofazimine, while olverembatinib showed marked synergy with bedaquiline and macrolides. Linezolid, another commonly used oral drug, was additive with both MshC inhibitors. Overall, drug interaction profiles of olverembatinib and SB-224289 were highly correlated (R2= 0.82).

Discussion

Mab pulmonary diseases pose a significant clinical challenge mainly because of their intrinsic resistance level to many antibiotic classes (11, 41, 42). This resistance highlights the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies. However, the current Mab drug pipeline remains largely underpopulated, emphasizing the need for novel chemical scaffolds and drug targets to combat this pathogen (43, 44). Drug repurposing of compounds for new therapeutic applications is particularly attractive in antibacterials, considering that the repurposing of previously approved drugs can shorten development time and lower costs. While the opportunities and challenges associated with drug repurposing for the treatment of Mab pulmonary infections have been proposed (11, 43, 45, 46), only a few reports discuss novel compounds that are being developed against these emerging pathogens (47).

In this study, CRISPRi-based gene silencing of mshC in Mab indicates that mshC is essential for Mab growth in vitro and during infection in zebrafish larvae, thus validating MshC as an attractive vulnerable site of inhibition, as suggested previously in M. tuberculosis (22, 23). This differs, however, from the situation in M. smegmatis, where mshC is dispensable (24). Our results, thus, support the importance of mshC and MSH production in Mab, as proposed in recent saturated transposon mutagenesis studies (25, 48).

To leverage the essentiality of MshC for drug design, in silico drug screening identified the tyrosine kinase inhibitors, rebastinib and olverembatinib, considered for the treatment of Chronic Myeloid Leukemia, and the human serotonine receptor antagonist SB-224289. While these molecules have not previously been reported as antibacterials, our results emphasize their potent antimycobacterial activity and their mode of action by targeting the MSH pathway in Mab. This is supported by various complementary findings: 1) spontaneous rebastinib-resistant mutants are cross-resistant to olverembatinib and SB-224289 and vice versa, suggesting that the three compounds, which are structurally unrelated, share the same target; 2) missense mutations in MshC at residues 87 and/or 227 are found in all spontaneous resistant mutants; 3) increased resistance to all three compounds in a Mab strain overproducing MshC; 4) reduced MIC values of rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289 in the mshC CRISPRi mutant treated at low ATc concentrations; 5) docking studies revealing binding of all three inhibitors into the same cavity in MshC; 6) a dose-dependent decrease of MSH production in Mab cultures exposed to sub-MIC levels of each inhibitor.

While MIC values of rebastinib and olverembatinib are higher than those of standard drugs, such as amikacin or clarithromycin, the MIC of SB-224289 is comparable to the one of rifabutin and found to be active in all Mab isolates tested. However, all three compounds were effective against a panel of M. abscessus complex clinical strains. Interestingly, these inhibitors also showed potent in vitro activity against other opportunistic NTM species, including Mycobacterium fortuitum as well as against M. tuberculosis, but were inactive against M. smegmatis, presumably because mshC is not essential in this species (24). Thus, the MSH pathway represents a therapeutic target to be further explored in Mab, which aligns with the recent demonstration that the NADPH-dependent MSH reductase (Mtr), catalyzing the reduction of oxidized mycothione (MSSM) into MSH, while not essential in vitro, is important for intramacrophage replication and survival of Mab in Galleria mellonella (49).

Rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289 are structurally unrelated and do not share structural relatedness with previously characterized M. tuberculosis MshC inhibitors, such as cysteinyl-sulfonamides (31), NTF1836 (50) or dequalinium (30). Our work shows that these three inhibitors represent promising molecular scaffolds targeting MshC in Mab. Structural superposition of modeled Mab-MshC⋅SB-224289 complex with the crystallographic structures of Ms-MshC⋅CysSA (PDB: 3C8Z) and Ms-MshC⋅Compound 7b (PDB: 8HFN) showed that all three ligands bound in the catalytic pocket (Fig. S8, A and B). Similar to the CysSA and 7b, the indole-oxadiazole moiety of SB-224289 likely occupies the same canonical cysteinyl-adenylate (Cys-AMP) reaction intermediate binding site. However, the multi-heterocycle group of SB-224289 extends toward the new binding channel formed the C-terminal residues of I178-R188 helix and N-terminal ends of the other two α-helixes (D86-G97 and W227-L239) (Fig. S8, C and D). As the three identified Mab-MshC inhibitors are affected by residues D87 and W227, both located in the same channel region, this suggests that Mab-MshC possesses an unexpected potential channel, as an auxiliary pocket, that can be targeted.

SB-224289, the most potent compound in vitro, could not be assessed in the zebrafish model due to its low solubility in water, which requires adding the compound directly to fish water (51). However, our data on a small set of structural analogs of SB-224289 (Fig. S5) clearly shows the possibility to manipulate these compounds, implying that future chemical improvements may help to translate into new analogs with optimized biophysical and pharmacological properties and to evaluate their safety profile and efficacy in pre-clinical models. The elucidation of the crystal structure of the Mab-MshC.inhibitor complexes should greatly assist in guiding structure-activity relationship studies to generate a new generation of analogs with enhanced effect. Structural characterization of these complexes is particularly important, as the poor aqueous solubility of rebastinib, olverembatinib, and SB-224289 prevented reliable measurement of binding constants with purified MshC (data not shown).

Given the potent anti-M. abscessus activity of MshC inhibitors, we also examined whether they could be effectively integrated into multidrug treatment regimens by assessing pairwise drug combinations. Consistent with prior reports in mycobacteria and other bacterial species, most drug interactions were not synergistic (52, 53). Nevertheless, MshC inhibitors showed strong synergies with several of the most effective oral agents currently used to treat Mab disease, including macrolides, clofazimine and bedaquiline. Linezolid, another important component of current oral regimens, showed additive interactions with olverembatinib and SB-224289. These findings suggest that MshC inhibitors have potential not only as potent standalone agents but also as promising partners for oral, long-term combination therapy for Mab.

In conclusion, our work unraveled new MshC-specific inhibitors as promising antimicrobial agents with potential for clinical application in Mab infections, and identified a new binding channel for drug discovery besides the canonical intermediate binding site. Their unique mode of action, synergistic effects with standard and non-standard antibiotics position them as candidates for further developments against Mab pulmonary diseases.

Experimental procedures

Plasmids and primers

Plasmids and primers are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Reagents

Rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289 were purchased from MedChemExpress. Rifabutin was obtained from Selleckchem. All reagents were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

Bacterial growth conditions

Smooth (S) and rough (R) variants of Mab CIP104536T (54) were used to inhibit mshC expression. All mycobacterial strains, including clinical isolates, were routinely cultured at 37 °C in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (BD Difco) supplemented with 0.025% Tyloxapol (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10% oleic acid, albumin, dextrose, and catalase (OADC enrichment; BD Difco) (7H9T/OADC) or on Middlebrook 7H10 agar (BD Difco) containing 10% OADC (7H10OADC) with antibiotics added when necessary. For drug susceptibility testing, bacteria were grown in cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CaMHB; Sigma-Aldrich). To evaluate the growth of the mshC_CRISPRi mutants, cultures were spotted onto 7H10OADC and CaMH agar (CaMHA), prepared by mixing 8.8g of CaMHB with 6g of agar (Sigma-Aldrich) in 400 ml of water.

Drug susceptibility testing

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined by using the resazurin microtiter assay (REMA) in which bacterial cultures from the exponential growth phase were diluted to an optical density 600 nm (OD600) of 0.01. 50 μl of bacterial culture was added to each well in a sterile 96-well plate along with 50 μl of serial two-fold dilutions of the test compound. Each plate also contained a drug-free growth control. 200 μl of sterile water was added to the outer perimeter wells to avoid evaporation during incubation. The plates were covered with self-adhesive membranes and incubated at 37 °C for one to five days, depending on the strain. Then, 40 μl of 0.025% (w/v) resazurin solution was added to each well before overnight incubation. Fluorescence was measured by using a spectrophotometer multimode microplate reader (Tecan Spark 10M; Tecan Group Ltd). Results are expressed as RFU (%), corresponding to treated RFU / untreated control RFU × 100, with the untreated control set to 100%, reflecting bacterial growth measured by the resazurin assay. The concentrations required to inhibit bacterial growth by 50% (MIC50) were determined by fitting the curve with a sigmoidal dose-response using the GraphPad Prism software (version 6.05).

To evaluate whether the compound had a bactericidal or bacteriostatic effect, plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3 days. Then, 10 μl of bacterial suspension was transferred to each well in new plates containing 7H10OADC and further incubated at 37 °C for three more days before taking a picture. The MBC (Minimum Bactericidal Concentration) is the lowest drug concentration that results in no visible bacterial growth. The drug effect was classified as bactericidal if the ratio between MBC and MIC was ≤4, bacteriostatic if the ratio >4 but ≤32, and resistant if the ratio was >32 (55).

In vitro growth inhibition assay

Mab CIP104536T S cultures from the exponential growth phase were diluted to an OD600 of 0.01 and exposed to 1×, 5×, or 10× the MIC50 of rebastinib, olverembatinib and SB-224289 in CaMHB at 37 °C. Rifabutin at 100 μM was used as the control drug. Aliquots were taken at various time points, plated onto 7H10OADC, and incubated at 37 °C for 3 days prior to CFU counting.

Generation of resistant mutants and target identification

250 μl of Mab CIP104536T S cultures from the exponential growth phase were spread onto 7H10OADC supplemented with either rebastinib, olverembatinib or SB-224289 (at up to 10× MIC50), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 3 days. Individual colonies picked up and transferred into 7H9T/OADC supplemented with either rebastinib or olverembatinib (at up to 10× the MIC50) until OD600 reaches 0.8. The MIC of laboratory-generated resistant mutants was determined by using the REMA. Identification of SNPs in the resistant strains was done by PCR amplification using MAB_2116 val F and MAB_2116 val R (Table S2) for full-coverage sequencing of mshC (MAB_2116).

Reversion of mutations in mshC into a wild-type copy

Restoring the wild-type mshC sequence into the spontaneous resistant strains O1 and R1 (carrying the W227R or D87A mutations, respectively) was done using the recombineering method (56). The wild-type mshC sequence was centered in lagging-strand oligonucleotides of 70 nucleotides. O1 and R1 strains were transformed with pJV53, grown to an OD600 of 0.8 in 7H9 broth containing 250 μg/ml kanamycin and exposed to 0.2% acetamide and 0.2% succinate for 24 h in the presence of 0.2 M glycine for 16 h. Competent cells were prepared and transformed with 100 ng of lagging-strand oligonucleotides. After overnight rescue at 37 °C, bacteria were plated on 7H10 agar plates containing 250 μg/ml kanamycin and transformants were screened for restoration of susceptibility to rebastinib, olverembatinib or SB-224289. Reversion of the wild-type mshC gene was subsequently confirmed by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

DNA constructs

To confirm whether mshC is the target gene of the three inhibitors, mshC was PCR-amplified using Mab CIP104536T S chromosomal DNA and the primers MAB_2116 InFus F and MAB_2116 InFus R (Table S2). The mshC gene in fusion with an HA-tag was cloned into the pMV261 (57) to generate pMV261:mshC-HA, and introduced into Mab CIP104536T S. Overexpression of MshC-HA was confirmed by Western blotting using anti-HA antibodies.

To generate the mshC conditional knockdown strains, the pLJR962 was used and sgRNAs targeting mshC were designed using the CHOPCHOP web tool. The forward and reverse 29-nucleotide sgRNA oligonucleotides were synthesized by Sigma-Aldrich. The sgRNA oligonucleotides were annealed and ligated into the BsmBI-linearized pLJR962 using T4 DNA ligase (New England BioLabs). The resulting constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli Stellar competent cells and selected on LB agar supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin (Euromedex). Plasmids were extracted and verified by sequencing using primer 1834 (Table S2) and electroporated into Mab CIP104536T (R). The transformants were selected in the presence of 500 μg/ml kanamycin. Mab CIP104536T (R) mshC_CRISPRi mutants were grown in 7H9T/OADC (OD600 of 1), and 10-fold diluted cultures were spotted onto 7H10OADC or CaMHA supplemented with increasing concentrations of anhydrotetracycline (ATc; Takara-Bio) or doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich) and 500 μg/ml kanamycin, and incubated at 37 °C for 3 days.

To generate Mab CIP104536T (R) mshC_CRISPRi mutants expressing tdTomato, the pTEC27 was electroporated into Mab CIP104536T (R) carrying pLJR962:mshC_sgRNA4 and selected in the presence of 1000 μg/ml hygromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 500 μg/ml kanamycin.

Western blotting

Mab CIP104536T (S) carrying pMV261:mshC was grown in Sauton’s broth containing 0.025% tyloxapol and 500 μg/ml kanamycin. Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 3000g for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in cold PBS containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Cell lysis was performed using 1-mm glass beads and four times 60 s high-speed cycles in a bead beater (Mixer Mill MM 301, Retsch). The lysates were collected, and total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Reagent Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein (10 μg) were separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Immobilon-P, Merck Millipore). Membranes were probed using either rat anti-HA antibodies (Roche) at a 1:5000 dilution or mouse monoclonal antibodies anti-Hsp65 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at a 1:3000 dilution. Membranes were then incubated with either anti-rat or anti-mouse secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP (horseradish peroxidase) at a 1:5000 dilution, and the signal visualized using the SuperSignal West Femto substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific) on a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Transcriptional analyses

Total RNA was isolated, followed by reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) as previously described (58), using the primers listed in (Table S2). The housekeeping gene rpoB served as the reference for relative gene expression analysis.

Metabolite extraction

Mab ATCC19977 strain was cultured in 7H9OADC in a shaking incubator set at 180 rpm and 37 °C. MSH inhibitors (rebastinib, olverembatinib, SB-224289) were spiked into media at 0.5× MIC50 at the same time of bacterial inoculation, together with a group of untreated controls. Bacteria were grown until the OD600 reached 0.5 to 0.8. 10 ml of bacterial cultures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm, 4 °C, to remove the supernatant and washed once with cold sterile PBS. Bacterial pellets were then metabolically quenched by resuspending in 1 ml of cold extraction solution composed of acetonitrile/methanol/H2O (2:2:1; v/v/v). Metabolites were extracted by mechanical lysis of the bacteria-containing solution with 0.1 mm acid-washed zirconia beads twice, each for 30 s using a FastPrep (MP Bio) set to 6.0 m/s, with a 5-min interval on ice. Cell debris was precipitated by centrifugation at 15, 000× g for 5 min, and the cell lysates were transferred to 0.22 μm Spin-X column filters (CoStar) and filtered by centrifugation at 15, 000g for 30 min at room temperature. A 100 μl aliquot of the metabolite solution was then mixed with 100 μl of cold acetonitrile and centrifuged for 10 min at 17, 000× g. The final concentration of 70% acetonitrile was compatible with the starting conditions of chromatography. 100 μl of the supernatant was then transferred into LC-MS V-shaped vials (Agilent 5188–2788), and 4 μl was injected into the LC-MS instrument (Agilent 6545 QToF). The bacterial biomass of the individual samples was determined by measuring the residual protein content of the metabolite extracts using the BCA assay kit (Thermo).

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Aqueous normal-phase liquid chromatography was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system equipped with a binary pump, temperature-controlled autosampler (set at 4 °C) and temperature-controlled column compartment (set at 25 °C) containing a Cogent Diamond Hydride Type C silica column (150 mm × 2.1 mm; dead volume of 315 μl). A flow rate of 0.4 ml/min was used. The elution of polar metabolites was performed using solvent A, consisting of deionized water (resistivity ∼18 MW cm) and 0.2% acetic acid, and solvent B, consisting of 0.2% acetic acid in acetonitrile. The following gradient was used at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min: 0 min, 85% B; 0 to 2 min, 85% B; 3 to 5 min, 80% B; 6 to 7 min, 75% B; 8 to 9 min, 70% B; 10 to 11 min, 50% B; 11.1 to 14 min, 20% B; 14.1 to 25 min, 20% B; and 5-min re-equilibration period at 85% B. For quantification of glucosamine inositol, chromatographic separation was performed with an Agilent InfinityLab Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm (p/n 675,775–924)) column. The HILIC-Z methodology was optimized for polar acidic metabolites. Column compartment was set at 50 °C. For easy and consistent mobile-phase preparation, a concentrated 10x solution consisting of 100 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0) in water was prepared to produce mobile phases A and B. Mobile phase A consisted of 10 mM ammonium acetate in water (pH 9) with a 5 μM Agilent InfinityLab deactivator additive (p/n 5191–4506), and mobile phase B consisted of 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9) in 10:90 (v:v) water/acetonitrile with a 5 μM Agilent InfinityLab deactivator additive (p/n 5191–4506). The following gradient was applied at a flow rate of 0.25 ml/min: 0 min, 96% B; 2 min, 96% B; 5.5 min, 88% B; 8.5 min, 88% B; 9 min, 86% B; 14 min, 86% B; 17 min, 82% B; 23 min, 65% B; 24 min, 65% B; 24.5 min, 96% B; 26 min, 96% B and 3-min of re-equilibration at 96% B.

Accurate mass spectrometry was performed using an Agilent Accurate Mass 6545 QTOF apparatus. Dynamic mass axis calibration was achieved by continuous infusion after chromatography of a reference mass solution using an isocratic pump connected to an ESI ionization source operated in the positive-ion mode. The nozzle and fragmentor voltages were set to 2000 V and 100 V, respectively. The nebulizer pressure was set to 50 psig, and the nitrogen drying gas flow rate was set to 5 L/min. The drying gas temperature was maintained at 300 °C. The MS acquisition rate was 1.5 spectra/sec, and m/z data ranging from 50 to 1200 were stored. This instrument enabled accurate mass spectral measurements with an error of less than 5 parts per million (ppm), a mass resolution ranging from 10, 000 to 45, 000 over the m/z range of 121 to 955 atomic mass units, and a 100, 000-fold dynamic range with picomolar sensitivity. The data were collected in the centroid 4-GHz (extended dynamic range) mode. The detected m/z data were deemed to be metabolites identified based on unique accurate mass-retention time and MS/MS fragmentation identifiers for masses exhibiting the expected distribution of accompanying isotopomers. Typical variation in abundance for most of the metabolites remained between 5 and 10% under these experimental conditions.

Metabolomics data analysis

The extracted ion spectra (EIC) of mycothiol (C17H30N2O12S, [M + H]+= 487.1592 m/z) were extracted from all samples in positive ion mode using the Agilent Qualitative Navigator B.08.00 software. The retention times of the peaks in each sample were aligned to ensure accurate annotation of the molecule, and the abundances over time were extracted from the software and processed using GraphPad Prism.

Zebrafish maintenance and ethics

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were kept and handled in compliance with the guidelines of the European Union for handling laboratory animals and approved by the Direction Sanitaire et Vétérinaire de l’Hérault for the ZEFIX-CRBM zebrafish facility (Montpellier) (registration number C-34–172–39). Experiments were approved by the Ministère de l’Enseignement Supérieur, de la Recherche et de l’Innovation under the reference APAFIS#24406 to 2020022815234677 V3. Experiments were conducted using wild-type AB zebrafish, raised in the ZEFIX-CRBM zebrafish facility and kept on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. Eggs were obtained by natural spawning, quickly bleached and incubated at 28.5 °C in Petri dishes containing E3 medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4).

Zebrafish infection and ATc treatment

At 24 h post-fertilization (hpf), embryos were dechorionated using Pronase (10 mg/ml; Millipore Sigma) for up to 5 min at room temperature, followed by extensive washing in E3 medium and placed in 60 mm Petri dishes containing E3 at 28.5 °C. At 30 hpf, embryos were anesthetized with 0.02% buffered MS222 (Tricaine; ethyl-3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate salt). Borosilicate glass capillaries (O.D. = 1 mm; I.D. = 0.78 mm; 10 cm length) were pulled using a micropipette puller (Sutter instruments P-97). Needles were opened with fine forceps and loaded with 8 μl of the desired inoculum resuspended in Polyvinylpyrolidone 2% (PVP, Cas#9003–39–8) supplemented with 0.05% phenol red for microinjection visualization. Larvae were injected in the caudal vein with 3 nl (containing 212 ± 14 CFU) of either the Mab R_pLJR962 (control) or the Mab R_pLJR962:mshC, previously transformed with pTEC27 expressing TdTomato (59) and selected in the presence of 1000 μg/ml hygromycin or injected with the PVP control. Injected larvae were then rinsed twice and transferred in 60 mm small Petri dish containing embryo media at 28.5 °C. Inoculum was checked a posteriori by microinjecting a PBS drop and plating it on 7H10OADC.

To repress expression of mshC upon infection, half of the larvae from both groups (control and infected) were injected at 1 day post-infection (dpi) intravenously with 10 ng of ATc (5 nl of a 2.0 mg/ml solution in sterile PBS) and the other half with the PBS control (29). Larvae were randomized over survival assays and monitored daily for 11 days (n ≈24 per group per experiment).

Homology modeling and docking studies

The MshC protein sequence from Mab (ATCC19977) was retrieved from the UniProtKB database (accession code: B1MPE6) and submitted to the SWISS-MODEL online workspace (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org) for structural validation. Suitable structural templates were identified using Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) and a Hidden Markov model (HMM-HMM)-based lightning-fast iterative sequence search (HHblits) (60, 61). The top-ranked template was selected to generate an energy-minimised 3D model using the ProMod3 modeling engine (62). The model's quality was evaluated using QMEAN and GMQE scores where a QMEAN score below −4.0 indicates low model quality and GMQE values range from 0 to 1, with higher scores reflecting greater reliability (62, 63). Additional structural validation, including stereochemical quality and Ramachandran plot analysis, was also performed using the SWISS-MODEL platform (64). The homology modeled MshC target protein in PDB format was downloaded from the SWISS-MODEL workspace. The protein structure was then prepared by AutoDock Vina (65).

The docking simulation of a ligand library from the DrugBank database (66), consisting of 15,236 compounds was performed using PyRx virtual screening software with AutoDock Vina (65, 67, 68). The Vina algorithm applies a gradient based search to predict binding affinities and interaction modes of ligands within the homology modeled MshC protein. The top-scoring docking results were further visualized to analyze molecular interactions using Discovery Studio Visualizer v16.1.0.15350.

Drug–drug combination and interaction assessments

Mab ATCC19977 cultures were grown in 7H9 medium supplemented with OADC (10%), glycerol (0.4%) and Tween 80 (0.05%) to logarithmic phase, stored at −80 °C, and diluted to approximately 3 × 106 CFU/ml prior to each assay. Approximately 1.2 × 105 CFUs were dispensed into each well of 384-well plates. Compounds were prepared in 7H9 medium and added to the plates, which were then sealed and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (250 rpm) for 72 h. Optical density (OD600) was measured, and bacterial fitness was calculated as background-corrected OD600 normalized to the median OD600 of growth-control wells on each plate. Twenty single drugs, including olverembatinib and SB-224289, were tested across nine concentrations in log√2-fold dilution steps, with MIC50 set to be around the sixth concentration out of nine for a complete growth spectrum. Pairwise combinations were assessed using the DiaMOND diagonal sampling design (diagonal of a 9 × 9 checkerboard), in which each drug contributes half of the final combination concentration (40). Additivity reference lines were determined according to the Loewe additivity model, which assumes additivity when the combined effect equals that of an equivalent total dose of either compound alone.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed on GraphPad Prism software (version 6.05; San Diego, CA, USA) and detailed in each figure legend. The normality of data was evaluated using a Shapiro-Wilk test.
