Skull base reconstruction strategy for high-flow CSF leaks during EEA surgery based on the quality of pedicled nasoseptal flap
Authors: Yi-Hao Fu, Xiu-Quan Wu, Yao-Wen Luo, Ji-Meng Zhang, Chang-Cai Xie, Yun-Chao Yuan, Yue-fei Zhou, Da-Kuan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a skull base reconstruction strategy for treating intraoperative high-flow cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks during endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) in the sellar region. This single-institution prospective study included patients who underwent EEA with multi-layer reconstruction between May 2023 and April 2025. Patients were divided into groups 1, 2, & 3, based on the quality of the pedicled nasoseptal flap (PNSF): high-quality, low-quality, and unavailable PNSF, respectively. We analyzed postoperative complications (failed skull base reconstruction and meningitis), time in bed, and length of hospital stay. Of 86 patients enrolled, we encountered three cases of failed skull base reconstruction (3.5%) and four cases of meningitis (4.7%), indicating a reduction in the postoperative complication rate. There were no significant differences in postoperative complications between the three groups (P > 0.05). There was no significant difference in lengths of hospital stay (10.82 ± 3.80 vs. 12.41 ± 2.26, P > 0.05) and time in bed (9.00 ± 4.06 vs. 10.00 ± 2.36, P > 0.05) between Groups 1 and 2, respectively. Group 3 had longer lengths of hospital stay and time in bed than the other groups (P < 0.001). Introduction

Intraoperative high-flow cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak during endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) surgery is a significant risk factor for postoperative CSF leak and intracranial infections.1 Clinical studies have demonstrated that postoperative infection rates associated with intraoperative high-flow CSF leaks can reach up to 15%, impairing healing, prolonging hospitalization, and increasing the patients’ financial burden.2 EEA takes advantage of the natural nasal corridors to achieve optimal lesion exposure; however, unavoidable intraoperative high-flow CSF leaks remain a surgical challenge. Consequently, effective CSF leak repair and skull base reconstruction during EEA have emerged as pivotal concerns in contemporary neurosurgical practice.

Neurosurgeons with integrated otorhinolaryngological expertise have established pedicled nasoseptal flap(PNSF) as an important technique for skull base reconstruction, with clinical studies demonstrating a 92% success rate.3 The application of the PNSF during EEA surgery plays a critical role in ensuring reliable skull base reconstruction.3–5 Particularly, the incorporation of autologous in-situ bone flaps and fascia lata, either individually or in combination, significantly enhances the biomechanical stability of the reconstructed skull base.6–9 However, the selection of the reconstruction strategy largely depends on the surgery teams’ mature expertise and preference in reconstruction techniques.10,11 Thus, the formulation of a reconstruction strategy for intraoperative high-flow CSF leak remains controversial, which has not yet formed a fixed pattern or standard.

Therefore, we established an individualized strategy based on the quality of the PNSF for skull base reconstruction. We collected clinical data from recent cases of intraoperative high-flow CSF leak, and analyzed surgical videos and imaging records to evaluate the efficacy of an individualized skull base reconstruction strategy. We aimed to compare complications (failed skull base reconstruction, meningitis), time in bed, length of hospital stay, and other clinical data in cases among the groups with different qualities of PNSF, and then summarize pivotal clinical experiences to provide evidence-based guidance for subsequent practice.

Methods

Recruitment and screening of patients

This study included 86 patients who underwent EEA lesion resection for sellar at the Department of Neurosurgery, Xijing Hospital of Fourth Military Medical University (Neuroendoscopy Training Base, China) between May 2023 and April 2025, experienced intraoperative high-flow CSF leaks, and underwent skull base reconstruction. All surgeries were performed by an experienced neuroendoscopy team. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (KY20252079-F-1) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500105717). Informed consents were provided from the enrolled patients and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria were patients aged between 18 and 60 years, with intraoperative high-flow CSF leak (a dural defect between 1 and 3 cm, grade 2 and 3 CSF leak),12 and in good general health without diabetes, pituitary dysfunction, or other conditions that may affect healing, without pre-operative nasal cavity intracranial infection.

Pre-operative assessment

Before surgery, demographic characteristics, medical history, and routine and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were collected. Both routine and contrast-enhanced MRI scans were performed to delineate the tumor volume and texture. EEA for lesion resection and skull base reconstruction

Patients were positioned supine in a head-up-foot-down position with the head tilted 25° to the left. A 0.01% epinephrine-saline solution was used to soak the cotton strips for nasal packing, which were placed in the nasal cavity to achieve vasoconstriction, minimize intraoperative bleeding, and maintain optimal visualization. While harvesting the PNSF, the surgeon first examined the condition of the nasal cavity and checked for deviations in the nasal septum or nasal spurs. A PNSF was separated from the subperichondrial dissection along the nasal septum, and the basal part was retained from the opening of the sphenoid sinus to the posterior nasal arch. Care was taken to preserve the sphenopalatine artery, and the vascular pedicle was kept sufficiently wide to ensure adequate blood supply to the PNSF. Following preparation of the PNSF, the vascular condition and integrity were assessed. It was then transposed into the nasopharynx for subsequent use in skull base reconstruction. Then fractured nasal septum and the nasal septum bone flaps were used as backup materials for the skull base reconstruction. The sphenoid sinus was widely exposed by drilling the anterior and lateral walls and smoothing the bony protrusions to prevent residual bony spicules from causing mechanical damage to the surface of the PNSF or its vascular pedicle. An in-situ bone flap (encompassing the planum sphenoidale, tuberculum sellae, and sellar floor) was created if bone quality of skull base permitted, enabling en bloc removal for subsequent complete bony reconstruction. The range of the bone window also included halfway through the bilateral optic nerve canals and extended to the surface of the internal carotid artery. When the in-situ bone flap was compromised due to intraoperative damage, excessive angulation, or tumor invasion of the skull base bone, nasal septum bone flap was used as a substitute for multi-layer reconstruction.

The fascia lata, valued for its tensile strength and plasticity, was harvested from the lateral thigh through a longitudinal incision (4 ~ 5 cm in length) along the upper third of the line connecting the greater trochanter to the lateral patella. After layered dissection, the fascia lata sized according to the skull base defect was excised. Meticulous hemostasis was achieved without drainage, and the wound was closed in layers using barbed sutures. The harvested graft was then trimmed of excess adipose tissue to minimize liquefaction risks and immersed in saline to preserve integrity before reconstruction.

During skull base reconstruction, an individualized strategy was selected based on the specific characteristics of the dural and/or bony defects and the quality of the PNSF. The assessment criteria for the quality of PNSF included absence of nasal septal spurs or deviation; structural integrity (absence of defects or lacerations); complete coverage of the skull base defect with a 5-mm margin beyond its edges; visual assessment of flap color and ischemic areas indicating adequate perfusion (a highly vascularized flap was without ischemic areas and maintained a red appearance) based on the team’s practical experience; and thickness > 2 mm to ensure reliability and effective barrier function.14 The patients were grouped as follows:

Definition of variables

The outcome variable was the presentation of a failed skull base reconstruction (postoperative CSF leak) and meningitis (intracranial infection). The diagnosis of postoperative CSF leak was made based on clinical criteria such as the existence of frank rhinoliquorrhea, intracranial pneumocephalus revealed by postoperative computed tomography (CT) scan and positive glucose in nasal discharge. The diagnosis of meningitis was made based on symptoms such as fever and the result of CSF test. The time in bed was defined as the postoperative period until MRI confirmed the enhancement of PNSF, without fever or CSF leaks observed.​ Discharge criteria included the removal of surgical packing and the cessation of intravenous medications and a postoperative CT scan confirmed the absence of pneumocephalus after ambulation.

Other variables: Mean tumor maximum diameter, mean tumor volume, mean body mass index (BMI), and mean operative time.

Postoperative evaluation and management

After lesion resection, specimens were sent for pathological examination to confirm the diagnosis. Postoperative MRI was routinely performed to assess the extent of resection. The standardized postoperative antibiotic regimen consisted of a third-generation cephalosporin, administering for five days. If intracranial infection was suspected, meropenem and vancomycin were initiated and then adjusted based on drug sensitivity results. Lumbar drainage was not a standard postoperative measure in our protocol. It was employed when severe meningitis or CSF leak occurred to drain CSF. Patients underwent the first follow-up MRI within 3 months postoperatively, with evaluation of T1- and T2-weighted, and contrast enhancement signal intensities. For cases involving in-situ bone flap reconstruction, additional thin-slice CT scans and three-dimensional reconstruction of the skull base were performed to evaluate bony reconstruction. Multidisciplinary management was coordinated by Endocrinology, Ophthalmology and Otolaryngology specialists.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The normality test confirmed that it follows a normal distribution. Intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). For group comparisons, the chi-square test was used to analyze categorical variables. Fisher’s exact test was used when the expected frequency was < 1. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and surgical characteristics

This study recruited 86 patients with high-flow intraoperative CSF leaks who underwent skull base reconstruction, including 36 males and 50 females with a mean age of 48.23 years. The mean length of hospital stay was 13.86 days, and the mean operative time was 174.79 min. The mean time spent in bed was 11.48 days. The mean BMI was 24.12 kg/m². The mean maximum tumor diameter was 2.95 cm, and the mean volume was 10.89 cm3. Gross tumor resection was achieved in 96.5% of cases. Three (3.5%) patients experienced failed skull base reconstruction, and four (4.7%) patients developed meningitis after surgery. Pathological classifications included pituitary adenoma (n = 38), craniopharyngioma (n = 24), meningioma (n = 18), Rathke’s cyst (n = 3) and suprasellar arachnoid cyst (n = 3)(Table 1).

Comparison of complications and clinical data between the three reconstruction plans

Among all patients with intraoperative high-flow CSF leaks, 28 were in Group 1 with a high-quality PNSF; 29 cases were in Group 2 where the fascia lata was used to reinforce the reconstruction (onlay) with the low-quality PNSF; and there were 29 cases in Group 3 where the PNSF was unusable, and double fascia lata reinforcement (inlay+onlay) was used.

Postoperatively, one patient (3.6%) experienced failed skull base reconstruction and required a reoperation for reconstruction in Group 1. The predominant cause of reconstruction failure was ischemic necrosis of the PNSF. Meningitis occurred in two patients (6.9%) in Group 2, and both were successfully treated with intravenous antibiotics. Two patients (6.9%) had meningitis accompanied by failed skull base reconstruction and underwent a second surgery for reconstruction in Group 3. In addition to intravenous antibiotics, lumbar CSF drainage was performed to treat meningitis. Discussion

Development of EEA surgery, postoperative complications and their hazards

With continuous advances in medical procedures and a deeper understanding of sellar anatomy, endonasal endoscopic surgery has become a cornerstone approach for the treatment of sellar region lesions.15–18 Compared to microscopic surgery, these advantages are more pronounced in skull base surgery.19 As surgical expertise improves, EEA surgery can now be used to resect larger and deeper tumors. However, by disrupting the natural barrier sealing the sterile intracranial compartment from the contaminated nasal cavity, this approach generates iatrogenic high-flow CSF leaks, which significantly increases the risk of postoperative CSF leakage and intracranial infection.10,20,21 Therefore, the demand for reliable skull base reconstruction is becoming increasingly critical with the increasing procedural complexity.22 The reported rates of postoperative CSF leak range from 8.1% to 10%, and the associated infection incidence is between 5.5% and 22%.20,23–26 Untreated CSF leaks may lead to life-threatening complications, such as meningitis, brain abscess, pneumocephalus, or even death.27.

Factors such as the patient’s constitution and the characteristics of the tumor affect the occurrence of postoperative CSF leaks. BMI is a risk factor for postoperative CSF leaks.28 When BMI increases by 5 kg/m² compared to normal levels, the incidence of postoperative CSF leaks increases by 1.61 times.29 The size and texture of the tumor and the size of the dural defect during surgery are also key factors that affect postoperative CSF leaks. Additionally, low protein levels, chronic respiratory diseases, and the use of certain drugs (leuprorelin and bromocriptine) are also related to the occurrence of postoperative CSF leaks and should be noted.30–32.

Application of multi-layered skull base reconstruction techniques

The outcome of the skull base repair directly affects the overall effectiveness of the surgery. When formulating a strategy for skull base reconstruction, surgeons need to comprehensively consider factors such as the size and location of the lesion, the severity of intraoperative CSF leaks, and appropriate skull base reconstruction materials.13 The multi-layer skull base reconstruction technique, as a stable and efficient method for skull base repair, significantly enhances the repair strength of the skull base by using autologous materials such as in-situ bone flaps, fascia lata, and PNSF, as well as artificial materials such as artificial biomembranes and biological protein glue.31 The key point of skull base reconstruction is to reconstruct layer-by-layer in the reverse direction of the surgical resection to strengthen the skull base, combining membranous reconstruction with bony reconstruction.10 Material selection varies across centers and is influenced by surgeon and institutional experience. Building upon the abovementioned foundational repair theory, more techniques have been developed, including dural suturing, hydroxyapatite sealing, and PNSF coverage.33–35.

The PNSF serves as a critical material in multi-layer skull base reconstruction

The PNSF plays a crucial role in skull base reconstruction, as confirmed from practice in various centers.36–38 A high-quality PNSF must maintain adequate integrity and thickness.35 During flap preparation, careful evaluation of the condition of the nasal cavity is necessary. The nasal septum should be in good condition without nasal spurs or deviation. The base and vascular pedicle of the flap should be sufficiently wide to ensure adequate blood supply. Moreover, the sphenoid sinus should be widely exposed by drilling the anterior and lateral walls and smoothing the bony protrusions to prevent residual bony spurs from damaging the surface of the PNSF or compressing vascular pedicle. And also reduce the damage caused by operational factors during the production process. Based on the location and size of the skull base defect, the flap should ideally cover the skull base defect, with the margin extending at least 5 mm beyond the defect edges. Insuring adequate blood supply and thickness are important criteria for determining the quality of the PNSF.39 When fixing a PNSF, the base should be protected to prevent excessive compression, which may affect the blood supply. Indocyanine green angiography can evaluate the vascularity and perfusion of the PNSF in real-time.39 Postoperative enhanced MRI remains a noninvasive and effective method for assessing the blood supply of the PNSF.26.

Individualized skull base reconstruction strategy and key points of the operation

This study emphasized the importance of an individualized strategy. We designed an individualized strategy for different skull base conditions and optimized the multi-layer skull base reconstruction technique. Importantly, we evaluated the quality of the patient’s PNSF and flexibly selected different repair materials based on the specific characteristics of the skull base defect. For dural defects < 1 cm in size, an artificial biomembrane is used for subdural repair. The outer layer is covered with a PNSF that can provide sufficient repair strength.40 When the diameter of the dural defect is 1 ~ 3 cm, it is considered extensive and results in a significant high-flow CSF leak.41 For the multi-layer reconstruction plan at our center, we first assess the quality of the PNSF. When the quality of the PNSF is ideal, an artificial biomembrane is selected for membranous reconstruction (inlay), and an in situ bone flap or nasal septum bone flap is used to cover the bony reconstruction. The outer layer is then covered by the PNSF to achieve the multi-layer reconstruction. When the PNSF is of poor quality, following the repair with an artificial biomembrane (inlay), the reconstruction is reinforced with a fascia lata (onlay) and finally covered with a PNSF. When the PNSF is unusable, after completing the membrane reconstruction with an artificial biomembrane, the fascia lata (inlay) is used for reinforcement repair under the dura, and another layer of fascia lata (onlay) covers the dural defect (Table 4). This individualized strategy mimics the normal skull base anatomical layers, conforms to the principles of biomechanics, and is consistent with the “stratification of patients” theory proposed by Gardner et al.10 Additionally, when the residual area is large after lesion resection, it is filled with fat after diaphragmatic sellae repair.41 A dual-layer fascia lata suturing with fat graft can ensure the repair strength and prevent the fat graft shifting and compression of the optic nerve after surgery (Fig. 6). This individualized strategy has wide applicability and can reduce reliance on expensive artificial materials. Therefore, it can ensure repair strength while also being economical and practical.

In this study, we found that when applying individualized strategies, the incidence rates of failed skull base reconstruction and meningitis among patients were relatively low (3.5% & 4.7%), and the success rates were significantly higher than that of the simple PNSF.12,26,42 There were no significant differences among the groups in this study, confirming that individualized strategies for solving high-flow CSF leaks in different situations are reliable. There were no significant differences in the mean operative time, mean maximum tumor diameter, mean tumor volume, or BMI among the groups. This individualized skull-based reconstruction strategy is applicable across a wide range of clinical scenarios and has demonstrated effectiveness in various situations.

In the high-quality PNSF group, the flap with a well-vascularized pedicle, which possesses a strong healing capacity, allowed for early postoperative ambulation and consequently shortened the length of hospital stay. In the low-quality PNSF group, the hospitalization period was extended appropriately to ensure patient safety and complete wound healing. In the unusable PNSF group, where fascia lata grafts were used, the reconstruction was performed entirely using avascular tissue, and fibrosis was prolonged, resulting in significantly longer bed rest and overall hospitalization duration than those in the other two groups. These patients required close monitoring of nasal discharge and body temperature.

In this study, one patient in the high-quality PNSF group experienced failed skull base reconstruction. During the reoperation, endoscopic examination revealed ischemic necrosis of the flap. This was likely attributable to injury to the feeding artery during drilling of the anterior sphenoid wall or compression of the pedicle by the packing material used for flap fixation. In subsequent practice, ensuring a wider pedicle and more extensive drilling of the anterior sphenoid wall should be performed to minimize sharp compression and mitigate such complications. In the poor-quality PNSF group, two patients developed postoperative meningitis. This was primarily associated with the inferior quality of the flaps, leading to prolonged healing, nasal packing and extended the time in bed, increasing the risk of opportunistic bacterial colonization in the nasal cavity, which increased the risk of opportunistic infections. In the unusable PNSF group, two cases of failed skull base reconstruction with meningitis occurred. The avascular fascia lata graft, with its inferior healing capability compared to that of the pedicled tissue, likely contributed to the failure to form an effective scar tissue within the conventional healing period. Additionally, incomplete drilling of the sphenoid septum, resulting in an uneven skull base, may have caused poor adherence of the graft edges, leaving gaps that impede healing. The prolonged time in bed in these two patients, coupled with communication between the sterile intracranial space and the nasal cavity, potentially facilitated bacterial infection, leading to meningitis.

It also should be particularly emphasized that we creatively used grinding drills to make a planum sphenoidale-tuberculum sellae-sellar floor in-situ bone flap8, whose advantage lies in the fact that the source is autogenous bone from the skull base, and there is no rejection reaction.43,44 High-flow CSF leaks resemble torrential floods affecting the PNSF, whereas the in-situ bone flap achieving bony reconstruction reduces a deluge into a mild trickle. During the repair of the skull base, covering the defect of the dura with an in-situ bone flap enables the dura to closely adhere to the skull, which can effectively block the pulsatile pressure of the CSF and reduce the impact on the repair material of the skull base.45 By relying on the anatomical characteristics of the sellar tubercle and optic canal, anatomical repositioning can be achieved during the repair of the in-situ bone flap, thereby reducing the formation of the residual cavity. Simultaneously, it provides a growth base for the outer PNSF and an effective attachment platform for scar repair in the surgical area, which plays a significant role in the process of skull base repair.46 When the in-situ bone flap cannot be properly reconstructed in its original position owing to defects, fractures, tumor invasion, or a steep angle, the nasal septum bone flap can be used for reinforcement based on the condition of the skull base. Based on years of production experience, relevant insights have been summarized as follows: during the preparation of the in-situ bone flap, the sphenoid sinus should be fully exposed, and key structures (optic canal, internal carotid artery bulge, optic carotid recess, etc.) must be visible. A 3/4-mm diamond abrasive drill is used to polish the skull base bone, smooth it out, and make the edges of the bone flap resemble an eggshell. The bone flap area should include the planum sphenoidale, tuberculum sellae, and sellar floor. The ideal shape of the in-situ bone flap should be “S” shaped. The expanded area should include the bone of the optic canal and the surface of the carotid prominence. The relative relationship between the bone window, bone flap, and dural defect should be such that the bone flap is larger than the dural defect and the bone window can accommodate the bone flap, ensuring that the bone flap does not slip into the subdural space and cause compression.

Research limitations

This study had certain limitations. The strict inclusion criteria may limit generalizability, but ensure cohort homogeneity. The research scope was limited to the sellar area, and the collected cases involved various lesions; however, the sample size for each disease was relatively small. No stratified analysis was conducted for each pathological type, which may have affected the efficacy of the analysis. Therefore, the statistical results may have inevitable deviations. Finally, the postoperative follow-up period was limited, and the long-term outcomes of individualized strategies remain unknown. In the future, our findings may require validation in a multicenter prospective study.

Conclusions

There is no single optimal solution for repairing intraoperative high-flow CSF leaks. The reconstruction strategy should be determined based on comprehensive factors, including lesion location, defect size, residual cavity dimensions, CSF leak level, and the patient’s physical condition. A complete and well-vascularized PNSF is crucial for skull base reconstruction. The in-situ bone flap has clear advantages as a rigid support that enables the combined application of membranous and bony reconstruction. The individualized skull base reconstruction strategy is associated with reduced postoperative complications. When performed by an experienced neuroendoscopic team, individualized strategies can be selected to achieve ideal skull base reconstruction.
