Developmental correlates of counterfactual reasoning of different structures in Chinese preschool children
Authors: Yanwen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLinguistic cues for expressing counterfactuality may affect children’s counterfactual reasoning performance. Subtractive and additive counterfactual premises contain different linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality, to interpret and reason from which may involve different cognitive abilities. This study investigated the developmental correlates of both subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning.MethodsFrom February to April 2023, 147 Chinese 4- to 6-year-olds received tasks assessing subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning, verbal short-term memory, inhibitory control, and language. Binary logistic regression and generalised estimating equations compared the associations between the two forms of counterfactual reasoning and other cognitive abilities.ResultsInhibitory control was significantly associated with counterfactual reasoning in general. Verbal short-term memory was significantly associated with subtractive but not additive counterfactual reasoning. For children with above-median language scores, task order was significantly associated with counterfactual reasoning in general.ConclusionsSubtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning had both similar and different developmental correlates. These findings support the proposal that the presence of different linguistic cues for expressing counterfactuality in subtractive and additive counterfactual premises may shape differences in development between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning. Future studies may further explore how language and cognition interact with each other in shaping children’s mental landscapes.Keywords: Counterfactual reasoning, Language, Executive functions

Introduction

In daily life, people often think about “what if” possibilities, such as “If I had left home 5 min earlier, I would have caught the bus”. This ability, or counterfactual reasoning, helps “explain the past, prepare for the future, modulate emotional experience, and support moral judgments” ([4], p. 136). Different forms of counterfactual reasoning are likely to involve different cognitive abilities, which is examined in this study.

Counterfactual reasoning

Counterfactual reasoning refers to the ability to infer what outcomes would have resulted following changes to specific past events or states [16]. For example, a match was soaked in water and was not scratched. If it had been scratched, it would not have lit. There are two criteria for counterfactual outcomes to be plausible following counterfactual premises [20]. First, the counterfactual antecedent holds, i.e., the match was scratched. Second, reasoners maximise resemblance between the counterfactual world and reality, i.e., they retain the fact that the match was wet. The second criterion is called the nearest possible world constraint. This constraint asks reasoners to “retain as many features of the past as possible and change only those that are causally dependent on the counterfactual supposition” ([15], p. 2). The present study considers the nearest possible world constraint as a defining feature of counterfactual reasoning. Only when people follow this constraint can counterfactual reasoning help them learn from the past to prepare for the future. If people disregard the past reality and reason by general regularities in the world (e.g., Generally, if a match is scratched, it will light), they engage in basic conditional reasoning not counterfactual reasoning.

Counterfactual reasoning is important for goal pursuit and science learning. In goal pursuit, triggered by unsatisfactory performance, counterfactual reasoning identifies a causal relation between an option and the achievement of a goal (e.g., If I had left home 5 min earlier, I would have caught the bus). This causal insight makes people realise the usefulness of the option, heightening their intention to take it (e.g., I want to leave home 5 min earlier tomorrow). When there are opportunities for action execution, counterfactual reasoning alleviates problems and enhances performance (e.g., leaving home earlier on the next day) [17]. It helps people achieve both promotion goals of advancement and accomplishment and prevention goals of safety, security, and responsibilities [8, 18]. In science learning, counterfactual reasoning raises sensitivity to alternative possibilities and boosts debiased reasoning [5], helping people overcome misconceptions in naïve theories. Also, counterfactual reasoning involves fixing the value of a variable and inferring downstream causal effects. It acts as a thought experiment, promoting experimentation skills and conceptual knowledge [14]. Overall, counterfactual reasoning scaffolds scientific learning as “a core objective for developmental psychologists and educators alike” ([12], p. 224).

Subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning

Studies have shown that preschool years (4 to 6 years old) are important for the development of counterfactual reasoning as a crucial cognitive ability [6]. During this period, counterfactual reasoning of different structures, more specifically subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning, may develop in different ways. Subtractive counterfactual reasoning removes existing elements from reality (e.g., If I had not left home 5 min earlier, I would not have caught the bus) while additive counterfactual reasoning introduces new elements to reality (e.g., If I had left home 5 min earlier, I would have caught the bus) [19]. To draw a fuller picture of counterfactual reasoning development, this study investigated the developmental correlates of both forms of counterfactual reasoning.

There are four accounts explaining the difference between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning, which are summarised in Table 1. The pragmatic cue account and the linguistic cue account are about counterfactual premise interpretation, while the counterfactual scenario complexity account and the reality contrast account are about counterfactual outcome deduction. These accounts are explained in more detail below.

Differences between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning

The first three accounts are about verbal short-term memory, or the storage of speechlike materials [1]. Verbal short-term memory is highly relevant for solving counterfactual reasoning tasks. In such tasks, to ensure that young children understand what really happened, typically, researchers explicitly describe event sequences. Verbal short-term memory stores event descriptions, facilitating children to follow the nearest possible world constraint when answering counterfactual questions. The first three accounts about verbal short-term memory are described below.

First, the pragmatic cue account is as follows. Subtractive but not additive counterfactual premises directly mention what really happened. For example, in a counterfactual reasoning task, children are told a story where it rained and a child A got wet. A subtractive counterfactual premise is “If it had not rained”. An additive counterfactual premise is “If A had taken an umbrella when going out”. Only the subtractive counterfactual premise explicitly refers to the past event of raining, which makes it easier for children to recall information about the past rain, which is crucial for making counterfactual inferences, following subtractive than additive counterfactual premises. Hence, the pragmatic cue account predicts that the verbal short-term memory load would be higher for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning. A higher memory load would make performance worse for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning.

Second, the linguistic cue account is as follows. Linguistic cues for expressing hypotheticality, factuality, and negation, all of which are key components of counterfactuality, are useful for interpreting counterfactual premises [21]. Negation is typically expressed by negators, which are present in subtractive but not additive counterfactual premises. For example, in Chinese, a subtractive counterfactual premise is “ruguo meiyou xiayu - If not-exist/have rain - If it had not rained”. An additive counterfactual premise is “ruguo A dai san chumen - If A take umbrella go-out - If A had taken an umbrella when going out”. While both subtractive and additive counterfactual premises contain a conjunction to suggest hypotheticality, only the subtractive premise contains the negator “meiyou” (not exist/have). Negators like “meiyou” (not exist/have) apply to events or entities that already existed at the time indicated in the premise, implying both negation and factuality [21]. Thus, there are more linguistic cues that are helpful for inferring counterfactuality in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises, making performance better for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Another prediction that the linguistic cue account makes is that as the interpretation of subtractive counterfactual premises involves mentally storing more linguistic cues, the verbal short-term memory load would be higher for solving subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning tasks.

To evaluate this account, it is helpful to study Chinese-speaking children. For languages in general, there are more linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality, i.e., an extra negator, in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises. The effect of the negator for facilitating counterfactual premise interpretation may be relatively large in Chinese. In languages such as English, counterfactuality is directly marked by subjunctive forms (e.g., If it had not rained). These forms suggest that an event did not happen but is assumed to have happened, reducing the importance of negators for enhancing counterfactuality. In contrast, Chinese does not have subjunctive forms. It often conveys counterfactuality by lexical items that express the various components of counterfactuality, such as “ruguo” (if) for expressing hypotheticality and “meiyou” (not exist/have) that implies both negation and factuality [26]. As a result, the presence or absence of negators like “meiyou” (not exist/have) has a relatively strong effect on counterfactual premise interpretation, leading to a difference between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning [24]. While this study did not compare different languages, it chose Chinese-speaking children as a valuable test case to assess the difference between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning.

Third, the counterfactual scenario complexity account is as follows. When subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning alter the same past scenario, subtractive premises remove events while additive premises add events to reality [19]. Therefore, there tends to be more events in additive than subtractive counterfactual scenarios. For the above example, the reality is: It rained and A got wet (two events). The subtractive counterfactual scenario is: A did not get wet (one event). The additive counterfactual scenario is: A took an umbrella when going out, it rained, and A did not get wet (three events). Hence, controlling for reality, additive counterfactual scenarios are more complex than subtractive counterfactual scenarios. Reasoners need to retain more events in mind to construct additive than subtractive counterfactual scenarios. Hence, the counterfactual scenario complexity account predicts that the verbal short-term memory load would be higher for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning. A higher memory load would make performance worse for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning.

Finally, the fourth account, the reality contrast account, is about inhibitory control, or “the ability to deliberately control or inhibit dominant or automatic behaviors, responses, or thoughts” ([2], p.14). This account is as follows. Subtractive but not additive counterfactual reasoning directly negates what really happened. For example, “If it had not rained” is in direct contrast to the past event of raining. In contrast, to reason from the additive counterfactual premise “If A had taken an umbrella when going out”, reasoners can retain the past event of raining and add an event to it (taking an umbrella). As subtractive but not additive counterfactual reasoning directly negates what really happened, reasoners need to more actively inhibit their knowledge of the past in subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Hence, the reality contrast account predicts that the inhibitory control load would be higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. A higher inhibitory control load would make performance worse for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning.

In short, the pragmatic cue account, the linguistic cue account, and the counterfactual scenario complexity account all predict that children’s performance would be better for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning, while the reality contrast account predicts that children’s performance would be better for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning.

Hitherto, only two studies have compared subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning using counterfactual reasoning tasks that could not be solved by basic conditional reasoning. Both tasks applied the Bird and Pig task as will be introduced in the Methods section. McCormack et al. [11] found in English-speaking 4- to 9-year-olds that performance was marginally significantly higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning (p =.08). Wu [23] found in Chinese-speaking 4- to 6-year-olds that performance was significantly higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning (p <.05). In short, in both English- and Chinese-speaking children, studies have shown a tendency for children to perform better in subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning tasks.

This pattern contradicts the prediction made by the reality contrast account. One possible explanation is as follows. As explained above, the inhibitory control load is higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning because subtractive but not additive counterfactual premises directly negate what really happened. As a result, reasoners need to more actively inhibit their knowledge of the past in subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Studies have shown that 4–5-year-olds could readily inhibit their reality knowledge in counterfactual reasoning tasks [11]. Hence, even if the requirement for reality inhibition was higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning, children aged 4 years and older are likely to be able to inhibit reality in all cases, not leading to a large difference between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning.

The other three accounts all predict that performance would be better for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning, which accords with the findings in English- and Chinese-speaking children as discussed above [11, 23]. To further differentiate between different accounts, it is worth noting that as Table 1 shows, only the linguistic cue account predicts that the verbal short-term memory load would be higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. The pragmatic cue account and the counterfactual scenario complexity account both predict that the verbal short-term memory load would be higher for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning. Hence, if verbal short-term memory is more strongly associated with subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning, this result will support the linguistic cue account rather than the other two accounts. Conversely, if verbal short-term memory is more strongly associated with additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning, this result will suggest that the linguistic cue account is less likely compared to the pragmatic cue account and the counterfactual scenario complexity account, requiring studies to further differentiate between the latter two accounts. As the first step to differentiate between various accounts, this study compared the associations between verbal short-term memory and subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning.

Rationale of the present study

This study investigated the developmental correlates of counterfactual reasoning of different structures in Chinese preschool children. The study aim was to test whether the association with verbal short-term memory differed between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning. Findings are helpful for understanding the cognitive underpinnings of counterfactual reasoning as an important cognitive ability.

This study analysed the same dataset as Wu ([23], Experiment 2). Writing two articles based on the same dataset was because in Experiment 1, the researcher found a developmental gap between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning. In Experiment 2, the researcher attempted to replicate the finding of Experiment 1 and relevant results were reported in Wu ([23], Experiment 2). At the same time, in Experiment 2, the researcher compared the associations between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning and verbal short-term memory. This separate study was reported in this article.

Methods

Participants

The sample was 147 4- to 6-year-olds (M = 67.53 months, SD = 7.41 months, 70 females) from two kindergartens in Nanjing, China. The sample size was decided as follows. A prior study that was exploratory [25] found a significant structure (subtractive/additive) × memory interaction in 109 Chinese preschool children. As the samples (preschool children in the same district), cognitive tasks, and analysis method were highly similar between the prior study and the present study, it was expected that a sample size of 109 might be sufficient. The final sample size was larger than 109. All children were Mandarin native speakers. None had developmental disorders according to class teacher report. Parent-reported mother education was: 52 (35.37%) high school or below, 43 (29.25%) junior college, 50 (34.01%) university and beyond, and 2 (1.36%) missing. Informed parental consent was gained for all children. The period of study lasted from February to April 2023.

Measures

In a quiet classroom in the kindergarten, children received the following cognitive tasks in two 15- to 35-min sessions.

Counterfactual reasoning

Counterfactual reasoning was tested by the Bird and Pig task [11]. The apparatus used in this task and examples of counterfactual questions were shown in Fig. 1 and Table 2 respectively.

As Fig. 1 shows, in the wooden box, there was a short/long track on the red/yellow side, along which a disc pictured as a red/yellow bird could be rolled down. When either bird fell to the box bottom, a trigger would be activated. Trigger activation would make the green toy pig at the box centre drop to the box bottom. Also, a red/yellow peg could be put in a hole in the middle of the short/long track on the red/yellow side, preventing the red/yellow bird from falling to the box bottom to activate the trigger to make the pig fall down.

The researcher introduced the three animals (two birds and one pig), then performed six familiarisation trials. In three familiarisation trials, the researcher did not put any peg in the box and rolled down the red bird/yellow bird/red and yellow birds, making the pig fall down. In three other familiarisation trials, the researcher put in the red peg and rolled down the red bird/put in the yellow peg and rolled down the yellow bird/put in both pegs and rolled down both birds, not making the pig fall down. After familiarisation trials, the researcher reset the machine to the initial state (no pegs in the box, the pig at the centre), and checked children’s understanding of physical causal structures by asking them six control questions. Each control question tapped one of the six scenarios shown in familiarisation trials. For example, a control question is: What will happen if I put a peg in here (the red side) and roll down the red bird? (Correct Answer: Pig will not fall down). Children were included in data analysis when they answered all control questions correctly.

Next, there were four test trials, which involved subtractive counterfactual premises about the red bird, subtractive counterfactual premises about the yellow bird, additive counterfactual premises about the red peg, and additive counterfactual premises about the yellow peg respectively. The four test trials were counterbalanced for both structure (subtractive/additive) and colour (red/yellow), leading to four task orders: 1. subtractive red - subtractive yellow - additive red - additive yellow; 2. subtractive yellow - subtractive red - additive yellow - additive red; 3. additive red - additive yellow - subtractive red - subtractive yellow; 4. additive yellow - additive red - subtractive yellow - subtractive red. In each test trial, no pegs were put in the box and both birds were rolled down. As a result, the pig fell down. Children were asked a subtractive counterfactual question on would the pig have fallen down if one bird had not been rolled down or an additive counterfactual question on would the pig have fallen down if one peg had been put in the box (see Table 2). The correct answer to all counterfactual questions was “Yes”. Children were unlikely to get full marks due to a “Yes” bias (see [23] for the full justification).

The reason for rolling down two birds was as follows. If rolling down only one bird, children could answer counterfactual questions correctly by basic conditional reasoning based on general regularities (Generally, the pig would not fall down if a bird was not rolled down/if a peg was put in the box to block a bird). There was no need to make counterfactual scenarios maximally similar to the past reality. Rolling down two birds ensured that correct answers reflected children’s ability to follow the nearest possible world constraint, i.e., children needed to recall that two birds were rolled down to deduce that if one bird had not been rolled down or if one bird had been blocked by a peg, the other bird would still have dropped to the box bottom to push the trigger to make the pig fall down.

There were two subtractive/additive counterfactual questions. Children got one mark for answering any counterfactual question correctly with a score of 0–2 for subtractive/additive counterfactual reasoning. It is worth noting that with two questions and two possible answers for each question, children who answered by chance would get a score of 1 (0.5 × 2 = 1). These children did not seem to have better counterfactual reasoning skills than children who consistently applied basic conditional reasoning with a score of 0. To enhance analysis accuracy, children getting a score of 0 and children getting a score of 1 were combined into one group (below-or-at-chance counterfactual reasoning performance) to be compared to children with a score of 2 that indicated above-chance counterfactual reasoning performance.

Verbal short-term memory

Verbal short-term memory was assessed by Forward Digit Span in the Chinese version of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Fourth Edition with good validity and reliability [27]. In this task, children heard a sequence of two to eleven digits and were instructed to repeat this digit sequence. For each length, children heard two different digit sequences, errors on both of which would lead to task termination. The number of digit sequences that a child successfully repeated was recorded (0–20).

Inhibitory control

Inhibitory control was assessed by two tasks in a computerised cognitive task battery (EF Touch, which was administered on an iPad) [22]. In Spatial Conflict Arrows, two buttons appeared at screen left and right respectively. Children saw an arrow above the left/right button, which either pointed to the left/right (in congruent trials) or right/left (in incongruent trials). They were instructed to touch the button to which the arrow pointed. The task score was the percentage of incongruent trials (17 in total) that children responded correctly. In Silly Sounds Stroop, children saw a dog and a cat on the screen. They were instructed to touch the animal that did not make a sound (e.g., touching the dog when hearing a cat meowing). The task score was the percentage of trials (17 in total) that children responded correctly. Scores for Spatial Conflict Arrows and Silly Sounds Stroop (r =.33) were averaged to form the inhibitory control score (in percentages).

Language

Receptive language was included as a covariate, as the counterfactual reasoning task involved verbal input. Language was assessed by the Chinese adaptation of the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test standardised in Chinese 3- to 8-year-olds with good psychometric properties [7]. The researcher pronounced a word twice, and the child selected from four pictures the one that reflected the meaning of the word. This process continued until children made incorrect responses for six in eight successive items. A child’s score was estimated by subtracting the number of errors from the highest item tested (maximum 120).

Statistical analysis

First, preliminary analysis presented descriptive statistics for all cognitive abilities and estimated correlations between demographic and cognitive variables by Pearson’s r and the phi coefficient.

Second, two binary logistic regression models were built with a robust estimator. In each model, the outcome was subtractive or additive counterfactual reasoning. Key predictors were verbal short-term memory, inhibitory control, and language. Potential covariates were age, gender, mother education, task order, and task order × cognitive abilities. For task order, this study focused on whether counterfactual reasoning trials were in the first block (the first and second test trials) or the second block (the third and fourth test trials) of the Bird and Pig task. For model parsimony, potential covariates were only included in regression models when they had significant effects or significantly improved model fit.

Third, generalised estimating equations suitable for repeated measures [10] compared the developmental correlates of subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning. Counterfactual reasoning in general was the outcome. Predictors were those in binary logistic regression models plus structure and structure × cognitive ability interactions.

Discussion

This study investigated the developmental correlates of counterfactual reasoning of different structures in Chinese preschool children. It showed that inhibitory control was uniquely associated with counterfactual reasoning in general. Also, verbal short-term memory was uniquely associated with subtractive but not additive counterfactual reasoning. In addition, for children with above-median language scores, counterfactual reasoning in general was significantly better when counterfactual questions were asked in the second rather than first block of the Bird and Pig tasks. The findings are discussed in the following sections.

Counterfactual reasoning and inhibitory control

Past studies that revealed an association between inhibitory control and counterfactual reasoning applied counterfactual reasoning tasks that did not require children to follow the nearest possible world constraint with questionable validity (Beck et al., 2009; [9]). The present study applied the Bird and Pig task, which required children to follow the nearest possible world constraint. It found that inhibitory control was uniquely associated with counterfactual reasoning in general, including both subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning. This result could not be explained by the fact that counterfactual reasoning required children to inhibit their knowledge of reality, as in the Bird and Pig task, the correct answer (the pig would still have fallen down) did not differ from the real outcome (the pig fell down). A more plausible explanation is that the Bird and Pig task tapped mechanistic (i.e., on machine functioning) causal relations, which were relatively transparent (how the machine operated remained perceptually available throughout the Bird and Pig task) and deterministic (causal relations tended not to change with task circumstances) [13]. Hence, general causal rules such as “IF not rolling down a bird/IF putting in a peg to block a bird, THEN the pig will not fall down” were readily learnt and straightforwardly processed. Inhibitory control helped children suppress such highly accessible rules for drawing accurate counterfactual inferences, i.e., when two birds were rolled down, even if a bird had not been rolled down/even if a peg had been put in the box, the pig would still have fallen down.

Counterfactual reasoning and verbal short-term memory

Prior studies revealed that children’s performance was better for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. This study intended to investigate whether this difference could be explained by the linguistic cue account, i.e., the better performance of subtractive counterfactual reasoning was due to more linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises. The linguistic cue account predicts that the verbal short-term memory load would be higher for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Accordingly, verbal short-term memory would be more strongly associated with subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. To test this prediction, this study adopted an experimental design, where the researcher manipulated the structure of the counterfactual scenario (subtractive/additive) to see whether this factor affected the association between verbal short-term memory and counterfactual reasoning. Consistent with the above prediction, this study found that verbal short-term memory was more strongly associated with subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning.

This finding cannot be explained by the pragmatic cue account. In the Bird and Pig task, there were more pragmatic cues that assisted children to recall past events in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises. Subtractive counterfactual premises focused on the event of bird-rolling that had just been observed, while additive counterfactual premises focused on the event of putting in a peg that were hypothetical. Hence, children were more likely to link subtractive counterfactual premises to the past event of rolling down both birds. Also, before rolling down birds, to attract children’s attention, the researcher verbally announced what was to expect (e.g., “I am going to roll both birds down now”). These instructions overlapped in content with subtractive counterfactual premises on not rolling down a bird but not additive counterfactual premises on putting in a peg. Hence, children were more likely to recollect researcher instructions following subtractive than additive counterfactual questions. Overall, there were more pragmatic cues that facilitated memory retrieval in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises, leading to a lower verbal short-term memory load for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Therefore, verbal short-term memory would be more strongly associated with additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning, which contradicts the present findings.

Also, the present findings cannot be explained by the counterfactual scenario complexity account. In the Bird and Pig task used in this study, complexity was higher for additive than subtractive counterfactual scenarios. Additive counterfactual reasoning added an event of putting in a peg to the observed event of rolling down two birds. Subtractive counterfactual reasoning removed an event of rolling down a bird from the observed event of rolling down two birds. Hence, there were more events to store in mind in additive (rolling down two birds, one bird was blocked by a peg) than subtractive (rolling down only one bird) counterfactual scenarios. As a result, verbal short-term memory would be more strongly associated with additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning, which contradicts the present findings.

In addition, the present findings cannot be readily explained by the reality contrast account. In the Bird and Pig task, the researcher explicitly stated that the red and yellow birds would be rolled down and children clearly saw both birds rolling down. Therefore, their memory of the past event of rolling down the red/yellow bird was relatively strong. Such memory needed to be actively suppressed when reasoning from subtractive counterfactual premises on not rolling down the red/yellow bird but not when reasoning from additive counterfactual premises that did not directly negate the past event. Hence, performance would be better for additive than subtractive counterfactual reasoning, and inhibitory control would be more strongly associated with subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Neither prediction was supported by the present findings.

More likely, the present findings might be explained by the linguistic cue account. There were more linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises. In the Bird and Pig task, additive counterfactual premises contained “ruguo” (if) for suggesting hypotheticality and “gangcai” (just now) for suggesting factuality. Subtractive counterfactual premises additionally contained the negator “meiyou” (not exist/have) (see Table 1). As this negator is often applied to completed events, it not only conveyed negation but also implied factuality. Among the above three linguistic cues, “ruguo” (if) could introduce a wide range of hypothetical scenarios and thus was not powerful for enhancing counterfactuality [21]. In contrast, the combination of “meiyou” (not exist/have) and “gangcai” (just now) were powerful for deriving counterfactuality. This combination of linguistic cues was present in subtractive counterfactual premises but was not present in additive counterfactual premises.

More linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises had two likely consequences. First, children could more readily interpret and reason from subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Second, as subtractive counterfactual premises contained three linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality (“ruguo” or “if”, “gangcai” or “just now”, and “meiyou” or “not exist/have”), whereas additive counterfactual premises contained only two cues (“ruguo” or “if” and “gangcai” or “just now”), verbal short-term memory for storing linguistic cues might be more important for interpreting and reasoning from subtractive than additive counterfactual premises. Accordingly, this study found that performance was better for subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. Also, verbal short-term memory was more strongly associated with subtractive than additive counterfactual reasoning. The only account in Table 1 that is consistent with both findings is the linguistic cue account. Hence, it is likely that the difference between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning performance might be explained by more linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality in subtractive than additive counterfactual premises.

From an integrated view, the association with subtractive counterfactual reasoning was stronger for verbal short-term memory than for inhibitory control. This result might be because to solve counterfactual reasoning tasks, children needed to firstly interpret counterfactual premises and next deduce counterfactual outcomes. In the Bird and Pig task, for subtractive counterfactual reasoning, the storage of multiple linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality (see above) was important for counterfactual premise interpretation, which tapped verbal short-term memory. Also, inhibitory control, more specifically the inhibition of error-prone general rules (e.g., IF not rolling down a bird, THEN the pig will not fall down), was important for counterfactual outcome deduction. If counterfactual premise interpretation was not achieved, children would not initiate counterfactual outcome deduction. Therefore, relative to inhibitory control that facilitated counterfactual outcome deduction, verbal short-term memory, which facilitated counterfactual premise interpretation, might be more important for solving subtractive counterfactual reasoning tasks.

Counterfactual reasoning and language

This study found that for children with above-median receptive language scores, counterfactual reasoning was significantly better when questions were asked in the second rather than first block in the Bird and Pig task. As this finding was relatively unexpected, no strong conclusions can be drawn and two speculative accounts are briefly discussed.

First, the language task is widely used to assess general cognitive development [7]. Children with above-median language scores might have relatively high cognitive development. For these children, the task order × language interaction might be explained as follows. Counterfactual reasoning as a higher-order cognitive skill heavily taxed children’s limited cognitive resources. In the Bird and Pig task, answering counterfactual questions in the first block provided children opportunities to practise solving counterfactual reasoning tasks. Also, engaging in counterfactual reasoning in the first block could elicit a mindset for thinking about alternative possibilities, which could be carried over to subsequent counterfactual reasoning tasks [5]. As a result, in the second block, children became more familiar with the Bird and Pig task and more adept at solving this task, and thus gained higher counterfactual reasoning accuracy. This effect did not occur for children with below-median language scores that reflect lower cognitive development, who consistently found counterfactual reasoning difficult regardless of task orders.

Second, temporal indicators like “gangcai” (just now) are “one of the most basic lexical methods to realize counterfactuality in Chinese” ([21], p. 101). Although the language test did not include temporal indicators, it assessed receptive vocabulary in general. A larger receptive vocabulary made it more likely for children to comprehend temporal indicators. Hence, children with above-median language scores were likely to comprehend “gangcai” (just now). For these children, the task order × language interaction might be explained as follows. For all three control questions involving a peg (what would happen if putting in the red peg/yellow peg/both pegs and rolling down the red bird/yellow bird/both birds), the answer was that the pig would not fall down (see Methods). Therefore, following control questions, the general regularity “IF putting in any peg(s), THEN the pig will not fall down” was particularly salient. Hence, when counterfactual questions were asked in the first block of the Bird and Pig task, accuracy was relatively low. In contrast, when counterfactual questions were asked in the second block of the Bird and Pig task, answering counterfactual questions in the first block increased the awareness that general regularities were not always applicable, which helped children suppress the salient tendency to reason by general regularities, raising counterfactual reasoning accuracy. Thus, counterfactual reasoning accuracy was higher when counterfactual questions were asked in the second rather than first block of the Bird and Pig task. This interaction effect was not found in children with below-median language scores, who were likely not to comprehend “gangcai” (just now). As additive counterfactual premises only contained “ruguo” (if) and “gangcai” (just now) as key linguistic cues for enhancing counterfactuality, and as counterfactuality could hardly be derived from “ruguo” (if) alone, these children would consistently find it difficult to interpret and reason from counterfactual premises.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is that while it addressed the linguistic cue account, it did not directly manipulate linguistic features. This was due to the difficulty with controlling for confounding variables. For example, two premises for testing the effect of negators are: “If I had not rolled down the red bird” and “If I had rolled down the red bird”. To ensure that scores reflect counterfactual reasoning rather than basic conditional reasoning, task trials need to be devised so that counterfactual questions cannot be solved by general regularities (IF rolling down/not rolling down birds, THEN the pig will fall down/will not fall down). The subtractive condition may involve putting in no pegs and rolling down two birds, which is the same as the subtractive condition in this study. The only possible additive condition involves putting in two pegs and rolling down the yellow bird, in which case even if the red bird had been rolled down, the pig would not have fallen down. Such subtractive and additive conditions differ vastly by both real and counterfactual scenarios, making it hard to differentiate between the effect of negators and non-linguistic cognitive factors. This study controlled for both real (two birds activated two triggers) and counterfactual (one bird activated one trigger) scenarios. The only difference between conditions was more events in additive than subtractive counterfactual scenarios. As noted earlier, this difference did not weaken the linguistic cue account. Rather, it helped differentiate the linguistic cue account from the counterfactual scenario complexity account. Future studies may devise means to directly manipulate linguistic features while controlling for non-linguistic cognitive factors.

Another limitation of this study is limited ecological validity, as counterfactual reasoning was tested in a relatively simple apparatus-based scenario that was less complex than real-life scenarios. Future studies may investigate the developmental correlates of counterfactual reasoning in naturalistic settings to enhance the utility of research findings for scaffolding real-life cognitive growth.

Conclusion

This study investigated the developmental correlates of counterfactual reasoning of different structures in Chinese preschool children. It found that inhibitory control was associated with both subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning. Verbal short-term memory was associated with subtractive but not additive counterfactual reasoning. In addition, for children with above-median language scores, counterfactual reasoning performance was better when counterfactual questions were asked in the second rather than first block. Thus, counterfactual reasoning of different structures had both similar and different developmental correlates. These findings support the account that the presence of different linguistic cues for enhancing counter factuality in subtractive and additive counterfactual premises might shape the difference between subtractive and additive counterfactual reasoning performance. Future studies may directly manipulate linguistic cues to further understand how language and cognition interact with each other to shape children’s mental landscapes.
