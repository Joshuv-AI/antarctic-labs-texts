Epidemiological characteristics and LSTM-based simulation of mumps incidence in Ningbo, China from 2005 to 2024
Authors: Zi Ye, Xiaoxia Zhu, Heng Fan, Weitao Yao, Yanghui Wu, Tianfeng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to analyze the epidemiological characteristics of mumps in Ningbo, China, from 2005 to 2024, and to develop a predictive model using a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network to forecast incidence trends.MethodsSurveillance data of mumps cases were obtained from the China National Infectious Disease Network Direct Reporting System (CNIDNDRS). Descriptive statistics, Cochran-Armitage trend tests, and chi-square tests were employed to analyze epidemiological distributions. An LSTM model was constructed using monthly incidence data from January 2005 to December 2021 for training and validated with data from January 2022 to December 2024. Model generalizability was assessed using a synthetic dataset generated via time-series decomposition with stochastic noise. A SARIMA model was developed as a statistical baseline for comparison.ResultsA total of 40,444 mumps cases were reported in Ningbo during the study period, with an average annual incidence of 32.7 per 100,000 population. A significant decreasing trend was observed from 2005 to 2024 (Z = -98.6, P < 0.001). The disease exhibited a bimodal seasonal pattern (April–July and November–January) before 2020, which weakened thereafter. Incidence was significantly higher in males than females (1.67:1 ratio, P < 0.001), and the majority of cases occurred in children aged 3–14 years (83.0%) and students (49.8%). Spatial analysis revealed higher incidence in suburban counties compared to urban districts (P < 0.05). The LSTM model demonstrated strong predictive performance, with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 32.04% on test set, and maintained robustness on synthetic data. The LSTM outperformed the SARIMA model (test set MAPE = 40.22%).ConclusionsMumps incidence in Ningbo has declined significantly, entering a sustained low-prevalence phase largely due to high vaccination coverage. The LSTM model effectively predicted incidence trends, particularly for epidemic peaks, and outperformed traditional time-series models.

Introduction

Mumps is an acute respiratory infectious disease caused by the mumps virus, characterized clinically by non-suppurative swelling and tenderness of the parotid glands [1]. It remains a globally prevalent condition with high transmissibility [2]. Despite its generally self-limiting clinical presentation, which has historically led to its under-recognition compared to more severe infectious diseases such as measles and varicella, mumps continues to exhibit considerable morbidity in numerous regions [3]. Notably, recent epidemiological studies have highlighted its significant role in causing severe complications, including aseptic meningitis, viral encephalitis and sensorineural hearing loss in children, affirming its status as an ongoing public health challenge worldwide [4–6].

Since being categorized as a Class C infectious disease in China in 1990, mumps case data have been collected nationally through the China National Infectious Disease Network Direct Reporting System (CNIDNDRS) established by the China CDC in 2004 [7–9]. As a coastal city in Zhejiang Province, Ningbo boasts a robust economy and high population mobility, which may facilitate the transmission and spread of mumps [10]. Additionally, there is currently no predictive model for mumps incidence in Ningbo. Therefore, this study selected Ningbo as the data source to systematically assess its epidemiological trends and scientifically evaluate the effectiveness of interventions, providing a reference for developing targeted prevention and control strategies in similar urban settings.

To forecast mumps incidence, various models have been applied, including traditional time-series methods like the seasonal autoregressive comprehensive moving average (SARIMA) model, the Joinpoint regression model and the generalized logistic differential equation (GLDE) model [11–13]. These models often assume linearity and may struggle with complex, long-term dependencies. In contrast, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks, a specialized Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), are designed to overcome these limitations through unique gating mechanisms that enable them to learn long-range dependencies and non-linear patterns from data without predefined assumptions. Given the success of LSTMs in predicting hand-foot-mouth disease and other seasonal infectious diseases, we posit that an LSTM model will provide a more accurate and robust forecast for mumps incidence in Ningbo [14, 15].

The primary objective of this study is twofold. First, we conduct a comprehensive epidemiological analysis of mumps cases in Ningbo from 2005 to 2024, with the aim of identifying transmission patterns, high-risk populations, and evolving trends over time. Second, building upon these epidemiological insights, we develop and evaluate an LSTM-based time-series forecasting model. While LSTM models can effectively capture historical temporal dependencies, their predictive performance may be limited by the exclusion of explicit demographic and spatial covariates. By integrating descriptive epidemiology with deep learning prediction, this research seeks not only to provide short-term forecasts but also to discuss the necessity of incorporating multi-source data for future modeling improvements.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Reported mumps case data for Ningbo from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2024, were obtained from CNIDNDRS, managed by the Ningbo Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The annual total resident population of Ningbo from 2004 to 2025, disaggregated by sex and region, was sourced from the Ningbo Municipal Bureau of Statistics. Mumps cases in this study refer to clinically diagnosed and confirmed cases that meet the Diagnostic Criteria for Mumps (WS 270–2007) issued by the Ministry of Health in 2007.

Data cleaning and preprocessing

A rigorous data cleaning and validation process was performed on the raw case data. We systematically addressed missing values, using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) for a small proportion (< 1%) of missing “Age” data. Duplicate reports were identified and removed by defining a unique case identifier based on “Patient Name, ID Number, and Date of Onset”. Logical errors, such as the “Date of Onset” later than the “Date of Diagnosis” were corrected, and outliers were verified against original reports.

For time series analysis, we aggregated the cleaned data by month to calculate the monthly incidence as our primary prediction target. We implemented a two-step preprocessing pipeline consisting of logarithmic transformation and min-max normalization, ultimately constructing a monthly time-series dataset. All data processing was conducted using Microsoft Excel 2019 and R 4.2.1.

Statistical analysis

Epidemiological analysis

To analyze the distribution characteristics of mumps incidence across different time periods, regions, and populations in Ningbo from 2005 to 2024, descriptive epidemiological methods were employed. Specifically, Microsoft Excel 2019 was used for data management and to create statistical charts describing the incidence characteristics across different years, regions, and age groups. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.1. For temporal trends, the Cochran-Armitage trend test was applied to assess the significance of changes in incidence over the years. The χ² test was used to compare incidence between different genders and regions. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

LSTM Model

The model uses monthly mumps incidence data from January 2005 to December 2021 as the training set, and the data from January 2022 to December 2024 was separated prior to training to serve as an independent test set. The modeling process incorporated logarithmic transformation, time-series feature engineering, and a deep neural network architecture. Hyperparameters such as learning rate, number of hidden units, and number of training epochs were optimized to improve model performance. The training performance of the model was evaluated using metrics such as mean absolute deviation (MAD), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE).

The network architecture consisted of a first LSTM layer with 64 hidden units, followed by a Dropout layer that randomly omitted 30% of neurons to prevent overfitting. During each training iteration, the Dropout layer randomly deactivates a subset of neurons, compelling the network to learn more robust and distributed feature representations rather than over-relying on specific pathways of a few neurons. A second LSTM layer contained 32 hidden units, again followed by a 30% Dropout layer. A fully connected layer with 16 neurons was then applied, succeeded by an additional Dropout layer discarding 20% of units. The output layer was a single-neuron dense layer. Furthermore, the model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 for stable and adaptive gradient updates. A relatively small batch size of 16 was employed to improve generalization by introducing noise into the gradient estimation. Additionally, training proceeded for up to 100 epochs with early stopping applied to prevent overfitting.

To evaluate the generalizability of the model, a synthetic dataset was constructed via time-series decomposition. The original incidence data was decomposed into trend, seasonal, and residual components using an additive model. A synthetic time series was reconstructed by preserving the original trend and seasonal patterns while introducing stochastic Gaussian noise to the residual component to simulate data variability. Negative values in the synthetic data were truncated to zero. The pre-trained LSTM model was applied to this synthetic dataset to assess its robustness against unseen data perturbations.

SARIMA Model

To evaluate the added value of the deep learning approach, we employed a SARIMA model as a classical statistical baseline. The modeling process strictly followed the Box-Jenkins methodology.

We performed the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) unit root test on the training data (January 2005 to December 2021). The results indicated that the original sequence was non-stationary, necessitating differencing to achieve stationarity. Optimal differencing orders were determined to eliminate trends and seasonal components. Using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) as selection standards, the auto.arima function was utilized to identify the optimal SARIMA(p,

d,q)(P, D,Q)[12] structure. Model parameters were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation. Residual diagnostics, including Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) plots, were conducted to ensure the residuals resembled white noise. Furthermore, the Ljung-Box test was applied to statistically verify the independence of the residuals.

The fitted model was used to perform out-of-sample forecasting for the testing period (January 2022 to December 2024). The forecasting performance was evaluated using the same metrics as the LSTM model, including MAD, RMSE and MAPE.

Results

Temporal distribution

From 2005 to 2024, a total of 40,444 mumps cases were reported in Ningbo, including one fatal case, with no recorded outbreaks or clustered epidemics. The annual incidence ranged from 10.7 to 73.2 per 100,000 population, with an average annual incidence of 32.7 per 100,000. From 2005 to 2013, a total of 27,036 cases were reported, with an average annual incidence of 52.7 per 100,000. The highest incidence occurred in 2005, with 4,075 cases reported. From 2014 to 2024, 13,408 cases were reported, with an average annual incidence of 20.1 per 100,000, indicating a significant decrease compared to the earlier period. From 2020 to 2024, mumps entered a sustained low-prevalence phase. A significant decreasing trend in incidence was observed from 2005 to 2024 (Z = -98.6, P < 0.001), reflecting a notable reduction in epidemic intensity (Fig. 1).

Population distribution

Among the reported mumps cases in Ningbo from 2005 to 2024, a total of 25,160 cases occurred in males and 15,284 in females. The average annual incidence was 42.9 per 100,000 population for males and 25.7 per 100,000 for females, resulting in a male-to-female incidence ratio of 1.67:1. This difference in incidence between genders was statistically significant (χ² = 2537.9, P < 0.001). The proportion of male cases consistently surpassed that of females throughout the surveillance period, with the gender-specific distribution remaining relatively stable over time (Fig. 3A). Analysis by occupation revealed that students constituted the largest proportion of cases, comprising 20,121 cases (49.8%), followed by children in childcare facilities and non-institutionalized children. Collectively, these three groups accounted for 90.3% of all reported cases (Fig. 3B). The majority of cases were observed in young children. Specifically, 33,572 cases (83.0%) were reported among children aged 3–14 years (Fig. 3C).

Spatial distribution

The epidemic trends of mumps across the counties and districts of Ningbo were generally consistent from 2005 to 2024, showing an overall fluctuating decline, though some areas experienced periodic high incidence. From 2005 to 2013, case numbers fluctuated considerably in various regions, with notably high values observed in Xiangshan County (incidence of 211.8 per 100,000 population in 2005), Cixi City (110.1 per 100,000 in 2005 and 104.9 per 100,000 in 2011), Beilun District (107.7 per 100,000 in 2006, 145.2 per 100,000 in 2009, and 122.0 per 100,000 in 2013), and Zhenhai District (145.3 per 100,000 in 2008). From 2014 to 2024, the annual incidence in almost all regions fell below 50 per 100,000, with significantly reduced fluctuation ranges. The average annual incidence in the Ningbo municipal districts was 33.8 per 100,000, while that in the surrounding counties was 34.7/100,000, which was significantly higher than that in the urban districts (χ² = 6.3, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

LSTM Model

The LSTM model was trained on data from January 2005 to December 2021. On the training set, the model’s fitted values aligned with the actual values, yielding a MAD of 0.91, RMSE of 1.39, and MAPE of 28.79%. The model was subsequently used to simulate the reported incidence of mumps from January 2022 to December 2024, resulting in a validation set MAD of 0.26, RMSE of 0.30, and MAPE of 32.04%, which is mainly driven by prediction errors during low-incidence months. Notably, for high-incidence months (> 1.0 per 100,000 population), the MAPE dropped to 15.23%. This indicates the model is particularly effective at forecasting peaks in mumps transmission, which are the most critical events for public health intervention, thus demonstrating its practical utility (Table 1; Fig. 5).

To validate the model’s generalizability, we evaluated its performance on the generated synthetic dataset. The model achieved an MAD of 0.70 and an RMSE of 0.88 on the synthetic data. As expected, these metrics are moderately higher than those observed on the original test set, which is attributable to the intrinsic unpredictability of the stochastic noise introduced during synthesis. Nevertheless, the model maintained a low absolute error, demonstrating that it effectively captures the underlying epidemiological patterns and maintains robust predictive performance even when facing unseen data variations.

Comparison with SARIMA Model

The performance of the SARIMA model was compared with the LSTM model using the same training and testing sets. As shown in Table 2, although the SARIMA model exhibited lower fitting errors on the training set, its performance degraded significantly on the testing set, with the MAPE increasing to 40.22%. This suggests a limited generalization capability when dealing with the complex non-linear patterns of the epidemic. In contrast, the LSTM model demonstrated more robust performance on the testing set, achieving a MAPE of 32.04%, which is significantly lower than that of the SARIMA model. This comparison validates the superiority of the LSTM model in capturing epidemic peaks and generalizing to unseen data.

Discussion

Mumps is an acute respiratory infectious disease characterized by parotid gland swelling and pain. It can lead to complications such as meningitis, orchitis, and oophoritis [17]. Highly contagious, it is second only to measles and varicella in terms of transmissibility and poses a substantial public health burden [18]. The results of this study show that a total of 40,444 mumps cases were reported in Ningbo from 2005 to 2024, with an average annual incidence of 32.7 per 100,000 population, which is slightly higher than the national average annual incidence of 21.4 per 100,000 from 2004 to 2018 [19]. The incidence decreased by 85.4%, from 73.2/100,000 in 2005 to 10.7/100,000 in 2024. This trend is consistent with previous studies in Zhejiang Province, which reported a decline from 56.1/100,000 in 2008 to 11.4/100,000 in 2019 [7].

However, these findings are not entirely consistent with reports from other regions. For example, studies in Jiangsu Province and Chongqing City reported a rebound in mumps incidence after an initial decline, while Yunnan Province experienced an initial increase followed by a decrease [11, 20, 21]. Significant downward trends were documented in Hubei Province and Quzhou, Zhejiang Province [22, 23]. Nationally, the incidence of mumps in China showed no clear decline from 2004 to 2021, with no significant change around 2008 and two epidemic peaks occurring during 2011–2012 and 2017–2019 [17–19].

The disparities may be attributed to the relatively high vaccination coverage achieved in Ningbo during the implementation of the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI). In 2007, China’s EPI included one dose of measles, mumps, and rubella combined attenuated live vaccine (MMR) for children aged 18–24 months; Ningbo initiated a one-dose MMR strategy for this age group in July 2007 and updated it to a two-dose schedule in November 2018, with doses administered at 8 months and 18 months, respectively. This strategy has helped establish immune protection among young children and effectively controlled mumps transmission [24, 25]. Additionally, Zhejiang Province introduced domestic mumps vaccines in 1995, imported MMR in 1997, and domestic MMR in 2000, available on a voluntary, self-paid basis, further supplementing herd immunity efforts [7].

Cases were reported in every month from 2005 to 2024 in Ningbo. From 2005 to 2019, mumps exhibited a typical bimodal seasonal distribution, with a primary peak from April to July and a secondary peak from November to January of the following year. From 2020 to 2024, the seasonality became less pronounced. This pattern aligns with epidemiological trends observed in Guangxi Province, Shandong Province, and Jiangsu Province, which may be associated with climatic factors [26–28]. The warmer temperatures in late spring and early summer facilitate the reproduction and transmission of respiratory viruses, while reduced outdoor activities and poor indoor ventilation during winter increase the risk of mumps transmission [29–32].

The sustained low incidence and attenuated seasonality of mumps observed from 2020 to 2024 coincide with the COVID-19 pandemic period, during which a series of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs)—such as mask-wearing, social distancing, and school closures—were widely implemented in Ningbo. These measures likely reduced person-to-person contact and airborne transmission, thereby suppressing the spread of mumps and other respiratory viruses [33]. Concurrently, shifts in healthcare-seeking behavior, reallocation of medical resources toward COVID-19 response, and potential underreporting due to overwhelmed surveillance systems may have affected the completeness and accuracy of mumps case reporting.

From 2005 to 2024 in Ningbo, the reported incidence of mumps was higher in males than in females, with a male-to-female incidence ratio of 1.67:1. This difference was statistically significant and is consistent with previous studies [22, 34, 35]. The disparity may be attributed to broader social activity ranges and relatively weaker hygiene habits and self-protection awareness among males. In terms of occupational distribution, students accounted for the largest proportion of cases, followed by children in childcare facilities and non-institutionalized children, which is consistent with previous studies and underscores that the focus of mumps prevention and control should remain on schools and childcare institutions [36–38]. The majority of cases (83.0%) were identified in children aged 3–14 years, a finding which is in line with the work of Jiang RJ et al. [19] This underscores the need to specifically monitor herd immunity levels in key populations and strengthen immune barriers. Yang T et al. further emphasized that improving vaccine coverage among the 5–9 age group nationwide is critical to preventing clustered outbreaks when children enter kindergartens and primary schools [39].

From 2005 to 2024, the epidemic trends of mumps across counties and districts in Ningbo were generally consistent, showing an overall fluctuating decline, though some areas experienced periodic spikes in incidence. The average annual incidence in the urban districts of Ningbo was 33.8 per 100,000, while that in the surrounding counties was 34.7 per 100,000 with a statistically significant difference. This urban-rural disparity aligns with findings from previous studies, which may be explained by better medical resources, more efficient infectious disease surveillance and management, and easier implementation of preventive measures in urban areas [40]. Increased attention should be given to vaccination coverage and incidence monitoring in outlying counties to effectively reduce mumps incidence, enable early detection and management of outbreaks, and mitigate the risk of public health emergencies.

This study aims to investigate whether LSTM models can more effectively capture the non-linear dynamic changes in mumps incidence under the influence of vaccination policies. The model demonstrated good fitting performance on the training data from 2005 to 2021, effectively capturing major fluctuations in incidence, which indicates that it successfully learned the underlying trends and periodicity of the historical data including the overall declining trend. For the test data validations from 2022 to 2024, it is particularly effective at forecasting peaks in mumps transmission. For months with incidence exceeding 1.0 per 100,000 population, the MAPE drops to 15.23%, which allows health authorities to anticipate the magnitude of an outbreak with reasonable confidence, enabling proactive allocation of vaccines, medical staff, and diagnostic kits. The fact that the model kept prediction errors low despite the added noise suggests that it learned the underlying seasonal and trend patterns rather than simply memorizing specific noise features in the training data.

This finding aligns with a growing body of literature that has increasingly adopted LSTM for epidemiological forecasting over the past decade. For example, LSTM has shown superior performance in modeling COVID-19, occupational pneumoconiosis, hand-foot-mouth disease and seasonal infectious disease [14, 15, 41, 42]. The comparative analysis with the SARIMA model provides critical insights into the suitability of deep learning for this specific context. The consensus across studies indicates that in comparison to conventional time series models like the autoregressive comprehensive moving average (ARIMA) model, LSTM-based or hybrid LSTM models are generally considered to possess a stronger ability to capture non-linear trends and long-term dependencies in data. This is largely due to their distinctive gating mechanisms. The SARIMA model’s tendency to smooth out peaks explains its inability to accurately forecast the epidemic’s trajectory during the testing period (2022–2024). Therefore, the inclusion of the SARIMA baseline objectively validates our hypothesis that advanced deep learning architectures offer distinct advantages over classical statistical methods for epidemic forecasting in this region.

However, certain deviations were observed in the test data validations, with a MAPE of 32.04%. Specifically, predicted values were generally higher than actual values during the low-incidence period from 2022 to 2024. This phenomenon can be interpreted from two perspectives. Firstly, it may be related to the model’s fitting characteristics. While the model successfully learned the primary epidemic trends from the training data, it might have been insufficiently fitted to the subtle random fluctuations during the low-incidence period. Secondly, this also reflects an inherent limitation of the LSTM architecture in such scenarios. During periods of low incidence, the variability of the time series diminishes while randomness increases, leading to a lower signal-to-noise ratio.

Although the LSTM model demonstrated satisfactory performance in capturing temporal trends, it is crucial to acknowledge its structural limitations. Our epidemiological analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in mumps incidence across different age groups and regions, suggesting that demographic and spatial factors are key drivers of transmission dynamics. However, the current model architecture relies solely on historical incidence sequences and does not explicitly integrate these covariates. This univariate approach may overlook the impact of complex socio-demographic factors, such as population density, vaccination rates, or mobility patterns, potentially limiting its explanatory power during non-stationary periods (e.g., unexpected outbreaks in schools). Therefore, future studies should aim to construct a Multivariate LSTM framework that incorporates these identified demographic and spatial variables to enhance the model’s predictive accuracy and epidemiological interpretability. For instance, Chen X et al. proposed a scalable and robust forecasting framework for dengue fever in Brazil, which effectively integrates temporal, climatic, and spatial information into an LSTM-based model [43]. This approach captures key transmission drivers and demonstrates the potential of LSTM neural networks for prediction. In another study, Yang E et al. developed a multivariate multi-step LSTM forecasting model to predict the incidence of tuberculosis by incorporating data such as maximum temperature, average relative humidity, and local fiscal budgets, thereby improving the accuracy of incidence forecasting [44].

This study has several limitations. First, the inherent nature of passive surveillance data makes it susceptible to underreporting and diagnostic uncertainties, potentially resulting in an underestimated true incidence. Furthermore, the reporting rate of mumps was likely affected by altered healthcare-seeking behaviors and hospital protocols during the COVID-19 pandemic. Second, regarding the LSTM model, we must clarify that the current model architecture was designed as a univariate time-series model based solely on historical incidence counts. Demographic variables (e.g., age, gender) and spatial data, although analyzed in the descriptive epidemiology section, were not explicitly included as input covariates in the training process. This exclusion limits the model’s ability to explicitly attribute prediction errors to specific population dynamics or spatial heterogeneity. Third, the model assumes that future epidemic patterns will resemble historical trends, potentially reducing its adaptability to sudden public health interventions or policy changes. Finally, as a “black-box” deep learning model, LSTM offers limited epidemiological interpretability regarding the causal drivers behind its predictions compared to mechanistic models.

Conclusions

This study reveals a significant decline in mumps incidence in Ningbo from 2005 to 2024, indicating a sustained low-prevalence phase. Cases showed bimodal seasonality, higher rates in males and children, and elevated incidence in suburban areas. The developed LSTM model effectively predicted incidence trends. Sustaining high two-dose MMR vaccination coverage and strengthening surveillance in schools and high-risk settings are recommended.
