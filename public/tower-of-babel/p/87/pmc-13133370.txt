A Chain mediation model linking experiential avoidance, cognitive flexibility, negative emotions, and sleep quality in university students
Authors: Yucong Jiang, Tao Peng, Ning Wang, Jie Peng, Xinxin Tan, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study aimed to investigate the relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality among university students, with particular attention to the chain mediating effects of cognitive flexibility and negative emotions. Using convenience sampling, 840 students were recruited from southwestern and southern China, and the final analytical sample included 833 participants with a mean age of 18.83 ± 0.99 years, including 242 males and 591 females. Demographic variables were statistically controlled in the analyses. Results revealed a significant negative cross-sectional association between experiential avoidance and sleep quality. Additionally, a significant chain mediation pathway was found, indicating that experiential avoidance influenced sleep quality through the sequential mediators of cognitive flexibility and negative affect. These findings suggest that implementing long-term psychological monitoring for students with high levels of experiential avoidance and effectively intervening in their cognition and negative affect, thereby reducing the levels of all three, may contribute to improving their sleep quality and promoting their overall healthy development.Keywords: Experiential Avoidance, Sleep Quality, Cognitive Flexibility, Negative Emotions, College StudentsSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Sleep is a physiological state in which motor and sensory functions are temporarily reduced for bodily restoration, from which an individual can be readily aroused by external stimuli1. Sleep quality refers to an individual’s overall assessment of their sleep experience2. In today’s rapidly developing society, the increasing pressure from work, study, and life, combined with the prevalence of portable devices such as smartphones and computers, results in individuals still receiving a large amount of information before sleep, which interferes with the sleep process3–7. Chronic low sleep quality can lead to sleep disorders such as difficulty falling asleep and short sleep duration8,9. In China, 25.7% of university students are affected by sleep disorders10. Globally, the prevalence of insomnia among university students reaches 18.5%, substantially exceeding the 7.4% observed in the general population11. Prior research has demonstrated that diminished sleep quality impairs endocrine and metabolic function, elevates the risk of obesity and hypertension, and precipitates psychological disorders including suicidal ideation12–14. It seriously affects an individual’s cognitive abilities, daytime functioning, emotions and other aspects of mental health15. Given the high prevalence of poor sleep quality and its detrimental effects on physical and mental health, investigating its determinants and interventions holds significant theoretical and practical implications for improving college students’ quality of life.

Experiential avoidance is a coping mechanism in which individuals try to either eliminate or modify the nature and occurrence of distressing experiences from past events, often leading to negative emotional states and the use of avoidance behaviors16. It is considered an undesirable psychological strategy17. Research indicates that elevated levels of experiential avoidance deplete cognitive resources, affect present emotional experiences, and trap individuals in a cycle of negative experiences18, thus hindering progress toward valuable goals19,20. Additionally, it causes excessive psychological stimulation, triggering psychological stress21, and individuals may continue this process before sleep22, increasing neural excitability and making it difficult to fall asleep23. According to Relational Frame Theory (RFT)24, the use of avoidance strategies before sleep gradually strengthens the association between negative experiences and sleep, increasing difficulty in falling asleep and causing distress toward sleep, further impacting sleep quality. Research indicates that heightened experiential avoidance, which is linked to fragmented sleep25,26, can be effectively mitigated through intervention, leading to sustained improvements in sleep quality27. Building on this causal pathway, the current investigation hypothesizes that experiential avoidance is associated with poorer sleep quality.

A theoretical framework positing cognitive flexibility as a core mediator offers a more nuanced understanding of how experiential avoidance impacts sleep quality. Cognitive flexibility, as one of the three core components of executive function, refers to the capacity to rapidly modify current thought processes and behavioral patterns in response to changes in external and internal environmental demands. This construct emphasizes individuals’ ability to promptly adjust cognitive strategies, reconfigure task sets, and execute goal-directed behaviors across varying contextual conditions28,29. People who exhibit high levels of experiential avoidance often resort to avoidance strategies, leading to the development of fixed cognitive patterns in response to negative events, which in turn hinders cognitive flexibility. The extant literature consistently reports a significant positive correlation between experiential avoidance and cognitive rigidity30–32. Prior study has identified that elevated levels of experiential avoidance negatively impact an individual’s cognitive flexibility33. Both can jointly influence an individual’s psychological state, leading to psychological distress34–37, which in turn affects sleep habits, the process of falling asleep, sleep efficiency, and sleep continuity38–40, thereby exerting a significant adverse effect on overall sleep quality41–45. An increase in experiential avoidance can also heighten the perception of burdensomeness, leading to persistent thoughts about negative experiences and exacerbating psychological inflexibility, which further reduces cognitive flexibility46. The resultant increase in sleep-related psychological stress is a known pathway to insomnia and impaired sleep quality47. Taken together, this study hypothesizes that experiential avoidance impacts sleep quality via the mediating mechanism of cognitive flexibility.

The relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality may also be associated with negative emotions. Negative emotions refer to excessive reactions that individuals have when confronted with events or stimuli, with anxiety and depression representing primary manifestations. These affective states induce aversive subjective experiences characterized by feelings of sadness, fear, and apprehension, and demonstrate a propensity for recurrence over time48–53. Anxiety affects 3% to 6% of the global population54,55, with a reported prevalence of 30% among Asian university students56. Concurrently, the prevalence of depression among Chinese university students reaches 38.7%57. Given the high prevalence of anxiety and depression among university students, the present study examines this population to explore the mediating role of negative affect in the relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality, thereby providing theoretical implications for mental health promotion in this demographic. Individuals with high levels of experiential avoidance frequently exhibit apprehension regarding the recurrence of adverse experiences and misinterpretations of emotional states, thereby imposing emotional distress upon themselves and exacerbating the emergence of negative affect53,58,59, fueling a cycle of distress and heightened negative affect30,53,58,60. According to the internalization of conflicts model, the excessive accumulation of negative emotions can lead to physiological hyperarousal, which causes insomnia and difficulty falling asleep61. This link is supported by a significant positive correlation with negative emotions62,63. The resource depletion and avoidance theory posits that those with elevated experiential avoidance engage in strategic avoidance to mitigate exposure to aversive stimuli and associated distress. This strategy consumes a large number of cognitive resources, leaving individuals with insufficient cognitive resources for their primary cognitive activities, leading to outcomes contrary to their expectations53,64. This results in negative emotional regulation expectations and reduced emotional regulation capacity65, which creates a vulnerability to the unchecked influence of negative emotions, resulting in heightened negative affect66,67. This causes excessive negative emotional arousal before sleep and increased cortical arousal, disrupting the sleep–wake regulating neural circuits, which leads to a decrease in sleep efficiency and an increase in awakenings during sleep, ultimately lowering the overall sleep quality of the individual68. Research has shown that interventions using Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) for individuals with high levels of experiential avoidance can enhance emotional regulation ability, thus diminishing both the influence and severity of negative emotions, a change that contributes to long-term improvements in sleep quality27,69,70. Relying on the above evidence, this study proposes that negative emotions operate as a mediating factor in the relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality.

Cognitive flexibility and negative emotions have a complex relationship, and this complex relationship exerts a crucial influence on the association between experiential avoidance and sleep quality. Given the established significant negative correlation between these two variables71–73, their combined effect warrants further investigation. Individuals with poor cognitive flexibility tend to rigidly adhere to existing cognitive frameworks and behaviour patterns, unable to respond to environmental shifts. This gives rise to negative rumination and a failure to effectively filter and process adverse information and emotions, ultimately triggering emotional disorders74–76, which in turn affects sleep quality77. Research has shown that cognitive training through mindfulness and meditation can regulate the activity of the prefrontal cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal areas in individuals with low cognitive flexibility, gradually reallocating limited cognitive resources to better perceive the present, which enhances emotional regulation ability78–80, alleviating negative emotions such as anxiety and depression81. Based on this, this study hypothesizes that cognitive flexibility and negative emotions are significantly negatively correlated, and that they have a chain mediating effect on the relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality.

In summary, this study establishes a chain mediation model, where experiential avoidance takes the form of the independent variable, sleep quality functions as the dependent variable, and cognitive flexibility along with negative emotions act as mediating variables. It explores the negative effects of high levels of experiential avoidance and the complex relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality, further contributing to research in this field and providing a theoretical basis. Methods

Participants

The present investigation was conducted in the Fall of 2025, employing a convenience sample of 840 university students from regions in Southwest and South China. Ethical endorsement was secured from the Guangxi Minzu University ethics board (Number: 202510608052). Participants engaged voluntarily without remuneration and received a comprehensive briefing covering study aims, guarantees of anonymity and confidentiality, data usage, and the option to discontinue participation at any time. The survey instrument was completed within a 10-minute timeframe. After screening the surveys, incomplete responses, aberrant response time (exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the mean response time) and those with patterned responses were excluded. In the present study, normal response completion time was 8.85 ± 2.61 min. The final analysis sample consisted of 833 participants, with an average age of 18.83 ± 0.99 years (242 males, 591 females; 165 only children, 668 non-only children). To improve the credibility of the research outcomes, demographic variables such as gender, age, and only-child status were included and controlled for in the analysis to minimize their potential influence on the research findings82–84.

Measurement tools

Experiential avoidance

Experiential avoidance was assessed using the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire (AAQ-II)85 in its Chinese version validated by Cao et al.86. The questionnaire consists of 7 items, rated on a 7-point scale (1 = “Never”, 7 = “Always”). An example of the scale items is: “Distressing experiences make me unhappy in life86.” The total score is calculated by summing all items, with higher scores indicating greater experiential avoidance. For the current sample, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.832.

Cognitive flexibility

To assess the participants’ cognitive flexibility, this study used the Cognitive Flexibility Subscale from the Executive Functioning Scale, developed by Huang et al.87. The scale comprises 8 items, scored using a 3-point scale (1 = “Frequently”, 3 = “Never”). An example of the scale items is: “I get upset when plans change87.” The total score represents the sum of the item scores, where higher scores indicate lower cognitive flexibility. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the sample was 0.897.

Negative emotions

This study utilized the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-2) developed by Byrd-Bredbenner et al. and Skapinakis88,89 and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) developed by Spitzer et al.90 to assess negative emotions such as anxiety and depression in college students. The two scales consist of 4 items, with GAD-2 containing 2 items to assess an individual’s anxiety level, and PHQ-2 consisting of the first 2 items of PHQ-9 to evaluate an individual’s depression level. Both scales have demonstrated good reliability and validity88,89,91. Both scales use a 4-point Likert scale (1 = “Not at all”, 4 = “Nearly every day”). Sample items include: “How often have you felt unable to stop or control your worrying88,89?” and “Little interest or pleasure in doing things90.” The range of scores for both scales is 2–8, with higher scores indicating more frequent or elevated levels of negative emotion over the past two weeks. In this research, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for these two scales are 0.861 and 0.728 in sequence. These measures have been subjected to validation and utilized in prior studies37,92–94.

Sleep quality

To measure the sleep quality of the participants, this study used a single-item scale95 with the following question: “Please rate your overall sleep quality. Consider the past 7 days, including factors such as sleep duration, how difficult it is for you to fall asleep, how often you wake during the night (excluding bathroom visits), how often you wake up early, and how refreshed you feel upon waking95.” A 1–10 rating is adopted for the scale, where higher scores indicate better sleep quality. This single-item component was most highly correlated with global scores of sleep quality96,97. It has also been proven effective in psychological studies related to anxiety and depression, and is widely used95,97–99.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0. First, Harman’s single-factor test was employed to assess common method bias in the present study, with a threshold of 40% with a threshold of 40%, below which no significant common method bias was deemed present100. Second, descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were conducted for demographic variables and main analytical variables. Finally, mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS v4.2 macro in SPSS (Model 81), with experiential avoidance as the independent variable, sleep quality as the dependent variable, and cognitive flexibility and negative emotions as mediators. Negative emotions were categorized into two dimensions: anxiety and depression, which served as parallel mediators in the analysis. Model testing utilized 5,000 bootstrap resampling iterations to estimate path coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), with 95% CI excluding zero indicating significant path relationships, thereby ensuring the robustness of the data analysis101.

Results

Common method bias test

This study employed Harman’s single-factor test to evaluate common method bias. Without conducting principal component factor rotation, the results revealed four factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1. The first factor accounted for 31.26% of the variance, falling below the 40% threshold, which suggests the absence of significant common method bias in the study.

Chain mediation model test

Following the control of all demographic variables, the data in Tables 2 and 3, along with Fig. 2, indicate a significant negative correlation between experiential avoidance and sleep quality (β = -0.402, SE = 0.032, p < 0.001). After incorporating the mediating variables of cognitive flexibility (higher scale scores indicate lower levels) and negative emotions, this negative correlation between experiential avoidance and sleep quality remained significant (β = -0.204, SE = 0.045, p < 0.001). The mediation model reveals that experiential avoidance exhibits a significant negative correlation with cognitive flexibility (β = -0.649, SE = 0.027, p < 0.001), as well as significant positive correlations with anxiety (β = 0.487, SE = 0.036, p < 0.001) and depression (β = 0.423, SE = 0.038, p < 0.001). Furthermore, cognitive flexibility is significantly negatively correlated with both anxiety (β = -0.150, SE = 0.036, p < 0.001) and depression (β = -0.155, SE = 0.038, p < 0.001), while also showing a significant positive correlation with sleep quality (β = 0.084, SE = 0.041, p < 0.05). Both anxiety (β = -0.152, SE = 0.051, p < 0.01) and depression (β = -0.105, SE = 0.048, p < 0.05) exhibit significant negative correlations with sleep quality. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values of the four variables (experiential avoidance, cognitive flexibility, anxiety, and depression) with the dependent variable (sleep quality) were all less than 5, indicating no multicollinearity102.

Discussion

This study examines how experiential avoidance influences sleep quality, focusing on the mediating roles of cognitive flexibility and negative emotions in this connection. The results indicate that individuals with elevated experiential avoidance report impaired sleep quality, with a significant negative correlation between the two. In this relationship, the mediating effects of cognitive flexibility and negative emotions are significant. Specifically, heightened experiential avoidance adversely affects both cognitive flexibility and negative emotion, thereby exacerbating the deterioration in sleep quality. This result corroborates the primary hypothesis of this study, and the interrelationships among these variables further reveal the psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality.

Supporting the well-established literature, a significant negative correlation was observed between experiential avoidance and sleep quality, a finding that aligns with prior studies in this field103–105. Notably, this relationship was partially mediated by cognitive flexibility and negative emotions, as the inclusion of these variables reduced the direct effect. This study puts forward the following theoretical hypotheses: This pattern aligns with the tendency for individuals high in experiential avoidance to become entangled in negative experiences60, which consumes substantial cognitive resources and fosters indifferent or negative attitudes toward current primary tasks106,107. This exerts detrimental effects on both psychological18,21 and physiological functioning, leading to issues such as neurotransmitter release, neurotransmitter dysregulation and endocrine disturbances108,109, thereby increasing neural activation before sleep and contributing to insomnia110,111. At the same time, individuals with high experiential avoidance frequently engage in avoidance strategies and show increased reliance on external behaviors (e.g., short videos)112,113, resulting in psychological rigidity that interferes with the sleep process27,114. As shown in Table 2, in the complete model, the explained variance for sleep quality is moderate (R² ≈ 0.224). This indicates that the relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality is not constant and may be related to more variables that were not measured in this study (e.g., academic stress, evening screen exposure, physical activity, chronotype, substance use)115–117. Furthermore, there are also more complex psychological118,119 and physiological mechanisms27 involved between the two.

Table 3 demonstrates a weakened correlation between experiential avoidance and sleep quality after accounting for the mediators, with cognitive flexibility emerging as a key mediator. Individuals with high levels of experiential avoidance subtly avoid adverse experiences during the process, rigidly adopting avoidance strategies120, becoming trapped in a cycle of avoiding problems and facing issues121,122, leading to psychological distress123 and psychological rigidity, which lowers their cognitive flexibility114. Individuals with low cognitive flexibility require substantial cognitive resources to process negative experiences, continuing this process both before sleep and during sleep, which raises autonomic and cognitive arousal levels61, resulting in insomnia and sleep cycle disturbances124,125, thus reducing sleep quality126. These findings suggest that individuals with high levels of experiential avoidance have low cognitive flexibility and are accompanied by sleep quality problems.

The results also demonstrate a significant mediating effect of negative emotions on the experiential avoidance-sleep quality relationship. This is consistent with the pattern that highly avoidant individuals are characterized by conscious avoidance of distress from adverse experiences. These efforts only magnify the emotions, leading to cognitive rigidity when facing negative emotions127, exacerbating the emergence of internalizing problems128, and impairing individuals’ mental health. Individuals’ internalizing problems are usually accompanied by social apathy, reduced physical activity, and increased sedentary behaviours106,129, which exert sustained stimulation on the nervous system, potentially leading to emotional dysregulation and endocrine disruption, interfering with cerebral activity130,131, and impose negative impacts on individuals’ circadian rhythms, thereby reducing sleep quality132,133. Previous research has identified a significant correlation between negative emotions and poor sleep quality61,134, and as many as 45%–90% of individuals with internalizing problems exhibit decreased sleep quality135–137 which aligns with the results of this study. Evidence also indicates that negative emotions are related to the activity of the individual’s monoaminergic system138,139, and the neurotransmitters secreted by this system play a crucial role during REM sleep140. In summary, this study supports the proposition that individuals with heightened experiential avoidance are accompanied by high levels of negative emotions, and there is a significant negative cross-sectional association between this and sleep quality.

Taken together, this study constructs a chain mediation model involving cognitive flexibility and negative emotions between experiential avoidance and sleep quality, further validating and revealing the correlation and complex relationship between experiential avoidance and sleep quality. The results indicate that when the mediating roles of cognitive flexibility and negative emotions are considered, the direct effect between experiential avoidance and sleep quality is weakened. Therefore, in the process of reducing an individual’s level of experiential avoidance, it is important to enhance their self-regulation ability, improve psychological adaptability, and create a supportive environment that fosters positive emotions. This approach will, in turn, improve and enhance long-term sleep quality and overall quality of life27,141,142. Overall, this study elucidates the complex mediating mechanisms linking experiential avoidance, cognitive flexibility, negative emotions, and sleep quality, thereby providing a robust empirical basis for supporting university students. Insights derived from these findings are crucial for developing targeted interventions aimed at fostering resilience and promoting health in the context of contemporary societal pressures.

This research examined the effect of experiential avoidance on sleep quality, focusing on the mediating pathway involving cognitive flexibility and negative emotions. Nonetheless, the study has some limitations: (1) The data were collected from university students via a convenience sampling approach. Their psychological traits and social experiences differ from those of clinical patients or cross-cultural populations; (2) To minimize participant burden, this study utilized a single-item questionnaire (SQS) to assess sleep quality. It precludes the acquisition of subscale-specific scores for distinct dimensions of sleep quality (e.g., sleep latency, sleep efficiency), which limits the interpretation of these dimensions. And there is a lack of objective sleep indices (e.g., actigraphy/PSG). Additionally, this questionnaire has not been validated in clinical trials95. Therefore, the generalizability of these findings to such populations warrants cautious validation; (3) This study is based on a cross-sectional design, and the long-term changes between variables require further investigation. (4) exclusive reliance on self-report (with potential common-method and desirability biases despite Harman’s test), which have limitations in terms of objectivity; (5) Omission of relevant confounders (chronotype, caffeine/alcohol/nicotine, health conditions, medication, workload, device use); (6) Due to the convenient sampling conducted from multiple regions in southern China, the generalizability of the results may be limited. (7) Potential conceptual overlap between experiential avoidance and cognitive flexibility, which may inflate associations. Based on these limitations, the following suggestions for future research are proposed: (1) collect multi-source data (such as increasing the sample size and including different population groups); (2) Explore the role of anxiety and depression as signs and symptoms in the model within clinical patients; (3) adopt longitudinal studies to track the relationships between variables and deeply explore gender differences; (4) Sleep quality is assessed using a multi-dimensional measurement approach (e.g., PSQI), and also incorporates objective indicators for each variable to further strengthen the reliability of the results; (5) investigate additional potential variables that may impact the association between experiential avoidance and sleep quality, as well as possible intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Through analyzing the mediating roles of cognitive flexibility and negative emotions, this research further clarifies the influence of experiential avoidance on sleep quality. The results suggest that these factors substantially mediate this relationship. This research highlights that providing timely positive emotional and behavioral interventions to individuals with elevated experiential avoidance levels can drive improvements in their sleep quality, thus forming a positive feedback loop.
