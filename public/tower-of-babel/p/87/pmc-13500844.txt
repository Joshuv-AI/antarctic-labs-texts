Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy contribute to developmental and metabolic multimorbidity in preterm infants
Authors: Cuihong Yang, Ranran Shi, Jiao Yuan, Yanjie Ding, Hongli Geng, Huan Li, Lili Zhao, Jiahui Li, Mingming Zhao, Xiaoxue You, Yongfeng Jia, Ming Zhang, Wenwen Zhang, Jie Gu, Yonghui Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preterm birth poses a global health challenge, frequently resulting in complex infant multimorbidity. While the adverse effects of maternal hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) on offspring are well recognized, their specific impact on distinct multimorbidity patterns in very preterm infants remains underexplored. In this multicenter cohort study, we utilize the Sino-northern Neonatal Network (SNN) database to systematically characterize these profiles. Here we show distinct patterns, including extrauterine growth restriction (EUGR) complicated by parenteral nutrition-associated cholestasis (EUGR-PNAC), congenital hypothyroidism (EUGR-CH), retinopathy of prematurity (EUGR-ROP), and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (EUGR-BPD). Notably, maternal HDP significantly increases the risk of developing these specific patterns. By elucidating these clinical consequences, our study advances the current understanding of the impact of adverse intrauterine exposures on offspring health. Ultimately, these insights highlight the critical need for multidisciplinary screening and management in this high-risk population, providing a crucial foundation for improving long-term neonatal outcomes.

Introduction

Preterm birth represents a significant global burden. Due to incomplete intrauterine development or exposure to adverse intrauterine environments, preterm infants frequently face long-term health challenges1,2. Consequently, metabolic syndrome and neurodevelopmental diseases are more prevalent in preterm than in term-born children, and tend to manifest as multimorbidity later in life2–4. For instance, a national Swedish birth cohort revealed that, at 30 years of age, 45% of adults born preterm had multiple comorbidities, compared to 37% of term-born adults1. Multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of multiple health conditions5, has largely been studied in youth and adult populations6,7. However, research into the epidemiology and systemic impact of early-life multimorbidity remains underdeveloped. Illustrating this early-life burden, data from 10 Vermont Oxford Network neonatal intensive care units (2014–2018) indicated that approximately 30% of extremely premature infants (23–27 weeks) experienced at least two complications during hospitalization8. Crucially, early-life multimorbidity represents profound maturational vulnerability and systemic physiological disruption, rather than a mere coincidental overlap of diseases9. Therefore, elucidating the etiology and clustering patterns of multimorbidity in preterm infants is essential. These insights hold profound implications for predicting long-term health trajectories, mitigating the onset of chronic adult multimorbidity, and reducing the compounding global healthcare burden9,10.

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework provides a robust theoretical foundation for understanding the genesis of early-life multimorbidity. The DOHaD paradigm posits that adverse fetal exposures induce persistent “fetal programming”—involving epigenetic modifications, metabolic adaptations, and vascular remodeling—that alters disease susceptibility across the lifespan11. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), a common and severe complication of gestation, represent a profound adverse intrauterine environment known to compromise fetal blood flow and nutrient supply. This often results in intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) and small for gestational age (SGA) infants, both of which are associated with severe sequelae12–18. Beyond SGA, HDP can precipitate other severe neonatal complications. Prolonged exposure to chronic fetal hypoxia due to HDP often leads to poor gastrointestinal function and feeding intolerance in low-gestational-age preterm infants after birth. This, in turn, can result in excessive dependence on parenteral nutrition and a range of growth, developmental, and metabolic disorders19–21.

Under the DOHaD paradigm, HDP not only triggers isolated neonatal morbidities but also predisposes the preterm infant to complex, multisystem multimorbidity. Most previous studies have focused on the association between HDP and single adverse outcomes in preterm infants. In our prior study based on the CARE-Preterm (Chinese Adverse Prognosis of Very Preterm Infants) cohort22, we found that HDP was associated with the occurrence of extrauterine growth restriction (EUGR) and congenital hypothyroidism (CH)23,24. Similarly, the Canadian Neonatal Network reported an elevated risk of severe retinopathy of prematurity (ROP)25, while the Korean Neonatal Network associated maternal HDP with an increased incidence of neonatal respiratory diseases26. Additional studies from Sweden and Japan have also established connections between HDP and neurodevelopmental disorders27,28. While the UK National Neonatal Research Database correlated a higher number of infant comorbidities with increased neurodevelopmental impairment at 2 years of age29, it did not explicitly establish a link to prenatal exposures. Consequently, the comprehensive landscape of multimorbidity patterns that emerge in preterm infants exposed to HDP remains largely underexplored. There is a significant knowledge gap regarding the association between HDP and the concurrent manifestation of growth, developmental, and metabolic disorders in very preterm infants, as opposed to their isolated incidence. Addressing this gap is essential for developing integrated clinical management strategies and improving long-term health outcomes, which necessitates research using large population-based data.

This study aims to fill knowledge gaps by prospectively investigating the association between maternal HDP and the multimorbidity pattern of growth, developmental, and metabolic disorders among very preterm infants (GA < 32 weeks) utilizing the CARE-Preterm cohort.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 7261 very preterm infants, with a gestational age (GA) ranging from 24 to 31 weeks, were included in this study from 47 participating neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), after excluding 1447 infants (63 due to congenital and chromosomal abnormalities, 529 due to missing data, and 855 who withdrew care) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of these, 1919 (26.4%) were comprised in the HDP group, comprising infants whose mothers had preeclampsia (64.8%), pregnancy-induced hypertension (20.2%), pre-pregnancy hypertension (11.5%), or eclampsia (3.4%). Only 237 infants (3.3%) were classified as SGA, which is lower than the expected 10% incidence based on the 10th percentile threshold. This discrepancy likely stems from two factors: first, the actual prevalence in our <32-week cohort may naturally deviate from the Fenton growth curve standards; and second, a potential selection bias due to care withdrawal in vulnerable cases. Within the SGA group, 65.8% (156) were born to mothers with HDP, compared to only 25.1% (1763) in the non-SGA group (7024 infants).

Compared to infants in the non-HDP group, those in the HDP group were more likely to have older mothers, higher rates of cesarean section, and increased use of magnesium sulfate. Moreover, they experienced longer durations of neonatal feeding intolerance and required a longer time to achieve full enteral feeding. Association between HDP and single adverse outcomes

As shown in Fig. 1a, infants in the HDP group were significantly more likely to experience EUGR, CH, parenteral nutrition-associated cholestasis (PNAC), metabolic bone disease (MBD), and death compared to those in the non-HDP group. Among SGA infants, a greater proportion in the HDP group experienced EUGR (83.3%) and CH (23.1%) compared to the non-HDP group (Fig. 1b). For non-SGA infants, those in the HDP group were more likely to develop PNAC and MBD, in addition to EUGR and CH. To account for potential differences between SGA and non-SGA infants, we conducted the same analysis within these subgroups. For SGA infants, the four most frequent multimorbidity patterns were EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-CH, EUGR-DEATH, and EUGR-PNAC-ROP (Supplementary Fig. 5). Meanwhile, among the non-SGA infants, the dominant patterns remained consistent with the overall cohort: EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-CH, and EUGR-BPD (Supplementary Fig. 6). Guided by the minimum Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), a 6-class model was identified as the optimal latent structure for both the overall cohort and the non-SGA subpopulation, whereas a 2-class model emerged as the optimal solution for the SGA subpopulation. All optimal models yielded relative entropy values exceeding 0.90, indicating exceptionally high classification certainty and robust inter-class discrimination (Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 7). Within the 6-class latent class analysis (LCA) model for the overall population, the unsupervised algorithm perfectly replicated the core clinical patterns initially identified via the UpSet plot. Specifically, the four distinct comorbidity combinations determined by the UpSet approach (EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-CH, and EUGR-BPD) were precisely mapped onto four independent latent classes with 100% purity and zero cross-class misclassification. This data-driven concordance provides statistical validation for the authenticity and robustness of these clinical multimorbidity phenotypes (Supplementary Tables 2–4).

We employed two models for multinomial logistic regression: a crude model (Model 1) and a full model (Model 2). The full model was adjusted for infant perinatal characteristics (cesarean section, multiple births, assisted reproduction, magnesium sulfate use, antenatal corticosteroids, gestational age, birth weight, and sex) and maternal characteristics. As illustrated in Fig.4, HDP exposure significantly elevated the relative risk for distinct multimorbidity patterns. Most notably, the risk was roughly doubled for patterns involving EUGR concurrent with other major morbidities, including EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-CH, and EUGR-BPD. Furthermore, SGA status modified the association between HDP and EUGR-BPD (p for interaction = 0.041), indicating that the impact of HDP on the risk of EUGR-BPD differs significantly between SGA and non-SGA infants (Supplementary Table 5). Given the distinct clinical profiles of SGA and non-SGA infants, we independently identified unique multimorbidity patterns within each subgroup to perform subgroup-specific analyzes (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). When analyzed based on these distinct outcomes, no significant differences in multimorbidity were observed among SGA infants (Fig. 4). This lack of significance may be attributable to the fact that SGA infants generally experience worse outcomes30,31, as well as limited sample size. Among non-SGA infants, HDP exposure consistently drove higher relative risks—approximately doubling the likelihood—for complex multimorbidity patterns involving EUGR paired with PNAC, ROP, CH, or BPD.

Discussion

Intrauterine exposure factors at the onset of life exert a profound impact on the growth and development of multiple organs, and this influence persists throughout the lifespan. This population-based, multicenter cohort study reveals a significant association between maternal HDP and multimorbidity patterns of growth, development, and metabolism disorders in preterm infants. We observed four predominant multimorbidity patterns in the study population: EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-CH, and EUGR-BPD. After adjusting for potential confounders, significant differences in multimorbidity risk emerged between the HDP and non-HDP groups. Within the non-SGA subgroups, we found that HDP also increased the risk of EUGR occurring concomitantly with PNAC, CH, or BPD. These multiple complications not only affect the survival and early development of preterm infants but also have implications for their long-term health, warranting considerable attention.

Multimorbidity is prevalent in adult chronic disease research32,33, as aging populations often live with multiple concurrent health conditions long-term. However, multimorbidity can manifest across all life stages, affecting an estimated 10–29% of children and adolescents6,34. In line with the DOHaD framework, our findings suggest that the adverse intrauterine environment induced by HDP may act as a critical early factor in fetal programming. Existing literature indicates that uteroplacental vascular insufficiency associated with HDP can lead to chronic fetal hypoxia, altering developmental trajectories and increasing the long-term risk of cardiovascular and metabolic morbidities35. While previous studies have primarily established the links between HDP and individual adverse outcomes, our study uniquely maps early postnatal multimorbidity patterns. This suggests that intrauterine exposure to HDP may induce systemic vulnerabilities that manifest as complex multimorbidity.

HDP is recognized as a placental vascular disease and is one of the most common complications of pregnancy. It is associated with various adverse offspring outcomes, including IUGR and SGA at birth13,18. In this study, 26.4% of preterm infants experienced intrauterine exposure to HDP. These infants often endure chronic intrauterine hypoxia and reduced placental blood flow, which is frequently compounded by exposure to various antihypertensive and vasoactive medications19,27,36. Such conditions are associated with poor organ function, particularly affecting gastrointestinal function, and an increased risk of SGA and EUGR. In our study, 83.3% of SGA infants with HDP exposure exhibited EUGR after birth. Among the non-SGA infants with HDP exposure, 44.0% also developed EUGR in the early postnatal period. Additionally, these infants often require prolonged parenteral nutrition due to immature gastrointestinal function; this increases the hepatic load and exacerbates the vulnerability of an already immature liver, predisposing them to concurrent PNAC37,38. Our findings also indicated a significantly elevated risk of EUGR-PNAC among the HDP-exposed infants. These findings emphasize the importance of vigilant gastrointestinal and hepatic function monitoring, early enteral nutrition management, and proactive interventions to prevent this comorbidity in HDP-exposed infants.

In addition to EUGR-PNAC, we found a high risk of EUGR-CH multimorbidity among infants exposed to HDP. Preterm infants with growth restriction require postnatal catch-up growth, which demands relatively high levels of thyroid hormones. However, their thyroid function is often immature, which can exacerbate existing growth restriction and thereby precipitate the EUGR-CH multimorbidity pattern. Our previous studies have reported relatively lower levels of serum thyroxine hormone and a higher incidence of CH in preterm infants, particularly those born at lower gestational ages23. Currently, standard newborn screening protocols for CH may yield falsely normal results in preterm infants due to an immature hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis and a delayed TSH surge. Given the highly elevated risk of the EUGR-CH pattern in HDP-exposed infants identified in our study, it is necessary to implement weekly screening (evaluating both venous TSH and free T4) for the first 4 weeks of life, or prior to discharge23. Early detection and timely initiation of levothyroxine replacement therapy are paramount to mitigating compounding growth failures and optimizing neurocognitive trajectories. The European Reference Network states that follow-up evaluations should be conducted every 1–2 months before 12 months of age; every 2–4 months between 12 months and 3 years of age; and every 3–6 months thereafter. As children transition into adulthood, individualized follow-up and educational plans should be developed. Individualized levothyroxine (LT4) replacement therapy regimens should be formulated based on the patient’s age-specific growth, developmental, and reproductive needs for thyroid hormones39. For preterm infants exposed to HDP, follow-up protocols should be strictly adhered to, with close monitoring of neurodevelopment.

Notably, among non-SGA infants, the EUGR-BPD pattern was one of the most frequently identified multimorbidities in the HDP-exposed cohort. Infants exposed to HDP typically require prolonged mechanical ventilation and non-invasive respiratory support after birth due to growth restriction and malnutrition, predisposing them to BPD. Furthermore, infants with BPD typically exhibit increased nutritional requirements, and the use of mechanical ventilation, diuretics, and glucocorticoids may also exacerbate EUGR, further increasing the risk of EUGR-BPD comorbidity40,41. Consequently, the postnatal NICU environment acts as a “second hit.” Necessary life-support interventions trigger oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in already vulnerable preterm infants. Therefore, the multimorbidity observed in our non-SGA cohort may not be solely a direct consequence of HDP, but rather the result of a heightened baseline susceptibility reacting to necessary postnatal clinical interventions. Regarding BPD management, international guidelines advise minimizing corticosteroid use. Both the American Academy of Pediatrics and the Canadian Pediatric Society recommend against the routine administration of systemic corticosteroids to prevent or treat BPD in very low birth weight infants. Additionally, long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up is mandatory for all neonates receiving corticosteroid therapy42. The European Respiratory Society guideline strongly supports long-term, structured, multidisciplinary follow-up for children with BPD, lasting from discharge through to adulthood, with a focus on lung function, pulmonary hypertension, neurodevelopment, and retinal and renal health. However, the guideline does not provide specific follow-up intervals. This should be determined by clinicians based on disease severity and local resources43.

This study addresses a significant gap in perinatal and neonatal medicine by systematically and comprehensively exploring the link between HDP as an intrauterine exposure factor and the multimorbidity of growth, developmental, and metabolic disorders. Unlike previous studies focusing on single outcomes, our study uniquely identifies and quantifies the increased risk of specific multimorbidity patterns. Investigating the association between early-life exposures and multimorbidity is profoundly important, as this early-life burden affects not only neonatal survival but also long-term quality of life, potentially increasing the risk of multimorbidity in adolescence and adulthood. It requires multi-party cooperation to tackle the challenge that multimorbidity poses to the quality of individual life and the health system. There is a need for mechanistic studies to explore the potential causal links between intrauterine exposure factors and multimorbidity. From a public health perspective, upstream strategies targeting social and environmental factors during the perinatal or prenatal periods are key opportunities to reduce the incidence of multimorbidity in preterm infants and to improve population health. Furthermore, as a prospective, multicenter, large cohort study based on the online clinical research database of the Sino-northern Neonatal Network (SNN), the data storage and acquisition processes are reliable. Additionally, most participating NICUs are located in regions with China’s largest population bases and highest fertility rates, and the data quality is controlled by well-trained quality control teams, minimizing the risk of information bias.

This study also has several limitations. First, as this study was based on observational data, our findings do not lend themselves to a causal interpretation of the association between HDP and preterm infant multimorbidity. Instead, they serve to generate hypotheses for future research rather than offering confirmatory conclusions. In addition, our findings could have been influenced by residual confounding from unknown or unmeasured confounders. Furthermore, our reliance on a complete-case analysis for handling missing data may have introduced potential selection bias. If the excluded infants with missing variables differed systematically from the included cohort, our effect estimates might under- or overestimate the true associations. Although we explored potential effect modifications by sex and gestational age in our models, no statistically significant interactions were observed. This lack of significance may suggest that the severe intrauterine insult caused by HDP exerts a uniformly detrimental impact on these vulnerable preterm infants, largely overriding potential sex-specific or gestational-age-specific adaptive mechanisms. Alternatively, it is important to acknowledge that tests for interaction typically require substantially larger sample sizes than main-effect analyzes. Therefore, future studies need larger, pooled cohorts to detect subtle effect modifications. We did not stratify by the subtype of maternal HDP, which may carry different risks. With an increased sample size in future research, we intend to explore the associations between distinct HDP subtypes and the risk of multimorbidity in preterm infants. While the overall study is well-powered, the subgroup analysis for SGA infants should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively smaller sample size. As this study was conducted exclusively within a Chinese population, the generalizability of our findings to other geographic or ethnic populations remains to be established. Potential variations in genetic backgrounds, environmental exposures, and regional healthcare practices may influence the observed associations. Despite this limitation, the necessity of integrated, multidisciplinary management for preterm infants remains a universally applicable clinical priority. Finally, the multimorbidity of growth, development, and metabolism disorders necessitates long-term follow-up, ideally through intergenerational research.

This study identifies specific multimorbidity risks in very preterm infants exposed to HDP, revealing a previously underexplored association and advancing our understanding of the early-life effects of intrauterine exposure. These findings underscore the critical need for a holistic clinical management strategy addressing adverse outcomes across growth, development, and metabolism. They also reinforce the importance of long-term follow-up, as early-life multimorbidity may influence health trajectories into later life. To this end, it is imperative to strengthen multidisciplinary screening and integrated management for this high-risk population—involving neonatologists, endocrinologists, gastroenterologists, and nutritionists—ultimately aiming to improve long-term outcomes and inform targeted public health strategies.

Methods

Data source and study population

We performed a multicenter cohort study of very preterm infants (GA: 24–31 weeks) in 47 NICUs based on the CARE-Preterm cohort in China from January 1, 2019, through December 31, 2023. The inclusion criteria for the CARE-Preterm cohort comprised all very preterm infants with a birth GA of less than 32 weeks or very low birth weight infants with a birth weight of less than 1500 g, who either born alive or transferred alive to the participating units within 28 days of birth. Further details regarding the CARE-Preterm cohort are available in its published profile22. Patient records were obtained from the SNN, a clinical research database in China, which has strict data entry and quality-control standards. Each participating center received secure database access via unique credentials, ensuring data confidentiality and limiting access to site-specific records. Trained data entry coordinators at each participating NICU prospectively collected infant information and entered the data into the database. To ensure a clear and uniform definition of each clinical variable in the database, a data entry manual of operations was designed during the initiation of the cohort. A robust data quality management system was employed to ensure data validity. During the data entry process, minor errors were automatically identified and corrected by the system. In addition, a dedicated data quality control team, established by the Principal Investigator association, provided oversight to ensure the accuracy and completeness of data from each center. After data upload, administrators of this team conducted a second round of data quality control, providing weekly feedback and queries to the data entry coordinators to maintain data accuracy and consistency.

All 47 hospitals collected data on preterm infants with standardized definitions during the 5-year study period. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or guardians of the infants at each participating institution. No participant compensation was provided. Data on live-born infants were collected prospectively until discharge or death. Exclusion criteria for this study were defined as follows: major congenital abnormalities, withdrawal of care, and missing data (including maternal characteristics). The exclusion of infants with withdrawn care, which was often due to non-medical factors such as socioeconomic issues44, was necessary to avoid potential bias arising from their typically brief hospitalizations and resulting incomplete medical records. Ultimately, a total of 7261 very preterm infants were included in the final analysis.

Study definitions

HDP were defined as hospital or in-office systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, based on the average of at least two measurements from the same arm at least 15 min apart. HDP included pre-pregnancy hypertension, pregnancy-induced hypertension (hypertension without proteinuria), preeclampsia, eclampsia, and preeclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension45.

SGA was defined as a birth weight below the 10th percentile for gestational age based on the Fenton growth curve46. CH was defined as the presence of any serum thyroid-stimulating hormone >20mU/L with or without decreased triiodothyronine, thyroxin or free triiodothyronine, free thyroxine23. EUGR was defined as a weight below the 10th percentile of the corresponding gestational age at discharge or at a corrected gestational age of 36 weeks according to the 2013 Fenton growth curve47. MBD was defined as peak serum alkaline phosphatase higher than 900 μ/L and serum phosphorus over than 1.8 mmol/L, which yielded a sensitivity of 100% at a specificity of 70%, with or without radiographic changes of long bones48. PNAC is defined as parenteral nutrition lasting >14 days, accompanied by yellow skin staining and/or light stool color, a direct bilirubin level≥34 µmol/L (2 mg/dL) or a direct bilirubin level≥17 µmol/L (1 mg/dL) when the total bilirubin level was ≤85µmol/L at any moment, and excluding any infections, such as viral, bacterial, and fungal, biliary tract developmental malformations and other causes49.

BPD was defined as the use of supplemental oxygen for more than 28 days of life and further stratified as mild, moderate, or severe according to the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development consensus-200150. Moderate-to-severe BPD was defined as any infant requiring continuous supplemental oxygen at 36 weeks of postmenstrual age or at hospital discharge, whichever occurred earlier. NEC was defined as stage 2 or higher NEC according to the modified Bell’s staging criteria51. Severe IVH was defined as III or IV grade of IVH according to the criteria of the Papile classification system, and the worst grading result during hospitalization was recorded52. PVL was diagnosed based on the results of brain ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging findings before NICU discharge. ROP was defined as stage 3 or higher ROP according to the International Committee for the Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity53.

Multimorbidity of growth, development and metabolic disorders

Based on biological and clinical plausibility, infants exposed to HDP are more likely to experience at least one of the aforementioned growth, developmental, or metabolic disorders. Overall, the multimorbidity patterns observed included EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-CH, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-BPD, and other co-occurring conditions grouped as “Other”. This composite category was used to capture all remaining multimorbidity patterns that were not designated as specific named categories, primarily due to their lower individual frequencies or lack of a strong established clinical relationship within our predefined criteria. This approach ensured that all multimorbidity cases were captured while focusing the primary analysis on the most clinically significant and prevalent co-occurring conditions. Specifically, for SGA infants, patterns included EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-CH, EUGR-DEATH, EUGR-PNAC-ROP, and other conditions grouped as “Other”. For non-SGA infants, the patterns were EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-CH, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-BPD, and other conditions grouped as “Other”.

Covariables

A priori confounders were selected based on biological plausibility and previous studies. These included: (1) maternal factors (maternal age, DM/GDM, multiple births, cesarean section, and assisted reproduction54), which can alter the intrauterine metabolic environment, placental perfusion, and fetal programming; (2) perinatal and inflammatory factors (chorioamnionitis55, preterm premature rupture of membranes ≥24 h, magnesium sulfate use, and antenatal corticosteroids), which are critically involved in the fetal systemic inflammatory response (FIRS) and organ maturation pathways, predisposing infants to morbidities such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and ROP56; and (3) neonatal baseline characteristics (gestational age, birth weight, sex [assigned at birth based on biological characteristics], SGA, and 5-min Apgar score ≤7), which directly reflect the absolute degree of physiological immaturity, perinatal hypoxia, and vulnerability to growth and metabolic/endocrine dysfunctions57,58.

Statistical analysis

Standard descriptive analyzes were conducted to summarize demographic and clinical data. Following normality testing via the Anderson-Darling test (Supplementary Table 8), all continuous variables were found to be non-normally distributed. Therefore, continuous variables were presented as medians with 25th and 75th percentiles (inter quartile range, IQR), while categorical variables were presented as percentages. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using the chi-squared (χ2) test (with Yates’ continuity correction when appropriate). To control the False Discovery Rate for multiple comparisons, a Benjamini & Hochberg (BH) correction was applied to all p-values derived from univariable analyzes. A BH-adjusted p-value of <0.05 was used as the threshold for statistical significance in these analyzes59. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d for continuous variables and odds ratios for dichotomous variables. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using logistic regression. For the multimorbidity outcomes, relative risk ratios (RRRs) were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model. The outcome variable was categorized into specific multimorbidity patterns (EUGR-PNAC, EUGR-CH, EUGR-DEATH, EUGR-PNAC-ROP, EUGR-ROP, EUGR-BPD, and other outcomes), with “no adverse outcomes” serving as the reference category. Interaction terms between HDP and SGA, sex, or gestational age group (<28 and≥28 weeks) were included in separate models (because of small numbers in each stratum) to determine the presence of effect modification in the association between HDP and multimorbidity patterns. We performed the same analysis within the SGA and non-SGA subgroups. To statistically validate the heuristically defined multimorbidity categories derived from the UpSet plot, we conducted a purely data-driven LCA as a sensitivity analysis. Models ranging from 2 to 6 latent classes were iteratively estimated based on the binary indicators of the included morbidities. The optimal number of latent classes was determined primarily by the minimum BIC, alongside a relative entropy threshold (>0.80) to ensure excellent classification certainty. The optimal LCA-derived phenotypes were then cross-tabulated against the UpSet classifications to verify phenotypic concordance. Regarding missing data, a pragmatic approach was employed. We first attempted to retrieve any missing information from the medical system. Any data that could not be recovered were subsequently excluded from the analysis. This constitutes a complete-case analysis, and while it may introduce a degree of bias if data are not missing completely at random, it was considered a reasonable and transparent method given the prospective nature of the cohort and the rigorous data quality control measures implemented. All analyzes were performed using R version 4.5.3.
