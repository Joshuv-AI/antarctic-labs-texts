Effectiveness of Nurse-led Interventions on Pain and Complications among Patients Admitted to Intensive Care Unit: A Quasi-experimental study
Authors: Meghashree Gajanan Naik, Hezil Reema Barboza, Jyothi Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsCritically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) require specialized care, and inadequate pain management remains a prevalent issue that contributes to long-term complications and adversely affects recovery. The aim of the study was to evaluate the impact of nurse-led interventions on pain related to critical care and the prevention of ICU-related complications among patients.Patients and methodsA quasi-experimental pretest posttest control group design was adopted for this study, which was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Mangaluru, Karnataka State, India. Patients admitted to the ICU (n = 60) were selected using purposive sampling and were divided equally into intervention and control groups. The intervention group received structured nurse-led care focused on ICU-related pain and strategies to minimize complications through supportive communication and a range of motion (ROM) exercises every morning for 1 week.ResultsIn the intervention group, 53.3% of patients were aged 60–69 years, whereas in the control group, 40.0% of them were between 50 and 59 years. The intervention and control groups reported a reduction in critical care pain scores and ICU-related complications (p < 0.05). Notably, the participants in the intervention group showed a greater reduction in the parameters of study outcomes compared to the control group, indicating a clinically significant positive impact of the nurse-led interventions (p < 0.05). There was a significant association between pain and age, type of family, and basal metabolic index (BMI), as well as ICU-related complications and age.ConclusionThe findings underscore the effectiveness of nurse-led interventions in mitigating critical care pain and reducing ICU-related complications, demonstrating their potential as an evidence-based strategy to improve patient outcomes.How to cite this articleNaik MG, Barboza HR, Rao J. Introduction

Intensive care units (ICUs) exhibit a wide range of practices across different global healthcare systems, highlighting the need for evidence-based standardization to optimize patient outcomes.1 Most adult patients in ICUs experience pain due to their critical illness, medical procedures, or underlying conditions. The intensity of this pain varies based on individual factors, the specific interventions performed, and existing diseases.2 Poorly managed acute pain is a significant factor in the development of chronic pain, with studies indicating that up to 33% of the ICU survivor population.2

Pain is a prevalent issue in the ICU, occurring both at rest and during routine procedures, such as insertion and removal of tubes, suctioning, and turning of a patient. To address this issue, clinical practice guidelines recommend a multimodal analgesic approach. This strategy aims to minimize the use of opioids by incorporating a combination of various pain relief methods, including non-pharmacological interventions such as massage, touch, and music therapy.3 Critically ill patients are susceptible to multiple complications related to both the severity of the primary illness and intensive care treatments. In many situations, these complications are associated with increased morbidity and mortality rates and often result in healthcare expenses and longer lengths of stay in the ICU. A number of these complications are potentially preventable, and their incidence rates are used as quality metrics within ICU settings.4

Patients in the ICU often communicate through gestures, sign language, writing, or incomprehensible words. Sometimes, their words may be unclear or hard to understand. Since ICU patients often struggle with verbal communication, nurses need more time to interact with them. This extended interaction allows nurses to accurately understand and helps them take the right actions and provide appropriate care. Nurses need to make frequent eye contact with patients during interactions to help gain trust in their communication.5 Range of motion exercises prevent the development of adaptive muscle shortening and contractures, and provide sensory stimulation.5,6 In this regard, evidence-based interventions are used to develop a nurse-led program to improve the outcomes of patients admitted to the ICU.

Patients and Methods

Formal permission was taken from the Scientific Review Board and Institutional Ethics Committee 2, with approved protocol number YEC2/2024/190 on September 18, 2024. The study is registered under CTRI with registered number CTRI/2025/01/079416 on January 1, 2025. The formal permission was obtained from the Medical Superintendent at the hospital prior to data collection. Confidentiality and privacy of patient information were maintained by the investigators. Data collection was conducted between February 1, 2025, and April 30, 2025.

The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the impact of nurse-led interventions on critical care-related pain and the prevention of ICU-related complications among patients. The secondary objective was to examine the associations between pain and complications with selected demographic variables.

Sampling Technique and Sample Selection Criteria

The samples for this research study were chosen using a purposive sampling technique. The inclusion criteria included patients aged 20–70 years, conscious and responding to verbal commands, and with a medical diagnosis of pulmonary and cardiovascular diseases. The exclusion criteria included patients with physical deformities that restricted their ability to perform certain types of exercises and those requiring mechanical ventilation and aggressive ventilation therapies such as BiPAP.

The study employed a quasi-experimental pretest posttest control group design, conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Mangaluru, Karnataka State, India. Patients admitted to the ICU (n = 60) were selected using purposive sampling and were divided equally into intervention (n = 30) and control (n = 30) groups.

Study Variables

Nurse-led Intervention

In this study, nurse-led intervention includes providing care exclusively by the nurse investigator through supportive communication and ROM exercises. Supportive communication includes touch, spending time with the patient, and being involved in patient care every morning for 1 week. The nursing care activities were performed during patient care, which included open communication and explaining medical information clearly, listening and understanding patient concerns, meeting nutritional needs, administering medications, positioning the patient, meeting comfort needs, and monitoring vital signs.

Range of motion exercises for the upper and lower extremities were performed by the patients with the assistance of the nurse investigator in the evening every day. Upper extremity ROM exercises included flexion and extension of the wrist, flexion and extension of the elbow, supination and pronation of the upper arms, and shoulder circles. Lower extremity ROM exercises included dorsiflexion and plantar flexion of the ankle. All the movements were performed 10 times in the evening for 1 week. The ROM activities were limited to simple joint movements within the routine nursing scope and did not overlap with specialized physiotherapy or occupational therapy procedures such as strengthening, gait training, chest physiotherapy, or rehabilitation. Hence, the intervention was distinct from routine ICU care and remained within the purview of nursing practice.

The control group received no specific intervention from the nurse investigator. Routine nursing care was provided by the nurses as per ICU policy and did not include any additional structured, scheduled, or protocolized components delivered by the nurse investigator. The routine ICU nursing care, as per ICU policy, included ongoing monitoring of vital signs, administration of prescribed medications, and assistance with hygienic, nutritional, and elimination needs. Pain assessment was a part of routine observations using a numerical pain scale, but without the structured use of the Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT) scale. Maintaining patient comfort, turning and positioning as required, and general communication related to clinical procedures.

Specific Clinical Outcomes

In this study, specific clinical outcomes were critical care pain and ICU-related complications.

Critical Care Pain

It is an unpleasant sensation expressed by patients admitted to the ICU and assessed based on the observation of facial expressions, body movements, vocalization, and muscle tension. The pain among patients was assessed by the critical CPOT developed by Gélinas et al.7 It is a standardized tool with domains such as vocalization, facial expression, body movements, and muscle tension, with scores ranging from a minimum of 0 to maximum of 8.

Intensive Care Unit-related Complications

These are adverse events that occur in critically ill patients during their stay in the ICU, which may require aggressive therapies for their care. Intensive care unit-related complications includes retention of secretions in the tracheobronchial tree, collapse of lung, pneumonia, extreme variation in the blood pressure, tenderness, generalized edema, hypovolemia, deep vein thrombosis (Wells criteria), constipation, vomiting, paralytic ileus, contractures, muscle weakness, numbness, hypoglycemia, pressure ulcers, skin dryness, fear, disorientation, alterations in behavior pattern, sleep disturbance, which was assessed by using a checklist developed by the investigator. The nurse investigator assessed these complications while the patient's care was being provided and verified them by the intensivist during the patient's stay in the ICU. This checklist consists of “Yes” or “No” options, and the investigator observed the patient for the presence of complications. The “Yes” option was scored 1, and the “No” with 0.

Estimation of Sample Size

The sample size was calculated to determine a significant effect of nurse-led interventions on critical care pain and ICU-related complications, which was the primary objective of the study.

The sample size for the study was estimated for an independent t-test using statistical software G* Power (power = 85%, α = 0.05, d = 0.8).

Z1−0.05/2 = 1.96,

Z1−0.15 (for power = 85%) = 1.04

The anticipated effect size was 0.8. In order to detect the anticipated difference with 85% power and a 5% level of significance, 30 subjects were included in each group. Therefore, the total sample size was 60 patients admitted to the ICU.

Data Collection Tools

In this study, the instruments used for data collection were a demographic proforma, a clinical proforma, a critical CPOT, and an observational checklist to assess the ICU complications.

Demographic Proforma

In the present study, age, gender, and type of family were considered, which were collected from patients by the interview method.

Clinical Variables

The reasons for ICU admission, the presence of comorbidities, and basal metabolic index (BMI) were recorded.

Clinical variables, CPOT, and ICU-related complications were assessed and documented by the nurse investigator.

Content Validity and Reliability of Tools

The data collection instrument was validated by seven specialists in the field of medicine and nursing. The critical CPOT demonstrated a high level of reliability, with a Spearman correlation coefficient (r value) of 0.88 and an obtained reliability score of 0.86, which was calculated by the inter-rater method. Similarly, the ICU-related complication checklist showed good reliability, with an obtained reliability score of 0.86 as measured by Cronbach's Alpha.

Data Collection Procedure

Based on the inclusion criteria, patients were selected from the ICU by using a purposive sampling technique. Written informed consent was obtained from participants, and a participation information sheet was given before data collection. A pretest was performed before providing interventions with routine treatment in the study groups. The intervention group received nurse-led interventions, and the control group had no specific intervention; they followed routine treatment. The posttest was conducted after 1 week of intervention.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics. Frequency and percentage were used for baseline data. The paired t-test was used to compare the outcomes within the study groups. The independent t-test was used to compare the outcomes between the groups, and the Chi-square test was used to find the association between the clinical outcomes and demographic characteristics. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

Results

Demographic Characteristics of Patients

The demographic characteristics of patients in the intervention group showed that 53.3% of participants were between 60 and 69 years, an equal percentage (50.0%) were males and females, and 73.3% belonged to nuclear families. In the control group, 40.0% of the participants were aged between 50 and 59 years, 60.0% were male, and 80.0% were from nuclear families.

The Chi-square test was used to test the homogeneity of demographic characteristics across groups. The p-values were > 0.05; hence, the age groups, gender, and type of family were homogenous (Table 1).

Association between Critical Care Pain and Selected Demographic Characteristics

There was an association between the scores of critical care pain and the selected demographic variables, such as age (p = 0.01) and type of family (p = 0.03). There was also a significant association between the scores of ICU-related complications and age (p = 0.04). Similarly, an association was found between the critical care pain and the BMI (p = 0.01).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that, in critically ill patients, nurse-led interventions consisting of supportive communication and ROM exercises significantly reduced pain and ICU-related complications compared with those receiving routine care.

Advancing age is a determinant of ICU outcomes, as it is often associated with multimorbidity, reduced physiological reserve, and polypharmacy, thereby increasing vulnerability to ICU-related complications.8 The observed association between the type of family and pain scores shows psychosocial and cultural influences on pain expression and coping, as family provides emotional support, patient advocacy, bedside presence, and communication of symptoms to healthcare providers.9 Similarly, a higher BMI may contribute to increased pain due to challenges in providing nursing care, and this finding is consistent with a previous study reporting that increased BMI is associated with a higher risk of mechanical ventilation and prolonged length of ICU stay, suggesting greater illness severity and increased exposure to invasive procedures that may intensify pain among critically ill patients.10

This study's strengths lie in its methodological precision and use of validated tools by the subject experts. The study employed validated and reliable tools, including the critical CPOT and the ICU complication checklist, to ensure the accuracy and credibility of the study outcomes. The study employed a quasi-experimental group design to assess the efficacy of the nurse-led interventions in the ICU by comparing outcomes between the intervention and control groups. The nurse-led intervention was evidence-based and integrated into the ICU routine, highlighting the vital contribution of nurses to patient-centered care. Additionally, ethical approval and CTRI registration strengthened the transparency and integrity of the study.

Despite these strengths, the study had several limitations. The relatively small sample size restricts generalizability to a broader population of ICU patients. The short duration of the intervention may have limited the ability to assess the significant impact of the intervention, its long-term benefits, readmission, and mortality rates. The use of a purposive sampling technique may have introduced selection bias, and the inclusion of only patients with pulmonary and cardiovascular diagnoses limits the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously and validated in future studies with larger, more diverse groups.

The findings of this study align with prior research emphasizing the benefits of nurse-led and multimodal interventions. Previous studies have reported that supportive touch and massage reduce pain, anxiety, and vital signs among ICU patients and that ROM exercises enhance circulation and prevent contractures.11–15 The present study strengthens this body of evidence by combining communication-based emotional support with physical mobilization strategies, demonstrating a synergistic effect.16–18

However, unlike larger randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this quasi-experimental design lacked randomization, which might have affected internal validity.19 Some earlier studies observed better outcomes of mobilization interventions on overall mobility, whereas the present study found minimal reduction in pain and complications, possibly due to a smaller sample size and the personal involvement of the nurse investigator in the patient care.14,20

The present study reinforces that nurse-led interventions are an effective, safe, and low-cost strategy to improve ICU patient outcomes. Supportive communication enhances patient trust, reduces anxiety, and can lower physiological stress responses, thereby alleviating pain. Range of motion exercises improve circulation, joint flexibility, and muscle tone while minimizing risks of complications.14,21–23

For intensivists and hospital administrators, these findings advocate for integrating structured nurse-led protocols into routine ICU care to ensure early mobilization and holistic comfort. For policymakers, the results suggest the value of developing nurse empowerment programs and continuous professional training to implement an evidence-based, patient-centered ICU care bundle.24

While the current study contributes valuable evidence, several areas require further exploration. The impact of nurse-led interventions on mechanically ventilated patients, post-surgical populations, and long-term ICU recovery remains to be determined. Future research should focus on conducting large-scale, multicenter RCTs with longer follow-up durations to validate these findings and assess the sustained benefits of such interventions. Studies could also explore the development of comprehensive ICU recovery bundles, combining nurse-led physical, psychological, and educational components to promote holistic rehabilitation. Additionally, integration of digital health monitoring or tele-ICU systems could further enhance the consistency of these interventions.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that the nurse-led interventions reduced critical care pain and ICU-related complications. While both groups showed improvements, the intervention group experienced a greater reduction in mean pain and complication scores. Future large-scale, multicenter studies are recommended to validate these findings and explore their generalizability to broader populations.
