Changes in office-hours doctor and nurse consultations: six-year register-based follow-up cohort study in the public primary health care of City of Vantaa, Finland
Authors: Katri Mustonen, Marko Raina, Timo Kauppila
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate how the number of face-to-face office-hours nurse consultations changed in response to the continuously decreasing number of consultations with general practitioners (GPs) in the fourth largest city in Finland, Vantaa we performed a retrospective register-based follow-up cohort study in public primary health care. The number of monthly consultations per practitioner in public primary health care were examined and counted from all recorded face-to-face office-hour consultations with GPs, practical nurses (PNs), registered nurses (RNs), and public health (PHNs) nurses of Vantaa’s public primary health care system between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2014. Over the follow-up period, the monthly mean number of GP consultations adjusted per practitioner decreased from 159 (mean; SD 29) in 2009 to 135 (16) in 2014 (p<0.001), and the number of RN consultations decreased from 34 (14) to 27 (7; p<0.001). There was no significant decrease in the practitioner-adjusted median monthly number of office-hour consultations with PN or PHN. The total number of monthly office-hour consultations per practitioner decreased from 40 (11) to 34 (6; p<0.001). In public office-hours PHC, face-to-face medical consultations have not automatically shifted from GPs to nurses. Surprisingly, recorded RN consultations may even decrease, and the overall productivity of PHC may worsen.

Introduction

It has been suggested that nurses could replace GPs in primary health care (PHC) [1,2], especially in cases where there is a shortage of General practitioners (GPs), to improve access to care [3,4]. A sufficient access to care is required in order to preserve or improve the health of population. However, services need to be relevant and effective as well, for the population to gain expected results for their seek for assistance [5]. Therefore, it is not surprising that the role of nurses in PHC is expanding throughout [6]. According to OECD statistics [7]. the number of doctors per 1000 population in Finland was in a slightly lower level compared to the EU average (Finland 2.7). Ratio of nurses to physicians in Finland were high, compared to general EU level: 4.3 to 2.5 [7]. Furthermore, shifting tasks from general practitioners to practice assistants and nurses, was a chosen strategy in Finnish PHC for decades [8]. Thus, in theory decreased work effort of GPs might increase workload of nurses. This replacement of GP work with that of practical nurses (PNs) and registered (RNs) nurses, has also been observed in the emergency department setting in Vantaa [9]. In the USA, replacing physicians' appointments to nurse practitioners and physician assistants in diabetic patients, led to cost savings, fewer emergency department visits and fewer hospitalizations, while maintaining the same quality of care [10]. In addition, effective long- term planning and role standardization would be required to successfully substitute GPs with nurse practitioners and physician assistants in primary health care [11].

In Finland, the number of GP consultations in public office-hour PHC, has been decreasing in recent years [12–14]. In the city of Vantaa, the point when the decrease in face-to-face office-hour GP consultations began was in 2008 [15]. The decreasing trend in GP consultations seemed to continue, despite attempts to reverse it through organisational reform, such as starting a new service provider model in 2011, which aimed to ensure sufficient nonurgent office-hour consultations for the most vulnerable populations [16]. This system is called the reduced list GP model [16]. To be eligible for enlistment in this system, the patient had to meet one of the following criteria: be 75 years or older, have had seven or more appointments to a GP during the previous two calendar years or have a certain chronic diagnosis that requires continuous management (e.g. diabetes or heart disease). Additionally, a patient could also be enlisted if a healthcare professional considered to require it, as in cases such as multi-morbidity, severe mental disorder of patient’s family member that could compromise objectivity or other special cases where ensuring continuity of care is essential. Patients aged 75 years or older were automatically listed at the onset of the system transition. Other patients were listed manually along the way. Patients not meeting the criteria above could not be assigned to any certain GP and were cared for by the GP-nurse teams with collective responsibility. At least one mechanism for the decrease in face-to-face office-hour GP consultations seems to be a decrease in the GP work force [16]. However, in Vantaa, the productivity of GPs also seemed to decrease when measured by a simple but crude metric, such as visits per GP [15,17,18].

In office-hours public PHC in Finland, nurses of various categories work with and alongside GPs: PNs, RNs and public health nurses (PHNs). PNs are trained in institutions offering upper vocational education. Their programs last two to three years and are worth 180 European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System (ECTS) course credits. RNs receive their education at a University of Applied Sciences on programmes that last three and a half years and comprise 210 ECTS course credits. PHNs undergo longer programs of four years at a University of Applied Sciences and accrue 240 ECTS course credits. In PHC, PNs and RNs often treat sick patients in PHC settings, whereas PHNs primarily work in preventive health care, such as in maternity and child health clinics and schools.

The expansion of nursing roles during office hours has been suggested to reduce the number of GP visits [19]. For example, in a Finnish study comparing appointments where nurses and GPs prescribed similar medications, patients who saw a nurse prescriber had fewer readmissions than those who saw a GP did [20]. Thus, it was reasonable to expect that a reduction in provided GP work would result in an increase in nurse consultations. Concerning the healthcare provision for ageing population it appears, that substituting physicians with nurses can achieve comparable patient and care process outcomes, but there is insufficient evidence to make conclusions about resource use and costs [21]. Older patients appear to benefit continuity in care in GP consultations, and simultaneously the continuity improves productivity [22]. However, a consistent way to assess the quality-of-care in substituting GPs with nurses is lacking [23].

The monthly number of patients seen by a GP decreased in office-hour practices of Vantaa during and after 2008 [15]. To examine how formerly described task shifting from general practitioners to practice assistants and nurses in Finnish primary health care [8] affected on workload of various types of nurses’ numbers of visits to them were studied. Thus, the aim of this study was to examine whether there were any reciprocal changes in the productivity of nurses by counting the number of monthly patients’ face-to-face consultations [17,18]. with PNs, RNs and PHNs in public office-hours PHCs.

Materials and methods

Setting

This retrospective register-based follow-up cohort study was conducted in the public PHC of the City of Vantaa, Finland. Vantaa is the fourth largest city in Finland, with a population of approximately 220 000 inhabitants. Demographics of the population of Vantaa is extensively described in official statistics of Vantaa city [24], and the demographics of those who use public PHC in Vantaa were profoundly reviewed in a recent study [25]. Data to the demographics of employees was not available. In Finland, health centres that offer public office-hours PHC are funded and maintained by municipalities via taxes. People can also use the private sector’s health care services, the costs of which they cover either themselves or via voluntary insurance, or the costs are paid by their employer. There was no access to private care data.

In this study, monthly GPs’, PNs’, RNs’ and PHNs’ office-hours face-to-face patient consultations adjusted to the number of practitioners in all of Vantaa’s public PHC centres were researched. The data were gathered from 1 January 2009 to 31 December 2014. We chose this period because at the beginning of 2009, every unit in public PHC was computerized and used an electronic health care system. The study was set to end in 2014 because specialized health care then overtook all emergency activities from PHC in Vantaa and thereby also significantly altered office-hour activities. Finland’s healthcare system is multifaceted. The health care system is mainly publicly funded, and the public sector is divided into PHC and specialized health care. In addition to public health care, the private sector and occupational health care sector are strongly funded by the private sector.

Data

Data on the size of the population and the number of official practical and registered nurses employed by the City of Vantaa were provided by the Vantaa City Administration. Data on the consultations were obtained from Vantaa’s electronic health record system (Graphic Finstar-patient chart system, GFS, Logica Ltd, Helsinki, Finland).

Outcome

Our primary outcome measures were the monthly number of recorded GP, PN, RN and PHN consultations per practitioner in public office-hours PHC between 2009 and 2014. As a secondary outcome we counted total number of recorded monthly office-hours consultations to all various practitioners (e.g. combined) adjusted per a practitioner or per 1000 inhabitants.

Ethical aspects

The register keepers, the health authorities of Vantaa and the scientific ethical board of Vantaa (TUTKE), granted permission (VD/8059/13.00.00/2016) to carry out the study. This study was conducted directly via the patient register, and no individual patients or nurses were identified. In accordance with the Finnish law on register studies (https://rekisteritutkimus.wordpress.com/luvat-ja-tietosuoja/), our study participants were not required to sign a statement of informed consent because the study was retrospective, based on patient charts, and the investigators did not contact the patients.

Statistical analysis

The number of nurse consultations was analysed via repeated measures analysis (RM-ANOVA), followed by the Bonferroni method as a post hoc test. This test was chosen because of the nature of the data; the number of consultations varied between different months, with summer months being systematically less busy than winter months [9]. Therefore, comparisons between years had to be performed by comparing the respective months. The results are expressed as the means and standard deviations (SDs). The rate of change, e.g. the trends in the numbers of practical and registered nurse consultations, was analysed via regression analysis (GLM procedure of SigmaPlot 10.0 Statistical Software, Systat Software Inc., Richmond, CA, USA). The GLM enabled us to calculate the mean slope (cofactor a) of the development of the monthly number of office-hour consultations with GPs per practitioner, along with its standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons were subsequently performed via the t test to detect whether the slope increased or decreased statistically significantly [9,15]. This allowed us to perform trend analysis to determine the direction in which the number of monthly visits developed. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

During the six-year follow-up period, the number of patients’ monthly office-hours face-to-face consultations to RNs decreased in public PHC simultaneously with the respective consultations of GPs. Over the same time frame, there was no change in consultations to PNs or PHNs. The total productivity of the present PHC system also decreased when all monthly office-hour consultations were analysed in total. Owing to an increase in the population, the present productivity was insufficient to maintain the former supply of office-hours public face-to-face PHC services.

This study had several strengths. The data were comprehensive, encompassing documentation of all patients’ office-hours face-to-face consultations to GPs, PNs, RNs and PHNs in the public PHC of the City of Vantaa, Finland. The number of consultations was substantial, and the follow-up period was long enough to ensure reliable results regarding the observed changes. Although the data were over 10 years old, replacing GPs’ work with nurses in PHC remains a topical theme that deserves attention and evokes discussion [6].

The main limitation of the study was the poor recoding of visits by RNs and PNs. The low recording rates suggest that they did not record their work as they were advised. Owing to the retrospective model of our study, the data that were entered into the database were not collected for research; therefore, we cannot guarantee that all the nurse consultations were systematically registered in accordance with the given instructions. Thus, some data that could impact the outcome may be missing. Nevertheless, we were able to observe increased use of RNs and PNs in the PHC emergency department of Vantaa by using the same codes and parameters [9], and the observed decreases in the present study were systematic and clear. Therefore, the observed changes are most likely true. Furthermore, the number of patients met by a practitioner is only a crude measure of productivity [17,18]. For example, quality aspects of care are missing. There was no information on possible changes in patient characteristics or alterations in the management of consultations. Moreover, no data were available concerning the quality of the consultations or whether the treatment provided by the different types of nurses varied. It is not known whether patient treatment shifted to the private sector. As the personal patient data were not available, the continuity of care cannot be evaluated.

The findings of the study raise concerns, as the decrease in the number of GP consultations does not appear to have shifted the workload to nurses’ office-hour consultations. If the task shifting from general practitioners to practice assistants and nurses in PHC had occurred in Finland at the time of this study [8] an increase in visits to office-hours nurses would have been a logical consequence. In contrast, face-to-face PN and RN consultations in the PHC emergency department have appeared to increase [9]. This happened although total number of employees increased during the follow-up. No increase in telephone or other electronic consultations was observed during our study period [16]. Interestingly, the observed shifts in RN consultations appeared simultaneously with the organisational reform of public PHC, e.g. starting a new service provider model in 2011 that attempted to ensure sufficient nonurgent office-hour consultations for the most vulnerable populations, the so-called restricted list GP model [16]. Considering both the present and former [9,17] studies, this model did not achieve its goals but instead led to further deterioration in PHC services. Nevertheless, the formerly observed decrease in the supply of GPs [16] was not the only reason for decreased face-to-face consultations to GPs; rather, a decrease in public PHC productivity in terms of decreased numbers of patients seen by a GP was also present.

It remains unclear why office-hours patients were not shifted to RNs or PNs. These groups of employees are often suggested as replacements for the missing GP work force [1–4]. PHNs were not intended to replace GPs, as they work in preventive health care, such as in maternity and child health clinics and schools. It is possible that planning how the shift of patients to nurses was supposed to be performed in the restricted list GP model was insufficient. It cannot be excluded that nurses react to a reduction in GP services by actively reducing their own capacity to provide patient consultations, as increased workload has been described as the most important factor reducing the quality of professional life of nurses [1]. Nevertheless, this may have resulted in a situation in which a change in PHC to a restricted list GP model led to administrative harm, e.g. the adverse consequences of administrative decisions within health care that impact work structure, processes, and programs [26]. Thus, changes eventually led to decreased productivity.

As Finnish public office-hours PHC focus strongly on the treatment of aged patients [27], the present study on the effects of workload shifts should be performed in different types of PHCs. Further studies are needed to determine how shifting tasks to nurses could be performed so, that it is a safe and cost-beneficial option in the care of patients.

Conclusions

When investigating face-to-face office-hour consultations at PCH during the six-year follow-up period, the number of monthly GP consultations decreased as well as the number of monthly RH consultations. There was no significant change in monthly office-hour consultations with PN or PHN. Therefore, the proportion of face-to-face office-hour consultations shifted to PNs, as PHNs mostly work in preventive health care units. The shift from GPs to RNs did not take place, and overall productivity may have worsened during the investigation period.
