Recurrent embolic cerebellar infarction due to bow hunter’s syndrome of the non-dominant vertebral artery
Authors: Hakim Baazaoui, Lukas Bastian Otto, Ferdinand Otto, Miklos Krepuska, Menno Germans, Susanne Wegener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe describe a rare presentation of bow hunter’s syndrome (BHS) involving the non-dominant vertebral artery (VA), leading to recurrent embolic cerebellar infarction. A male in his mid-70s presented with sudden-onset immobilising vertigo. MRI revealed acute cerebellar ischaemia with occlusion of the left superior cerebellar artery. Dynamic duplex ultrasonography and angiography revealed an occlusion of the left VA in the V2 segment during head rotation to the left due to an osteophyte protruding from the C3/C4 vertebrae. The patient was diagnosed with embolic strokes due to compression of the left, non-dominant VA. He was treated with osteophyte resection and anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. BHS is the medical syndrome associated with the compression of the VA induced by movement or rotation of the head, resulting in symptoms of vertebrobasilar insufficiency or stroke. As a rare cause of stroke, it can be diagnosed through dynamic duplex ultrasound and angiography with head rotation. This case is an important addition to the literature because it highlights three key learning points: (1) BHS is a frequently underdiagnosed and underreported cause of transient ischaemic attack, stroke, disability and death; (2) early diagnosis and intervention can prevent devastating outcomes; (3) BHS can cause embolic stroke even from the non-dominant VA.

Background

Bow hunter’s syndrome (BHS) describes the occlusion of a vertebral artery (VA) during rotation of the head, usually caused by entrapment stemming from neighbouring anatomical structures such as vertebral osteophytes.1 It is a rare condition with fewer than 200 cases reported, mainly affecting adults between the fifth and seventh decades of life.2 Symptoms stem from vertebrobasilar insufficiency and may include ataxia, vertigo, nausea, nystagmus, dysphagia and dysarthria, typically resolving after returning to anatomical head position. Ischaemic stroke in the posterior circulation may occur due to embolisms from the transiently occluded VA. The diagnosis can be established through computed tomographic imaging, functional duplex ultrasonography and dynamic digital subtraction angiography (DSA).3 Treatment is either performed conservatively with antiplatelet medication or invasively through surgical decompression or neurointerventional treatment (eg, stenting or occlusion) of the vessel.2

Case presentation

A male in his mid-70s presented to our neurological emergency department with sudden-onset positional vertigo and ataxia of the upper and lower limbs. The patient did not recall any specific trigger or movement preceding the symptoms. Risk factors included hypertension, dyslipidaemia, coronary heart disease and former smoking with 30 pack years. On admission, the neurological examination was notable for second degree spontaneous nystagmus to the left and dysmetria in the finger-to-nose test and heel-knee test on both sides (right>left), corresponding to 2 points on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). MRI revealed acute infarction in the territory of the left superior cerebellar artery and two right cerebellar ischaemic lesions.

Two weeks prior to the current admission, the patient had suffered an ischaemic stroke due to occlusion of the left posterior inferior cerebellar artery. He had reported no specific preceding head movement or other trigger, was discharged on double antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel for treatment of minor stroke and was scheduled for a complete outpatient diagnostic workup.4 5

Investigations

Following the second cerebellar stroke, CT angiography (CTA) of the head and neck demonstrated a high-grade focal stenosis of the left VA in the V2 segment, in close anatomical proximity to an osteophyte protruding from the C3/C4 vertebrae. Duplex ultrasonography also showed focal stenosis of the left, non-dominant VA in the same location. In the dynamic exam, that is, Doppler-based flow measurement performed with head-turning of the patient, head rotation to the left resulted in a reversal of blood flow in the left VA distal to the stenosis, indicating a functional external compression of the artery by the osteophyte. Of note, mere head rotation did not evoke any symptoms as vertebrobasilar insufficiency was avoided by sufficient blood flow through the unaffected, dominant right VA. Treatment

Due to the presumed thromboembolic aetiology of the cerebellar stroke, the patient received therapeutic heparinisation during the acute hospitalisation phase. To prevent further cerebral ischaemic events, an anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) with a ventral plate at level C3/C4 with resection of the compressing osteophyte was performed (figure 3). Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound showed regular flow in the left VA. After the intervention, anticoagulation therapy was de-escalated to acetylsalicylic acid and vascular risk factors were optimised.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up CTA showed good positioning of the inserted material and a normalisation of the course of the left VA without external compression. The patient was discharged on the third postoperative day for outpatient rehabilitation with mild ataxia of the right leg in heel-knee test and no residual vertigo (NIHSS 1 point). On follow-up 4 months after surgery, the patient reported no further vertebrobasilar symptoms and ataxia had fully resolved, corresponding to an NIHSS score of 0 points. Duplex ultrasonography showed normal blood flow of the formerly affected VA without stenosis or occlusion during head rotation.

From the patient’s perspective, the disease course was marked by three severe episodes of vertigo with intense nausea and transient inability to walk, each resolving completely, followed by surgical treatment. Postoperatively, the patient reported only temporary mild dysphagia and neck discomfort and subjectively made a full recovery without functional limitations. He expressed overall high satisfaction, particularly regarding the absence of further strokes.

Discussion

BHS is a rare condition with symptoms of vertebrobasilar insufficiency due to compression of the VA induced by rotation or extension of the neck and was first described in 1978 in a 39-year-old patient who developed symptoms while practising archery, hence the eponymous name of the condition.1 Adjacent culprit structures are typically vertebral osteophytes, ligaments or herniated discs compressing the VA during head rotation and/or extension.6 Stasis and endothelial damage can lead to thrombus formation and distal embolisation with repeated ischaemic posterior circulation strokes.7 BHS remains a largely underdiagnosed and underreported clinical syndrome that can lead to transient ischaemic attack, stroke, disability and even death. Awareness of this condition is essential for early recognition and intervention. Diagnosing BHS can be challenging due to its variable presentation and overlap with other causes of cerebrovascular events.

In the patient presented here, the left VA at level C3/C4 was compressed by an osteophyte. History, which is typically revealing in BHS with vertigo and ischaemic brainstem and cerebellar symptoms on head rotation, was inconclusive in our case. Since the non-dominant VA was affected, symptoms were not observed during rotation but developed due to embolic stroke. Our diagnostic process included dynamic duplex ultrasound with head rotation, MRI/MR angiography and DSA to confirm VA compression and rule out alternative aetiologies. Workup revealed transient occlusion of the vessel during rotation of the head to the left. While conservative management, including antithrombotic therapy, cervical collars and avoidance of provocative head movements, may be considered in selected cases, recurrent ischaemic events often favour surgical treatment.8 Surgical management generally shows favourable outcomes and ACDF, as performed in our case, can provide both decompression of the VA and stabilisation of the affected segment of the cervical spine.
