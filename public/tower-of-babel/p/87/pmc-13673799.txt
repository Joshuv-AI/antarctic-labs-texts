Beyond chewing: a three-month prospective evaluation of swallowing, nutrition, and muscle strength in older adults using removable dentures
Authors: Ikbal Leblebicioglu Kurtulus, Sibel Akin, Kerem Kilic, Nazli Ceylan Kabasakal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEdentulism in older adults can impair chewing and swallowing functions, potentially leading to malnutrition and a decline in muscle strength. The role of various removable prosthesis types in reducing these effects remains under-researched.PurposeTo evaluate the effects of various removable prosthesis types on nutritional status, self-reported dysphagia risk, and handgrip strength (HGS) in elderly patients with complete or partial edentulism.Materials and methodsThis prospective study included 27 patients aged 60 or older, categorized into Kennedy Class I, Kennedy Class II, and complete denture groups. Before and three months after prosthesis delivery, the following parameters were assessed: Body Mass Index (BMI), Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form (MNA-SF), Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10), and HGS via dynamometer.ResultsNo significant difference was found in age distribution among the groups (p = 0.272). BMI was significantly higher in the Kennedy Class I compared to the complete denture group (p = 0.011). No significant changes were observed in MNA-SF or HGS scores between groups or over time (p > 0.05). EAT-10 scores were significantly reduced in the Kennedy Class I and II groups following prosthesis delivery (Wilcoxon signed-rank test; p = 0.011 and p = 0.017, respectively), indicating improved self-reported swallowing function. A statistically significant inverse correlation was observed between baseline MNA-SF and EAT-10 scores in Kennedy Class I group (Spearmann’s rho = − 0.808; p = 0.008), and a statistically significant positive correlation was observed between baseline handgrip strength and nutritional status scores in the Kennedy Class II group (Spearman’s rho = 0.840; p = 0.009).ConclusionRemovable partial dentures significantly improved self-reported swallowing function in older adults with partial edentulism. No significant changes were observed in nutritional status, BMI, or handgrip strength over the three-month follow-up.Clinical implicationsProsthetic rehabilitation in elderly patients should be integrated with nutritional and functional screening to optimize both oral and systemic health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09214-5.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, edentulism is recognized as a physical condition with substantial impacts on oral and systemic health, quality of life, nutritional adequacy, and satisfaction with eating [1, 2]. Complete tooth loss compromises masticatory function and influences dietary choices and preferences, even among individuals who have been rehabilitated with prosthetic appliances [3, 4]. The American Medical Association’s Council on Food and Nutrition defines nutrition as ‘the science of food, the actions and interactions of nutrients and other substances, and the balance and processes involved in health and disease [5].

Malnutrition can compromise health and reduce the capacity for independent living, potentially leading to complications such as poor general health, polypharmacy, and decreased social interaction [6, 7]. Malnutrition can adversely affect the quality of life in elderly individuals [7]. Tooth loss is prevalent among older adults and significantly affects quality of life, disability, and mortality rates. The prevalence of severe tooth loss notably increases around the seventh decade of life [8]. In 2010, it was reported that 158 million people worldwide were edentulous [8]. Tooth loss and the number of occluding tooth pairs have a significant impact on oral health-related quality of life [9]. Furthermore, tooth loss is a risk factor for the onset of disability and mortality in older adults [10]. Some evidence suggests that compromised oral health status, particularly edentulism, may affect nutritional intake [10, 11]. The loss of all teeth, even with the use of dentures, reduces masticatory efficiency and influences food taste, preferences, and consumption habits [2, 11–15].

Edentulism may also be linked to inadequate intake of various nutrients, which can hinder edentulous individuals from meeting current nutritional recommendations [16, 17]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that edentulous older adults consume fewer fruits and vegetables compared to dentate individuals; they intake lower amounts of dietary fiber, carotene, calcium, and protein, while consuming higher levels of cholesterol and saturated fats [14, 18, 19]. Furthermore, these nutritional changes are believed to increase the risk of significant weight loss and may potentially elevate the risk of certain systemic diseases, such as cancer and cardiovascular disease [20, 21]. Dysphagia is characterized by difficulty or discomfort in the passage of chewed food from the oral cavity to the esophagus [22]. Due to the decline in swallowing function associated with aging, dysphagia is common among older adults [23–25]. Dysphagia is significantly correlated with elevated morbidity and mortality rates.

Moreover, individuals afflicted with dysphagia demonstrate a higher propensity for psychological conditions such as anxiety and depression [22]. The development of dysphagia leads to various health complications, including dehydration, malnutrition, and aspiration pneumonia [24]. Therefore, early detection and prevention of declining swallowing function are crucial for extending healthy life expectancy in the current aging population [25]. Moreover, elderly patients frequently report a decline in masticatory function due to tooth loss [26]. Several studies in the literature have examined the association between the number of remaining teeth in the oral cavity and the incidence or severity of dysphagia [22, 26–29]. A study examining the interrelationships among tooth count, nutritional status, and dysphagia has also been reported in the literature [30].

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of removable partial dentures (Kennedy Class I and II) and conventional complete dentures on nutritional status, self-reported swallowing function, handgrip strength, and body mass index (BMI) in adults aged 60 years and older, before and three months after prosthesis delivery.

The following null hypotheses were tested:

Prosthesis type does not significantly affect MNA-SF, EAT-10 (self-reported dysphagia risk), handgrip strength (HGS), or BMI scores over a three-month follow-up period. There are no significant correlations among MNA-SF, EAT-10, HGS, and BMI scores within each prosthesis group.

Materials and methods

Population

This study enrolled patients aged 60 years and older who presented with partial or complete edentulism to the Department of Prosthodontics at Erciyes University Faculty of Dentistry. Ethical approval was obtained from the Erciyes University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no: 2021/471), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: age 60 years or older ; ability to perform daily activities independently (determined by review of medical records and confirmed by the consulting geriatrician); having edentulous areas corresponding to the specified Kennedy classifications (confirmed by clinical and radiological examination by the prosthodontist); absence of gastrointestinal disorders causing malnutrition, depression, or other psychiatric illnesses (determined by review of medical records and confirmed by the consulting geriatrician); absence of any condition impairing comprehension or response to the questionnaire ; cooperativeness (clinically assessed during the initial interview); and no history of smoking (confirmed by self-report during medical history intake).

Study groups and sample

The initial cohort comprised 30 participants. Three individuals were excluded due to non-attendance at follow-up appointments, resulting in a final sample of 27 participants. Participants were stratified into three groups according to their prosthetic treatment modality: Group 1 (Conventional Complete Dentures, n = 10) consisted of patients receiving conventional complete dentures for both the maxilla and mandible; Group 2 (Kennedy Class I, n = 9) consisted of patients fitted with Kennedy Class I removable partial dentures in both jaws; and Group 3 (Kennedy Class II, n = 8) comprised patients rehabilitated with Kennedy Class II removable partial dentures for both the maxilla and mandible.

Sample size was estimated based on a preliminary power analysis informed by published data on EAT-10 score changes following prosthetic rehabilitation in older adults [26]. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 8–9 participants per group. The final group sizes in the present study (n = 8–10 per group) are consistent with this estimate. We acknowledge that this sample size may limit the statistical power to detect small-to-medium changes in secondary outcomes such as MNA-SF, HGS, and BMI, and this is discussed as a study limitation.

Follow-up and data collection

Following medical, clinical, and radiological examinations, each patient was informed about the most suitable prosthesis type and referred to the Departments of Geriatrics and Internal Medicine at .Erciyes University for further evaluation. Baseline assessments were conducted prior to prosthesis delivery, and all assessments were repeated at the three-month follow-up. The prostheses were fabricated and delivered by skilled prosthodontist (NCK) between the two assessment time points.

To minimize measurement variability, all objective clinical measurements — including anthropometric measurements for BMI calculation and handgrip strength assessment via dynamometer — were performed by a single experienced prosthodontist (I.L.K.). The MNA-SF and EAT-10 questionnaires were self-administered by participants under standardized supervision, without verbal prompting, to preserve the self-report nature of these instruments. Geriatric evaluation, including medical history review and comorbidity assessment, was conducted by the specialist in geriatric medicine (S.A.). As all questionnaire-based outcomes were patient-reported and objective measurements were obtained by a single operator, formal inter-rater reliability testing was not applicable in this study.

Body Mass Index (BMI)

Participants’ height and body weight were measured using a standard hospital scale and stadiometer. During measurements, participants wore light clothing only, with shoes, jackets, hats, and heavy accessories removed [31]. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). In this study, the international BMI classification established by the World Health Organization (WHO) was used. Accordingly, participants were categorized into three groups: underweight (BMI < 18.5), Normal weight (BMI ≥ 18.5 and < 25), and Overweight (BMI ≥ 25) [32].

Mini Nutritional Assessment- Short Form (MNA-SF)

Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA-SF) [33]. The six items evaluate: changes in appetite, weight loss within the past three months, mobility, psychological stress or acute illness experienced in the last three months, presence of neuropsychological problems, and body mass index (BMI). When used as a standalone instrument, the MNA-SF classifies individuals as well-nourished (scores 12–14), at risk of malnutrition (scores 8–11), or malnourished (scores less than 8) [33].

Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10)

Self-reported swallowing function was assessed using the Eating Assessment Tool-10 (EAT-10), a validated, rapid, and self-administered screening instrument designed to identify individuals at risk for swallowing difficulties [34]. The EAT-10 consists of 10 items, each scored on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (no impairment) to 4 (severe impairment), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 40. A total score of ≥ 3 is considered indicative of self-reported dysphagia risk and warrants further clinical evaluation [34]. It is important to note that the EAT-10 is a screening tool based on patient self-assessment and does not constitute a clinical diagnosis of dysphagia; accordingly, outcomes derived from this instrument are reported as “self-reported dysphagia risk” throughout this study.

Handgrip Strength (HGS)

Handgrip strength (HGS) was measured using a dynamometer (Baseline Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer, Model 12–024). Participants were instructed to hold the dynamometer with their dominant hand, with the elbow flexed at 90 degrees and the forearm positioned parallel to the ground. The HGS score was defined as the highest value recorded from three attempts. HGS scores below 20 kg for women and 30 kg for men were considered indicative of an increased risk of malnutrition [35].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 23. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons of categorical variables across groups employed Fisher’s Exact Test with Monte Carlo simulation to adjust for small sample sizes. For normally distributed continuous variables, between-group differences were analyzed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with subsequent pairwise comparisons performed using Dunn’s post hoc test. Within-group comparisons between baseline and three-month follow-up measurements utilized paired sample t-tests for parametric data and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-parametric data. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical data, and as means ± standard deviations or medians with ranges for continuous data. A two-tailed significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

Results

The distribution of participants’ gender across the groups did not show a statistically significant difference (Fisher’s Exact Test with Monte Carlo simulation; p = 0.794; Table 1). There was no statistically significant difference in median age values among the groups (one-way ANOVA; F = 1.428; p = 0.272; Table 1). However, the comparison of median Body Mass Index (BMI) values revealed a statistically significant difference between groups (Kruskal-Wallis H = 9.034; p = 0.011; Table 1). The median BMI in the Kennedy Class I group was 30.85 (range: 22.05–32.8), compared to 27.0 (range: 25.3–30.2) in the Kennedy Class II group, and 24.31 (range: 21.09–31.25) in the conventional complete denture group. Post-hoc analysis indicated that the BMI in the Kennedy Class I group was significantly higher than that in the conventional complete denture group.

No statistically significant difference was observed in baseline median self-reported dysphagia risk (EAT-10) scores among the groups (Kruskal-Wallis H = 4.161; p = 0.125; Table 4). However, a statistically significant difference was found in median self-reported dysphagia risk scores at the three-month follow-up between the groups (Kruskal-Wallis H = 6.907; p = 0.032; Table 4). Median scores at three months were 1 for the Kennedy Class I group, 0 for the Kennedy Class II group, and 5 for the conventional complete denture group. Pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences between individual groups. Within-group analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in median self-reported dysphagia risk scores from baseline to three months in the Kennedy Class I group (Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Z = − 2.536; p = 0.011), with median scores decreasing from 7 at baseline to 1 at three months. Similarly, the Kennedy Class II group showed a statistically significant decrease in median self-reported dysphagia risk scores (Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Z = − 2.395; p = 0.017), from 9 at baseline to 0 at three months. No statistically significant difference was observed in the conventional complete denture group between baseline and three months (Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Z = − 0.411; p = 0.681; Table 4). It is noteworthy that at baseline, all three groups had median EAT-10 scores at or above the self-reported dysphagia risk threshold of ≥ 3. At the three-month follow-up, the Kennedy Class I (median = 1) and Kennedy Class II (median = 0) groups had fallen below this threshold, whereas the conventional complete denture group remained above it (median = 5), suggesting a clinically meaningful differential recovery of swallowing function between partial and complete denture wearers.

Discussion

This prospective study evaluated the effects of three types of removable prostheses — conventional complete dentures, Kennedy Class I removable partial dentures, and Kennedy Class II removable partial dentures — on self-reported swallowing function, nutritional status, handgrip strength, and body mass index in adults aged 60 years and older over a three-month follow-up period. The principal finding of this study was that removable partial dentures were associated with a statistically significant reduction in self-reported dysphagia risk scores in the Kennedy Class I and Kennedy Class II groups, whereas no significant change was observed in the conventional complete denture group. Notably, at three months, only the conventional complete denture group remained above the EAT-10 dysphagia risk threshold of ≥ 3. No statistically significant changes were observed in nutritional status (MNA-SF), body mass index, or handgrip strength in any group over the follow-up period. A significant inverse correlation between nutritional status and self-reported dysphagia risk was identified in the Kennedy Class I group, and a significant positive correlation between handgrip strength and nutritional status was found in the Kennedy Class II group.

A key strength of this study is its prospective design with standardized baseline and follow-up assessments conducted by a single clinician, minimizing measurement variability. The simultaneous evaluation of swallowing function, nutritional status, and muscle strength using validated instruments represents a multidimensional approach that is relatively novel in the prosthodontic literature. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the absence of an untreated control group — which was not feasible on ethical grounds — means it is not possible to determine whether improvements in EAT-10 scores reflect a prosthesis-type-specific effect or a general effect of receiving any prosthesis. Second, the three-month follow-up period, while sufficient to detect changes in self-reported swallowing function, may be too short to capture meaningful changes in nutritional status, BMI, or muscle strength, which require longer dietary adaptation periods; follow-up periods of 6–12 months are recommended in future studies. Third, the relatively high baseline MNA-SF and HGS values across all groups suggest that participants were generally well-nourished at the outset, which may have created a ceiling effect limiting detectable improvements in these outcomes. Fourth, significant baseline BMI differences between groups (p = 0.011) may have influenced the pattern of results. Fifth, important covariates including socioeconomic status, dietary habits, physical activity level, and polypharmacy were not collected. Sixth, the absence of objective functional measures — such as masticatory performance tests, denture retention and stability scores, tongue pressure assessment, or videofluoroscopic swallowing evaluation — limits interpretation of the mechanisms underlying the observed swallowing improvements. Finally, the exclusion of fixed and implant-supported prosthesis users limits the generalizability of findings across prosthesis types.

Advancing age is associated with an increased risk of nutritional deterioration in older adults. Among the available nutritional indicators, BMI is one of the most commonly employed and serves as a significant outcome measure reflecting overall nutritional status. Evidence suggests that low BMI or underweight status adversely impacts both physical and mental well-being, and has been linked to increased morbidity and mortality risks [35]. In the present study, mean BMI values across all groups exceeded 25, indicating that participants were on average in the overweight category at baseline. It is also worth considering that the relatively high BMI values observed across all groups may be associated with increased tongue volume and parapharyngeal soft tissue bulk, which could potentially influence prosthesis stability and oral transit during swallowing. Although tongue volume was not directly assessed in this study, future research should consider including tongue function assessments — such as the Iowa Oral Performance Instrument — to clarify the potential contribution of body habitus to prosthesis performance and swallowing outcomes.

In addition to BMI, nutritional status is frequently assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) score and biochemical analyses [3, 36–38]. In this study, blood analyses were not performed to avoid invasive procedures; moreover, such analyses were deemed unsuitable due to potential confounding effects from inflammation and medication use. Additionally, it has been reported that MNA scores alone provide a sufficient assessment of nutritional status in elderly patients [19, 39, 40].

At baseline, the MNA-SF test results indicated that patients across all groups were generally well-nourished, with no statistically significant differences observed between groups. Similarly, at the three-month follow-up, MNA-SF scores showed no statistically significant differences either between or within groups. Notably, the Kennedy Class II group exhibited a numerical decline in MNA-SF scores from 13.13 ± 1.36 to 11.88 ± 3.64 following prosthesis delivery; however, this reduction was not statistically significant. This decrease may be attributed to the unilateral edentulous condition in the Kennedy Class II group, which could have caused more difficulty adapting to the prosthesis compared to the Kennedy Class I and total denture groups.

Banerjee et al. reported in a study involving 200 conventional complete denture wearers aged 60 and above that the use of traditional dentures may increase the risk of malnutrition in elderly individuals due to inadequate mastication and food consumption [41]. In our study, neither the patients using conventional complete dentures nor those rehabilitated with Kennedy Class I and II removable partial dentures were found to be at risk of malnutrition. This may be attributed to the relatively high baseline BMI values observed across all groups.

The number of studies investigating the impact of different types of prosthetic restorations on malnutrition remains limited in the literature.

A study conducted by Goel et al. demonstrated that prosthetic rehabilitation has positive effect on the nutritional status of older individuals, as evidenced by improvements in biochemical markers associated with malnutrition [3]. They specifically reported that the use of complete dentures resulted in significant improvements in diet quality and biochemical parameters [3]. These findings underscore the critical importance of appropriate prosthetic treatment for nutritional status and overall health as the level of edentulism increases. Therefore, nutritional assessment and counseling should be an integral part of geriatric-centered treatment planning during the prosthetic rehabilitation process [41].

Adequate masticatory function positively influences handgrip strength (HGS), whereas impaired mastication, particularly in individuals with fewer teeth, has a negative impact on nutrient intake. This is further supported by studies demonstrating that individuals using properly adjusted prostheses exhibit better handgrip strength compared to those with ill-fitting prostheses. Additionally, in patients with oral health problems, the development of new morbidities during the follow-up period has been associated with a decline in muscle strength [42].

Lee et al. reported that tooth loss can lead to significant masticatory dysfunctions, resulting in chewing difficulties and inadequate nutrient intake [43]. They noted that this condition may lead to nutritional deficiencies, which in turn can cause a decrease in handgrip strength (HGS) [43]. In recent years, the relationship between oral health status and handgrip strength (HGS) has been examined, considering intrinsic factors such as age. Although oral health issues such as tooth loss and poor oral habits are associated with HGS, no study has definitively established a causal relationship between HGS and any specific factor [44–46].

At baseline, handgrip strength (HGS) measurements for all groups exceeded the malnutrition risk threshold (minimum value: 24.14 ± 6.82 kg; Table 2). Three months following prosthesis delivery, a slight numerical increase in HGS was observed only in the conventional complete denture group; however, no statistically significant differences were found between or within groups at either time point (p = 0.441, p = 0.755, p = 0.269, respectively; Table 2). Hamalainen et al. compared handgrip strength according to the number of teeth in a cohort of 193 participants aged 80 years [46]. In the same study, a positive correlation was observed between the number of remaining teeth and handgrip strength in males; however, this association was not evident in females [46]. Two other studies conducted on the elderly Japanese population did not find a significant association between tooth loss and handgrip strength [47, 48]. These results appear broadly consistent with our findings. Lee et al. investigated the relationship between several types of prostheses and handgrip strength [49]. According to their results, individuals using removable prostheses (partial or complete) exhibited lower handgrip strength compared to those using fixed prostheses or those without any prostheses [49]. This finding suggests that the use of removable prostheses may be indicative of a decline in overall physical function. However, no statistically significant difference was found between patients using removable partial dentures and those using complete dentures, which is consistent with the findings of our study.

According to the EAT-10 test results obtained at baseline, all three groups had median self-reported dysphagia risk scores at or above the threshold of ≥ 3, with no significant differences between groups (maximum median value 9.13 ± 2.7; Table 4). Although a numerical decrease in self-reported dysphagia risk scores was observed in all groups three months after prosthesis delivery, only the reductions in the Kennedy Class I and Kennedy Class II groups were statistically significant. While intergroup comparisons showed significant differences after three months, pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences between individual group pairs. Therefore, the first null hypothesis of the study was only partially rejected.

Son et al. conducted video fluoroscopic swallowing evaluations on 24 patients to examine the relationship between removable prostheses and swallowing function [26]. They concluded that removable prostheses may have adverse effects, particularly on oral transit time and swallowing efficiency [26]. In our study, however, patients in the Kennedy Class I and Class II groups demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in self-reported dysphagia risk scores three months after receiving their prostheses. This finding appears to contradict the results of Son et al. The discrepancy may be due to differences in study design: our study evaluated only patients using removable partial and complete dentures, whereas Son et al. included both fixed and removable prostheses and employed different swallowing assessment methods. Furthermore, Son et al.’s study was retrospective, assessing swallowing function based on patients’ existing prostheses, while in our study, baseline measurements were taken when patients were edentulous, and follow-up measurements were taken three months after prosthesis delivery. Improved mastication with the prosthesis may have contributed to enhanced self-reported swallowing function.

Aquino et al. reported a significant association between the reduced number of teeth, the use of removable dentures, and complaints of swallowing difficulty in elderly individuals [50]. Specifically, individuals with 10–19 natural teeth have been reported to have a 52% higher risk of self-reported dysphagia compared to those with a greater number of teeth. This result suggests that the integrity of intraoral structures and the number of functional teeth have a direct impact on swallowing function. Tooth loss reduces both masticatory efficiency and the oral control required during swallowing. Aquino et al. emphasized that the presence of removable dentures does not adequately compensate for this condition and may be insufficient in reducing self-reported dysphagia risk. According to the findings of our study, patients who received removable partial dentures exhibited a significant decrease in self-reported dysphagia risk scores three months post-delivery. The discrepancies between studies may be attributed to differences in sample sizes and the fact that, unlike our study, which used the EAT-10 as a validated screening tool-the other research assessed swallowing difficulty solely based on patients’ subjective complaints.

According to our results, in the Kennedy Class I Group, a statistically significant inverse correlation was observed between baseline MNA-SF score and self-reported dysphagia risk score (Spearman’s rho = − 0.808; p = 0.008). Therefore, the second null hypothesis of this study was also rejected. Tran et al., in a screening involving 1,007 elderly individuals using MNA-SF and EAT-10, reported high prevalence rates of malnutrition and dysphagia and found a strong association between these two conditions [51]. These findings are consistent with our study. Additionally, in the Kennedy Class II group, a statistically significant positive correlation was observed between baseline HGS scores and MNA-SF scores (Spearman’s rho = 0.840; p = 0.009). Good masticatory function positively influences hand grip strength, whereas poor masticatory function, particularly in individuals with fewer teeth, adversely affects nutrient intake. This relationship is further supported by studies showing that individuals using well-fitted dentures exhibit better hand grip strength compared to those with ill-fitting prostheses [42]. Moreover, in patients with oral health problems, the likelihood of developing new comorbidities during the follow-up period is associated with decreased muscle strength [42]. Based on the limited number of studies investigating the relationship between prosthesis type, nutritional status, handgrip strength, and self-reported swallowing difficulties in patients classified according to removable partial denture design, this study provides new evidence that further clarifies the subject. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up periods of at least 6–12 months to capture potential nutritional and muscle-strength changes; objective functional assessments including masticatory performance testing, videofluoroscopic swallowing studies, maximum bite force measurement, and tongue pressure assessment; fixed and implant-supported prosthesis comparison groups; collection of socioeconomic and dietary covariates; and larger, multicenter samples to improve generalizability.

The findings highlight the critical importance of assessing both swallowing function and nutritional status using simple validated screening tools upon hospital admission of elderly patients, facilitating early intervention and prevention of complications. This study evaluated the effects of several types of removable prostheses applied in cases of complete and partial edentulism on nutritional status, self-reported swallowing function, and handgrip strength in elderly individuals. The results suggest that appropriate prosthetic treatment can support masticatory and swallowing functions in cases of partial edentulism, as evidenced by significant reductions in self-reported dysphagia risk scores in the Kennedy Class I and Kennedy Class II groups. Notably, at three months, only the conventional complete denture group remained above the EAT-10 dysphagia risk threshold of ≥ 3, whereas the Kennedy Class I and II groups fell below this threshold, suggesting a clinically meaningful differential recovery of swallowing function between partial and complete denture wearers. However, no statistically significant improvements were observed in nutritional status, body mass index, or handgrip strength over the three-month follow-up period. The reduction in self-reported dysphagia risk scores observed after prosthesis use in Kennedy Class I and II groups indicates a significant association between partial denture use and improved self-reported swallowing function. Additionally, the positive correlation between handgrip strength and nutritional status supports the association between oral functions and overall physical health.

Prosthetic rehabilitation not only provides aesthetic and functional benefits but also plays a significant role in improving the general health status and quality of life of elderly individuals. Therefore, it is recommended that prosthetic treatment planning for elderly patients incorporate a multidisciplinary approach, including nutritional counseling, swallowing screening, and functional assessments.
