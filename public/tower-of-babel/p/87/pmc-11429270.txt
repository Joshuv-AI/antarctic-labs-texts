Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis complicated by carbimazole-associated myositis
Authors: Harsan Kanagaretnam, Kajanan Parameshwaran, Kirtan Ganda, Markus Joachim Seibel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryA male of East Asian background in his 30s presented to the emergency department with acute onset global muscle weakness, elevated creatine kinase, profound hypokalaemia and hyperthyroidism. A diagnosis of thyrotoxic periodic paralysis was made and the myopathy resolved promptly with potassium replacement. However, 3 months after being commenced on carbimazole for hyperthyroidism, the patient developed myalgias without weakness associated with an elevated creatine kinase. The myositis panel was negative, while a muscle biopsy showed nonspecific, mild myopathic changes with minimal lymphocytic inflammation. As a change in therapy from carbimazole to propylthiouracil resulted in prompt symptom improvement and normalisation of serum creatine kinase levels, a presumptive diagnosis of carbimazole-induced myositis was made. Genetic testing for hereditary skeletal muscle channelopathies did not identify any gene of interest.Keywords: Thyroid disease, Thyrotoxicosis, Endocrine system, Muscle disease

Background

While most cases of hypokalaemic periodic paralysis are inherited (in an autosomal dominant pattern), acquired cases are described in association with hyperthyroidism and are referred to as thyrotoxic periodic paralysis (TPP).1 While TPP is a rare complication of hyperthyroidism, it is more frequent in patients of Asian ethnicity (2% vs 0.1–0.2% in non-Asian populations). Unlike hyperthyroidism due to Graves’ disease, more than 95% of TPP cases occur in males; hence, Asian men account for the large majority of cases.1 2 It usually presents with sudden onset muscle weakness associated with symptoms of thyrotoxicosis and is typically precipitated by strenuous physical activity or consumption of high-carbohydrate loads.

The acute episodes of TPP are primarily managed with potassium replacement. Non-selective beta blocker therapy has also been demonstrated to be effective in reversing weakness in patients unresponsive to potassium replacement alone. Subsequent restoration of a euthyroid state eliminates the risk of future episodes. Carbimazole, a commonly used antithyroid medication, has been rarely associated with myositis, with documented cases also predominantly involving patients with East Asian ethnicity.3 4

Case presentation

A male of East Asian background in his 30s presented to the emergency department with acute onset generalised upper and lower limb weakness, which had developed over the past 18 hours. The night prior he had played high-intensity sport followed by a carbohydrate-rich meal of rice and noodles. He reported 6 kg unintentional weight loss in the months preceding the admission. He had neither noted weakness previously nor did he have other symptoms of hyperthyroidism, such as anxiety, heat intolerance, diarrhoea, palpitations or excessive sweating. He had no significant medical history and did not take any regular prescribed or over-the-counter medications. There was no history of neck pain, recent iodinated contrast or excessive iodine intake. He had no symptoms to suggest Graves’ ophthalmopathy.

On examination, his heart rate (HR) was 80 beats per minute, blood pressure was 135/74 mm Hg, respiration rate was 24 breaths per minute and he was saturating 98% on room air. The patient was afebrile and had normal muscular tone but reduced power to 4/5 bilaterally on shoulder abduction and adduction, elbow flexion and hip flexion. He had brisk biceps and knee reflexes, and his sensory examination was normal. The remainder of his neurological examination (including cranial nerves) was, otherwise, normal. A thyroid examination revealed a visible goitre and a thyroid bruit. He had no cushingoid features and no signs of heart failure or ophthalmopathy.

Investigations

An initial ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with a HR of 78 beats per minute. There was evidence of limb lead reversal. There was a fusion of the T and U wave in leads V3–V6 and a prolonged QTc of 515 ms (using V5 maximal tangent method and Bazett formula), both of which are suggestive of hypokalaemia (figure 1). Blood tests

Notably, potassium was 1.9 mmol/L, and creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 1231 U/L. TSH (Thyroid stimulating hormone) was low at 0.02 mIU/L with elevated free T4 and T3 levels (76 and 17.1 pmol/L, respectively). Thyroid microsomal and TSH receptor antibodies were detectable at 292 IU/mL and 8.0 IU/mL respectively, consistent with hyperthyroidism secondary to Graves’ disease. The patient also had significant hypophosphatemia. This was likely secondary to respiratory alkalosis from anxiety-related hyperventilation. Respiratory alkalosis causes increasing intracellular uptake of phosphate, as the rise in intracellular pH enhances glycolysis and subsequent phosphate consumption within cells. There were no other causes of hypophosphatemia, such as a recent iron infusion.

Treatment

Given the profound hypokalaemia, intravenous potassium was administered at a rate of 10 mmol/hr with cardiac monitoring. A second ECG (figure 2) taken 3 hours later demonstrated ST depression in the inferior leads (II, II and aVF) and U waves in leads V2–V3, features characteristically associated with hypokalaemia. The QTc had normalised to 400 ms.

ECG 3 hours after treatment with potassium chloride. ECG taken 3 hours after treatment with potassium chloride demonstrated ST depression in the inferior leads (II, II and aVF) and U waves in leads V2–V3, features characteristically associated with hypokalaemia.

A total of 70 mmol of intravenous potassium chloride was administered over 8 hours, resulting in the normalisation of serum potassium levels to 4.1 mmol/L and concurrent resolution in muscle weakness. No rebound hyperkalaemia was noted.

The hypophosphatemia was treated with 20 millimoles of sodium dihydrogen phosphate with an improvement in serum phosphate level to 0.98 mmol/L. His next blood test 7 hours later demonstrated a high phosphate level of 1.74 mmol/L, suggesting that hypophosphatemia was due to redistribution and, thus, would likely have self-resolved with resolution of the respiratory alkalosis.

The patient was commenced on carbimazole 10 mg two times per day for Graves’ disease. He was advised to avoid triggers for the episodes of TPP, such as intense exercise and large carbohydrate loads. Table 2 lists the blood tests on follow-up.

Outcome and follow-up

2 months after the initial presentation

There were no further episodes of TPP. The patient’s TSH remained significantly suppressed but FT4 and FT3 had normalised. His TSH receptor antibody remained positive and carbimazole was reduced from 10 mg two times per day to 15 mg daily. However, 2 weeks later, he developed severe muscle cramps and myalgia with no symptoms of paralysis or weakness to raise suspicion for a repeat episode of TPP. Repeat blood tests revealed elevated serum CK levels of 1335 U/L but serum potassium levels were within normal limits. The patient did not partake in any strenuous activity or exercise after discharge from hospital to explain the CK rise and myalgia. Due to concerns for possible carbimazole-induced myositis, alternative management options were discussed with the patient, including switching to propylthiouracil, as well as definitive management with radioactive iodine therapy or thyroidectomy. The patient opted to trial switching to propylthiouracil. Carbimazole was ceased and the patient was commenced on propylthiouracil 50 mg three times a day. At the time of change, serum TSH levels remained suppressed with normal FT4 and FT3. Within 3 weeks of ceasing carbimazole and commencing propylthiouracil, the patient reported improvement in symptoms and his serum CK had almost normalised to 285 U/L. TSH was once again suppressed with normal FT4 and FT3.

6 months after his initial presentation

The patient reported ongoing occasional episodes of muscle cramps but he no longer had any myalgia after the switch to propylthiouracil. His serum CK and potassium levels remained within reference limits. Serum TSH had now normalised and the FT4 and FT3 remained normal. PTU (propylthiouracil) was reduced to 50 mg morning and 25 mg evening. Neurological examination was remarkable for mild bilateral proximal upper limb and lower limb weakness, with 4+/5 power on shoulder abduction, hip flexion and hip abduction testing and 5/5 power elsewhere.

Given concerns for ongoing mild myopathy, the patient was referred to neurology for further assessment. There was normal motor and sensory nerve conduction but subtle myopathic features on electromyography of the left deltoid and gluteus muscle. A myositis antibody panel was negative. A muscle biopsy showed mild myopathic changes evidenced by mild increase in internal nuclei and perivascular lymphocytic infiltration, which is predominantly in the perimysium (figure 3). There were no features of immune-mediated inflammatory or necrotising myopathy. The findings were nonspecific and could reflect regeneration following the previous episodes of rhabdomyolysis or myositis, such as from prior carbimazole use. Chronic thyrotoxic myopathy can also cause similar changes, but this was considered to be less likely, given the patient’s hyperthyroidism had resolved with antithyroid drugs at the first follow-up appointment. The patient underwent genetic testing using a panel for skeletal muscle channelopathy to evaluate for the inherited causes of hypokalaemic periodic paralysis. No genetic variants of note were identified.

The patient’s myalgia progressively improved during follow-up. At 24 months after his first presentation, he continues on propylthiouracil 50 mg two times per day, as his thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin levels remain positive but his TSH, FT4 and FT3 levels have remained within reference range. At all follow-up appointments, definitive management with thyroidectomy or radioactive iodine was offered; however, the patient preferred to remain on propylthiouracil. The cessation of propylthiouracil was also discussed; however, given the considerable risk of recurrence of hyperthyroidism in the context of persistently positive thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin, a joint decision was made to remain on propylthiouracil. The patient understands that trialling cessation of propylthiouracil and considering definitive management of Graves’ hyperthyroidism will be an ongoing point of discussion at future appointments (especially in the event of relapse after the cessation of propylthiouracil).

Discussion

As far as we are aware, this is the first documented case of TPP followed by a presumed diagnosis of carbimazole-induced myositis. This discussion will summarise the current literature on TPP (including pathophysiology, clinical manifestations and management) and carbimazole-induced myositis.

Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis

Pathophysiology

Hypokalaemia in TPP is caused by a dysregulation in the transcellular distribution of potassium rather than total body depletion of potassium. While the mechanism is not completely understood, it is thought to relate to the stimulating effects of thyroxine on sodium–potassium Adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) within skeletal muscle tissue.5 Thyroid hormone upregulates the transcription of the gene encoding for the Na+-K+ ATPase pump within skeletal tissue and promotes the membrane insertion of the pump within skeletal tissue. These actions ultimately promote potassium ions (K+) to be driven intracellularly, depleting extracellular potassium and leading to muscle paralysis by interfering with cell membrane depolarisation. Recent studies have also revealed the role of Kir2.6 gene mutations in the pathophysiology of TPP. Patients with TPP are more likely to have mutations in the Kir2.6 gene, which encodes for inward rectifying K+ channels, resulting in decreased K+ efflux from cells and exacerbating extracellular hypokalaemia.5 6

Clinical manifestations

Consistent with the clinical presentation of this case, patients with TPP most commonly present with acute onset muscle weakness, which is more prevalent in the lower limbs compared with the upper limbs. The pattern of this weakness can be symmetrical or asymmetrical and more commonly affects the proximal muscles rather than the distal muscles. More serious manifestations, including respiratory compromise, bulbar weakness and life-threatening arrhythmia, have also been reported in the literature.1 2 Our patient presented with few signs of thyrotoxicosis apart from weight loss, with sudden onset weakness after a high-carbohydrate meal, which is consistent with similar cases described in the literature. Chang et al examined 135 patients with TPP over a 10-year period (130 men and 5 women) and found that only 17% of patients had overt symptoms of thyrotoxicosis on presentation. They also noted that 34% of patients had an identifiable trigger for their TPP episode; a high-carbohydrate load was the most common followed by recent acute respiratory tract infection, strenuous exercise, high salt diet and trauma.2 Other triggers reported in the literature include alcohol ingestion, exposure to cold, steroids and emotional stress.2 In the current case, two of these triggers (high-carbohydrate load and strenuous exercise) were present.

Management of TPP

A diagnosis of TPP is made by the identification of overt hyperthyroidism and hypokalaemia during an episode of paralysis.7 Potassium replacement is the cornerstone of initial management; however, there is a risk of rebound hyperkalaemia with rapid supplementation, as hypokalaemia is due to transcellular potassium shift rather than total body depletion of potassium. A retrospective analysis of 40 cases of TPP in Turkey by Cesur et al showed that patients treated with intravenous potassium had a significantly shorter recovery time compared with those treated with oral potassium.8 Due to the high risk of rebound hyperkalaemia, patients should have cardiac monitoring and have their serum potassium measured regularly while being replaced and after their potassium has normalised. Our patient’s potassium was monitored every 3 hours for the first 9 hours (potassium normalised after 5 hours), and he was on cardiac monitoring during this time. A total of 70 mmol of potassium chloride was administered over 8 hours with no rebound hyperkalaemia. The frequency at which potassium levels need to be measured is not established in the current literature.

Non-selective ß-blockade can also play a role in the management of TPP by counteracting the catecholamine-mediated upregulation of the Na+/K+-ATPase that occurs in TPP. Lin and Lin describe two cases of TPP managed with 3 mg/kg oral propranolol alone. Both patients showed the amelioration of paralysis and rapid normalisation of potassium and phosphate levels without rebound hyperkalaemia.9 Unlike potassium supplementation, there is also evidence for the use of non-selective b blockers, such as propranolol, in reducing the severity and frequency of attacks until a euthyroid state is achieved.10 Patients should also be advised to avoid activities that precipitate TPP including but not limited to strenuous exercise, high-carbohydrate meals and alcohol intake.10 Beta blockers were not used in our case as the patient did not have any adrenergic symptoms of hyperthyroidism and his symptoms promptly responded to potassium supplementation.

Carbimazole-induced myositis

Carbimazole is a thionamide, which is commonly used in the treatment of Graves’ disease. When converted into its active form of methimazole, it prevents the thyroid peroxidase enzyme from coupling and iodinating the tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin, hence reducing the production of the thyroid hormones T3 and T4. Side effects associated with carbimazole include joint pain, rash, elevated serum transaminases and agranulocytosis.11 12 Carbimazole-induced myositis is a rare side effect, which has been documented in only a few case reports so far.1113,17 Interestingly, like TPP, carbimazole-induced myositis seems to be more common in the Asian population, suggesting a strong role for genetic predisposition for this adverse effect. However, no specific gene mutation has been found to date. While carbimazole-induced myositis may not be the definitive diagnosis in this case, we believe that it is a pertinent differential. Myositis seems to occur early during treatment, ranging from anywhere from 1 week to 3 months.11 16 This is consistent with our case, where symptoms developed within 3 months after the commencement of carbimazole. Documented strategies that have been used to resolve carbimazole-induced myositis include switching to PTU, reducing the dose of carbimazole, ceasing carbimazole then subsequently treating with carbimazole and thyroxine and thyroidectomy followed by thyroxine replacement. Our patient’s myositis resolved with the cessation of carbimazole and switching to PTU. However, it should be noted that PTU-related myositis has also been reported, although it appears to occur even more infrequently than with carbimazole.11 13 16 It is important to promptly recognise symptoms, such as muscle pain, aches and muscle weakness, in patients who have commenced antithyroid drugs due to the possible debilitating consequences of myositis, including rhabdomyolysis, significant renal impairment and permanent reduction in muscle strength. In these patients, cessation of the antithyroid drug and switching to an alternate agent may be a useful strategy, as it proved to be in this case and in the documented literature.11,14

Learning points

In patients, especially Asian men, who present with sudden onset muscle weakness and hypokalaemia, check thyroid function to assess whether thyrotoxic periodic paralysis (TPP) is the underlying cause.
