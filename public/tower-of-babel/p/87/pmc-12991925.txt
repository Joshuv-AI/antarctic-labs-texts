Detection of Fungal Translocation in Patients With Alcohol Use Disorder Using a Real‐Time PCR Assay
Authors: Nathalie Pansu, Pascal Drakulovski, Donika Krasteva, Virginie Bellet, Jean‐Philippe Lavigne, Catherine Dunyach‐Remy, Florian Salipante, Alex Yahiaoui‐Martinez, Hélène Donnadieu‐Rigole, Sébastien Bertout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGut microbiota plays a key role in triggering various diseases. However, translocation studies focus on bacteria, neglecting fungal elements, while fungi seem also involved in triggering various diseases. Aim of this study was to assess whether fungal elements were able to translocate in patients with AUD (alcohol use disorder) and to verify if withdrawal from alcohol was beneficial on such translocation. Sixty‐five patients with AUD were included. Blood samples were collected at baseline and 3 and 6 weeks after alcohol withdrawal. Total DNA was extracted from blood. Fungal DNA was searched by qRT‐PCR with panfungal primers and sequenced to determine the species detected. Out of 42 patients tested, 30.9% (13/42) had positive signals on one or several of their samples. Identified DNAs were mostly Candida albicans . No significant variation in mean level of fungal DNA copies was found over time in these patients. No correlation was found between intestinal integrity markers and fungal translocation. Fungal DNA was found in the blood of AUD patients, showing that fungal elements can translocate across the intestinal barrier. Absence of correlation between intestinal integrity markers and fungal translocation indicates that fungal elements may be able to translocate independently of the integrity of gut barrier.

Introduction

The gut microbiota is defined as the microbiological populations that colonize the intestinal tract of their host. Comprising several billion microorganisms, this population is diverse, complex and plays key functions in host metabolism. The gut microbiota is also increasingly shown to have a tremendous influence on various host pathologies [1, 2, 3]. Various publications have shown a correlation between variations in the microbiota and viral infections, diabetes, obesity and Crohn's disease, among others [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, until recently, most studies focused on the microorganisms forming the vast majority of the microbiota, that is, bacteria, although microbiological populations have been known to be formed of more elements than only prokaryotic cells for many years. Indeed, aside from gut bacteria, the microbiota contains at least archaeal and fungal cells [8, 9]. Fungal elements, despite their underrepresentation in the literature [10], have also shown critical roles in various host pathologies, from irritable bowel syndrome [11], liver and colorectal cancers [12, 13], peptic ulcers [14], cirrhosis and alcohol‐associated liver disease [15, 16] and as a source of infection [17]. For these reasons, fungal populations are now studied alone (or in association with bacteria) under the name of mycobiota [18, 19, 20, 21].

The involvement of both bacterial and fungal communities in various diseases seems to occur in three ways. First, the gut microbiota is fundamental to host immune system education, and any failure in that process could result in various inflammatory disorders [22]. The second mechanism is linked to an imbalance in the microbial communities (or dysbiosis) with an interplay between the various microorganisms that will ultimately favor infections [23]. The third mechanism is the microbial translocation phenomenon. Translocation is defined by the passage of whole microorganisms (or components of microorganisms) from the gastrointestinal tract across the intestinal epithelium [24, 25]. Normally, this mechanism is controlled in healthy hosts to avoid deleterious effects through the overloading and overactivation of the immune system. However, a low level of IgA, the alteration of the integrity of the gastrointestinal barrier, or a decrease in microbial clearance can increase microbial translocation up to a systemic level [25]. In this case, various complications appear, from cancer to insulin resistance and acute inflammation [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30] including during or after serious viral infections [31, 32] In particular, bacterial translocation has been shown to be a major element in alcohol‐associated liver disease. In AUD (Alcohol Use Disorder) patients, excessive chronic alcohol consumption seems to be responsible for bacterial overgrowth and the modification of the species distribution in the gut microbiota [33, 34, 35]. Acetaldehyde, an ethanol metabolite, is also known to alter the regulation of tight junctions, leading to their opening, which creates a pathway for the translocation of whole bacteria or microbial products [36]. Moreover, the liver, because of its location and function, is highly exposed to intestinal venous blood content and more specifically to endotoxins from the intestines [37]. It has also been shown that increased LPS translocation promotes fibrogenesis [38], while liver damage markers decrease alongside LPS levels during alcohol withdrawal [38]. Thus, alcohol withdrawal proves beneficial for gut permeability, bacterial translocation [39] and ultimately liver integrity. Although the relationship between bacterial translocation and alcohol‐linked liver diseases is widely investigated, the same is not true for the elements belonging to the gut fungal community. Indeed, the involvement of fungal elements in alcohol‐associated liver diseases (AALD) remains poorly investigated, with only a limited number of studies suggesting a link between fungal translocation and hepatic injury [40]. In contrast to bacterial translocation, the mechanisms by which fungal translocation affects liver integrity in patients with alcohol use disorder are still largely unknown. Alcohol consumption is known to alter the dynamics of intestinal fungal populations, particularly by promoting an overgrowth of Candida albicans [16, 41]. This may contribute to liver injury, as C. albicans is a highly adaptable yeast with a broad arsenal of enzymes that can compromise the intestinal barrier and exert further deleterious effects on the liver [42]. In addition, another yeast, Malassezia restricta, has been shown to trigger inflammatory responses in Kupffer cells in a mouse model [43].

Alcohol consumption also alters inter‐kingdom interactions between bacteria and fungi within the gut microbiota, leading to profound ecosystem changes that may contribute to disease development and organ pathology [44]. Therefore, a better understanding of the potential role of fungal translocation in AUD is of considerable importance, particularly given the limited number of studies available and the uncertainty regarding whether fungi are actively involved in microbial translocation in this context.

To address this knowledge gap, we investigated AUD patients undergoing a withdrawal protocol for the presence of fungal DNA in plasma. To our knowledge, this represents the first attempt to assess fungal translocation in this clinical setting. We conducted an ancillary study using a previously characterized patient cohort that had been investigated for bacterial translocation patterns during alcohol withdrawal [45]. While most studies on fungal translocation have focused on antigenemia (e.g., serum β‐1,3‐D‐glucan levels) or serological responses (antibodies against specific species, primarily C. albicans ), we employed a broader approach, combining qRT‐PCR and sequencing, to determine whether fungal DNA was detectable in plasma and to identify the fungal species involved.

As this cohort has already been extensively studied for bacterial translocation, our work provides a unique opportunity to compare fungal and bacterial translocation within the same patients and plasma samples. To our knowledge, this is the first study to perform such a comparison in AUD patients.

Methods

Population

The cohort in this study is the same cohort of patients studied for bacterial translocation in a previous study; thereafter, labeled original study Donnadieu‐Rigole et al. [45].

Briefly, patients hospitalized for alcohol withdrawal at the Department of Addictology (University Hospital of Montpellier/Nîmes) for 6 consecutive weeks between April 2015 and March 2016 were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were alcohol use disorder (AUD, meeting two of the 11 DSM‐5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) criteria during a 12‐month period) and daily drinking in adult patients who gave informed consent. The exclusion criteria were a history of chronic inflammatory disease and the presence of a current infection, positive HIV serology or an immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive treatment. Clinical data were collected by the principal investigator through a physical examination, and an anonymous survey form was used to collect data, including age, sex and various treatments received in the last month; the type, duration and quantity of alcohol consumption using a Timeline Follow back (TLFB) questionnaire; associated addictions; body mass index; and signs of decompensated cirrhosis (ascites, jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy). Standard blood parameters were measured to eliminate liver failure (aspartate amino transferase and alanine amino transferase (ASAT/ALAT), gamma glutamyltransferase (GGT), bilirubin, platelet count and albumin), inflammatory syndrome (fibrinogen level, complete blood count and C‐reactive protein) and C and B hepatitis serologies. Sixty‐five patients were pre‐included. Two refused to participate, two were sober prior to hospitalization, and twenty were lost to follow‐up or experienced alcohol‐use relapse during the study, resulting in 41 patients definitively included, as reported in [45]. A forty‐second patient, whose samples arrived too late for processing in the original study, was included in the present study.

Three blood samples were collected according to standard care in the clinical department at baseline (±1 day before withdrawal) and 3 and 6 weeks (±3 days) after alcohol withdrawal.

Healthy controls were obtained from 24 randomly selected EFS (Etablissement Français du Sang) donors which are legally questioned or checked on the absence of any major health issue (HIV, HCV, HBV, Syphilis, fever, malaria or stay in malaria infected area 6 months before donation, chronic inflammatory disease, multiple sexual partners, intravenous drug users, excessive alcohol consumption, invasive surgery including dental or visceral surgery) before having their blood accepted. Standard population data from the EFS database indicate that 51% of French volunteer blood donors are women (mean age: 37) versus 49% of men (mean age: 39.7) [46].

This study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2008.

DNA Extraction

Blood was collected into EDTA tubes and stored at −80°C. DNA was extracted from 200 μL of plasma using the EZ1 advanced XL with the “Bacteria” program and the EZ1 DNA Tissue kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France), according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA was eluted in a final volume of 100 μL. A negative control with PCR‐grade water was used for each experiment.

18S Ribosomal DNA Real‐Time Quantitative PCR

Primers and Probes

Forward and reverse primers targeted the fungal DNA 18S and 28S ribosomal genes, including internal transcribed spacers 1 and 2. The primer sequences were adapted from the fungal universal primers ITS 1 and 4, as described by White et al. [47]: ITS1 5′‐TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCG‐3′, ITS 4 5′‐TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGCTTAAG‐3′.

Polymerase Chain Reaction

Fungal 18S rDNA levels were quantified by qPCR using a 20 μL amplification reaction with 2 μL of LC FastStart DNA Master SYBR green (Roche, Meylan, France), 0.3 μmol/L of forward and reverse primers, 3 mol/L of MgCL2, and 14.4 μL of DNA sample. DNA amplification was performed in a Lightcycler 2.0 (Roche) using the following conditions: 10 min at 95°C, 50 amplification cycles (95°C for 10 s, 65°C for 5 s, 72°C for 30 s) and a melting‐curve step (95°C for 1 s, 51°C for 30 s and 51 to 95°C, 0.2°C/s). A standard curve was created with serial dilutions from 108 to 101 copies/μL of a plasmid containing the amplicon. Absolute quantification was performed with Lightcycler 480 software, version 1.5 (Roche), according to the manufacturer's recommendations. DNA was measured in duplicate for each patient and each sampling point. A negative control with PCR‐grade water was used for each experiment.

Species Identification

Five microliters of qPCR product was amplified in a 50 μL reaction containing 0.05 IU/μL of Taq polymerase (Ozyme, Montigny le Bretonneux, France), 1.5 mmol/L of MgCl2, 0.2 μM of diluted ITS 1 and ITS4 forward and reverse primers, 0,2 mmol/L of dNTP and 1X buffer under the following conditions: 5 min at 94°C, 30 amplification cycles (60 s at 94°C, 60 s at 50°C, 60 s at 72°C), and 5 min at 72°C. A negative control with PCR‐grade water as well as a positive control with Cryptococcus neoformans H99 strain DNA were used during each experiment. Amplified DNA was sequenced according to the Sanger method (Genewiz, South Plainfield, UK) using the same primers for sequencing, and the results were analyzed using standard nucleotide BLAST (NCBI blast n, on standard databases with megablast algorithm).

LBP, sCD14, and Zonulin Assays

LBP (Liposaccharide Binding Protein) and sCD14 (soluble Cluster of Differentiation 14) markers were used to monitor bacterial translocation as well as inflammation. Zonulin was measured to explore intestinal permeability. All were assessed as described in 46. Briefly, LBP levels were measured using Enzyme Immunoassay for Quantification of free human LBP (Enzo Life Sciences, Villeurbanne, France) kit following the manufacturer's recommendations with a dilution factor of 1:800. sCD14 was measured using Qntikine ELISA Human sCD14 kit (BioTechne, Lille, France) according to manufacturer recommendations with a dilution factor of 1:200. Zonulin levels were measured using Human Zonulin ELISA Kit (MyBiosource, San Diego, CA). All factors were compared at baseline (before alcohol withdrawal) between healthy controls and Alcohol Use Disorder as well as during the 6 weeks of alcohol withdrawal in the original study [45].

Statistical Analysis

The current report was performed with R (version 4.0.1) software. First, fungal DNA was measured at the initial point (W0) and then 3 weeks (W3) and 6 weeks (W6) after the initial point. The fungal DNA variable was transformed into a qualitative variable with two modalities. Second, an investigation of biomarker expression was performed. Indeed, boxplots were generated according to the new fungal DNA variable. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare the boxplots. A p‐value < 5% was considered significant. Moreover, to identify relationships between qualitative and quantitative biomarkers, a correlation report was used. The same was done for the multivariate analysis. Correlations between patients characteristics (age, sex, BMI‐Body Mass Index, TFLB‐Time Follow back Method for Alcohol, number of previous alcohol withdrawal and volume and type of alcohol consumption) and presence or absence of translocation were researched at baseline and Week 3 and Week 6 after alcohol withdrawal with the nonparametric Spearman rank‐order coefficient test. Finally, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for comorbidities to determine some risk factors for microbial translocation in univariate and multivariate analyses. Performances were measured by AUCs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Relevant relationships were indicated with an AUC and lower CI > 0.5. Further investigations were performed using multinomial logistic regression to determine the risk ratio (RR).

Fungal DNA Translocation in AUD Patients

Results by Samples

One hundred and twenty‐three exploitable samples were obtained from the 42 studied patients (samples retrieved at W0, W3 and W6). Among all these samples, 11.9% (15/123) had a positive translocation signal. Two out of 15 (13.3%) samples were positive at W0. Seven samples were positive at W3 (46.6%), while 6 others were positive at W6 (40%). Identified fungal DNAs from those samples indicated that 69% (11/15) were Candida albicans , 6.6% (1/15) were Candida mesorugosa, 6.6% (1/15) were Alternaria alternata , and 13.3% were Yarrowia divulgata (2/15). Results by Patients

Out of the 42 patients tested, 30.9% (13/42) had a positive signal by qPCR performed on DNA extracted from one or several of their blood samples, allowing us to obtain an equivalent number of amplified DNA copies. Six of the identified positive patients were positive at week 3 of sampling (6/42; 14.3%). Seven were positive at week 6 of sampling (7/142; 16.6%). Only two (2/42; 4.7%) were positive at inclusion. In most patients with identified fungal DNA, only one of the three samples collected was positive (84.6% (11/13)). Only 15.4% (2/13) of the positive patients had two positive blood samples.

Comorbidities and Fungal Translocation

To identify risk factors for fungal translocation, presence or absence of fungal elements in samples at Baseline, Week 3 and Week 6 were analyzed in regard of patients' characteristics with Spearman rank correlation coefficient. No correlations were found between any patients's characteristics and fungal detection whatever sampling point considered (r ranging from −0.084 to 0.06 < 0.6). Then, the areas under the curve (AUCs) and confidence intervals (CIs) at 95% were computed (Table 2). The top five risk factors were displayed in the following array and ordered according to their AUCs. In the univariate analysis, only the alcohol type variable achieved better performances than randomness. Indeed, the AUC and lower interval of the alcohol type variable were > 0.5 but remained irrelevant (AUC < 0.7). It seemed interesting to further investigate which alcohol type was able to increase or prevent fungal translocation. A multinomial logistic regression was performed to compute the risk ratio (RR). We reported an RR close to 1 (beer: 1.18; liquor: 1.18; wine: 1.74) compared to aperitifs. That is, there was no association between alcohol type and fungal translocation. In addition, the 95% CI for the risk ratio includes 1; thus, the results are not statistically significant. In the multivariate case, AUCs and CIs were computed at W0, W3, and W6. At W0, three variables, including alcohol type, BMI and estimated time of intoxication, obtained interesting performances (Table 3). However, as previously described, the 95% CI for the risk ratio included the value 1, and the following risk ratios were not interpretable. At W3, the same result was observed for the alcohol type variable. The RRs were not interpretable. To finish, no relevant variables were reported at W6.

Discussion

Although of growing interest, there are few data available concerning the microbial translocation (MT) of fungal elements, especially for uninfected and not severely immunocompromised patients. In this study, a cohort of patients with alcohol use disorder syndrome under a withdrawal protocol, which was previously analyzed for MT [45] of bacterial elements, was studied to determine if fungal products could be found in their systemic circulation by qPCR. The qPCR technique using the SYBR green method described by Dunyach et al. [48] was improved to conform to the criteria of quality for DNA amplification of fungal ribosomal genes [47] and allowed us to detect extremely small quantities of DNA (minimum 3000 copies/mL or 3 copies/μL) with a very low risk of contamination. The sequencing of the PCR products confirmed the fungal origin of the DNA. Fungal dysbiosis has already been described in alcohol‐consuming patients, including patients with cirrhosis or alcohol‐associated liver disease [15, 16]. In such studies, it was supposed that either fungal β glucan or mycotoxins induced inflammation leading to the onset of pathologies, as assessed for other liver diseases, including liver cancers [13, 49]. However, none of those studies assessed fungal translocation concerning other fungal components (whole fungal cells or fungal nucleic acid fragments) that could be implicated in physiopathological processes. In this study, fungal DNA was identified in the plasma of patients with alcohol use disorder using an improved qPCR technique. Out of the 42 patients tested, 30.1% (13/42) had at least one positive signal, while none of our healthy controls had a positive signal. This result is similar to the results for the bacterial MT markers, in particular 16S rDNA amplification, observed in the same patient cohort studied previously, where AUD patients had significantly higher MT marker values than healthy controls [45].

On the other hand, the distribution of the positive signals over time differed between both studies. Indeed, in most patients in the present study, positive signals occurred in the late phases of the protocol (13/15; 86.6% for both W3 and W6), while bacterial MT 16S signals were positive in the baseline samples. Furthermore, most patients (13/15; 86.6%) were positive for only a single sampling point for fungal MT, while the majority were positive for bacterial MT at several sampling points. These differences in behavior are thus far unexplained, although it has already been described in the literature that bacterial and fungal concomitant interactions occur [50, 51]. One explanation could be that the higher number of bacteria in the microbiota in comparison with fungi and other eukaryotes could make bacterial MT more probable and thus easier to detect [52, 53, 54, 55]. Another possibility could be that prokaryotic fragments (including genomes and genome portions) are smaller than those of eukaryotic cells, including fungi, which would facilitate bacterial MT and their further presence in the general circulation.

We also experienced a variation in the mean levels of DNA copies for the fungal agents in the positive patients between W0 and W3/W6 (from 4645 to 11,905 copies/mL and decreasing to 7361 copies/mL, respectively), but the variations were not significant. This is in accordance with the BT translocation results, where the mean level of bacterial 16S rDNA remained unchanged from W0 to W6 [45], showing that a 6‐week period of alcohol abstinence may be too short a time to allow the full recovery of epithelial barrier impermeability [56] or the clearance of a neutrophil population possibly inducing more damage than healing [57, 58]. Moreover, there were no significant correlations between the CD14, LBP, and Zonulin intestinal integrity biomarkers and fungal translocation.

This is interesting because it may indicate that the detected fungal elements could or could not translocate through the gut barrier independently of its overall integrity, which is in contrast to what was seen for bacterial MT. Indeed, some fungal cells— Candida albicans in particular—possess escape mechanisms following phagocytosis [59], which may facilitate macrophage‐mediated translocation across the gut barrier [60]. Candida yeasts also exhibit a rich enzymatic arsenal that may promote leakage across an otherwise intact gastrointestinal barrier [61, 62, 63]. However, whether the fungal elements translocated in our study consisted of intact yeast cells or merely DNA fragments cannot be determined, owing to the limitations of the technique used.

Furthermore, they may also have alternatively translocated in the blood/serum from other mucosal sites (oral cavity for example). Direct gut samplings or stool samplings (not performed during normal care for AUD patients) would have been useful to remove any doubt about the origin of the detected DNA.

The translocation results seem also contradictory with documented mycobiome dynamics in AUD patients. Indeed, Hartmann et al. [41] have observed a reduction in the intestinal abundance of several yeast species after alcohol withdrawal. In particular, the amount of fecal C. albicans decreases after 2 weeks of alcohol detoxification in their study. However, it should be noted that any diet change can modify microbiota populations within days [64] while it doesn't necessarily prefigure the integrity of the gut barrier that can take time to recover [65]. As such, intestinal leaks may still be present and explain our late DNA detections even if the guts' micromycetes population is modified or reduced.

Regarding the identified fungal agents, at the patient or sample level, the majority were Candida albicans (for approximately 70%), followed by minor amounts of Candida mesorugosa, Yarrowia divulgata and Alternaria alternata. All these species have been previously described in the human mycobiome [11, 17, 66, 67, 68]. Numerous major genera exist in the human gut microbiota, including Penicillium, Malassezia, Saccharomyces, Candida and Cladosporium. Saccharomyces was found to be the most prevalent genus, followed by Candida and Cladosporium, in samples from 48 healthy participants [8, 69]. More recently, an analysis of the fungal content from 307 stool samples from healthy volunteers positioned Saccharomyces as the most prevalent genus, followed by Malassezia and Candida [70]. Thus, the detection of mainly Candida albicans in our population of patients is in accordance with the literature [10, 18]. In this study, however, none of the other most prevalent fungal genera, Saccharomyces, Malassezia or Cladosporium, were found. This raises questions about the seemingly easier translocation capability of Candida species in comparison with other genera. Indeed, tight junctions are described as the barrier that hinders Candida from crossing the intestinal epithelium [71], but excessive alcohol consumption is known to increase intestinal permeability and generate abnormalities in tight junction regulation. However, here, translocation occurred independently of the level of the tight junction integrity biomarker zonulin. Furthermore, the enzymatic arsenal in Candida species is rich in adhesins and aspartic proteases [72] that are lacking in other yeasts, such as Saccharomyces and Malassezia. These enzymes can be facilitating factors to allow Candida yeasts to translocate through an already altered epithelium. Moreover, Candida albicans has also been shown to mediate translocation through the healthy intestinal barrier by damaging the epithelium with the help of its candidalysin toxin [61, 62, 63]. It has also been shown that candidalysin helps to promote alcohol‐associated liver disease [73]. This enzymatic weaponry could thus explain why Candida albicans represents the vast majority of the translocated fungal elements in ICU patients [74, 75] as well as in this study and would be in accordance with the lack of correlation of the intestinal integrity biomarkers assessed and the overall fungal translocation in our patient panel. This over‐representation of Candida albicans material is anyway of particular clinical interest in our cohort because this yeast is known to promote cellular damages on the liver [40, 73] in particular in alcohol‐associated liver disease, thanks to its exotoxins equipment. Thus, translocation of this yeast species should be carefully monitored in this type of patients to try to prevent any further complications.

Concerning identification of Yarrowia divulgata and Alternaria alternata , these fungal elements may come from food intake [66, 76] as previous studies described them to be found in animal‐related [77] and vegetal sources respectively [78]. It is also possible that Alternaria alternata spores were inhaled from the environment and were later swallowed by the patient, subsequently being transported to the GI tract. As such, these species are unlikely to be normal members of the symbiotic human gut mycobiome. Presence of Alternaria, although limited in our cohort, may however have a deleterious impact on the patient's liver health thanks to various mycotoxins produced by this mold. These toxins (alternariol and derivatives, tenuazonic acid and tentoxin) were shown to be capable of inducing hepatotoxic effects up to necrosis [79]. This may be particularly true in patients with already weakened liver due to alcohol use disorder.

This is the first work that describes the translocation of fungal products into the systemic circulation of uninfected and not severely immunocompromised patients. The physiopathological consequences of this phenomenon remain unclear. Indeed, no link between fungal DNA translocation and alcohol withdrawal was found, as most samples were positive at late sampling times, in contrast to what was initially expected. This is in accordance with the work of Donnadieu‐Rigole et al., where 6 weeks of alcohol withdrawal did not decrease bacterial 16S DNA plasmatic levels. It could be hypothesized that the translocation of fungal antigens could trigger the same systemic inflammatory response as observed during bacterial translocation [80, 81]. Indeed, the recognition systems are similar: TLR‐4 recognizes LPS and is also able to detect fungal mannan antigens, TLR‐2 detects peptidoglycan and fungal ß‐D glucan, and TLR‐9 detects both fungal and bacterial unmethylated DNA [82], implying that the consequences of both bacterial and fungal MT may be identical. Furthermore, the immune system's chronic activation created by the translocation of fungal material across an altered intestinal epithelium may participate in or amplify the complications induced by bacterial translocation through a synergistic effect. As such, the detection of fungal translocation alongside bacterial translocation is of interest; as more (both in quantity and variety) components of the microbiota translocate through the gut epithelium barrier, there will be more physiopathological consequences that are deleterious for patients with various diseases [83, 84, 85]. This possible combination of effect begins only to be assessed [86].

In conclusion, the authors described the translocation of fungal products in AUD patients, most of which were from the yeast Candida albicans . The fungal translocation did not seem to exactly match the bacterial translocation observed previously in the same cohort of patients. The effect of such fungal translocation in AUD patients is still unclear, but its coexistence with bacterial translocation raises questions about a possible combined effect that should be clarified in future studies, including in other populations with altered intestinal permeabilities.
