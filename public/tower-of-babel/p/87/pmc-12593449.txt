Exercise intolerance and time to medical clearance to return to sport following sport-related concussion in adolescents
Authors: Matthew G Neill, Lauren N Miutz, Michaela K Chadder, Jean-Michel Galarneau, Josh J Burkart, Jonathan D Smirl, Carolyn Emery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRecovery duration following adolescent sport-related concussion (SRC) is variable. Previous research revealed exercise intolerance is associated with delayed recovery (predominantly male Australian cohort). Additional sport contexts and considerations of sex are necessary. This study investigated sex-specific associations between exercise intolerance and time-to-medical clearance to return-to-sport (RTS) following SRC in adolescents (ages 10–19).MethodsAdolescent athletes participating in the pan-Canadian SHRed Concussions cohort study were included within 14 days following a physician diagnosed SRC. Participants completed the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT). Pre-BCTT Sport Concussion Assessment Tool 5 (SCAT5) Symptom Severity Score (SSS;/132) and concussion Overall Condition score (OC;/10) were recorded. Termination criteria for BCTT were participants who experienced ≥3/10 increase for OC (exercise intolerant) or reached volitional fatigue (≥180bpm, ≥18/20 rating of perceived exertion, exercise tolerant).Results281 adolescents (126 male and 155 female), of whom 204 (72.6%) (100 male (79.4%), 104 female (67.1%)) were exercise tolerant and 77 (27.4%) (26 male (20.6%), 51 female (32.9%)) were intolerant following SRC. Based on the point estimate, females had a 1.45 times higher risk of exercise intolerance compared with males (risk ratio=1.45, 95% CI 0.96 to 2.20). Exercise intolerance was associated with a 36% longer medical clearance time to RTS following SRC for females (time ratio=1.36, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.64), while for males, no association was observed (time ratio=1.13, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.42) (adjusted for age, BCTT duration, SCAT5 SSS and days from SRC to BCTT).ConclusionExertion testing may identify female adolescents at higher risk of delayed medical clearance recovery to RTS, informing the comprehensive clinical management of SRC.Keywords: Exercise testing, Adolescent, Concussion, Exercise physiology

Furthermore, postconcussion exercise intolerance is associated with autonomic and cerebrovascular dysfunction. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that exercise intolerance may be associated with delayed recovery time following sport-related concussion (SRC) in adolescents.

Introduction

Adolescent participation in sport is associated with a myriad of beneficial outcomes, including developmental benefits in mental and physical health.1 However, sports participation is also the main context for adolescent injury.2 Injuries can hinder the physical and mental health benefits promoted by sport.3 Unique among sports injuries, sport-related concussion (SRC) is caused by biomechanical forces to the head, neck or body, which are transmitted to the brain.4 With an estimated one in nine adolescent players suffering a concussion each year5 and with typical recovery for youth participants ranging from 14 to 28 days,4 SRC is a burdensome injury. Moreover, due to the vulnerability associated with brain neurodevelopment, adolescents may be more susceptible to SRC and the associated consequences.6 Often referenced as a snowflake injury, SRC produces a heterogeneity of signs and clinical symptoms.4 Moreover, recovery times range widely, with the majority of adolescents recovering in weeks, while roughly 30% experience persisting symptoms taking longer than a month before medical clearance to return to sport (RTS).7 It remains unclear if females experience longer recovery following concussion compared with males, with a roughly even number of studies finding longer recovery in females as studies detecting no difference.8 Conversely, prior research suggests that adolescent females are at higher risk of developing persisting symptoms following concussion.9 Therefore, the clinical diagnosis and management of SRC is complex. A multifaceted approach is necessary to guide recovery including diagnosis, management, rehabilitation and prevention of persisting postconcussive symptoms.4 10 11

The formerly recommended rest-is-best approach has quickly become outdated, replaced with recommendation of an early return to symptom-tolerated exercise.412,14 Within the current multifaceted approach to SRC management, adolescents are encouraged to abstain from activity for just 24–48 hours following injury.4 Prolonged strict/complete cognitive and physical rest until total symptom resolution may prolong recovery.12 13 Current evidence suggests engagement in light-to-moderate-intensity physical activity (PA) that only mildly exacerbates SRC symptoms promotes a quicker recovery time course.12 This shift in clinical management recognises the potential negative effects of prolonged inactivity, including deconditioning, mood disturbances, academic disruption and delayed neurological recovery.13 However, to prescribe symptom-limited exercise following SRC, clinicians are encouraged to perform incremental exertion testing to determine an individual’s symptom-exacerbation heart rate (HR) threshold.4 15

Previous literature has assessed the utility of exertional testing for determining exercise (in)tolerance post-SRC and predicting recovery trajectories.16 17 Exercise intolerance is traditionally defined as a symptom provocation of ≥3 on a 0–10 Overall Condition scale (OC) during incremental exertion testing, sustained before achieving volitional fatigue. Exercise intolerance appears to manifest concordantly with all other symptom subtypes.18 A study within a mostly male Australian cohort found the prevalence of exercise intolerance following SRC in adolescents was estimated at ~50%. The same study found exercise intolerance at initial clinical assessment is associated with prolonged recovery in adolescents.17 These results have not been confirmed in larger cohorts of adolescent athletes, and the influence of sex on the association between exercise intolerance and recovery is not well understood.

Thus, the purpose of this study was to investigate the influence of exercise intolerance on time to medical clearance to RTS following SRC in male and female adolescent athletes. It was hypothesised exercise-intolerant males and females would both take longer to recover from SRC than the exercise-tolerant adolescents. Furthermore, a main effect for females taking longer to recover than males was hypothesised.

Methods

Participants and study design

This prospective cohort study recruited participants from the broader longitudinal cohort study: Surveillance in High School and Community Sport to Reduce Concussions (SHRED Concussions), which enrolled adolescents (10–19 years of age) from eligible high schools and club teams competing in at least one of 16 high-risk concussion sports.19 20 Demographics (height and weight) were collected during preseason baseline assessment. Participants were included in the present study if they sustained an SRC, diagnosed by a study sport medicine physician within 14 days of injury (time from injury to medical assessment and exertion testing are provided in table 1). Following physician diagnosis, participants completed a series of assessments at the Sport Injury Prevention Research Center.

As part of the physician visit, participants completed the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT)21 on a SportsArt T635M Treadmill (SportsArt, Mukilteo, Washington) administered by a clinical exercise physiologist. To be eligible to perform the BCTT, participants required a PA Readiness Medical Examination completed by a physician clearing for maximal exercise, a resting HR <100 bpm and blood pressure (BP) <160/90 mm Hg and OC <6 as measured on a 0–10-point Wong-Baker faces pain rating scale.22 Three cases were explicitly cleared by a physician for exertion testing despite reporting a OC of 6 or higher and were included in the study. If participants were too symptomatic to complete the BCTT at their initial visit, they completed the BCTT at the next weekly follow-up visit, provided their OC was below the 6/10 threshold. These participants were included in the analysis if they were still within 14 days of injury at time of BCTT.

For the BCTT, the participants walked on a treadmill at 3.2 miles/hour at a 0% incline. The incline increased by 1% per minute to a maximum 15% incline, after which, the treadmill speed increased by 0.2 miles/hour. Each minute, HR was collected via chest-strap HR monitor (Polar H10: Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland), rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was self-reported on a Borg visual analogue scale from 6 to 20,23 and OC on a 0–10 point scale was reported.22

While participants were encouraged to exercise until volitional fatigue, they were told they could stop any time. The BCTT was terminated if any of the following occurred: HR of ≥180bpm, RPE ≥18, the participant reached self-determined volitional fatigue, the participant experienced a ≥3/10 point increase in OC test initiation, or the CEP terminated for safety reasons. Following completion of the BCTT, participants could walk on the treadmill at 0% incline as needed.

Pre-test Sport Concussion Assessment Tool 5 (SCAT5) Symptom Severity Score (SSS, /132) was recorded.24 Number of stages completed (corresponding with test duration) was recorded. If the exertion test was stopped due to exacerbation of concussion-like symptoms, the participant was considered exercise intolerant.13

Participants were followed prospectively until they were provided medical clearance to RTS from a sport medicine physician. The current Concussion in Sport Group consensus guidelines recommend a brief rest period of 24–48 hours, following the RTS stepwise strategy with at least 24 hours between each subsequent step. Following this approach, the fastest one should be able to complete the RTS steps following concussion would be approximately 8 days (24 hours rest, then steps 1, 2a, 2b, 3, 4, 5, 6).4 All data were stored using the RedCap platform.

Patient and public involvement

The present study is informed by clinical gaps identified during the establishment of the broader SHRED Concussions longitudinal cohort study. Specifically, the research question was informed by a transdisciplinary team of clinicians and scientists during the development of the SHRED Concussions cohort study. Members of the research team bring considerable lived experience in concussion, included perspectives on delayed recovery following SRC. This lived experience informed the methodological approach of this study.

Statistical analysis

Mean and 95% CI for participant characteristics were stratified by sex and exercise intolerance status. Bivariate binomial regression models were fit between sex, age, sport contact level, BCTT duration, pretest SSS, pretest OC, days from injury to medical assessment, days from injury onset to BCTT and exercise intolerance, obtaining risk ratios (RR). Due to collinearity, multivariable binomial models of risk factors for exercise intolerance were fit with either SCAT5 SSS or OC, and time to BCTT instead of time to medical examination as the former would be more relevant to subsequent analysis.

A bivariate log-logistic accelerated failure time (AFT) model was employed to examine the relation of the same above factors and the time ratio (TR) of time to medical clearance to RTS.25 26 Multivariable AFT models were fit to assess the effects being exercise intolerant might have on time to medical clearance to RTS. In addition, moderation of the effects of exercise intolerance by sex was examined by producing two individual effects, one by each sex, and comparing the magnitude and significance of the sex-specific effects.27 All multivariable models were adjusted for time to BCTT, sex and age irrespective of statistical significance. Analyses were conducted in Stata V.18 (StataV.2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release V.18. College Station, Texas: StataCorp LLC).

Results

In total, 347 participants were recruited to this substudy following SRC diagnosis by a physician. Twenty-five participants did not complete exertion testing and were excluded (figure 1). Forty-one did not complete exertion testing within 2 weeks of their injury. Thus, the final sample included for analysis with complete data is 281 participants (figure 1). The bivariate AFT analysis identified associations between several independent variables and time to medical clearance to RTS (table 3). Of note, females took 16% longer to recover than males, though this association was not statistically significant (TR: 1.16, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.34, p=0.054). In the sex, age and time to BCTT-adjusted model, SCAT5 SSS (TR: 1.01 (1.01 to 1.02), p<0.001) and time to BCTT testing (TR: 1.04, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.06, p<0.001) were associated with time to RTS. Exercise intolerance in males was not associated with longer clearance to RTS time relative to exercise-tolerant males (TR: 1.13, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.42, p=0.290) while exercise-intolerant females took 36% longer to recover relative to exercise-tolerant females (TR: 1.36: 95% CI 1.12 to 1.64, p=0.001). Regarding sex differences within exercise-tolerance groups, there were no differences between male and female participants in time to RTS for either the exercise-tolerant (TR: 1.01 95% CI 0.86 to 1.17 p=0.932) or exercise-intolerant (TR: 1.21, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.55, p=0.141) individuals (table 4).

Discussion

The present study built on the previous finding that exercise intolerance occurs broadly following concussion,18 and exercise-intolerant adolescents take longer to recover following SRC compared with exercise-tolerant adolescents.17 The study observed exercise-intolerant adolescent females took 36% longer to recover following SRC than exercise-tolerant females adjusted for the influence of SSS, time to medical attention and other BCTT-related factors (figure 2). Furthermore, the present study demonstrated a potentially clinically relevant 45% higher risk of exercise intolerance in female compared with male participants (table 2). Whether the potentially higher susceptibility to exercise intolerance in female participants is also related to the protracted recovery in this cohort is unclear. However, the results from the present investigation include adjustment for symptom severity at initial visit following SRC. Thus, it is unlikely the increased time to medical clearance observed in the exercise-intolerant females resulted from concussion ‘severity’ or symptom reporting differences between sexes.

The current study did not observe an association between exercise intolerance and time to medical clearance to RTS for male participants (figure 2). This contrasts with a recent study with a primarily (74%) male sample.17 There were some key differences between the present study and the previous research. A study by Orr and colleagues employed a modified Bruce exercise test, which involved running on a treadmill at increasing speeds.28 The present study, however, employed the BCTT.21 Running may elicit greater symptom provocation than walking due to the mechanical force transmission of up to roughly 2 g from the lower body to the head.29 Moreover, the BCTT is known to elicit a greater cerebral blood flow (CBF) response compared with the CCCT,30 likely due to the ‘beat phenomenon’, which describes the contribution of the mechanical pressures of running to the cardiac pressures of the cardiac cycle.31 This phenomenon is likely exacerbated during the Bruce test, which is predominantly running compared with the BCTT, which is mostly an uphill walking test. Symptoms exacerbated by these head accelerations are not necessarily indicative of exercise tolerance, as it may conflate mechanical brain stress with the effects of increasing HR, BP and ventilation associated with exercise. Moreover, the study by Orr et al recruited from emergency departments, which may have increased the relative injury severity compared with the present study. Thus, the present study may be more generalisable across the spectrum of SRC severity. Future investigations on differences in exercise mode, concussion symptom severity and sex are needed to reconcile the contrasting findings between the previous evidence and the present study.

While some studies have suggested females take longer to recover following SRC,32 a review as part of the 6th International Consensus on Concussion in Sport found males and females recover at approximately the same rate.4 8 Conversely, there is evidence that female youth are at greater risk than males of developing persisting postconcussion symptoms.9 Moreover, there is evidence female youth have higher symptoms and more functional impairments following SRC, that resolve post-recovery.33 In the current study, the TR for time to RTS between exercise-tolerant females and males was estimated at 1.01, indicating when adolescents were exercise tolerant, both sexes recover similarly. In contrast, exercise-intolerant females took 36% longer to recover compared with exercise-tolerant females. This is considerably different than the point estimate for exercise-intolerant males (non-significant 13% longer, p=0.290) compared with exercise-tolerant males. Thus, it is possible that previously observed sex differences in recovery trajectory following SRC are driven by differing rates of exercise intolerance, cerebrovascular dysfunction34 or autonomic dysfunction.35 36

The precise mechanism by which exercise intolerance may prolong recovery is unclear. It is widely held that exercise intolerance following SRC is related to autonomic dysfunction,37 which can occur following SRC.36 Moreover, exercise intolerance may occur as a result of CBF impairment.34 When the cerebrovasculature is unable to moderate fluctuating systemic BP (dynamic cerebral autoregulation, dCA), an increase in cerebral perfusion may exacerbate SRC symptoms.38 Thus, since exercise increases BP, improper dCA regulation may provoke symptoms,39 which is evidenced by studies identifying impaired dCA in individuals following SRC.34 40 Previous research also suggests autonomic dysfunction following SRC leads to inadequate exercise ventilatory control, resulting in a disproportionate increase in arterial carbon dioxide levels during exercise.13 41 This elevation would in turn augment CBF increases, due to cerebrovascular reactivity during exercise,42 which increases in sensitivity following SRC.34 43 44 Thus, the findings observed in the study by Orr et al may also differ due to the differing cerebrovascular responses to exercise, whereby running elicits greater CBF compared with other exercise modalities.45 Moreover, there is evidence of sex-specific cerebrovascular differences. Female adolescents have higher resting CBF compared with male adolescents.46 47 Furthermore, females have a greater absolute cerebrovascular reactivity response compared with males.48 Likewise, females display a lower buffering to systolic BP49 and subtly reduced dCA gain across parts of the cardiac cycle.50 Thus, cerebrovascular sex differences may explain the potentially clinically important disparity in risk of exercise intolerance observed in the present sample. Further research investigating sex differences in cerebrovascular regulation following SRC is needed to clarify these mechanisms.

While exercise intolerance may be related to preinjury aerobic fitness, there is limited evidence that fitness is protective against exercise intolerance following SRC.51 52 There is evidence more aerobically trained athletes have fewer SCAT symptoms at baseline.51 Aerobic fitness may improve recovery trajectory, though there is also limited evidence of this relationship.52 Furthermore, increased cardiovascular fitness may equip an individual to tolerate the neurometabolic stress of acute concussion, by facilitating increased nutrient delivery during the excitotoxity of acute SRC.53 Moreover, the BCTT is terminated by predetermined HR or RPE cut-offs rather than number of stages. Thus, the BCTT elicits a similar ‘intensity’ of exercise across aerobic fitness levels. Only when individuals are tolerant enough to exercise can they achieve the cut-offs without symptom exacerbation.54 Since aerobic fitness is only partially approximated by the BCTT,54 and since SRC might acutely modify cardiovascular fitness, future cohort studies where preinjury cardiovascular fitness was collected may be better equipped to elucidate this relationship.

Finally, tolerance to exercise may influence postinjury movement behaviours. It is well established a symptom-limited return to exercise improves recovery time.12 Moreover, increased participation in PA and reduced sedentary time are associated with improved recovery trajectories.12 55 56 Furthermore, sleep, which is often impaired following SRC and associated with recovery,57 is directly influenced by PA and sedentary behaviours.58 Limited exercise tolerance may pose a barrier to PA participation, exacerbating impairments in sleep, which may together influence the observed delays in recovery. Presently, there is a lack of research exploring the composition of complete 24-hour movement behaviours following SRC.59 While exercise intolerance can limit PA following SRC, future research should quantify the composition of 24-hour movement behaviours following SRC in exercise-intolerant and exercise-tolerant adolescents to further understand this relationship.59

Limitations

The present study is limited by the distribution of our sample. Fewer males were exercise intolerant following SRC compared with females. This limited the statistical power when exploring male-specific associations for medical clearance to RTS time. Therefore, the present finding of no association between exercise intolerance and time to medical clearance in males, requires further research, especially considering contrasting findings which detected longer recovery associated with exercise intolerance within a primarily male cohort.17 Moreover, participants completed the BCTT between 1-day and 14-day postinjury. While statistical adjustment for time to testing was included in the analysis, it is likely that a greater proportion of the sample would have presented with exercise intolerance had they completed testing in the acute phase of concussion (within 72 hours).

Conclusion

In this study, female adolescents who exhibit exercise intolerance on the BCTT took 36% longer to recover than exercise-tolerant females. Conversely, there was no difference found in time to medical clearance following SRC between exercise-intolerant and exercise-tolerant males. Moreover, sex and exercise tolerance should be considered when developing management strategies for adolescents following SRC. Future research is warranted to determine whether the potential delay in female recovery following SRC is primarily related to exercise intolerance. Studies should investigate sex differences in the risk of being exercise intolerant following SRC, and the mechanisms that may underlie differences in recovery duration, including cerebrovascular function, autonomic function and movement behaviour engagement. Targeted treatments of exercise intolerance may be beneficial in promoting recovery in adolescents following SRC.
