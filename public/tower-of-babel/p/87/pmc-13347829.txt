Resurgence of Malaria in Protected and Rural Areas After Successful Control Program in a Brazilian Amazon Municipality
Authors: E. B. Skinner, D. de Angeli Dutra, É. Pelegrino, O. Damasceno, S. Burza, E. A. Mordecai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria eradication requires reducing transmission in endemic areas and maintaining these reductions in areas where elimination has been achieved. In Altamira, a municipality in the Xingu Basin of the Brazilian Amazon, a malaria control program was implemented during the construction of the Belo Monte Dam which eliminated local malaria cases, but resurged in rural areas after these efforts ceased. To elucidate different drivers of malaria transmission in Altamira, before (2006–2012) and after (2017–2020) the construction of Belo Monte Dam and the localized malaria control program, we analyzed annual notified malaria cases alongside geospatial variables including forest edge, population size and travel time to urban centers. Due to the low incidence, malaria case data was clustered based on the geographic proximity of health centers. A total of 17,578 malaria cases were reported in Altamira between 2006 and 2020. Malaria cases exceeded 1,200 per year prior to dam construction, then dropped below 100 during construction, resurging to over 700 cases by 2020 predominantly in rural clusters. Using generalized additive models we found that malaria cases increased significantly with increases in forest edge habitats and local population size, both before and after dam construction, while neither distance to deforestation nor climatic variables explained incidence variation. This study emphasizes that interruptions to control efforts, even when localized, can rapidly reverse gains, and that understanding landscape epidemiology is critical for achieving and maintaining malaria elimination in Amazonian settings.

Introduction

Malaria elimination has been a global health priority for decades and remains a goal of the World Health Organization: globally, more than $4.3 billion is invested each year in elimination efforts (Feachem et al., 2019). Significant progress has been made toward elimination: global malaria incidence declined by 37% between 2000 and 2015, and over half of the world's countries are now free from malaria (Cibulskis et al., 2016). However, this progress has not been uniform globally, particularly in malaria‐endemic regions where reductions have been less pronounced, and in some cases where progress has been reversed (Ferreira & Castro, 2016; Grillet et al., 2021). Although malaria incidence has declined across Africa over the past two decades, with countries like Morocco and Egypt achieving malaria‐free status, incidence has risen in Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Uganda. Further, the total estimated number of malaria cases in Africa has increased from an estimated 204 million in 2000 to 246 million in 2023 (World Malaria Report 2023, 2023). In Latin America, malaria resurgence has become a significant concern in the last 4 years, with some countries experiencing increases in cases which have reversed years of gains (Cumbrera et al., 2024; Grillet et al., 2021). In Nicaragua, malaria cases rose from approximately 2,300 in 2015 to more than 23,000 in 2021 (World Malaria Report 2023, 2023), while in Costa Rica there had been fewer than 100 annual malaria cases for more than a decade (2010–2021), but the country experienced an outbreak in 2023 with 570 malaria cases and approximately 200 cases in 2024 (World Malaria Report 2023, 2023), though recent reports state that malaria transmission is now declining (Pan American Health Organization, 2024). Understanding why malaria can resurge is vital to ensuring that malaria‐free targets are met and maintained.

Several factors contribute to endemic malaria transmission, defined as areas that report locally acquired cases of malaria annually. For example, environmental conditions (including localized climate and land use) can moderate the interactions between vectors, hosts, and pathogens. Such changes can increase the habitat suitability and abundance of vectors, and/or affect the biological competence of vectors to transmit malaria (Vittor et al., 2009; Vittor et al., 2006; Colonia et al., 2024; Laporta et al., 2021). However, the drivers of malaria resurgence, defined as “an increasing trend in malaria incidence or prevalence following suppression achieved through the implementation of control efforts” (Cohen et al., 2012), are recognized to differ from those that contribute to endemic malaria transmission. Several primary studies and reviews have investigated the conditions that allow malaria to resurge and have identified four key drivers of malaria resurgence: (a) reduced funding of malaria control programs, (b) increased potential for malaria transmission driven by environmental or biological factors (e.g., favorable climate conditions, changes in vector populations), (c) technical challenges such as drug and insecticide resistance, and (d) socioeconomic factors such as human mobility (Chioro et al., 2023; Cohen et al., 2012; De Castro et al., 2006; Lu et al., 2023; Shanks & Wongsrichanalai, 2022). Studying the differences in malaria before and after elimination can ensure that interventions are context‐specific and effectively target the dominant drivers over time.

This study aims to compare the drivers of malaria transmission in Altamira, Brazil for two time periods: before (2006–2011) and after (2017–2020) local malaria control was achieved. The construction of the Belo Monte Dam in Altamira (the fourth‐largest hydroelectric dam in the world) was a significant undertaking that brought thousands of workers to the area between 2013 and 2017 (Calvi et al., 2020). Belo Monte's environmental licensing required that dam developers provide an improvement in services to meet the needs of the growing population (Johansen et al., 2024; Pires et al., 2019), which included a malaria control program to be implemented by the dam developers. This program was an addition to Brazil's malaria control program based on active surveillance that was first installed in the 2000s (Ferreira & Castro, 2016). This additional program adopted multiple strategies (e.g., indoor spraying, delivery of mosquito nets, expanded malaria diagnosis using rapid tests, and educational activities) that led to control of malaria in Altamira City and the wider region (Pires et al., 2019). In 2018, a year after the dam construction had been completed and the malaria control program financed by the dam developers ended, malaria cases resurged in the wider rural region of Altamira, returning to pre‐control transmission levels along the Xingu River but remaining low in the urban area.

In this region, we hypothesize that deforestation and other changes in land cover and land use are important drivers of malaria transmission both before and after malaria control, while human movement increases malaria resurgence after the end of the local malaria control program. Deforestation within the Amazon region has been strongly associated with malaria transmission, as it creates breeding habitats for mosquitoes and increases human exposure to vectors (Arisco et al., 2024; Laporta et al., 2021; MacDonald & Mordecai, 2019). For example, of the approximately 70 species of Anophilidae mosquitoes that are vectors of malaria globally, Nyssorhynchus darlingi, the main malaria vector in the Amazon, inhabits the forest edge and areas of high forest cover (Vittor et al., 2009; Vittor et al., 2006). Before malaria control, local factors such as environmental changes likely played a larger role in driving transmission, given that transmission was widespread and occurred consistently across multiple ecological contexts. After resurgence, however, human movement and population dynamics are hypothesized to be more influential, as localized malaria‐free areas such as urban Altamira became vulnerable to imported cases from surrounding regions along the Xingu River. While temperature and rainfall are known precursors for malaria transmission, we hypothesize that these climatic variables have limited influence in this region because it has year‐round climatic suitability for malaria. Instead, we tested whether socio‐environmental factors, including land cover changes and human movement, were associated with increases in malaria incidence during both pre‐control and post‐resurgence periods.

Methods

Study Area

The study was conducted in the Altamira municipality, situated in northern Brazil within the state of Pará (3.2044°S, 52.2106°W). Altamira municipality covers approximately 159,695 km2, making it the largest municipality by area in Brazil. The municipality is in the State of Pará between the Brazilian Highlands and Amazonian Lowlands and is traversed by the Xingu River, a major tributary of the Amazon River. The climate is tropical, characterized by high temperatures all year (average minimum and maximum temperatures are 21.7°C and 30.7°C, respectively) and average annual rainfall of 1844 mm. There is a distinct wet season from November/December to May/June, and a drier season between July and October, with malaria cases reported annually, but peak transmission coincides with the wet season. The 2022 census reported 126,279 people living in Altamira, with most of the population living in urban areas.

Data Types and Sources

Malaria Cases

Annual malaria infection data was provided by the Brazilian Epidemiological Surveillance System for Malaria (SIVEP‐Malária) for 150 health centers and included the year of infection, the location of the health center, the species of the malaria parasite, and the total population in the region but excluded cases in Indigenous people. All malaria data was collected by trained malaria technicians and was reported to the national malaria database (SIVEP‐Malária). Due to the low number of cases at each of the health centers, data was posteriorly aggregated into 41 clusters (herein referred to as “Clusters”) based on geographic proximity using a k‐means method (see Figure 1). Authorization for data use was obtained from the 10° Regional Center for Health (10° CRS/SESPA), Secretary of Health from Pará State.

Climate and Environmental Variables

Mean annual precipitation and temperature were extracted from ERA5 for each Cluster and year with Google Earth Engine. To identify areas of deforestation, we obtained data on forest cover (which was extracted from MapBiomas Collection 7.1 for each Cluster and year). Using the cummulative.cost function in Google Earth Engine, we calculated the distance in kilometers of each Cluster to deforestation and forest edge perimeter. The distance to deforestation was measured as the distance (in kilometers) from each Cluster centroid to the nearest deforested area within a 50 km radius. Additionally, we calculated the forest edge perimeter for each Cluster, representing the total length (in kilometers) of the forest edge within each Cluster. Forest edge represents a measure more mechanistically linked to the impacts of deforestation on malaria transmission, via increasing mosquito habitat.

Population

Population data was provided within the case data from the SIVEP‐Malária database. This provided the estimated number of people served by each healthcare center annually. However, population data has not been updated since 2013.

Human Movement

Using the Google Earth Engine platform, we estimated the mean travel time from each health diagnosis Cluster to nearby urban areas, such as Altamira City. This was incorporated into the model as a proxy for human movement, based on the expectation that communities closer to urban centers would experience greater mobility. To make this calculation, we utilized highway data from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) and riverway data from the World Wildlife Fund (WWF). For highways, we assumed a maximum speed of 100 km per hour, which is the standard limit for highways in Brazil, and rivers, a maximum speed of 9 km per hour. Following the methods proposed by Carrasco‐Escobar et al. (2020) (Carrasco‐Escobar et al., 2020), we incorporated elevation and land cover data to account for delays due to varying terrain types. We considered only urban areas larger than 10,000 m2 and located within a 400 km radius of each Cluster.

Spatial Analysis

To assess whether our models needed to account for spatial structure, we tested for evidence of spatial clustering using Moran's I calculated for malaria cases across Clusters for each year. A Monte Carlo simulation (1,000 permutations) of Moran's I was used to assess the significance of Moran's I statistic by generating random spatial arrangements of the data and comparing the observed Moran's I value with a distribution of Moran's I values obtained from these random arrangements. This was done to confirm the absence of significant spatial structure in our data, ensuring that our analysis did not need to account for confounding variation linked to spatial structure across infection sites (i.e., the distance between infection sites).

Predictive Model for Malaria Resurgence

We modeled the total number of malaria cases within each cluster as the response variable and included the local population as a predictor. Values of the local population from the SIVEP‐Malária database have not changed since 2013. Population values of 2013 were used as population estimates for the years of 2013–2020. We expect that if the population size is important for malaria transmission (i.e., more cases are reported with more people), this would be represented as a significant relationship within the regression analysis. Since the purpose of this study was to investigate the role of environmental change in the resurgence of malaria in Altamira, and the resurgence of malaria occurred only in rural areas, we only considered Clusters that were not located in deforested or urban areas for our models (i.e., distance to deforestation > 0.5 km). The total number of Clusters included in our models was 23.

Using Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMM), we analyzed the number of malaria cases within each Cluster per year as a function of the distance to deforestation, forest edge perimeter, population size (as reported by the Brazilian Ministry of Health in the SIVEP‐Malária database), travel time to urban areas, mean annual precipitation, and mean annual temperature. In our analysis, we included an interaction term between population size and travel time to account for the influence of population size on human mobility rates. This means that our model evaluates the relationship between notified malaria cases and distance to deforestation, forest edge perimeter, population size, travel time to urban areas, and climate, as well as the interaction between population size and travel time to urban areas. Additionally, we also included Cluster and year as random effects in our models to account for spatial and temporal variation. A smooth term was added to the random effects to account for potential nonlinear variation across the interaction of year and Cluster. A negative binomial distribution was employed, given the positive right‐skewed nature of the malaria case data. Since Moran's I values were small (Moran's I < 0.01), spatial autocorrelation was not included in our models, as there was no evidence of a spatial pattern in the distribution of malaria cases. To assess differences in drivers of malaria in the pre‐and post‐control periods, we ran the models on two distinct data sets: the period 2006–2011 (pre‐Belo Monte Dam construction) and the period 2017–2020 (post‐Belo Monte Dam construction). Model fit was assessed by examining the variance explained by each model and selection was made based on Un‐Biased Risk Estimator (UBRE) values (details in Table S1). The GAMM analysis of post‐Belo Monte malaria data explained approximately 38% of the deviance, whereas the pre‐Belo Monte data GAMM explained 50%.

We obtained consent and authorization to use malaria data in Altamira, Para, Brazil from Dr. Renan Rocha Granato, Director of the 10° Centro Regional de Saude (10° CRS/SESPA), Secretary of Health, Para State Government.

Results

A total of 17,578 malaria cases were reported in Altamira from 2006 to 2020 (inclusive). Before the construction of the Belo Monte Dam, Altamira city experienced a high number of malaria cases exceeding 1,200 annually. During the dam's construction and associated malaria control efforts, however, malaria cases declined significantly to less than 60 each year in the entire Altamira municipality (i.e., rural and urban areas). Following the completion of the dam, malaria cases rose in the wider rural regions—reporting 700 cases in 2020—but remained low within Altamira City (Figures 1 and 2). No Plasmodium falciparum infections have been reported in Altamira since 2014 (Figure 2). The reported malaria cases varied widely among the Clusters annually and over the study years (Table 1). Those are based on the number of malaria cases for each health center, which was later aggregated into Clusters due to the low number of malaria cases. Summary statistics of malaria case counts across Clusters and the entire study period (2007–2020) showed that the mean number of malaria cases for each Cluster summed over the whole study period was 295.9, while the median was 73 and the maximum was 2,425. On an annual basis, the mean number of cases at each Cluster was 21.98 with a median of 1 and a maximum of 781. Infections were dominated by Plasmodium vivax, which accounted for 86% of all malaria cases, followed by P. falciparum which accounted for 10% of cases. Interestingly, we found a spatial shift in malaria transmission in Altamira after the Belo Monte construction. Before Belo Monte, most malaria cases were reported in Altamira City Clusters. Afterward, malaria concentrated in rural Clusters outside of Altamira City (Figure 1).

Drivers of Malaria Cases in Altamira

During the periods of both 2006–2011 (pre‐Belo Monte Dam construction) and 2017–2020 (post‐Belo Monte Dam construction), malaria cases in Altamira municipality increased with forest edge perimeter and population size (Figures 3a–3d). No association was found between travel time to urban areas, distance to deforestation, or climate and the number of malaria cases in Altamira municipality (Tables 2A and 2B). Interestingly, prior to dam construction, the impact of population size on the number of malaria cases was amplified with higher travel time to urban areas. In other words, malaria cases were higher in clusters with large population sizes that were also more distant from urban centers. Travel time to urban centers no longer amplified the effect of population size on the number of malaria cases after the construction of the Belo Monte Dam (Tables 2A and 2B).

Discussion

Malaria has been reemerging in Altamira steadily since 2017 following years of malaria control during the construction of the Belo Monte Dam between 2012 and 2015. Using long‐term surveillance data from 2006 to 2020, we found that malaria cases increased with higher forest edge habitat and population sizes before and after the construction of the Belo Monte Dam. Understanding the drivers of malaria resurgence, which may differ from the drivers of transmission pre‐control program, is important as the world moves toward malaria elimination targets and attempts to sustain progress. Several factors have been reported to cause malaria resurgence after elimination, including the risk of reintroduction (e.g., through changes in human movement) (Tatarsky et al., 2011), a weakening in control programs (Nasir et al., 2020), and increasing potential for malaria transmission in each area (i.e., the presence of vectors and hosts and the environmental suitability) (Cohen et al., 2012). Despite similar underlaying drivers of malaria in rural areas before and after Belo Monte dam construction, malaria resurgence did not occur in urban areas of Altamira. This is likely a result of the easier access to malaria diagnoses and treatment, the main pillar of Brazil's malaria control program, which is more easily achieved and maintained in an urban setting (Ferreira & Castro, 2016). In Altamira, despite the change in the geographic distribution of malaria cases, shifting from urban to rural, we found almost no difference in the malaria drivers before and after intensive control programs. In both time periods, increases in forest edge perimeter and population size were associated with increases in malaria cases. Increases in 1% of forest edge perimeter and population size are associated with 0.59%–0.702% and 1.315%–1.370% increase in malaria cases, respectively. The influence of population size on malaria cases increased with longer travel time to urban centers only before Belo Monte. This suggests that there is a persistent risk of malaria transmission in Altamira municipality and that eliminating the disease requires consistent intervention programs and surveillance to catch imported cases before they seed chains of local transmission. Counter to hypotheses from prior work (Carrasco‐Escobar et al., 2022; Gomez et al., 2025), our proxy of mobility (distance to urban center) did not significantly contribute to malaria incidence during the resurgence period.

Land use, including deforestation, urbanization, dam construction, and mining are all recognized to have complex and varied relationships with malaria across different geographic contexts (Arisco et al., 2024; Cortinhas Ferreira Neto et al., 2024; Laporta et al., 2021; MacDonald & Mordecai, 2019). For instance, we know that deforestation substantially increases malaria incidence (MacDonald & Mordecai, 2019), particularly in areas with high forest cover (Arisco et al., 2024), and that malaria incidence is lower in more urbanized environments possibly due to increased malaria control and reduced vector habitat (Tatem et al., 2013). In the Amazon, practices such as deforestation and dam construction may increase malaria transmission. This happens because they can provide larval habitats for Nyssorhynchus darlingi vectors, which inhabit the forest edge (Vittor et al., 2009; Vittor et al., 2006). This, in turn, increases the abundance of vectors and exposure risk of people in these environments. However, these deforestation–malaria relationships are not universal. Although we found no effect of distance to deforestation on malaria cases in any period in our study, there was a positive relationship between forest edge and malaria both before and after dam construction. When considered together, our findings highlight an important ecological consideration for malaria transmission in Altamira. The finding that malaria was significantly associated with forest edge but not deforestation suggests that in Altamira municipality, malaria risk may be related to exposure to forest edge habitat that sustains a high density of vectors, rather than direct effects of modifying forest cover. Amazon‐wide analyses of malaria transmission have identified deforestation as a driver of malaria cases, whereby for every 1 km2 of deforestation there are an estimated 6.7 new malaria cases (MacDonald & Mordecai, 2019). At the same time, the impact of deforestation on malaria is particularly important on smaller scale deforestation. Chaves et al. (2018) showed that deforested areas were only correlated with increases in malaria transmission when deforestation patches were smaller than 5 km2. However, this previous study found that the effects of deforestation on malaria were twice as strong in municipalities in interior Amazonian states than in exterior states (where Altamira is located) (MacDonald & Mordecai, 2019), aligning with our findings that in Altamira deforestation itself does not increase malaria but the resulting increase in forest edge and vector breeding habitats and density may.

Human mobility showed distinct impacts on malaria transmission in Altamira pre‐ and post‐dam construction. During the construction of the Belo Monte Dam, a substantial influx of workers moving to the area rapidly increased the local population and urbanization (Calvi et al., 2020). However, despite this surge in population, malaria transmission remained low during the dam construction phase due to intensified malaria interventions implemented by health authorities and the dam's developers (Johansen et al., 2023; Pires et al., 2019). These interventions included widespread access to malaria prevention tools, prompt diagnosis, and treatment services, which effectively contained the potential for outbreaks. This highlights how robust, targeted malaria control measures can offset the risks associated with rapid population changes in highly endemic regions (Johansen et al., 2023). However, despite the successful control of malaria cases during the dam construction, it had other health consequences. The influx of workers and lack of infrastructure to support the incoming workers led to surges in syphilis, car accidents, and violence (Grisotti, 2016). After dam construction, many individuals left the area, the malaria control program created by the Belo Monte's developers ceased, and malaria incidence began to rise again, likely due to the reduction of malaria control interventions, indicating the importance of sustained control efforts in maintaining low malaria rates (Calvi et al., 2020; Pires et al., 2019). In the post‐construction period, malaria incidence has remained low in Altamira City, which potentially sustained greater access to malaria control and prevention tools and facilities but resurged in surrounding rural areas.

As we hypothesized, changes in temperature and precipitation were not associated with variation in malaria transmission because the area remained highly climatically suitable throughout the study period. This is possibly because the temperature has little annual variation in this region and is currently around the optimal temperature for malaria transmission (i.e., approximately 25°C) (Mordecai et al., 2013, 2019), and because rainfall is typically sufficient to sustain abundant vector populations.

Malaria surged in the Brazilian Amazon following the construction and occupation of major roads during the military regime (1964–1985) (Ferreira & Castro, 2016; Oliveira‐Ferreira et al., 2010). This increase was driven by large‐scale, uncontrolled migration, extensive natural resource exploitation (e.g., logging, farming, and mining), and widespread deforestation (Brasil et al., 2022; Ferreira & Castro, 2016; Oliveira‐Ferreira et al., 2010). To control and prevent malaria transmission, in the 2000s, the Brazilian government launched a new program to prevent malaria, the Programa Nacional de Prevenção e Controle da Malária, a national initiative to reduce malaria transmission through early diagnosis and effective treatment throughout the Amazon, including Altamira municipality (Oliveira‐Ferreira et al., 2010). As a result, malaria cases declined significantly, from 500,000 to 600,000 annual cases in the 1990s to approximately 200,000 cases in the 2010s (Ferreira & Castro, 2016). The proportion of P. falciparum infections also decreased, from around 40% in the 1990s to less than 15% by 2009 (Brasil et al., 2022; Oliveira‐Ferreira et al., 2010). Building on this success, Brazil launched the Plano Nacional de Eliminação da Malária in 2015, aiming to eliminate malaria‐related deaths by 2030 and locally acquired malaria cases by 2035 (Brasil et al., 2022). The implementation of this new malaria elimination plan in Brazil is based on early diagnosis, effective treatment, rapid notification and epidemiological investigation of malaria cases, and supervised malaria treatment in areas with low malaria transmission (i.e., parasite index lower than 1) (Brasil et al., 2022). One of the pillars of the Brazilian national plan to eliminate malaria is to prevent the reestablishment of malaria in areas without autochthonous transmission (Brasil et al., 2022). For this reason, understanding the land use drivers of malaria transmission and resurgence in the Amazon is pivotal to eliminate malaria in Brazil.

In Altamira municipality, we observed decreases in malaria incidence from early 2000s to 2017, with a later resurgence of P. vivax infections (Figure 2a). We found that increases in forest edge habitat, rather than deforestation per se, were associated with increased malaria transmission (Figures 3a and 3c; Tables 2A and 2B). Managing malaria resurgence in Altamira requires tailored intervention strategies that address increases in forest edge that emerge in response to environmental degradation. With persistent malaria risk linked to forest edges and proximity to forested environments, vector control efforts might benefit from targeted interventions in areas with high forest‐perimeter exposure. Programs could focus on enhanced monitoring of changes in forest edges for early detection of malaria resurgence (Landier et al., 2016). Strengthening the health infrastructure and intensifying malaria diagnosis and treatment, particularly in areas far from urban centers, can also contribute to malaria control and elimination. Improved access to rapid malaria diagnosis in remote areas, distribution of long‐lasting insecticide‐treated nets (LLINs) to rural communities, and supervised treatment in areas where malaria recently resurged could contribute to preventing resurgence of malaria in areas with low malaria transmission (World Health Organization, 2019). Interestingly, P. falciparum malaria did not resurge in Altamira or was diagnosed after malaria control and its local elimination. This might be a consequence of the relatively low human mobility in and out and connectivity of Altamira within the Amazon. As a result, P. falciparum malaria would only resurge in the region if introduced in the region by people coming from other parts of the Amazon. Additionally, the Brazilian malaria control program is more effective against P. falciparum malaria as symptoms precede parasite infective stages in the bloodstream, which means that most people are diagnosed and treated before infecting vectors (Oliveira‐Ferreira et al., 2010; Vitor‐Silva et al., 2016). Strengthening early malaria diagnosis and treatment in areas experiencing rapid land‐use changes or population shifts is essential to prevent resurgence and eliminate local malaria transmission.

While our study provides valuable insights into malaria drivers in Altamira over a multi‐year period, there are limitations to consider. The reliance on health center data may underrepresent malaria cases, particularly in remote or underserved areas, potentially leading to an underestimation of cases in regions with limited health service access. Without active surveillance programs it is difficult to assess the full burden and transmission potential of malaria. Our population estimates might not be very accurate, since population data from the Brazilian Ministry of Health has not changed since 2013. In addition, local population data has not been updated, which could bias our estimates as population numbers could be underestimated or overestimated in certain Clusters. This also means that we could not consider demographic changes during and post‐Belo Monte construction. Additionally, although we analyzed land‐use variables, further research incorporating high‐resolution spatial data on smaller‐scale land‐use changes (such as smallholder farming or secondary forest regrowth) and complementing it with qualitative work to identify how local vector habitat and human behavioral and socioeconomic risk factors relate to the forest edge, may offer deeper insight into the nuanced interactions between land‐use dynamics and malaria risk. It is also important to note that our models only explained a part of the variance in the data (50.1% and 38.2%), meaning that other factors that were not considered in our analysis, including socio‐economic trends and stochasticity, influence malaria transmission in the region. For this reason, future studies could also benefit from longitudinal analyses that include additional socio‐economic and behavioral factors influencing malaria exposure in the region, including increases in crime rate, poverty, and food insecurity in Altamira City. Modeling efforts that simulate alternative land use and climate scenarios may further clarify how specific interventions or policy shifts could impact malaria trends in Altamira. Finally, our analyses are correlational rather than causal and cannot rule out the influence of variables that are correlated with forest edge density on malaria incidence.

Conclusions

The resurgence of malaria in Altamira following the Belo Monte Dam construction underscores the need to sustain malaria control efforts in the Amazon. For Brazil to achieve its goal of eradicating malaria deaths by 2030 and malaria cases by 2035 (Brasil et al., 2022), the resurgence of malaria must be prevented. Our research indicates that sustained malaria control can be particularly challenging in rural and isolated communities, particularly those undergoing rapid environmental degradation and increases in forest edge habitats where vectors breed. Our findings also reveal a consistent association of malaria cases with forest edges rather than distance to deforestation, suggesting that the endemic risk of malaria may be tied to the ecological interface between forest, mosquito behavior, and human settlements. Although the influx of people during the dam construction increased population density, intensive malaria interventions effectively prevented a rise in cases during this period. This points to the critical role of sustained malaria control measures in achieving long‐term malaria reduction, particularly in areas undergoing rapid socio‐environmental shifts. For Altamira and similar regions, continuous control efforts that address local ecological and social factors are essential to reduce the malaria burden and progress toward Brazil's malaria elimination targets. Finally, the resurgence of malaria in Altamira indicates that malaria control programs installed to mitigate the impact of considerable social and environmental impact should be planned as a long‐term investment.
