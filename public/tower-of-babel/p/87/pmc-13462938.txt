Quasi-static and dynamic mechanical properties of functionally graded triply periodic minimal surface structures and their application exploration in interior design
Authors: Yu Huang, Yanyan Chen, Lihua Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Functionally graded design has proven highly effective in enhancing the mechanical performance of porous structures, yet the influence of nonlinear gradient distributions on the quasi-static and dynamic response of triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structures remains insufficiently understood. This study systematically investigates Gyroid TPMS structures with linear and nonlinear (logarithmic, sigmoid, exponential) wall thickness gradients through quasi-static experiments, validated finite element simulations, and analytical modeling. Under quasi-static compression, graded structures exhibit a layer-by-layer collapse mode with localized densification, significantly enhancing energy absorption; the sigmoid gradient achieves the highest specific energy absorption of 13.01 J/g—a 45% improvement over uniform counterparts. The Gibson-Ashby and iso-stress models accurately predict elastic modulus and yield strength for both uniform and graded configurations. Under dynamic impact (10–100 m/s), the exponential gradient structure attains superior specific energy absorption of 24.17 J/g at 100 m/s, with the rigid-perfectly plastic-locking model successfully predicting dynamic plateau stress. These results demonstrate that nonlinear gradient distributions enable precise tuning of mechanical response and energy absorption capacity. When integrated into interior design applications, functionally graded TPMS structures offer unique opportunities for lightweight, impact-resistant architectural elements that combine structural performance with aesthetic expression—providing a foundation for sustainable, multi-functional interior systems.

Introduction

Porous materials exhibit excellent mechanical properties1–3 and lightweight characteristics4–8, making them widely applicable in aerospace, architectural engineering, and rail transportation9,10. As a representative class of porous materials, triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS) are composed of spatially continuous surfaces with zero mean curvature. This topological feature reduces stress concentration under compressive loading, enhances deformation stability, and improves yield strength and energy absorption capacity11,12. In the context of interior design, these mechanical attributes are particularly valuable for structural components such as load-bearing partition walls, decorative columns, and impact-resistant cladding, where both safety and material efficiency are critical. With increasing research attention, it has been found that the mechanical behavior of TPMS structures is highly dependent on their topological configuration, geometric parameters, constituent materials, and relative density13,14. This tunability offers designers the flexibility to tailor structural performance to specific spatial requirements.

To further regulate the mechanical performance of TPMS structures, various strategies have been explored, including twin boundary design15, hybrid TPMS architectures16, multi-material interpenetrating phases17–19, and functionally graded design20. Among these, functionally graded design has proven to be an effective approach for enhancing structural performance. By introducing gradual variations in wall thickness or relative density, functionally graded TPMS structures enable localized stiffness, which is particularly relevant in interior design applications where different zones of a structural element may experience varying mechanical demands—such as a partition wall that requires higher strength at its base and greater flexibility at its top.

In nature, numerous biological systems exhibit functionally graded characteristics. For instance, the leg bones of vertebrates display a gradual increase in calcification from the inner to the outer region21, while the cuticles of insects possess a discontinuous exponential stiffness gradient through their thickness22. These natural paradigms have inspired researchers to adopt functionally graded design strategies in engineered structures. Zhao et al.23 investigated the mechanical behavior of functionally graded Gyroid and Primitive TPMS structures through finite element simulations and compression tests. Their results showed that graded structures exhibit a layer-by-layer collapse mode under compression, accompanied by a progressive increase in stress levels and an approximately 60% improvement in energy absorption compared to uniform counterparts. From an interior design perspective, this layer-by-layer deformation mode suggests potential applications in seismic-resistant walls or energy-absorbing backing panels, where controlled progressive failure can enhance occupant safety. Similarly, Yu et al.24 reported that introducing linear thickness gradients into Primitive structures transformed the deformation mode from diagonal shear banding to layer-by-layer collapse, resulting in a 50% enhancement in energy absorption. Recently, Yao et al.25 proposed a multi-performance co-optimization method for TPMS lattice structures under multi-level stress field driving, aiming to balance multiple mechanical performance indicators simultaneously through systematic optimization. This controlled deformation behavior is particularly advantageous in designing modular interior components that require predictable mechanical response under accidental impact.

The distribution pattern of the functional gradient significantly influences the mechanical response of TPMS structures. Notably, the gradient effect is direction-dependent: mechanical performance can only be effectively tuned when the gradient orientation aligns with the loading direction. This inherent anisotropy poses challenges for maintaining stable performance under complex loading conditions—a consideration that extends to interior design applications where structural elements may be subjected to loads from multiple directions. To address this limitation, Qiu et al.26 proposed a local spherical gradient enhancement strategy, wherein the wall thickness increases radially from the center outward rather than unidirectionally. Numerical simulations and experimental results indicated that this design improves energy absorption stability under multiaxial compression, although the overall performance gain remained modest. In a subsequent study, Qiu et al.27 introduced a hybrid gradient design that simultaneously incorporates thickness and lattice constant gradients. Their findings revealed that this hybrid approach can double the energy absorption capacity compared to uniform structures, effectively combining the advantages of geometric gradient-induced stable deformation and thickness gradient-enhanced energy absorption. Wang et al.28 developed a stress field-driven lightweight design method for multi-level TPMS lattice structures, embedding secondary TPMS structures into the primary Gyroid lattice guided by the stress field under compressive load. Their multi-level Gyroid structures exhibited significantly enhanced elastic modulus, yield strength, and a distinct layer-by-layer failure mode. This strategy holds significant promise for engineering applications and, by extension, for interior design scenarios requiring multifunctional performance—such as freestanding partitions that must resist both lateral and vertical loads while maintaining visual lightness.

Beyond their mechanical advantages, TPMS structures possess intrinsic aesthetic qualities that align with contemporary interior design trends. Their complex, organic morphologies and periodic spatial arrangements create rich visual effects, dynamic light and shadow interactions, and a sense of fluidity that can transform conventional interior spaces. When fabricated using high-resolution additive manufacturing techniques such as stereolithography (SLA) or liquid crystal display (LCD) photopolymerization, these structures can be realized as translucent screens, sculptural room dividers, or lightweight ceiling installations that integrate structural function with artistic expression. The parametric nature of TPMS design further enables customization to suit specific spatial contexts, allowing designers to modulate porosity, surface texture, and gradient distribution to achieve desired aesthetic and functional outcomes29–32.

Despite these promising attributes, the application of functionally graded TPMS in interior design remains largely unexplored. Most existing research has focused on linear thickness gradients, with limited investigation into nonlinear gradient configurations that may offer enhanced performance or novel design possibilities. Furthermore, while the quasi-static mechanical behavior of graded TPMS structures has been relatively well documented, their dynamic response under impact loading—critical for assessing performance in real-world interior environments—has yet to be systematically examined.

To address these research gaps, the present study constructs five wall-thickness gradient schemes encompassing both linear and nonlinear distributions (logarithmic, sigmoid, and exponential gradients). Through a combination of finite element simulations and compression experiments, the quasi-static and dynamic mechanical behaviors of these graded Gyroid structures are systematically investigated. The influence of gradient distribution patterns on mechanical performance and deformation mechanisms is elucidated, with particular attention to how these characteristics can be leveraged in interior design applications. By bridging the gap between advanced mechanical analysis and spatial design practice, this study aims to provide a scientific foundation for the integration of functionally graded TPMS structures into innovative, safe, and aesthetically compelling interior environments.

Material properties and quasi-static compression test setup

In this study, Gyroid structures were fabricated using photosensitive resin via stereolithography (SLA) additive manufacturing. To characterize the mechanical properties of the base material, three tensile test specimens were prepared and tested following the ASTM D638 standard for tensile properties of plastics. The specimens were fabricated using the same stereolithography (SLA) parameters and the same batch of photosensitive resin as used for the Gyroid structures, ensuring material consistency. Quasi-static tensile tests were conducted at a constant crosshead speed of 5 mm/min under ambient conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 5% relative humidity). The measured tensile properties showed high reproducibility: the average elastic modulus was 1.67 GPa (standard deviation 0.05 GPa), yield strength 41.51 MPa (standard deviation 1.23 MPa), and Poisson’s ratio 0.4 (standard deviation 0.02). The specimen dimensions and tensile test setup are illustrated in Fig. 2(a), and the resulting tensile stress-strain curves are presented in Fig. 2(b). Based on the experimental data shown in Fig. 2(b), the mechanical properties of the photosensitive resin were calculated and are summarized in Table 1. All parameters in Table 1 are therefore derived from our own testing. The selection of photosensitive resin as the fabrication material is particularly relevant for interior design applications, as this material class offers advantages in terms of dimensional accuracy, surface finish, and translucency—properties that are highly valued in decorative architectural elements. The ability to produce complex TPMS geometries with high resolution enables the realization of intricate spatial structures that serve both functional and aesthetic purposes, such as translucent partitions, light-diffusing ceiling panels, or sculptural room dividers.

Quasi-static compression tests were conducted on the Gyroid structures using an electronic universal testing machine, following the ASTM D695-15 standard for rigid plastics. Photographs of the fabricated specimens and the compression test setup are shown in Fig. 3. The average mass of each specimen type is listed in Table 2. All compression tests were performed at a constant loading rate of 5 mm/min. The quasi-static compression test protocol is designed to simulate the type of low-strain-rate loading that interior structural elements may experience under normal service conditions, such as the weight of furnishings, occupant movement, or gradual settling of building components. Understanding the mechanical response under such conditions is essential for ensuring the long-term safety and durability of TPMS-based interior installations. Furthermore, the experimental data obtained from these tests serve as validation benchmarks for subsequent finite element simulations, enabling accurate prediction of structural behavior under more complex loading scenarios. The mass measurements reported in Table 2 provide critical data for evaluating the lightweight potential of functionally graded TPMS structures—a key consideration in interior design where ease of installation, transportation, and structural loading on existing building frameworks are important practical concerns.

Mechanical performance evaluation metrics

To quantitatively assess the mechanical behavior of the functionally graded Gyroid structures, several key performance indicators were adopted, including elastic modulus, yield strength, average plateau stress, densification strain, energy absorption, and specific energy absorption30. The average plateau stress represents the mean stress level from the onset of yielding to the onset of densification, and is expressed as31:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{p} = \frac{1}{{\epsilon _{D} - \epsilon _{y} }}\mathop \smallint \limits_{{\smallint _{y} }}^{{\epsilon _{D} }} \sigma _{\epsilon } d\epsilon$$\end{document}

where εD is the densification strain. Physically, densification marks the transition from the plateau region to the final stage where opposing cell walls contact each other, leading to a rapid stress increase. To determine this strain quantitatively, the energy absorption efficiency method is employed. The densification strain is determined from the energy absorption efficiency, which is defined as the ratio of the area under the stress-strain curve to the corresponding stress. When the structure enters the densification stage, the stress increases sharply over a very small strain increment, causing a decline in energy absorption efficiency. where η denotes the energy absorption efficiency. From the perspective of interior design, these mechanical evaluation metrics provide essential guidance for material selection and structural optimization. Elastic modulus and yield strength are fundamental indicators for ensuring the dimensional stability and load-bearing capacity of interior structural elements under normal service conditions. For instance, partition walls, load-bearing columns, and suspended ceiling systems must maintain their integrity and shape under sustained or intermittent loads. The average plateau stress and densification strain are particularly relevant for safety-critical applications, such as impact-resistant walls or protective cladding in public spaces, where controlled energy dissipation and predictable deformation behavior are essential for occupant protection. Energy absorption and specific energy absorption are key considerations in the design of lightweight, movable interior components—such as modular partitions, freestanding screens, or temporary exhibition structures—where ease of handling and installation must be balanced with structural robustness. The systematic evaluation of these metrics across different gradient configurations enables a comprehensive understanding of how functional grading influences mechanical performance. This understanding, in turn, supports the rational design of TPMS-based interior systems that satisfy both structural requirements and spatial-aesthetic objectives.

Finite element simulation model validation and mesh convergence analysis

In this study, finite element simulation was employed to investigate the dynamic mechanical behavior of Gyroid structures. Due to the curved thin-walled geometric characteristics of Gyroid structures, using solid elements for finite element analysis would result in an excessively high number of elements and prohibitively long computation times. To address this challenge, the Belytschko-Lin-Tsay thin shell element formulation16 was adopted to model the Gyroid structures, which significantly improves computational efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Five integration points were assigned through the thickness direction to ensure precise stress and strain capture. The wall thickness of the shell-based Gyroid structures was discretized according to the gradient distributions, with thickness values at each layer corresponding to the nodal points illustrated in Fig. 1(b).

In the finite element simulations, the Gyroid structure was positioned between two rigid plates. The bottom plate was fully constrained, while the top plate was prescribed a constant downward velocity to compress the structure until densification was achieved. All simulations were performed using the explicit finite element software LS-DYNA. Self-contact within the Gyroid structure during compression was defined using the *AUTOMATIC_SINGLE_SURFACE keyword, while contact between the rigid plates and the structure was modeled with the *AUTOMATIC_NODES_TO_SURFACE keyword. The static and dynamic friction coefficients were set to 0.2 and 0.3, respectively, following established practices in the literature30. The complete finite element model of the TPMS structure is presented in Fig. 4. From the perspective of interior design applications, the ability to accurately simulate the mechanical response of functionally graded TPMS structures under various loading conditions is of paramount importance. Finite element analysis enables designers and engineers to evaluate structural performance virtually before physical fabrication, facilitating the optimization of gradient configurations for specific design requirements. For instance, in the development of impact-resistant wall panels or load-bearing decorative columns, parametric simulations can be conducted to assess how different gradient profiles influence deformation patterns, stress distribution, and energy absorption capacity. This predictive capability not only accelerates the design iteration process but also ensures that final interior installations meet safety and performance standards.

The accuracy and efficiency of finite element simulations are highly dependent on mesh resolution. Finer mesh sizes generally yield higher computational accuracy but at the cost of increased calculation time, while coarser meshes reduce computational expense but may compromise precision. To establish an appropriate mesh configuration that balances accuracy and efficiency, a mesh convergence study was conducted. Four mesh sizes—0.5 mm, 1 mm, 1.5 mm, and 2 mm—were evaluated, corresponding to element counts of 173,655, 51,600, 27,060, and 18,540, respectively. The stress-strain curves obtained from simulations using these mesh sizes were compared against experimental results, as shown in Fig. 5(a). The comparison revealed that the 0.5 mm mesh produced stress-strain curves in excellent agreement with experimental data, indicating high accuracy. The 1 mm mesh yielded slightly higher predicted yield strength compared to experiments, but the overall trend and shape of the stress-strain curve closely matched the experimental results, with significantly improved computational efficiency relative to the 0.5 mm mesh. In contrast, simulations using 1.5 mm and 2 mm meshes exhibited considerable deviations in stress levels compared to experimental values, indicating insufficient accuracy. Based on these findings, a mesh size of 1 mm was selected for subsequent simulations, providing an optimal compromise between computational accuracy and efficiency. To further validate the reliability of the finite element modeling approach, a quasi-static compression simulation was performed for the logarithmically graded G-Log structure, and the results were compared with experimental data obtained from physical compression tests. The experimental and simulated stress-strain curves for the G-Log structure are presented in Fig. 5(b). Close agreement was observed between the two curves, with consistent trends throughout the compression process.

Quantitative validation was performed by extracting key mechanical performance metrics—including elastic modulus, yield strength, plateau stress, densification strain, and energy absorption—from both simulations and experiments for the uniform G-45 structure and the graded G-Log structure. The results demonstrate that the discrepancies between simulated and experimental values are acceptable for additively manufactured polymer cellular structures, especially considering the inevitable geometric imperfections and surface roughness (measured Ra ≈ 8.5 μm) inherent to the SLA printing process, which are idealized as perfectly smooth and uniform in the simulation model. While the yield strength discrepancy for the uniform G-45 structure reaches 16.82%, such deviation is within the typical range reported in the literature for similar TPMS structures23,24. More importantly, after accounting for manufacturing-induced effects, the simulation error for the functionally graded G-Log structure reduces to 9.93% (yield strength) and 7.95% (elastic modulus), confirming that the validated simulation framework reliably captures the relative performance trends among different gradient configurations. Therefore, despite the absolute error for one specific uniform case, the overall conclusions regarding the comparative advantages of nonlinear graded structures for interior design applications remain robust and well-supported.

Regarding the material model in LS-DYNA, the photosensitive resin was modeled as an elastic-plastic material with properties from Table 1, using the MAT_PIECEWISE_LINEAR_PLASTICITY model. Since strain-rate sensitivity data for this specific resin were not available, rate-independent material properties were assumed. This is a conservative simplification; consequently, the observed dynamic enhancement (e.g., increased plateau stress and energy absorption with impact velocity) is primarily attributed to inertial effects and structural compaction, rather than material strain-rate sensitivity.

The validated simulation framework provides a reliable foundation for subsequent parametric investigations into the dynamic mechanical response of functionally graded Gyroid structures under various impact loading conditions. For interior design applications, this validated modeling capability enables the systematic exploration of how different gradient configurations perform under realistic loading scenarios, supporting the development of innovative structural systems that combine mechanical functionality with aesthetic expression (Table 3).

Results and discussion

Quasi-static mechanical response

Quasi-static compression tests were conducted on both uniform and functionally graded Gyroid structures, with the resulting stress-strain curves presented in Fig. 6. The mechanical performance metrics for each configuration, calculated according to Eqs. (8)–(13), are summarized in Table 4. As shown in Fig. 6(a), increasing relative density in uniform structures leads to a progressive enhancement in overall stress levels. This behavior is consistent with classical cellular material theory, where higher density corresponds to increased material volume available for load bearing. From Fig. 6(b), it is evident that different functionally graded configurations exhibit substantially different stress-strain responses. Although the G-Log and G-Sig structures follow distinct gradient trends, both possess relatively high overall densities, resulting in superior elastic modulus, yield strength, plateau stress, and energy absorption characteristics compared to other graded configurations. In contrast, the exponentially graded G-Exp structure, characterized by its lower relative density, exhibits comparatively reduced mechanical performance across all metrics.

A notable observation is the correlation between stress-strain curve evolution and the underlying wall thickness gradient distribution. Taking G-L1 and G-L2 as illustrative examples, Fig. 1(b) shows that G-L1 has thinner walls than G-L2 before the midpoint but becomes thicker after the midpoint. Correspondingly, the stress-strain curves reveal that G-L1 exhibits lower stress levels at small strains compared to G-L2, but surpasses G-L2 at a strain of approximately 0.35, maintaining marginally higher stress thereafter. This behavior demonstrates that the mechanical response of functionally graded structures can be systematically tuned through gradient profile design—a capability with direct implications for interior design applications where spatially varying mechanical properties may be required.

Among the five functionally graded configurations, G-Log exhibits the highest compressive strength, while G-Sig demonstrates superior elastic modulus, average plateau stress, and specific energy absorption. Notably, the specific energy absorption of G-Sig is approximately 45.07% higher than that of the uniform G-45 structure. In contrast, G-Exp shows the lowest specific energy absorption at 6.03 J/g. From an interior design perspective, these findings provide quantitative guidance for selecting appropriate gradient configurations based on specific functional requirements. For instance, the high compressive strength of G-Log makes it suitable for load-bearing applications such as structural columns or support walls, while the exceptional energy absorption capacity of G-Sig suggests potential applications in impact-resistant partitions or protective cladding in high-traffic public spaces. The relatively modest performance of G-Exp, combined with its distinct gradient characteristics, may find application in lightweight decorative elements where structural demands are secondary to aesthetic considerations. The deformation modes of functionally graded structures differ substantially from those of uniform structures. Figure 7 illustrates the deformation patterns of uniform G-45 and the five functionally graded structures at compressive strains of 15%, 30%, and 45%. Under quasi-static compression, the uniform G-45 structure exhibits mid-section expansion at 15% strain, followed by the formation of diagonal shear bands at 30% strain, and ultimately global densification with continued compression. In contrast, functionally graded structures, characterized by progressively increasing wall thickness along the loading direction, exhibit initial yielding at the contact end adjacent to the top plate. This behavior establishes a direct relationship between yield strength and the thinnest wall layer. As stress in the upper layers exceeds the yield strength of underlying layers, sequential layer-by-layer collapse occurs, accompanied by localized densification. This progressive failure mode is reflected in the stepwise increasing stress-strain curves observed for graded configurations.

Comparing the deformation modes of G-L1 and G-L2, the more pronounced gradient in G-L1 results in distinctly layered collapse behavior, as substantial thickness differences between adjacent layers necessitate greater compression of upper layers before lower layers yield. Conversely, G-L2, with its more gradual thickness variation, exhibits deformation patterns more closely resembling those of uniform G-45 structures. G-Log, G-Sig, and G-Exp structures all display macroscopically similar layer-by-layer collapse modes, but with notable distinctions. G-Log and G-Sig, featuring thicker walls at the contact end, demonstrate higher yield strengths, greater overall mass, and superior plateau stress and energy absorption compared to G-Exp. Although G-Log and G-Sig have comparable overall mass, differences in intermediate layer thickness distributions lead to distinct stress evolution trends during compression.

From an interior design perspective, these deformation characteristics offer valuable insights for application-specific design. The layer-by-layer collapse mode observed in functionally graded structures suggests potential applications in seismic-resistant partition walls or energy-absorbing backing panels, where controlled progressive failure can enhance occupant safety during extreme events. The relationship between gradient characteristics and deformation behavior enables designers to tune structural response for specific performance requirements. For instance, structures with pronounced gradients (such as G-L1) may be suitable for applications requiring clearly delineated zones of mechanical behavior, while more gradual gradients (such as G-L2) may be preferred when uniform deformation is desired. The enhanced densification strain and energy absorption associated with pronounced gradient distributions are particularly valuable in impact protection applications, where maximizing energy dissipation within limited space is critical. Furthermore, the aesthetic possibilities inherent in these deformation patterns—such as the visual expression of progressive collapse in exposed structural elements—offer opportunities for integrating mechanical functionality with architectural expression in contemporary interior design.

Dynamic mechanical response

In this study, finite element simulation was employed to investigate the dynamic mechanical response of both uniform and functionally graded Gyroid structures under impact loading. Due to inertial effects and structural compaction mechanisms, the mechanical behavior of cellular structures under dynamic impact differs substantially from that observed under quasi-static conditions, with corresponding changes in deformation patterns. To elucidate the influence of loading rate on the mechanical performance of uniform and functionally graded structures, finite element models were constructed for four impact velocities: 10, 40, 70, and 100 m/s. The model parameters were consistent with those described in Sect. 2.4.

Regarding the impact velocity range (10–100 m/s) used in this study, we acknowledge that 100 m/s represents an extreme condition rarely encountered in normal interior environments. However, this upper bound is selected to explore the mechanical limits of functionally graded TPMS structures and to provide a conservative safety margin for accidental impacts, such as those caused by falling equipment, vehicle collisions in parking structures integrated with interior spaces, or blast-related events in high-security buildings. Lower velocities (10–40 m/s) correspond to more realistic scenarios, such as a moving cart (5–10 m/s) or a person falling (3–6 m/s). The wide velocity range allows us to capture the transition from quasi-static to dynamic deformation modes. In Sect. 4, we have revised the application discussion to clearly distinguish between low-velocity impacts (10–40 m/s) relevant to daily interior use and high-velocity scenarios (70–100 m/s) representing extreme safety design cases.

Figure 8 presents the stress-strain curves for the uniform G-45 structure and the functionally graded configurations under dynamic impact. At impact velocities of 10 and 40 m/s, the stress-strain curves exhibit similar overall trends, with differences primarily confined to the initial peak stress region. As impact velocity increases, both the initial peak stress and the plateau stress level increase markedly. For functionally graded structures, the progressive increase in wall thickness along the loading direction introduces pronounced stepwise fluctuations in the stress response at elevated impact velocities. Under high-velocity impact, the stepwise stress characteristics in the plateau region are strongly correlated with the underlying wall thickness gradient distribution. Comparing the stress-strain curves of G-L1 and G-L2 at 100 m/s impact velocity, G-L1 exhibits distinct stepwise strengthening in the plateau region, reflecting its more pronounced gradient characteristics. Similarly, the stress evolution trends for G-Log and G-Sig structures correspond to their respective wall thickness growth patterns, while G-Exp demonstrates slow stress growth at small strains (ε < 0.35) followed by rapid stress increase until densification, mirroring its exponential thickness distribution.

From an interior design perspective, these dynamic response characteristics provide critical insights for applications in spaces where impact loads may occur. For instance, in high-traffic public environments such as museums, airports, or educational facilities, partition walls and structural elements may be subjected to accidental impacts from moving equipment or crowds. The ability of functionally graded structures to exhibit controlled, stepwise stress increases under dynamic loading suggests potential applications in impact-resistant cladding systems that can absorb energy progressively while maintaining structural integrity. Furthermore, the correlation between gradient profile and dynamic stress evolution enables designers to tailor structural response to specific impact scenarios, optimizing the balance between occupant safety and material efficiency.

To quantitatively compare the performance of functionally graded and uniform structures, key mechanical parameters were calculated according to Eqs. (9)-(13) and are presented in Fig. 9. Figure 9(a) shows the average plateau stress and densification strain for all configurations across the four impact velocities. Under dynamic impact, G-Log and G-Sig structures exhibit the highest average plateau stress values at 24.14 MPa, followed by the linear gradient structures G-L1 and G-L2, with G-45 showing slightly lower values than G-L2. The exponentially graded G-Exp structure demonstrates the lowest average plateau stress, reaching only 60.06% of the G-Sig value. However, due to its lower relative density, G-Exp exhibits larger densification strains compared to other configurations. This behavior is not solely due to density reduction; the exponential gradient profile also plays a crucial role. In G-Exp, the thinnest region near the impact end collapses first and densifies locally, while the thicker lower portion remains less deformed, allowing the structure to sustain larger global strains before full compaction. In contrast, the more uniform deformation in G-Sig leads to earlier global densification. Thus, the larger densification strain of G-Exp results from a combination of lower overall density, localization of deformation at the impact end, and the exponential wall thickness gradient that delays propagation of densification to the fixed end. This characteristic may be advantageous in applications requiring substantial deformation before structural compaction.

Figure 9(b) presents the energy absorption and specific energy absorption characteristics for all six structures. Energy absorption increases with impact velocity for all configurations, with G-Sig showing the most pronounced enhancement—from 171.919 J at 10 m/s to 398.497 J at 100 m/s, representing a 131.8% increase. Among all structures, G-Log and G-Sig demonstrate superior energy absorption due to their higher overall densities, while G-Exp exhibits the lowest absolute energy absorption at 294.64 J. However, owing to its lower mass, G-Exp achieves the highest specific energy absorption under high-velocity impact at 24.171 J/g, representing an approximately 10% improvement over the uniform G-45 structure.

In addition to plateau stress and energy absorption, other crashworthiness indicators are relevant for interior impact protection. For the G-Sig structure at 100 m/s impact, the initial peak stress reaches 32.5 MPa, while the average plateau stress is 24.1 MPa, yielding a crushing force efficiency (CFE = plateau stress / peak stress) of 0.74. For G-Exp, the CFE is 0.68. These values indicate that the graded structures, particularly G-Sig, provide relatively low force transmission during impact while maintaining high energy absorption—a desirable characteristic for protective interior elements such as wall cladding in high-traffic areas. For comparison, uniform G-45 exhibits a lower CFE of 0.58 due to its sharper initial peak. The deformation uniformity, as shown in Fig. 10, also demonstrates that G-Sig achieves more progressive crushing with less force fluctuation. These metrics further support the suitability of sigmoid and exponential gradients for impact-resistant interior applications.

These energy absorption characteristics have direct implications for interior design applications. The exceptional specific energy absorption of G-Exp under high-velocity impact makes it particularly suitable for lightweight protective elements where mass reduction is critical, such as movable partition systems, temporary exhibition structures, or retrofittable impact barriers. The substantial energy absorption capacity of G-Sig and G-Log under dynamic loading suggests applications in permanent protective installations, such as impact-resistant wall linings in sports facilities, children’s play areas, or corridors subject to equipment movement. The ability to achieve different combinations of energy absorption and mass efficiency through gradient selection provides designers with a versatile toolkit for addressing diverse spatial and safety requirements.

Impact velocity not only influences mechanical properties but also affects deformation patterns. Figure 10 illustrates the deformation behavior of G-45, G-Sig, and G-Exp structures at 15%, 30%, and 45% strain under impact velocities of 10 m/s and 100 m/s. Due to inertial effects, G-45 exhibits pronounced localized crushing at the contact end under 100 m/s impact, distinctly different from the mid-section expansion observed at 10 m/s. For functionally graded structures G-Sig and G-Exp, the progressively increasing wall thickness along the loading direction results in initial crushing at the thinner loaded end under 10 m/s impact, with global yielding achieved by 45% strain. Under 100 m/s impact, G-Sig shows enhanced localized crushing due to inertial effects, with the lower portion of the structure remaining largely undeformed at 45% strain. G-Exp, characterized by exponential thickness growth, exhibits even more pronounced localized densification at the contact end compared to G-45 and G-Sig. Notably, at 10 m/s impact and 45% strain, G-Sig demonstrates global compressive deformation, while G-Exp retains significant undeformed material at the bottom (indicated by red dashed boxes in Fig. 10).

From an interior design perspective, these deformation patterns offer both functional and aesthetic possibilities. The localized crushing behavior observed under high-velocity impact suggests applications in sacrificial protective layers or energy-absorbing zones that can be designed as replaceable elements, facilitating maintenance after impact events. The differential deformation between G-Sig and G-Exp structures under identical loading conditions demonstrates how gradient selection can be used to tune failure localization—enabling designs where damage is confined to specific, accessible regions rather than propagating throughout the structure. Furthermore, the visual expression of progressive deformation in transparent or semi-transparent TPMS materials could be exploited aesthetically, creating interior elements that visually communicate structural response to loading—a concept aligned with contemporary trends in expressive structural design and material honesty in architecture.

In contrast, the G-Sig structure exhibits a more progressive energy dissipation profile, with the top 30% absorbing only 42% of the energy at the same global strain, owing to its smoother sigmoidal transition. These quantitative indicators provide direct guidance for interior design: for sacrificial impact zones (e.g., baseboards or corner protectors in high-traffic areas), the exponential gradient can be deliberately chosen to localize damage to a replaceable layer; for uniform load distribution in structural partitions, the sigmoid gradient offers balanced deformation. Thus, simulation-based quantitative data enable performance-driven gradient selection in real interior environments.

Exploration of TPMS applications in interior design

This study explores how triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS) can effectively function both as load-bearing structural systems and innovative decorative design elements in interior spatial design. Exhibition and display spaces, as a common and significant type of interior and exhibition design, require material selection strategies that balance lightweight, strength, and environmental sustainability based on budget, construction timelines, and display objectives, achieving an equilibrium among functionality, aesthetics, and efficiency. Currently, mainstream construction materials include metal profiles (e.g., TRUSS structures), engineered wood boards (e.g., PVC foam boards, fireproof boards), and surface finishing materials (e.g., polypropylene synthetic paper, lightbox film/fabric). However, these materials present several shortcomings: First, they lack environmental sustainability and eco-friendliness. A large number of temporary wooden structures are discarded as waste after exhibitions, leading to resource depletion and environmental pollution. Additionally, wood-based engineered boards release harmful gases such as formaldehyde during processing and use. Second, economic and cost pressures are significant. Customized wooden exhibition stands are unique in design but expensive and non-reusable, resulting in high long-term costs. Third, the leasing, transportation, and storage of recyclable materials are costly, leading many enterprises to forgo such options due to budget constraints. Fourth, design and functional limitations persist. Although environmentally friendly materials like metal profiles are recyclable, they lack flexibility in achieving complex and organic forms, thereby restricting design creativity.

To quantitatively link the mechanical performance with interior design requirements, the key metrics of graded Gyroid structures are compared with conventional interior materials. The specific energy absorption of the G-Sig structure under quasi-static compression reaches 13.01 J/g, which is approximately 45% higher than that of uniform G-45 structures and significantly exceeds the energy absorption capacity of conventional wooden boards and metal foams at equivalent mass1,11. Under dynamic impact at 100 m/s, the G-Exp structure achieves a specific energy absorption of 24.17 J/g, representing a 10% improvement over uniform G-45 structures and outperforming many traditional impact-resistant materials in lightweight applications. These quantitative comparisons indicate that while conventional materials (solid wood, aluminum) offer higher absolute load-bearing capacity, functionally graded TPMS structures provide a unique combination of lightweight (mass reduction of 40–75% compared to wood) and controlled progressive failure, making them particularly suitable for applications where weight reduction and energy dissipation are critical, such as movable partitions, temporary exhibition modules, and impact-resistant cladding.

The following case study focuses on an exhibition space measuring 6000 × 12,000 × 4000 mm, covering an area of 72 m2. In this design proposal, the supporting columns feature curved, irregular geometries (Fig. 12). Traditional construction methods typically involve building internal support structures with wooden keels and cladding them with decorative wood panels. This approach requires extensive materials, involves complex and labor-intensive processes, and results in materials that cannot be reused after dismantling. The mechanical properties of Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS) structures offer superior load-bearing capacity. Leveraging this characteristic, TPMS can replace traditional support structures such as columns, ceilings, and walls, while also reducing structural volume. Additionally, TPMS structures are lightweight and convenient to fabricate. Compared to conventional construction materials, they significantly reduce material usage and weight, lowering transportation and labor costs, shortening construction timelines, and improving construction efficiency. Furthermore, TPMS can be fabricated using 3D printing technology, either as integrated monolithic structures or as modular components. Monolithic printing enables the creation of custom-shaped walls or columns with intricate detailing, tailored to specific design requirements (Fig. 13). Modular designs facilitate on-site assembly and disassembly, allowing for repeated use. Unlike traditional materials, which are typically discarded after dismantling, modular TPMS components can be recycled and reused multiple times, reducing construction costs and environmental impact (Fig. 14).

From an aesthetic perspective, columns and walls in interior spaces often act as spatial dividers, creating visual barriers that can make spaces feel confined and enclosed. In contrast, the solid–void configuration of TPMS structures generates a layered sense of “virtual and real” interplay. Light can penetrate through the voids, freely traversing the structure, thereby breaking the physical separation imposed by columns and walls and enhancing spatial transparency. Moreover, the periodic nature of TPMS translates into a distinct sense of rhythm and sequence in column design. When multiple TPMS structural columns are arranged in a regular pattern within a space, they form a visually rhythmic sequence that is not static but continuously transforms as viewers move through the space. This dynamic and flowing spatial atmosphere contributes a unique aesthetic experience to the interior environment.

Although this study focuses on the Gyroid topology, the functionally graded design concept and the mechanical insights derived are expected to extend to other TPMS architectures, such as Diamond and Schwarz P (Primitive). From a mechanical perspective, Diamond structures exhibit higher stiffness-to-density ratios due to their nodal connectivity, while Schwarz P structures offer isotropic deformation under compression11. Our preliminary finite element simulations (data not shown for brevity) suggest that functionally graded Diamond structures would achieve even higher specific energy absorption than Gyroid under the same gradient profile, whereas graded Schwarz P structures provide more uniform stress distribution, making them suitable for large-span ceiling panels. From an interior design standpoint, each TPMS topology offers distinct aesthetic expressions: Gyroid generates continuous, flowing organic voids ideal for translucent screens; Diamond produces faceted, crystalline patterns suitable for modern minimalist partitions; Schwarz P creates regular cubic perforations that can be used for acoustic panels with predictable perforation ratios. The present methodology—including the iso-stress model, R-PP-L dynamic prediction, and gradient design equations—is directly transferable to these structures. Furthermore, the effect of loading direction on the mechanical response of functionally graded structures deserves attention. In the present study, all graded specimens were loaded with the thinner end compressed, representing the most common scenario in interior applications (top less loaded, base more loaded). If loaded from the opposite direction (thicker end compressed), based on literature reports on functionally graded foams and TPMS structures21,23, the initial yield strength would increase due to higher local stiffness at the contact interface, but the energy absorption would decrease because the progressive layer-by-layer collapse would be replaced by a less stable global shear banding mode. A systematic experimental and numerical investigation of direction-dependent behavior is planned as future work. For interior design applications where structural elements may experience loads from multiple directions (e.g., lateral impact on a wall), symmetric gradient designs—increasing thickness from both ends toward the mid-height—would be more appropriate, and this represents another promising research direction. A systematic comparative study of functionally graded Diamond and Schwarz P architectures for interior applications is a promising direction for future work.

Through quasi-static compression experiments and finite element simulations at four impact velocities (10, 40, 70, and 100 m/s), the influence of loading regime and wall thickness distribution on key mechanical parameters, including average plateau stress and energy absorption characteristics, was comprehensively analyzed. Although G-Exp exhibits relatively lower absolute energy absorption due to its reduced overall density, it achieves superior specific energy absorption at 24.171 J/g—an approximately 10% improvement over G-45—highlighting its efficiency in weight-sensitive applications.

In summary, this study establishes a comprehensive understanding of how linear and nonlinear gradient distributions influence the quasi-static and dynamic mechanical behavior of Gyroid TPMS structures. The integration of experimental validation, finite element simulation, and analytical modeling provides a robust foundation for the rational design of functionally graded TPMS structures. For interior design applications, these findings demonstrate that functionally graded TPMS structures can serve as versatile architectural elements that simultaneously address structural requirements, spatial articulation, and aesthetic expression. By enabling precise tuning of mechanical response through gradient selection, this research opens new possibilities for the development of innovative, safe, and materially efficient interior systems that respond to the diverse demands of contemporary built environments. Future work should systematically investigate the effect of reverse loading direction on graded TPMS structures, as well as symmetric gradient designs for multidirectional load scenarios.
