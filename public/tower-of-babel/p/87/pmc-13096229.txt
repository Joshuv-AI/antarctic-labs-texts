Development and validation of a risk classification integrating the location index to predict renal function after robotic partial nephrectomy
Authors: Haruyuki Ohsugi, Junichi Ikeda, Kenta Takayasu, Hisanori Taniguchi, Masaaki Yanishi, Hidefumi Kinoshita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNephrometry scoring systems are used to predict the surgical complexity of partial nephrectomy (PN) but are insufficient to predict renal function after robotic PN (RPN). The study aimed to calculate a new location factor for predicting postoperative renal function and develop a classification including the location factor. We calculated a location index (L-index) and verified an optimal cutoff value to predict renal function after RPN in 163 patients (development cohort). Then, we developed a new classification with the L-index and validated it in 127 patients (external validation cohort). The primary endpoint was an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) reduction of ≥ 20% from baseline to 6 months after RPN. This outcome occurred in 24 patients (14.7%) in the development cohort and 28 patients (22.0%) in the external validation cohort. The accuracy for predicting the endpoint was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The L-index cutoff values were ≤ 15 and ≤ 30 mm. Using the L-index and tumor volume, we developed the LIVED (L-index and volume for prediction eGFR decline) classification dividing patients into three groups. The classification showed a high AUC compared to other nephrometry scoring systems (AUC = 0.858 vs. 0.674–0.744) in a validation cohort. Introduction

Surgery remains the only curative treatment for non-metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC). Partial nephrectomy (PN) is recommended for managing localized small RCC according to renal function and oncological outcome1. Robotic PN (RPN) provides better perioperative outcomes than open PN, such as fewer major complications and shorter hospital stay2. Additionally, recent meta-analyses also have shown that the warm ischemia time (WIT) is significantly shorter in RPN than in laparoscopic PN (LPN)3.

Nephrometry scoring systems are used for predicting surgical complexity and potential perioperative morbidity before PN4–6. Although whether these nephrometry scoring systems help predict loss of renal function after PN is still controversial7, several factors are correlated with postoperative renal function. The R.E.N.A.L. score is among the most commonly used nephrometry scoring systems, including several factors such as R (tumor radius), E (endophytic nature), N (nearness of the tumor to the collecting system), A (tumor located anterior or posterior), and L (tumor location relative to the polar lines). For instance, Watts et al. showed that the R and the E factors in the R.E.N.A.L. score are associated with a significant decrease in split renal function in LPN or RPN8. Meanwhile, we previously reported that tumor crossing of the axial renal midline, which was part of the L factor in the R.E.N.A.L., and tumor volume substituted for R factor were independent preoperative factors in predicting renal function after RPN9. Unfortunately, these findings were not validated sufficiently and are premature for use in daily clinical practice. We also considered that the L factor could be modified and quantified as a location factor for predicting renal function.

Therefore, this study aimed at (1) calculating a new location factor to improve predictive ability and (2) developing a simple risk classification tool for predicting postoperative renal function. Finally, the accuracy of the new risk classification in predicting postoperative renal function was validated by comparing it with other nephrometry scoring systems in the temporal external validation cohort.

Development of a new risk classification system

A risk classification system was developed by adding the L-index with an appropriate cutoff value to the tumor volume (≥ 14.11 cm3), which was an independent predictor of the primary endpoint in the previous report9. The risk classification system, named the LIVED (L-index and volume for prediction eGFR decline) classification, could divide patients into 3 groups (high-, moderate-, or low-risk; Table 2).

External validation of the LIVED classification system

Table 3 lists the clinical characteristics of 127 patients in the external validation cohort. The primary outcome occurred in 28 patients (22.0%). Patients in the external validation were divided into three groups by the LIVED classification (Supplemental Fig. 1). We analyzed the accuracy of it in the external validation cohort, to ascertain whether the LIVED classification was useful in predicting postoperative renal function. The LIVED classification showed a higher AUC than the C-index and other nephrometry scoring systems, such as the R.E.N.A.L., PADUA, and DAP scores (AUC = 0.858 vs. 0.674–0.744) (Fig. 2a). Additionally, we compared an eGFR decline rate from baseline to 6 months after RPN according to the LIVED classification. The eGFR decline rate was significantly higher in the moderate-risk group than in the low-risk group and in the high-risk group than in the moderate-risk group (P = 0.006 and P = 0.043, respectively) (Fig. 2b).

Discussion

The major urological guidelines recommend PN as a standard treatment for localized clinical T1 RCCs1,10. PN is a nephron-sparing surgery aimed to preserve renal function after surgery; thus, predicting a postoperative decline in renal function is crucial. Despite several models predicting renal function after non-metastatic renal surgery, most of them are not externally validated, especially in PN11. Hence, we developed a simple risk classification for predicting renal function following RPN and confirmed its availability with external validation.

The LIVED classification developed in this study incorporates two factors: the L-index and tumor volume. The L-index was defined as the distance from the axial renal midline to the tumor center plus the nearest distance from the renal calyx to the tumor. It was inspired by N and L factors in the R.E.N.A.L. and quantified instead of these factors. The centrality index (C-index), as a quantified factor, is a reported morphological descriptor of renal masses that incorporates the size and location of the tumor12. The C-index is associated with eGFR decrease percent after LPN and is considered one of the most important preoperative factors13. In this study, the accuracy of the C-index for predicting renal function after RPN was high (AUC = 0.726), while the accuracy of the LIVED classification was even higher in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.858). The differences in the patient background (LPN and RPN settings) might influenced these findings. Moreover, the LIVED classification was based on the separate evaluation of two factors, which have suspected to contribute to led to this result. When discussing the location factor expressed as the L-index, the way in which renal blood flows from the renal artery supply to the kidney should be understood. Almost all renal arteries radiate out from the renal hilum, with segmentary arteries or interlobar arteries flowing to each segment14. Ligation of these arteries is required for the partial resection of the tumor located in the center of the kidney, which might cause irreversible ischemia to the area supplied by the vessels and subsequent partial renal infarction. Recent studies have identified the contact surface area (CSA) of the tumor with adjacent renal parenchyma as a preoperative factor for predicting PN outcome, such as operative time, complications, and renal function15,16. The CSA is calculated from the total surface area (TSA) and tends to increase as the tumor size increases, implying that the CSA is a size factor. The CSA is actually correlated with tumor size (R = 0.82)17. However, the CSA is calculated by multiplying TSA with the parenchymal component percentage. Thus, the relative location factor between the kidney and the tumor is involved in the CSA; hence, the CSA is influenced by the size factor. Therefore, the factor to be added to the L-index was the tumor volume (≥ 14.1 cm3), a pure size factor and an independent predictor of the renal function decline after RPN9.

Despite these novel findings, our study should be interpreted with caution because of several limitations. First, the study was a retrospective analysis using a prospectively maintained database, and no statistical power calculations were conducted. Therefore, the possibility of selection bias could not be ruled out entirely. Second, RPN was performed by different surgeons, which could introduce variability in surgical outcomes. Third, as the surgeries were conducted by expert surgeons at a single high-volume center in Japan, caution should be exercised when applying these results to lower-volume centers or non-Asian populations. Fourth, the accuracy of the new classification was compared with nephrometry scoring systems developed in the LPN era for predicting tumor complexity. Finally, the primary outcome in the present study was defined as an eGFR reduction of ≥ 20% from baseline to 6 months after RPN to avoid missing a clinically important decline in renal function. Some reports used upstaging of chronic kidney disease in terms of the eGFR to assess renal function after PN, but this definition might overestimate patients with preoperative renal function in the borderline region17. On the other hand, an eGFR reduction of ≥ 10% from baseline, which was occasionally incorporated into one of the trifecta in PN, might be utilized for evaluating postoperative renal function. The achievement rate under this definition was about ≤ 50% in patients with RPN18. The definition was deemed too strict to select patients with clinically significant renal dysfunction. Thus, the eGFR reduction of ≥ 20% from baseline was adopted for the primary outcome in the present study. Further, the LIVED classification was not only a powerful predictor of the primary outcome, but also a potential classification tool to predict a continuous variable, the eGFR decline rate, after RPN in the external validation cohort (Fig. 2b).

Conclusions

The LIVED classification using the L-index, quantified as a location factor, and tumor volume showed a high accuracy for predicting postoperative eGFR decline after RPN. The new classification tool validated by external validation might contribute to recently developed nephrometry scoring systems in the RPN setting.

Methods

Patient population (development cohort)

The development cohort comprised the patient population from our previous report9. The medical records of 163 patients who underwent RPN for localized cT1 renal tumors with warm ischemia at the Kansai Medical University Hospital between August 2014 and December 2019 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients with multiple renal tumors or a solitary kidney were excluded. Patients whose renal function could not be assessed at 6 months postoperatively or whose procedure was changed were also excluded. All operations were performed by experienced urological robotic surgeons using the da Vinci-Si surgical system (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Covariates and outcomes

Demographic data included sex, age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension (HTN) as comorbidities, previous abdominal surgery, surgical approach, surgical side, perioperative outcomes (WIT and estimated blood loss), and perioperative renal function (assessed by serum creatinine [SCr] and estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] established for the Japanese population19. The renal function reduction percent was calculated using the preoperative and postoperative (6 months after RPN) eGFRs. All patients underwent preoperative three-dimensional computed tomography with contrast or magnetic resonance imaging without contrast. Based on the imaging findings, nephrometry scoring systems, including R.E.N.A.L., preoperative aspects and dimensions used for anatomic (PADUA), and diameter-axial-polar (DAP) scores, were evaluated with several urologists at a preoperative conference. The P factor in the DAP showed the polar distance from the middle plane to the closest tumor edge, with 1 point for > 2 cm, 2 points for ≤ 2 cm, and 3 points for the tumor crossing of the axial renal midline, which was used for comparison with the new location factors quantified. The tumor volume was calculated assuming that the tumor is an ellipsoid as shown in the previous report9. The centrality index (C-index) was also calculated with reference to the original report by two observers (HO and KT)12.

Locational index

A location index (L-index) was calculated as location factor to numerically evaluate the locational relation between a kidney and a tumor. The L-index was defined as the distance from the axial renal midline to the tumor center plus the nearest distance from the renal calyx to the tumor (Fig. 3). The distance from the axial renal midline to the tumor center was measured in the coronal plane, setting the middle of the renal pelvis as the axial renal midline. The nearest distance from the renal calyx to the tumor was measured at the transverse, coronal, or sagittal plane. These lengths of the distance were measured independently by two observers (HO and JI), each of whom was blinded to the clinical outcome. In cases where discrepancies were observed between the two observers, the average of their measurements was used.

The location index (L-index) is calculated by adding the distance from the axial renal midline to the tumor center (I) and the nearest distance from the renal calyx to the tumor (II). (a) Schematic diagram. (b) Representative three-dimensional computed tomography in the coronal plane.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this study was an eGFR reduction of ≥ 20% from baseline to 6 months after RPN. We selected ≥ 20% reduction in eGFR as the clinically relevant threshold, as it is considered a more significant marker of renal dysfunction compared with other potential cut-offs, such as chronic kidney disease upstaging or a ≥ 10% reduction in eGFR. All continuous data are shown as median values and interquartile ranges (IQR). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to decide the cutoff value for continuous variables, including the L-index. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The Kruskal-Wallis test with the Steel post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons of continuous variables in three groups. The abilities of the new classification and previous nephrometry scoring systems for predicting renal function after RPN were compared using an AUC analysis in the external validation cohort. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi, Japan), with a P-value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance20.

External validation cohort

We developed a prediction model to predict renal function after RPN in the developmental cohort. Afterward, we performed external validation to evaluate the predictive ability of the model using the AUC. Criteria for patient eligibility in the temporal external validation cohort were the same as those used for the development cohort, selecting 127 patients treated with RPN at the Kansai Medical University Hospital between January 2020 and December 2022.
