Hybrid metallic nanozyme with nitric oxide-releasing photothermal coating for accelerated infected diabetic wound healing
Authors: Sri Ganga Padaga, Milan Paul, Tonmoy Banerjee, Saptami Goswami, Balaram Ghosh, Swati Biswas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfected diabetic wounds are prone to developing bacterial biofilms and are difficult to treat due to a lack of strategies that can eliminate drug-resistant bacteria. Conventional antibiotics can’t achieve the desired antibacterial effect due to their limited penetration into the biofilm, which makes the treatment challenging. In this study, we developed NIR-responsive nitric oxide (NO) releasing Ce: Zn nanoflowers (PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs) to combat the drug-resistant bacteria by the synergistic antibacterial effect of metal ions and photothermal effect. PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs exhibited significant antibacterial and antibiofilm effects against Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Staphylococcus aureus (SA). The nanoflowers exhibited significant inhibitory effects on the virulence of MRSA and SA, including spreading motility, secretion of phenol-soluble modulin proteins, and staphyloxanthin, after laser irradiation. The PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs were able to cause membrane disruption and eradicate the MRSA and SA biofilms that were analyzed by scanning electron microscope. Additionally, these nanoflowers significantly accelerated wound healing in MRSA-infected diabetic rats by reducing the inflammation and promoting angiogenesis at the wound site. Introduction

Diabetic wound healing remains a critical challenge worldwide. The impairment of diabetic wound healing is due to the inhibition of angiogenesis, inadequate supply of oxygen to the wound site, and microvascular damage [62]. The hyperglycemic condition of diabetes contributes to the development of vascular issues [17]. The high blood glucose levels in the diabetic condition cause vascular complications, including dysfunction of endothelial cells [59], prevent the nutrient supply to the wound site, hinder reepithelialization by reducing the migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, and also decrease the activity of antioxidants that result in oxidative stress. The elevated ROS levels at the wound region restrict angiogenesis.

Diabetic wounds are more sensitive to bacterial infections. The formation of biofilm contributes to the hindrance of healing in diabetic wounds. Infection of diabetic wounds increases the risk of amputation of lower limbs [5]. The biofilm formation at the wound site is one of the crucial virulence traits of S. aureus. After arriving at the wound site, the bacteria adhere to the wound surface, aided by the presence of proteins and exposed tissues, and colonize into microcolonies. Bacteria utilize a communication system called quorum sensing in which they secrete and detect signaling molecules that regulate the expression of several genes and coordinate the behavior of bacterial communities. These biofilms are more resistant to antibiotics because of the presence of an exopolysaccharide matrix that hinders the entry of antimicrobial agents, reaching the embedded bacteria inside the matrix. Oral or systemic administration of antibiotics is commonly used to manage acute wounds [3, 22], and chronic wounds are typically treated by the topical administration of antibiotics, which helps to prevent the spreading infection to the deeper tissues. The prolonged use of antibiotics causes the bacteria to develop resistance, which makes the treatment ineffective. 70% of bacteria causing wound infection are resistant to at least one of the commonly used antibiotics [31].

Exogenous administration of nitric oxide (NO) is a promising antibacterial treatment strategy due to its ability to diffuse and kill bacterial biofilms while minimizing the risk of resistance development [35, 57]. NO exhibits a broad spectrum of activity against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, including Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), due to its reactive byproducts such as peroxynitrite (ONOO−) and dinitrogen trioxide (N2O3) that cause oxidative damage of bacterial membrane by lipid peroxidation [15, 25]. Various sustained-releasing nitric oxide-releasing systems have been developed to enhance the therapeutic efficacy and minimize side effects, demonstrating potent antibacterial and antibiofilm effects [12, 19].

The use of metal nanoformulations as carriers gained increasing attention for antimicrobial applications to overcome the challenges associated with conventional therapy [26]. The shape of nanoparticles has a significant influence on the interaction with bacterial membranes. The sharp and pointed nanoparticles can cause leakage of cytoplasm by puncturing the cell wall of bacteria [65] [40]. Among these, nanoflowers are a type of nanoparticles that provide unique properties like smaller size and high surface-to-volume ratio with good stability [49]. These nanoflowers are also termed nanozymes because of their distinct ability to mimic the catalytic activity of enzymes like oxidase and peroxidase that play a crucial role in maintaining oxidative balance, oxygen levels, and infection control at the wound site [39]. The catalytic and antibacterial properties of metal oxides are mainly dependent on the surface morphology and their dispersity. The combination of unique properties of different metals for the fabrication of nanoflowers enables the utilization of properties of each metal and offers a synergistic effect in managing bacterial infection, promoting angiogenesis, and accelerating wound healing. Among different metal oxides, zinc oxide (ZnO) and cerium oxide (CeO2) nanocomposites offer unique advantages, including tunable physical and chemical properties, thermal stability, and biocompatibility [21, 45]. CeO₂ introduces oxygen vacancies and mixed valence states (Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺), which facilitate redox cycling, making it ideal for photocatalytic and biomedical applications. ZnO nanoflower petals promote bacterial adhesion and provide more active sites for drug loading or surface functionalization.

Physical stimulation-based antimicrobial nanomaterials exhibit highly effective antibacterial activity and reduce the risk of the development of bacterial drug resistance [16]. In recent years, stimuli-responsive polymer-based hydrogels and hydrogel adsorbents were developed for various applications, including targeted drug delivery [60] and wastewater treatment [61]. Light-sensitive, specifically near-infrared (NIR) -assisted antibacterial therapies, such as photothermal therapy (PTT), are the best strategy because of their minimally invasive nature, localized treatment, safety, and ease of operation [7, 48]. PTT, in contrast to traditional antibiotics, utilizes heat energy to induce bacterial death by damaging the outer membrane and increasing the permeability [55]. Metal nanoparticles coated with photothermal agents have been explored as effective antibacterial agents in the treatment of infected wounds [32].

Photothermal agents can transform light energy into heat energy that can effectively eradicate bacterial biofilms. PDA has become a versatile material that can be easily coated on different surfaces because of its inherent adhesive properties [27, 50]. A combination of PTT with other antimicrobial approaches has been developed to improve the applications. Among these, gas-releasing systems like nitric oxide (NO) are potential approaches because of their unique properties like biocompatibility, tissue penetration, and antibacterial effects. Additionally, the combination of PTT with NO enhances the migration of fibroblasts and promotes angiogenesis, which helps in the healing of infected wounds [30].

Here, we demonstrated the fabrication of ceria zinc nanoflowers by the hydrothermal method, and the surface of nanoflowers was coated with PDA to increase their photothermal conversion ability and further modified with SNP as a NO-releasing source. The developed nanoflower system can overcome the challenges associated with conventional antibiotic administration by exhibiting nitric oxide-mediated antibacterial and angiogenesis effects at the wound site. The therapeutic efficacy of the nitric oxide-releasing ceria zinc nanoflowers has been proven in vitro against Staphylococcus aureus (SA) and Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in diabetic rat wound models infected with MRSA.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cerium (III) nitrate hexahydrate (Mw- 434.22) was purchased from Himedia Labs (India). Zinc acetate dihydrate was procured from Avra Synthesis Private Limited, Hyderabad. SYTO9 and propidium iodide (PI) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Griess reagent was procured from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Wistar female rats (200–300 g) were procured from Vyas Labs, Hyderabad. Fertile chicken eggs after 4 days of incubation were purchased from the Indian Council of Agricultural Research – Directorate of Poultry Research, Hyderabad. The animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee (BITS-HYD-IAEC-2024-019), BITS-Pilani Hyderabad, India. The animals were maintained in a temperature range of 19–25℃ and a relative humidity of 50–60%.

Staphylococcus aureus was kindly provided by L.V.Prasad Eye Institute, Hyderabad. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus was procured from ATCC (33591). The bacterial cultures were grown in aerobic conditions at 37℃. TSB broth is used to grow the bacterial cultures, and the bacterial density was adjusted by measuring the optical density at 600 nm.

Methods

Synthesis of ceria zinc nanoflowers

Ceria Zinc nanoflowers in an equimolar ratio were synthesized using the hydrothermal method. Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2.2H2O) and citric acid were dissolved in a mixture of ethanol and water and kept under vigorous stirring for 1 h for homogenization. The pH of the solution was adjusted to alkaline by adding NaOH dropwise under stirring conditions. After stirring for 30 min, the homogeneous solution was transferred into a stainless steel autoclave bottle and Heated at 150℃ for 17 h in a Muffle furnace. Then, the solution was allowed to cool to room temperature and centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 15 min. The pellet was washed and kept drying for 12 h at 80℃. The SNP was loaded into the nanoflowers by overnight stirring, and the samples were centrifuged and washed with ethanol and water. The samples were dried at 40℃ for 10 h.

Synthesis of PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs

Initially, Ce:Zn NFs (200 mg) were added in 2 mL of Tris buffer (pH 8.5) and sonicated for 5 min. Dopamine hydrochloride (40 mg) was added to the above solution and kept in magnetic stirring (500 rpm) for 12 h at room temperature. The PDA-coated Ce:Zn NFs (PDA@Ce:Zn NFs) were collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with water, and dried. 100 mg of PDA@Ce:Zn NFs were added to the aqueous solution of SNP (0.1 M, 20mL) and kept stirring for 24 h in dark conditions. The resulting dispersion was centrifuged, washed, and kept in a vacuum dryer for 12 h.

Physicochemical characterization

The synthesized nanoflowers were physicochemically characterized by different techniques. For SEM analysis, the samples were prepared by dropping the aqueous dispersions of different nanoflowers on the glass substrates and kept for drying. The coverslips were placed on the carbon tape attached to the stubs, and the samples were sputter-coated with gold before analysis. The presence of elements in the Ce:Zn NFs and modified Ce:Zn NFs was analyzed by EDAX analysis and elemental mapping. The elemental composition is further confirmed by XPS analysis. The absorption spectra of SNP, SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, PDA@Ce:Zn NFs, and PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs were measured by using a UV spectrophotometer. The particle size and zeta potential of nanoflowers were determined by DLS. The functional groups present in the coated and uncoated nanoflowers were detected by FTIR analysis. The nature of nanoflowers was analyzed by ULTIMA-IV, a Rigaku X-ray diffractometer. The analysis was carried out in the range from 250 to 700.

Evaluation of in vitro photothermal effect

The photothermal effect of nanoflowers after irradiation with a laser (808 nm) was evaluated. The temperature changes in PBS, SNP, SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, PDA@Ce:Zn NFs, and PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs dispersions at a concentration (25 µg/mL) were evaluated after laser irradiation for 5 min. Different concentrations of PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 µg/mL) dispersions in PBS were prepared, and the temperature changes were recorded after laser irradiation. The power of the laser was adjusted to 1 W/cm2 at 808 nm, and the samples were irradiated for 5 min. PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs (25 µg/mL) solution was prepared and exposed to different laser powers (0, 0.5, 1, 2 W/cm2) to evaluate the effect of laser power density on the photothermal heating effect. The photothermal images of the suspensions were captured using an IR camera at 30s intervals.

Nitric oxide (NO) release assay

At first, the calibration curve of NO was prepared by accurately diluting 1 M NaNO2 to 5 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 40 µM, and 60 µM. 1mLof the prepared NaNO2 dilutions was added with 1 mL of Griess reagent in a 1:1 ratio, incubated for 10 min, and the absorbances of the mixtures were evaluated at 540 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. The concentrations of NaNO2 were plotted on the X-axis and the absorbance on the Y-axis. The standard curve was plotted and utilized for the determination NO release study. NO release from PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs with and without laser was detected using the Griess reagent. The release of NO was indirectly quantified to generate a characteristic red-pink colour. Firstly, 2 mL of 1 mg/mL suspension of PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs was taken and irradiated at 1 W/cm2 power using an 808 nm laser. Then, 1 mL of samples were collected at defined time intervals (0.5,1,2,4,6,8,12,48 h) and replaced by 1mL of PBS 7.4 to maintain sink condition under intermittent laser irradiation.1 mL of Griess reagent was added to 1 mL of the collected samples and incubated for 10 min to develop the colour. The absorbance of the mixture was then measured at 540 nm using a UV -vis spectrophotometer. Further, the release kinetics were analysed by adjusting the collected data into the zero-order, Higuchi, first-order, Korsmeyer Peppas, and Hixson Crowell mathematical models to explore the release behavior of NO from the formulations. The experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are reported (mean ± SD).

Loading stability assessment of SNP

The loading stability of SNP was determined by performing the stability study of the freshly prepared batches of PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs at different temperature conditions, 4 ℃ and room temperature (RT). The samples were withdrawn, replenished with an equal volume of freshly prepared buffer, and drug loading was analyzed at different time points (1, 3, and 7 days).

Catalytic activity

To examine the oxidase-like activity of synthesized nanoflowers, TMB was used as a substrate for oxidation. The nanoflowers were dispersed in PBS, added with TMB, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The absorption spectra of the samples were analyzed by UV spectroscopy. The evaluation of the Peroxidase (POD) like catalytic activity efficiency of Ce:Zn NFs, SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, PDA@Ce:Zn NFs, and PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs was determined by the evaluation of Michaelis-Menten parameters (Km and Vmax), using 100 µg/mL nanozyme dispersions and varying concentrations of TMB substrate in the presence of a fixed concentration of H2O2. In brief, nanozymes were incubated with varying concentrations of TMB solutions (0.01-1mM) in citrate buffer (pH 6.6) at a final volume of 200 µL, and the reactions were initiated by the addition of H2O2, and the absorption spectra were recorded. The initial reaction rates were determined based on the linear part of the absorbance-time profile at each TMB concentration. The Michaelis-Menten plots were constructed as initial reaction rates versus substrate concentration, whereas double-reciprocal Lineweaver–Burk plots were employed to calculate Km and Vmax.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of nanoflowers on the mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line NIH3T3 was evaluated by MTT assay. In brief, the NIH3T3 cells were homogeneously dispersed and seeded in the wells of a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in DMEM complete growth media and incubated overnight. The cells were treated with different concentrations of nanoflower formulations (1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.3, 15.6, 7.8, 3.9, 1.9 µg/mL) with and without laser exposure and incubated for 24 h and 48 h. Following incubation, the supernatants were removed, and the cells were incubated with MTT solution (50 mg/mL in PBS) and further incubated for 4 h, followed by dissolving the formazan crystals in DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 590 nm (reference wavelength at 630 nm). The % cell viability was calculated by the following formula.

In vitro wound closure by scratch assay

The in vitro wound healing potential of nanoflowers was evaluated by using NIH3T3 cells and Human keratinocytes (HaCaT). Both the cells were grown separately in DMEM media in the wells of a 96-well plate until 90% confluency was reached. Then the media was removed, and the monolayer of cells was scratched with a sterile 200 µL tip. The debris was removed by gentle washing with sterile PBS, and the cells were incubated with different nanoflower formulations with and without laser exposure, and incubated at 37℃ for 12 h. At predetermined time intervals (0, 4, 8, and 12 h), the images of the scratch area were captured, and the % wound closure was calculated.

The %wound closure was calculated with ImageJ software using the following equation.

Scratch Area control = Scratch wound area of untreated sample

Scratch Area treated = Scratch wound area of treated sample

Evaluation of angiogenesis by chorioallantoic membrane(CAM) assay

The chorioallantoic membrane of chick embryos (4-day-old fertile eggs) was used to evaluate the angiogenesis potential of nanoflowers with and without laser. The outer shell of the eggs was disinfected with 70% ethanol, and a small portion was carefully removed from the air sac side with the help of a drill and forceps and treated with different nanoflower formulations. The control eggs were treated with sterile PBS. For laser groups, the nanoflowers were dispersed in sterile PBS in Eppendorf tubes and irradiated with laser for 5 min, and the CAM was topically administered with irradiated samples. The eggs were covered with parafilm, incubated at 37℃, and 60–70% RH for 8 h. The density of blood vessels in the eggs of each treatment group was monitored, and the images were captured at specific time points during the incubation period. The % fold increase in blood vessels in response to different treatments was analyzed by ImageJ software.

In vitro antibacterial studies

Determination of minimum inhibitory oncentration (MIC)

The MIC of different nanoflowers against SA and MRSA cultures was determined by the broth microdilution method. In brief, the bacterial cultures (100 µL) were treated with formulations and kept in an incubator at 37℃ with shaking conditions for 24 h. In case of laser exposure, the bacterial cultures were kept in incubation for 15 min for internalization of the formulation inside the bacteria. After 15 min, the cultures were laser irradiated for 5 min and incubated for the next 24 h. During incubation, at specific time points, the optical density of bacterial cultures was measured using Spectramax. The minimum concentration of nanoflowers that can inhibit bacterial growth is considered the MIC.

Determination of zone of inhibition

The zone of inhibition assay was carried out to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of the prepared nanoflowers by agar well diffusion assay. The TSB agar plates were prepared, and wells were created on the solidified agar plates by using a sterile 200 µL tip after spreading the lawn of cultures of MRSA and SA in aseptic conditions. The nanoflower formulations were placed in the wells with and without laser exposure and incubated for 14 h in static conditions. After incubation, the diameter of the zone of growth inhibition around the wells was measured.

Interaction of nanoflowers with bacteria by SEM analysis

The bacterial cultures were inoculated into sterile TSB broth, placed in the wells of 12-well plates containing sterile glass coverslips, and incubated overnight under static conditions. Following incubation, the culture media were removed, and the bacterial cells adhered to the glass substrate were treated with the nanoflowers and further incubated for 15 min at 37℃. Then, the coverslips were gently washed with sterile PBS to remove the nanoflowers that were not attached to the bacterial surfaces. The bacteria were fixed by adding 3% glutaraldehyde solution, kept at 4 ℃, and dehydrated with increasing concentrations of ethanol. The samples were sputter-coated with gold and visualized by SEM to observe the physical interaction of nanoflowers with the bacterial surface.

Leakage of proteins from the bacteria

The leakage of proteins from the bacteria after treatment with nanoflowers was quantified by the Bradford reagent. Overnight-grown cultures of MRSA and SA cultures (105 CFU/mL) were added with the nanoflowers and incubated for 12 h at 37℃ under shaking conditions. Following incubation, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected to quantify the protein content of each treatment group. The supernatants (1600 µL) were added with Bradford reagent (400 µL), and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm.

Live-dead analysis of planktonic cultures

The bactericidal efficacy of nanoflowers on the planktonic cultures of MRSA and SA was evaluated by live-dead staining of bacteria after treatment with the formulations. In brief, the subcultured MRSA and SA cultures (OD600 of 0.3) were transferred into eppendorf tubes and supplemented with formulations. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C under shaking conditions. After 4 h of incubation, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged and washed with sterile PBS. The pellets were redispersed in 100 µL of PBS and stained with SYTO9 and PI solutions for 5 min under dark conditions. The images were captured by a confocal microscope using SYTO9 (Ex/em 485/501 nm) and PI (Ex/em 493/636 nm). The live/dead bacterial cells were quantified by flow cytometry.

Inhibition of staphyloxanthin

The bacteria (1 × 108 CFU/mL) were cultured in TSB in the presence and absence of formulations (1/2 MIC) with and without laser and kept in incubation for 12 h. The control wells were added with PBS. After incubation, the control and treated bacterial cultures were centrifuged, followed by washing with PBS, and visually observed for pigment production, and the images of pellets were captured. The pigment was extracted from the pellets by resuspending in ethanol and incubating at 40℃ for 15 min. After centrifugation, the ethanol was collected, and the same procedure was repeated until the pellet became colorless. The collected ethanol was concentrated and added to the mixture of ethyl acetate/sodium chloride (1.7 M) (1:1). The organic layer was collected and evaporated to dryness. The carotenoid was dissolved in ethyl acetate, and the absorbance was measured at 286 nm, 435 nm, 455 nm, and 462 nm.

Biofilm studies

Biofilm inhibition by congo red assay

The effect of nanoflowers on the formation of biofilm by MRSA and SA cultures was evaluated by the Congo red agar plate method. Congo red agar plates were prepared by dissolving Brain Heart Infusion broth (37 g/L), sucrose (36 g/L), and agar (18 g/L) in water and autoclaved. After sterilization, the Congo red dye (0.8 g/L) was added to the media, poured into petri plates, and kept for drying. The nanoflowers treated bacterial cultures were streaked on the solidified agar surface and incubated for 3 days. The appearance of black colour in the streaked region indicates the biofilm formation, signifying the production of exopolysaccharide. The biofilm inhibition was quantified by measuring the absorbance of untreated and nanoflower-treated bacterial cultures in Congo red broth at 462 nm.

Biofilm inhibition by crystal violet assay

For the formation of biofilms, the bacterial cultures (1 × 107 CFU/mL) were inoculated into the TSB broth in the wells of 96-well plates. The MIC concentrations of nanoflowers were added to the cultures, and the plates were kept in incubation for 24 h under static conditions. The control wells were added with sterile broth. Following incubation, the broth was removed, and the biofilms formed at the bottom of the wells were gently washed with sterile PBS to remove the free-floating bacteria. Then, the biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution and kept aside for 30 min. Later, the CV solution was aspirated from the wells, and the biofilms were photographed after drying. The quantification of biofilms formed in the presence and absence of nanoflowers was performed by dissolving the CV incorporated into the MRSA and SA biofilms, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer. The % biofilm inhibition was calculated by the following formula.

ODcontrol = Absorbance of untreated sample

ODtreated = Absorbance of treated sample

Biofilm live/dead assay

Biofilm Live/dead staining was performed to differentiate the live and dead populations of bacteria in the EPS matrix. For biofilm formation, the overnight-grown cultures of MRSA and SA were inoculated into TSB broth (1:100), and 500µL was added to sterile glass coverslips placed in wells containing 1.5 mL of TSB in 12-well plates. The plates were incubated for 48 h under static conditions at 37℃. Following incubation, the planktonic bacteria were removed from the wells, and the biofilms on the coverslips were gently washed with sterile PBS. 100 µL of nanoflower formulations at MIC concentrations were added and further incubated for 4 h. After removing supernatants, the biofilms were stained with SYTO9 and PI for 15 min in the dark. After staining, the biofilms were gently washed with sterile PBS. The stained biofilms were visualized, and 3D images were captured by a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Biofilm eradication by SEM analysis

The biofilms were developed as mentioned above. The biofilms were incubated with nanoflowers for 4 h with and without laser exposure. Following incubation, the biofilms were gently washed with PBS and fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde at 4℃ overnight. Then, the biofilms were dehydrated with gradient ethanol concentrations (30, 50, 70, 90, 100%), 10 min in each concentration. The samples were sputter-coated with gold after drying and analyzed by SEM.

Spreading assay

Phenol-soluble modulin proteins are biosurfactants produced by S. aureus that are regulated by the quorum-sensing system (Agr). These are the significant virulence factors of SA that promote biofilm formation and spreading motility, which play a crucial role in the dissemination of biofilm-associated infections. The motility assay was performed according to the reported procedure with some modifications [24]. In brief, overnight grown cultures of MRSA and SA cultures were adjusted to a density of 1 × 108 CFU/mL, treated with and without nanoformulations in the presence and absence of laser exposure, and incubated for 12 h. Then, the cultures (10 µL) were inoculated in the center of TSB agar plates (0.3% agar) and further incubated for 72 h under static conditions at 37℃. The control cultures were treated with sterile PBS. The diameters of spreading zones were measured.

Phenol-soluble modulin protein detection by SDS-Gel

Bacterial cultures were grown in TSB overnight under shaking conditions in the presence and absence of formulations with and without laser irradiation. After incubation, the bacterial cultures were normalized to the same optical density, and the pellets were collected by centrifugation. The PSMs in the bacterial cells were extracted by the Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation method. In brief, the bacterial pellets were treated with TCA, sonicated for 2 min, and incubated on ice for 2 h. Then, the supernatants containing PSMs were separated from the pellet, centrifuged, and washed with cold acetone three times. After washing, the protein pellets were dried at room temperature. The protein pellets were resuspended in PBS (50µL) and stored at −20℃. 1 volume of protein samples was added with 4 volumes of loading dye, and the mixture was heated for 10 min at 90℃ for denaturation of proteins. The proteins were separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel for 2 h at 120 V. Then, the gels were stained with 0.1% crystal violet (CV) solution for 4 h and destained in water overnight. After destaining, the bands located below the dye front are considered the bands of phenol-soluble modulin proteins because of their low molecular weight.

In vivo wound healing activity

In vivo wound healing ability of nanoflowers was evaluated in Wistar rats. Diabetes was induced in rats by intraperitoneal administration of streptozotocin (50 mg/kg) once a day, and the blood glucose levels were monitored with a glucometer. The onset of diabetes was confirmed by increased blood glucose levels above 200 mg/dL. On the dorsal side of rats, full-thickness wounds were created with the help of a biopsy punch (10 mm in diameter). An overnight grown culture of MRSA (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was topically administered to the wound, and the animals were kept aside to develop the infection. The rats were randomly divided into 10 groups (n = 3). Control (-L/+L), SNP (-L/+L), SNP@Ce:Zn NFs (-L/+L), PDA@Ce:Zn NFs (-L/+L), PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs (-L/+L). In each group, the wounds were topically administered with 100 µL nanoflower formulations every 2 days. For laser groups, immediately after administration, the nanoflowers were irradiated with an NIR (808 nm) laser for 5 min. The wounds were photographed every 2 days, and the % of wound closure was calculated by ImageJ software. After 14 days, the skin at the wound region was collected under anaesthesia.

The skin tissues were embedded in the OCT media and kept at −80℃. The cryosections of frozen skin tissues were collected by using a cryostat and mounted on glass slides. The skin tissues were stained with Hematoxylin/Eosin to observe the pathological changes in the control and treated groups. Masson’s trichome staining is also performed to evaluate the collagen deposition.

Immunofluorescence was carried out to detect the expression of inflammatory and angiogenesis markers. The skin tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, dehydrated with ethanol, and washed with PBS. Then, the tissues were blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing with TBST. The skin sections were kept in incubation with the primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. The tissues were washed with TBST and incubated with Alexa Fluor-488 conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the sections were stained with DAPI for 15 min under dark conditions. The slides were observed under a confocal microscope.

In vivo biochemical parameter evaluation

The evaluation of several blood biochemical parameters, such as aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), WBC count, RBC count, blood haemoglobin, blood platelet count, and percentage haematocrit, was evaluated after dosing 100 µL of the different nanoflower formulations at concentrations of 100 µg/mL on wounds of Wistar rats, followed by an NIR (808 nm) laser irradiation (1 W/cm2) for 5 min time interval. All the parameters were evaluated in triplicate using the respective kits.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of nanoflowers

The nanoflowers were prepared by the hydrothermal method. Figure 1A represents the procedure for the synthesis of PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn nanoflowers. Initially, Ce:Zn NFs were synthesized by the hydrothermal method. Dopamine hydrochloride and Ce:Zn NFs were dispersed in an alkaline medium to enable the self-polymerization of polydopamine on the surface of Ce:Zn NFs. The PDA@Ce:Zn NFs were further modified with SNP. Figure 1B, C represent the antibacterial effect at the wound site and biofilm disruption mechanisms of the synthesized nanoflowers, respectively.

Figure 4E depicts the photothermal effect of PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs after irradiating with an 808 nm laser for 5 cycles, exhibiting its stability under photothermal conditions.

The drug loading (DL%) and entrapment efficiency (EE%) of SNP onto the PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs were found to be 14.41 ± 0.06% and 86.44 ± 0.38% respectively. The high entrapment efficiency indicated that the SNP was loaded into the prepared PDA@Ce:Zn NFs effectively, possessing the optimum capability of NO release on photolysis. The release study of NO from PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs was conducted both in the presence and absence of intermittent laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 4F, NO was not released from PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs during the 2 h in the absence of laser irradiation. However, with laser irradiation (808 nm, 1 W/cm2), a burst release of NO was observed, which was required for effective anti-bacterial activity. With the increase of time followed by intermittent laser irradiation, the NO was released up to 96.37% within 48 h. Without the laser irradiation to PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, only 15.32% NO release was observed. From the above release data, we can conclude that with laser, the release of NO from SNP was more effective than without laser irradiation. Such that a higher laser density caused a higher NO release. The above consequences showed that with a combination of laser irradiation, PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs could provide a controlled NO release. The release kinetics were determined by fitting the data with different kinetics models (Fig. 4G, H,I, J). The NO release obeyed the first-order reaction kinetics [33]. The mechanism involved in the release of NO from PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs is due to the strong and broad-spectrum absorption properties of PDA. Upon laser exposure, PDA in the nanoflowers absorbs light and converts it into heat, which induces the thermal decomposition of sodium nitroprusside. Elevated temperature results in cleavage of Fe-NO coordination bonds and releases free NO molecules from the SNP complex structure [13].

Catalytic activity and in vitro cell studies

Catalytic activity plays a crucial role in maintaining sufficient oxygen levels and improving angiogenesis at the wound site. The peroxidase-like activity of nanoflowers was shown in (Fig. 5A). After the addition of H2O2, the colour of nanoflower dispersions was changed to orange colour and exhibited absorption spectra in the 400–450 nm range, confirming the oxidation of nanoceria by hydrogen peroxide [29]. As shown in Fig. 5B, after incubating the nanoflowers with TMB, the colour of the solution was changed from colourless to blue colour and exhibited an intense peak of oxidized TMB at 652 nm. This chromogenic reaction indicates the catalytic oxidation of TMB and the oxidation potential of nanoflower formulations.

The UV spectra of nanoflower dispersions after treatment with H2O2 + TMB (A); The UV spectra after adding nanoflower dispersions in Citrate buffer with TMB solution representing the oxidase mimetic activity (B); Kinetics of NO release, Michaelis-Menton plots(C); Lineweaver Burk plots (D); Representative microscopic images from the scratch wound healing assay of nanoflowers in NIH3T3 cells (E) and HaCaT cells (G) with and without laser irradiation, The bar graphs represents the % wound closure in NIH3T3 cells (F) and HaCaT cells (H). *** p < 0.0001 indicates statistical significance

The POD-like catalytic activities of the synthesized nanoflowers, Ce:Zn NFs, SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, PDA@Ce:Zn NFs, and PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, were systematically evaluated through the determination of their Michaelis-Menten kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) using TMB as the substrate and H2O2 as the co-substrate. All nanoflower formulations displayed typical Michaelis-Menten kinetics when initial reaction velocities were plotted against increasing concentrations of TMB, confirming their enzyme-mimetic catalytic profiles (Fig. 5C). The plotted Lineweaver-Burk plots for the various nanoflower formulations suggest the trend shift of POD-like catalytic activities (Fig. 5D). Ce:Zn NFs exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency, as evidenced by the lowest Km value of 0.376 mM and the highest Vmax value of 3.561 µM/min. The significantly lower Km indicates a greater substrate affinity compared to the modified nanoflowers. Upon SNP functionalization (SNP@Ce:Zn NFs), the Km value increased to 0.671 mM and Vmax decreased to 3.009µM/min, suggesting a modest reduction in both substrate affinity and maximal catalytic rate. PDA coating (PDA@Ce:Zn NFs) resulted in a Km value of 0.627 mM and Vmax of 3.047µM/min, which also indicates a decrease in both kinetic parameters relative to unmodified Ce:Zn NFs, but with slightly better substrate affinity than SNP@Ce:Zn NFs. The dual modification (PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs) exhibited the highest Km value (0.769 mM) and the lowest Vmax (2.337µM/min) among all prepared nanoflower formulations (Table S3), denoting the lowest affinity towards TMB and the least catalytic efficiency. This trend suggests that surface engineering with SNPs and PDA imparts additional functional properties, such as NO release and photothermal responsiveness, but at the expense of raw POD-like activity. The reduction in Vmax and increase in Km upon surface modification could stem from partial surface masking of active catalytic sites or steric hindrance, which limits substrate accessibility [47]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while Ce:Zn NFs provide the superior catalytic performance, functionalization with SNP and PDA enables the acquisition of multifunctionality with only a moderate compromise in enzymatic kinetics [6]. This balance between catalytic activity and extra functionalities is vital for tailoring nanozyme platforms for diverse biomedical applications, such as infectious wound healing, where both catalytic and therapeutic effects are required.

The cytotoxicity of nanoflowers was determined by the MTT assay. The results showed that the nanoflower formulations were nontoxic to the NIH3T3 cells at low concentrations. At high concentrations (> 250 µg/mL), the % cell viability is reduced after 48 h of incubation (Fig. S11A, B). These findings suggested that nanoflower formulations did not exhibit cytotoxic effects on NIH3T3 cells. A scratch wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the wound closure ability of nanoflowers with and without laser exposure using NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 5E) and HaCaT cells (Fig. 5G). PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs (+ L) demonstrated significantly enhanced wound healing effect with % wound closure values of 98.21 ± 12.32 in NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 5F) and 98.97 ± 2.13% in HaCaT cells (Fig. 5H) after incubation for 12 h compared to the PBS-treated control, with negligible visible cellular aberrations. In the control wells, the wound gap was not completely closed. The wound area in all treatment groups was analyzed by ImageJ software. This enhanced wound closure performance is attributed to the synergistic effect of its photothermal, NO-releasing activities, and the unique properties of PDA. The incorporation of SNP facilitates the controlled release of NO, a vital endogenous molecule often deficient in diabetic wounds [54]. NO exerts potent anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and vasodilatory effects, stimulating skin cell activation and crucially promoting angiogenesis, cell proliferation, collagen formation, and re-epithelialization, all essential steps for efficient wound repair [18]. Significantly, the PDA coating plays a crucial role due to its inherent versatile adhesion abilities and excellent cell affinity, facilitating strong interaction with biological surfaces [28]. This adhesive property promotes cell attachment and creates a favourable microenvironment for cell-material interactions, which indirectly supports cellular activities like migration and proliferation, crucial for re-epithelialization [64]. Collectively, these multi-functional attributes of the PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs create an optimal microenvironment that actively combats infection, modulates inflammation, and stimulates the regenerative processes of human keratinocytes, leading to the observed rapid and efficient wound closure.

Inhibition of spreading motility

The motility of bacteria and colonization on different surfaces have a key impact on their ability to adapt to diverse environmental conditions. As SA does not contain motility structures like flagella and pili, it uses sliding and quorum-sensing-mediated secretion of phenol-soluble modulin proteins (PSMs) for exhibiting spreading motility on soft surfaces [41]. These PSMs act as surfactants and enable the spreading of bacteria on different surfaces. The plates inoculated with untreated cultures exhibited extensive spreading motility. In contrast, the plates with nanoflowers (-L+/L) treated cultures inhibited the spreading motility of MRSA (Fig. 10A) and SA (Fig. 10B) cultures. These findings suggested that the nanoflowers could effectively suppress the Agr QS-regulated PSMs-mediated spreading ability of MRSA and SA cultures.

Bacterial biofilms are distinct sponge-like structures with channels to supply nutrients and water. In Staphylococcus species, the quorum sensing regulated Agr system has been recognized as a significant mechanism by secreting PSMs that control the development of channels in the biofilm structure [51] and also disrupts non-covalent hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions in the biofilm matrix and helps in the dispersal of biofilms to the adjacent tissues, resulting in chronic infection [23]. PSMs are amphipathic small peptides having α-helical secondary structures that can aggregate and form amyloid fibers that stabilize the biofilm of SA [46]. As surfactants, these PSMs are not only involved in disseminating biofilm but also have a significant impact on the thickness of the biofilm matrix and the formation of channels inside the matrix to transport nutrients. The low molecular weight (approx. 2–3 kDa) PSM proteins were detected as single bands below the dye front. The intense bands were observed in the control groups, while the intensity of bands was significantly reduced in the treatment groups, confirming that the laser irradiation of nanoflowers reduced the efficiency of PSMs secretion by MRSA (Fig. 10C, D) and SA (Fig. 10E, F) cultures.

Inhibition of staphyloxanthin

Staphyloxanthin is a carotenoid pigment that acts as an antioxidant by neutralizing the reactive oxygen species generated by the neutrophils, provides protection against the host defence system, and protects the S. aures from oxidative stress [58]. The impact of nanoflowers on the secretion of staphyloxanthin was evaluated. The control and SNP groups showed orange-colour pigment production, whereas in the nanoflower-treated groups, the pigment was not detected (Fig. S10). The bar graphs showed that inhibition of intermediates of staphyloxanthin biosynthesis by MRSA (Fig. 10G) and SA (Fig. 10H), including 4,4′-diapophytoene (286 nm), 4,4′- diaponeurosporene (435 nm), and staphyloxanthin (462 nm). These findings suggest that the nanoflowers enhanced the susceptibility of MRSA and SA cultures to oxidative stress by inhibiting the biosynthesis of staphyloxanthin. The nanoflowers inhibited the carotenoid pigment production without affecting the bacterial viability at sub-MIC concentrations.

The bacteria exhibited intact structure in biofilms treated with PBS with laser exposure, whereas in the PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs group, the biofilm matrix was disrupted, and the bacteria lost their structural integrity, indicating that the nanoflowers could kill the bacteria embedded in the deeper layers of the biofilm matrix of MRSA (Fig. 13E) and SA (Fig. 13F) cultures after laser exposure Therefore, the nanoflowers demonstrated a greater biofilm disintegration effect after laser irradiation compared to without laser due to the release of metal ions from the nanoflowers. Nitric oxide also impairs the vital cellular functions of bacteria in biofilms, interrupting their physiological activities. Recent studies demonstrated that NO molecules could weaken the protective effect of the antioxidant system in biofilms and increase the penetration of ROS into the biofilms [20].

In vivo wound healing effect

To evaluate the in vivo antibacterial and wound-healing activity, we conducted MRSA-infected wound-healing experiments in diabetic rats. The images of infected wounds on days 3,7,11, and 14 days post-treatment are shown in Fig. 14A. The results showed that after laser irradiation, the % wound closure was faster in PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs compared to other groups. On the 11th day, the % wound closure rate of PDA@SNP@Ce: Zn NFs (-L) was 77.17% and for (+ L) it was 87.21% which was better than the control and SNP groups (Fig. 14E). On the 14th day, rats from the PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs group exhibited a complete healing effect. In contrast, the wounds in the control group remained incompletely healed. Figure 14B represents the tracing of wound closure images. The tissue samples collected at the wound site on the 14th day were homogenized and spread on the TSB agar plates. As shown in Fig. 14C, very few bacterial colonies were observed in the wounds treated with PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs (+ L). The bar graph represents the number of CFU in tissue homogenate from each treatment group (Fig. 14D). These results demonstrated that the combination of photothermal and nitric oxide can effectively eradicate the biofilms at the wound site and accelerate the healing process by reducing the inflammation in the diabetic wound region. A recent study reported that the combination of NO and PTT effectively eliminated the drug-resistant bacteria while minimizing the risk of inducing drug bacterial resistance due to their physical disruption mechanisms that target multiple cellular functions rather than targeting specific cellular targets [9].

To evaluate the healing progress in infected diabetic wounds, IHC staining for CD-44 and TNF-α was performed with the skin tissues from each treatment group. The expression of pro-inflammatory marker TNF-α is used to assess the inflammatory state in the wound region [63]. CD44 is an endothelial cell marker used to confirm the formation of new blood vessels [56]. The upregulation of CD44 in PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs treatment groups demonstrates the angiogenesis at the wound site, and the low expression of CD44 in the PBS-treated group indicates the inhibition of blood capillary growth due to the elevated ROS levels in the wound microenvironment (Fig. 15A). As shown in Fig. 15B, PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs with laser irradiation significantly reduced TNF-α expression in skin tissues, indicating the healing effect. The wounds treated with PBS and SNP exhibited high expression of TNF-α, indicating an inflammatory response associated with oxidative stress and bacterial infection (Qi, Li, et al., [43]). These findings demonstrated that PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs could contribute to new blood vessel formation and support the repair of infected wounds through the combined effects of PTT-mediated release of NO and bacterial killing efficacy of metal ions released from the nanoflowers upon laser irradiation. NO has the ability to improve the migration of endothelial cells in diabetic wounds [8]. In a recent study, NO-releasing copper-based metal organic frameworks exhibited promising diabetic wound healing potential by inducing angiogenesis and promoting collagen deposition [53]. The wounds treated with PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs showed reconstitution of the epithelial layer and increased angiogenesis, indicating their potential role in supporting wound healing (Qi, Lan, et al., [42]).

Histopathological analysis of skin tissues was performed to assess the healing of skin in response to the nanoflower treatment. As shown in Fig. 15C, PBS and SNP-treated groups did not exhibit significant pathological changes at the wound region. In contrast, PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs showed the formation of dense epithelial layers and hair follicle-like structures in the skin sections. The photothermal effect of nanoflowers can release the NO to control the infection at the wound site and promote tissue regeneration. A study by Hui Ma et al. reported that a glucose-responsive hydrogel with NO-releasing multienzyme nanoflowers effectively reduced the infection and inflammation while promoting angiogenesis and tissue regeneration [34].

Masson’s trichome staining of skin sections was performed to evaluate epithelial regeneration and collagen deposition. As shown in Fig. 15D, the results confirmed the deposition of collagen (stained blue) and keratin (stained red). Nanoflower-treated groups exhibited abundant and organized collagen deposition. PDA@SNP@Ce: Zn NFs exhibited a significant amount of collagen at the wound site compared to the other test groups. In diabetic wounds, healing is impaired due to disturbance in the redox balance of cells that damage the cell membranes under oxidative stress conditions, impaired angiogenesis, and risk of infection [52]. These results demonstrated that the PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs with NIR laser exposure significantly enhance the angiogenic effect within the regenerated skin tissue, facilitating the transition from the inflammatory phase to the repair and remodelling stages [42].

The in vivo biochemical parameter evaluation of Ce:Zn NFs, SNP@Ce:Zn NFs, PDA@Ce:Zn NFs, and PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs revealed no significant deviations in key biochemical parameters compared to the control group (Fig. S11), suggesting that the nanoflower formulations were biocompatible and did not show detectable local toxicity in rats. These findings are consistent with the recent reports indicating that rational design of multifunctional nanozymes and zinc-based nanomaterials can support wound healing while maintaining acceptable systemic and local biochemical profiles, attributed to their controlled reactivity and surface engineering. In addition, safety evaluations in similar in vivo wound healing models have suggested that nanomaterials with optimized release properties and low cytotoxicity can facilitate tissue repair while limiting risks of organ toxicity and local adverse effects [11], which aligns with the findings of the current study. Thus, the outcomes of the biochemical assessments suggest that the developed nanoflowers were compatible in the diabetic wound model, in agreement with safety profiles reported for other nanozyme-based systems.

Conclusion

MRSA biofilms at the wound site produce an inflammatory response, inhibiting the vascular endothelial growth factor that hinders reepithelialization. In this study, a multifunctional nanozyme system was designed by modifying the surface of Ce:Zn NFs with a photothermal agent, polydopamine, to improve its photothermal properties, followed by the incorporation of a NO donor, sodium nitroprusside, to support angiogenesis and exhibit antibacterial effect. The synthesized nanoflowers exhibited a high surface area with sharp-edged petals. The PDA@SNP@Ce:Zn NFs demonstrated effective photothermal conversion after laser irradiation for 5 min. After laser exposure, the NFs generated localized hyperthermia and released NO, which contributed to biofilm disruption at the wound site. The combined effect of metal ions and NO release was associated with the membrane disruption-mediated antibacterial effect. These findings suggest that the multifunctional nanoflowers with laser irradiation have the potential for the removal of biofilm at the wound site and support wound repair and angiogenesis in the MRSA-infected rat model. However, as chronic wounds are often associated with polymicrobial biofilms, the efficacy of this system against polymicrobial infection models and the long-term safety assessments need to be explored.
