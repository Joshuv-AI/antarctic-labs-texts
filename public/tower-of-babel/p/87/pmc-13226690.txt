China’s City-level CO2 emissions from power sector between 2000 and 2019
Authors: Boyang Chen, Ming Gao, Yu Liu, Jiafu An, Zhengxian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The power sector is a major contributor to global energy use and greenhouse gas emissions. In 2020, China’s power sector accounted for over 40% of national carbon emissions, underscoring the importance of its decarbonization for achieving global climate targets. Effective emission-reduction policies require detailed and reliable carbon data. Given China’s “top-down” target-setting system, accurate regional data are essential for designing differentiated local policies and evaluating their effectiveness. In this study, we systematically estimate CO2 emissions from the power sector in 352 Chinese cities from 2000 to 2019, providing broader spatial and temporal coverage than existing datasets. Using a particle swarm optimization–back propagation algorithm, we established relationships among provincial and prefectural power generation, socioeconomic indicators, and operating revenue for the electricity and heat production and supply sectors. City-level power generation was derived based on provincial training results, combined with the 2021 electricity CO2 emissions factor published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics, providing a valuable foundation for regional low-carbon research and policymaking.

Background & Summary

The power sector is a significant contributor to global energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. Mitigating these emissions is vital to achieving sustainable development goals. In 2023, China’s carbon emissions reached 13 billion tons, accounting for 31.71% of the global1. As the world’s largest emitter of carbon dioxide, China has assumed the responsibility for emission reduction, committing to achieving “carbon peak” by 2030 and “carbon neutrality” by 2060. Research indicates that carbon dioxide emissions caused by the production and consumption of China’s power sector are the primary contributors to national carbon emissions, accounting for more than 40% of total emissions, the highest share among all industries2,3.

China has established a “top-down” emission-reduction policy system, in which national-level emission reduction targets (e.g., the “dual carbon” goals) are stepwise decomposed to local governments and industrial sectors. While this principle ensures policy consistency and coordinated implementation, it may limit the flexibility of local governments to design emission-reduction strategies that reflect regional differences in economic development, energy structures, and industrial characteristics. Against this backdrop, cities, as key drivers of urbanization and industrialization4, have become major centers of electricity demand and carbon emissions. The effectiveness of their emission reduction directly determines whether China can achieve its “dual carbon” goals as scheduled. Consequently, the promotion of emission reduction in the power sector across various Chinese cities is not only an effective strategy for China to achieve its “dual carbon” goals, but also an inevitable choice in the global effort to address climate change.

Effective mitigation policies rely on robust data. China’s “top-down” mechanism for achieving emission-reduction targets should be designed based on regional emissions data. Precise regional emission information, especially downscaled data, is crucial for evidence-based policy making. It enables local governments to formulate differentiated policies based on local conditions, scientifically assess the effectiveness of emission reductions, and allocate resources efficiently. What is the scale of carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from the power sector in various Chinese cities? What are the differences and long-term trends in carbon emissions from the power sector across various regions? The answers to the above questions are directly related to the ability of China’s power sector to achieve emission reduction and low-carbon transformation, as well as the realization of the major strategic goals of “dual carbon.” However, owing to the unavailability of downscaled carbon-emission data for the power sector, current studies on carbon dioxide emissions from China’s power sector are largely limited to national or provincial levels. These studies examined temporal changes and regional differences in aggregate power sector carbon emissions5–11. Alternatively, studies have focused on analyzing power sector carbon emissions within a single province or city12–14. However, there are significant deficiencies in research conducted from the perspective of the city’s power sector. Regarding the methods for calculating carbon emission data, existing studies primarily employ the carbon emission factor method, as published by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change15, and the annual greenhouse gas emission factors for electricity and heat generation in various countries, as published by the International Energy Agency16. These include CO2, CH4, and N2O produced by power generation, among others17–19. Additionally, Carbon Emission Accounts and Datasets (e.g., CEADs) provide substantial data for carbon emission calculations that can be used in provincial-level and country-level power sector emission statistics20. They reveal the overall trend and changes in the growth rate of national or provincial power-sector carbon emissions and highlight significant interprovincial differences5,6,8. However, these research perspectives and approaches cannot characterize the heterogeneity of power-sector carbon emissions among cities.

Provincial aggregates obscure substantial inter-city differences in economic development, energy structure, technological level, and power generation. The use of the provincial average value can easily lead to oversight of these crucial details. Besides, policy recommendations derived from the provincial data analysis are primarily applicable to provincial government decision-making. However, the design, implementation, and evaluation of the policy effects of refined, location-specific emission-mitigation policies often require data at the city or county level. This is because actual emission-reduction measures (such as energy structure transformation, technological upgrading, industrial adjustment, and regulatory enforcement) are usually implemented at the micro level. Furthermore, provincial emissions data primarily reflect macro-level trends. The absence of power-sector emissions data from prefecture-level cities may have two possible consequences. First, it hinders in-depth research into the contributions of unique city-level emission drivers (such as power-sector carbon intensity, population size, and industrial structure) to carbon-emission differences among regional power sectors. Second, it makes it difficult for local governments to assess their local emission-reduction potential and set reasonable targets accurately. This may lead to policies being divorced from reality, low efficiency in resource allocation, and affect the effectiveness of the national overall emission-reduction strategy.

Given the deficiency of city-level power sector carbon emission data, this study aims to systematically quantify carbon dioxide emissions from the power sector in 352 cities in China from 2000 to 2019, thereby providing local governments with the scientific basis for formulating precise emission-reduction policies. This study addresses the significant challenge of the lack of city-level power-generation data by employing an original approach that utilizes city electricity consumption, operating revenue from the electricity and heat production and supply sector, and other socioeconomic indicators, using the particle swarm optimization-back propagation (PSO-BP) method, we established prediction relationships between provincial power generation and multidimensional variables. This enabled us to estimate power generation for each city. Based on the aforementioned data, carbon dioxide emissions from the prefectural power sector are calculated using the authoritative dataset “2021 Electricity CO2 Emissions Factor”, released by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics.

The possible marginal contributions of this study are as follows. First, the constructed dataset covers long-term (2000–2019) and wide-coverage (352 cities) carbon dioxide emissions from China’s prefectural power sector. This not only addresses the long-term lack of data at this scale but also provides a valuable foundation for in-depth investigation of spatiotemporal patterns, influencing factors, simulation studies, and related research on carbon emissions from China’s prefectural power sector. Second, it estimates the cities’ power generation and provides a feasible solution to the data deficiency problem. Given the lack of city-level power-generation and carbon-emissions data, this study proposes and applies the PSO-BP method to identify and model the nonlinear relationship between provincial power generation and multidimensional city-level variables, achieving high-precision estimation of power generation at the prefectural scale. This study provides a replicable and generally applicable framework for estimating non-power-source emissions. It serves as a crucial methodological reference for research on other city-level energy and environmental issues. Third, the dataset provides local governments with essential data support, thereby enabling them to accurately assess the current state of regional power-sector emissions (including emission levels, regional disparities, and historical trajectories). This, in turn, enables them to formulate more practical, location-specific power-sector emission-reduction strategies and policy-evaluation mechanisms. This will contribute to China’s efficient implementation of the national “dual carbon” strategic goals; thus, downscaling data should be provided to support decision-making at the downscaling level.

Methods

Overview of the research area and research method

This study employed a top-down approach to estimate city-level electricity generation and carbon dioxide emissions associated with electricity production. The research area was defined using available provincial and prefectural data. The key to a top-down approach is the identification of the instrumental variables used in the inversion. This study used provincial power generation data from 2000 to 2019, as reported in the China Energy Statistical Yearbook. Primary operating revenue data from prefectural electricity and heat production and supply were also used. The electricity consumption data at the prefectural level were sourced from Chen et al.21. The spatial analysis tool of ArcGIS 10.7 software was utilized in conjunction with the standard map bearing review number GS(2019)1835, which was downloaded from the Standard Map Service (Standard Map Review No. GS(2019)1835) of the Ministry of Natural Resources of China22. This process enabled the extraction and matching of the electricity consumption data, including 352 prefecture-level cities and autonomous prefectures, for the purpose of analysis. The city-level data mentioned in the following text all refer to the data of prefecture-level cities and autonomous prefectures, which will not be elaborated on later. Along with other socio-economic indicators reported in the statistical yearbooks and statistical communiqués of 352 prefecture-level cities and autonomous prefectures, as input factors. Multiple variables influence power generation, including economic growth, industrial transformation, financial development, and policy implementation23–26. These data cover 30 provinces in mainland China, excluding Tibet, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan, owing to limited data access.

Subsequently, this study employed a machine learning method at the macro level to establish a predictive relationship between multidimensional variables and provincial power generation. Based on this model, the potentially predicted power-generation values at the city level were treated as instrumental variables, and the power-generation data for each city were estimated based on the weighting of its provincial-region. These estimates were then combined with the provincial average power-sector CO2 emissions factors provided in “2021 Electricity CO2 Emissions Factor”, published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics27. It is clarified that these factors can be used to calculate the CO2 emissions from the power sector.

The statistical calibre adopted in this study was the carbon dioxide emissions per unit of power generation (across all power sources, including thermal power, hydropower, wind, nuclear, and solar). These data represent the first officially released provincial-level electricity carbon dioxide emissions factors in China, accompanied by the simultaneous disclosure of their calculation method and data sources. Due to the lack of city-level power carbon emission factor data for the period 2000–2019, this study has used the initially published 2021 provincial average carbon dioxide emissions factor for electric power as the basis for calculations.

Introduction to the latent characteristic variables of the instrumental variables used for inversion

Given that the primary operating revenue of electricity and heat production and supply industries originates from electricity production and supply, this study calculates the operating revenue of enterprises above a designated size within the industry. When electricity prices exhibit minimal fluctuations, the primary operating revenues of the regional electricity, heat production, and supply industries demonstrate a robust correlation with power generation. Consequently, this study employed the primary operating revenue of electricity and heat production and supply industries as the input factor for the inversion of city power generation. It is important to note that data omissions exist for many cities and years in the statistical yearbooks and statistical communiqués of prefecture-level cities. The study assumes that electricity prices remain constant and uses the growth rate of power generation to extrapolate missing data on the operating revenue of the cities’ power sectors. The calculation of city-level power-production data is based on power-consumption data, power-production elasticity, and power-consumption elasticity. To address missing values, this study used global 1 km × 1 km grid electricity consumption data from Chen et al.21 to derive prefectural-level electricity consumption. The advantages of these data include extensive coverage, a long time span, and objectivity. To predict prefectural power generation, this study has used a multifaceted approach with primary operating revenue data from electricity and heat production and supply; however, it also incorporates a range of input factors, including infrastructure, industrial structure, financial development, and technological innovation, energy conservation and emission reduction policies, administrative intervention, and pollution control. The basis for data selection was as follows. First, infrastructure is highly correlated with electricity transmission and can affect both local electricity generation and carbon dioxide emissions28–30. For example, Tang et al.31 examined the evolving carbon footprint trajectory of China’s power system and evaluated the impact of deploying low-carbon power infrastructure. Research has demonstrated that the effects of infrastructure on carbon dioxide emissions vary by regions due to differences in resource endowments and energy structures. The carbon footprint in the northern areas was comparatively high, whereas in certain southern provinces, hydropower accounted for a significant share, resulting in a lower carbon footprint of electricity. Second, industrial structure has a substantial influence on power generation and carbon dioxide emissions32–34. High-energy-consuming industries rely on coal-fired power, which promotes power generation while intensifying emissions35. For instance, Zhu et al.36 employed a three-stage Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) model to identify spatiotemporal patterns and factors influencing carbon-emission efficiency in energy-intensive industries across 30 provinces in China from 2005 to 2017. Research has found that industrial structure has a substantial impact on power generation and carbon dioxide emissions. Specifically, energy-intensive industries, such as manufacturing and processing of ferrous metals industry, consume substantial energy, resulting in extensive amount of carbon emissions. Third, the development of green finance is inextricably linked to the low-carbon transformation of the local power sector37–39, thereby affecting the proportion of thermal power generation40. For instance, Du et al.41 studied the impact and mechanisms of green finance on the energy transition in 30 provinces of China from 2001 to 2019. They highlighted that green finance, through mechanisms such as green credit and green investment, effectively channels capital toward renewable energy projects, thereby providing essential financial assurance for the power sector’s transition from traditional energy sources to clean energy alternatives. It has promoted the development of clean energy projects, such as wind and photovoltaic power. Simultaneously, it is crucial to acknowledge the pivotal role of green patent R&D in advancing clean energy technologies and facilitating the energy transition42. This phenomenon may also interact with local power generation43. Moreover, it is imperative to recognize the significance of energy conservation and emission-reduction strategies44,45, as well as pollution-control policy instruments, in shaping power generation46,47. For example, Xu et al.48 used the difference-in-differences (DID) model. The propensity score matched difference-in-differences model (PSM-DID) was used to examine the asymmetric impact and mechanisms of heterogeneous environmental standards on green technological innovation, finding that improvements in clean production standards significantly promote green technological innovation in enterprises, thereby influencing their production and emissions. Finally, Lin and Huang49 evaluated the emission-reduction effects of the Carbon Emission Trading Pilot policy, indicating that administrative intervention is a key channel for effectively implementing prevailing energy conservation and emission-reduction policies. Consequently, the intensity of the regional administrative interventions should also be considered.

Inversion work based on the PSO-BP algorithm

The relationship between provincial-level power generation and cities’ aggregated primary operating revenue is not fixed due to variations in data quality and statistical calibre. This study used artificial neural networks (ANNs) to explore relationships in provincial-level data. Specifically, the particle swarm optimization (PSO) back propagation (BP) algorithm, which has been shown to yield favorable outcomes when training inversion models for socioeconomic indicators, including carbon dioxide emissions and GDP50,51. The PSO-optimized BP algorithm avoids local optima and increases the probability of attaining the global optimum52,53. This algorithm was used to train the relationships among variables and to improve the accuracy of estimated power-generation data.

Calculation of carbon dioxide emissions based on power generation

Carbon dioxide emissions from the power sector refer to the emissions generated by the combustion of fossil fuels during power generation. The carbon emission factor is a commonly used method for estimating carbon emissions. The carbon emissions factor is defined as the carbon dioxide emission coefficient per unit of production or consumption activity, such as the carbon dioxide emissions per unit of fossil fuel combustion. For instance, the National Development and Reform Commission (2016)55 has clearly defined the calculation method for carbon dioxide emissions from the power sector as the sum of carbon dioxide emissions from coal combustion, fuel oil, gas, and electricity transferred into regional power grids minus the carbon dioxide emissions contained in the electricity transferred out of the region. The fuel’s emission factor was derived from data in the 2005 National Greenhouse Gas Inventories. The Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics27 issued the average carbon dioxide emission factor for electricity in 2021. Furthermore, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)15, the United States Environmental Protection Agency56, the European Environment Agency57, and other relevant authorities have provided national or industry-level carbon dioxide emission factors. The research object of this study is the carbon dioxide emissions of China’s power sector. Therefore, the emission factor data released by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics has been adopted for this study. Compared with other datasets, this emission factor better reflects China’s power sector CO2 emissions.

The Ministry of Ecology and Environment and the National Bureau of Statistics released the first set of provincial-level CO2 emission factors for electricity in 2021. Data Records

For cities in China, CO2 emission and power generation data are available from the open-access source Figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.30338773)58. Temporal changes in the CO2 emissions from 352 cities and their power generation from 2000 to 2019 are provided in the file named “Power generation and carbon emission of cities in China.xlsx”. The number of cities included was based on the 2019 standard map (Review No. GS(2019)1835) from the Standard Map Service of the Ministry of Natural Resources of China22. It contained 7,040 records on emissions and power generation. The predicted power generation value of Urumqi in 2013 was negative; therefore, it was excluded from the analysis, and the average values for 2012 and 2014 were used instead.

Technical Validation

The validation test is crucial as it ensures the effectiveness of the training program. To prevent overfitting of the optimization parameters, the validation set was randomly sampled from the training set (Fig. 1). The training set indicates that the proposed algorithm achieves an optimal fitting performance, thereby evidencing its robust predictive capacity. Specifically, the coefficient of determination (R2) was 98.03% for the training set and 97.71% for the test set. These results demonstrate the PSO-BP algorithm performs with remarkable accuracy and efficiency, thereby enabling reliable estimation of power-generation data for 352 cities from 2000 to 2019. This provides a solid foundation for the subsequent calculation of carbon dioxide emissions in the power sector.
