Effect of Heat and Moisture Exchanger vs Heated Humidifier on Ventilator-related Adverse Events in Children: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Sivamurukan Palanisamy, Sreedeep Kodakkattil Sreevilasan, Lolam Venkatesh, Divakar Jose, BC Gowtham, Narayanan Parameswaran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsHeating and humidification of ventilator gas are essential to ensure optimal ventilator support in critically ill children. Heat and moisture exchangers (HME) offer a simpler and more cost-effective method of humidification compared to active systems like heated humidifiers (HH). However, the efficacy and impact of HME on ventilator-related adverse events (VAEs) in children remain underexplored. This study aims to compare the incidence of VAEs in children managed with HME versus those using a HH during mechanical ventilation.Patients and methodsThis open-label randomized controlled trial was conducted in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) of a tertiary care hospital in South India from January 2022 to July 2023. Sixty children aged 2–15 years, who were expected to require mechanical ventilation for more than 48 hours, were enrolled. Patients were randomized into two groups after age-based stratification (2–5 years and 6–15 years). The intervention group received humidification via HME, while the control group received a HH. Patients were monitored until discharge, and daily assessments were performed to identify any VAEs for the first 7 days of mechanical ventilation.ResultsThe primary outcome was the incidence of VAEs, expressed per 1,000 ventilator days. The HME group reported 28.36 VAEs per 1,000 ventilator days, while the HH group had 12.98 VAEs per 1,000 ventilator days. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.42). Other variables, including the average frequency of suctioning per day, total ventilator days, maximal peak inspiratory pressure, and mortality, were comparable between the groups.ConclusionThis pilot randomized controlled trial did not find a statistically significant difference in the incidence of VAEs between use of HME and HHs in children. Given its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, HME may be cautiously considered for use in mechanically ventilated pediatric patients aged 2–15 years.How to cite this articlePalanisamy S, Kodakkattil Sreevilasan S, Venkatesh L, Jose D, Gowtham BC, Parameswaran N. Introduction

Invasive mechanical ventilation in critically ill children bypasses the natural humidification of the airways, exposing the lungs to dry air. This can adversely affect respiratory cells and contribute to lung problems like micro-atelectasis and ventilation–perfusion mismatch.1–3 Thus, it is necessary to provide an artificial system (humidifying device: active or passive) to provide heat and moisture to the gas while mechanically ventilating a patient to avoid these adverse effects. Studies have proven that humidification of air given through a ventilator has definitive beneficial effects.2,4

The American Association of Respiratory Care recommends maintaining a humidity level of 33 mg H2O/L–44 mg H2O/L of water vapor while providing active humidification with the gas temperature of 34°C and 41°C at the Y piece of the ventilator circuit and relative humidity of 100% and for the passive humidification method to provide at least 30 mg H2O/L of water vapor.5 Most pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) are using HH (pass-over type). However, the active and passive humidification methods have various advantages and disadvantages over each other.5 Although HH provides precise temperature and humidity control, they are often more costly and require frequent monitoring. Conversely, heat and moisture exchangers (HMEs) are simpler, more cost-effective, and easier to use, but they can increase dead space and resistance. Although some studies suggest HMEs may be associated with a lower infection risk, there is no conclusive evidence to recommend a superior humidification method overall.

A systematic review of humidification methods, including 34 trials with 2,848 subjects involving both adults and children, concluded that there was no significant difference in the occurrence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) or artificial airway occlusion (AAO) between the two methods of humidification.6 Pediatric data on HMEs from this review are limited and outdated, with only three studies published before the 2000s and none after that, primarily in operating room and NICU (neonatal intensive care) settings.6 There are no RCTs exclusively looking at children admitted to ICUs where a longer duration of mechanical ventilation is required. Existing research on HMEs is primarily focused on the adult population. These findings cannot be directly applied to children due to their unique airway anatomy, physiological differences, and distinct disease patterns. In a survey regarding the usage of HME, it was found that 63% of French ICUs used HME compared to 13% usage in Canadian ICUs, suggesting significant variations in practices.7 Thus, with the available knowledge, HME may be an excellent alternative to a HH because of its ease of use, low cost, and relatively acceptable effectiveness.

Crucially, the use of HMEs as an alternative to HH in children receiving prolonged ventilation in the PICU has not yet been studied. Consequently, there is limited understanding of the feasibility of HMEs and their impact on ventilator-related complications, such as VAP and AAO, in critically ill pediatric patients. Therefore, dedicated research is essential to establish evidence-based guidelines for humidification strategies in this vulnerable population. Given the lack of data on HMEs in children receiving prolonged ventilation and being the first trial of this intervention in a vulnerable population, we chose to conduct a pilot study. The main objective of this study was to compare the occurrence of ventilator-related adverse events among children started on HME vs HH. Ventilator-related adverse event was considered as a composite outcome with the occurrence of either of the following events at the end of seven ventilator days.

Incidence of AAO requiring endotracheal tube change.

Incidence of AAO requiring endotracheal tube change.

Incidence of VAP (as per CDC definition) (VAP CDC) between the two groups.

Incidence of VAP (as per CDC definition) (VAP CDC) between the two groups.

Incidence of physician diagnosed VAP between the two groups.

The secondary objectives were to compare the requirement of suctioning, duration of ventilation, maximum peak inspiratory pressure (PIP), and hospital discharge between the HME vs HH groups. The number of children in whom the Heat moisture exchanger was discontinued was also evaluated.

Patients and Methods

Our study was a non-blinded, parallel, 2-arm pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in a 15-bedded, Level III PICU at a public sector tertiary care center in South India. This PICU provides care to approximately 500 children per year, including medical and surgical (including post-cardiac surgery) patients. The study was conducted from January 2022 to July 2023. Institute ethics committee approval (JIP/IEC/2021/026) was obtained. The study was also registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) (registration number CTRI/2021/08/035878). Parents or the legally authorized representative provided written informed consent. Children were randomized (1:1) to two groups using computer-generated block randomization with variable block sizes (4, 6, 8) stratified by age (2–5 vs 6–15 years) and allocation was concealed using the SNOSE technique. Randomization was performed by an individual who was not part of the study. The duty resident or the principal investigator enrolled participants and assigned them to the allocated intervention. Blinding was not possible due to the type of intervention.

All children aged 2–15 years requiring invasive mechanical ventilation and expected to be ventilated for a duration of >48 hours based on clinical judgment were eligible for participation. Children with severe malnutrition, severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) diagnosed at admission [as per Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (PALICC) definition], hemoptysis, who had airway manipulation in the last 90 days (mechanical ventilation or surgery), and children being ventilated for primary airway pathology (bronchial asthma, bronchiolitis, upper airway obstruction, bacterial tracheitis, tracheobronchomalacia) were excluded.8 The exclusion criteria were chosen to avoid patients at high risk for respiratory deterioration or with significant primary airway pathology, as compromised humidification could severely impact their respiratory status.

Children were randomly assigned to HH (Group A) or heat moisture exchanger (Group B). Participants in group A received a Fisher & Paykel MR850™ humidifier. The associated ventilatory circuit used a heated wire only in the inspiratory limb to prevent condensation and maintain gas temperature, along with a water trap in the expiratory limb. Participants assigned to group B received an HME filter (Thermo shield™, mini HME, Flexicare), with a dead space of 25 mL, resistance of 2.9 cm H2O at 30 liter/minute, which would provide humidification of 30.8 gm/m3 for 24 hours. The HME filter was positioned at the Y-piece of the ventilator circuit, between the patient's airway connection and the ventilator tubing. Heat and moisture exchangers was electively changed once in 24 hours or earlier if it was visibly clogged. Standard VAP prevention bundles were implemented for all patients, regardless of their study arm. These bundles included regular oral care, head elevation, proper positioning, avoiding proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) unless indicated, and adequate respiratory toileting by open suctioning. The unit's standard policy of employing pressure-controlled ventilation with lung-protective strategies, in accordance with the PALICC guideline, was consistently followed throughout the study period. There were no changes made to the trial after commencement.

The principal investigator monitored all subjects daily for composite primary outcomes (occurrence of any one of the following: AAO, VAP CDC, or VAP physician diagnosed) and secondary outcomes (frequency of endotracheal suctioning, maximum peak inspiratory pressure, and need for rescue to the HH group). The outcome assessment for VAP was not blinded in this study, which might have a subjective component. The primary outcome (VAE) was assessed till the end of day 7. Hence, all patients were followed for 7 days for the primary outcome. The primary outcome (VAEs) was calculated as the number of VAEs per 1,000 ventilator days. This information was available for all patients, whether they were extubated before or at 7 days.

Being a pilot RCT, a sample size of 60 children was chosen to assess the feasibility and safety.

All data were analyzed using SPSS 14.0. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test determined data normality. Frequencies and percentages represented categorical variables. Continuous data were presented as mean ± SD for normal data and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normal data. Independent Student's t-test and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare continuous variables between groups for normal and non-normal data respectively. Chi-square/Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables. Intention-to-treat analysis was used for comparing the primary and secondary outcomes. The primary outcome was compared between groups and is expressed as a relative risk with its corresponding 95% confidence interval. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

All 362 children who needed mechanical ventilation were screened consecutively for eligibility. A total of 256 children did not meet the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Among the 60 eligible children, post randomization, 31 children were enrolled in the HH arm and 29 in the HME arm.

Consort flow diagram

The baseline characteristics of the recruited children were similar between the two groups except for gender (Table 1). The median age of children in the HH group was 63 months, compared to 59 months in the HME group. The groups had similar body mass index (BMI) and weight for height (WFH) for 6–15 and 2–5 years, respectively. The median PRISM 3 score at enrollment was 9 in the HH arm and 10 in the HME arm, suggesting 4.6% and 4.2% mortality, respectively. The median number of days of PICU stay was comparable between the two groups, 8 days in the HH arm vs 10 days in the HME arm. Though only children expected to have a minimum 48-hour duration on invasive mechanical ventilation were enrolled, this subjective criterion proved to be an inaccurate estimate, and only 47 (78.3%) of 60 children completed two calendar ventilator days and started the third. The HH group had 25 out of 31 (80%) children entering day 3 of mechanical ventilation, while the HME group had 22 out of 29 (75.9%) (p-value of 0.67) children receiving mechanical ventilation for 3 days or more. The duration of mechanical ventilation was also similar between the groups.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the feasibility and utility of HME in children aged 2–15 years in an intensive care setting. This study recruited children on the day of admission to PICU if the expected duration of mechanical ventilation was more than 48 hours to align with the VAP definition by the CDC.9 During the study, 13 (21.7%) children were extubated within two calendar days of intubation and hence only 47 (78.3%) children could complete two calendar days of mechanical ventilation and enter day 3. The judgment of anticipated 48 hours of mechanical ventilation at the time of intubation was challenging due to multiple factors affecting it (provisional diagnosis, severity of illness, physiological derangements, and rate of recovery), and it was purely based on the clinician's experience and primarily subjective.10 A failure of judgment approximately by 20% observed in our study may help plan similar studies in the future, especially while determining sample sizes.

The most common indication for mechanical ventilation in our study was children with neuroventilation, accounting for 36.7% of all participants. This may be due to the inclusion criteria of recruiting children with an expected duration of mechanical ventilation of more than 48 hours, which is more likely in CNS diseases. This could affect the primary outcome since children with primary nervous system disorders need longer ventilator support and hence can have higher chances of having VAEs. In our study, ventilator days (a secondary outcome) were similar between groups, though the number of children with neurologic issues was higher in the HH group (41.9%) as compared to the HME group (31%).

The major risks while initiating a patient on mechanical ventilation are infection risk (VAP) and life-threatening mechanical events (AAO). The risk of occurrence of VAP between HH vs HME methods has shown conflicting results from adult studies, with few studies showing similar rates, while others showing higher incidence in the HH group.6,11–21 The definitions used to diagnose VAP were not consistent across the studies. From past studies, it is clear that the incidence of AAO is very less overall. However, few studies have shown that the incidence of AAO was higher in the HME method, while few showed no difference.6,12,13,15,16,21–26 With this background, to capture the entire risk profile, we chose a composite primary outcome that included VAP and AAO. For VAP, we used the standard CDC definition as an event and/or also included another event of physician diagnosis of VAP (child treated as VAP by change of antibiotics, though not satisfying the CDC definition of VAP) as part of the composite outcome so that all real-life events were captured. In diagnosing AAO, we used an objective criterion of need for a change of ET tube following a tubal block, rather than subjective criteria or percentage change in diameter (baseline obstructed) of the ET tube, which has less clinical significance. We believe that the composite outcome we had chosen was a clinically relevant primary outcome parameter.

In this study, children in the HME group had more VAEs than the HH group (28.36 vs 12.98 per 1,000 ventilator days). While we found no statistically significant difference, possible factors for a numerically higher VAE rate in the HME group could include subtle differences in humidification efficacy leading to thicker secretions and subsequent VAP, or due to the random occurrence in a pilot study, or due to the small sample size. We emphasize that these are speculative and require confirmation in larger trials. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have looked at VAP and AAO as individual or composite outcomes to assess humidification methods among children in intensive care settings.

To date, five systematic reviews/meta-analyses have evaluated the impact of humidification methods on VAP and AAO, predominantly in adult populations. Only one neonatal study by Daoud et al. addressed AAO specifically in the pediatric and neonatal age groups.22 In 2003, Bench reviewed 19 RCTs, but only two met the inclusion criteria (590 adults), reported no difference in ET tube occlusion, and a higher incidence of VAP in the HH group.15 Kola et al. analyzed 8 RCTs (693 in HME vs 675 in HH arms) and found reduced VAP risk in the HME group (RR 0.7, 95% CI: 0.5–0.9).17 Niël-Weise et al. included 10 RCTs, 9 of which compared HME and HH for VAP; 8 showed no difference.27 Vargas et al. analyzed 18 RCTs comparing HH vs HME for VAP (RR 0.932, 95% CI: 0.73–1.19) and AAO (RR 1.85, 95% CI: 0.79–4.33), with no significant differences.21 The 2017 Cochrane review found VAP incidence of 32/1,000 ventilator days in the HH group vs 30/1,000 in the HME group (RR 0.93, 95% CI: 0.73–1.19), and AAO incidence of 23/1,000 vs 37/1,000 (RR 1.59, 95% CI: 0.6–4.19).6 These findings support no clear superiority of either method. Although our pilot study's findings are consistent with previous research, the results should be interpreted with caution until larger pediatric trials are completed.

The merits of our study are as follows. It is the first pediatric randomized controlled trial to compare humidification methods in an intensive care setting and assess ventilator-associated adverse events (VAEs). Secondly, we employed a clinically relevant composite outcome that included both CDC and physician-diagnosed VAP and AAO, enhancing the study's potential clinical utility. But our study had several limitations. The sample size, being a pilot RCT, was small. Second, the same size HME was used for a wide range of children aged 2–15 years. Third, we did not measure specific parameters such as work of breathing, percentage of leakage during invasive ventilation, total flow, or dead space. Fourth, direct measurement of humidity at the Y piece was not done in either group. Hence, we were not able to directly compare the efficacy of humidification between HH and HME. Fifth, the absence of independent, blinded adjudication for outcomes such as VAP, which can have an element of subjectivity, might have introduced an element of observer bias. Finally, we have excluded children with airway pathology and severe ARDS from this study, which limits the generalizability of our findings to these specific patient populations, who may have different humidification requirements or VAE risks. Being a single-center study with a limited sample size, the results of our study may not apply to other settings.

Future studies could be improved by using various-sized HMEs according to the child's weight or age, employing an objective measurement of work of breathing, monitoring leak percentage and dead space before and after HME placement, measuring humidity level by both methods, and monitoring air flows, as it could influence absolute humidity levels.

Conclusion

This pilot randomized controlled trial, conducted in a tertiary-level PICU, found no significant difference in the incidence of VAEs between HME and HH in mechanically ventilated children aged 2–15 years. These findings suggest that HME may be cautiously considered a feasible and cost-effective humidification option for a similar study population, provided there are no other contraindications. Although this study offers preliminary insights, larger and multicentric trials are warranted to further explore these outcomes.
