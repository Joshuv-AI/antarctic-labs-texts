Absolute oral bioavailability of milvexian spray‐dried dispersion formulation under fasted and fed conditions in healthy adult participants: An intravenous microtracer approach
Authors: Praneeth Jarugula, Sharif Soleman, Hyunmoon Back, Lisa J. Christopher, Dara Hawthorne, Ronald Aronson, Anh Bui, Angela Mirzac, Antoinette Ajavon‐Hartmann, Vidya Perera, Bindu Murthy, Samira Merali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMilvexian is an oral, small‐molecule factor XIa inhibitor being developed to prevent thromboembolic events. This study assessed the absolute bioavailability (F) of milvexian following single doses of milvexian spray‐dried dispersion (SDD) formulation under fed and fasted conditions, and milvexian solution, in healthy adult participants using an intravenous microtracer approach. This was a phase I, open‐label, partially randomized, 4‐sequence, 5‐period crossover study. After fasting for ≥10 h, participants received milvexian 200‐mg oral solution with a 100‐μg 14C milvexian intravenous microtracer at the time of maximum observed plasma concentration. Following a 3‐day washout, participants were randomized to 1 of 4 milvexian SDD treatment sequences in a crossover fashion: 25 mg fasted, 25 mg fed, 200 mg fasted, or 200 mg fed. Pharmacokinetic data were collected up to 72 h postdose. Seventeen participants were dosed, and 14 completed treatment. Under fasted conditions, milvexian F was ~100%, 58.2%, and 54.2% following administration of the oral solution, 25 mg SDD, and 200 mg SDD, respectively. Under fed conditions, milvexian F following 25 mg and 200 mg SDD was 44.3% and 75.6%, respectively. The milvexian SDD formulation at 25 mg and 200 mg resulted in similar F in a fasted state; under fed conditions, milvexian F decreased at 25 mg and increased at 200 mg. These findings clarify pharmacokinetic‐related gaps observed in previous studies.

INTRODUCTION

Thromboembolic disease, including arterial and venous thrombosis, is a major cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide. 1 Anticoagulants have a long history of use for the prevention and treatment of thromboembolic disease. 2 , 3 The relative safety of anticoagulants has improved with recent advances, but dose‐dependent bleeding remains a concern. 4 , 5 Therefore, the development of novel antithrombotic therapies with safer bleeding profiles continues to be an unmet need.

Coagulation factor XIa is a new target for the prevention of thromboembolic events. 6 , 7 Findings from clinical, preclinical, and epidemiologic studies suggest that factor XIa inhibitors may provide an improved benefit–risk profile for anticoagulation over the current standard of care by preventing thrombotic events without increasing the risk for bleeding. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Milvexian is an oral, small‐molecule factor XIa inhibitor. In recent clinical studies, differences were observed in milvexian disposition that may be due to the formulation and food effect. An absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) study of an oral solution of milvexian 200 mg found a twofold higher exposure compared with that observed in a first‐in‐human (FIH) study utilizing a SDD formulation of milvexian 200 mg, which led to increased renal excretion (30% vs. <15%, respectively, with both formulations evaluated in a fasted state). 12 , 13 Administration of once‐daily milvexian (20–200 mg) under fasted conditions led to a dose‐proportional increase in exposure, while greater than dose‐proportional exposure was observed after twice‐daily dosing when food intake was not controlled in the FIH study. 13 Furthermore, in the FIH study, increasing bioavailability was observed at doses of 200 and 500 mg when administered with food. However, the food effect was not studied at 25 mg, the lower end of the clinically relevant dose range.

In order to understand these differences in milvexian disposition and identify contributing factors, this study evaluated the absolute bioavailability (F) of single varying doses of the milvexian SDD formulation that encompasses the clinically relevant dose range, and oral milvexian solution in healthy adults using an intravenous (IV) microtracer. Further, milvexian doses were given under fed and fasted conditions to assess for a potential food effect on different doses.

METHODS

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles that have their origin in the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice, as defined by the International Council for Harmonization and in accordance with the ethical principles underlying European Union Directive 2001/20/EC and the US Code of Federal Regulations, Title 21, Part 50 (21CFR50). The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04965389). The protocol, amendments, and participant informed consent received appropriate approval by the Institutional Review Board/Independent Ethics Committee prior to the initiation of the study at the site. Prior to the beginning of the study, all participants provided written informed consent.

Study design

This was a phase I, open‐label, partially randomized, 4‐sequence, 5‐period crossover study, with a three‐day washout between milvexian treatment groups, conducted in healthy adults (Figure 1). Participants underwent screening evaluations to determine eligibility within 28 days before study treatment administration. Participants were admitted to the clinic the day prior to treatment initiation and remained at the facility for the duration of the dosing periods, including washout. In Treatment Period 1, after fasting for ≥10 h, all participants received a single dose of milvexian 200‐mg oral solution followed by 100‐μg 14C milvexian IV microtracer that was infused over 15 min and given at the time of maximum observed plasma concentration (T max) of the oral dose (1 h postdose). The treatment in period 1 was fixed without randomization for operational simplicity so that the radiolabel‐related monitoring requirements did not need to be applied to all treatment periods. Following washout (72 h), participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 4 treatment sequences where they received a single dose of milvexian (25‐ or 200‐mg SDD capsule) under fed (high‐fat meal consumed within 30 min predose) or fasted (overnight fast of ≥10 h) conditions. Participants were discharged from the clinic 3 days after the final dose of study treatment and followed up by phone 5–7 days after the final dose of study treatment. The total duration of study participation was ~7 weeks.

Participants

Eligible participants included healthy men and women (not of childbearing potential or breastfeeding) who were 18–65 years of age with a body mass index of 18.0–32.0 kg/m2.

Participants were ineligible if they had a history of gastrointestinal (GI) disease, a history of upper or lower GI bleeding, or any GI surgery that could affect absorption of the study drug; history or evidence of intracranial bleeding; history or evidence of abnormal bleeding or coagulation disorder; use of nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, anticoagulants, or any other drug or agent known to increase the risk for bleeding within 2 weeks of dosing with the study drug; or any acute or chronic illness or history of illness considered clinically significant by the study investigator.

Pharmacokinetic assessments

The pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of milvexian (plasma and urine) and 14C milvexian (plasma only) were derived from plasma and urine concentration versus time data. Milvexian plasma PK was collected predose (before the oral dose) and at 0.5, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 18, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h postdose (after the oral dose). Milvexian urine PK was collected predose (before the oral dose) and at 0–6, 6–12, 12–24, 24–36, 36–48, and 48–72 h postdose (after the oral dose). 14C milvexian plasma PK were collected predose (before the oral dose) and at 5, 10, 20, 30, and 45 min and 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 17, 23, 35, 47, and 71 h postdose (after the 14C milvexian). Assessed parameters included maximum observed plasma concentration (C max), T max, area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) from time zero to time of last quantifiable concentration (AUC[0–T]), AUC from time zero extrapolated to infinite time (AUC[INF]), terminal plasma half‐life, apparent clearance of drug after extravascular administration (CLT/F), apparent volume of distribution at terminal phase after extravascular administration (V z /F), percentage of urine recovery, and F.

K2EDTA plasma samples were analyzed for milvexian concentration using a validated liquid chromatography mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) assay (for measurement of unlabeled drug from the oral regimens) or by a qualified accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) assay (for the measurement of 14C milvexian from the IV route of administration). Urine samples for milvexian were analyzed by a validated LC–MS/MS assay. Both LC–MS/MS assays had a lower limit of quantification of 1.00 ng/mL and an upper limit of quantification of 1000 ng/mL.

The AMS assay had a lower limit of quantification of 1.08 mBq/mL or 7.72 pg/mL and an upper limit of quantification of 215 mBq/mL or 1537 pg/mL.

Both the plasma and urine study samples (50.0 μL sample aliquot) were extracted using protein precipitation (acetonitrile), followed by HPLC separation and MS/MS detection for the determination of milvexian. Stable labeled milvexian [M + 4] was used as the internal standard. The urine samples only were treated with CHAPS detergent (3‐((3‐cholamidopropyl) dimethylammonio)‐1‐propanesulfonate) at a concentration of 0.25% before analysis to prevent non‐specific binding of milvexian to the urine collection containers and other labware. Standards, quality controls (QCs), and blanks in urine were also prepared with 0.25% CHAPS as the urine matrix to mimic the study samples. For the AMS analysis, the plasma samples were also extracted using protein precipitation. Fractions containing [14C]‐milvexian were collected via chromatographic separation using UPLC and photodiode array (PDA) detection at 254 nm and were further processed prior to the AMS analysis. The QC performance summary during sample analysis for both validated assays is given in Table S1.

Intravenous microtracer

An IV microdose of milvexian containing microtracer amounts of 14C was delivered following administration of an oral dose of milvexian to determine the bioavailability of the oral dose compared with the IV dose. The IV dose was 100‐μg milvexian containing no more than 15.99 kBq. This IV dose was 1/2000th of the highest single oral dose administered in the study and 1/1000th of the likely highest therapeutic dose and hence was an appropriate IV microtracer dose. A potential concern about possible nonlinearity of PK following the milvexian IV microdose relative to the milvexian oral therapeutic dose was addressed by administering the IV microdose at the T max of the unlabeled extravascular therapeutic dose, 14 as has been demonstrated by other studies. 15 , 16 This approach exposes the body to therapeutically relevant concentrations of the compound, addresses concerns regarding PK linearity, and improves precision of the F estimate due to the lack of interoccasion variability. 14 In order to achieve sufficient bioanalytical sensitivity for the microdose and differentiate the plasma concentrations resulting from the radiolabeled dose versus the oral dose (measured by LC–MS/MS), the ultra‐sensitive method of AMS was used to quantify plasma concentrations of 14C milvexian derived from the IV route, 17 with most of the 14C milvexian signal expected to come from the parent drug. 12

Safety assessments

Safety and tolerability were assessed based on medical review of adverse event (AE) reports and the results of vital sign measurements, electrocardiogram measurements, physical examinations, and clinical laboratory tests.

Statistical analyses

The safety population included all participants who received at least 1 dose of milvexian. The PK population included all participants who received at least 1 dose of milvexian and had any available concentration–time data. The evaluable PK population included all participants who received at least 1 dose of milvexian and had any available concentration–time data with adequate PK profiles to allow for accurate estimation of PK parameters. The determination of the study sample size was not based on statistical power considerations. However, it was calculated that data from 13 participants would provide 80% probability for the 90% confidence interval (CI) of the geometric mean ratio for milvexian to be within 80%–125% of the point estimate. A sample size of 16 participants to yield 13 evaluable participants was considered appropriate to meet the objectives of the study.

To assess relative bioavailability, a linear mixed‐effects model with treatment as a fixed effect and measurement within each participant as a repeated measure was fitted to the natural log‐transformed PK parameters (i.e., C max, AUC[INF]) for use in the estimation of effects and the construction of CIs. Kenward‐Roger degrees of freedom were specified in the model. Point estimates and 90% CIs were exponential, and results were reported for 14C milvexian 100‐μg IV microtracer versus milvexian 200‐mg oral solution and milvexian (25‐ or 200‐mg) SDD in fasted and fed conditions. Parameter estimates were dose‐normalized for comparisons.

Descriptive statistics, including number of observations, arithmetic mean, standard deviation (SD), median, minimum, and maximum, were calculated for continuous variables. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. Geometric mean and coefficient of variation were calculated for plasma and urine PK parameters only. PK parameters were derived using non‐compartmental analysis by a validated PK analysis program (plasma: Phoenix® WinNonlin® Version 8.0, Certara, Princeton, NJ; urine: SAS software Version 9.4®, SAS Institute, Cary, NC). All statistical analyses and calculations were performed using SAS software Version 9.4® (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

A total of 36 healthy participants were enrolled in this study, of which 17 met eligibility criteria and were randomized to 1 of 4 treatment sequences (4–5 participants per sequence). Of the 17 participants, 14 (82.4%) completed the study; reasons for not completing the study included poor compliance/noncompliance (incomplete IV dose), participant withdrawal (personal reasons), and other (left knee injury) (n = 1, each). The mean (SD) age was 42.3 (13.0) years, and the mean (SD) body mass index at screening was 26.3 (3.9) kg/m2; 76.5% of participants were male (Table 1).

Pharmacokinetics

A total of 16 of 17 participants were included in the PK population. One participant was excluded from both populations where no concentration–time data were available due to dosing issues for 14C milvexian 100‐μg IV microtracer. Quantifiable predose concentrations >5% of the corresponding C max were observed for 12 participants (17 profiles in total) across milvexian 200‐mg SDD fasted (7%), milvexian 25‐mg SDD fed (67%), and milvexian 25‐mg SDD fasted (40%); however, no PK data from the affected participants were excluded and these predose concentrations were handled as in the analysis.

Mean milvexian plasma concentration–time profiles in fasted and fed states are shown in Figure 2. Peak (C max) and total (AUC[INF]) exposure were highest following administration of milvexian 200‐mg oral solution (2929 ng/mL and 31,435 ng∙h/mL, respectively; Table 2). Peak and total dose‐normalized exposures were 49%–64% lower after administration of milvexian 25‐mg SDD compared with milvexian 200‐mg oral solution, with the SDD formulation exhibiting 25% lower exposure under fed conditions compared with fasted conditions at the 25‐mg dose. Peak and total dose‐normalized exposures were 28%–60% lower after administration of milvexian 200‐mg SDD compared with milvexian 200‐mg oral solution, with the SDD formulation exhibiting 38% higher exposure under fed conditions compared with fasted conditions at the 200‐mg dose.

For all SDD treatments, peak concentrations of milvexian occurred at a median T max between 4 and 5 h postdose irrespective of fed condition. Peak concentration occurred earlier for the oral solution, with a median T max of 1.27 h postdose (Table 2). After peaking, mean concentrations declined in a multiphasic manner, with measurable concentrations observed up to 72 h postdose for both SDD and oral solution formulations.

Under fasted conditions, the F of milvexian was ~100% following administration of the 200‐mg oral solution, and 58.2% and 54.2% following administration of milvexian 25‐mg and 200‐mg SDD, respectively (Table 2). Under fed conditions, the F of milvexian 25‐mg and 200‐mg SDD was 44.3% and 75.6%, respectively. For milvexian 25‐mg SDD, there was an approximately 24% decrease under fed versus fasted conditions (F of 44.3% vs. 58.2%, respectively). For milvexian 200‐mg SDD, there was an ~40% increase under fed versus fasted conditions (F of 75.6% vs. 54.2%, respectively).

Mean urinary excretion of milvexian was 25.6% for the 200‐mg oral solution and ranged from 13.2% to 19.1% for the 25‐mg and 200‐mg SDD formulations (Table 2). For milvexian 25‐mg SDD, urinary excretion was lower in the fed versus fasted condition (13.2% vs. 17.6%), whereas it was higher in the fed versus fasted condition with milvexian 200‐mg SDD (19.1% vs. 13.9%).

Safety and tolerability

Administration of 14C milvexian 100‐μg IV microtracer and milvexian SDD (25 and 200 mg) under fed and fasted conditions was generally well tolerated. There were no deaths or serious AEs during the study, and no participants discontinued the study due to an AE. Overall, 9 participants (52.9%) reported at least 1 AE during the study (Table 3), 8 of whom reported an AE after the first treatment period (milvexian 200‐mg oral solution with 100‐μg IV microtracer). The most common (≥10%) AEs were headache, reported in three participants (17.6%), and abdominal discomfort, back pain, constipation, and diarrhea, reported in two participants each (11.8%). All other AEs were reported by one participant each (5.9%). All AEs were mild in intensity except for a moderate AE of joint injury in one participant and moderate AEs of back pain and neck pain in another participant. Three participants had physical examination results that were reported as AEs: chest erythema, maculopapular rash, and swollen left knee. No clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, or electrocardiogram results were reported as AEs. Two participants (11.8%) each reported AEs of abdominal discomfort and diarrhea, which were considered related to study treatment (milvexian 200‐mg oral solution with 14C milvexian 100‐μg IV microtracer); 1 participant (5.9%) reported an AE of urticaria, which was considered related to study treatment (200‐mg oral solution with 14C milvexian 100‐μg IV microtracer); 1 participant (5.9%) reported an AE of diarrhea, which was considered related to study treatment (milvexian 200‐mg SDD fasted).

DISCUSSION

The current study evaluated the F of the SDD formulation of milvexian at 25 and 200 mg, which was chosen as the dose range as it represented the lower and higher end of the dose range in the milvexian phase II studies. 18 , 19 This study found that the SDD formulation of milvexian at 25 and 200 mg under fasted conditions resulted in similar F, while under fed conditions, milvexian F decreased at 25 mg and increased at 200 mg. The milvexian SDD formulation had an F of 58% and 54% under fasted conditions (25 and 200 mg, respectively) and an F of 44% and 76% under fed conditions (25 and 200 mg, respectively). F was ~100% following administration of a 200‐mg milvexian oral solution relative to the 100‐μg IV microdose, which was considered complete bioavailability, when accounting for assay variability.

Peak concentrations of milvexian occurred at a median T max of between 4 and 5 h postdose following administration of milvexian 25‐mg and 200‐mg SDD, which was independent of food effect. Under fed conditions, peak (C max) and total (AUC[INF]) plasma exposures of milvexian were reduced following a single administration of milvexian 25‐mg SDD and increased following a single administration of milvexian 200‐mg SDD. Urinary recovery was observed to increase with food at 200 mg and decrease with food at 25 mg.

In the current study, the exposures of each dose of milvexian in the fasted state were consistent with findings from prior studies, as were the exposures after administration of milvexian 200‐mg SDD in the fed state. 12 , 13 Exposures after administration of milvexian 25‐mg SDD in the fed state had not been evaluated in prior studies, but when food effect was studied at 200 and 500 mg in the FIH study, milvexian exposures increased by 43% and 111% in the fed state compared with the fasted state, respectively. 13 In the current study, milvexian exposures increased by 38% at 200 mg in the fed state compared with the fasted state, which was similar to what was observed in the FIH study, 13 and milvexian exposures decreased by 25% at 25 mg in the fed state compared with the fasted state. Overall, the food effect across doses of 25–500 mg suggests that the bioavailability of milvexian, when administered with food, increases with increasing dose, which is a reflection of a dose‐dependent food effect on milvexian F.

The dose‐dependent increase in milvexian SDD F under fed conditions explains the greater than dose‐proportional exposures that were previously observed following twice‐daily administration. Milvexian is lipophilic with poor aqueous solubility and good permeability. It is also a P‐gp substrate, 20 which may limit bioavailability at the lower doses, as was demonstrated by greater than dose‐proportional exposures between 4 mg and 20 mg in the FIH study. 13 At these lower doses, where absorption is limited by permeability, food may cause a decrease in exposures. 21 , 22 In line with this, the reason for the observed dose‐dependent food effect in the current study is hypothesized to be due to the differential influence on P‐gp saturation, and/or differential solubility limitations in the fed and fasted state in the presence or absence of food, specifically increasing milvexian solubility at the higher dose. The 25% decrease in exposures in the fed versus fasted state at 25 mg is within the range of variability observed (32.6%–40.0%). In contrast, in the FIH study, milvexian exposures were observed to be less than dose‐proportional at higher doses (>200 mg) due to potential solubility limitations. 13 Thus, at these doses, food may help to overcome this solubility limitation and thereby increase bioavailability.

Differences in milvexian F between the oral solution and SDD formulation explain the previously observed variations in urinary fraction excreted between formulations. In this study, the same urinary excretion was observed for the IV formulation and oral solution, consistent with the 100% relative bioavailability of the oral solution relative to the IV formulation. Urinary excretion was ~32% of the total dose in the ADME study of milvexian 200‐mg oral solution, while urinary excretion was 6.9%–17.8% of the total SDD dose in the FIH study of milvexian at doses of 200–500 mg. 12 , 13 In this study, the SDD formulation led to similar mean urinary excretion, ranging from 13.2% to 19.1% across doses (25 and 200 mg) and food intake states (fed or fasted), thus confirming that the observed differences in urinary excretion were driven by bioavailability.

Quantifiable predose concentrations >5% of the corresponding C max were observed for 12 participants; however, the majority (65%) were <15% of the corresponding C max. Despite having a washout period (72 h) for more than 5 half‐lives of milvexian (~55 h), these quantifiable predose concentrations were observed potentially due to sequences in which a low‐dose (25‐mg) treatment period followed a high‐dose (200‐mg) treatment period. This is supported by the fact that the 25‐mg treatment periods, both fed and fasted, had the most quantifiable predose concentrations >5% of the corresponding C max. Despite the quantifiable predose concentrations, this study provides insights into milvexian PK at 25 mg and a qualitative assessment of the food effect. These quantifiable predose concentrations were not expected to affect the assessment of the primary objective of evaluating milvexian F for the oral solution and SDD formulation under fed and fasted conditions. Therefore, no PK data from the affected participants were excluded, and all participants were included in the calculation of concentration and parameter summaries and all statistical analyses.

In conclusion, these findings help fill PK‐related gaps observed in previous milvexian studies that appear to reflect absorption differences by formulation and by dose when administered in a fed state. Furthermore, regardless of the fed condition, both the oral and SDD formulations of milvexian were generally well tolerated by the healthy participants in this study. These insights support the application of these findings to formulation, dose, and regimen optimization across the milvexian development program.
