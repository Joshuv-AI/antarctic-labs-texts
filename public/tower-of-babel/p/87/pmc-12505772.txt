The surgical team’s amputation approaches in orthopaedic traumas following the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake
Authors: Gül Özlem YILDIRIM, Bektaş SARI, Gülseren KESKİN
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to explore and document the surgical team’s experiences with amputations in orthopaedic trauma cases following the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake.MethodsThis research utilized a phenomenological approach to explore the meaning of experiences related to amputation surgery after the earthquake. Twelve orthopaedic surgeons and surgical nurses were selected through purposive sampling. After obtaining ethical approval (Report No: 23-12T/22), data collection commenced on January 6, 2024. Data were collected via 424-minute semi-structured interviews, each lasting 22 to 63 min, and analyzed using MAXQDA software.ResultsElective procedures were cancelled at two remote trauma centres, and patients were evacuated to free up hospital wards. Surgical shifts were organized, and a dedicated communication channel was established via WhatsApp. Triage was performed in the operating room, focusing on pediatric cases. Common cases included lower extremity fractures, with fasciotomy as the typical procedure. The surgical team noted the importance of extending the amputation indication period to save extremities and give patients a chance. The team experienced symptoms such as sorrow, exhaustion, and moments of crying.ConclusionOrthopaedic surgeons and nurses play a crucial role in earthquake-related surgical procedures. After the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake, they volunteered for extended hours beyond their scheduled shifts, deriving moral satisfaction from their efforts. While amputation is complicated and often unwanted, it can be necessary to save lives, resulting in profound emotional experiences such as sadness and fatigue for surgeons and nurses. Mandatory training for all healthcare professionals on earthquake injuries is recommended.Reporting methodAdhered to the COREQ checklist.Keywords: Orthopaedics, Trauma, Surgical nursing, Earthquakes, Phenomenology

Background

Türkiye lies on the seismically active Anatolian plate, making it one of the countries most affected by devastating earthquakes [1]. On February 6, 2023, Türkiye was struck by two powerful earthquakes, measuring Mw7.7 and Mw7.6, centered in the Pazarcık and Elbistan districts of Kahramanmaraş [2]. A state of emergency was declared across ten provinces, and the World Health Organization categorized it as a Level 3 Emergency [1, 3]. The death toll reached 50,783, with over 107,204 injured. The earthquakes affected an area of 500 km, damaging over 500,000 buildings and disrupting vital infrastructure [4]. Hospitals sustained significant damage, forcing health workers to operate from ground floors until temporary facilities were established, while aftershocks continued. Most hospitals could only begin admitting new patients once the situation stabilized [5–7].

After the earthquake, the Turkish Ministry of Health sent the National Medical Rescue Team (UMKE) and Emergency Medical Teams (EMSs) to the affected areas. The quake struck in freezing February, making many roads impassable. Despite these challenges, the teams reached disaster zones to conduct search and rescue operations and establish emergency response units, providing limited treatment to injured individuals [6–8]. Injured individuals were transported to local hospitals with minor structural damage, initially classified as green and yellow during triage. Local capacity was quickly exceeded, necessitating the transfer of patients to neighboring facilities for advanced medical care [9]. Conventional methods, including ambulances and alternative transport, were used to move the injured to nearby provinces [4, 5]. Following the earthquake, many critically injured patients were urgently relocated to the Ankara Bilkent Level I Trauma Center, 595 km away, and Mersin City Hospital, 300 km away [9, 10]. Reports indicated that the overall condition of the injured individuals transferred to distant medical centers was critical, primarily due to complications such as tissue compression, advanced crush syndrome, compartment syndrome, and the need for interventions like amputation, fasciotomy, and dialysis [9, 10]. These factors emphasize the crucial role of orthopaedic surgeries for patients, resulting in the Orthopaedics and Traumatology Clinic staff experiencing the highest workload [11]. Most of the treatable injuries were orthopaedic in nature, with fractures of the lower limbs identified as the most common earthquake-related injuries, accounting for over half of the recorded cases [12–15].

Given the complexity of orthopaedic injuries in disaster settings and the high incidence of extremity trauma following major earthquakes, understanding the experiences of surgical teams is essential to improving disaster preparedness and medical response strategies. Recent studies analyzing the orthopaedic impact of the Kahramanmaraş Earthquake primarily focus on retrospective analyses of patient outcomes, fracture distributions, and surgical interventions [6, 7, 10, 13, 16, 17]. However, none have examined the lived experiences of surgeons, the ethical challenges of amputation decisions, or the emotional toll of disaster medicine. By utilizing a phenomenological approach, this study provides a novel perspective on orthopaedic surgical response in disaster scenarios. This study aims to explore the lived experiences, decision-making processes, and ethical dilemmas faced by orthopaedic surgeons and surgical nurses performing amputations following the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake.

Methods

Research design

This study used the phenomenological approach, commonly employed in qualitative research. Van Manen’s hermeneutic phenomenological method was utilized to understand various phenomena, serving as a guideline for collecting and analyzing data regarding lived experiences in clinical practice settings [18, 19]. This approach aims to uncover the essence or meaning of an event by focusing on lived experiences [20, 21]. In hermeneutic phenomenological research, it is essential to maintain impartiality, identify the subject, formulate precise research questions, determine the research design, establish participant criteria, collect, and analyze data rigorously, and draft a comprehensive final report [18, 22].

Participants

The participants comprise 12 individuals, including seven orthopaedic surgeons and five surgical nurses, who work at Ankara and Mersin City Hospitals and performed more than half of the total orthopaedic surgeries following the earthquakes. In this study, the purposive sampling method was used for participant selection and they were approached by telephone. This method applies to selecting people who can deliver the most practical acuities to achieve a profound knowledge of the subject. The strength of this technique lies in its ability to explore detailed aspects comprehensively [18, 23]. The participation criteria for this study are being a surgeon or a surgical team member who performed amputation surgery on patients admitted to the hospitals after the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake. The first participant, identified through the Web of Science, was a surgeon who performed orthopaedic surgeries after the earthquakes and published his findings. The selection continued using the snowball technique. Two participants declined to participate in the study due to their busy work schedules. Data collection

The researchers decisively collected data online using a semi-structured interview form. The meetings were scheduled between 1 January and 30 April 2024 and included 12 parties. The combined time of all the interviews was 424 min, ranging from 22 to 63 min each. In qualitative studies, concerns regarding sample size typically focus on achieving data saturation. However, factors such as research objectives, sampling methods, and the qualifications of participants are also crucial. In this study, seven orthopaedic surgeons served as team leaders, each having performed hundreds of amputations and fasciotomies following the earthquake (they reported that the number is over 500, but specific details and patient information are confidential). Consequently, they possess extensive experience relevant to the study’s goals. Prior to conducting the interviews, the researchers introduced themselves and outlined the purpose of the research and questions. Participants decided on the date and time of the online meeting and joined alone from their homes, workplaces, and outside locations. Researchers inquired about participants’ comfort levels with this format to minimise external influences and distractions during online meetings. The participants expressed that they felt comfortable and believed that online meetings could enhance their openness, allowing them to share their feelings and emotions without hesitation. Two pilot interviews were conducted with a surgeon and a nurse, followed by axial coding to identify broader themes relevant to the research questions. Participants were asked open-ended questions to explore their opinions, feelings, and behaviors, and the questionnaire was added to the end of the document.

A probing strategy was implemented using three questions: “Can you explain more?“, “What do you mean by that?” and “How do you feel about that?“. Each participant provided written and verbal consent before the interview, granting permission for audio and video recordings, and they received the transcripts after the interviews. The first author (Ph.D.) and the second author (Ph.D.) conducted all interviews jointly, recording the audio of the responses and taking notes. They were lecturers at the university at the time of the study and published several articles using a qualitative approach. Facilitators are also interested in the research topic, which is one of their characteristics. Participants verified the accuracy of their answers, and the data was coded after each interview. Data collection continued until enough information was gathered to achieve saturation, which happens when existing codes are repeated rather than new ones being constructed [24]. Two researchers who conducted the coding process agreed on saturation when new themes ceased to emerge. Each participant was interviewed once, and no repeat interviews were conducted.

Data analysis

The thematic data analysis method, which provides a systematic strategy for analyzing qualitative data sets, was conducted using the MAXQDA qualitative study software [18]. Initially, all interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Participants were identified by numbers instead of their names for being confidential. The participants were given an opportunity as a member-checking procedure to review the results and ensure their accuracy [25]. Nine of the participants provided positive feedback on the results; others did not provide any feedback. Subsequently, two researchers identified the semantic units within the sentences. Coding involves organizing data to answer research questions, with semantic units coded by their similarities and differences [18]. Coded units in the study should convey facts pertinent to the analysis and prompt the reader to consider implications beyond the specific information. Additionally, the codes should represent the smallest unit of information about something that can exist independently [26]. After reviewing the data, the two researchers agreed on the codes, categories, and themes derived from the data. The coders chose to create a coding table instead of a coding tree.

Trustworthiness

In qualitative research, ensuring validity and reliability is of utmost importance, and it hinges on the researcher’s credibility, accuracy, and competence. Guba and Lincoln’s credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability criteria have been widely embraced by researchers who evaluate the research’s quality [18, 23, 27–29]. Credibility refers to the trustworthiness of findings based on data, while transferability involves applying those results to other contexts. Dependability emphasizes consistent results across studies, allowing outsiders to find them logically. Confirmability ensures that findings are rooted in participants’ narratives and free from researcher biases [18]. In qualitative research, validity and reliability are ensured through various methods, including prolonged interchange, expert inspection, and inclusion and exclusion criteria [18, 30]. This study rigorously applied inclusion criteria, expert sentiments, and inter-coder agreement strategies to guarantee the validity and trustworthiness of the results.

Results

The study focuses on the surgical team’s experiences performing amputation and fasciotomy on patients who survived the earthquake. Our analysis revealed four primary themes concerning the surgical team’s experiences: (1) surgical planning and organization, (2) the nature of earthquake surgery, (3) decision-making regarding fasciotomy and amputation, and (4) the overall experiences of the surgical team.

In Fig. 1, the document describes how hospital operating rooms were prepared for patients after the earthquake. Orthopaedic surgeons organized round-the-clock surgeries with specialized teams and repurposed tables from other departments to expand capacity while postponing non-urgent cases. Despite the demanding workload, the surgical staff was adequate. A WhatsApp group was created to enhance communication among healthcare professionals. The team ensured timely transfers of trauma patients, particularly pediatric cases, to the operating room to minimize delays, with quick triage conducted in the non-sterile area.

The affected body parts primarily included lower extremities, upper extremities, spine, and pelvis. Case types encompass injury, multiple traumas, freezing, and amputation, with fractures being the most common. Various medical supplies were used during and after surgery, and all participants confirmed that the supplies were sufficient with no shortages. In Fig. 3, the experiences of the surgical team when deciding on fasciotomy and amputation are depicted. According to the study, all participating surgeons extensively emphasized that they opted to wait despite indications for limb salvage, extending the indication period and affording patients a chance. Some orthopaedic surgeons admitted that these actions contradicted information in the literature, but they found that more limbs were saved without resorting to amputation. Before proceeding with amputation and fasciotomy, all patient information was shared in a WhatsApp group that included orthopaedic, vascular, general, and plastic surgeons, as well as physicians specializing in internal medicine, radiology, nephrology, and psychiatry. The surgical team based their amputation decisions on clinical findings such as necrosis, tension, pain with passive movement, edema, pallor, and circulatory abnormalities.

Figure 4 details the experiences of the surgical team members in which the most prevalent psychological and physical symptoms reported were sorrow, physical exhaustion, anxiety, and crying. Within the administrative problems category, personnel assignment was a prominent issue, followed by children without parents, patient transport problems, and patient registration problems. The most common ethical consideration was announcing the amputation decision, which the surgical team described as the most difficult. Additional codes in this category included no clinical dilemma, unpleasant treatment, and dilemmas in delayed cases. As for suggestions, the participants emphasized the need for crush injury training and suggested disaster preparedness training and the assignment of child development specialists. The surgeons described themselves as calm and resilient but found the decision to perform fasciotomy or amputation, especially in children, to be particularly challenging. One highly experienced surgeon shared that he struggled to even see or touch the fingers of injured young children due to emotional difficulty. Another participant noted that they often opted for limited debridement to preserve young patients’ extremities, while amputation was more frequently considered for frail, older patients. Overall, the surgeons did not face ethical dilemmas in their decision-making, but the age of their patients—either very young or very old—affected them emotionally.

P4 “On the first day, we performed 127 surgeries, a significant number involving children. After returning home the following morning for the first time, I did not return for another 25 days. Discussion

Amputation has a a significant place among injuries after earthquakes. Acute compartment and crush syndrome may develop in many patients after earthquakes, leading to limb loss and even death [33, 34]. These patients can only survive with appropriate treatment [34]. There are publications that explicitly recommend emergency fasciotomy in the treatment of diagnosed compartment syndrome. A consensus is that an appropriately performed fasciotomy can be both limb-saving and often life-saving [10]. Timely and appropriate fasciotomies can prevent permanent nerve damage and muscle death [35]. Early fasciotomy plays a crucial role in the treatment of injured extremities [34, 36]. However, severe damage to arteries, nerves, skin, and bone, along with prolonged ischemia and failed fasciotomy, are the primary causes of amputation following earthquakes [14, 37]. In cases of crush syndrome characterized by extensive and severe crushing, resulting in impaired circulation, irreparable arterial injury, and the inability to free the limb due to entrapment, or in situations involving multiple organ failure and uncontrollable sepsis, limb amputation is recommended as a life-saving intervention. This approach prioritizes preserving the patient’s life over pursuing limb-sparing treatments [38]. In our study, orthopaedists stated that to save the extremities, they waited for the decision of amputation if there was no apparent life-threatening condition and prolonged the indication period. In the meantime, they made efforts to save the limb by performing serial wound debridements and fasciotomies. Amputation of irreparable limbs eliminate fatal complications and can save lives. Therefore, the management of crushed limbs after earthquakes continues to pose a significant challenge for orthopaedic trauma surgeons [14, 37]. The literature suggests that patients should be discussed regarding the severity of the impact on a patient’s life, regarding the options of amputation or limb salvage, however, in extraordinary situations, such as disasters, the surgical team may adopt a paternalistic approach, which can compromise the patient’s chances of survival [39].

The orthopaedists who participated in our study reported that although there are medical protocols for evaluating the viability of the limbs, they make decisions according to the clinical findings of the injured people who come to their centers and stated that they trust their experience. Compartment syndrome may develop after an earthquake, especially after fractures in the extremities [40]. Although measurement of intracompartmental pressure provides information about the process of the syndrome, it has also been reported in publications that orthopaedic surgeons rely on clinical examination due to the low availability of measurement equipment in a disaster environment [41, 42]. It has been reported that intracompartmental pressure monitoring may be helpful when consciousness is altered, but false-positive results when performed at a single time and differences between measurements near or far from the injury site make the use of pressure measurement alone inappropriate [43, 44].

The surgical team members who participated in our study stated that patients sent from the emergency department were re-triaged in the non-sterile corridors of the operating rooms. However, one important topic is that the aseptic technique is essential to surgical care [45]. In situations where ongoing examinations are not feasible, and there is a sudden surge in the number of patients, the surgeon may opt for an early fasciotomy. The primary concern in such scenarios is the heightened risk of compromised tissue infection in an unhygienic setting. Nevertheless, the dangers associated with overlooking compartment syndrome in these instances are recognized to be more immediately severe than the risk of infection [41, 42]. In this case, it can be said that the orthopaedists who participated in our study made decisions based on the principle of saving life. In extremity contusions after earthquakes, acute compartment syndrome should be diagnosed immediately.

Amputation is a traumatic procedure for both the surgery itself and its consequences. The decision to amputate is difficult for the medical staff, the patient, and the family. In addition to the physical pain, the experience of amputation as an assault on bodily integrity can exacerbate a variety of issues that affect the patient’s well-being. The prospect of losing a visible part of the body can evoke a genuine sense of bodily fragmentation [46, 47]. However, amputation may be necessary to save life [39]. In the aftermath of the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake, the condition of the patients trapped under the collapsed structures was critical. Most of the patients had surpassed the expected recovery time for their injuries, their trauma scores had worsened, and it was determined that amputation was necessary [48]. It has been found in the literature that most patients prefer limb salvage over amputation at the time of injury, but after unsuccessful limb salvage, an overwhelming number of patients express a wish to undergo primary amputation instead [39].

Considering the significant levels of stress and anxiety that surgical teams often experience, it is crucial to note their potential impact on possible complications. Studies indicate that surgeons frequently cite complex or uncommon procedures and inadequate support as the most stressful factors, with this anxiety heightening the risk of intraoperative complications [49]. In our study, high anxiety was one of the significant emotional themes found in the surgical team. In their study, Dupley et al. found that participants who showed high levels of anxiety about their own clinical competence also reported higher levels of burnout [50] The experience of losing a part of one’s body can be emotionally overwhelming, requiring an empathetic approach to support the understanding of patients’ experiences. Empathic interactions enable healthcare staff to see the world from a patient’s perspective and assist in decision-making. While these interactions positively affect patient outcomes, there is a hypothesis that high levels of empathy can result in compassion fatigue and emotional burnout [51–53]. In the literature, it has been determined that surgeons prefer to avoid empathic emotions to minimize the emotional impact of trauma and to minimize compassion fatigue [54, 55]. In our results, we found that the themes of empathy and burnout were prominent in the surgical team. Contrary to the findings of our study, the literature has reported that empathy may be a protective factor against burnout; putting oneself in the patient’s shoes and accepting that emotions are separate from the patient’s is a self-other distinction, and this reduces empathy associated with negative emotions, thus helping to prevent burnout [56]. Sturzu et al. found that an empathic caregiver reduces patients’ anxiety, improves clinical outcomes, and increases healthcare personnel’s sense of usefulness and job satisfaction [57]. While numerous studies have highlighted the positive and protective effects of empathy, this research draws attention to the less commonly discussed negative aspects. It underscores that healthcare professionals may experience psychological impacts when confronting traumatic events.

During the study assessing the amputation experiences of the orthopaedic trauma surgical team in the aftermath of the Great Kahramanmaraş Earthquake, the surgical team expressed feelings of profound sadness, physical fatigue, anxiety, and frequent episodes of crying as part of their psychological and physical symptoms. These codes were followed by empathy, gratitude, pride codes and sleep disturbences. Prolonged participation in emotionally demanding work can reduce healthcare workers’ productivity and the quality of patient care while also increasing the likelihood of depressive symptoms, sleep disorders, fatigue, and even suicidal tendencies among healthcare personnel [58]. Shalhub et al. found results that support our findings, with a significant number of surgeons who were concerned about negative outcomes for their patients having higher reported anxiety levels [59]. Surgeons are at exceptionally high risk for burnout syndrome due to the high risks associated with their speciality [60]. At the same time, working in a disaster area may contribute to employee burnout in addition to known risk factors [61]. It has been reported that the risk of infection in the surgical team during emergency care after a disaster and limited hospital resources support higher depression, anxiety, distress, and job dissatisfaction [62]. Gratitude is a feeling of grace, deep appreciation, or thankfulness. A sense of gratitude is strongly associated with the experience of positive emotions of appreciation, and individuals who experience a sense of gratitude are less likely to suffer from feelings of depression and anxiety [63]. In disaster zones, it is crucial to thoroughly examine the mental obstacles that healthcare workers in surgical teams may face during critical procedures such as amputations. This assessment is vital for implementing necessary preventive measures to avert potential crises in surgical interventions, particularly in disaster settings.

Personnel assignment problem, pediatric patients, patient transportation errors were identified under the category of administrative problems. In cases where the hospital building could not be used in the first hours of the earthquake, it was reported that there were patients who were intervened in front of the emergency department and in the open area, and that there were problems with anamnesis records, documentation of examination findings and transportation of patients. It was found that there were problems especially in the long-term follow-up of patients who developed crush syndrome after the earthquake, received limb-sparing treatment or underwent amputation [38, 64, 65]. Family support and counseling is important, especially in children. However, the team may experience difficulties during amputation intervention especially in children who experienced parental loss during the disaster period [66, 67]. It is stated that additional difficulties, such as the lack of special equipment required to maintain body temperature and minimize excessive blood loss in injured pediatric patients after surgical intervention in children after disasters, postoperative care in children, and lack of prosthetic facilities sustain the anxiety of the surgical team [68].

In the category of ethical considerations, the most intensely occurring code was the making and explaining of the amputation decision. Professionals evaluate the adventages and disadventages of an amputation for the patient, the patient’s condition, the level of amputation and the level of functionality after amputation. If the amputation is decided upon, the decision will be explained to the patient [69]. The decision for amputation should be made by taking the psychological factors that the patient may experience related to amputation in consideration such as pain, depression, anxiety, fear, and impaired body perception. Also, duration of injury, risk of infection and other comorbidities may affect the amputation decision [64, 65]. The decision to amputate after an earthquake should not be considered as a failure. This decision should consider the fact that the patient, the medical team often survived a disaster, the risk of disaster is still present, and the life-saving role of amputation and the protective role of organ function should not be forgotten [43]. The literature supporting our findings highlights that ethical decision-making in amputation and limb salvage should respect autonomy, benevolence, and non-maleficence. Each decision must be tailored to the individual patient, considering their preferences while aiming to maximize well-being and prevent harm [70]. It has even been mentioned that the surgeon should review his own experience with amputation and complex reconstruction, and if he does not feel competent and ready for such an intervention, he should avoid this intervention [44]. Our study demonstrates that the children are a challenging group regarding the decision to amputate. For children, this process may be more chaotic, as children often react differently to stressful traumatic events compared to adults, and posttraumatic stress symptoms may be worsened with functionality, cosmetic concerns and psychological difficulties [66, 67].

When orthopaedists informed conscious and communicative patients about the decision to amputate, they sought to balance the patient’s autonomy with the physician’s duty to promote beneficence. This decision involves weighing survival against the risk of long-term disability. Surgical nurses played a crucial role in this process; while doctors explained the medical necessity of the amputation, nurses offered emotional support and fostered a safe environment for patients to express their feelings. Additionally, nurse volunteers cared for young children in the wards, highlighting their compassionate approach during such critical moments. Notably, the data gathered through qualitative research enabled a thorough examination of the challenges faced by healthcare professionals and their coping mechanisms and needs in response to disasters. Key factors, such as personal experiences and emotional responses, can only be uncovered through qualitative methods. The insights gained can inform future disaster response strategies, and the analysis of these experiences may contribute to the establishment of post-disaster psychological support and educational programs.

Limitations

As participants retrospectively recounted their experiences, specific details may have been subject to memory distortion. Additionally, three participants did not provide feedback on the member-checking procedure, which may pose a risk of misinterpretation specifically for those individuals.

Conclusion

In contrast to earlier studies that mainly emphasized quantitative evaluation of earthquake-related injuries, this research offers a qualitative perspective on the experiences of surgical teams. By exploring the emotional, ethical, and procedural challenges encountered by orthopaedic surgeons and nurses, this study reveals new insights into the psychological burdens and complexities of decision-making in disaster medicine. The surgical team’s most critical issue was their choice to extend the amputation indication period, allowing patients a chance to save their limbs. In the decision on amputation indication, surgeons mostly acted according to their clinical experience and the patient’s condition. Nurses took an active role in explaining the amputation decision to the patients and their relatives and organizing the traffic in the operating rooms. The orthopaedic team voluntarily treated the wounded in the clinics for days, and they experienced moral satisfaction as they helped the team by working outside of work hours. Amputation was the most challenging treatment for the surgical team, but it was accepted as a last resort to save lives. The team experienced deep sadness in this respect. Hospitals and disaster response agencies should establish simulation-based training programs that closely mimic real-life earthquake scenarios, enabling surgical teams to refine their trauma triage and amputation decision-making skills. Furthermore, it is essential to implement interdisciplinary training that involves orthopaedic surgeons, anesthesiologists, emergency physicians, and rehabilitation specialists to improve coordination in disaster situations. By shedding light on the ethical dilemmas, psychological burden, and clinical challenges surgical teams face in mass-casualty disasters, this study emphasizes the urgent need for structured training programs, mental health support, and standardized surgical protocols. Strengthening these areas will not only improve surgical preparedness but also ensure better patient outcomes in future disasters. Furthermore, developing concise guidelines for critical surgical interventions, such as fasciotomy and amputation, is vital in disaster medicine education. These guidelines should integrate technical skills, decision-making processes, psychosocial factors, and ethical considerations to improve medical policies and education.
