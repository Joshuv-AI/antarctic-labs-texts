Comparison of the effects of vocal and instrumental traditional music on anxiety in candidates for general surgery: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Mohammad Amin Nouri, Mansour Rezaei, Alireza Khatony
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety is a common issue among patients who are candidates for surgery. Limited studies have explored the impact of two types of vocal and instrumental traditional music on anxiety levels, and their results have been different. Therefore, this study was conducted to compare the effects of vocal and instrumental traditional music on anxiety levels among candidates for general surgery.MethodsIn this clinical trial, 102 general surgery patients were randomly assigned to one of three groups with 34 participants each: instrumental music, vocal music, and a control group. The data collection tools consisted of a personal information form and the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-State Form. The intervention duration was set at 15 min. The instrumental and vocal music interventions were implemented for the respective intervention groups, while the control group used headphones connected to a silent playback device. The collected data were analyzed using descriptive and analytical statistics with SPSS-18 software.ResultsThe mean score of state anxiety before the intervention in the instrumental music, vocal music, and control groups was 40.1 ± 10.7, 40.7 ± 11.7, and 38.5 ± 10.2, respectively. After the intervention, the mean scores changed to 34.3 ± 8.9, 35.8 ± 9.8, and 38.8 ± 10.0, respectively. The paired t-test revealed a statistically significant difference in anxiety levels before and after the intervention within both the vocal and instrumental music groups (P < .001).ConclusionsBoth instrumental and vocal traditional music demonstrated a positive impact on reducing patients’ anxiety. Therefore, considering the low cost and absence of side effects associated with this method, its utilization is recommended for candidates undergoing general surgery.

Introduction

One common issue faced by patients undergoing general surgery is anxiety [1]. Anxiety is an unpleasant and indistinct feeling [2] that often emerges in unfamiliar situations, fear of death, or the possibility of harm to the body or one’s functioning [3–5]. Factors contributing to preoperative anxiety can include pain, sleep disturbances, and separation from family [6]. Preoperative anxiety can lead to negative consequences such as increased blood pressure, heart rate, sleep problems, sweating, nausea, and impaired concentration [7]. The prevalence of anxiety in adult patients scheduled for surgery is approximately 20% [8]. Pharmacotherapy is a commonly employed method for treating anxiety, involving the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), buspirone, and benzodiazepines [8]. Although these medications are effective, they often come with multiple side effects such as restlessness, nausea, headache, tremors, sweating, diarrhea, and constipation [8]. Consequently, the use of complementary medicine approaches like hypnosis, aromatherapy, and music therapy has gained popularity in recent times [9, 10]. Non-pharmacological methods typically entail minimal side effects or potential risks and can be used independently or in conjunction with other treatments [11].

Music therapy is a non-pharmacological method [10] that offers cost-effective and risk-free alternatives compared to pharmacological approaches [12–14]. Music has the ability to modulate the activity of brain structures involved in emotional and motivational processes, including the amygdala and limbic system [15]. It can suppress the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system, which is activated by anxiety, while activating the parasympathetic branch, thereby inducing a calming response [16]. Music also has the potential to increase dopamine secretion, which plays a vital role in human behavioral, cognitive, and emotional functioning [17]. Furthermore, music has been found to reduce physiological responses such as respiration, blood pressure, and heart rate [16, 18, 19]. It diverts the patient’s attention from pain and anxiety, alleviates the sense of being in an unfamiliar environment, and enhances adaptation to the surroundings [16, 20–22].

Numerous studies have investigated the impact of music on preoperative patient anxiety, yielding varied results. Some studies suggest that music can reduce anxiety [23–26], whereas others indicate no significant effect [27, 28]. Interestingly, certain studies have even found that music can increase anxiety levels in patients [29]. In terms of the type of music, one study suggests that music with lyrics has a greater impact compared to instrumental music [30]. However, another study reports that instrumental music has a greater effect compared to music with lyrics [20]. These diverse findings highlight the variability in the impact of music on anxiety levels, which may be influenced by factors such as the type of music, individual characteristics of the participants, and environmental conditions [24, 31]. Therefore, the present study was designed and conducted to compare the effects of vocal and instrumental traditional music on the anxiety levels of surgical patients. This study aimed to address the following research questions:

Methods

Study design

The study was conducted from March 11, 2022, to March 17, 2023, using a randomized controlled clinical trial design with a 1:1:1 allocation ratio among patients undergoing general surgery. The data reporting followed the CONSORT reporting guidelines [32].

Study hypothesis

This study hypothesized that vocal and instrumental traditional music would have an equal impact on the apparent anxiety levels of general surgical patients.

Samples and sampling method

The study population consisted of all general surgical patients admitted to Imam Reza and Taleghani Hospitals in Kermanshah, western Iran. These hospitals are specialized centers for surgery and are considered major surgical facilities in the region. The sample size was determined using the percentage comparison formula with a 95% confidence level and 90% test power, assuming a 10% sample dropout rate. As a result, the sample size was calculated to be 34 individuals in each group, totaling 102 participants [33]. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: being a candidate for general surgery, willingness to participate in the study, age 18 years and above, experiencing anxiety, having an interest in traditional music, no drug addiction (as reported by the patients), no thyroid diseases (based on patient statements), no history of active psychiatric disorders (according to patient self-disclosure), and no hearing impairment. The Whisper Test was used to assess the patients’ hearing ability.

Patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study using convenience sampling and then randomly assigned to one of the intervention groups (instrumental music, vocal music) or the control group using a six-person block randomization method. Each block contained numbers one to six, and each patient was assigned one of these numbers. Patients were allocated to one of the study groups based on their assigned numbers. The randomization blocks were prepared by a statistical consultant, who served as the second author of the article. The first author of the article performed the process of random allocation of patients to the study groups. The randomization process followed the following steps: (a) Six-person blocks were used for randomization. (b) Each study group was assigned a specific letter: A for instrumental music, B for vocal music, and C for the control group. (c) Seventeen opaque envelopes were prepared, corresponding to the 102 samples. The blocks were written on each envelope, and each envelope contained six cards. (d) Each card had the name of the respective study group (A, B, or C) written on it.

The exclusion criteria for the study included unwillingness to continue cooperation, transfer of the patient to another department, and receiving sedative or narcotic medications during the intervention period or before completing the second questionnaire session [31].

Instruments

The study utilized several tools, including a personal information form, the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-State Form (STAI-S), a music player, and wireless headphones. The personal information form collected age, gender, education, marital status, type of surgery, and surgical history. The STAI-S was designed by Spielberger and colleagues in 1970 to assess levels of state anxiety [34]. The validity and reliability of this tool have been examined and confirmed in previous studies, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.92 [35]. The Persian version of the STAI-S has also been psychometrically evaluated in Iran, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.85 for internal consistency [36]. The STAI-S consists of 20 items that measure an individual’s feelings of anxiety at the moment of responding. The questionnaire’s score range is between 20 and 80, and individuals are categorized into one of the following groups based on their acquired scores: mild anxiety (20–39), moderate anxiety (40–59), and severe anxiety (60–80). Additionally, it should be noted that the study used the LD-30 Lander branded music player and the Lenovo Livepods branded wireless headphones, both made in China, for music playback.

Intervention

After obtaining study approval, the first author visited the surgical departments of Imam Reza and Taleghani hospitals daily to identify patients who met the inclusion criteria. The eligible surgical candidates were informed about the study objectives, and if they expressed interest, they were enrolled in the study. Participants completed the personal information form and STAI-S questionnaire 15 min before the intervention. In the instrumental music group, participants listened to the song “Bidar,” composed by the renowned Iranian musician Parviz Meshkatian, through headphones. In the vocal music group, participants listened to the traditional song “Ey Namat,” performed by Maestro Alireza Eftekhari, through headphones. During the intervention, participants were instructed to lie calmly in bed, and they were allowed to adjust the volume of the music according to their preference. It is important to note that before playing the music for each participant, the devices used were calibrated to ensure their proper functioning and the quality and quantity of sound output. Furthermore, in line with hygiene standards, the headphones were thoroughly disinfected by the researcher using an appropriate disinfectant solution before being used by each participant.

The music intervention lasted for 15 min for both groups. In the control group, headphones connected to a silent player were used for 15 min to eliminate environmental distracting noises.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS-18 software, employing descriptive and inferential statistics. Descriptive statistics, such as frequency distribution, mean, and standard deviation, were utilized for the descriptive analysis. In the inferential analysis, analysis of variance (ANOVA), chi-square tests, and paired t-tests were employed. ANOVA was used to examine the homogeneity of demographic variables across the study groups for quantitative variables, while chi-square tests were used for qualitative variables. One-way analysis of variance was performed to compare the mean anxiety levels among the study groups before and after the intervention at each stage. Additionally, paired t-tests were conducted to compare anxiety scores within each group before and after the intervention. The designated level of significance for all tests was set to be less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The present trial was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, with the code IR.KUMS.REC.140000.804. The study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (www.irct.ir) with the code IRCT20100913004736N25 on 04/03/2022. The study objectives were thoroughly explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained from them. Patients were assured that their information and responses would be treated as confidential.

Result

The mean age of the participants in the study groups was 43.4 ± 13.3 years. Among the samples, the majority were male (n = 55, 53.9%), married (n = 78, 76.5%), had a history of surgery (n = 66, 64.7%), and had educational attainment below the diploma level (n = 58, 56.9%). The most prevalent type of surgery was related to the gastrointestinal system (n = 58, 56.9%). Discussion

This clinical trial aimed to investigate the impact of instrumental and vocal traditional music on anxiety levels in general surgical patients. The results indicated that both instrumental and vocal music have the potential to reduce anxiety levels in these patients. Previous studies have also explored the influence of music on anxiety levels in patients. For instance, a clinical trial conducted in 2020 involved 46 patients who were randomly exposed to different interventions for 15 min daily over five consecutive days, including pop music, rock music, Western classical music, Iranian traditional music, or silence. The findings demonstrated that “silence” followed by Western classical music had the most significant impact in reducing anxiety [30]. These findings are consistent with our study and suggest the effectiveness of music. However, it is important to note that the interventions in the mentioned study, conducted over five consecutive days, may have been tiring for the patients, which could have influenced the results. In contrast, our study was conducted one hour before surgery and lasted for 15 min, providing a different context for the intervention. In a 2015 study that aimed to examine the effect of music on anxiety levels in elective surgical patients, 159 participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: classical music, New Age music (instrumental music), or a control group. The results indicated that New Age music had a greater impact than vocal music in reducing anxiety [20]. These findings differ from the results of our study, where both types of vocal and instrumental music had the same effect on reducing patients’ anxiety. Furthermore, in a randomized clinical trial conducted in 2021, aimed at investigating the effect of music on preoperative anxiety in 90 children with congenital heart disease, the participants were randomly assigned to either a music therapy group or a control group. In the music therapy group, the participants listened to calming or children’s music for 30 min. Anxiety levels were assessed using the Modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale-Short Form. The results demonstrated the effectiveness of music in reducing anxiety, while anxiety levels increased in the control group [23]. Although our study utilized different assessment tools and intervention durations compared to this study, our findings are consistent with it. In a clinical trial conducted in 2021 to examine the effect of music on preoperative anxiety in women undergoing hysterectomy, the samples were allocated into two groups: music therapy and control. The music therapy intervention consisted of three stages, including the performance of improvised instrumental music, listening to music accompanied by breathing control techniques, and listening to participants’ preferred music. The control group did not receive any intervention. The results indicated the effectiveness of music in reducing patient anxiety [24]. Despite methodological differences, our study aligns with the findings of this research. Regarding the mechanism of music’s effect, it has been suggested that music can modulate the activity of brain structures such as the amygdala and limbic system [15]. It can deactivate the sympathetic system and activate the parasympathetic system [16]. Additionally, music has been found to increase dopamine secretion [17]. Music also diverts the patient’s attention from pain and anxiety, eliminates the sense of being in an unfamiliar environment, and enhances adaptation to the surroundings [16, 20–22].

Despite the results of the current study, which indicate the effectiveness of vocal and instrumental music in reducing anxiety levels, some studies have reported no effect of music on patient anxiety [27–29]. A trial conducted in 2022 on 176 patients undergoing lithotripsy allocated the samples into four groups: music, aromatherapy, combined music and aromatherapy, and control. The music group listened to traditional instrumental music for 15 min before and during the lithotripsy procedure. However, the results of this study showed no significant effect of music on anxiety reduction [29]. Similarly, in another trial conducted in 2021 to examine the effect of music on anxiety levels in 102 women undergoing radiation therapy, the participants were divided into two groups: music and control. The music group listened to their favorite music before and after the first radiation therapy session, while the control group did not receive any intervention. The results indicated no significant difference in post-intervention anxiety levels between the two groups [27]. Furthermore, a study conducted in 2008 aimed to investigate the impact of music on hemodynamics, anxiety, pain, and analgesic drug consumption in 80 women undergoing cesarean section. The participants were randomly assigned to two groups: music and control. The music group listened to music for 30 min, while the control group used headphones without music. The results of this study revealed no statistically significant difference in post-intervention anxiety levels between the two groups [28]. The findings of our study are not consistent with the aforementioned studies, which could be attributed to differences in the patients’ clinical conditions, individual characteristics, and methodology employed.

Limitations

When interpreting the results, it is crucial to consider the limitations of this study. The first limitation pertains to the absence of bias control regarding the comorbidities and corresponding treatment drugs in the included cases. Consequently, the results do not exclude the potential influence of patient condition and drug efficacy on anxiety scores. Another limitation is the potential mismatch in sound exposure between the control and experimental groups, which may compromise the establishment of a fully matched control condition. Additionally, the lack of consideration given to patients’ music preferences when selecting the type of music emerges as a limitation, as it can impact their response to the intervention. Furthermore, the relatively short duration of the music intervention poses another limitation that may affect the effectiveness of music therapy. Finally, the exclusive focus on general surgery patients raises concerns regarding the generalizability of the findings to other populations.

Conclusions

The findings of the study indicate that both instrumental and vocal traditional music have the potential to decrease anxiety levels in patients undergoing general surgery. Given the therapeutic qualities of music, including its ease of implementation, non-invasiveness, and cost-effectiveness, it is recommended to incorporate music therapy as a strategy for reducing preoperative anxiety in healthcare settings. Additionally, future studies should consider the comorbidities and corresponding treatment drugs of the participants, as well as their music preferences, and extend the duration of the intervention to further investigate its effectiveness.
