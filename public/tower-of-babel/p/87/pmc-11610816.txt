Nerve conduction velocity studies in diabetic peripheral neuropathy involving sural nerve—A meta-analysis
Authors: Praveen Prashant, Sat Pal, Abhishek Bansal, Sanjay Fotedar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Diabetes mellitus (DM) poses a significant health burden globally, impacting millions due to its metabolic disruptions. Among its complications, diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is a prevalent concern, affecting sensory and motor nerves. While various diagnostic methods exist, discrepancies persist in prevalence estimates of DPN among diabetic populations.Objective:This meta-analysis aimed to assess the prevalence of DPN in individuals with DM, focusing on sural nerve conduction velocity (NCV) measurements. Additionally, the study explored how different evaluation techniques influenced prevalence estimates.Methods:A systematic literature search was conducted following PRISMA guidelines across multiple databases. Studies reporting sural nerve NCV measurements in diabetic patients were included. Data extraction, quality assessment, and statistical analyses were performed to synthesize findings.Results:Twenty-six studies met the inclusion criteria, providing insights into sural nerve NCV among diabetic individuals. Pooled analysis revealed a mean sural nerve NCV of 42.12 m/s (95% CI: 39.87-44.36), indicative of reduced conduction velocity in diabetic populations. Furthermore, sural nerve amplitude was significantly lower in diabetic individuals, with a pooled mean of 4.68 μV (95% CI: 3.11-6.25).Conclusion:Individuals with type 2 DM exhibit impaired sural nerve function, as evidenced by reduced NCV and amplitude measurements. Timely NCV assessment, alongside glycemic monitoring, is crucial for identifying and managing DPN to prevent further complications. Interventions addressing glycemic control, dietary regulation, and lifestyle modifications are recommended to mitigate the progression of neuropathy in diabetic populations.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a clinical metabolic condition characterized by elevated blood glucose levels due to a complete or relative lack of insulin. DM encompasses several variations due to hereditary and environmental variables. Diabetes patients are prone to complications due to pathophysiologic and metabolic alterations. DM can be classified as type 1 and type 2 while type 2 DM accounts for 90% of all diabetes cases. Type 2 DM, also termed as insulin resistance, occurs when the body cannot respond to insulin adequately. This leads to an initial surge in insulin synthesis, followed by a subsequent decline in insulin production.[1]

It primarily affects adults and elderly persons although younger individuals are also at risk due to unhealthy eating habits, obesity, and lack of exercise and other physical activity. Other risk factors include impaired glucose tolerance, smoking, and a previous history of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Due to its gradual onset, DM sometimes goes misdiagnosed for extended periods, as there are no apparent symptoms initially. A prolonged neglection in the absence of a suitable diagnosis might lead to problems associated with persistent hyperglycemia.[2]

Urbanization and the adoption of a modern lifestyle, characterized by sedentary behavior and the intake of unhealthy foods, are contributing factors to the development of DM.[3] Randomized controlled studies conducted in many regions like Finland, the USA, China, India, and other parts of the world have demonstrated that lifestyle modifications such as increased physical activity and/or a healthy diet can effectively prevent the development of type 2 DM.[3]

Moreover, studies have indicated that the occurrence of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) might be as high as 35% (95% CI: 15% to 55%) when type 2 diabetes is initially diagnosed, indicating the presence of an early subclinical phase of the illness. According to the Toronto International Agreement for Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy, a conclusive diagnosis necessitates the presence of at least one symptom and/or at least one indication of neuropathy, as well as an anomaly in nerve conduction tests. Nevertheless, deviations in nerve conduction studies (NCS) that evaluate the functioning of big myelinated nerve fibers responsible for touch, proprioception, vibration, and motor function are further indications of DPN. In contrast, it is believed that small nerve fiber impairments, which impact unmyelinated or sparsely myelinated C and A-delta nerve fibers responsible for temperature, pain, and autonomic function, arise at an earlier stage in DPN. However, the natural progression of small and larger nerve fiber disorders remains uncertain.[34]

While several researches have shown the occurrence of peripheral neuropathy in individuals with diabetes, the results in this field are inconsistent. Some studies indicate a significant incidence of peripheral neuropathy while others imply a lower prevalence. In individuals with diabetes, the presence of persistent or episodic pain is frequently an initial indication of minor nerve fiber losses, even when neuropathy cannot be clinically detected. Both skin biopsy and corneal confocal microscopy (CCM), which are methods capable of measuring early tiny nerve fiber damage, provide aberrant results in individuals with diabetes. Additional proof of little participation of larger fibers may be observed in nerve conduction study (NCS) investigations, which demonstrate no discernible distinction between individuals with diabetes and those with normal glucose tolerance (NGT).[35]

Aside from NCS and CCM, many screening and diagnostic procedures may be used to evaluate DPN, including physical examination, quantitative sensory testing (QST), and skin biopsy. These tests have different levels of sensitivity and specificity.[6] The UK’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence advises using a 128 Hz tuning fork with a 10 g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament for vibration perception tests to screen for DPN. Nevertheless, these tests detect severe DPN at a stage that is both late and irreversible and occurs before the development of ulcers in the diabetic foot. The sural nerve is purely sensory, and it supplies sensation to the lower lateral leg, lateral heel, ankle, and dorsal lateral foot and is mainly involved in DPN. Being superficial, it is easily accessible for the non-invasive nerve conduction velocity studies.[7]

Medical professionals including the family medicine specialists play a crucial role in the management of individuals with diabetes and possess a deep understanding of the adverse impacts of DPN on patients’ well-being and overall health results. Despite the existence of multiple diagnostic techniques, there continue to be inconsistencies in accurately determining the prevalence of DPN within diabetic communities. This comprehensive meta-analysis review was aimed at estimating the prevalence of peripheral neuropathy in persons with diabetes and investigating how prevalence estimates are affected by the technique of neuropathy evaluation by NCV studies for sural nerve. We propose that the diagnostic yield will be contingent upon the evaluation tool’s capacity to identify anomalies in tiny nerve fibers.

Methodology

This meta-analysis adheres to relevant reporting guidelines such as Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).

Literature search

The relevant studies using multiple electronic databases such as PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar were searched. A comprehensive search strategy using relevant keywords such as “sural NCV,” “DM,” “nerve conduction studies,” etc., was employed. The relevant journals and conference proceedings were searched by hand for additional studies. The search included studies published in various languages to minimize language bias and later translated to English using online tools available within the research scope.

Study selection

The retrieved studies based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were selected.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form to collect relevant information from selected studies was developed. The data including study characteristics (authors, publication year, study design), participant characteristics (sample size, age, gender, diabetic status), methodology details (NCV measurement techniques), and outcome data (mean NCV, standard deviation, P values) were extracted.

Quality assessment

The included studies using appropriate tools such as the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies or the Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized controlled trials were assessed for quality. The risk of bias in each study based on factors like selection, performance, detection, attrition, and reporting were evaluated.

Data synthesis

A statistical analysis to synthesize the extracted data from individual studies was performed using StataSE ver 16 (64-Bit) for Windows. The summary estimates such as mean difference, standardized mean difference, or odds ratio using appropriate statistical methods (random-effect model) were calculated. The forest plots to visualize the effect sizes and confidence intervals across studies were generated. The heterogeneity among included studies using statistical tests, i.e. Cochran’s Q test, I2 statistic was assessed.

Result

Our research comprised a total of twenty-six research papers that met the qualifying criteria for our study. These articles specifically focused on studying the association between DM and sural NCV.

Discussion

Diabetic neuropathy (DN), with significant effects in people with DM, has garnered the interest of researchers over the past few decades. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) strongly recommends using both clinical history and examination to diagnose diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN). Nevertheless, the assessment of DSPN using clinical history and examination differs since there is a requirement for standardized baselines. Accurately determining the suitable group of patients is crucial for the correct and meticulous diagnosis of DSPN in clinical research. The ADA suggests using proven clinical tools with electrophysiology and measures of small fiber damage and repair acquired by NCS or CCM. Consequently, scientists and medical experts have carried out many clinical investigations on DSPN by employing alternative screening techniques and, frequently, several ways to identify DSPN precisely.[13]

Diabetic patients are frequently encountered in the healthcare facilities and the family physicians, and the clinicians have a crucial role in the early identification and treatment of DPN in order to minimize its negative consequences. This meta-analysis provides insights into the frequency of DPN in persons diagnosed with DM, specifically examining measurements of sural NCV. It is imperative for primary care physicians to comprehend the ramifications of diminished sural nerve NCV and amplitude assessments, as these parameters function as significant indications of neuropathic alterations in individuals with diabetes.[13]

Moreover, the ramifications of compromised sural nerve function transcend neurological manifestations, impacting the holistic welfare and functional capacity of individuals. The recognition of the wider ramifications of DPN on patients’ everyday activities, mobility, and quality of life is crucial for primary care physicians. Equipped with this comprehension, individuals can embrace a strategy for diabetic care, encompassing not just regulating blood sugar levels but also making changes to their lifestyle and implementing preventive actions to effectively treat DPN.[3512]

The subsequent diagnostic factors were considered for the meta-analysis and systematic review: SSNCV and SSNamp are abbreviations used to refer to sural NCV and sural nerve action potential for nerve conduction studies. This study investigated the correlation between the sural nerve and diabetes in individuals with a glycated hemoglobin level greater than 6.50%. Diabetologists generally agree that non-invasive and straightforward methods like NCS may be effectively utilized in clinical practice to detect early stages of DPN in asymptomatic patients. This early diagnosis can help reduce the occurrence of diabetic complications, including diabetic foot ulcers and the need for amputation being a late complication or not treated. Screening individuals with diabetes for neuropathy at the subclinical stage with frequent HbA1c readings and nerve conduction testing will help decrease poor glycaemic control and the high expenses associated with diabetic complications in Sudan. The study findings indicate that the prevailing symptoms seen in T2DM individuals with neuropathy were numbness and searing pain, with numbness being the most often reported symptom.[3512]

Assessing NCV levels in individuals with diabetes aids in determining the risk classification for diabetic neuropathy, a prominent source of morbidity in patients with DM. This study’s pooled mean sural nerve NCV was lower than the reference range. This finding is consistent with other studies done in India in 2019, the UK in 2017, China in 2018, another study in India in 2016, and a study conducted in Sudan in 2017.[61516]

The activation of the aldose reductase enzyme is believed to be the underlying pathophysiological process responsible for the microvascular neuropathy observed in diabetic individuals. Activation of aldose reductase can result in a decrease in the production of nitric oxide (NO), which in turn causes vasoconstriction, prolonged hypoxia, ischemia, nerve injury, and ultimately a reduction in NCV. Hyperglycemia, a metabolic disorder, can also induce low levels of NO in diabetic individuals. Conversely, unregulated high blood sugar causes more sorbitol to be produced, which then builds up. This buildup of sorbitol harms the nerves and slows down their ability to transmit signals. Therefore, reducing sorbitol levels can be advantageous for increasing NCV.[1124]

The study conducted by Peterson M et al. and 2017[27] discovered a correlation between the amplitude of the sural nerve and HbA1c levels. However, only the sural nerve was utilized in the investigation. The reduction in amplitude was more significant than the decrease in conduction velocity, indicating that axonal degeneration is an earlier and more noticeable consequence of hyperglycemia compared to demyelination.

In contrast to the study conducted by Velde et al. and 2020,[13] their findings indicated that individuals with elevated HbA1c levels had diminished amplitudes and decreased NCV in the evaluated nerves. Furthermore, greater HbA1c levels were associated with further reduced NCV, specifically in the sural nerve.

Our findings indicate no statistically significant link between NCV and the duration of DM. Conversely, a cross-sectional study conducted in India has determined that prolonging the duration of diabetes is a notable risk factor for aberrant NCV.[22]

According to a 2018 study conducted in India, it was found that there was a notable decline in NCV among individuals with diabetes who were five years or older, compared to those who were younger than five years. The study concluded that the risk of neuropathy complications increases as the duration and severity of high blood sugar levels (hyperglycemia) worsen.[22]

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that individuals with type 2 DM experience impairment in their median nerves. Specifically, there is a notable decrease in the velocity at which sensory signals travel in patients with inadequate blood sugar levels (HbA1c) management and a decrease in the strength of these signals with a longer duration of diabetes. Consequently, individuals with a prolonged duration of DM and inadequate management of blood sugar experience hindered nerve transmission. Therefore, it is advisable to regard NCS as a preliminary assessment method, alongside frequent HbA1c testing, for patients to hinder the advancement of neuropathy. Additionally, dietary regulation, medication usage, and adjustments to one’s lifestyle should also be implemented for better diabetic management.
