Plasma apolipoprotein A-IV deficiency as a proteomics-identified diagnostic biomarker for heat stroke: A prospective 2-center study
Authors: Ye Zhou, Qingwei Lin, Longping He, Qingbo Zeng, Lincui Zhong, Tao Wang, Jingchun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore proteomic changes from heat exhaustion (HE) to heat stroke (HS) and identify biomarkers for HS diagnosis.MethodsThis prospective 2-center study, conducted from October 2022 to February 2024, included 18 patients with HS and 23 patients with HE from a hospital in China. We used 4D-data-independent acquisition proteomics to analyze protein expression and identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) with a fold change ≥1.5 or ≤0.67, considering p < 0.05 as significant. Bioinformatics and Cytoscape were used to characterize DEP functions and identify key proteins. Receiver operating characteristic curves and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator algorithm were used to assess diagnostic potential, and parallel reaction monitoring validated DEPs. An independent cohort was used to validate biomarkers by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay.ResultsA total of 2604 overlapping proteins were identified, with 176 DEPs screened for significance. These DEPs were predominantly linked to biological processes such as inflammation, immune response, cell signaling, and metabolic regulation. Analysis revealed that apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4) exhibited the highest area under the curve (AUC) value among the hub proteins and was identified as a significant variable by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression. Furthermore, 18 proteins identified through 4-dimensional data-independent acquisition proteomics were confirmed using parallel reaction monitoring. Plasma levels of APOA4 were significantly reduced in HS patients compared to those with HE (p = 0.001). The AUC for diagnosing HS using plasma APOA4 was 0.81, with 77.8% sensitivity and 78.3% specificity. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay results corroborated these findings, with plasma APOA4 levels in HS patients being significantly lower than in HE patients, yielding an AUC of 0.74, with 66.7% sensitivity, and 84.2% specificity.ConclusionPlasma APOA4, which was significantly downregulated, may serves as a novel biomarker for the diagnosis of HS.

Introduction

As global temperatures escalate, the annual mortality attributed to extreme heat has surpassed the combined death toll from natural disasters such as hurricanes, earthquakes, and floods, with a mortality rate of 27%.1,2 Both incidence and mortality rates continue to climb.3 Prolonged heat exposure can precipitate heat-related illnesses, including heat exhaustion (HE) and heat stroke (HS).4 HE typically presents with severe fatigue, nausea, and headache, with body temperature below 40°C and unchanged mental status.5 In contrast, HS is characterized by a substantial increase in core body temperature (>40°C) coupled with central nervous system dysfunction.6 If HE is not promptly and effectively managed, it can rapidly progress to HS. Under heat stress, the body may undergo significant metabolic dysregulation, leading to severe cytotoxic effects. These effects can trigger excessive inflammation and coagulation, ultimately resulting in tissue damage, coagulopathy, and organ failure.4,7 Currently, HS diagnosis relies primarily on the patient's medical history and clinical presentation. In some cases, early hyperthermia may be subtle or masked by underlying comorbidities, complicating timely diagnosis by clinicians and delaying optimal treatment. This delay can lead to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) or even death.4,8 Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the molecular changes in HS patients to facilitate early diagnosis and the initiation of cooling therapies.

Proteomic analysis reveals the expression patterns of specific proteins under defined conditions, facilitating a comprehensive assessment of the proteome's dynamic and heterogeneous landscape. This methodology significantly enhances our understanding of disease pathogenesis.9 Li et al.10 analyzed the proteomic characteristics of extracellular vesicles derived from liver cells in HS, and found that extracellular vesicles can serve as a medium for communication between liver cells to induce liver damage. Fang et al.11 employed multi-omics analysis to demonstrate that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key mechanism underlying HS-induced cerebral cortical neuronal injury, and further highlighted the potential role of Tomm40 as a molecular target in this process. Wen et al.12 employed label-free liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry to analyze protein expression in renal tissues of rats with exertional HS, and identified Ahsg, Bpgm, Litaf, Acsm2, Hadha, Keg1, Sh3glb1, Eif3d, Ambp, and Ddah2 as potential biomarkers for acute kidney injury induced by exertional HS. However, these studies have predominantly focused on molecular alterations at the level of specific organs or tissues and have been conducted exclusively in animal models, lacking validation in clinical cohorts. Their clinical translational value remains to be further evaluated. In our study, we employed 4D-data-independent acquisition (4D-DIA) proteomics, which incorporates ion mobility as a 4th dimension to enhance scanning speed and detection sensitivity, thereby providing a high-throughput, high-resolution, highly reproducible, and quantitatively precise analytical platform.13

In our study, we have pioneered the application of 4D-DIA proteomics to assess plasma protein profiles in subjects afflicted with HE and HS. This study aimed to identify potential biomarkers for early HS diagnosis, which may substantially augment diagnostic specificity.

Methods

Study design and participants

The diagnostic criteria for HS are as follows: (1) medical historical information: exposure to high temperature and high humidity environments; engagement in high-intensity exercise; (2) clinical manifestations: evidence of central nervous system dysfunction; core temperature exceeding 40°C; functional impairment of multiple organs (≥2); severe coagulopathy or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). A diagnosis of HS was confirmed when at least 1 criterion from the medical history information was met alongside at least 1 clinical manifestation, with alternative explanations for the symptoms being excluded.14 HE is characterized by an elevated core temperature between 37°C and 40°C, accompanied by symptoms such as intense thirst, weakness, and anxiety.15,16

The exclusion criteria included: (1) chronic hepatic or renal insufficiency; (2) more than 3 days between symptom onset and hospitalization; (3) younger than 16 years old; (4) underlying hematological disorders, including but not limited to leukemia and hemophilia; and (5) a history of oral medication use prior to onset.

Ultimately, a total of 18 patients with HS and 23 patients with HE, admitted to the No. 908th Hospital of Joint Logistic Support Force between October 2022 and February 2024, were enrolled as a test set for proteomics analysis. Additionally, 21 patients with HS and 19 patients with HE from the Hainan Hospital of the Chinese PLA General Hospital during the same period were included for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) validation of protein biomarkers. Clinical data and sample collection

We systematically gathered comprehensive clinical data from all enrolled patients. Furthermore, within 2 h of hospital admission, a 2-mL venous blood sample was collected from each patient. This sample underwent centrifugation at 4244g for 10 min at 4°C to separate the plasma. The isolated plasma was then stored at -80°C for proteomic analysis and ELISA.

4D-DIA proteomics analysis

Protein quantification was performed using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, USA). Twenty μg of protein from each sample was mixed with 5× loading buffer, boiled at 95°C for 5 min, and resolved by SDS-PAGE (4% − 20% precast gradient gel, constant voltage 180 V, 45 min). A "Pool" sample was generated by combining equal protein amounts from all individual samples for spectral library construction.

All samples, including the pool sample, were subjected to in-solution digestion. The desalted peptides were lyophilized and then reconstituted in 40 μL of 0.1% formic acid. An appropriate amount of indexed retention time (iRT) standard peptides was added to both the pool sample and the digested peptides of each sample prior to data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry analyses. Detailed steps can be found in Supplementary Methods 4D-DIA.

For both DDA and DIA analyses, chromatographic separation was performed using the Evosep One system liquid chromatography (Evosep, Denmark), followed by mass spectrometry analysis using the timsTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany).

For DDA library data, the FASTA sequence database was searched with SpectronautTM 14.4.200727.47784 (Biognosys) software. The database was downloaded at website: http://www.uniprot.org. The iRT peptides sequence was added (Biognosys iRT Kit). DIA data was analyzed with SpectronautTM searching the above constructed spectral library.

Subsequently, bioinformatics analysis was performed. DEPs were defined based on the criteria of a fold change > 1.5 (upregulation) or < 0.67 (downregulation) with p value < 0.05. Using Fisher's exact test, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis.

Hub protein screening

Protein-protein interactions were analyzed based on the STRING 12.0 version database (https://cn.string-db.org/). Identifying hub proteins using the “CytoHubba” plugin in Cytoscape (version 3.7.1) is recommended. To further screen the hub proteins, we evaluated the diagnostic power of each hub protein using receiver operating characteristic curves. In parallel, feature selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, implemented in R software (version 4.5.0) using the glmnet package (version 4.1-9).

Parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) analysis

The protein extraction method is the same as 4D-DIA. The peptides obtained through enzymatic hydrolysis were desalted and lyophilized, then reconstituted with 0.1% formic acid, and the peptide concentration was determined by measuring OD280.

Import peptide information suitable for PRM analysis into Xcalibur 4.1 software to set up PRM methods. Take approximately 1 μg of peptides from each sample and spike in 20 fmol of standard peptide (PRTC: ELGQSGVDTYLQTK) for detection. The sample undergoes gradient separation on the chromatographic analysis column at a selected flow rate.

After high-performance liquid chromatography separation, samples are analyzed by PRM mass spectrometry using Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Finally, the Skyline3.5.0 software (Agilent Technologies, USA) is used for data analysis of PRM raw files.

ELISA

Apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4) concentrations in 19 HE patients and 21 HS patients were measured using the commercial ELISA kit (Catalog No. JM-7130H1, Jiangsu Jingmei Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). A microplate pre-coated with APOA4 antibodies was sequentially supplemented with samples, standard solutions, and horseradish peroxidase-labeled detection antibodies. Plasma samples were diluted 1:5. Following incubation, washing, and substrate addition, the optical density at 450 nm was measured using an MB-530 plate reader (Huisong, China). Plot a linear regression curve using the standard product concentration as the X-axis and the corresponding optical density values as the Y-axis. Calculate the concentration values of each sample according to the equation of the curve.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, United States) and GraphPad Prism version 8.01 (GraphPad Software, United States). Data with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, and intergroup differences were evaluated using Student's t-test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (Q1, Q3), and intergroup differences were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages), with intergroup differences analyzed using the chi-squared (χ2) test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To gain a deeper understanding of the gene functions and biological pathways associated with the DEPs, a GO pathway enrichment analysis was performed. For the GO biological process, these proteins were primarily involved in the response to hypoxia and cellular response to stress. For the GO molecular function, the proteins mainly participated in L-lactate dehydrogenase activity and cell adhesion molecule binding. For the GO cellular component, significant enrichment was identified in Weibel-Palade bodies (WPBs) and clathrin-coated vesicles (Fig. 2D−F).

Moreover, a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis was separately conducted on the upregulated and downregulated DEPs, revealing major involvement in critical biological processes such as inflammation and immune response, cell signaling, and metabolic regulation. Notably, the pathways identified were associated with fat digestion and absorption, tumor necrosis factor signaling pathway, and leukocyte transendothelial migration (Fig. 2G).

Hub protein identification

By the 4 algorithms (Maximal Clique Centrality, Edge Percolated Component, EcCentricity, and Betweenness), the following 12 proteins: von Willebrand factor (vWF), l-lactate dehydrogenase A chain, actin-related protein 2, intercellular adhesion molecule 1, glycoprotein Ib platelet subunit alpha, lymphocyte cytosolic protein 1, matrix metalloproteinase-9, heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 8, fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A, cystatin C, serpin family A member 5, and APOA4, were consistently obtained as hub proteins by each algorithm (Fig. 3A−E). The area under the curve (AUC) values of each hub protein were used to evaluate their sensitivity and specificity for HS diagnosis (Fig. 3F). Only APOA4 achieved an AUC greater than or equal to 0.8, indicating high diagnostic value for HS. In parallel, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression refined the 12 overlapping proteins, with the optimal penalty parameter (lambda) determined by 10-fold cross-validation. According to lambda.1se, 0.1858826 was determined as an appropriate λ value. Confirmation of key proteins by PRM

Based on the results of protein qualification and enrichment analysis, 18 DEPs were selected for targeted validation via PRM, including B4E0X1, A0A5C2G922, P04275, A0A5C2GKA1, P11142, A0A5C2GT88, A0A5C2GDX5, A0A5C2GTH7, A0A5C2GTS4, A0A5C2GRS7, A0A5C2FZT4, A0A5C2GGX3, A0A5C2GH60, A0A5C2G2X3, A0A5C2GIQ8, A0A5C2GF46, Q6MZU6, and P06727 (Table 2). Discussion

This study marks the pioneering exploration of HS-related biomarkers via proteomic analysis, revealing 176 DEPs in HS patients, predominantly involved in molecular pathways associated with inflammation and immune responses, cellular signal transduction, and metabolic regulation. Notably, the study corroborates the downregulation of plasma APOA4 as a robust diagnostic biomarker for HS, as validated by PRM and ELISA.

APOA4, a key apolipoprotein synthesized primarily in the small intestine, coalesces with triglycerides, cholesterol esters, and phospholipids to form chylomicrons.17, 18, 19 This protein is pivotal in lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and the inhibition of gluconeogenesis.20, 21, 22 APOA4 enhances glycemic control by facilitating glucose uptake, stimulating insulin release, and improving insulin sensitivity. Additionally, APOA4 has been shown to interact with NR1D1, leading to a reduction in gluconeogenesis and contributing to the maintenance of glucose homeostasis.22,23 Previous research has suggested the potential of APOA4 as a biomarker for prediabetes and alcoholic hepatitis.24,25

In alcoholic hepatitis, APOA4 inactivated the reactive oxygen species activity induced by alcohol.25 Moreover, the concentration of APOA4 increases in association with declining renal function, contributing to reverse cholesterol transport to alleviate cholesterol overload in mesangial cells. The elevation of APOA4 serves as an early biomarker of renal impairment.26 These findings elucidate the intricate relationship among APOA4, metabolic dysregulation, and hepatorenal injury, suggesting a potential link to the pathological processes associated with HS.

APOA4 also exerts anti-inflammatory effects27 and inhibits platelet aggregation and thrombosis.28 The adhesion mediated by P-selectin facilitates the release of neutrophil extracellular traps.29 By modulating the expression or functionality of P-selectin, APOA4 diminishes leukocyte-platelet adhesion, thereby playing a pivotal role in mitigating inflammatory responses.30,31 Furthermore, APOA4 significantly inhibits lipopolysaccharide-induced monocyte activation and pro-inflammatory cytokines release.27 Begue et al.32 quantified the plasma APOA4 in COVID-19 patients and sex-matched controls using mass spectrometry. They found that the plasma concentration of APOA4 was significantly reduced in COVID-19 patients. In addition, study by Charlotte Birner has indicated that patients with sepsis exhibit reduced levels of APOA4, with low concentrations serving as an early indicator of infection.33 Notably, HS induces a systemic inflammatory response akin to septic shock, leading us to postulate that the exacerbated inflammatory response observed in HS may be associated with decreased APOA4 levels.34

Extensive research has demonstrated that cellular injury, end-organ dysfunction, and mortality associated with HS are predominantly driven by the hyperactivation of the coagulation cascade and the pronounced features of DIC.4 In our investigation, we noted a marked increase in D-dimer levels and a concurrent reduction in platelet counts among individuals afflicted with HS. GO cellular component enrichment analysis revealed that the DEPs were predominantly enriched in WPBs.

The inflammatory and coagulation responses elicited by HS can inflict damage upon endothelial cells, consequently compromising the integrity and function of WPBs. This impairment results in the release of key constituents, such as vWF and P-selectin, into the circulatory system, which are integral to hemostasis and inflammatory processes.35,36

vWF plays a crucial role in facilitating platelet adhesion to the vascular endothelium, platelet aggregation, and thrombosis.37 The integrin αIIbβ3, which is the most abundant integrin in platelets, is vital for platelet aggregation.38 APOA4 has been demonstrated, both in vitro and in vivo, to inhibit thrombosis.39 APOA4 competitively inhibits the fibrinogen-αIIbβ3 interaction, which diminishes platelet granule release and ultimately leads to the negative regulation of αIIbβ3-mediated platelet aggregation and thrombosis, along with the indirect suppression of P-selectin expression.28

Our study identifies a significant downregulation of APOA4 in HS, suggesting a potential association between coagulation disorders in HS and reduced APOA4 levels. The decrease in APOA4 diminishes its antithrombotic effects, consequently increasing the risk of platelet aggregation and thrombosis, which further exacerbates the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying HS. This observation aligns with the clinical manifestations of thrombocytopenia and heightened thrombosis observed in HS patients.

In inflammatory bowel disease, APOA4 expression is markedly reduced, with pro-inflammatory cytokines likely contributing to the decreased production of APOA4.40,41 Among HS patients, plasma APOA4 levels are significantly depressed, potentially correlating with compromised intestinal barrier function and heightened inflammation. Heat stress can precipitate intestinal injury, which may not only impede APOA4 synthesis but also augment intestinal permeability, thereby facilitating the translocation of noxious substances, such as endotoxins, into the bloodstream and triggering a systemic inflammatory response syndrome.34,42 During this process, inflammatory factors such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interleukin-1β are significantly elevated,42 leading to the activation of neutrophils and promoting their release of neutrophil extracellular traps. This phenomenon not only amplifies the inflammatory response but also activates the coagulation cascade, resulting in the formation of numerous microthrombi. The excessive clotting consumes coagulation factors and platelets, thereby contributing to the occurrence and progression of DIC.43, 44, 45.

This study, while providing valuable insights, is not without its limitations. The sample size of both the discovery and validation cohorts is modest, and the validation was confined to a single-center dataset. And there may be an optimism bias in the diagnostic efficacy. Therefore, further validation using a large-scale multicenter prospective cohort study is essential to enhance the generalizability of our findings. Although we propose that the decrease in APOA4 levels during HS may be linked to heightened inflammatory responses and coagulation dysregulation, empirical evidence to substantiate this hypothesis is currently lacking. Future studies should address this gap through experimental validation and aim to advance beyond biomarker discovery toward mechanistic validation and, ultimately, therapeutic target development.

In conclusion, our comprehensive 4D-DIA proteomics analysis of plasma samples from patients with HE and HS identified 176 DEPs, which predominantly implicated excessive inflammatory responses and metabolic dysregulation in HS. Notably, downregulation of plasma APOA4 emerged as a promising diagnostic biomarker for HS. Although our results are promising, large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to confirm the clinical utility of APOA4.
