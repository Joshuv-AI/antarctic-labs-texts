P-wave characteristics in Bayés syndrome predict feasibility of anterior mitral line ablation: The Left Anterior Bayés-Derived Mitral Linear Ablation Outcome and Conduction score
Authors: Suguru Nishiuchi, Hirohiko Kohjitani, Kohki Nakamura, Kojiro Hattori, Akiko Kodama, Taiki Masuyama, Shingo Yoshimura, Takehito Sasaki, Kenichi Kaseno, Shigeto Naito, Ren Kawaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBayés syndrome is characterized by a P-wave showing interatrial block (IAB) from impaired Bachmann bundle conduction. In patients without overt IAB, persistent epicardial conduction via the Bachmann bundle can hinder a successful completion of an anterior mitral line (AML) ablation.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine whether preprocedural P-wave duration and inferior-lead P-wave morphology predict the feasibility of AML and to derive a preprocedural score.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed data of 69 consecutive patients who underwent AML radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation or tachycardia (May 2022–February 2026), of whom 57 met inclusion criteria. Preprocedural electrocardiograms in sinus rhythm were assessed for P wave. AML feasibility was classified as feasible or difficult. Firth penalized logistic regression and a random forest classifier identified predictors. The Left Anterior Bayés-Derived Mitral Linear ablation Outcome and Conduction (LA-BLOC) score was derived in the discovery cohort (n = 35) and evaluated in the temporal validation cohort (n = 22).ResultsAML block was feasible in 24 patients and difficult in 11 of the discovery cohort. The feasible AML had longer P-wave duration (169 ± 28 ms vs 139 ± 31 ms; P = .012) and more frequent biphasic P waves in inferior leads. P-wave duration (continuous; area under the curve, 0.80), with a 130 ms receiver operating characteristic-derived cutoff, and P-wave morphology in II (odds ratio, 8.49; P = .008) were key predictors. The LA-BLOC score (P-wave duration, ≥130 ms = 1; biphasic P in II = 2 and in III = 1) with a threshold of ≥3 predicted AML feasibility (area under the curve, 0.94; sensitivity, 0.88; specificity, 1.00).ConclusionPreprocedural Bayés syndrome P-wave characteristics are associated with successful AML completion. The LA-BLOC score may facilitate preprocedural planning.

Key Findings ▪In patients undergoing radiofrequency anterior mitral line (AML) ablation, biphasic P-wave morphology in lead II is the sole independently significant predictor of AML feasibility using Firth penalized logistic regression, consistent with impaired Bachmann bundle conduction as the mechanistic substrate enabling endocardial transmural block.▪The LA-BLOC score derived from the discovery cohort—a simple integer score assigning 2 points for biphasic P-wave in lead II, 1 point for P-wave duration of ≥130 ms, and 1 point for biphasic P-wave in lead III—predicted AML feasibility with a score threshold of ≥3 points, achieving accurate prediction in the temporal validation cohort.▪The LA-BLOC score provides a practical, noninvasive preprocedural tool derived from a routine 12-lead electrocardiogram to guide patient selection and procedural planning for AML ablation, and prospective multicenter validation is required before clinical adoption.

Introduction

Bayés syndrome,1, 2, 3 characterized by interatrial block (IAB), is significantly associated with increased risks of atrial fibrillation (AF) and stroke.4, 5, 6 IAB, defined by a prolonged P-wave duration lasting ≥120 ms, particularly advanced IAB (aIAB) with a biphasic P-wave in inferior leads, predicts both new-onset and recurrent AF after catheter ablation. Furthermore, IAB is independently associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke, even in patients without prior AF.7,8 Therefore, electrocardiogram (ECG) assessment of P-wave characteristics, especially IAB, has important value for risk stratification. Although multiple interatrial conduction pathways exist, the Bachmann bundle is thought to play the dominant role in producing the biphasic P-wave morphology characteristic of Bayés syndrome.7

In AF ablation, the anterior mitral line (AML) is a linear lesion that connects the mitral annulus to the pulmonary vein region or roofline, aiming to modify the atrial substrate and target macro-reentrant tachycardias.9,10 Anatomically, the anterior wall of the left atrium is prone to generating low-voltage zones (LVZ) related to arrhythmogenic substrates.11 However, the long-term efficacy of AML for arrhythmia suppression is often limited by technical challenges in achieving complete and durable transmural lesions.12 These challenges stem from variations in atrial anterior wall thickness and the epicardial course of the Bachmann bundle, which can permit bypass conduction around endocardial lesions. The Bachmann bundle, as the principal interatrial tract traversing the anterior atrial wall,13 may not be fully ablated from the endocardial side alone.9,12 Acute AML success rate varies with patient characteristics,14 and this variability partially reflects the pre-existing conduction impairment through the Bachmann bundle.

Therefore, mitral ablation strategies must account for these underlying conduction factors when selecting the optimal and feasible lesion set. With the advent of pulsed field ablation, methods for achieving durable mitral block lines have been re-examined.15, 16, 17, 18 Mitral block lines remain an important therapeutic option, and debates continue regarding their efficacy and optimal strategy.

In this study, we evaluated whether preprocedural surface-ECG P-wave characteristics could predict the feasibility of AML ablation and help screen patients most likely to be suitable candidates.

Methods

Patient population

Between May 2022 and Feb 2026, we retrospectively enrolled all consecutive patients at Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center who underwent AML radiofrequency ablation for symptomatic AF or atrial tachycardia (AT), including macro-reentrant AT involving the mitral annulus or the anterior left atrial wall. During this period, a total of 3595 patients underwent AF or AT ablation at our center, of whom 69 underwent AML ablation and were screened for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they had not undergone an ECG in sinus rhythm within 6 months before the procedure, if 3-dimensional electroanatomic mapping data were inadequate, or if they had a prior valvular intervention. The cohort was split chronologically by procedure date—earlier cases comprised the discovery cohort used to evaluate the relationship between ECG findings and AML feasibility, and later cases comprised the temporal validation cohort of the AML feasibility prediction. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Gunma Prefectural Cardiovascular Center (Approval Nos. 2024004 and 2024026), and all participants provided written informed consent. The research reported in this study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

ECG criteria

For each patient, the last 12-lead ECG recorded in sinus rhythm prior to ablation was reviewed. P-wave duration and morphology were analyzed using the following settings: drift filter (high-pass), ≥0.5 Hz; muscle filter (low-pass) turned off or set to ≤50–100 Hz; sweep speed, 25 mm/second; and the amplitude scale 1 mV/cm with ×2 magnification. P waves in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF) were assessed, and morphology was stepwise categorized as single-peaked, double-peaked, or biphasic (Figure 1). Two independent electrophysiologists, blinded to procedural outcomes, performed all measurements. Ablation procedure

All procedures were performed under deep sedation maintained with intravenous dexmedetomidine, propofol, and pentazocine. Electroanatomic mapping and ablation were carried out using 1 of 3 systems: EnSite™ X or Precision™ EP system (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) with Advisor™ HD Grid and either TactiFlex™ or TactiCath™ ablation catheter; Rhythmia HDx™ (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) with IntellaMap™ Orion and IntellaNav™ StablePoint ablation catheter; or CARTO® 3 (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) with PentaRay™ or OctaRay™ and Thermocool SmartTouch™ SF ablation catheter.

Prior to ablation, voltage mapping was performed during sinus rhythm or AT to delineate LVZ (bipolar voltages below 0.5 mV), and the total area of LVZ was measured within the anterior (segment #4) and anteroseptal (segment #8) regions of the left atrium.19

Following pulmonary vein isolation or box isolation, AML ablation was performed. In a subset of patients in whom pre-ablation activation mapping was available, baseline conduction status across the AML was assessed before ablation. AML was initiated at the superior edge of the mitral annulus and extended toward the right superior pulmonary vein; when LVZ extended more laterally, the line was designed toward the roofline. Point-by-point radiofrequency energy (30–40 W) was delivered at each site for 30–40 seconds at the operator’s discretion. If first-pass conduction block across AML was not achieved, gaps adjacent to the planned line were identified, and additional applications were delivered along the initial line. Patients in whom AML block was completed at this stage were classified as the feasible AML group. If the line remained incomplete, remapping was performed, and additional applications outside the initial line were delivered. These patients comprised the difficult AML group, which included both those who achieved block after off-line applications and those in whom AML block could not be completed.

To confirm bidirectional conduction block along AML after ablation, we used 2 complementary methods. First, bidirectional activation maps were generated on the mapping system to verify the absence of conduction across the ablation line. Second, a differential pacing maneuver was performed by pacing on either side of the line and demonstrating local conduction block. In addition, to exclude residual epicardial conduction via the Bachmann bundle, block across the Bachmann bundle was confirmed by pacing from the right atrium or by evaluating the loss of Bachmann bundle conduction during sinus rhythm, verifying the absence of biatrial conduction via the epicardial pathway. AML was considered complete when mapping and pacing criteria for bidirectional block were met.

Statistical and machine learning methods

Analyses were performed in Python (version 3.13.2). Data handling used pandas (version 2.2.3) and numpy (version 2.1.3). Figures were produced with matplotlib (version 3.10.1) and seaborn (version 0.13.2). Machine-learning and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) procedures used scikit-learn (version 1.6.1), and multicollinearity diagnostics used statsmodels (version 0.14.4). Continuous variables were compared with Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test according to distribution; categorical variables were compared with χ2 tests. A 2-sided P-value of <.05 was considered significant. ROC analysis with the Youden index determined optimal cutoff values. Inter- and intra-observer agreement were quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous variables and Cohen’s κ for categorical variables.

Multicollinearity was assessed using Spearman rank-order correlations and variance inflation factors (VIFs). Variables with pairwise correlation rho of >0.90 or VIF of ≥5.0 were excluded from multivariable models. Independent predictors were identified using a from-scratch implementation of Firth penalized logistic regression (penalized likelihood with Jeffreys prior, BFGS optimization), given near-complete separation of the primary predictor, and are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with profile-likelihood 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and likelihood-ratio-test P-values, as appropriate under separation.

A random forest classifier (n_estimators = 100, max_depth = None, random_state = 42) was trained on the selected predictors to explore nonlinear effects and interactions. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and F1 score, with leave-one-out cross-validation for internal validation. Feature importance was quantified by mean decrease in impurity.

The Left Anterior Bayés-Derived Mitral Linear ablation Outcome and Conduction (LA-BLOC) score was derived by integrating logistic regression and random forest results. Discovery-cohort performance was assessed as an in-sample estimate, and the score was applied without modification to the temporal validation cohort as the primary performance estimate.

Results

The cohort was split chronologically by procedure date: earlier cases comprised the discovery cohort (N = 35) and later cases comprised the temporal validation cohort (N = 22).

Of the 11 difficult cases, 7 ultimately achieved block after additional off-line applications (difficult-achieved) and 4 did not achieve complete block (difficult-incomplete). Mapping systems used were CARTO (feasible: 12, difficult: 6), EnSite (feasible: 5, difficult: 2), and Rhythmia (feasible: 7, difficult: 3), distributed similarly between groups. ECG findings

Mean P-wave duration was 169 ± 28 ms in the feasible AML group vs 139 ± 31 ms in the difficult AML group (P = .012, Figure 2). To explore nonlinear relationships and interactions, a random forest classifier was trained using 3 predictors (P-wave duration and P-wave morphology in II and in III). The leave-one-out cross-validation model performance was an accuracy of 0.886, sensitivity of 0.917, specificity of 0.818, AUC of 0.932, and F1 score of 0.917 (confusion matrix: TP = 22, TN = 9, FP = 2, FN = 2). Feature importance values were P-wave morphology in II of 0.442, P-wave duration of 0.310, and P-wave morphology in III of 0.248, indicating a prominent role for P-wave morphology in II in the model.

Score-based prediction

The LA-BLOC score was derived from the discovery cohort. Biphasic P-wave morphology in lead II was the sole independently significant predictor in the multivariable model (OR, 8.49; P = .008) and carried the highest random forest feature importance, and was therefore assigned 2 points. P-wave duration of ≥130 ms received 1 point, based on its significance in univariable analysis and second-ranked random forest importance. The 130 ms threshold was ROC-derived and achieved a sensitivity of 1.00, with no feasible cases observed below this value. We acknowledge that P-wave duration may contribute redundantly to the score from a purely statistical standpoint, given its collinearity with lead II morphology (Spearman’s ρ = 0.60) and its non-significance in the multivariable model. However, from an electrophysiological perspective, P-wave duration and inferior lead morphology reflect partially distinct aspects of Bachmann bundle impairment: prolonged duration reflects slowed interatrial conduction velocity, whereas biphasic morphology reflects a more advanced directional shift in terminal atrial activation. Therefore, these 2 parameters capture complementary pathophysiological substrates beyond their statistical correlation, supporting the retention of P-wave duration as an independent component of the LA-BLOC score. P-wave morphology in lead III received 1 point based on its electrophysiological significance as a directional marker of Bachmann bundle impairment: biphasic morphology in lead III reflects a vectorial shift in terminal atrial activation consistent with partial Bachmann bundle dysfunction, representing a less advanced but mechanistically related stage of the interatrial conduction continuum. This weight was assigned conservatively, reflecting the limited case-level evidence and the non-dominant contribution of lead III morphology relative to lead II in both logistic and random forest analyses. The Youden index selected a threshold of ≥3 points.

In the discovery cohort, the LA-BLOC score demonstrated in-sample performance of accuracy of 0.94, sensitivity of 0.92, specificity of 1.00, AUC of 0.96, and F1 score of 0.96. As the score weights and threshold were both selected within the discovery cohort, this in-sample estimate is optimistically biased. The temporal validation cohort result, reported below, represents the sole unbiased performance estimate.

The score was then applied without modification to the temporal validation cohort (n = 22) to obtain a less biased performance estimate. Baseline characteristics and comorbidities related to AML feasibility did not significantly differ between groups (Table 3), and AML was feasible in 16 cases. The discovery metrics should be interpreted as optimistic in-sample estimates. In the temporal validation cohort, these figures represent the primary performance estimate of accuracy of 0.91, sensitivity of 0.88, specificity of 1.00, AUC of 0.94, and F1 score of 0.93. Discussion

P-wave duration

P-wave prolongation is a well-recognized marker of delayed interatrial conduction. Although a P-wave duration ≥120 ms is commonly used to define IAB,7,8 prolonged duration does not always reflect left atrial enlargement1 and can instead indicate conduction disturbance, including isolated Bachmann bundle dysfunction.3,20

In the context of AML ablation, prolonged P-wave duration may therefore reflect impaired Bachmann bundle conduction between the right and left atria and help predict AML feasibility. In our cohort, the optimal cutoff was 130 ms, which was associated with feasible AML completion. However, P-wave duration can be influenced by atrial fibrosis and structural remodeling,4 limiting its specificity (sensitivity, 1.00; specificity, 0.64).

Because biphasic P-wave morphology in the inferior leads is more mechanistically specific for Bachmann bundle impairment, combining duration with morphology improves discrimination for Bachmann bundle dysfunction and enhances prediction of AML ablation feasibility.

P-wave morphology in inferior leads

Biphasic P-wave morphology in inferior leads is a key diagnostic ECG marker of aIAB.3,7,8 The criteria for aIAB typically require 2 components: (1) P-wave duration of ≥120 ms and (2) biphasic P-wave morphology in the inferior leads.1,2,4

In our analysis, biphasic P-wave morphology in II showed the greatest discriminatory power for AML feasibility in multivariable logistic regression. Variation in morphology in II is also an important indicator for the classification of IAB.21 From a vectorial perspective (Figure 6), a biphasic P-wave in II likely reflects the most pronounced shift in terminal atrial activation and therefore serves as a specific, noninvasive surrogate for severe Bachmann bundle impairment. The prevalence of IAB patterns involving lead II in our cohort (63% based on a biphasic P-wave in lead II alone) is substantially higher than the approximately 0.7% reported for lead II-containing IAB patterns in general primary care populations aged 50–90 years.22 This likely reflects the enriched atrial substrate of our study population, which consisted exclusively of patients selected for AML ablation—a subset with more advanced structural and electrical atrial remodeling compared with unselected populations. This enrichment should be considered when generalizing the LA-BLOC score to broader AF ablation populations. The LA-BLOC score predicting AML feasibility

As illustrated in Figure 3, advanced IAB (biphasic P-wave in inferior leads) was consistently associated with AML feasibility. In contrast, other P-wave morphologies—reflecting partial interatrial conduction delay or normal conduction—were predominant in the difficult AML group. This observation supports the concept that the degree of Bachmann bundle impairment exists on a continuum, and that only patients with advanced, complete dysfunction are likely to achieve endocardial AML block without extensive additional lesions. In this study, the LA-BLOC score demonstrated promising discriminative performance in a small temporal validation cohort (accuracy, 0.91; AUC, 0.94; n = 22 with 6 difficult cases), indicating potential utility for preprocedural planning. The score, which integrates both linear and nonlinear contributions, showed consistent performance across cohorts. From a clinical and electrophysiological perspective, P-wave duration and morphology are plausible parameters of the progression of IAB. Overall, the LA-BLOC score, designed to predict AML feasibility in the context of aIAB, represents a promising hypothesis-generating finding from a small single-center cohort that warrants prospective multicenter validation as a tool.

Clinical perspective

Preprocedural assessment of simple surface-ECG features, the LA-BLOC score with prolonged P-wave duration and biphasic P-wave morphology in inferior leads, may help identify patients in whom AML ablation is more likely to be completed with standard endocardial lesion sets. Incorporating these ECG markers into preprocedural planning could inform lesion strategy, operator preparedness, and resource allocation, and may improve procedural success and durability by selecting the most appropriate mitral blockline location or by prompting consideration of alternative approaches when ECG features predict difficulty.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a retrospective, single-center study with a modest sample size. The temporal validation cohort represents a chronological split of the same series rather than true external validation, and findings should be considered hypothesis-generating pending prospective multicenter confirmation. Direct mechanistic validation was limited, as systematic assessment of Bachmann bundle conduction was not performed in all cases. In a subset of 4 patients in whom pre-ablation left atrial activation mapping under right atrial pacing was available, Bachmann bundle conduction was evaluable in 3. The remaining patient had extensive low-voltage area in the anterior left atrial wall precluding reliable assessment and was therefore classified as unevaluable rather than discordant. In all 3 evaluable patients, ECG findings and Bachmann bundle conduction status were concordant with the proposed mechanism (concordance 3/3 evaluable), although the limited number of evaluable cases precludes definitive conclusions. Furthermore, the classification of AML feasibility as feasible or difficult is inherently dependent on operator judgment regarding when to extend the ablation lesion set beyond the initial line. This subjectivity introduces potential inconsistency in outcome classification that cannot be fully controlled in a retrospective single-center study. All ablation was performed with radiofrequency energy, and generalizability to pulsed field ablation-based mitral line strategies has not been established. Variability in mapping systems and operator technique may have influenced outcomes. In cases with prolonged P-wave duration and short PR interval, surface ECG may underestimate true P-wave terminal negativity, potentially affecting IAB assessment. Finally, rhythm surveillance was performed at clinician discretion, and atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence may be underestimated.

Conclusion

P-wave characteristics in Bayés syndrome, prolonged P-wave duration, and biphasic morphology in inferior leads were associated with feasible completion of AML ablation. The LA-BLOC score derived from this hypothesis-generating single-center study demonstrated promising discriminative performance and may assist preprocedural planning pending prospective multicenter validation.
