Immunomodulatory copper-based polyphenol nanozyme for diabetic infectious wound healing via NIR amplified cuproptosis bacteriostat in synergy with ferroptosis inhibition anti-inflammation
Authors: Huyang Gao, Juan Luo, Xixi Chen, Yaohui Huang, Mengxue Mo, Qianyu Luo, Yan Liu, Binbin Zou, Yanjun Lei, Zhengzhao Li, Linhai Huang, Xiaolin Huang, Peng Liang, Meihua Lin, Jingju Huang, Hongsheng Lu, Hualin Huang, Huile Gao, Jianfeng Zhang, Junyu Lu, Ming Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic infectious wound is usually in the environment of high glucose levels and immune disorder. It always results in high reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, serious immune dysfunction, and extreme susceptibility to infection, delaying wound healing. Herein, we prepared an immunomodulatory polyphenol metal organic framework nanozyme (CE) combined with near infrared (NIR) irradiation for diabetic infectious wound healing. This nanozyme was formed by the self-assembly of Cu ions and ellagic acid. In vitro experiments confirmed that CE + NIR could efficiently lower the inflammatory factors (IL-6 (50.72 %), IL-1β (61.44 %) and iNOS (57.33 %)) expression, and upregulate anti-inflammatory factor (IL-10 (111.44 %)) expression of lipopolysaccharide induced RAW264.7, decreasing the ROS levels, promoting cellular migration (527.91 %), and accelerating angiogenesis (91.81 %) of high glucose treated L929, together with inhibiting the bacterial growth of E. coli (92.21 %) and MRSA (95.89 %). Transcriptome sequencing results and their related validation experiments demonstrated that CE + NIR achieved antioxidant and anti-inflammation through ferroptosis inhibition. And transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis proved the efficient antibacterial activity of CE + NIR through NIR amplified cuproptosis. Markedly, it also confirmed that the strategy of CE + NIR was helpful to immunoregulation activation via inducing macrophage M2 directional polarization, and immune activating T cells number, thereby restoring immune homeostasis, and accelerating tissue repair. It might offer an efficient strategy of other infectious diseases’ immunotherapy with high efficacy and biosafety.

Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of diabetes is increasing year by year. Among them, delayed wound healing is one kind of the common complications in diabetes, which can seriously affect people's normal life [1]. The main reasons for the delayed diabetic wound healing are peripheral nerve injury, blood circulation disorder, and decreased immune function caused by hyperglycemia [2]. In the meantime, these types of wounds are prone to infection, thereby inhibiting healing [3].

When the skin is damaged due to abrasions, traffic accidents, or other reasons, it is easy to cause wound infection [4]. The elevated blood glucose levels will weaken the antibacterial effect, and greatly increase the risk of diabetic wound infection [5]. At the same time, the wound is contaminated with bacteria, which multiply at the wound site. And the immune system initiates an inflammatory response to combat infection [6]. During the inflammatory process, immune cells such as neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes are activated, releasing various inflammatory mediators such as cytokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and chemokines [7]. Excessive ROS and persistent inflammation may lead to cell death and tissue damage, delaying healing. However, slow and even worsening wound healing can lead to repeated infections and persistent inflammation, thus forming a vicious cycle [8]. In view of the unique diabetic wounds microenvironment, scavenging ROS levels, and inhibiting bacterial growth are the key strategy for diabetic infectious wound healing [9].

As an essential element, copper (Cu) participates in various biochemical reactions in the body, maintains normal growth and development, promotes iron absorption and utilization, participates in hematopoietic processes, and maintains skin health [10]. Specifically, Cu based nanomaterials like Cu oxides [11], Cu based metal organic framework (MOF) [12], and ultrasmall Cu loaded nanomaterials [13], etc., with the conversion between Cu+ and Cu2+, have unique redox properties, which further protect cells from the damage of oxidative stress, and reduce the risk of inflammation related diseases [14]. In addition, Cu based nanomaterials are also involved in regulating cell growth and function, and excessive Cu ions can induce the mitochondrial pathway of cuproptosis, applied for killing bacteria [15,16] and tumors [17]. The above functions could be enhanced by external stimuli like near infrared (NIR) irradiation [18,19], ultraviolet (UV) radiation [20], and ultrasonic (US) driven [21], etc. Zhou, etc. integrated nitric oxide (NO)-prodrug (BNN6) into Cu2+-tannic acid (TA) to form a multifunctional nanozyme for accelerating the infectious skin wounds healing. It effectively eliminated MRSA through the synergistic effect of NIR triggered NO release and NIR induced local hyperthermia. In addition, it also had strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and angiogenic properties through activating Nrf-2/HO-1 pathway, and inhibiting NF-κB pathway to reprogram the microenvironment of infected wounds.

On the other hand, patients with diabetes show hyperglycemia and systemic immune disorders. The elevated blood glucose concentration can easily cause bacterial infections in wounds, leading to the formation of biofilms that prevent antibiotic penetration, and intensify infection [22]. The formation of biofilms impairs the recognition and clearance of the immune system, leading to the delayed wound healing [23]. In addition, immune dysfunction can lead to excessive activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages (M1 type), inhibit anti-inflammatory macrophages (M2 type) polarization, prolong the inflammatory phase of wounds, and inhibit wounds healing [24]. Prominently, the balance between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells will be disrupted. CD4+ T cells mainly coordinate immune responses by recognizing antigens, activating other immune cells, and secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines like arginase-1 (Arg-1) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), playing the central role in anti-infection, maintaining immune balance, and forming immune memory [25]. The lack of CD4+ T cells will lead to an increased risk of systemic immune deficiency and infectious diseases [26]. In addition, immunoactivation of CD4+ T cells can regulate macrophage polarization [27]. Therefore, regulating immune homeostasis by macrophage M2 directional polarization, and T cells immune activation is expected to be a new strategy for treating diabetic infectious wound [28].

For example, natural polyphenols include epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) [29], TA [30], ellagic acid (EA) [31], etc., which are powerful antioxidants that can eliminate free radicals in the body, reduce the impact of oxidative stress on cells, and maintain cellular redox homeostasis. In addition, natural polyphenols also enhance the activity of immune cells, helping to improve the body's ability to fight various diseases [32]. Through nanosized self-assembly, it not only improves stability, and enhances blood circulation in the body, but also fully utilizes their inherent functions under control [33]. Li et al. successfully prepared ultrasmall nanoenzymes by non-covalent assembly of natural polyphenols, iron ions and polyvinylpyrrolidone, which efficiently scavenged intracellular ROS, reduced intracellular oxidative stress levels, and alleviated oxidative damage. In addition, in an inflammatory environment, the nanozymes could effectively regulate the function of immune cells, especially by inhibiting the M1 polarization of macrophages, and promoting macrophages M2 polarization, thereby achieving bidirectional regulation of inflammatory response [34]. In addition, Shi et al. constructed a Fe-TA polyphenol network coated with fibronectin, which could significantly release Fe ions under weakly acidic conditions (pH = 5.5), to enhance ferroptosis, upregulate CD4+ and CD8+ T cell expression, downregulate regulatory T cells, and upregulate NK cell expression, demonstrating the chemical/chemical kinetics/immune combination therapy for tumors [35].

Herein, we proposed a novel therapeutic strategy based on an immunomodulatory polyphenol nanozyme (CE) through lowering ROS levels, inhibiting bacterial growth, accelerating tissue repair, and restoring immune regulation to promote diabetic infectious wound healing. CE was constructed by the self-assembly of Cu2+ and EA under aqueous condition. The coordination structure, enzymatic activity, controllable Cu ions release, in vitro and in vivo phototermal effect, as well as the therapeutic effects and related therapeutic mechanisms of CE + NIR in cellular models, bacterial levels and rat models were systematically investigated, evaluated and explored. This study had proved that the assembled CE combined with NIR irradiation could synergistically achieve effective diabetic infectious wound healing, with ROS scavenging, ferroptosis inhibition mediated anti-inflammation, NIR amplified cuproptosis antimicrobia, phototheraml induced angiogenic and immunomodulatory properties (Fig. 1). While few studies of polyphenol based nanozymes were reported to accomplish such multifunctional features, the strategy of CE + NIR endowed with good biocompatibility and effective infectious wound healing properties, provided a promising approach to develop clinically infected diabetic skin wound drugs, and could be further applied for the prevention and therapy of other infectious diseases.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the preparation of CE, and the corresponding in vivo therapy of diabetic infectious wound via NIR enhanced ROS scavenging, ferroptosis inhibition anti-inflammation, cuproptosis bacteriostat, macrophage M2 polarization, and T cells immunoactivation to reprogram redox homeostasis, restore immune regulation and promote tissue repair.Fig. 1

Experimental sections

Materials and chemicals

EA (C14H6O8, ≥96 %, Mw = 302.19), copric chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O, ≥99.99 %, Mw = 170.48), D-glucose (≥99.5 %, Mw = 180.16), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30 % w/w), pentanediol (C5H12O2, ≥ 98 %, Mw = 76.09), methanol (CH3OH, ≥99.9 %, Mw = 32.04) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98 %, Mw = 40.00) were commercially obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Anhydrous ethanol (C2H6O, 99.5 %, Mw = 46.07) was commercially obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, ≥97 %) was commercially supplied from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, US). Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Solarbio (Shanghai, China). All chemicals were in analytic grades, and directly used as received.

Preparation and physicochemical characterization of CE

CE was fabricated by the traditional aqueous reaction as previously reported [36]. In brief, 5 g CuCl2 and 0.25 g EA were dissolved in a flat-bottomed flask with 100 mL deionized (DI) water. After mixing, the pH of mixture was adjusted to 8.5 by NaOH, and the mixture reacted at 40 °C overnight with magnetic stirring (500 rpm/min). After reaction, the product (CE) was centrifuged, and dispersed in ethanol for 3 times, and isolated and obtained after vacuum drying.

To study the chemical structure, molecular structure, and crystallization of CE, ultraviolet–visible spectrum (UV–vis, Shimadzu, Japan), Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Shimadzu, Japan), and X-ray diffraction (XRD, MiniFlex 600, Japan) were utilized. The morphology and the corresponding elemental distribution of CE were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Japan). To identify the elemental composition and Cu element contents of CE, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250XI+, USA) and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Varian, USA) were applied. Meanwhile, the zeta potential of CE was tested by zeta sizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK). Besides, the thermal stability of CE was investigated by thermal gravimetric analyzer (TGA, STD650, TA, USA). And the pore volume and size, and specific surface area of CE were analyzed by Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET, Micromeritics ASAP, USA).

In addition, the photothermal effects were investigated. EA or CE was prepared in various concentrations (0, 50, 100 or 200 μg/mL), and irradiated at different irradiation intensity (1, 2 or 3 W/cm2) of NIR light (15 min, 808 nm). Specifically, for photothermal stability investigation, 100 μg/mL CE was implemented for 3 “on” and “off” cycles of NIR irradiation (2 W/cm2). The photothermal imaging camera (FLIR, USA) was used to monitor the temperature changes, and the images and corresponding temperatures were recorded every minute. The extinction coefficient was tested by UV–vis spectroscopy, and calculated by Beer-Lambert Law (Equ. 1) [37]. And the photothermal conversion efficiency was also calculated based on the formula (Equ. 2) [38]. Furthermore, the Cu ions release was investigated by immersing CE in different conditions (PBS and pH = 5.0). And the corresponding solution was collected at predetermined time points, and its Cu ions concentration was tested by Cu ions testing kit (Solarbio, China) by following the protocols. For pH = 5.0+NIR, NIR irradiation was implemented for 10 min per time, 3 times every 2 h.

Biological functions investigation in cellular levels

Mouse mononuclear macrophages (RAW264.7), mouse fibroblasts (L929) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were commercially obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher, USA) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Procell, China). When reaching the confluence of 85 %, cells were passaged, and utilized for further cellular experiments. And the cell viability was initially evaluated by using cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Biosharp, China) by following the protocols. Briefly, cells including RAW264.7, L929 and HUVECs were cultured in 96 well plate with the density of 8000 cells per well. Next, the cultured medium was replaced with 100 μL mixtures containing CuCl2, EA or CE with different concentrations (2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200 or 500 μg/mL), and incubated for another 24 h. The treated cells were then washed against with PBS for 3 times before adding 100 μL 10 % CCK-8, and incubated for 2 h. The final supernatant was observed at 450 nm by the microplate reader (Thermal Fisher, USA). To confirm the feasibility of photothermal therapy (PTT) in cellular levels, NIR irradiation was implemented on the cultured cells with or without CE (100 μg/mL) incubation. The corresponding images and temperatures were photographed and collected.

The cells models were established by LPS induced RAW264.7 to establish inflammatory cells models, and high glucose induced L929 and HUVECs to build high glucose cells models [40,41]. In details, cells were treated with 1 μg/mL LPS or 25 mM D-glucose for 24 h, and incubated with 100 μg/mL CE for another 24 h. In specific, NIR irradiation was implemented for 3 times (10 min once, 2 W/cm2) at 0, 1 and 2 h after mixing with CE for CE + NIR. The corresponding groups were divided as: cells without treatment (normal group), and LPS or D-glucose induced cells followed by PBS (control group), 100 μg/mL CE (CE group), NIR irradiation (NIR group), and 100 μg/mL CE combined with NIR irradiation (CE + NIR group) treatments.

The live/dead staining was implemented to evaluate the protection ability of treated cells away from oxidative stress. After treatments, the cells were incubated with calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) (Beyotime, China) after PBS washing for 3 times, and observed by fluorescent microscopy (Olympus BX53F, Japan). And the intracellular ROS levels of D-glucose induced L929 were investigated by ROS testing probes. Briefly, the cultured cells were replaced with fresh medium containing 2′, 7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF), hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF), or dihydroethidium (DHE, maokangbio, China) for testing intracellular ROS levels respectively. After incubation for 30 min, the treated cells were rinsed with PBS for 3 times before the fixation of 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosharp, China), and then observed by fluorescent microscopy.

The anti-inflammation capacities were initially evaluated by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). After collecting the supernatant of treated RAW264.7, their interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) expression levels were tested by ELISA kits (Meimian, China) following the instruction of manufacture. In addition, reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was applied to investigate the relative genes expression levels of treated cells including inflammation related genes (IL-6, IL-1β and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)), anti-inflammatory gene (IL-10), M1 type gene (cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86)), and M2 type gene (cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206)) of treated RAW264.7, and tissue repair genes (heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)) of treated L929. Simply, the total RNA of treated cells was extracted by RNA extraction kit (Magen, China), and RT-qPCR was implemented by using Applied Biosystems™ 7500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). And the relative genes expression levels were analyzed by 2−ΔΔCt method, and compared with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). The corresponding primer sequences were illustrated in Table S1.

Furthermore, the inflammation related proteins expression levels of treated cells were investigated by immunofluorescent staining. In brief, the treated cells were handled with 4 % PFA fixation before goat serum (Beyotime, China) blocking. After washing with PBS for 3 times, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies: anti-IL-1β, anti-TNF-α, anti-CD86, anti-CD206, anti-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and anti-nuclear factor-erythroid 2 related factor 2 (Nrf-2) for treated RAW264.7, and HSP70 and CD31 for treated L929 (Proteintech, China) at 4 °C. Later, the cells were incubated with the secondary antibody (fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) conjugated AffiniPure goat Anti-rabbit IgG, Boster, China) for another 2 h before rinsing with PBS. After 4, 6-diamidino-2-phenyindole dilactate (DAPI, Biosharp, China) staining, the treated cells were imaged by fluorescent microscope. And the CD86 and CD206 expression levels of treated cells were also analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman, USA).

Specially, the cellular migration ability was evaluated by cell scratch testing. In details, L929 was cultured in 6 well microplate (3 × 105 cells per well) with cultured medium, and a straight line was drawn on the monolayer of cells by a pipette tip. And then the treated cells were incubated with DMEM without FBS (control group), 100 μg/mL CE (CE) or 100 μg/mL CE + NIR (CE + NIR) for 24 h. The images at predetermined time points were observed by optical microscopy. And the migration ratio was calculated as (A0-A1)/A0, where A0 and A1 represented the scratch area at 0 and 24 h respectively. Similarly, the angiogenesis experiment was also implemented. In brief, 24 well microplates were coated with 200 μL of Matrigel (BD, Biosciences) per well. After gelation, HUVECs with the density of 1.0 × 105 per well were plated on the matrigel. After 12 and 24 h’ culture in the incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, the morphology of HUVECs was observed by optical microscope. And the number of nodes were quantified using Image J.

Finally, transcriptome sequencing was applied to study the collection of all transcripts. LPS induced RAW264.7 were cultured, and treated with PBS (control group) or 100 μg/mL CE + NIR (CE + NIR) respectively. The total RNA was extracted by RNA extraction kit, and the genomic DNA was digested by DNase I (Takara, Japan) before implementing high throughput RNA sequencing. The gene expression levels between two groups were compared, and the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained by conducting differential gene analysis by using R software (version 4.5.1), loading the relevant R packages ((“dplyr”)(“ggplot2”) (“ggrepel”) and setting logFC < −0.05 and adjPVal < −0.05. To understand the potential biological functions of cells after treatments, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were applied. To verify the anti-inflammation mechanism, western blotting (WB) was applied to investigate the pathway related proteins expression levels of treated cells. In details, the treated cells were washed against with the cold PBS before treating with lysis buffer containing the protease and phosphatase inhibitors. And the supernatant was collected, and the corresponding protein concentration was adjusted by BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China) before being separated by protein gel electrophoresis. Later, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, IPVH00010, Millipore, USA) membranes. After blocking with blocking buffer (MEIMIAN, China) for 2 h, and rinsing with tris buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST, Servicebio, China) for 3 times, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody (anti-GPX4, anti-solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) or anti-actin, Proteintech, China) at 4 °C respectively. After washing with TBST for 3 times, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit, Sangon, China) for another 1 h. Finally, the membranes were immersed with UltraSignal ECL WB detection reagent (Beyotime, China), and scanned by the automatic chemiluminescence image analysis system (e-BLOT, China).

In vitro antibacterial capacities studies

To investigate the antibacterial ability, Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) were selected as the research target. The bacteria were cultured into LB medium (Biosharp, China), and incubated with CE, NIR or CE + NIR (100 μg/mL) overnight. Specifically, NIR irradiation was implemented with the irradiation intensity of 2 W/cm2 for 10 min after mixing. Later, 100 μL mixture was dispersed into the agar plate, and incubated at 37 °C for another 24 h. And the number of bacteria was calculated to quantify the bacterial viability. Besides, the treated bacteria were fixed with 2.5 % pentanediol after PBS washing, and observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-T300, Japan) after gold coating. In the meantime, the treated bacteria were washed against with PBS for 3 times, and treated with SYTO-9 and PI staining for observation by confocal scanning microscope (ZEISS, Germany). Furthermore, 200 μL of bacterial suspension was inoculated onto a 96 well plate to form a biofilm, and treated with CE, NIR or CE + NIR. NIR irradiation was implemented for 10 min with the irradiation intensity of 2 W/cm2. After treatments, the medium was removed, and the biofilm was fixed with anhydrous ethanol, and stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, China) for 20 min. After washing against with PBS, the biofilm was photographed by the camera. Subsequently, the biofilm was dissolved in 30 % glacial acetic acid (Macklin, China), and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured by microplate reader. In specific, to investigate the phagocytotic ability of bacteria, RAW264.7 were cultured in the confocal dish, and incubated with FITC labeled bacteria at 37 °C for 2 h. After treatments, the cells were washed with PBS for 3 times, fixed by PFA, and stained with 1, 1-octyl-3, 3, 3′, 3′-tetramethylindole dicyanate (Dil, Beyotime, China) and DAPI. Afterwards, the cells were observed by confocal scanning microscopy.

Finally, to explore the antibacterial mechanism, the total RNA of treated bacteria (control group and CE + NIR) was extracted by TRIzol kits (Beyotime, China) for transcriptome sequencing and metabolome analysis. Similarly, the total RNA from treated bacteria was subjected to metabolomics sequencing using a liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). And the DEGs between control group and CE + NIR were analysized by using R software (version 4.5.1). KEGG enrichment was applied to enrich the expression levels of DEGs, and GO functional analysis was considered to study the biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) of treated bacteria. To facilitate the observation of the changes in metabolite contents, the main differential metabolites were identified and treated by unit variance scaling. And the heatmap was drawn by using R software (version 4.5.1). KEGG enrichment analysis was implemented by loading R packages ((“clusterProfiler”) (“org. Hs. eg. Db”) (“enrichplot”) (“ggplot2”) (“pathview”) (“extrafont”)), and setting logFC < −0.05 to reveal the related pathways and regulatory mechanisms in bacterial metabolism. Significantly, to confirm the cuproptosis mechanism, the ferredoxin-1 (FDX1) and lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS) genes expression levels in treated bacteria were analyzed by RT-qPCR. Similarly, the FDX1 expression levels of treated bacteria were also investigated by WB following the above procedures. Besides, the activities of respiratory chain complex I (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) dehydrogenase) and complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) were evaluated by using the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). And the intra-bacterial lipid peroxidation was tested by lipid peroxidation malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) by following the protocol.

In vitro therapy evaluation

All in vivo experiments were approved by the ethics committee of animal experiments of Guangxi Medical University (No. 202310028). SD rats (200–220 g) were operated by following the local guide for the care and use of laboratory animals of Guangxi Medical University. All animal experiments were conducted in a specific pathogen free (SPF) grade cleanroom, and operated under sterile conditions to avoid the effects from external environment. After treatments, all animals were euthanized through intravenous injection of excessive pentobarbital sodium (Protein Biotechnology, China). To establish type I diabetic rats models, the male SD rats were treated by tail vein injection of 1 % streptozotocin (STZ, Yeasen Biotechnology, China) for once. And their blood glucose levels were measured every day. When the blood glucose levels were above 16.7 mmol/L, the diabetic models were established for further experiments [42].

To assess the blood compatibility of CE, the hemolysis testing was applied. The fresh blood was saved from rats, and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min to obtain the red blood cells pellet. And 500 μL red blood cells suspension was mixed with 500 μL PBS containing CE within the concentration range from 0, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200–500 μg/mL, where DI water was applied as the positive control. After 1 h’ incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and observed at 540 nm by microplate reader. The hemolysis ratio (HR) was finally calculated as: HR = [(ODs-ODn)/(ODp-ODn)] × 100 %, where ODn, ODp and ODs were the corresponding optical density (OD) of negative control, positive control and samples. Besides, the hemostatic ability was evaluated with tail cutting, liver cutting, and liver dissecting followed by CE treatment. The bleeding process was recorded by images, and the filter papers were weighed to calculate the amount of blood loss. Furthermore, the feasibility of in vivo PTT was implemented as following. In brief, 0.5 mL (100 μg/mL) CE was smeared on the wound area of rats, and irradiated by NIR light (2 W/cm2) for 15 min. The images and temperatures were saved and recorded by thermal camera.

The infectious wound was created with a diameter of 2 cm on the back of diabetic rats after anesthesia to build diabetic wound models [43]. In order to simulate the infectious state of diabetes wounds, 100 μL mixture of E. coli (1 × 108 CFU/mL) and MRSA (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was added to the wound site. And the treated rats were randomly separated into 3 groups (12 rats per group): control group, 100 μg/mL CE, and 100 μg/mL CE + NIR. For CE + NIR, NIR irradiation (2 W/cm2) was implemented once per day within 7 days. At day 0, 3, 7 and 14, the wound was imaged, and analyzed by Image J. The wound closure ratio was calculated as: (A0-At)/A0 × 100 %, where A0 and At represented the initial wound area, and the wound area on a specified day respectively. In addition, in vivo antibacterial ability was investigated by collecting the wound homogenate for bacterial spreading testing.

And the wound tissue was also fixed by 4 % PFA for 24 h, and treated with H&E and Masson (Solarbio, China) staining for observation. Besides, the ROS levels of skin wound were also tested by ROS testing probe (DCF) following the protocols before observation. Meanwhile, the slides were initially incubated with primary antibody (rabbit polyclonal anti-TNF-α, anti-IL-10, anti-SLC7A11, anti-GPX4, anti-CD86 and anti-CD206 for immunohistochemical staining, and anti-VEGF, anti-HSP70, anti-CD4+ and anti-CD8+ for immunofluorescent staining, Zenbio, China) at 4 °C overnight before washing with PBS for 3 times. And the slides were treated with biotinylated secondary antibody or FITC labeled secondary antibody (Servicebio, China). After sealing, the slides were observed by microscope, and the corresponding average optical density (AOD) or mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was statistically analyzed by Image J. Furthermore, the skin wound, fresh blood and spleen of treated rats were homogenized with PBS, and washed with red blood cell lysis buffer (BioLegend, USA). After FITC labeled anti-CD4+, phycoerythrin (PE) labeled anti-CD8+, and allophycocyanin (APC) labeled anti-CD3+ (BioLegend, USA) staining respectively, the cell suspension was quantified analyzed by flow cytometry. In particular, after isolating the spleen cells, the BFA/Monensin and PMA/Ionomycin mixture (Liankebio, China) was applied to induce the spleen cells for 4 h before staining with FITC or Cy5.5 labeled anti-CD4+ and PE labeled anti-CD25+. Afterwards, the spleen cells were treated with intracellular fixation/permeabilization buffer kit (Elabscience, China), and stained with PE labeled anti-IL-17A+ (Liankebio, China) or APC labeled anti-Foxp3+ (Elabscience, China) before being analyzed by flow cytometry.

Finally, the in vivo therapeutic biosafety was also evaluated. After 14 days, the treated rats were sacrificed, and their blood was collected for blood routine (blood analyzer, BC-6800Plus, Mindray, China), biochemistry (biochemical analyzer, 7600, HITACHI, China), and coagulation function (coagulation detection analyzer, ACL TOP750, Werfen, China) testing. And the corresponding heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney at day 14, and skin tissue around the wound at day 1, 3, 7 and 14 of treated rats were collected, and treated with H&E staining before observation. Meanwhile, the Cu ions contents in the major organs and blood of rats at day 3, 7 and 14 were also tested by Cu ions testing kit.

Statistical analysis

The values reported were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the significance was defined as ∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗: p < 0.01, ∗∗∗: p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗: p < 0.0001, where ∗ indicated the comparison with normal group in cellular levels or control group in bacterial and animal levels.

Results and discussion

Preparation and physicochemical characterization

CE was fabricated by the traditional aqueous solution reaction of CuCl2 and EA with the molar ratio of 45: 1. In brief, CuCl2 and EA were dissolved in DI water, and reacted for 24 h after adjusting the pH by NaOH to weak alkaline (pH = 8.5) (Fig. 2A). After centrifuge and re-dispersing in ethanol for 3 times, CE was finally isolated by vacuum drying. The assembled CE was formed due to the physical interaction of phenolic hydroxyl group and Cu2+. As displayed in Fig. S1, the color of CuCl2 and EA was blue and khaki. And it became blackish green for CE. From UV–vis spectrum results, for CuCl2 with no curves appeared, obvious curves were observed at 260 nm and 400 nm in EA, corresponding to the double bond of ester group and benzyl group respectively. After forming CE, the corresponding curves disappeared (Fig. S2), indicating no free EA existed in CE. Similarly, from the results of FTIR, compared to CuCl2, apparent curves at 3700∼3200, 1700∼1630 and 1270∼1010 cm−1 happened to EA, corresponding to OH, C=O, and C-O-C groups in EA respectively. However, the above curves still saved in CE (Fig. 2B), indicating that EA still maintained its unique molecular structure after forming CE. And the crystallization of CE was also investigated by XRD. Compared with CuCl2 and EA with a series of irregular peaks, the obvious peaks at 8.2, 26.9 and 28.1° happened to CE (Fig. S3), indicating the formation of stable MOF structure. Next, the zeta potential was 2.95 ± 1.00 and −31.97 ± 0.79 mV in CuCl2 and EA, while it became −18.77 ± 0.49 mV in CE (Fig. 2C and Table S2). Significantly, the morphology of CE was separately characterized by transmission electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive spectroscopy (TEM-mapping). In addition, from XPS results, obvious Cu, C and O elements were displayed in CE (Fig. 2F), maintaining the consistent with those of TEM-mapping. For detailed C1s spectra, three types of C including C=O, C-O and C-C existed in CE. And there were two types of O observed in O1s detailed spectra. After peak fitting, the Cu+/Cu2+ ratio was 21.74 %. The existence of multivalent states ensured CE with multiple enzymatic activities [44]. Cu+ is oxidized to form Cu2+, which is then reduced to obtain Cu+, further leading to redox homeostasis [45]. Furthermore, from the TGA results, the weight remaining ratio was 0.37 %, 25.14 % and 46.80 % in CuCl2, EA and CE respectively (Fig. 2G and Table S2). The decreased weight loss of CE was attributed to the existence of EA. By the analysis of ICP-MS, the content of Cu element was 16.35 ± 0.15 % in CE (Table S2). Meanwhile, the specific surface area of CE was analyzed by BET method. As shown in Fig. 2H, the obvious adsorption and desorption curves were closed in CE, displaying its obvious MOF structure. And the pore size and volume, and surface area was 13.51 nm and 0.03 cm3/g, and 16.98 m2/g in CE (Table S3), indicating the formation of polyphenol MOF with obvious mesoporous structure [46].

The photothermal effects of CE were also investigated. For PBS and EA, almost no temperatures changed during NIR irradiation for 15 min. However, for CE, the temperatures were significantly ascended versus irradiation time (Fig. 2I). Thus, the temperatures increased to 50.4 °C in 100 μg/mL CE after NIR irradiation (2 W/cm2), while it became 27.3 and 33.1 °C in PBS and EA (i of Fig. 2J). And if increasing the concentration of CE from 0, 50, 100–200 μg/mL, the temperatures were also ascended from 28.5, 41.4, 50.9–55.8 °C with the same irradiation intensity of NIR for 15 min (Fig. S4 and ii of Fig. 2J). For 100 μg/mL CE, if increasing the NIR irradiation intensity from 1, 2–3 W/cm2, the corresponding temperatures changed from 38.1, 50.2–57.3 °C after irradiation for 15 min (Fig. S5 and iii of Fig. 2J). Prominently, the photothermal stability was also shown in iv of Fig. 2J. After 3 cycles of “on” and “off” irradiation, the temperatures still maintained the similar changes. After calculation, the extinction coefficient was 1.17398 mL/(μg·cm), and photothernal conversion efficiency was 64.82 %. To confirm the controllable Cu ions release, the Cu ions release rate under different conditions was investigated. As shown in Fig. S6, for CE in pH = 7.4 and pH = 7.4+NIR, the relative slow release of Cu ions happened, indicating that CE maintained the stable MOF structure under these conditions. Under pH = 7.4, NIR irradiation had almost no effects on the release of Cu ions. However, the obvious Cu ions release happend to CE in pH = 5.0, induced by the destablization of hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interaction in CE under acidic condition. Prominently, the enhanced Cu ions release happened to CE in pH = 5.0+NIR. With NIR irradiation, it could speed up the destablization of physical interaction in CE [47].

Finally, the ROS scavenging capacities were initially evaluated by ESR. As illustrated in Fig. 2K, the intensity of ·OH was relatively high in control group, significantly declined in CE and CE + NIR. It was the similar tendency for ·O2− and 1O2 scavenging. By ROS testing kits, the H2O2 scavenging ratio was 42.46 ± 1.28 % in 100 μg/mL CE, increased to 50.50 ± 1.00 % in CE + NIR with the same concentration. If increasing the concentration of CE + NIR from 50 to 200 μg/mL, the H2O2 scavenging ratio was ascended from 31.02 ± 0.72 % to 59.78 ± 0.23 %. Similarly, the ·OH and ·O2− scavenging ratios were 46.16 ± 0.92 % and 25.94 ± 2.03 % in CE, both enhanced in 100 μg/mL CE + NIR (58.31 ± 0.36 % and 34.77 ± 0.60 %). Increasing the concentration of CE + NIR from 50 to 200 μg/mL, the ·OH and ·O2− scavenging ratio also changed from 32.67 ± 0.28 % and 20.50 ± 2.51 % to 66.64 ± 0.56 % and 55.91 ± 2.33 % respectively (Fig. 2L and Tables S4 and S5). Particularly, to investigate the effect of NIR irradiation, the ROS scavenging ability of NIR, CE and CE + NIR were compared. As illustrated in Fig. S7, H2O2 and ·O2− scavenging ratio was 1.46 ± 0.29 % and 2.84 ± 0.92 % in NIR alone, with almost no effects. Compared to CE + NIR, it became 50.59 ± 0.24 % and 33.15 ± 0.52 % in CE + NIR. Thus, for NIR irradiation alone, it could not affect the ROS scavenging ratio. Only when photothermal effects happened, ROS scavenging ratio could be enhanced in CE + NIR. With the stable irradiation intensity, the dynamic equation of concentration depended ROS scavenging rate was tested and calculated. As shown in Fig. S8, both ROS scavenging rates were initially enhanced with the increase in the concentration of CE and CE + NIR, and gradually tended to reach the equallirium. On the basis of these, the dynamic equation of ROS scavenging rate was built, with Vmax = 31.69 μM/min and Kcat = 61.76 μg/mL in CE, and Vmax = 37.85 μM/min and Kcat = 68.72 μg/mL in CE + NIR for H2O2 scavenging rate. And for ·OH scavenging rate, the Vmax and Kcat was 6921 μM/min and 103.4 μg/mL in CE, and the Vmax and Kcat was 8061 μM/min and 80.18 μg/mL in CE + NIR. It confirmed that CE + NIR had obvious higher ROS scavenging rate that of CE. NIR irradiation speed up the ROS scavenging ratio.

From the above, it confirmed the successful fabrication of CE. After forming CE, the molecular structure, crystallization and zeta potential of CE underwent changes with some characteristics of EA. It confirmed that CE was in the rectangular structure with C, O and Cu elements observed, and possessed the characteristics of MOF. And CE possessed the excellent photothermal effects and photothermal stability. Increasing the concentration of CE and power intensity of NIR irradiation both contributed to the improved photothermal effects. Prominently, it displayed the excellent ROS scavenging capacities in CE, significantly enhanced in CE + NIR. The enhanced ROS scavenging behaviors happened to CE + NIR due to the fact that NIR irradiation could increase the local temperature of nanomaterials, accelerate the charge movement, promote valence state changes, and thus improve ROS scavenging efficiency [48]. Meanwhile, the controllable Cu ions release happened to CE, which presented the relatively slow release of Cu ions in normal condition (pH = 7.4 or NIR irradiation alone), and accelerated Cu ions release in acidic condition, especially in pH = 5.0+NIR. During the destabilization of CE, it also provided free EA, leading to the controllable antioxidant and anti-inflammation capacities in CE. All of these provided the feasibility for follow-up reparative therapy of diabetic infected wound.

They have functions such as phagocytosis, antigen presentation, participation and promotion of inflammatory responses, defense, secretion and immune regulation [49]. Thus, 100 μg/mL was considered to be applied as the working concentration of CE for further experiments. In order to explore the antibacterial mechanism, the whole transcriptome sequencing was performed on E. coli with or without CE + NIR treatment. From the volcano plot, there were 890 genes significantly upregulated, and 281 genes downregulated for E. coli after treatment (Fig. 5G). Subsequently, from KEGG enrichment analysis, there were 20 most significant pathways displayed. Compared with control group, CE + NIR significantly affected the pathways of lipoic acid metabolism, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, pyruvate metabolism, glutathione metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and peroxisomes (Fig. 5H). These highly correlated metabolic pathways indicated that Cu element had a profound impact on bacterial metabolism and physiological functions, involving multiple aspects such as energy metabolism, redox balance, metabolite synthesis and decomposition. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis was implemented to determine the importance of DEGs in BP, CC and MF. Prominently, stress response related pathways of Cu ions were particularly evident in GO enrichment analysis. Mitochondrial respiration changes, oxidative stress, and metabolic changes caused by cuproptosis were important causes of bacterial death, leading to protein misfolding and metabolic disorders (Fig. 5I) [58]. Meanwhile, the heatmap of DEGs between control group and CE + NIR was shown in Fig. 5J. And genes related to TCA cycle, such as STHA, SDHA, SDHC, FUMB, FUMC, FRDA and FRDB, were significantly downregulated after treatment, further confirming their significant impacts on the TCA cycle of bacteria. In addition, COPA, COPD, CUEO, COMR, CUSR, PHOP and RSTA genes related to Cu transport were upregulated. Among them, COPA is the main Cu efflux channel, which transports Cu from the cytoplasm to the cytoplasm, and excretes excess Cu out of the cells. Under Cu poisoning stress, bacteria strive to upregulate the expression of efflux genes to protect themselves [59]. Multiple studies had shown that bacteria underwent a process similar to cuproptosis, affecting the mitochondrial respiration and TCA cycle [60]. To confirm this process, the relative genes expression was analyzed. As displayed in Fig. 5K, the expression of FDX1 and LIAS genes was in the highest levels (1.00 ± 0.04 and 1.00 ± 0.02) in control group. Treatments could decrease their expression levels. Among them, CE + NIR played the most effective role, with the expression levels of 0.08 ± 0.01 and 0.11 ± 0.01 respectively. And compared to control group (1.09 ± 0.08 and 1.05 ± 0.09), the LIAS and FDX1 genes expression levels were almost in the similar levels in NIR (1.00 ± 0.06 and 1.01 ± 0.14) (Fig. S29). Significantly, the FDX1 expression was also analyzed by WB. As displayed in Figs. S30 and S31, compared to control group, the FDX1 expression levels sequentially decreased in NIR, CE and CE + NIR. After quantified analysis, the relative FDX1/GAPDH ratio was 1.00 ± 0.04 in control group, 0.95 ± 0.05 in NIR, 0.81 ± 0.04 in CE, and 0.73 ± 0.04 in CE + NIR. FDX1 could regulate protein acylation by directly binding to LIAS, jointly affecting the TCA cycle and metabolic of bacteria, and thus participating in various physiological and pathological processes of bacteria, such as cuproptosis [61]. Thus, compared to control group, NIR irradiation alone almost did not affect the FDX1 and LIAS expression levels, while CE slightly decreased FDX1 and LIAS expression levels. However, CE + NIR significantly downregulated their expression levels, contributing to enhanced cuproptosis.

Besides, metabolomics was applied to further analyze the effects of CE + NIR on bacteria, identifying 40 significant differences between control group and CE + NIR, including 6 upregulated metabolites and 34 downregulated metabolites (Fig. S32). The heatmap analysis of the main differential expressed metabolites showed that they mainly affected glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, and TCA cycle (Fig. S33). Among them, the increase in the accumulation of pyruvate might be due to the inhibition of the activity of certain key enzymes in TCA cycle, which hindered the pathway of pyruvate, and prevented it from entering the TCA cycle for oxidation and decomposition in a timely manner. Acetyl coenzyme A (CoA) is an important initiator of TCA cycle, and its decrease may indicate a reduction in substrates entering the TCA cycle, thereby affecting the operation of the entire TCA cycle [62]. From KEGG enrichment analysis, the synthesis of amino acids related to glycolysis and TCA cycle was affected, which could further affect the metabolic function of bacteria (Fig. S34) [63]. To confirm the effect of CE + NIR on the metabolic function of bacteria, the respiratory chain activity of treated bacteria was evaluated. As revealed in Fig. 5L, the complex I and complex II were in the high levels for control group, which were significantly declined to 49.08 ± 0.62 % and 61.53 ± 12.03 % for CE, and 22.88 ± 2.41 % and 28.01 ± 2.54 % in CE + NIR. However, compared to control group, NIR irradiation could not affect the respiratory chain activity of bacteria, with the same levels of complex I and complex II (Fig. S35). And the MDA levels of treated bacteria were also evaluated, which was 1.98 ± 0.50 nmol/mg for control group, 5.09 ± 0.88 nmol/mg for CE, and 10.80 ± 1.31 nmol/mg for CE + NIR (Fig. S36).

From the above, it confirmed the excellent antibacterial ability happened to CE + NIR. CE + NIR could efficiently disrupt the cellular membrane of bacteria, induce bacterial cuproptosis, and promote bacterial phagocytosis, thereby achieving the excellent antibacterial ability. For NIR irradiation alone, it almost had no effects on the above antibacterial activities [12]. However, it displayed that CE could disrupt the cellular membrane of bacteria, and induce bacterial cuproptosis, while it could not improve the bacterial phagocytosis. Significantly, it also confirmed that CE + NIR could efficiently affect the metabolic function of bacteria, helpful to NIR amplified bacterial cuproptosis, while no effects happened to NIR irradiation alone. Meanwhile, in the normal cellular microenvironment, CE maintained the relatively stable state, and Cu ions were slowly released. A trace amount of Cu ions helped to promote cell proliferation [64]. In the infectious microenvironment, with low pH or high ROS levels, the release rate of Cu ions was accelerated. Combined with NIR irradiation, the release of Cu ions was further accelerated in CE + NIR, thereby inducing bacterial death.

The blood biocompatibility was studied by hemolysis testing. As revealed in Fig. S37, the hemolysis ratio was below 5 % in CE within the concentration ranges from 0 to 500 μg/mL, indicating the good hemocompatibility of CE. The hemostasis experiment was implemented in 3 different conditions. Compared to control group, CE showed obvious hemostatic ability in different conditions. From the macroscopic observation, the size of the wound gradually decreased over time, where the effect of CE + NIR was the most efficient (Fig. 6D and E). After quantification, the relative wound area was 100.00 ± 0.85 % at day 0, subsequently decreased to 86.59 ± 3.17 % at day 3, 51.94 ± 2.44 % at day 7, and 10.63 ± 0.37 % at day 14 in control group, while it was 100.00 ± 3.15 % at day 0, 52.87 ± 2.84 % at day 3, 20.89 ± 0.84 % at day 7, and 1.46 ± 0.24 % at day 14 in CE + NIR (Fig. 6F). As display in Fig. 6G, compared to control group with a lot of bacteria, treatments obviously decreased the number of bacteria, especially in CE + NIR. After quantification, the relative colony count was 100.00 ± 15.08 % in control group, declined to 16.28 ± 1.48 % in CE, and 1.64 ± 0.33 % in CE + NIR respectively (Fig. 6H).

Meanwhile, the pathological characteristics in tissue sections were also assessed. As revealed in Fig. 6I, compared to control group, obvious tissue repair happened to CE and CE + NIR. After analysis, the wound length was 2.03 ± 0.08 and 1.44 ± .0.09 mm in control group and CE, and it was totally healed in CE + NIR (Fig. 6J). And from Masson staining images, the collagen deposition was in relatively low levels in control group, with low blue intensity existed. However, CE and CE + NIR significantly increased the blue intensity (Fig. 6K). On one hand, the inflammation storm induced by bacterial biological activities contributed to the high ROS levels in skin wound. And CE + NIR generated ROS to kill bacteria, also contributing to the enhanced ROS levels. At day 7, the ROS levels decreased in all groups, especially in CE + NIR. It indicated that the tissue had started to recover in low ROS levels. The lower ROS levels were euqal to the improved tissue repair. During the process of infectious wound therapy, the ROS levels of skin tissue initially increased in reponse to infectious microenvionment, and gradually decreased for tissue healing, similar to the results as previously reported [66].

Conclusions

In summary, a polyphenol MOF nanozyme (CE) was developed to act as a catalytic nanomedicine for diabetic infectious wound healing via NIR amplified cuproptosis bacteriostat, ferroptosis inhibition anti-inflammation together with immunoregulation activation. It confirmed that CE + NIR presented the excellent ROS scavenging capacities and good biocompatibility. Combining with NIR irradiation, CE + NIR could effectively scavenge intracellular ROS levels, lower inflammation related factors' expression levels, increase anti-inflammatory factors' expression levels, promote cellular migration ability, and accelerate angiogenesis through ferroptosis inhibition. In addition, it also possessed the outstanding antibacterial activity through NIR amplified cuproptosis. Significantly, the strategy of CE + NIR had confirmed its function of inducing macrophage M2 polarization, and activating T cells number to restore immune homeostasis and accelerate tissue repair. This work finally provided a therapeutic strategy of CE + NIR applied for diabetic infectious wound healing, which could extend its application for other infectious diseases’ immunotherapy.
