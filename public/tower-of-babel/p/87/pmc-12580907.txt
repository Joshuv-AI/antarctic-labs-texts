Cerebellar White Matter Microstructure Is Associated With Age, Cerebrospinal Fluid Amyloid Beta Levels, and Cognition in Cognitively Unimpaired Older Adults
Authors: Elizabeth R. Paitel, Corinne Pettigrew, Daniel D. Callow, Abhay Moghekar, Michael I. Miller, Andreia V. Faria, Kenichi Oishi, Marilyn Albert, Anja Soldan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStructural changes in the cerebellum contribute to cognitive decline due to aging and Alzheimer's disease (AD). However, it is unclear whether age and AD pathology are associated with structural alterations in the cerebellum among cognitively unimpaired individuals and how these alterations relate to cognition. This study examined the association of age and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) AD biomarkers (amyloid beta [Aβ42/Aβ40], phosphorylated tau [p‐tau181]) with cerebellar gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) volumes and cerebellar WM microstructure, measured via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) among 176 cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged and older adults (mean age = 66.70, range = 34–89). Cognition was measured with executive function and visuospatial composite scores. Older age was associated with lower cerebellar GM and WM volumes (ps < 0.01) and greater mean diffusivity (MD) in the cerebellar peduncles (p < 0.01). In contrast, more abnormal Aβ levels were associated with lower MD in three regions of interest, including the middle cerebellar peduncle (MCP, p < 0.01), a composite of superior, middle, and inferior peduncles (p < 0.05), and within‐cerebellar WM (p < 0.05). Patterns were similar when comparing biomarker positive versus negative groups, particularly for the MCP. Further, lower MD in the peduncles and cerebellar WM was associated with better executive function and visuospatial composite scores (ps < 0.05), whereas cerebellar volumetric measures were not related to cognition. Results suggest that older age is associated with microstructural and volumetric cerebellar GM and WM alterations. In contrast, Aβ levels are associated with WM microstructural properties in cognitively unimpaired individuals. These findings highlight the importance of cerebellar WM microstructure to cognition and are consistent with, and expand on, previous reports that have linked more abnormal amyloid levels to WM microstructure in cerebral tracts. They also suggest that cerebellar WM alterations may be markers of preclinical AD.

Introduction

It is now recognized that the cerebellum is involved in cognitive processing (Buckner 2013; Schmahmann 2019; Schmahmann et al. 2019; Stoodley 2012), and cerebellar changes, including increased gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) atrophy, are associated with age‐ and Alzheimer's disease (AD)‐related cognitive decline (e.g., Arleo et al. 2024; Bernard 2022; Gellersen et al. 2021, 2017; Hoxha et al. 2018; Jacobs et al. 2018; Schmahmann 2016; Thomann et al. 2008). Poorer performance on cognitive tasks has been associated with lower cerebellar GM volumes (Lin et al. 2020; Thomann et al. 2008; Toniolo et al. 2018) and altered cerebro‐cerebellar functional connectivity (Gonzales et al. 2024; Olivito et al. 2020; Tang et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2017) during the symptomatic stages of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia. Yet, AD pathology, including amyloid beta plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles, begins accumulating during the preclinical phase of AD, decades prior to the onset of cognitive impairment (Sperling et al. 2011; Villemagne et al. 2013). It is unclear whether structural AD‐related cerebellar changes are evident prior to the onset of clinical symptoms. Further, relatively little is known about how structural alterations in the cerebellum relate to AD biomarker levels in cognitively unimpaired participants, and how such cerebellar alterations relate to cognitive function.

The cerebellum has comprehensive connections with the rest of the brain via three WM tracts: the inferior, middle, and superior cerebellar peduncles. The inferior cerebellar peduncle primarily routes information between the cerebellum and the spinal cord. The middle and superior peduncles are the primary afferent and efferent pathways, respectively, relaying information throughout the cerebral cortex (Ashida et al. 2018; Lara‐Aparicio et al. 2022). Within the cerebellum, WM branches off from the peduncles, forming internal cerebellar circuits (Haines and Dietrichs 2012; Houck and Person 2014). The remainder of the cerebellum, including the outer cerebellar cortex and deeper cerebellar nuclei, is comprised of GM.

Cross‐sectional studies indicate that older age is associated with smaller structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐measured cerebellar white and GM volumes among individuals without cognitive impairment (e.g., Andersen et al. 2003; Bendlin et al. 2010; Bernard and Seidler 2013, 2014; Gellersen et al. 2021; Hoogendam et al. 2012; Jernigan et al. 2001; Koppelmans et al. 2015; Stalter et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2017), and a few longitudinal studies have shown marked cerebellar atrophy with increasing age (Raz et al. 2010, 2005). It is unclear, however, whether macrostructural (i.e., volumetric) cerebellar changes associated with older age are similarly evident for the cerebellar peduncles, as prior studies have predominantly examined WM within the cerebellum without separating the peduncles.

In addition to macrostructural volumetric changes, WM tissue microstructure can also be measured using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). Generally, higher fractional anisotropy (FA) and lower mean diffusivity (MD) are considered to reflect better WM microstructure integrity (Bennett and Madden 2014; Madden et al. 2012), though the biological interpretability of FA and MD is limited (Jones et al. 2013). Previous cross‐sectional research in participants without cognitive impairment most consistently reported lower FA with older age in cerebellar WM and peduncles. In contrast, findings for MD have been less consistent; some studies reported greater MD with older age (Abe et al. 2008; Bennett et al. 2010; Cavallari et al. 2013; Coutu et al. 2014), while others reported non‐significant associations between age and MD (Bendlin et al. 2010; Burzynska et al. 2010; Hoagey et al. 2019).

Even less is known about how biomarkers of AD pathology, including amyloid and tau, relate to cerebellar volumes and microstructure among cognitively unimpaired participants. Although the cerebellum is thought to accumulate less amyloid than other brain regions and is commonly used as a reference region in amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) studies, neuropathological studies indicate that amyloid is present in the cerebellum in sporadic AD, most often in the form of diffuse plaques (e.g., Dinkel et al. 2020; Mavroudis et al. 2019, 2010; Singh‐Bains et al. 2019; Wegiel et al. 1999). It has traditionally been thought that amyloid is not present in the cerebellum until advanced stages of AD (e.g., Thal et al. 2002). However, there is recent evidence to suggest that amyloid may accumulate earlier in the course of AD, as cerebellar amyloid deposition, measured with PET imaging, has been reported in presymptomatic, cognitively unimpaired autosomal dominant AD presenilin‐1 mutation carriers up to 10 years before their estimated age of MCI onset (Ghisays et al. 2021).

Prior studies examining associations between amyloid burden and WM microstructure in the cerebrum among cognitively unimpaired participants have suggested a biphasic model in which initial amyloid accumulation, measured by PET imaging, may be associated with a transient period of greater “diffusion restriction” as indicated by higher FA and lower MD (Benitez et al. 2022; Collij et al. 2021; Dong et al. 2020; Racine et al. 2014; Wolf et al. 2015). This greater diffusion restriction in the preclinical phase of AD is hypothesized to reflect early stages of Aβ‐induced neuroinflammation, which is followed by lower diffusion restriction in later symptomatic stages, coinciding with neurodegeneration (Benitez et al. 2022; Dong et al. 2020). It is unclear whether similar associations with Aβ are evident for the cerebellum, and further, whether biomarkers of tau pathology are associated with cerebellar macro‐ or microstructure.

To address these gaps, the primary aim of the current study was to examine the relationship of AD biomarkers with cerebellar GM and WM volumes, as well as WM microstructure within the cerebellum and in the cerebellar peduncles in a sample of cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged and older adults. We measured amyloid levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using the ratio of Aβ42/Aβ40, which is thought to become abnormal earlier in the course of AD than amyloid on PET imaging (Jack Jr et al. 2013; Palmqvist et al. 2016; Salvadó et al. 2024), allowing us to examine very early AD‐related cerebellar alterations. Expanding prior work, we additionally examined whether levels of CSF phosphorylated tau (p‐tau181) relate to cerebellar micro‐ and macrostructural measures. As a second aim, we examined associations between these structural cerebellar measures and cognitive performance. We hypothesized that older age would be associated with smaller GM and WM volumes, and with lower FA and higher MD. Furthermore, based on previous research with cerebral WM tracts (e.g., Benitez et al. 2022; Racine et al. 2014), we hypothesized that lower (i.e., more abnormal) Aβ42/Aβ40 levels would be associated with higher FA and lower MD. Finally, we hypothesized that better executive function and visuospatial performance would be associated with better WM microstructure, as indexed by higher FA and lower MD.

Methods

Participants

Data for the current study were obtained from the ongoing prospective, longitudinal Biomarkers for Older Controls at Risk of Dementia (BIOCARD) study (https://www.biocard‐se.org), which began in 1995 at the National Institutes of Health (NIH; Albert et al. 2014). The study stopped in 2005 for administrative reasons and was re‐established in 2009 at the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) School of Medicine. Both at the NIH and at JHU, clinical and cognitive assessments have been completed annually. Since 2015, 3T MRI scans and CSF have been collected at JHU approximately every 2 years. The BIOCARD study was approved by the JHU Institutional Review Board and all participants provided written informed consent.

The current study used cross‐sectional data from each participant's first available 3T MRI scan, which occurred between 2015 and 2022, and included 177 cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged to older adult participants with CSF data collected within 1 month of the MRI scan. One participant was excluded from analyses due to extreme CSF p‐tau181 values (> 5 SD from the mean), resulting in a final sample of 176 participants in the current analyses.

Clinical and Cognitive Assessment

Comprehensive clinical evaluations and cognitive assessments were conducted at each annual visit, which included a battery of neuropsychological tests (see Albert et al. 2014 for details) and a semistructured interview based on the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale (Morris 1997). The JHU BIOCARD Clinical Core staff generates a consensus diagnosis annually using procedures comparable to those established by the National Institute on Aging (NIA) Alzheimer's Disease Centers program. First, a syndromic diagnosis is generated based on the following data: (1) clinical information pertaining to the medical, neurological, and psychiatric status of the individual; (2) reports of cognitive changes by the individual and collateral sources based on the CDR interview; and (3) cognitive test scores relative to age‐matched published norms and prior performance. Syndromic diagnostic categories included: (1) cognitively unimpaired, (2) MCI, (3) impaired not MCI, and (4) dementia, and were based on criteria of the NIA/Alzheimer's Association working groups for a diagnosis of MCI (Albert et al. 2013) and AD dementia (McKhann et al. 2011). An “impaired not MCI” diagnosis is given in the case of contrasting information from the CDR interview and cognitive test scores (i.e., participants or collateral sources reported concerns for cognitive changes in daily life, but the cognitive testing did not show changes, or vice versa). Because participants who are “impaired not MCI” do not meet MCI criteria, they were included with the group of cognitively unimpaired participants, consistent with prior BIOCARD study publications (see Albert et al. 2014).

Cognitive performance was assessed using two composite scores for the domains of executive functioning and visuospatial processing, given prior findings reporting associations between these domains and cerebral‐cerebellar functional connectivity (Bellebaum and Daum 2007; Molinari and Leggio 2007; Schmahmann 2019; Stoodley 2012; Stoodley and Schmahmann 2009). These composite scores were derived from a confirmatory factor analysis of 12 neuropsychological test scores (for details, see Soldan et al. 2019). The executive function composite included digit span backwards from the Wechsler Memory Scale‐Revised (WMS‐R; Wechsler 1987), Trail Making Test part B (Reitan 1958), and the Digit Symbol Test from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale‐Revised (WAIS‐R; Wechsler 1981). The visuospatial composite consisted of Rey‐Osterreith Complex Figure copy (Rey 1941), Rey Complex Figure recall, and WAIS‐R Block Design subtest.

APOE Genotype

APOE genotyping was conducted by the digestion of polymerase chain reaction‐amplified genomic DNA using restriction endonucleases (carried out by Athena Diagnostic, Worcester, MA). APOE ε4 genetic status was coded dichotomously: participants carrying at least one ε4 allele were designated as ε4 carriers; those without an ε4 allele were classified as non‐carriers. Participants with ε2/ε4 alleles were included in the APOE ε4 carrier group, given their risk for AD pathology is similar to that of ε4 carriers (ε3/ε4), rather than ε2 carriers (Goldberg et al. 2020).

MRI Acquisition

MRI scans were acquired on a 3T Phillips Achieva scanner (Eindhoven, The Netherlands). The multi‐modal protocol included a magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) scan used for anatomical reference and image registration (TR = 6.7 ms, TE = 3.1 ms, shot interval = 3000 ms, flip angle = 8°, FOV = 240 × 256 mm2, 170 slices with 1 × 1 × 1.2 mm3 voxels, and scan duration = 5 min 59 s). Diffusion‐weighted images were acquired from two spin‐echo sequences (TR = 7.5 s, TE = 75 ms, FOV = 260 × 260 mm2, slice thickness = 2.2 mm, flip angle = 90°, b = 700, number of gradients = 33, axial plane).

Image Processing

The diffusion‐weighted images were preprocessed and segmented using MRICloud (https://braingps.mricloud.org; Mori et al. 2016). The tensor reconstruction and quality control followed the pipeline of DTIStudio (www.MRIStudio.org; Jiang et al. 2006). Specifically, preprocessing included registration based on 9‐mode affine transformation, in which both 6‐mode rigid motion and 3‐mode gradient‐dependent Eddy‐current distortion were simultaneously fitted and estimated using the normalized mutual information as the cost function (Penny et al. 2011). DTI reconstruction and quality control were also performed using MRICloud, which follows the pipeline of DTIStudio (Jiang et al. 2006). MRICloud is a web‐based platform that uses a fully automated multiatlas image parcellation algorithm that combines the image transformation algorithm, Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Mapping, based on two complementary contrasts, b0 and FA (e.g., MD, RD, FA, and fiber orientation; Ceritoglu et al. 2009), and a likelihood fusion algorithm for DTI multiatlas mapping and parcellation (Tang et al. 2014). All tensor maps were visually inspected, and no participants needed to be excluded following preprocessing. This generated 168 regions of interest (ROIs) based on JHU‐MNI atlas (Oishi et al. 2009), from which DTI scalar metrics (three eigenvalues) were extracted. Parcellations for each participant were visually inspected to ensure that the automated segmentation process yielded accurate delineations of the structures of interest. Tensor metrics were further thresholded to minimize contamination by other tissues, including CSF partial volume effects (WM threshold FA > 0.25).

The microstructural analyses focused on FA and MD values for three cerebellar WM ROI measures: (1) the peduncle composite included averaged values for bilateral superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles; (2) the bilateral middle cerebellar peduncle (i.e., average of left and right hemispheres); and (3) bilateral within‐cerebellar WM (i.e., left and right hemisphere average). The middle cerebellar peduncle was specifically examined in addition to the cerebellar peduncle composite because the DTI metrics obtained with the current pipeline are more robust for this ROI compared to the inferior and superior peduncles, which, due to their small structure, are more prone to misalignments and misrepresentation in an ROI approach (Rezende et al. 2019). Each DTI measure was Fisher's z‐transformed before being averaged.

Structural MRI scans were also processed with MRICloud to obtain cerebellar volumetric measures. Volume composites of interest included averaged values for (1) volume of the cerebellar peduncles, (2) bilateral within‐cerebellar WM volume, and (3) bilateral cerebellar GM volume. Volume of the fourth ventricle, which is adjacent to the cerebellum, was also obtained and used as a covariate in sensitivity analyses, as described below. All GM and WM volumes, as well as fourth ventricle volume, were adjusted for total intracranial volume (ICV) by regressing each bilateral volume on ICV (i.e., total volume of tissues, ventricles, and sulci from the MPRAGE scans); the standardized residuals were used in the analyses described below. CSF Biomarker Measures

Details of CSF collection, processing, storage, and biomarker measurement (including coefficients of variation) have been published previously (Greenberg et al. 2022). Briefly, CSF was collected via lumbar puncture after an overnight fast; samples were aliquoted into polypropylene cryotubes and stored in a −80°C freezer. Samples were thawed for the first time to measure amyloid beta (Aβ40 and Aβ42), phosphorylated tau (p‐tau181), and total tau using fully automated electrochemiluminescence assays (Lumipulse G1200 platform; Fujirebio Diagnostics Inc.). Biomarkers of AD‐specific pathology used in these analyses included Aβ42/Aβ40 and p‐tau181. We used the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio instead of Aβ42 alone to adjust for individual differences in total Aβ production and reduce the impact of preanalytic factors (e.g., Gervaise‐Henry et al. 2017; Lewczuk et al. 2016).

Statistical Analysis

Multiple linear regression models were used to examine the associations of age and CSF AD biomarker levels with cerebellar WM microstructure and cerebellar WM and GM volumes. The first set of regression models examined the associations of age, sex, regional volume, APOE ε4 status, p‐tau181, and Aβ42/Aβ40 with cerebellar microstructure. Outcomes included FA and MD of the three WM ROIs: cerebellar peduncle composite, middle cerebellar peduncle, within‐cerebellar WM. For each model, the regional volume measure reflected the volume of the same regions from which microstructure metrics were derived, to account for the potential influence of atrophy in each region. The second set of regression models examined the associations of age, sex, APOE ε4 status, p‐tau181, and Aβ42/Aβ40 with GM and WM cerebellar volumes. The three outcome variables in these models were the volume of bilateral cerebellar GM, the volume of WM within the cerebellum, and the volume of the cerebellar peduncles. The third set of regression models examined the associations between the six cerebellar microstructural measures and the two domain‐specific cognitive composite scores (outcomes), covarying age, sex, years of education, APOE ε4 status, regional volume, p‐tau181, and Aβ42/Aβ40. To address concerns regarding multiple comparisons, a false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and results of interest (i.e., age and Aβ42/Aβ40) that remain significant at an FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05 threshold are highlighted in the corresponding results tables (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995).

Several sensitivity analyses were run. The first sensitivity analysis additionally covaried volume of the fourth ventricle to further address potential CSF partial volume contamination of FA/MD metrics (see Table S1). A second sensitivity analysis examined whether results changed when covarying a summary vascular risk score (see Table S2), given the influence of vascular health on both WM microstructure and cognitive performance (e.g., Jacobs et al. 2013; Kennedy and Raz 2009; Williams et al. 2019). A third sensitivity analysis examined whether the results from all primary models changed when excluding participants with a diagnosis of “impaired not MCI” (see Tables S3–S5). Finally, an additional sensitivity analysis substituted binary CSF AD biomarker values based on clinically derived cut points (Pettigrew et al. 2022) for the continuous biomarker values (see Table S6).

Associations of Age and AD Biomarker Levels With Structural Cerebellar Metrics

In the first set of regression models for cerebellar WM microstructure, older age was associated with greater MD in the cerebellar peduncle composite, as well as the MCP (ps < 0.01, Table 2). Age was not significantly associated with FA metrics (ps > 0.831). Furthermore, more abnormal Aβ levels were associated with lower MD in the peduncle composite (p = 0.018), the MCP (p = 0.004; see Figure 2), and in the cerebellar WM (p = 0.042). Associations with MCP alone remained significant following FDR adjustment for multiple comparisons (Table 2). Patterns were consistent when using clinically derived CSF cut‐points for AD biomarker positivity, particularly for the MCP: MD levels were lower in amyloid‐positive compared to amyloid‐negative participants (see Table S6). In contrast, there were no associations between p‐tau181 levels and WM microstructure. Results were consistent when the volume of the fourth ventricle or a vascular risk summary score were included as additional covariates (see Tables S1 and S2).

Associations Between Cerebellar Structural Measures and Cognitive Performance

As shown in Table 4, better executive function performance was associated with higher FA and lower MD in the peduncle composite and the MCP (Figure 3, top), as well as cerebellar WM (Figure 3, bottom; all ps < 0.05). MD in the peduncle composite and FA in the MCP and within‐cerebellar WM remained significant following correction for multiple comparisons. Better visuospatial performance was associated with lower MD in the peduncle composite and cerebellar WM (both p ≤ 0.05), with similar non‐significant trends for MD in the MCP (p = 0.08) and FA in the peduncle composite (p = 0.07). Neither regional cerebellar GM nor WM volumes were associated with cognitive performance (ps > 0.09). Volumes were also not associated with cognition when DTI measures were not included in the models (data not shown).

Discussion

The current study analyzed cerebellar micro‐ and macrostructure in a sample of middle‐aged and older cognitively unimpaired participants, evaluating relationships with age, CSF biomarkers of AD pathology, and cognitive performance. Older age was associated with lower cerebellar GM and WM volumes and with greater MD in the cerebellar peduncles, the tracts that connect the cerebellum to the rest of the brain and spinal cord. In contrast, more abnormal CSF Aβ levels were associated with lower MD in the middle cerebellar peduncle, in a composite of the superior, middle, and inferior peduncles, and within‐cerebellar WM. Similar patterns were evident using clinically derived CSF cut‐points for AD biomarker positivity, which were strongest for the middle cerebellar peduncle. Further, lower MD in the peduncles and cerebellar WM was associated with better executive function and visuospatial composite scores, whereas cerebellar volumetric measures were not related to cognition. These results highlight the importance of cerebellar WM microstructure to cognition among cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged and older adults and further suggest that cerebellar WM is associated with the early accumulation of amyloid in the absence of clinical symptoms. As described in greater detail below, the directionality of these findings is consistent with those reported in cerebral WM and may reflect inflammatory responses to early Aβ deposition.

The finding that more abnormal CSF Aβ levels were associated with lower cerebellar MD values in the cerebellar peduncles and cerebellar WM is consistent with prior findings from cerebral WM tracts in which Aβ, measured on PET imaging, was associated with higher FA and lower MD in cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged to older adults (Benitez et al. 2022; Dong et al. 2020; Racine et al. 2014). To explain these findings, Benitez et al. (2022) proposed a biphasic model that describes WM microstructure dynamics across different stages of AD. In the preclinical phase, they propose that amyloid deposition is associated with greater diffusion restriction, reflecting inflammation and myelin repair, which they suggest may be a compensatory mechanism that supports the maintenance of cognitive performance in the presence of early amyloid pathology. As AD pathology progresses, such adaptive processes fail, microstructure deteriorates, and WM atrophy becomes evident, potentially driven by myelin breakdown during the early symptomatic phase of the disease (e.g., MCI). During the transient period of greater diffusion restriction in preclinical AD, they propose that diffusion may be strongly influenced by Aβ‐related neuroinflammation; during later stages, diffusion may primarily be driven by neurodegeneration and myelin breakdown (Benitez et al. 2022; Dong et al. 2020). Relatedly, biphasic models of neuroinflammation in AD suggest that decreases in MD during the preclinical phase may be driven by cytotoxic edema, an inflammatory response in which neurons and microglia swell due to intracellular water accumulation, which is associated with greater diffusion restriction. Increases in MD during later disease stages are proposed to reflect tissue degeneration and extracellular expansion (Mizuno 2012). While this study was not designed to examine these issues, our results showing associations between more abnormal Aβ and lower MD in participants who are cognitively unimpaired are consistent with the initial phase of this model and further suggest that Aβ‐related WM microstructure changes are not only evident in the cerebrum but also the cerebellum. Future longitudinal studies directly examining measures of neuroinflammation, microglial activation and reactivity, and myelin integrity are needed to more directly test how amyloid impacts WM microstructure over time, including across early and later disease stages.

Of note, CSF p‐tau was not associated with cerebellar microstructure, while the association with amyloid was significant even after accounting for p‐tau levels. These findings may suggest that cerebellar diffusion metrics are more sensitive to amyloid than p‐tau. Alternatively, the absence of a relationship between CSF p‐tau and WM microstructure might reflect the relative sparing of the cerebellum with respect to tau pathology in AD. While prior work has documented that the cerebellum may harbor Aβ, primarily diffuse plaques, tau tangles are rare and tend not to accumulate until late disease stages (for reviews see Arleo et al. 2024; Jacobs et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2024, but see Sepulveda‐Falla et al. 2011). For example, a previous neuropathological study showed that cerebellar amyloid, but not hyperphosphorylated tau, significantly increased across Braak stages and corresponded with higher protein expression associated with neuroinflammation and microglial activation (Garland et al. 2023). This supports the selective association of cerebellar DTI metrics with Aβ but not p‐tau in the current study. These findings may also be attributable to the earlier presence of Aβ versus p‐tau in the preclinical stage of AD in general (e.g., Hanseeuw et al. 2019; Sperling et al. 2011).

Regarding age effects, the association of lower cerebellar GM and WM volumes with older age is consistent with previous research with non‐demented samples (e.g., Andersen et al. 2003; Bendlin et al. 2010; Bernard and Seidler 2013, 2014; Gellersen et al. 2021; Hoogendam et al. 2012; Jernigan et al. 2001; Koppelmans et al. 2015; Raz et al. 2010, 2005; Stalter et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2017), suggesting cerebellar atrophy with increasing age occurs in both tissue types.

The present study also found that older age was associated with greater MD in the cerebellar peduncles, with a similar non‐significant trend for greater within‐cerebellar WM MD. This is consistent with a few previous cross‐sectional studies in participants without cognitive impairment that reported greater MD with older age in cerebellar WM (Abe et al. 2008; Bennett et al. 2010; Coutu et al. 2014) and the cerebellar peduncles (Cavallari et al. 2013). However, in contrast with some prior studies that reported associations between lower FA in cerebellar WM and older age (Bendlin et al. 2010; Bennett et al. 2010; Burzynska et al. 2010; Cavallari et al. 2013; Coutu et al. 2014; Hoagey et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2016), the present results were specific to MD. Reasons for these differences are not entirely clear, but may reflect differences in participant characteristics, such as age, cognitive functioning, vascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, high cholesterol, diabetes), or AD biomarker levels (e.g., Aβ, tau), which were not assessed in previous studies. Additional research is needed to understand the timing of possible differences between MD and FA effects in the context of age and AD biomarkers.

Supporting the hypothesis that the associations between more abnormal CSF Aβ and lower cerebellar WM MD may play a functional role in maintaining cognitive performance during early stages of amyloid pathology, both lower MD and higher FA in the cerebellar peduncles and cerebellar WM were associated with better cognitive performance. Specifically, both lower MD and higher FA were associated with better executive function performance, consistent with the demonstrated role of the cerebellum in this domain (Bellebaum and Daum 2007; Paitel and Nielson 2023; Schmahmann 2019; Stoodley 2012; Stoodley and Schmahmann 2009). While less robust, lower MD was also associated with better visuospatial performance. These relationships were unique to microstructure metrics, as the associations between the cerebellar volumetric measures and cognitive performance were not significant. To date, few studies have examined the relationship between diffusion‐based measures of cerebellar microstructure and cognitive performance in adult participants without cognitive impairment or cerebellar lesions. Consistent with our results, the existing studies have reported that among young‐ to middle‐aged adults without cognitive impairment, greater FA and lower MD in the cerebellar peduncles were associated with better performance on working memory, procedural learning, language, and phonemic fluency measures (Bianco et al. 2023; Jossinger et al. 2024; Sobczak‐Edmans et al. 2019; Takahashi et al. 2010; Zekelman et al. 2025). Taken together, these results expand our understanding of the associations between cerebellar structural characteristics and cognitive performance in cognitively unimpaired adult samples into older age.

Limitations of the present study should be noted. First, the cohort is primarily non‐Hispanic White and highly educated. Future research with larger, more diverse, and community‐based cohorts is needed to evaluate applicability to the larger population. The current sample also over‐represents APOE ε4 carriers and those with a family history of AD, which limits the generalizability of the present findings. Second, the current study analyzed AD biomarkers derived from CSF, thus providing a global measure of biomarker levels. While CSF AD biomarkers, particularly Aβ42/Aβ40, change earlier in the course of AD than amyloid and tau PET imaging markers, thus providing a particularly early window into AD‐related brain changes (Jack Jr et al. 2013; Palmqvist et al. 2016; Salvadó et al. 2024), future research is needed to examine how regionally specific deposition of Aβ and tau relate to regionally specific changes in cerebellar macro‐ and microstructure. Third, future analyses will benefit from larger sample sizes to minimize concerns regarding potential model over‐fitting from the inclusion of numerous relevant covariates. Fourth, we focused on the middle cerebellar peduncle and a composite of the superior, middle, and inferior peduncles. Future DTI research with tract‐based spatial statistics (TBSS) or comparable approaches that can provide more regionally specific measures of cerebellar WM microstructure will be necessary to both replicate the present findings and analyze the superior, middle, and inferior tracts separately. This will also enable analysis of age‐ and AD‐related changes in regionally specific cerebellar WM tracts, which may reveal convergent or divergent patterns, similar to those reported in lobule‐level cerebellar GM volumes in normative aging versus AD dementia (Gellersen et al. 2021). The biophysical specificity provided by multi‐shell diffusion imaging protocols and multi‐compartment modeling approaches will also be advantageous to address underlying mechanisms including neuroinflammation, microglial activation and reactivity, and myelin integrity. Future longitudinal research is necessary to assess age‐ and AD pathology‐related cerebellar WM changes and their relationship with cognitive change over time, as well as how WM changes relate to cerebral‐cerebellar functional connectivity patterns in preclinical AD (e.g., Paitel et al. 2025).

Conclusions

The current study indicates that older age was associated with microstructural and volumetric cerebellar GM and WM alterations, while Aβ levels were associated specifically with cerebellar WM microstructure among cognitively unimpaired middle‐aged and older adults. WM microstructure metrics were also associated with cognitive performance, most strongly with executive function, highlighting the importance of cerebellar WM to cognition. These findings expand accumulating evidence of cerebellar involvement in age‐ and AD‐related cognitive decline, and suggest that cerebellar WM microstructure alterations may be sensitive markers of preclinical AD.
