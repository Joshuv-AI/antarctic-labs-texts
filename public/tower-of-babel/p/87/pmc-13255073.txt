Effects of protease and lipase supplementation in starter diet on growth performance, small intestine morphology, and immunity in young broilers
Authors: Milad Ghavipanjeh, Sina Payvastegan, Seyyed Ali Mirghelenj, Seyyed Meysam Abtahi Froushani, Negin Delfani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Protease supplementation in the starter diet improved feed efficiency, intestinal morphology, antioxidant status, and upregulated hepatic Nrf2 gene expression in broiler chicks.•Lipase supplementation increased body weight gain, improved feed conversion ratio, and boosted total antioxidant capacity in both plasma and liver during the starter phase.•Concurrent supplementation of protease and lipase did not produce additive or synergistic effects, highlighting the importance of precise formulation of multi-enzyme starter diets.

Introduction

Soybean meal is recognized as the primary source of protein in poultry diets; however, the presence of anti-nutritional factors, such as trypsin inhibitors, reduces nutrient availability and limits the amount of soybean meal that can be included (Park et al., 2020a). Protein digestibility in broilers is around 80–85%, which is lower than the 90% digestibility of starch, suggesting that some protein is excreted from the digestive system (Duque-Ramírez et al., 2023). Exogenous protease enzymes complement endogenously secreted proteases, thereby enhancing the efficiency of dietary protein hydrolysis and digestion. Exogenous proteases complement endogenous protease activity (Liu et al., 2025). Reduced anti‑nutritional factors, improved crude protein and amino acid digestibility, and lower nitrogen excretion and ammonia emissions have been reported in broiler chickens, indicating improved protein utilization and environmental sustainability (Alshelmani et al., 2024; Amer et al., 2021; Peñuela-Sierra et al., 2024).

Soybean oil, corn oil, sunflower oil, and canola oil are commonly used as energy sources in broiler diets (Vertiprakhov et al., 2023). Broilers fed oil‑included diets exhibited improved growth performance relative to those fed oil‑free diets while maintaining comparable nutritional value (Attia et al., 2020). Lipases are enzymes classified as triacylglycerol acyl hydrolases, responsible for the hydrolysis of fats and oils into glycerol and free fatty acids (Arshad et al., 2021). Hu et al. (2018) reported that supplementing broiler diets with 0.3 g/kg of lipase, despite reduced metabolizable energy, enhanced growth performance and nutrient digestibility, and reduced abdominal fat percentage during days 1 to 28. Similarly, Castro and Kim (2021) observed that 0.1 g/kg lipase improved feed conversion ratio (FCR) and body weight (BW) gain in broilers receiving diets reduced in metabolizable energy, crude protein, and essential amino acids.

The digestive system of young chicks is initially immature, with low secretion and activity of peptic and pancreatic proteases (Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021). This leads to inefficient protein digestion and high endogenous amino acid losses during the first week post-hatch, making them prone to amino acid deficiencies (Dersjant-Li et al., 2022; Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021). As the birds mature, protease activity increases, improving protein digestion, amino acid absorption, and overall protein utilization while reducing endogenous losses (Barua et al., 2021). Similarly, newly hatched broiler chicks have limited ability to digest and utilize dietary lipids during the first week post-hatch, due to the delayed maturation of pancreatic lipase (which reaches maximal levels only after 10–16 days), together with the age-related insufficiency of bile salts, their inefficient recycling, and the inadequacy of fatty acid-binding proteins essential for lipid utilization (Jaapar et al., 2020; Oketch et al., 2023; Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021).

The first week of broiler chicken rearing is the most critical period, as key physiological and immune functions undergo rapid development (Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021; Yerpes et al., 2020). Early‑life growth rate is a key determinant of later performance, health, and welfare in broilers (van Der Wagt et al., 2020). Upon hatching, broiler chicks carry a residual yolk equivalent to approximately 12% of their BW (Han et al., 2025). The residual yolk is crucial for chick growth and development during the first day’s post-hatch (Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021). The protein and energy utilization may be positively affected by the residual yolk (Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021). The residual yolk also serves as an important source of maternal antibodies that support chick immunity in the first days after hatch (Wong & Uni, 2021). Variations in yolk utilization during incubation and the post‑hatch period may influence chick quality and have long‑term effects on growth performance and health (van Der Wagt et al., 2020).

It is important to note that utilizing residual yolk lipids and proteins as an energy source is inefficient (Wong & Uni, 2021). Lipids should primarily provide phospholipids, choline, and triglycerides essential for cell membrane development, while proteins should supply maternal antibodies to support passive immunity (Wong & Uni, 2021). The authors of the current study hypothesize that supplementing exogenous protease and lipase during the early post‑hatch period, when digestive capacity for dietary proteins and lipids is physiologically limited, can improve growth performance, gut development, and yolk sac residue absorption through enhanced utilization of dietary protein and fat. To date, while protease and lipase have been studied individually, limited information exists on their combined effects on physiological and performance parameters of broilers during the critical starter phase. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of concurrent protease and lipase supplementation in the starter diet on performance, yolk sac absorption, internal organ development, antioxidant status, intestinal morphology, and immune response in young broiler chickens, aiming to clarify their role in improving dietary protein and fat utilization during the early post‑hatch period.

Material and methods

Bird care

The trial was carried out at Urmia University’s Research Farm in Iran, under the approved protocol of the Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. IR-UU-AEC-3/2; approval date: 2025.04.07).

Treatments, birds' husbandry, and diets

A total of 408 one‑day‑old male Ross 308 broiler chicks (BW: 39.7 ± 1.81 g) were obtained from a commercial hatchery and randomly assigned to four dietary treatments arranged in a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement in a completely randomized design, comprising two levels of protease (0 or 0.1 g/kg) and two levels of lipase (0 or 0.2 g/kg), with six replicates and 17 chicks per replicate. At the beginning of the experiment, all chicks were individually weighed to record the total weight of each experimental unit (with a floor area of 1.1 m²). There were no significant (P > 0.05) differences in initial BW among treatments prior to the start of the experiment. The experimental treatments included: a control group fed a diet without protease and lipase enzyme supplements, 2) a group fed a diet containing only a protease enzyme supplement at 0.1 g/kg, 3) a group fed a diet containing only a lipase enzyme supplement at 0.2 g/kg and 4) a group fed a diet containing both protease and lipase enzyme supplements at 0.1 g/kg and 0.2 g/kg, respectively. The experiment lasted 30 days to ensure that performance parameters for the entire period did not deviate significantly from the timing of treatment administration and was divided into three phases: starter (1–10 days), grower (11–24 days), and finisher (25–30 days). Protease and lipase enzyme supplementation was applied only during 1–10 days of age (starter diet) in the present experiment. Fresh water and feed were provided ad libitum, and the feed was offered in mash form. Feed was provided using manual plastic tube feeders, and drinking water was supplied via automatic bell drinkers. Fresh wood shavings were used as bedding material on the concrete floor at an approximate depth of 10 cm. During the first three days, the room temperature was maintained at 31 °C with 60% relative humidity, then gradually decreased to a steady 21 °C by the fourth week, remaining constant for the rest of the experiment (Aviagen, 2025). Consequently, the temperature–humidity index (THI) values were calculated according to the formula described by Philatha et al. (2026) and presented graphically in Fig. 1. The lighting program also consisted of 23 h of light and 1 h of darkness during the first week, and from day 7 onward, the dark period was gradually increased to 4 h (Aviagen, 2025; Philatha et al., 2026). The protease enzyme's activity in this study was 200,000 units per gram. Similarly, the lipase enzyme used, BONAPAZ AK®, was an alkaline lipase in powdered form with an activity of 100,000 units per gram (Bioluence Co, Tehran, Iran). Performance and internal organ weights

During the 30-day experimental period, the chicks and their corresponding feed intake (FI) for each pen were weighed collectively at 10, and 30 days of age. The BW gain, FI, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for the early phase (1–10 days) and for the overall experimental period (1–30 days). Mortality was recorded immediately upon observation to ensure accurate adjustment of the FCR (Delfani et al., 2023).

At the end of days 3 and 5 of the experiment, two chicks from each replicate (12 chicks per treatment) were randomly selected and euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation (Lee & Kong, 2026). After evisceration, the carcass and internal organs, including the heart, gizzard, bursa, spleen, and small intestine at 3 days of age, as well as the carcass and internal organs such as the heart, gizzard, pancreas, bursa, spleen, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum at 5 days of age, were weighed and expressed as a percentage of live BW. The weights of the internal organs were measured using a scale with an accuracy of one-hundredth of a gram (0.01 g). Furthermore, residual yolk were also collected from chicks at 3 and 5 days of age and weighed using a high‑precision analytical balance with an accuracy of ± 0.001 g. Based on the observation of Wang et al. (2020) that the residual yolk became negligible at 168 hours post‑hatch (day 7), while accurate measurement was possible at 72 and 120 hours post‑hatch (days 3 and 5), the chicks in the present study were therefore slaughtered on days 3 and 5 of age.

Morphology of the small intestine

At 10 days of age, 12 chicks per treatment (two chicks from each replicate) were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation. Following euthanasia, the chicks were dissected, and tissue samples (5 cm in length) were excised from the middle segments of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. The duodenum was defined as the section extending from the pylorus of the gizzard to the end of the duodenal loop. The jejunum extended from the end of the duodenal loop to Meckel’s diverticulum, while the ileum extended from Meckel’s diverticulum to the ileocecal junction. The samples were then flushed with distilled water and fixed in 10% formalin. After fixation, the samples were dehydrated using a graded alcohol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections, 5 µm thickness, were prepared from the paraffin-embedded tissues using a microtome (Microm, HM 335), mounted on slides, and subjected to deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration through a descending alcohol series. Tissue sections were stained using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff staining methods. The evaluated parameters included villus height (VH), villus width (VW), crypt depth (CD), villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH: CD), muscular layer thickness, and goblet cell (GC) count. The VH was defined as the length from the tip of the villus to the base of the lamina propria, while VW was determined at the midpoint, measured between the upper and lower thirds of the villus. The distance from the base of the villus to the mucosal layer was referred to as CD. The ratio of VH: CD was calculated by dividing the VH value by the CD value. Measurements were taken from 10 villi per bird, considering only intact and unbroken villi. The number of GC was determined for the same 10 villi per bird, and the average was computed. The GC density was calculated by dividing the total GC count per bird by the average VH.

Blood metabolites

On day 10, blood samples were collected from two birds in each replicate (12 birds per treatment) and transferred into vials containing heparin as an anticoagulant. The samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature to separate the plasma, which was subsequently stored at −20 °C for further analysis of blood metabolites. Plasma samples were analyzed in duplicate for glucose, total protein, albumin, uric acid, cholesterol, and triglycerides using commercial quantitative diagnostic kits (Pars Azmoon, Iran). Measurements were conducted with an autoanalyzer (Alcyon 300, Abbott, USA).

Antioxidant status

The remaining plasma (12 birds per treatment) were used to measure malondialdehyde (MDA) and total antioxidant capacity (TAC). To measure the contents of MDA and TAC in liver tissue, 1 g of liver tissue samples was homogenized in 10 ml of phosphate buffer and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The levels of TAC and MDA in both plasma and tissue homogenate supernatants were determined in duplicate spectrophotometrically using colorimetric assay kits (ZellBio GmbH, Germany). The TAC assay is based on the reduction of ferric ions to ferrous ions under acidic conditions, resulting in the formation of a blue-colored complex measurable at 560 nm. The MDA assay is also based on the reaction of MDA with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) under acidic conditions and elevated temperature, forming a pink-colored MDA–TBA adduct that is quantified by measuring absorbance at 535 nm. The levels of MDA and TAC in the tissue homogenates were expressed as nanomoles per milligram of tissue protein (nmol/mg protein) and micromoles of Trolox equivalent per gram of protein (µmol/g protein), respectively.

Gene expression analysis

On day 10 of age, following autopsy, liver tissue samples (12 birds per treatment) were immediately placed into cryotubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Total RNA isolation: 100 mg Tissue samples were mixed with KIAZOL reagent (KIAZIST, Iran) and stored at −80 °C for 24 h, then thawed and processed with chloroform, vortexing, and centrifugation to separate RNA from other components. The RNA-containing layer was extracted, combined with isopropanol, and frozen to facilitate RNA precipitation. After centrifugation, the resulting RNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, dissolved in RNase-free water, and stored at −80 °C. It is noteworthy that to eliminate genomic DNA contamination, the extracted RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I and its appropriate buffer (Sinaclon Bioscience, Karaj, Iran). The RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA), ensuring an optical density (OD 260/280) ratio between 1.8 and 2.2. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis: The cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Pars Tous, Tehran, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, thawed RNA (1000 µg) was combined with oligo-dT nucleotides and RNAase-free water in a microtube. Reagents for cDNA synthesis, including RNase inhibitor, reverse transcriptase, and buffer, were then added to the reaction. The mixture was vortexed and incubated at 45 °C for 60 min for reverse transcription, followed by incubation at 70 °C for 10 min to inactivate the reverse transcriptase, and finally cooled to 4 °C. The synthesized cDNA was subsequently stored at −20 °C. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR): The target genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, inflammatory responses, and antioxidant defenses are peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα), nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), and nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2), respectively, linking these pathways to metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress (Zhang et al., 2023). The β-actin gene was selected as the endogenous (housekeeping) gene. The target cDNAs (PPAR-α, Nrf2, and NF-κB) and reference cDNA (β-actin) were amplified using SYBER Green PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa Biotechnology, China) on the AB StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Grand Island, NY, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Each reaction was performed in a total volume of 20 μL, containing 1 μL of cDNA (200 ng), 10 μL of SYBR Green mix, 7 μL of DEPC-treated water, and 1 μL of each primer (10 pmol). Each target gene was tested in duplicate, and the mRNA levels were normalized to the β-actin ratio using the 2−ΔΔC(t) method, with the relative fold change calculated and presented as values compared to the control group (Jia et al., 2024). Immune status

All tests used to assess immune status were performed on 2 birds from each replicate (12 birds per treatment).

Innate cellular immune activity

Heterophil isolation: Heterophils were isolated from the peripheral blood of birds according to a previously described protocol (Swaggerty et al., 2003). Blood was collected in tubes containing disodium ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) and mixed thoroughly. The blood and EDTA were pooled, diluted 1:1 with RPMI 1640 media containing 1% methylcellulose, and centrifuged at 25 g for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new conical tube, washed with Ca²⁺- and Mg²⁺-free Hanks’ balanced salt solution (1:1), and layered onto discontinuous Histopaque gradients with specific gravities of 1.077 over 1.119. The gradients were centrifuged at 250 g for 1 h. The Histopaque layers were collected, washed with RPMI 1640 (1:1), resuspended in fresh RPMI 1640, counted using a hemocytometer, and diluted to the desired concentration in RPMI.

Evaluation of heterophil phagocytosis: The assay was conducted with modifications to a prior protocol (Shushtari & Froushani, 2017). Heterophils were incubated with a neutral red-stained, heat-stabilized zymosan suspension at a 1:10 ratio for 30 min. After discarding the supernatant, the phagocytosis process was halted by adding Baker’s formol calcium solution. The heterophils were washed three times. Internalized neutral red was then solubilized using acidified alcohol, and absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 550 nm.

Assessing the respiratory burst potential of heterophiles (NBT assay): The respiratory burst activity of heterophils was assessed by measuring the reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) to formazan, serving as an indicator of reactive oxygen species production, following modified protocols from previous studies. Specifically, heterophiles (2 × 106 cells/ml) were combined with 0.1% NBT in PBS (pH 7.4), and either heat-killed opsonized Baker’s yeast (108 cells/ml) or non-opsonized Baker’s yeast. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Afterwards, excess NBT was washed away, and the reduced dye was extracted with N, N-dimethylformamide and quantified at 540 nm. Results were expressed as the ratio of the respiratory burst induced by opsonized yeast to that induced by non-opsonized yeast.

Evaluation of acquired humoral immunity

To evaluate the acquired humoral immune response, an anti-sheep red blood cell (SRBC) antibody assay was conducted. To prepare SRBC for injection into birds, blood was drawn from a healthy sheep and transferred into EDTA-containing tubes to prevent clotting. The blood was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma. The red blood cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged again. This washing and centrifugation step was repeated three times until the supernatant was completely clear. Finally, the red blood cells were collected, suspended in PBS at a 10% (vol: vol) ratio, and prepared for injection. On the 3rd and 10th days of the study, two chicks from each replicate group were selected and given an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 ml containing a 10% suspension of SRBC (Alipour et al., 2015; Rasouli et al., 2015). Blood samples were collected from the brachial vein 7 days after each injection (on days 10 and 17 of the study). The serum was separated by centrifuging the samples at 3000 rpm for 10 min and then stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. The total anti-SRBC antibody titers were assessed using the micro-hemagglutination technique, as described by Wegmann and Smithies (Wegmann & Smithies, 1966). The total anti-SRBC antibody titer was determined by calculating the base-2 logarithm of the highest dilution at which hemagglutination occurred.

Evaluation of acquired cellular immunity

To assess acquired cellular immunity, the cutaneous basophil hypersensitivity (CBH) reaction and lymphocyte proliferation tests were conducted. CBH reaction test: On day 10, as described by Hashemipour et al. (2013), the thickness of the toe web between the third and fourth digits of the right foot was measured in millimeters using a constant-tension micrometer. Following this initial measurement, 100 μg of phytohemagglutinin, suspended in 0.10 mL of sterile saline, was injected into the toe web. Swelling of the toe web was measured again at 12- and 24-hours post-injection. The response was calculated by subtracting the initial skin thickness measurement from the subsequent measurements.

Lymphocyte proliferation assay (MTT assay): The assay quantifies the conversion of yellow tetrazolium salt into purple formazan crystals by proliferating lymphocytes, where formazan production correlates with the number of viable cells and reflects changes in proliferation rates caused by different vaccination programs and treatments. In brief, blood samples (2 ml) were collected from three chickens per group at the end of the study using heparinized syringes. Each sample was mixed with 2 ml of cell culture medium, layered on Ficoll, and centrifuged at 400 g for 35 min. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) floating between the Ficoll and diluted blood were gently collected and centrifuged at 300 g for 15 min. The precipitated PBMCs were retained and the supernatant discarded; this washing step was repeated. Then, 2 ml of cell culture medium was added to the PBMCs and shaken to resuspend the cells. For cell viability and counting, 20 μl of PBMCs were mixed with 20 μl of trypan blue and counted using a hemocytometer. A cell suspension of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL was prepared, and 100 μL was added to 96-well plates, then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 72 h. Each sample was prepared in triplicate with 10 μL of phytohemagglutinin (PHA) at 5 µg/mL, and three replicates without PHA. Afterward, 25 μl of MTT(3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) reagent (5 µg/ml in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. The MTT solution was removed, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm with a 690 nm reference using a microplate reader.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using the general linear model procedure of SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), according to a completely randomized design with a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement of treatments. The two factors were protease supplementation (0 and 0.1 g/kg) and lipase supplementation (0 and 0.2 g/kg). The following statistical model was applied for the factorial analysis of the four treatments:Yijk=μ+PROi+LIj+(PRO×LI)ij+eijk

In this model, Yijk is the dependent variable, μ is the overall mean, PROi and LIj are the main effects of supplemental protease (i = 1 to 2) and lipase levels (j = 1 to 2), respectively, (PRO×LI) ij represents their interaction effect, and eijk is the random error. Prior to the analysis of variance, all data were tested for normality using the univariate procedure in SAS. Homogeneity of variance was also assessed using Levene’s test. When a significant effect was detected, multiple comparisons of treatment means were performed using the PDIFF option with Tukey’s test adjustments, with significance determined at P ≤ 0.05. Main effects were interpreted only when no significant interaction effect was observed.

Discussion

The reduction in FI observed in young broilers supplemented with protease during the first 10 days is likely due to improved amino acid supply and utilization, which enhances nutrient efficiency and consequently reduces feed requirements, as reported by Walk et al. (2019). Amino acids play a key role in regulating FI and energy balance in the brain Ghashghaei et al. (2025), as studies in broiler chickens have shown that amino acids such as methionine and lysine can influence feeding behavior through their action on nutrient-sensing neurons (Khwatenge et al., 2020; Maynard et al., 2023). Similar reductions in FI following protease supplementation have been reported by Cowieson et al. (2018); Fru-Nji et al. (2011), and Walk et al. (2019).

In the current study, lipase supplementation improved BW gain during the 1–10-day period, likely through enhanced nutrient digestibility (dry matter, fat, energy, and nitrogen) and increased pancreatic secretory activity (Arshad et al., 2020; Brenes et al., 2008; Castro & Kim, 2021; Chen et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2018). Similarly, Arshad et al. (Arshad et al., 2020) found improved BW gain during 1–21 days with 0.3 g/kg lipase in reduced-energy diets, but not in later phases. Castro and Kim (2021) also reported improved BW gain with the inclusion of 0.1 g/kg lipase, even in diets formulated with reduced energy levels. In contrast to the present findings, other studies have shown that lipase supplementation does not significantly affect BW gain Wang et al. (2018) and may even reduce and BW gain and FI in broilers (Al-Marzooqi & Leeson, 1999). This effect may be associated with differences in dietary energy levels, which play a key role in regulating FI, as well as variations in the type of lipase enzyme used, such as possible contamination with cholecystokinin—a peptide known to suppress appetite (Al-Marzooqi & Leeson, 1999, 2000).

The combined supplementation of protease and lipase did not produce additive effects on FCR. Instead, improvements were observed when each enzyme was supplemented alone in diets lacking the other enzyme. The interaction effect may be linked to the lower FI observed with protease supplementation. Previous studies have demonstrated synergistic effects of various enzyme combinations, such as protease with phytase (Farrokhi et al., 2021), protease with carbohydrase (Yuan et al., 2017), and protease with both phytase and carbohydrase (Rodríguez-Soriano et al., 2025). However, there are no reports on the combined supplementation of protease and lipase. It should also be noted that enzyme co-supplementation does not always result in additive or synergistic effects (Dos Santos et al., 2017). Combined enzyme supplementation may limit the effectiveness of individual enzymes, potentially due to substrate limitation or the bird's inability to optimize performance in response to enzyme combinations (Dos Santos et al., 2017). In an in vitro study, Wang et al. (2018) reported that lipase activity decreases when exposed to proteases and bile salts. Key limitations of lipase supplementation in animal feed include poor stability in the acidic environment of the stomach, inactivation by physiologically relevant bile salt concentrations, and susceptibility to proteolytic enzymes in the digestive tract (Wang et al., 2018). Other mechanisms may also contribute to these effects. Exogenous enzyme supplementation can increase endogenous secretions (Cowieson et al., 2006), while protease has been shown to accelerate digesta transit in broilers (Dos Santos et al., 2017) and enhance gastric emptying in vitro (Zhou et al., 2025). Additionally, alterations in gut microbiota, such as increased Lactobacillus populations, may reduce fat digestion efficiency (Farrokhi et al., 2021; Uzair et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2015). A reduction in FCR following protease supplementation in young broilers has been reported by Farrokhi et al. (2021) and Qiu et al. (2023). Reduced FCR following lipase supplementation was also observed in studies by Hu et al. (2018); Wang et al. (2018) and Fernandes et al. (2023).

The increase in relative yolk sac weight at day 3 in chicks fed the protease-supplemented diet may be associated with their reduced FI, as feed consumption stimulates peristaltic movements and facilitates the release and absorption of yolk sac contents (Van Der Wagt et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). However, by day 5, supplementation with protease alone resulted in an increase in relative yolk sac weight. The yolk sac, which contains approximately 35–40% lipids, mainly in the form of triglycerides at hatching (Wang et al., 2020), suggests that lipase supplementation in young chicks with an underdeveloped digestive system may enhance fat digestion (Arshad et al., 2021). Interestingly, the reduction in yolk sac residue at day 5 following lipase supplementation was observed only in diets without protease, possibly due to the lower feed intake in protease-fed birds or to interactions between protease and lipase activities.

The observed reductions in the relative weights of the small intestine and its segments, as well as the pancreas, may be explained by a decrease in the antinutritional effects of soybean meal, especially reduced trypsin inhibitor activity, as a result of dietary protease supplementation (Erdaw et al., 2018; Wedekind et al., 2020). Pancreas weight is directly influenced by dietary trypsin inhibitor levels, escalating as inhibitor concentrations rise (Wedekind et al., 2020). The reductions in the relative weights of the jejunum, ileum, and the entire small intestine following protease supplementation are aligned with the findings of Erdaw et al. (2018). The reduction in relative pancreas weight observed with protease supplementation is also in line with findings reported by Amiri et al. (2021); Erdaw et al. (2018); Wedekind et al. (2020), and Song et al. (2023). This study also observed that protease supplementation decreased cecal relative weight. Cecal development and function are influenced by several factors, including feed composition, fermentable substrate levels, and digesta retention time in the gut (Martínez et al., 2021). Studies by Borda-Molina et al. (2019) and Yi et al. (2024) have demonstrated that adding protease to broiler diets can alter both the composition and metabolism of gut microbiota. This modulation may result from enhanced protein digestion and the direct antimicrobial effects of protease on particular bacterial species (Amiri et al., 2021). Additionally, the decrease in FI linked to protease-supplemented diets might also explain the reduction in cecal weight.

In birds, the bursa of Fabricius serves as a primary lymphoid organ, while the spleen functions as a secondary lymphoid organ; both play essential roles in immune defense (Martínez et al., 2021). Moreover, lymphoid organs such as the bursa, thymus, and spleen are intimately connected with the small intestine in young birds. Improved activity and health of the small intestine enhance the absorption of vital nutrients, thereby boosting the production of B and T cells and increasing the relative mass of these immune organs (Martínez et al., 2021). Furthermore, the enhanced absorptive capacity of the ileum suggests that the increased relative weights of the bursa and spleen may result from improved nutrient uptake and subsequent stimulation of the immune system (Martínez et al., 2021). Consistent with the present study, Hu et al. (2018) and Arshad et al. (2020) also reported that lipase supplementation had no significant effect on the relative weights of the gizzard, liver, bursa of Fabricius, carcass, or heart in broilers.

Morphological changes in the small intestine are affected by feed composition, particularly antinutritional protein compounds such as trypsin inhibitors (Cowieson et al., 2018). Exogenous protease can partially mitigate their detrimental effects and increase protein and amino acid digestibility, improving intestinal morphology (Cowieson et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2023). Several studies (Duque-Ramírez et al., 2023; Huyan et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2017) have reported increased VH and absorption capacity in the ileum of broilers following protease supplementation. In the present study, supplementation with protease enhanced absorptive capacity in the ileum, while no significant effects were observed on the morphology of the duodenum and jejunum. This is primarily because the activity of exogenous proteases is highly dependent on the pH (Cowieson et al., 2018) and feed retention time in different sections of the small intestine. Proteases, like other enzymes, are pH-specific, and the alkaline protease used in this study is well-suited to the neutral to mildly alkaline environment of the ileum (pH 7–7.5) (Castro & Kim, 2021; Ravindran, 2013). In addition, the longer feed retention time in the ileum (approximately 2 h) compared with the duodenum and jejunum (about 1 h) allows the enzyme sufficient time to act effectively (Svihus & Itani, 2019). Together, these factors explain why protease improved absorptive capacity in the ileum but had little effect in the upper small intestine.

Given that uric acid is a byproduct of purine metabolism and is tightly regulated by both its rate of production and net renal and intestinal reabsorption or secretion (Mandal & Mount, 2015; Qiu et al., 2023), the observed decrease in uric acid concentration with protease supplementation in the present study may reflect reduced protein intake and/or diminished protein catabolism (Qiu et al., 2023). Reduced plasma uric acid concentration following protease supplementation has also been reported in previous studies (Law et al., 2017; Qiu et al., 2023). The observed increase in plasma glucose level may also be attributed to enhanced starch digestibility resulting from protease supplementation, consistent with the findings of by Kalmendal and Tauson (2012). Other blood metabolites, including total protein, albumin, creatinine, cholesterol, and triglycerides, were not affected by either protease or lipase supplementation, in agreement with prior studies (Ahmad et al., 2023; Arshad et al., 2020; Ndazigaruye et al., 2019). These results suggest that exogenous enzymes can selectively modulate nutrient metabolism, enhancing amino acid and carbohydrate utilization without broadly altering other blood biochemical parameters.

Aerobic metabolism naturally generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which must be maintained at low levels to prevent oxidative damage (Kouvedaki et al., 2024). Excess ROS, indicated by biomarkers such as MDA, reflects lipid peroxidation and cellular damage, while TAC represents the overall effect of all antioxidants (Liang et al., 2024). The third level of the antioxidant defense system includes lipases, proteases, and DNA repair enzymes, which remove or repair cells damaged by oxidative stress (Surai et al., 2016). In broilers, the immature digestive system and insufficient endogenous enzyme secretion during the first week post-hatch limit protein, amino acid, and fat digestion (Hu et al., 2018; Mahmood et al., 2017; Oketch et al., 2023; Ravindran & Abdollahi, 2021). Protease supplementation improves amino acid and fat digestibility (Freitas et al., 2011; Lee et al., 2023; Park et al., 2020a; Peñuela-Sierra et al., 2024), supplying essential nutrients that support antioxidant defense mechanisms (Bacou et al., 2021). Moreover, protease can hydrolyze allergenic soybean proteins, such as glycinin and β-conglycinin, which are known to induce oxidative stress (Bacou et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020b; Yi et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2013), and may generate bioactive peptides with antioxidant properties (Famuwagun et al., 2021; Karamać et al., 2016). Collectively, these effects enhance antioxidant capacity and reduce oxidative stress in broilers during early development. Limited endogenous lipase secretion restricts fat digestion in young broilers (Yi et al., 2022). Supplementing exogenous lipase improves fat and dry matter digestibility (Arshad et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2018), thereby enhancing the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins essential for antioxidant defense (Félix et al., 2020). Improved yolk sac utilization may further increase the supply of antioxidants such as retinol, vitamin E, carotenoids, and coenzyme Q (Karadas et al., 2011), supporting the chick’s defense system. In this study, dietary lipase supplementation in protease-free diets significantly reduced hepatic MDA concentrations, suggesting an attenuation of oxidative stress. However, this beneficial effect was not observed when lipase was combined with protease, likely due to proteolytic degradation of the supplemental lipase (Wang et al., 2018; Yi et al., 2022), substrate competition from enhanced protein and fat digestion (Dos Santos et al., 2017), changes in intestinal transit time (Wang et al., 2018), or protease-induced alterations in the gut microbiota (Farrokhi et al., 2021; Uzair et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2015) that influence lipid metabolism (Sharifi et al., 2012).

There is limited information on the effects of protease and lipase supplementation on the hepatic expression of PPAR-α, Nrf2, and NF-κB genes in broiler chickens. PPAR-α is essential for regulating fatty acid oxidation in the liver (Zhang et al., 2023), while Nrf2 and NF-κB are redox-sensitive transcription factors that collaborate to maintain oxidative balance (Lee et al., 2018). NF-κB promotes inflammation by inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Liu et al., 2022; Surai et al., 2021), whereas Nrf2 defends against oxidative stress by stimulating the activity of antioxidant and detoxification enzymes (Bacou et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2018). In this study, dietary protease supplementation significantly upregulated hepatic Nrf2 expression in broiler chicks. This finding suggests a potential role of protease in enhancing the antioxidant defense system through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway. Several studies have demonstrated that Nrf2 activation promotes the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes, neutralizing free radicals and preventing oxidative damage to cells (Tarek et al., 2023). The improvement in antioxidant status observed in the present study may therefore be associated with the elevated hepatic Nrf2 expression. Soybean meal, a major protein source in poultry diets, contains antinutritional factors such as glycinin and β-conglycinin Kalmendal and Tauson (2012). Although the adverse effects of these compounds on Nrf2 signaling have been reported in aquatic species (Duan et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2015), their influence in poultry remains unclear. This study proposes that protease supplementation may enhance Nrf2 gene expression by mitigating the antinutritional effects of soybean meal components. The immune function remained unaffected by dietary supplementation of protease, lipase, or their interaction. The NF-κB signaling pathway is recognized as a major regulator of immune and inflammatory gene expression (Xing et al., 2024); however, our study showed that hepatic NF-κB gene expression remained unaffected by the experimental treatments.

Conclusion

Overall, protease supplementation in the starter diet reduced FI and FCR during days 1–10 and decreased the relative weights of the small intestine and pancreas, while enhancing ileal absorptive capacity, plasma total antioxidant capacity, and hepatic Nrf2 gene expression. Lipase supplementation also improved BW gain and feed efficiency and increased antioxidant capacity in plasma and liver during the starter period. However, the combined supplementation of protease and lipase did not result in additive or synergistic effects on FCR, yolk residue utilization, or oxidative status, indicating potential interactions between these enzymes. A limitation of the present study is that enzyme supplementation was restricted to the starter phase, which may have constrained the manifestation of longer-term effects. Therefore, further studies are recommended to investigate the optimal strategies and mechanisms of simultaneous enzyme supplementation in starter diets and their potential impacts on overall broiler performance.
