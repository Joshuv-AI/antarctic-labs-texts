High-precision non-destructive blade surface inspection via self learning transformer networks
Authors: Priyadharshini Kannusamy, D. Gayathri, S. Mirdula, Ramasubramanian Bhoopalan, D. Manikandan, Krishnaraj Ramaswamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate identification of defects in Jet Engine Turbine and compressor blade surface plays a vital role in ensuring engine efficiency, safety and secure life. Traditional detection techniques were manual and consumed more time, error prone and need of specialized experts. To address these challenges, this work proposes an automated defect detection system using Swin Transformer Based Deep learning model. High resolution blade surface images were captured, pre-processed and augmented to improve robustness with varying surface condition. The novelty of the proposed work is that it adapts the Swin Transformer architecture to the specific challenges of turbine and compressor blade surfaces and it can detect micro-scale defects with high fidelity, by detecting both local and global features. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed Swin transformer model produces high detection performance compared to the conventional CNN model with an accuracy of 98.4%, precision of 97.9%, recall of 98.7%, F1-score of 98.3% and mean Average Precision (mAP) of 97.6% on a dataset consisting of 172 high-resolution turbine and compressor blade images. The performance of the proposed method indicate that Swin Transformer model is an efficient tool for Non-Destructive Inspection of jet engine turbine and compressor blade surface which can be integrated into automated maintenance systems for better reliability and minimized operational risks.

Introduction

Jet engines are critical parts of today’s flown vehicles and they work under harsh thermal, mechanical and aerodynamic loads. The blades of the turbines and compressors are among the most prone components in these engines as they have to face high temperatures, pressure variations and corrosive atmosphere. In order to protect these parts and ensure high reliability, the blade surfaces are coated with advanced surface coatings. These coatings act as thermal insulator, corrosion inhibitor and wear resistant on the blades for an extended life of service, in turn, enhancing engine performance1,2.

In practical terms long-term exposure to severe operational environments may cause the degradation of these coatings with time. Typical surface coating failures are micro-cracking, delamination, pitting, erosion, metallic and corrosion spots as well as spalling of the coated material. Undetected, defects such as these can severely compromise the structural integrity of the blade elements with corresponding reduction in aerodynamic efficiency and increase in fuel burn and even catastrophic engine failure3–5. Early and precise assessment of such defects is then necessary for safety of flight, as well as for optimizing the maintenance plans.

Conventional Non-Destructive Inspection (NDI) methods including visual examination, ultrasonic inspection and thermography are widely employed in the aerospace industries. Although these approaches are efficient, they have notable drawbacks such as dependency on human expertise, time intensive inspections and difficulties in identifying micro level surface defects6,7. Particularly, manual visual inspections are prone to human error, highly when dealing with complex geometries and minute surface anomalies8.

In recent days, Computer Vision and Deep learning play a vital role for automated detection of defects. Particularly, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have proven a good performance in numerous industrial applications9 with a limitation in understanding global context and modelling long range dependencies. It also fails to recognize subtle and irregular defect patterns mainly on curved blade surfaces.

Planar industrial surfaces like PCB boards, steel strips, or generic manufacturing flaws are the main focus of earlier CNN-based and ViT-based defect-detection techniques. The intricate curvature, reflective coatings, and uneven illumination characteristics of aerospace blades are absent from these surfaces. In contrast, to guarantee robustness under these domain-specific circumstances, our model incorporates a customized preprocessing pipeline and defect-specific augmentation. Recent transformer-based architectures have demonstrated remarkable success in analogous fine-grained defect detection tasks across domains, such as medical imaging and materials analysis, where subtle anomaly identification is critical10,11.

Existing ViT-based models operate on global tokens and lack the locality-aware hierarchical representation needed to detect very small, spatially discontinuous anomalies present on curved blade surfaces.

To overcome these challenges, the authors introduced a Hierarchical Vision Transformer named Swin Transformer, as an effective alternative solution. This model utilizes a shifted window based self-attention mechanism which extracts both local and global features effectively with high computational efficiency12. This mechanism helps for high resolution defect detection in aerospace components.

Related Work

Thermal Barrier Coatings (TBCs) are widely applied to compressor blades and turbines in jet engines to protect them from severe thermal, chemical and mechanical stresses. These coatings significantly enhance engine efficiency and durability by providing thermal insulation and resistance to oxidation1,2. However, with prolonged service conditions, TBCs undergo gradual degradation because of thermal cycling, foreign particle erosion and oxidation, resulting in surface anomalies like micro cracks, delamination, pitting and corrosion3,4. Detecting these defects at the beginning stage is crucial for maintaining engine reliability and advert critical failures.

Conventional Non-Destructive Inspection (NDI) methods including thermographic, visual inspection, borescope imaging and ultrasonic testing, have been the industry standard in the aerospace industry6,7. While effective in identifying macroscopic defects, these methods are to be time consuming, heavily dependent on skilled inspectors, and may be inadequate to reliably detect micro scale coating damage on complex blade geometries13. These challenges have motivated research into automated, image-based defect detection approaches.

Recently in Computer Vision (CV) and Deep Learning (DL) methods, particularly convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have gained prominence in surface defect detection. Models like Mask R – CNN and YOLO have been utilized successfully for inspecting turbine blades and wind turbine blades, showing enhanced accuracy and faster analysis times compared to traditional manual methods. These CNN based methods are good at local feature extraction, but often limited in modeling global contextual relationships, which are important for detecting subtle coating degradation patterns. Convolutional neural networks have been extensively studied recently for automated turbine and compressor blade inspection. Zhang et al.14 achieved better segmentation accuracy by proposing an improved Mask R-CNN framework for wind turbine blade defect detection. Zubayer et al.15 employed YOLOv8 for jet engine blade coating inspection and reported real time detection performance. Similar CNN based techniques have shown promise in the detection of coating anomalies, PCB inspection, and steel strip flaws. However, CNN models primarily rely on local receptive fields and often struggle to capture long range contextual dependencies, which are essential for identifying subtle coating degradations distributed across complex blade geometries.

To address these limitations, Vision Transformer (ViT) models have emerged and gained attention in defect identification research. The Swin Transformer has achieved state-of-art performance across various computer vision applications12,16. By integrating local and global feature frameworks, Swin Transformers are highly effective for identifying multi scale and irregular defect patterns.

Many researchers have looked into self-attention mechanisms for fine grained defect detection since the introduction of vision transformers17. Liu et al. introduced the Swin Transformer with shifted window attention, demonstrating superior performance in multi scale visual recognition tasks. Swin based architectures have been used in recent research for cylinder wear analysis, steel strip defect detection, and PCB inspection18, demonstrating improved sensitivity to small and irregular anomalies. Transformer models are especially useful for identifying micro defects because they successfully combine local texture patterns with global contextual relationships. However, domain specific modifications for curved reflective surfaces have not been thoroughly investigated, and the use of transformer-based architectures for aerospace blade coating inspection is still restricted.

In summary, previous studies established a distinct progression from conventional NDI techniques to CNN based automated methods, and more recently, to Transformer based models for defect identification. From the above review, it is evident that although CNN based methods have achieved notable success in blade defect detection, their limited capability in modeling long range dependencies restricts accurate identification of micro scale coating degradations. The Novelty of the proposed work lies in developing a Swin Transformer based automated blade defect detection model for an aerospace system which is capable of detecting microscale damages with high accuracy and minimum inspection time suitable for real time deployment, not explored in earlier CNN based approaches.

Proposed methodology

The objective of the proposed system is to automatically identify and localize defects on the coating of turbine and compressor blades based on a transformer based self-attention deep learning architecture of Swin Transformer. The methodology is composed of five main phases: (1) Image acquisition and dataset preparation, (2) Pre-processing and augmentation, (3) Model architecture design, (4) Training and optimization, and (5) Evaluation and performance measurement. A. Image acquisition and dataset preparation

High resolution pictures of turbine and compressor blade surfaces were obtained from several sources such as onsite laboratory inspection, borescope images and publicly available datasets19. The dataset used in this study consists of 172 high-resolution turbine and compressor blade images, The defect size encountered in practice are micro-cracks ranging from 0.12 to 3.5 mm, pitting from 0.3 to 4 mm, inclusions/porosity from 0.2 to 2 mm, and coating delamination spanning 2–25 mm, covering both micro and macro level anomalies. Each image was manually annotated by domain specialists in terms of bounding boxes and segmentation masks for accurate localization of surface defects. Then the data set was divided into three parts with 70% of images for training the model, 20% for sampling validation and 10% kept for testing without adding additional defect types.

B. Pre-processing and data augmentation

Turbine and compressor blade surfaces are difficult to image because they have non-planar geometry, coated with reflective coatings, and the illumination is not uniform. A structured pre-processing pipeline was implemented on acquired images in order to achieve reliable defect detection. Illumination normalization using adaptive histogram equalization was firstly conducted to remove uneven illumination, and the bilateral filter was then adopted to suppress high frequency sensor noise while preserving defect edge information19. Successively, these images in the RGB plane are converted to Lab Color Plane and Optimized for subtle surface anomalies like micro level cracks, pitting as well as delamination. To enhance the diversity and robustness of the training dataset, a continuous series of augmentation procedures were followed by applying rotation, scaling, flipping, brightness jittering and synthetic defect overlaying which is mainly for underrepresented defect types in the training phase. This approach aligns with data-driven optimization strategies employed in other complex engineering domains to handle material variability and improve model generalization20. As a result of this enhancement procedure, the generalization of the model to different surface condition, defect size get improved which in turn suppress its memorization capacity and applicability on real samples.

C. Swin transformer-based architecture and model training

The uniqueness of the detection part is mainly the Swin Transformer, which has hierarchical representation learning and efficient self-attention. Compared with linear structured Probe CNNs, which struggle in handling both local details and global context, through the design of shifted-window MHSA, Swin Transformer is able to represent fine-grained surface defect patterns as well as their larger structural relationships and its architecture is shown in Fig. 2. The input images are divided into 4 × 4 patches, and then embedded in a fixed-dimensional latent space21. The embedding’s fly through the series of stacked Swin Transformer blocks that add multi-scale feature hierarchies successively. The cross-region attention mechanism overcomes this by using shifted windows, which increase the sensitivity to discontinuous or irregular defects i.e., pitting and coating spallation. A feature pyramid network (FPN) is constructed on the top of Swin backbone to deal with the variable defect sizes. In the detection stage, a hybrid detection head is utilized to learn both detecting class and predicting boxes in region-based object detectors, enabling the accurate localization and categorization of defects.

Architecture of Swin Transformer.

The training was performed in a supervised mode and the training set of blade surfaces were image-culled from an annotated dataset. The model was trained with AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ and a cosine annealing schedule for consistent convergence. A batch size of 8 was employed during 100 epochs and early stopping to avoid overfitting. The loss function was then constructed as a combination of cross-entropy loss, Smooth L1 for bounding box regression and focal loss to balance class imbalance with respect to the small classes. To minimize the computational cost, we used data parallelism and mixed-precision training. Combining the efficient attention mechanism of Swin Transformer and optimized training strategy, the model showed the robust convergence, strong detection performance and rapid inference speed for operational applications.

D. Deployment considerations

The proposed system will allow for effortless integration into the automated online maintenance process of turbine engines. After training, the model can be deployed to edge devices or inspection stations with GPUs, interfacing directly with high-resolution borescope cameras or robotic arms. It takes 38 ms per image to obtain real-time inference, which allows on-the-fly monitoring of turbines and compressor blades for maintenance purposes without blocking in-service inspections. The Deployment Pipeline adds a series of continuous defect detection and location, image acquisition automation, real-time analysis of defects, notification scheduling for maintenance based on severity and type of observed defects. This helps the maintenance engineers in identifying critical defects, reduces time to inspect for damage, and reduces need for manual visual inspection. What’s more, the modular architecture of the system allows for future enhancements such as coupling with digital twins and predictive maintenance solutions to create condition-based monitoring and maximize engine life-cycle management. Similar AI-enhanced system integration approaches have proven effective for optimizing performance in complex energy and infrastructure systems23.

B. Implementation details

The training and implementation are done with Hierarchical Swin Transformer. The Adam optimizer is used and the suitable β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999 values are chosen for the faster convergence with low noise. To avoid the overfitting issues the learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁵ is chosen based on experiments for stable convergence. The model is trained for 20 epochs with a batch size of 64. In order to minimize the local minima annealing rate is scheduled and also 1e-4, weight decay is applied for multi-layer perceptrons for regularization. The hardware used is Tesla T4 GPU,16 GB VRAM for accessing the input images with high resolution and window-based attention mechanism is used and the real time inference is kept as (~ 38 ms per image) for fast computation. For the software framework the data sets are taken from Roboflow software tool. The python 3.12 IDE along with Pytorch is used for processing purposes. The coding is done in OpenCV for image processing. The environment to run is Google colab with CUDA acceleration.

C. Classification metrics

The proposed Swin Transformer model was evaluated on a dataset of turbine and compressor blade surface images consisting of four defect types: Dent, Crack, Porosity, and Inclusion. The model was evaluated on the basis of Image-level metrics with Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, mAP, Localization metrics with IoU per defect class, Confidence metrics with Min, Max, Mean, Standard Deviation of predicted class probabilities and Timing metrics with Average inference time per image (ms) as the parameters24.

For the image level metrics, accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and mean Average Precision (mAP) are calculated and the output obtained for Swin Transformer are shown in Fig. 3 in comparison to conventional CNN models.

The Swin Transformer achieved 98.4% accuracy, 97.9% precision, 98.7% recall, 98.3% F1-score, and 97.6% mAP, outperforming all baseline CNN models (VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, DenseNet121) and Vision Transformer (ViT).

This noteworthy gain is significant rather than gradual, suggesting that the architectural distinctions between transformer-based models and convolutional networks are crucial for improving performance on this task. The Swin Transformer achieves a value of 98.7% in the recall parameter, which shows the biggest gain. This suggests that the model catches subtle and scattered information in the image that CNN architectures frequently overlook, as it is highly effective in identifying almost all true positive cases. The self-attention mechanism’s capacity to comprehend global contextual relationships across several visual regions rather than concentrating only on local textures is reflected in the high recall. In the same vein, the precision value of 97.9% shows that the Swin model keeps a very low false rate. This suggests that the classifier can produce more accurate predictions because the learnt feature representations create distinct decision boundaries in the feature space.

The model achieves consistent and impartial classification performance, as seen by the better F1-score of 98.3% that results from the balanced improvement in both precision and recall. The Swin Transformer’s overall robustness is further validated by the accuracy improvement to 98.4%. The Swin Transformer uses hierarchical self-attention with shifted window techniques to facilitate effective interaction between distant image regions, in contrast to CNN models that progressively improve with higher depth but remain limited by convolutional locality. This enables the model to acquire spatial linkages and global structural patterns that are essential for this specific visual task.

Our research findings show that the Swin Transformer’s attention-based hierarchical representation learning successfully gets beyond convolutional networks’ location restrictions. The results show compelling empirical evidence that transformer-based vision models provide better spatial dependency modeling and enhanced feature representation through global context awareness.

The individual mean average precision (mAP) is calculated for the four defects namely Cracks, Porosity, Inclusion and Dent. And the values obtained are plotted in Fig. 4.

The suggested Swin Transformer’s superiority over traditional CNN designs is further supported by the class-wise mean Average Precision (mAP) analysis. Although CNN models like VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, and DenseNet121 show progressive improvement as architectural depth increases, their performance is still limited across all defect categories. The Swin Transformer, on the other hand, continuously attains the greatest mAP in each class, demonstrating its capacity to generalize feature learning across a variety of fault patterns.

ResNet50 performs the best among CNN models in the Dent category, with mAP values roughly falling between 0.91 and 0.94. Nevertheless, with a mAP of 0.980, the Swin Transformer much exceeds this range. This significant improvement suggests that dent-related features contain more extensive structural deformations that are needed for global spatial comprehension, which CNNs are unable to adequately explain because of their limited convolutional operations.

A similar pattern is seen in the Crack category. As the architecture deepens, CNN-based models exhibit a moderate gain in performance, which reaches approximately 0.935 with ResNet50. The Swin Transformer, however, attains a far higher mAP of 0.975. Usually, cracks create lengthy, asymmetrical patterns that cover several areas of an image. Because CNNs’ receptive fields grow slowly and rely on pooling layers, they have trouble capturing such distributed patterns. In contrast, the Swin Transformer’s self-attention mechanism enables direct contact between distant regions, improving crack pattern identification.

By simulating long-range relationships that CNNs are naturally unable to handle, Swin’s hierarchical self-attention successfully catches these scattered patterns. The distinction is even more noticeable when it comes to inclusion. mAP values below 0.95 are shown by CNN and even ViT models, suggesting that it is challenging to reliably identify inclusion faults. However, the Swin Transformer achieves a mAP of 0.974. Subtle changes ingrained in the material structure are frequently included in inclusion faults, necessitating the extraction of local details as well as global contextual awareness. This dual capability is made possible by Swin’s shifting window technique, which enables the model to focus on specific details while keeping an eye on the wider structural environment.

Overall, the class-wise mAP results show unequivocally that the Swin Transformer’s performance benefit is not restricted to any one category but rather is constant across all fault kinds. Strong generalization abilities and robust feature representation learning are shown by this consistency. Because attention-based hierarchical transformers bypass the locality constraint and enable extensive global feature interaction, the results empirically demonstrate that they are superior to convolutional architectures for identifying complicated, geographically dispersed fault patterns.

Conclusion

A deep learning architecture based on Swin Transformers was used to create an automated flaw detection framework for jet engine turbine and compressor blade surfaces. By efficiently capturing both local micro scale flaws and global coating degradation patterns, the suggested system was created to get over the drawbacks of human inspection and traditional CNN based methods. A hierarchical shifted-window self-attention model was trained for multi-class defect identification and localization after high resolution blade surface images were obtained, pre-processed, and enhanced. The suggested method outperformed traditional CNN models with an overall accuracy of 98.4%, precision of 97.9%, recall of 98.7%, F1-score of 98.3%, and mAP of 97.6%, according to a thorough experimental evaluation. The efficacy and resilience of the suggested framework for high-precision non destructive aerospace blade surface inspection were validated by these findings.

To further improve the suggested model’s capacity for generalization, future studies should concentrate on enlarging the dataset to cover a greater range of blade materials, coating kinds, operating conditions, and unusual fault categories. To increase the robustness of defect characterization, it is advised to combine optical images with multimodal inspection data, such as thermography, ultrasonic signals, and eddy current measurements. To enable real time deployment on edge devices and embedded inspection platforms, model compression and lightweight transformer variations should be investigated. To evaluate long term dependability and operating performance, thorough validation on in service engines and large scale industrial inspection scenarios is also required. Finally, condition based monitoring and proactive maintenance planning can be made possible by combining the suggested framework with digital twin models and predictive maintenance systems, which will maximize engine availability, safety, and lifecycle efficiency.
