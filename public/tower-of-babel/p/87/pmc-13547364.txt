An integrated global resource of wetland microbiomes linking environmental metadata, community profiles, and genome-resolved metabolic traits
Authors: Yuxiang Wei, Jing Xiao, Junpan He, Kaihang Zhang, Chenchao Xu, Naifang Zhang, Lei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWetlands are biogeochemical hotspots pivotal to global carbon and nutrient cycling, yet genome-resolved studies across diverse wetland types remain limited. To address this, we constructed a global wetland metagenomic dataset, integrating environmental metadata, community profiles, and genome-resolved metabolic traits. This dataset comprises 1,962 samples—including 129 newly sequenced field-collected samples—from lakes, rivers, paddies, marshes, and coastal wetlands, spanning water, soil, and sediment habitats. We generated comprehensive taxonomic profiles for all 1,962 samples, and used 251 samples to reconstruct 5,704 sample-specific metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). These MAGs were subsequently dereplicated to establish a normalized, non-redundant catalog of 4,164 representative genomes. We further mapped gene repertoires to 549 KEGG modules to decode the metabolic potential of all 5,704 MAGs. This dataset depicts an overview of microbial genomic diversity across global wetlands and provides a comprehensive resource for understanding the metabolic capabilities, ecology, and evolution of wetland microbiomes.

Background & Summary

Wetlands, as unique ecosystems formed through land-water interactions, are an integral part of terrestrial ecosystems, covering approximately 6.4% of the Earth’s land surface1. Broadly defined, wetlands encompass both natural (e.g. shallow lakes, reservoirs, swamps, marshes and peatlands) and artificial wetlands (e.g. ponds and paddy fields)2. Wetland ecosystems not only provide critical ecological services but also play key roles in global biogeochemical cycling and climate regulation3,4. Global wetlands, particularly wetland soils or sediments, serve as significant carbon reservoirs5–9. For instance, peatlands alone store 400–500 Pg C, accounting for approximately one-fifth of the global soil organic carbon pool6. Emerging studies suggest that subsurface processes in wetland ecosystems are highly sensitive to global change factors such as warming and elevated CO23,4,10,11. However, while wetlands are a high-priority focus of global climate change research with numerous genome-resolved investigations12,13, a centralized and standardized global genomic catalog specifically dedicated to wetland microbiomes has been lacking. Compared to other ecosystems with well-established global datasets (e.g., the Tara Oceans project14 and the ocean MAG dataset15), this absence hinders macro-scale syntheses and cross-biome comparisons of wetland biological carbon sequestration potential.

Microorganisms play an indispensable role in driving elemental cycles in wetland ecosystems16,17. While insights from terrestrial ecosystems have demonstrated that microbial community structure and function are tightly coupled with environmental factors, a similar systematic understanding in wetlands has yet to be established18–20. Currently, wetland microbial datasets are fragmented, generated via heterogeneous sampling protocols, and rarely linked with high-resolution environmental metadata21,22. This lack of standardization limits cross-site comparisons and hinders our ability to link genetic potential to environmental constraints. Furthermore, most existing studies rely on marker-gene surveys or aggregate metagenomes, leaving a critical gap in genome-resolved insights regarding the specific metabolic traits and evolutionary adaptations of wetland microbiomes.

To address these challenges, we present the Global Wetland Microbiome (GWM) dataset, a comprehensive resource linking environmental metadata, community profiles, and genome-resolved metabolic traits. GWM integrates 1,962 samples across 957 distinct sampling sites, combining 129 newly sequenced field-collected samples with 1,833 public data retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and NGDC Genome Sequence Archive (GSA). We generated comprehensive taxonomic community profiles for all 1,962 samples. Furthermore, to achieve genome-resolved resolution, we reconstructed 5,704 medium- to high-quality, sample-specific metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from 251 samples, comprising 129 field-collected samples and 122 public samples, which collectively encompass all wetland types present in the entire dataset. We further comprehensively decoded the metabolic potential of these genomes by mapping gene-level annotations to a curated set of 549 KEGG modules.

We acknowledge that the current GWM dataset has certain limitations regarding geographic and categorical representation. The distribution of wetland types within the dataset is uneven. Though it encompasses ecosystems such as marshes, rivers, lakes and paddies, several other types such as bogs, peatlands, fens and mires are not included in this initial release. Geographically, a substantial portion of those samples were collected from wetlands located in China. Furthermore, part of the publicly sourced data outside of China is primarily derived from lakes, rather than a diverse spectrum of other wetland types. Regarding the “lake” category, it is important to clarify that our field-collected lake samples specifically targeted shallow lacustrine wetlands at the aquatic-terrestrial interface with water depths of less than 2 meters. For the publicly sourced lake data, we have explicitly categorized the samples into “sediment” and “water” to provide clearer environmental context, though we acknowledge that some public pelagic water samples might extend beyond the strict interface definition. Consequently, while the GWM establishes a global framework, we view the current database as an important foundation for future efforts to continuously expand and balance global coverage, rather than an exhaustively sampled final end-point. Future updates will prioritize the systematic integration of these underrepresented wetland types and broader geographical regions to further enhance the representativeness of the GWM.

Methods

Field sampling

A total of 129 field-collected samples, which constitutes a subset of the GWM dataset (Fig. 1b), were obtained from wetlands across China. They encompass multiple wetland ecosystems, including marshes (n = 21), rivers (n = 18), lakes (n = 36), paddy fields (n = 45), and coastal wetlands (n = 9). During sampling, geospatial metadata such as latitude, longitude, and altitude were recorded for each site.

Overview of the Global Wetland Microbiome (GWM) dataset. (a) Global geographic distribution of the 1,962 metagenomes included in this study. (b) Geographic distribution of the 129 field-collected samples across China. (c) The composition structure of the dataset, illustrating the relationships among data sources, wetland types, and habitats.

Riverine and lacustrine sites were sampled for sediments, while marsh, paddy field, and coastal wetland sites were sampled for soils. All samples were sealed in sterile plastic bags, placed immediately on ice packs in a cooler and transported to the laboratory. For DNA analysis, subsamples of approximately 20 g were frozen on dry ice and stored at −80 °C until extraction; the remaining samples was kept at 4 °C for other analyses.

Public data collection

We composed a dataset of 1,833 publicly available wetland metagenomes by searching curated repositories, including the NCBI Sequence Read Archive and the NGDC Genome Sequence Archive23–27. Candidate datasets were retrieved using targeted search queries combining “metagenome” with wetland-related keywords (e.g., “lake”, “river”, “marsh”, “paddy”; formal query example: “lake” [All Fields] AND “metagenome” [All Fields]). To ensure exact data traceability, all corresponding source databases, BioProject IDs and accession numbers are detailed in the file Source_Info.xlsx available on Zenodo28. All retrieved entries were subjected to manual inspection to ensure that the samples represented in situ communities without laboratory manipulation or experimental treatment (e.g., excluding microcosms or enrichment cultures) and provided sufficient metadata for downstream analyses. The public dataset comprised 1,095 lake, 223 marsh, 234 paddy, and 166 river metagenomes, along with 115 samples categorized as “unclassified” (i.e., wetland-associated samples lacking explicit wetland type information).

The field-collected samples (n = 129), in combination with the curated public data, yielded a final dataset of 1,962 samples (Fig. 1a). Based on sampling habitat, the dataset included 567 soil, 194 sediment and 1,201 water samples (Fig. 1c). Detailed metadata, including geographical location, environmental factors and physicochemical properties, sequencing information, are provided in Zenodo repository28.

Metagenomic community profiling and MAG reconstruction

For the 129 field-collected wetland samples, DNA was extracted from soil or sediment using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, The Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Quality-approved DNA samples were subjected to library preparation using the ALFA-SEQ DNA Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. The size distribution of the prepared libraries was evaluated on the Qsep400 High-Throughput Nucleic Acid-Protein Analysis System (Hangzhou Houze Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China), while library concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Finally, the DNA metagenome libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (Guangdong Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) using PE150 sequencing, yielding approximately 50 Gb of raw data per sample. Raw sequencing data from publicly available data were downloaded using the SRA Toolkit (for NCBI SRA) and wget (for NGDC GSA).

All raw reads, including both publicly sourced and field-collected datasets, were subject to quality control using Trimmomatic (v.0.39)29. Low-quality bases at the 5′ and 3′ ends were removed when their Phred scores fell below 3 (LEADING: 3; TRAILING: 3). A 4-nt sliding window (SLIDINGWINDOW: 4:15) was then applied, and reads were trimmed when the average Phred quality within the window was dropped below 15. Reads shorter than 36 nt after trimming were discarded (MINLEN: 36).

As illustrated in Fig. 2, after quality control, taxonomic profiling of each sample was performed using MetaPhlAn (v.4.2.2) with default parameters to obtain relative abundance profiles at species and higher taxonomic levels30. In parallel, trimmed reads were assembled into contiguous sequences (contigs) using MEGAHIT (v.1.2.9) with the “–presets meta-large” option, optimized for metagenomic datasets31. Contigs of each sample were binned into MAGs using the MetaWRAP (v.1.3.2) binning module, with the use of three complementary binning methods: MetaBAT2 (v2.12.1), MaxBin2 (v2.2.6), and CONCOCT (v1.0.0)32. The bin sets generated by the three algorithms were consolidated and optimized for each sample using the MetaWRAP bin_refinement module. Subsequently, the quality of the resulting MAGs was evaluated using CheckM2 (v.1.1.0)33. Taxonomic assignments for the obtained MAGs were performed using the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk v.2.5.2, classify_wf) with reference data version r22634. Furthermore, to precisely quantify the abundance of the recovered genomes without relying solely on public reference databases, the MAGs generated from the subset of 251 samples were first dereplicated using dRep (v3.6.2) to create a non-redundant genome catalogue35. To determine the relative abundances of these non-redundant genomes, the initial quality-controlled reads were mapped back to this catalogue using CoverM (v0.7.0)36.

Genomic novelty estimation

The genomic novelty of the dereplicated MAGs was assessed using the two following methods. First, the genome-wide average nucleotide identity (ANI) and the query alignment fraction (AF) were calculated between these dereplicated MAGs and the reference genomes in the GTDB (version r226; comprising 143,614 species representative genomes) using skani v0.3.137. Genomes exhibiting an ANI below 95%, an AF below 65%, or a complete absence of alignment hits were designated as putative novel species. Among the 4,164 queried MAGs, 3,671 (~88.16%) lack species-level matches in the GTDB database.

Second, the genomic novelty was evaluated based on the taxonomic assignment provided by GTDB-Tk. In the GTDB-Tk pipeline, species-level classifications are determined through a comprehensive assessment of ANI values and phylogenetic placement. Therefore, any recovered genome lacking a definitive species epithet in the results was categorized as a putative novel species. Based on the GTDB-Tk annotation, the 4,164 genomes encompass 3,639 putative novel species.

Metabolic potential inference

Protein-coding sequences were predicted for each MAG using Prodigal (v.2.6.3)38. Functional annotation was subsequently performed using eggNOG-mapper (v.2.1.13), yielding KEGG Orthology (KO) identifiers, Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers, and functional descriptions39–41. To systematically assess specific metabolic capabilities of each MAG, the annotated genes were mapped to a curated set of 549 KEGG modules (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/module.html). These modules were organized into 11 major categories and 45 sub-categories based on the standard KEGG functional hierarchy (Fig. 3a,b), covering a diverse array of essential biological pathways, including carbohydrate and energy metabolism, lipid and amino acid biosynthesis, as well as environmental information processing.

Overview of metabolic potential inference. (a) Distribution of the 549 curated KEGG modules across 11 major functional categories. (b) Hierarchical organization of all metabolic modules, comprising 11 major categories and 45 sub-categories.

Module completeness was calculated by parsing the logical expressions defined in the KEGG syntax. Within each logical expression, a space or a plus sign, representing a connection in the pathway or the molecular complex, is treated as an AND operator and a comma, used for alternatives, is treated as an OR operator. A minus sign designates an optional item in the complex42,43. For the detailed completeness mapping and visualization of all 549 modules across each MAG, please refer to the “Usage Notes” section.

Data Records

The raw metagenomic reads and the generated MAGs have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject PRJNA143016444,45. All other data pertaining to the present study are available at the Zenodo repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20263536)28, encompassing metadata for both samples and MAGs, taxonomic community profiles, genome sequences, and functional annotations.

To ensure rigorous data management and traceability, a standardized unique identifier system was implemented within the GWM dataset. All wetland samples are designated with unique internal identifiers prefixed with “SMP” (e.g., SMP0001), while the metagenome-assembled genomes are identified by IDs prefixed with “MAG” (e.g., MAG00001). These identifiers serve as the primary keys linking metadata, genomic sequences, and functional annotations across the platform.

Technical Validation

Quality assessment of the trimmed metagenomic data was performed using FastQC (v0.12.1) and MultiQC (v1.5.0). All samples were maintained at a Q30 threshold across all positions, ensuring >99.9% base-calling accuracy, with adapter sequences effectively removed, thereby providing high-quality data for downstream MAG analysis.

The completeness and contamination of the reconstructed MAGs were systematically evaluated using CheckM2 (v.1.1.0). To rigorously filter for high-quality genomic bins, a consolidated Quality Score (QS) was calculated for each MAG using the formula QS = Completeness - 5 × Contamination. Only MAGs achieving a QS > 50 were deemed sufficiently quality and retained in the final dataset (Fig. 4)46–48. Furthermore, to align with the Minimum Information about a Metagenome-Assembled Genome (MiMAG) standards, we systematically categorized these retained MAGs49. We employed Barrnap (v0.9.0) to predict the 5S, 16S, and 23S rRNA genes, and tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.12) to identify tRNA sequences50. By integrating these genomic feature counts with the aforementioned completeness and contamination metrics, the final MAGs were robustly classified into High-quality (n = 187) and Medium-quality (n = 5,517) tiers according to the MiMAG guidelines.
