Identifying populations with faster cognitive decline using blood‐based biomarkers
Authors: James Russell Pike, Yongmei Liu, Theresa Chisolm, Jennifer Deal, Jingzhong Ding, Rebecca F. Gottesman, Alison Huang, Timothy M. Hughes, Kurt Lohman, Mason McCray, Thomas H. Mosley, Anh Tram Nguyen, Priya Palta, Nicholas Reed, Kevin J. Sullivan, Bharat Thyagarajan, Keenan A. Walker, Josef Coresh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIdentifying individuals who undergo cognitive decline is vital to the success of prevention trials that aim to slow cognitive decline. Yet, the benefits of using blood‐based biomarkers of neurodegeneration, as well as amyloid and tau, to enrich population‐based prevention trials have not been quantified.METHODSThe association of thresholds of Quanterix single molecule array (SiMoA) assays with subsequent change in cognition was estimated in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities cohort (N = 1826) using linear mixed effects models, validated in the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis cohort (N = 383), and extended to Alamar Nucleic acid Linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA) assays in the Aging and Cognitive Health Evaluation in Elders cohort (N = 552).RESULTSElevated plasma biomarker levels identified dementia‐free older adults with faster cognitive decline. By selecting participants with Quanterix SiMoA measurements of neurofilament light > 30.65 pg/mL, the sample size needed to detect a 33% reduction in cognitive decline in a clinical trial decreased by 57%.DISCUSSIONClinical trials can use plasma biomarkers as a screening tool to increase statistical power.

INTRODUCTION

Over the last three decades, the number of older adults aged ≥ 65 years with Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD) increased from 18.7 million to 49 million. 1 Addressing this escalating public health challenge requires the implementation of randomized controlled trials that test pharmacological 2 , 3 and non‐pharmacological 4 , 5 , 6 interventions designed to reduce late‐life cognitive decline. A critical factor that often complicates such trials is the heterogeneity of cognitive decline in older adults who are initially free of dementia. 7 In trials with overrepresentation of healthy volunteers who show little or no cognitive decline, the overall progression rate may be closer to the null and the effect of a treatment designed to reduce cognitive decline may become too small to detect. Conversely, if trial participants have already undergone cognitive decline there may be minimal opportunity for improvement.

Enrichment strategies that identify dementia‐free individuals likely to undergo faster cognitive decline constitute one method that may improve randomized trials. 8 Prior studies have used cognitive tests 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 and positron emission tomography (PET) scans 2 , 3 to enrich AD/ADRD trial populations. However, cognitive tests from a single time point often fail to produce control groups who experience significant cognitive decline 4 , 5 , 6 and PET scans are costly and burdensome. A viable alternative is blood‐based biomarkers. Obtaining blood samples is safe, inexpensive, and minimally burdensome. Moreover, faster cognitive decline has been observed in older adults with abnormal levels of glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP), 9 , 10 , 11 neurofilament light chain (NfL), 10 phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)181, 11 p‐tau217, 11 and amyloid beta (Aβ)42 to Aβ40 (Aβ42:Aβ40). 9 , 11

To inform future randomized trials, the current investigation examined whether distribution‐based thresholds of plasma biomarkers defined in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort 12 would identify dementia‐free older adults who undergo faster cognitive decline in the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort. 13 The utility of distribution‐based thresholds was also examined among participants enrolled in the Aging and Cognitive Health Evaluation in Elders (ACHIEVE) cohort. 5 Collectively, these examinations assessed the efficacy of plasma biomarkers as a screening tool in dementia prevention trials.

METHODS

Discovery sample: ARIC

Thresholds for plasma biomarkers were defined using the ARIC cohort. 12 ARIC recruited 15,792 predominantly Black and White participants from four US communities (Washington County, Maryland; Forsyth County, North Carolina; selected suburbs of Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Jackson, Mississippi) between 1987 and 1989. The baseline assessment was followed by three triennial assessments through 1998. The ARIC Neurocognitive Study (ARIC‐NCS) was initiated 15 years later at Visit 5 (2011–2013, N = 6538) and succeeded by Visit 6 (2016–2017, N = 4214), Visit 7 (2018–2019, N = 3589), Visit 8 (2020, N = 3226), and Visit 9 (2021–2022, N = 2105). The ARIC and ARIC‐NCS protocols were approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) at each participating institution. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their legal representative.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors reviewed publications describing the use of blood‐based biomarkers to enrich randomized controlled trials of interventions designed to reduce cognitive decline in dementia‐free older adults. A review was also conducted of the association between plasma biomarkers and cognitive decline in community‐dwelling older adults. Interpretation: A cross‐cohort analysis revealed that median thresholds of Quanterix single molecule array (SiMoA) assays defined in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities cohort identified participants in the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis cohort who underwent faster cognitive decline. During ARIC Visit 5, participants were screened for eligibility for a neuroimaging substudy (Figure 1). 14 Participants were eligible if they had no contraindications and previously participated in an ARIC brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study, 15 had evidence of cognitive impairment, or were part of a random sample of participants without cognitive impairment. Among 1977 participants who completed a valid MRI scan at Visit 5, a subset of 1829 participants had stored plasma samples that were analyzed using Quanterix assays. 16 Three of the samples produced invalid results, resulting in an analytic sample of 1826 initially dementia‐free participants with plasma samples obtained from 2011 to 2013 and cognitive assessments conducted between 2011 and 2022.

Flowchart of participants analyzed in each sample.

Validation sample: MESA

Thresholds identified in ARIC were applied to the MESA cohort. 13 MESA enrolled 6814 White, Black, Hispanic/Latino, and Chinese adults from six US communities (Baltimore, Maryland; Chicago, Illinois, Forsyth County, North Carolina; Los Angeles County, California; Northern Manhattan and the South Bronx, New York; and St. Paul, Minnesota) from 2000 to 2002. Five follow‐up assessments were conducted between 2002 and 2008 and succeeded by Exam 6 (2016–2018, N = 3303) and Exam 7 (2022–2024, N = 2276). The MESA protocol was approved by the IRBs at each institution and written informed consent was obtained for each participant.

At Exam 6, participants from select sites took part in a biomarker substudy (Figure 1). Among 2591 participants with stored plasma samples, 743 samples were analyzed using Quanterix assays. Within this subsample, 360 participants did not complete a follow‐up cognitive assessment. Consequently, the validation sample comprised 383 dementia‐free participants with plasma samples obtained from 2016 to 2018 and cognitive assessments conducted between 2016 and 2024.

Exploratory sample: ACHIEVE

Data from the ACHIEVE trial 5 were used to evaluate whether distribution‐based thresholds of an Alamar assay could be used to identify older adults at risk of faster cognitive decline. ACHIEVE is a parallel‐group, unblinded randomized controlled trial 5 that investigated the effects of a best‐practice hearing intervention versus a health education control on 3‐year change in cognition among community‐dwelling older adults (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03243422). Recruitment methodologies, eligibility criteria, and screening procedures have been reported. 17 The ACHIEVE protocol was approved by the IRBs at each institution and each participant provided written informed consent.

A total of 977 participants underwent 1:1 randomization between 2018 and 2019. At the 3‐year follow‐up assessment (2021–2022, N = 862), each participant randomized to the health education control was offered the hearing intervention and each participant randomized to the intervention was offered the control program. All participants were invited to complete additional annual follow‐up assessments as a cohort. Among participants who completed the 3‐year follow‐up (Figure 1), 571 had plasma samples that were analyzed using an Alamar assay. Nineteen samples generated invalid results, resulting in an analytic sample of 552 participants with plasma samples obtained from 2021 to 2022 and cognitive assessments conducted between 2021 and 2025.

Plasma biomarkers

Plasma samples were collected, stored, and analyzed in each cohort (Methods S1 in supporting information). ARIC and MESA used single molecule array technology (Simoa) Neurology 4‐Plex E (N4PE) from Quanterix. ACHIEVE used the Nucleic acid Linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA) central nervous system (CNS) panel 120 from Alamar Biosciences. All cohorts measured GFAP, NfL, p‐tau181, and Aβ42:Aβ40.

The 50th and 75th percentiles were defined in the ARIC cohort for GFAP (50th: 174.75, 75th: 237.82), NfL (50th: 22.12, 75th: 30.65), p‐tau181 (50th: 2.68, 75th: 3.77), and Aβ42/Aβ40 (50th: 0.059, 75th: 0.052). A rank‐based inverse normal transformation was applied to each biomarker and composite scores were calculated by averaging the z scores. Composite scores were generated for (1) GFAP and NfL, (2) GFAP and p‐tau181, (3) NfL and p‐tau181, (4) GFAP, NfL, and p‐tau181, and (5) GFAP, NfL, p‐tau181, and an inverted Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio. The 50th and 75th percentiles were determined for each composite score. Thresholds identified in the ARIC cohort were applied to the MESA cohort.

In the ACHIEVE cohort, NPQ units generated by the Alamar NULISA CNS kit were on a sample relative scale rather than an absolute scale. As a result, it was not possible to apply Quanterix thresholds defined in ARIC to Alamar assays in ACHIEVE. Instead, the process of defining sample‐specific percentile thresholds and composite scores conducted in ARIC was replicated in ACHIEVE.

The composite score of GFAP and NfL was selected as the primary measure of interest. Although prior evidence suggests that each plasma biomarker is associated with cognitive decline in older adults, 9 , 10 , 11 the choice of a specific biomarker or composite score for trial enrichment depends on the target of the intervention. Individuals with low levels of Aβ42:Aβ40 are likely to have amyloid plaque and may benefit from pharmacological anti‐amyloid therapies. 2 , 3 Similarly, individuals with high levels of p‐tau181 or p‐tau217 are likely to have tau tangles for which pharmacological treatments are currently being developed. 18 High levels of GFAP may be an indicator of neuroinflammation, 19 making it a reasonable option for pharmacological interventions that target astrocytes 20 as well as non‐pharmacological interventions that reduce neuroinflammation. 21 High levels of NfL are indicative of neurodegeneration, 22 making it an ideal choice for non‐specific lifestyle interventions. 4 , 5 , 6 For this investigation, the composite score of GFAP and NfL was chosen as the primary measure because its use as a tool for trial enrichment may be relevant to a wide range of interventions beyond those specific to AD pathways.

Cognition

In ARIC and ACHIEVE, cognition was assessed by the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE)] 23 and either a 10‐test cognitive battery administered in person or a 6‐test cognitive battery administered over the phone (Methods S1). A factor score model 24 was used to compute a composite measure of cognition. In MESA, cognition was assessed by a 3‐test battery (Methods S1) and a mean of z scores was computed.

Baseline measures

Trained interviewers asked participants about their date of birth, sex, race/ethnicity, and education. Date of birth was used to calculate age. The location of the field site where each participant was recruited was documented.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. 25 , 26 In ARIC and MESA, a creatinine–cystatin C 27 , 28 estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was generated. 29 In ACHIEVE, physician‐diagnosed kidney dysfunction was self‐reported.

Diabetes was defined as fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, non‐fasting glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, use of glucose‐lowering medication, or self‐reported physician diagnosis. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, use of anti‐hypertensive medication, or self‐reported physician diagnosis. In ARIC and MESA, total cholesterol was determined using enzymatic methods. 30 , 31 In ACHIEVE, physician‐diagnosed high cholesterol was self‐reported. Current use of cigarettes or alcohol was self‐reported. The presence of apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 alleles was ascertained by the TaqMan assay. 32 , 33 , 34

Analysis

Analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) and Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp). Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were generated to compare the analytic samples for ARIC (N = 1826), MESA (N = 383), and ACHIEVE (N = 552).

In the ARIC cohort, the 50th and 75th percentiles of age (50th: 76.48 years, 75th: 80.96 years) and an inverted MMSE score (50th: 28, 75th: 26) were identified. Point estimate and 95% confidence intervals of annualized cognitive change above specific thresholds of age, lower MMSE scores, plasma biomarkers, and composite scores of plasma biomarkers were estimated using linear mixed effects models. To mitigate bias caused by informative attrition, 35 multiple imputation by chained equations 36 with auxiliary variables 37 was used to impute measures of cognition among participants who were alive during each visit but did not complete an assessment (Methods S1). To reduce bias from selection into the neuroimaging substudy, 16 sampling weights were included in each model (Methods S1).

Linear mixed effects models estimated the association between thresholds of the composite score of GFAP and NfL and annualized cognitive change in MESA and ACHIEVE. In MESA, thresholds of Quanterix assays defined in ARIC were applied. In ACHIEVE, thresholds of the Alamar assay were defined based on sample‐specific percentiles. Heterogeneity in each sample was visualized by generating histograms of annualized cognitive change for (1) all participants, (2) participants in the bottom half of the composite score, (3) participants in the top half, and (4) participants in the top quartile.

In a sensitivity analysis, covariate‐adjusted biomarker levels were generated for each participant in each cohort. Separate linear regression models specified a biomarker or composite score as the outcome and select measures as covariates. The residuals were used to reclassify participants. For example, an 85‐year‐old participant with a NfL level of 23.00 pg/mL who was originally classified as above the median (> 22.12) might be reclassified as below the median after determining their age‐adjusted NfL is equivalent to 21.57 pg/mL. The first set of covariate‐adjusted biomarker levels accounted for the age of each participant. The second set mitigated the influence of dilution 38 and inadequate filtration 39 , 40 by adjusting for BMI and either eGFR (ARIC, MESA) or kidney dysfunction (ACHIEVE). The third set adjusted for cardiovascular risk factors including hypertension, diabetes, cigarette use, and either cholesterol in mg/dL (ARIC, MESA) or high cholesterol (ACHIEVE). The fourth set adjusted for all covariates. Linear mixed effects models estimated the association between discretized covariate‐adjusted biomarker levels and annualized cognitive change.

Power analyses were conducted to quantify the effect of selecting dementia‐free participants for a trial based on specific thresholds of the composite score averaging standardized GFAP and NfL. Power was calculated under the assumptions that (1) there would be three repeated assessments of cognition over 3 years, (2) the effect of the intervention would be determined from a linear mixed effects model, (3) the intervention would reduce the rate of cognitive decline by 33%, (4) the variability of the rate of cognitive decline in the linear mixed effects model would be 0.2, and (5) the variability of measurement error would be 0.1. These assumptions were based on parameter estimates from past trials. 2 , 3 , 5 The sample size required to reach 80% power with these assumptions was calculated using Optimal Design. 41 The same power calculations were performed using only NfL. Various scenarios were tested including different percentage reductions in cognitive decline and variability in the rate of cognitive decline.

RESULTS

Characteristics of ARIC, MESA, and ACHIEVE participants

The median (interquartile interval) follow‐up time for repeated cognitive assessments after the measurement of biomarker levels was 9.2 years (7.6, 9.9) in the ARIC discovery sample (N = 1826), 5.6 years (5.3, 5.9) in the MESA validation sample (N = 383), and 2.1 years (2.0, 2.2) in the ACHIEVE exploratory sample (N = 552). Compared to participants in ARIC (Table 1), MESA participants were younger (71.0 years vs. 76.8 years, p < 0.001) and less likely to be White (52.5% vs. 72.7%, p < 0.001). ACHIEVE participants were older (79.8 years vs. 76.8 years, p < 0.001) and more likely to be White (86.2% vs. 72.7%, p < 0.001) than ARIC participants.

Association of plasma biomarkers with cognitive change in ARIC

In ARIC, faster cognitive decline (Table 2) was observed among participants in the upper half of age and upper quartile of age. A similar but weaker trend was observed for lower MMSE scores. Each individual Quanterix plasma biomarker was associated with faster cognitive decline (Table 2, Figure 2, and Figure S1 in supporting information). The rates associated with thresholds of GFAP, NfL, and p‐tau181 were similar. The rate of cognitive decline was numerically fastest in the upper quartile of the composite score of four biomarkers (GFAP, NfL, p‐tau181, Aβ42/Aβ40). However, the difference between this rate and the rate in the upper quartile of the composite score of GFAP and NfL were not substantial.

Power analyses for ARIC, MESA, and ACHIEVE samples

Power analyses revealed the magnitude of the reduction in the sample size required for 80% power in a hypothetical trial in which participants were selected based on their composite score of GFAP and NfL (Figure 3). In MESA, the sample size decreased from 2241 in the full sample to 1106 and 968 using the top half and quartile thresholds, respectively. In ACHIEVE, the sample size decreased from > 20,000 in the full sample to 1232 and 575 using the top half and quartile. Similar patterns were observed when using NfL (Figure 4). The reduction in sample size was proportionally similar under different scenarios (Table S2 in supporting information), although the reduction in the total number of participants decreased as variability in the rate of cognitive decline and the percentage reduction in cognitive decline caused by the hypothetical intervention increased.

DISCUSSION

In this investigation of dementia‐free community‐dwelling older adults, the upper half and upper quartile thresholds of Quanterix assays defined in the ARIC cohort identified participants in the MESA cohort who underwent ≈ 2‐fold faster cognitive decline despite differences in cohort characteristics and measures of cognition. Similar percentile thresholds of Alamar assays were associated with faster decline among participants in the ACHIEVE cohort demonstrating the feasibility of trial enrichment using different biomarker platforms. Power analyses of MESA and ACHIEVE showed that selecting participants in the upper half of plasma biomarkers of neurodegeneration enabled a hypothetical trial to attain adequate power with half the sample size.

These findings offer several practical insights for trialists. The first insight is that GFAP, NfL, p‐tau, or Aβ42:Aβ40 may each serve as a tool for identifying dementia‐free older adults who undergo faster cognitive decline. Like prior studies, the association between plasma biomarkers and cognitive decline was weaker for Aβ42:Aβ40 42 , 43 , 44 and stronger for GFAP, 10 , 42 NfL, 43 , 44 and p‐tau. 43 However, the similarity between GFAP, NfL, and p‐tau suggests that the choice for a trial may depend on the specific mechanisms through which an intervention is hypothesized to operate. A pharmacological trial of anti‐amyloid 2 , 3 or anti‐tau 18 treatments might choose to enroll participants with elevated p‐tau. A therapy that specifically targets neuroinflammation 20 might use GFAP to screen participants. A non‐specific lifestyle intervention 4 , 5 , 6 with general neuroprotective effects might select eligible individuals based on NfL levels.

A second insight is the identification of specific thresholds of Quanterix assays that might be used to enrich dementia prevention trials. One prior study 45 showed that cognition declined by 0.070 standard deviation (SD) per year when GFAP was > 337 and 0.066 SD per year when NfL was > 36. In this study, cognition declined by 0.107 SD per year when GFAP was > 237.82 and 0.105 SD per year or when NfL was > 30.65. Although direct comparisons are hindered by differences between populations, thresholds, and measures of cognition, these two studies nonetheless provide a preliminary benchmark. Sensitivity analyses also revealed the choice trialists face when deciding whether to use unadjusted or covariate‐adjusted biomarker levels. Participants selected based on unadjusted levels often had faster rates of cognitive decline than those selected based on age‐adjusted levels. The former would likely provide greater power to detect an effect while the latter might lead to a sample with less etiologic heterogeneity. Similar logic applies to the decision to only use one biomarker versus a comprehensive profile that may include additional information such as cytokine profiles. 46 While additional information might improve the identification of individuals who experience faster cognitive decline, it might also diversify the causes of decline among participants. Ultimately, the best approach for a specific trial may be to conduct a pilot study or perform target trial emulation 47 power analyses using data available from such resources as the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center or UK Biobank.

A third insight confirmed by this investigation is that composite scores from multiple biomarkers outperform individual biomarkers but only modestly. This aligns with previous research 48 documenting greater explanatory accuracy when Aβ42:Aβ40, p‐tau181, and NfL were combined (R2 = 0.14) than when each individual biomarker was used separately (R2 = 0.09–0.11). The current findings also suggest there may be diminishing returns when non‐specific biomarkers (GFAP and NfL) are combined with biomarkers of AD pathology (p‐tau181 and Aβ42:Aβ40), supporting an enrichment strategy for lifestyle interventions 4 , 5 , 6 that focuses on reversible neurodegeneration biomarkers as a target for selection.

While these findings have practical utility, their direct applicability should be considered against several limitations. One limitation is that the tests used to assess cognition and the method for deriving a composite score in MESA differed from those used in ARIC and ACHIEVE (Methods S1). Consequently, differences in the annualized rate of cognitive change are not directly comparable. However, the consistent increase in power despite the difference in cognitive tests demonstrates the broad applicability of blood‐based biomarkers. Another limitation is that the frequency of cognitive assessments and length of time over which cognition was assessed differed for each cohort. These differences may explain the greater variability in the rate of cognitive change in ACHIEVE relative to MESA and ARIC (Figure 2). A third limitation is that thresholds for this investigation were defined using Quanterix assays, which prior research suggests may have less classification accuracy than newer assays. 49 Studies that test different assays and facilitate crosswalks between assays will allow further refinement.

Strengths of the current investigation include the analysis of demographically diverse samples of community‐dwelling older adults, 50 the consistency of findings across cohorts, and the actionable implications for future dementia prevention trials. Overall, our findings indicate that selecting dementia‐free participants with elevated plasma biomarker levels should result in a faster rate of cognitive decline and a markedly smaller sample size needed to reach prespecified power in trials aiming to slow cognitive decline. These results can inform the choice of biomarkers and thresholds depending on the hypothesized mechanism of action (e.g., anti‐amyloid vs. general neuroprotection) and the relative costs of screening and clinical trial conduct.
