A panel of microsatellite loci for studying parti-coloured bats
Authors: Z. Hałat, I. Ruczyńska, I. Ruczyński, M. Tokarska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe parti-coloured bat (Vespertilio murinus) is a widely distributed species across Eurasia, notable for its facultative, seasonally occurring male-only colonies, which are uncommon in the bat world. Males benefit from social warming and social foraging during spermatogenesis. However, little is known about the relatedness between individuals within these groups. Our aim was to create a panel of microsatellite loci for use in genetic studies of parti-coloured bats, as understanding the evolutionary mechanisms leading to male sociality cannot be done without using genetic techniques.FindingsOf 27 microsatellite loci that had previously been used for other species of the Vespertilionidae family, we selected 12 that provided reliable quality results with Vespertilio murinus samples. The number of alleles at each locus ranged from 3 to 40, and observed heterozygosity ranged from 0.002 to 0.589. Almost all markers showed a departure from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and presence of null alleles.ConclusionThe presented microsatellite panel will be a valuable tool for studying the genetic relatedness of male colonies of parti-coloured bats.

Findings

The widely distributed bat family Vespertilionidae (order Chiroptera) comprises more than 400 species, making it the third most species-rich family of mammals, after Muridae and Cricetidae from the order Rodentia [2]. In a species of this family, the parti-coloured bat Vespertilio murinus, males form short-term colonies during sperm production, which is unique behaviour pattern among bats in the temperate zone [23, 24]. Consequently, this bat species has been intensively studied to understand the mechanisms that promote evolution of social behaviours [7–9, 13–15]. The genetic background of this behaviour remains poorly understood, and there is currently limited knowledge in the literature about the relatedness of colony members. Understanding this topic would help explain the evolutionary mechanisms underlying male sociality in bats. Because in bats the degree to which kinship is reflected in social associations varies among species, genetic tools are required to assess the relatedness of group members [33]. A study examined the genetics of three colonies on the periphery of the parti-coloured bat range in Switzerland and Germany [25], but its results were based on only three microsatellite loci– this study provided some preliminary insight, but more markers are necessary in comprehensive genetic studies, concerning genetic variance, genetic distance between populations or individuals’ relatedness. Studies of relatedness in bats commonly use microsatellite panels comprising around a dozen loci (e.g. 11–16 loci; [6, 18, 26, 27]). The literature describes cross-species applications of microsatellite markers for many bat species, including Vespertilionidae members. Such cross-species amplification approaches concern may be of both: inter-species and inter-genera character [11, 17]. We selected some of these loci for our project. Our aim was to construct a microsatellite panel of a dozen or so informative primers useful for detecting allelic diversity in parti-coloured bats. Such a panel would enable comprehensive genetic studies comparing colonies from the central and peripheral areas could potentially verify sex-specific immigration rates into peripheral areas.

Samples were collected from female and male parti-coloured bats in Białowieża Forest (NE Poland; N = 341), the Bieszczady region (SE Poland; N = 73), the surroundings of Wrocław (SW Poland; N = 6) and Switzerland (N = 38). Three-millimetre wing membrane punches were collected from bats after capturing in mist nets and harp traps. Samples were then stored in 99% ethanol and frozen at −18 °C. We preliminarily chose 27 bat microsatellite loci from the literature that had already been used for studying closely related bats from the Vespertilioniidae family: Npla38, Npla66, WW6, PA127-2, L45, P20, PAU3, G2, A45, A34, 51, 59, G18, G13, B023, EF4, EF5, EF6, EF13, EF14, EF21, NLEIS2, NLEIS3, NLEIS5, NN18, P217, P219 [1, 3, 10, 16, 17, 20, 31, 32]. Markers were amplified individually using a PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reaction mixtures contained approximately 1 µl of template DNA in a total volume of 5.0 µl and were performed in a DNA Engine Dyad Peltier Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). The range of DNA concentration varied from 1.9 to 172.8 ng/µl with mean equaled 30 ng/µl. The concentration of primers was always 10 pmol/ml. The thermal conditions for amplifying markers during PCR were adjusted depending on the sequences of given pairs of primers. The amplified fragments were separated by electrophoresis using an ABI 3130XL Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems) with the internal size standard GeneScan 400HD ROX (Applied Biosystems). Based on the results of the electrophoresis, out of 27 potential primer pairs we selected 12 loci which were delivering most informative, unequivocal products for further analysis (Table 1). We scored amplification products using GeneMarker version 2.6.3 (SoftGenetics, State College, Pennsylvania, USA). We tested all microsatellite loci for evidence of null alleles and scored error due to stutter or large allele dropout using a Monte Carlo simulation of expected allele size differences implemented in MicroChecker version 2.2.3 [30]. Markers were tested for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium with GENEPOP version 4.7 [21, 22].

We calculated the numbers of alleles, and the observed and expected heterozygosities in the program GenAlEx 6.5 [19]. All 12 loci were polymorphic and the numbers of alleles per locus ranged from 3 to 40 (Table 1).

From the Białowieża, Bieszczady and Switzerland sites, all loci deviated from the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, except PA127-2 from Switzerland, which stayed within the equilibrium (GENEPOP). Probably, because of the small sample size collected from the Wrocław population, only WW6 and L45 deviated from the equilibrium, while estimates for EF4 and G18 could not be counted. All loci, except for P217, showed evidence for the presence of null alleles.

P20 primers resulted in two different, distinct allele ranges: from 81 bp to 120 bp with 18 alleles, and from 153 bp to 188 bp also with 18 alleles. Their linkage disequilibrium P-value was < 0.001 (GENEPOP); hence, we excluded them from the final panel, despite the results with and without P20 not differing significantly.

Overall genotyping success for the 12 loci was 93.73%, varying for different loci from 87.99 to 98.47% (Table 1). The genotyping success rates were calculated according to the equations:

Limitations

For the majority of the loci we analysed observed heterozygosity values (Ho) were lower than expected (He). This could be explained by various factors, such a inbreeding [12, 29], Wahlund effect [4, 5] etc., but may also be a result from the presence of null alleles [5]. It is practically impossible to determine inbreeding levels or the presence of the Wahlund effect without robust genetic analyses, which require reliable genetic markers. The optimal procedure would be de novo genomic sequencing (without a reference genome) and designing genus or even species-specific panel of microsatellite or SNP markers. But this approach is not always available. An alternative approach, which we adopted, is to adapt previously developed markers used for related species and validate their applicability. These may not be as effective and precise, as de novo-developed markers, which may result in weaker detection of some alleles (allelic drop-out or the presence of null alleles). This may lead to heterozygotes being scored as homozygotes and thus affect the estimates of Ho and He.

Bats from temperate zones are small animals, so we collected only tiny amounts of their tissues. As a result, the quantity of extracted DNA was usually very small, limiting the number of tests and repetitions in our analyses. That was the main reason why we could not re-design the primers to try to overcome null allele presence.
