Mapping outcomes in randomised radiology trials: Protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Benjamin Skov Kaas-Hansen, Christoph Felix Müller, Martin Lundsgaard Hansen, Mia Kolmos, Simona Gentile, Lea Marie Pehrson, Cecilie Juul Mørck Offersen, Silvia Ingala, Lars Birger Lönn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis scoping review aims to systematically map how outcomes are defined and operationalised in contemporary radiological randomised clinical trials (RCTs). The findings will inform a subsequent Delphi process to develop a core outcome set for clinical studies in radiology, including adaptive platform trials (APTs), conventional RCTs, and observational studies.Methods including end pointsThe scoping review will be conducted in accordance with PRISMA-P and PRISMA-ScR guidelines and will encompass articles published from 1 January 2023 in select high-impact general medical and radiological journals, using a search strategy developed and vetted by an independent information specialist. The search strategy yielded 4,202 hits on 7 April 2026. All steps, from screening through data extraction, will be done in duplicate by two independent authors. Extracted trial data will include number of trial sites, number of participants, sub-speciality, intervention type, and interventions under study. For each outcome in each trial, we will extract the name and type of the outcome, operationalisation/definition/tool used, assessment of temporality (e.g. truncation, censoring, repeated sampling), missingness proportion, missingness handling, effect quantification, and statistical analyses of effects (e.g. unadjusted or regression-model based). Data extraction will start with a 10-trial pilot phase for maximised data quality. Data synthesis will be descriptive without risk of bias evaluation.ImplicationsThis scoping review will map how outcomes are defined and operationalised in contemporary radiological RCTs and inform a Delphi process to identify core outcomes for APTs and RCTs, supporting the advancement of value-based radiology.

Introduction

Value-based radiology,1–4 alongside the rapid development of promising technologies–particularly AI-based–underscores a need for systematic evaluation of clinical utility and cost-effectiveness before implementation. Although diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) studies are widespread in radiology, their scope is limited to the first two levels of the technical efficacy hierarchy,5,6 they do not lend themselves easily to health-economic evaluation, 7 and technical performance is but one consideration before implementation of new technology in routine practice.8,9 Indeed, the randomised clinical trial (RCT) is the gold standard for ascertaining or refuting clinical effectiveness and the prime source for health-economical evaluations of cost-effectiveness. 10 Continuous re-evaluation of existing technologies is needed to optimise resource use, but multiple stand-alone RCTs are impractical due to the substantial and largely redundant efforts that result.

APTs aim to address these inefficiencies and are increasingly used across clinical disciplines as efficient frameworks for evidence generation.11–19 They comprise multiple domains, each akin to a sub-trial that compares several interventions within a shared infrastructure. Especially in the wake of the COVID19 pandemic, the interest in and uptake of the APT design have risen as it can accelerate evidence generation, compared to stand-alone RCTs, and encourages standardisation of many processes into lasting systems to minimise duplicate and redundant work with respect to, i.a., data entry, ethical approvals, and legal matters. In radiology, an example could be all patients in need of an acute computed tomography (CT) of the thorax and/or abdomen; two domains might, then, be conventional CT versus dual-energy CT versus photon-counting CT (PCCT) (three arms) and high versus low contrast-agent dose in PCCT (two arms). Standardised data entry and outcome metrics are key to the efficiency gains of APTs over multiple, stand-alone RCTs.

Such standardisation, however, requires some core outcomes appropriate for most, if not all, participants in an APT. 20 Even if stand-alone RCTs are conducted, choosing from a catalogue of core outcomes will facilitate meta-analyses that can help establish or rebut clinical utility. Patient-centred outcomes in radiology have received attention,21,22 and colleagues recently reviewed RCTs evaluating AI across disciplines. 9 To the best of our knowledge, however, no systematic general overview exists over outcomes used in RCTs in radiology. Radiology plays a central role in patient care, 23 yet outcome selection and operationalisation vary widely across studies, even for similar constructs. Systematically mapping these outcomes is a necessary first step toward developing a core outcome set.

Purpose

Thus, the scoping review detailed here will map definitions and operationalisations of outcomes in contemporary radiological RCTs. The results are intended to inform a subsequent Delphi process with the tentative goal of identifying a core outcome set for clinical studies in radiology – both APTs, RCTs, and observational. Based on clinical and academic experience, we expect that the results will be relevant also to colleagues beyond radiology as very few patient trajectories involve imaging only.

Materials and methods

This protocol was devised in accordance with PRISMA-P and PRISMA-ScR24,25; filled-out checklists are available in the supplementary materials. The protocol was accepted for publication before data collection and extraction.

Context and research questions

Inspired by previous works outside radiology,26,27 we aim to answer the following questions:• Which outcomes are used in contemporary RCTs in radiology?• How are the outcomes defined and prioritised?• How common are missing data, and how are they handled?• How do outcomes, that seek to measure the same thing, differ?• How are effects quantified and analysed statistically?

Search strategy and study eligibility

The search is multi-stranded 28 and will include RCTs published in 4 general and 43 specialised journals, as detailed in the supplement materials. The search strategy was developed with the help of and vetted by an independent information specialist at the Medical Library at Copenhagen University Hospital - Rigshospitalet. The list of specialised journals was compiled using the top-20 cited journals in radiology according to Google Scholar on 23 March 2026 29 and a list of journals from Radiopaedia.org. 30 Using MEDLINE-indexed journals from the latter, we will search only PubMed and restrict the results to publications from 1 January 2023 onward. This cutoff is arbitrary, but a marked number of included RCTs in a recent scoping review had started enrolment even a decade before that review’s cutoff 31 ; thus, we expect to capture trials that were conceived and carried out several years before our cutoff.

We will use the Cochrane Collaboration’s highly sensitive search filter for RCTs (ch. 4 in 32) with a small addition for added sensitivity; the full search string is given in the supplementary materials. The search string yielded 4,202 hits on 7 April 2026. The final search will be conducted and results frozen as close as possible to publication of this protocol, and the freeze date will be reported.

The unit of analysis will be RCTs. We will include any RCT in humans in which at least one arm requires involvement of a radiological department, and for which any results and/or protocol are reported in a publication captured by our search. For example, if the primary results were published in 2022, but an article with results of secondary analyses was published in 2024 and captured by our search, that RCT would be eligible, and we would add the primary report to the results. Of note, published protocols captured by our search will be included even if no results have been published. Thus, any RCT main publications cited by publications captured by our search will be included, even if not themselves captured; all reports pertaining to the same RCT will be assessed jointly to characterise each RCT as wholly as possible. We will not contact authors for information that is unavailable in published form.

Secondary studies merely using RCT data will not be eligible as our interest revolves around how intervention effects are defined, quantified, analysed, and compared. RCTs whose main publications were not written in English will be ineligible.

Outcomes

Due to the very explorative nature of this review, we forewent any prior definition of or criteria for relevant outcome types.

Study selection

We will use Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/) for study screening and selection. Each step in the selection process will be done in duplicate and independently; disagreements will be resolved by consensus, involving a third extractor if need be. After removal of duplicates, we will first screen studies for seeming eligibility using titles and abstracts. Seemingly eligible studies then undergo full-text screening for final eligibility assessment.

Data extraction

Data extraction will happen in duplicate by two independent authors, using an online extraction form. Two authors will run a 10-study pilot to test and, if needed, refine the extraction form before full data extraction.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis will be descriptive, using median (inter-quartile range) for numeric data and count (proportion) for categorical. Aggregates will be tabulated by intervention type and outcome type; grouping of both will be based on the extracted data. Being a scoping review, risk of bias evaluation will be foregone. 25

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is unnecessary due to the public nature of the data collected and synthesised. The results will be reported in an international peer-reviewed journal regardless of findings. Protocol amendments or deviations, if any, will be reported and motivated.

Discussion

This scoping review will elicit important aspects of definitions, operationalisation, and statistical analysis of outcomes in contemporary radiological RCTs. We expect lots of outcomes to be used, with much heterogeneity. This will be the first systematic overview of outcomes used in radiological RCTs and will form a strong basis for defining core outcomes to be used in future radiological research on clinical utility–be that in the form of APTs, RCTs, or observational studies.

Delivery of modern health care usually involves diagnostic and/or interventional radiology, which makes radiology a potent creator of clinical value but also a potential source of waste: if we can exclude acute pathology, for example, some patients can be discharged to outpatient follow up. That can spare both financial, environmental, and human resources–the latter being crucial for systems with shortage of, for example, nursing staff. On the flip side, the growing availability of also advanced radiological examinations and interventions can further encourage the practice of defensive medicine. Thus, the ever-increasing number of technologies with promising technical results necessitates lasting systems that facilitate the testing of such technologies ‘in the wild’ where purely technical performance does not necessarily translate into added clinical value.33,34

This scoping review will enjoy a number of strengths such as adherence to well-established guidelines for its design and reporting; prior publication of this protocol and statistical analysis plan prior to data collection and analysis; and the use of an inclusive search strategy covering both high-impact general and radiology-specific international, peer-reviewed journals. Further, we employ an unrestrictive definition of outcomes that includes, for example, health-economical outcome metrics – important to ascertain the practice of value-based radiology – and we include protocols to also learn from RCTs that are underway but yet to conclude.

There will be limitations, however. First, the inclusive search strategy notwithstanding, we will only include publications from select journals. Although we are likely to miss some published RCTs that could be relevant for the purpose of this review, we do not expect this to be a substantial issue that would compromise the results of the review and their usefulness. Second, we will focus on central aspects of outcomes in RCTs and how these are handled. Other aspects could be interesting but would not strictly underpin the purpose of this review. Third, this scoping review will not comprise DTA studies. This is a conscious decision grounded in several observations, including, the elusiveness of DTA studies to even well-designed search strategies,28,35 the likely unrealistic number of studies we would need to include, and the distinct purpose of DTA studies compared to RCTs.

In conclusion, by eliciting an overview of contemporary definitions and operationalisations of outcomes in radiological RCTs, this scoping review will directly inform a subsequent Delphi process to identify core outcomes for APTs, RCTs and observational studies in radiology and, more broadly, underpin the materialisation of value-based radiology.
