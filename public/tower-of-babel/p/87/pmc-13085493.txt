Prognostic value of peripheral blood fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio in pancreatic cancer patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jingjing Lu, Cuilin Yuan, Jianfeng Pan, Zonghan Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is a highly lethal malignancy with poor prognosis, largely due to late diagnosis and limited therapeutic efficacy. Identifying accessible and reliable prognostic biomarkers is essential for individualized patient management. The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) has emerged as a potential indicator reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies assessing FAR in PC patients. Databases including PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library were searched up to September 2025. Eligible studies reported hazard ratios (HRs) for overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS). Pooled analyses, subgroup evaluations, sensitivity tests, and publication bias assessments were conducted using standard meta-analytic techniques.ResultsSix retrospective studies encompassing 1,295 patients were included. High FAR was significantly associated with worse OS (HR = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.73–2.96; p < 0.001) and shorter PFS (HR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.34–2.66; p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses confirmed the prognostic value of FAR across different treatment modalities and cut-off thresholds. Sensitivity analyses and publication bias tests supported the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsHigh FAR is a simple, cost-effective, and reproducible biomarker predicting poor survival in PC, integrating tumor-related inflammation and host nutritional status. FAR may aid in risk stratification and individualized management, though prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate its clinical utility and clarify underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15840-7.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains one of the most devastating human malignancies, defined by a covert onset, rapid clinical progression, and extremely limited therapeutic efficacy. Among all cancer types, PC exhibits the most dismal outcome, with death rates that nearly parallel its incidence [1]. It stands as the sixth most frequent cause of cancer mortality in China, accounting for 2.82% of deaths [2], and occupies the fourth position globally [3]. Owing to its persistently increasing fatality rate, projections indicate that by 2030, PC will rank as the second predominant cause of cancer-related death in the United States [4]. Based on the most recent Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results statistics, the overall 5-year survival rate among affected individuals in the United States remains as low as 10% [5]. Although curative-intent pancreatectomy continues to represent the cornerstone and most effective treatment modality [6], survival outcomes remain unsatisfactory, with a 5-year OS below 20% [7]. Furthermore, merely one-fifth of newly diagnosed patients are suitable for surgical removal at the time of detection [8]. The unfavorable clinical course primarily arises from its silent progression during early stages, resulting in delayed diagnosis and a high frequency of distant dissemination [9].

For those with inoperable or metastatic disease, systemic chemotherapy constitutes the principal therapeutic approach. Protocols incorporating gemcitabine or 5-fluorouracil confer measurable survival advantages and are regarded as first-line options for advanced pancreatic malignancies [10]. Despite substantial progress in multidisciplinary management and refined therapeutic standards, the overall outcome remains extremely poor. Thus, discovering accessible, reproducible, and economically feasible biomarkers capable of forecasting prognosis is critical for achieving personalized clinical management.

As understanding of the tumor microenvironment deepens, the significance of inflammatory pathways in neoplastic initiation, invasion, and dissemination has become increasingly evident. Circulating inflammatory and biochemical indices have emerged as promising prognostic tools in oncology [11, 12]. Various systemic inflammatory indicators—such as the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio—have all been shown to possess predictive value for overall survival across multiple tumor entities [13, 14].

Beyond inflammation, tumor-associated coagulopathy constitutes another crucial hallmark of malignancy. Fibrinogen, a fundamental coagulation substrate, plays an essential part in maintaining hemostatic equilibrium, promoting thrombosis, and modulating both immune and inflammatory cascades [15]. Elevated fibrinogen concentrations have been correlated with enhanced tumor aggressiveness, metastatic potential, and unfavorable clinical outcomes [16]. In contrast, serum albumin—the predominant plasma protein synthesized by hepatocytes—serves as an indicator of systemic nutritional and inflammatory conditions. Lower albumin levels often signify compromised nutrition, systemic inflammation, and poorer survival outcomes in oncologic patients. The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR), which concurrently reflects coagulative activation and nutritional decline, has recently gained attention as a novel prognostic metric and has been validated in malignancies including gastric, colorectal, and bladder cancers [17–19].

Considering the intertwined biological functions of fibrinogen and albumin in inflammation, coagulation, and tumor progression, the FAR may act as a unified biomarker mirroring both tumor-driven systemic inflammation and host nutritional balance. Nonetheless, to date, no comprehensive meta-analysis has specifically assessed the prognostic implications of FAR in pancreatic carcinoma. Consequently, we undertook the present systematic review and meta-analysis to elucidate the prognostic relevance of peripheral blood FAR in patients with pancreatic cancer. Our analysis aims to furnish conclusive evidence supporting the clinical applicability of FAR as a simple, inexpensive, and practical prognostic tool that may enhance risk classification and therapeutic decision-making in this highly lethal malignancy. However, despite these promising findings, there remains no comprehensive, evidence-based synthesis evaluating the prognostic value of FAR in PC patients. A systematic review and meta-analysis are therefore warranted to quantitatively assess the association between FAR and survival outcomes. Such an analysis could clarify the utility of FAR as a standardized biomarker, inform individualized risk stratification, and guide therapeutic decision-making in oncology.

Methods

Literature search and study selection criteria

This systematic review was conducted in strict accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) framework [20]. To identify eligible studies, an extensive search was carried out across three major databases—PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library—covering all publications available up to 22 September 2025. The search strategy incorporated predefined keywords, including “fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio,” “fibrinogen/albumin ratio,” and “fibrinogen–albumin ratio,” to achieve comprehensive retrieval of relevant articles (Supplementary material 1). In addition, the bibliographies of the selected papers were manually reviewed to identify additional studies that might have been omitted through electronic screening. Furthermore, we conducted additional searches for grey literature on Google Scholar and manually scrutinized the reference lists of eligible studies. To minimize potential bias, two independent reviewers performed the literature selection process, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a senior investigator.

Studies were deemed eligible when they satisfied the following conditions: (1) retrospective or prospective design evaluating the prognostic relevance of the FAR in relation to survival outcomes such as overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS); (2) provision of hazard ratios (HRs) accompanied by corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs); and (3) availability of the full text in English. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) duplicate publications; (2) case reports, small case series, conference abstracts, narrative reviews, editorials, or practice guidelines. In instances where several reports analyzed overlapping patient cohorts, the study with the largest sample size and the most rigorous methodology was selected for inclusion [21].

Data extraction and quality evaluation

For each eligible investigation, relevant data were meticulously compiled, encompassing the first author’s name, year of publication, research time frame, country or region of origin, therapeutic strategies, total sample size, and baseline characteristics such as patient age and sex distribution. The specific cut-off criteria employed to stratify FAR levels were also documented. Whenever possible, HRs and corresponding 95% CIs were extracted from multivariate analyses to minimize confounding; if multivariate estimates were not reported, those derived from univariate analyses or reconstructed from Kaplan–Meier survival plots were used instead.

When direct numerical data were not available, relevant values were estimated using WebPlotDigitizer version 5 based on published Kaplan–Meier survival curves. For studies that lacked explicit HR and 95% CI values, indirect calculations were performed based on the method described by Tierney et al. [22–24].

The methodological integrity of the included non-randomized studies was evaluated through the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), a validated tool designed to assess the risk of bias in observational studies, particularly within cohort and case–control frameworks [25]. Studies attaining six or more points on the NOS were classified as demonstrating high methodological quality.

Statistical methods

All quantitative syntheses were conducted using Stata software (version 18.0), and the aggregated outcomes were visualized through forest plot representations. Inter-study heterogeneity was assessed by employing Cochran’s Q test in conjunction with the I² statistic, where heterogeneity was considered notable if the I² value exceeded 50% or the Q test produced a p-value < 0.1 [26, 27]. In the presence of considerable variability among studies, the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects framework was utilized; otherwise, data were analyzed under a fixed-effects model based on the inverse-variance weighting method.

Potential publication bias was examined through both Begg’s rank correlation and Egger’s linear regression approaches. In addition, the trim-and-fill method was applied to further assess the potential impact of publication bias on the pooled results. To evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates, sensitivity analyses were carried out by sequentially excluding individual studies and re-estimating the combined hazard ratio [28]. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed p-value threshold of 0.05.

Results

Literature search and characteristics of included studies

The systematic literature search, conducted across multiple electronic databases and supplemented by a manual examination of reference lists from relevant publications, initially identified 364 potentially eligible records. After the removal of 68 duplicate entries, the remaining studies underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 296 articles that failed to meet the predefined inclusion criteria. Subsequently, full-text assessments were carried out for 61 articles, of which 55 were excluded due to noncompliance with the established eligibility standards, including study design, patient population, or availability of survival outcomes. Ultimately, six studies were retained for the final quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis [29–34], forming the dataset for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1).

Pooled analysis of the association between FAR and overall survival

In total, this analysis integrated six eligible investigations, collectively encompassing 1,295 individuals diagnosed with pancreatic cancer. Within these datasets, participants were classified into groups characterized by high or low FAR levels, according to study-specific cut-off thresholds. The quantitative synthesis of available evidence revealed a significant negative prognostic effect associated with higher FAR values. Specifically, the combined HR for OS was 2.26 with a 95% CI ranging from 1.73 to 2.96 and a p-value < 0.001 (Fig. 2).

Forest plots depicting the association between the baseline fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio and overall survival in pancreatic cancer patients. HR, hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval

Assessment of between-study variability demonstrated marked heterogeneity among the included analyses. The Cochran’s Q statistic reached statistical significance, and the I² index was calculated to be 75.0% (p < 0.001), indicating considerable inconsistency across studies in terms of effect magnitude and direction. Given the presence of this substantial heterogeneity—likely reflecting variations in patient demographics, treatment strategies, and analytical cut-off definitions—a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects modeling framework was adopted for pooling effect estimates. This approach was chosen to provide a more conservative and generalized summary, acknowledging that the true effect size might vary across different clinical settings and study populations.

Statistical evaluation of potential publication bias was conducted using both Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s linear regression method. Neither approach demonstrated a statistically significant bias, indicating that the results were unlikely to be affected by selective reporting or small-study effects within the included literature. In detail, Begg’s test produced a p-value of 0.230, whereas Egger’s regression analysis yielded a p-value of 0.118, collectively supporting the robustness and reliability of the synthesized findings with respect to publication-related influences. In addition, we further evaluated the potential impact of publication bias on the pooled results using the trim-and-fill method, and no additional studies were imputed.

Sensitivity analysis and robustness of the association between FAR and overall survival

To evaluate the robustness and reliability of the pooled findings, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. This analytical procedure involves the sequential exclusion of each individual study from the overall model to determine whether any single dataset exerts a disproportionate influence on the combined hazard ratio. The results demonstrated that removing any single investigation did not materially alter the magnitude or direction of the overall effect, thereby confirming the stability and internal consistency of the analytic outcome (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis of the association between fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio and overall survival. CI, confidence interval

A closer inspection of the sensitivity profile revealed only minor fluctuations in the pooled estimates. When the dataset from Deng et al. 2021 was omitted, the recalculated summary HR and its 95% CI shifted slightly toward the left, yielding HR = 2.09; 95% CI: 1.62–2.69; p < 0.001, representing the most conservative estimate observed. Conversely, the exclusion of the study by Fang et al. 2021 (V) resulted in a modest rightward movement of the summary effect, with an updated HR = 2.48; 95% CI: 1.77–3.48; p < 0.001, corresponding to the most pronounced risk estimate among all iterations.

Collectively, these sensitivity results underscore that the overall association between an elevated FAR and unfavorable overall survival in pancreatic cancer patients remains statistically significant and directionally consistent, irrespective of the inclusion or exclusion of any individual study. This finding suggests that the synthesized evidence is methodologically sound, not unduly driven by outlier datasets, and therefore reliable for clinical inference and translational application.

Subgroup analyses based on treatment modality and FAR cut-off values

To further explore the prognostic value of the FAR in PC, subgroup analyses were conducted according to treatment modality and FAR cut-off thresholds. In the subgroup of patients receiving combined therapeutic approaches, higher FAR levels were significantly associated with poorer OS, with a pooled HR of 2.43 (95% CI: 1.54–3.83; p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Similarly, within the subgroup undergoing surgical treatment, higher FAR was also strongly correlated with shorter OS (HR = 2.24; 95% CI: 1.83–2.74; p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Subgroup analysis stratified by treatment modality showing the association between fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio and overall survival. HR, hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval

Subgroup analysis stratified by treatment modality showing the association between fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio and overall survival. HR, hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval

We further stratified patients based on the predefined FAR cut-off values to assess whether the prognostic impact was influenced by different thresholds (Fig. 5). The results consistently demonstrated that higher FAR predicted shorter OS regardless of the cut-off used (Fig. 5). Specifically, for studies applying a FAR threshold greater than 0.8, the pooled HR was 2.87 (95% CI: 1.69–4.86; p < 0.001), whereas for those with a FAR cut-off below 0.8, the HR remained significant at 1.98 (95% CI: 1.47–2.67; p < 0.001). Collectively, these subgroup analyses suggest that the adverse prognostic significance of high FAR is robust across different treatment strategies and FAR classification criteria.

Pretreatment FAR and progression-free survival

A total of two studies comprising 391 pancreatic cancer patients were included in this analysis to evaluate the prognostic significance of pretreatment FAR with respect to PFS. Patients were stratified into groups based on high versus low FAR levels, as defined by each study’s cut-off criteria. The pooled analysis revealed a statistically significant association between higher FAR and shorter PFS, with a combined HR of 1.88 (95% CI: 1.34–2.66; p < 0.001; Fig. 6).

Assessment of inter-study heterogeneity demonstrated minimal variability, as reflected by a Cochran’s Q test and an I² value of 0% (p = 0.717), indicating that the effect estimates were highly consistent across the included studies. Given the absence of notable heterogeneity, a fixed-effect model was appropriately applied to synthesize the results. These findings suggest that elevated pretreatment FAR serves as a robust predictor of earlier disease progression, highlighting its potential utility as a simple and accessible biomarker for risk stratification in pancreatic cancer patients.

Discussion

The FAR has recently emerged as a convenient, reproducible, and cost-efficient biomarker derived entirely from standard biochemical testing. In this analysis—the first comprehensive synthesis addressing its prognostic relevance in PC—we demonstrated that elevated FAR levels were consistently associated with unfavorable survival outcomes. This finding suggests that FAR may serve as a readily applicable marker for clinical risk stratification, enabling the identification of patients with a heightened likelihood of postoperative recurrence or rapid disease progression. Given its simplicity and accessibility, FAR holds promise as a valuable adjunct to established prognostic systems, potentially guiding treatment intensity and surveillance strategies in a more individualized manner.

Fibrinogen, a 340-kDa soluble glycoprotein synthesized in the liver, circulates as a key mediator of both coagulation and inflammatory processes [35]. Inflammation is a recognized hallmark of carcinogenesis [36], and ample experimental and clinical evidence underscores its integral role in tumor initiation and progression [37]. Chronic inflammatory states, such as colitis, have long been linked to malignant transformation, and animal studies have demonstrated a direct mechanistic relationship between fibrinogen and colitis-associated carcinogenesis [38]. Clinically, elevated preoperative fibrinogen levels have been repeatedly associated with worse outcomes in solid tumors, including colorectal, lung, nasopharyngeal, and pancreatic cancers [39, 40]. In PC, plasma fibrinogen concentration correlates positively with disease stage and the likelihood of distant metastasis [41]. Collectively, these findings highlight fibrinogen not only as a coagulation factor but also as a pro-tumorigenic molecule reflecting systemic inflammation and tumor aggressiveness.

Mechanistically, fibrinogen contributes to cancer progression through multiple biological pathways. PC frequently induces a hypercoagulable state, predisposing patients to thromboembolic events, with venous thromboembolism incidences reported in up to one-third of cases [42, 43]. Fibrin deposition establishes a provisional extracellular matrix enriched with growth factors that foster tumor proliferation [44]. It facilitates platelet adhesion to malignant cells, while activated platelets promote additional fibrin formation via thrombin generation, jointly creating a protective barrier that shields tumor cells from natural killer (NK) cell–mediated cytotoxicity [45]. Fibrinogen degradation products further stimulate angiogenesis, a prerequisite for tumor growth and metastasis [46]. Moreover, fibrinogen binds directly to angiogenic and growth-regulatory molecules—including VEGF, platelet-derived growth factor, fibroblast growth factors, and transforming growth factor-β—reinforcing its multifaceted involvement in tumor neovascularization, proliferation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [47]. Through the induction of EMT, fibrinogen enhances cellular migration and invasiveness, promoting metastatic dissemination [48].

From a pancreas-specific perspective, the prognostic relevance of FAR is biologically plausible because coagulation dysregulation is an integral component of the pancreatic cancer microenvironment rather than a mere bystander phenomenon [49]. Experimental studies have shown that pancreatic cancer cells can activate the coagulation cascade through tissue factor, and that the tissue factor–thrombin–PAR1 axis promotes PDAC growth and metastatic behavior by shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in KPC-derived models [49]. In parallel, emerging preclinical evidence indicates that fibrin(ogen) deposition may actively remodel the pancreatic tumor stroma: depletion of fibrinogen suppresses orthotopic tumor growth and spontaneous liver metastasis, while altering extracellular matrix remodeling programs and increasing tumor-restraining stromal components, including specific cancer-associated fibroblast populations [50]. At the immune level, fibrinogen can activate macrophage signaling through TLR4- and integrin-related pathways and induce pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, TNF-α, MCP-1, and matrix-remodeling enzymes [51], whereas tumor-associated macrophages in PDAC are predominantly skewed toward protumor phenotypes that foster fibrosis, immune evasion, and EMT-related tumor plasticity [52]. Therefore, a high FAR may reflect a biologic state in which hyperfibrinogenemia, malnutrition, desmoplasia, macrophage polarization, and metastatic potential converge, thereby explaining its association with adverse survival outcomes in pancreatic cancer.

In contrast, albumin reflects the host’s nutritional and inflammatory state. Hypoalbuminemia is prevalent among cancer patients and strongly correlates with poor prognosis. Progressive malnutrition contributes to the development of cancer cachexia, particularly in pancreatic and gastric malignancies [53]. Numerous studies have established low pretreatment serum albumin levels as predictors of adverse outcomes across various tumor types, including head and neck, lung, renal, gastric, and ovarian cancers [54, 55]. The reduction in serum albumin levels may result from chronic inflammation mediated by cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interferon-γ, which disrupt hepatic albumin synthesis [53]. Notably, IL-6 can upregulate VEGF secretion, leading to increased vascular permeability and secondary hypoalbuminemia [56].

By integrating fibrinogen and albumin into a single composite index, FAR captures both tumor-driven inflammation and host nutritional depletion. Elevated FAR values thus reflect a biological state characterized by excessive coagulation activation, systemic inflammation, and malnutrition—all of which contribute to poor survival in PC. Fang et al. compared the predictive efficacy of FAR, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio in PC and found that the AUC value of FAR was the highest, indicating the strongest predictive performance [31]. In addition, recent evidence has further supported the prognostic relevance of inflammation- and nutrition-related biochemical indices in pancreatic cancer. A recent retrospective cohort study showed that both the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score and the Albumin-Bilirubin (ALBI) grade were independent predictors of poor overall survival in patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer [57]. These findings reinforce the concept that host inflammatory, nutritional, and metabolic status plays a critical role in determining prognosis in pancreatic cancer. In this context, FAR may be considered not only as a convenient standalone biomarker, but also as a potentially valuable component of a composite prognostic model when integrated with other laboratory and clinicopathological parameters. Our findings suggest that FAR may serve as a reliable, objective indicator for identifying poor outcomes in PC patients.

Although this analysis provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all included studies were retrospective in design, which may have introduced selection bias, residual confounding, and methodological variability across studies. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed in the pooled analysis of overall survival. Although subgroup analyses according to treatment modality and FAR cut-off values were performed and explained part of this heterogeneity, additional sources of between-study variability could not be fully explored. In particular, differences in treatment strategies, patient characteristics, and incomplete reporting of clinicopathological variables may have contributed to the observed inconsistency. Notably, disease stage was not adequately reported in several included studies, which precluded further stage-based subgroup analyses, even though stage is a major determinant of prognosis and treatment selection in PC. Third, the current evidence base remains geographically limited, as all included cohorts were derived from East Asian populations, mainly from China and Japan, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups and healthcare settings. Fourth, there is currently no universally accepted FAR cut-off value. The threshold of 0.8 used in subgroup analyses should not be interpreted as a clinically validated standard, but rather as an exploratory stratification point derived from the available literature. Because this meta-analysis was based on published aggregate data rather than individual patient-level data, we were unable to perform receiver operating characteristic curve analysis with Youden index optimization to determine context-specific cut-offs for resectable versus metastatic disease, or to compare the incremental prognostic value of FAR beyond established tools such as ECOG performance status and TNM staging using likelihood ratio tests or net reclassification improvement metrics. In addition, serial FAR measurements during neoadjuvant therapy and their association with pathological response were not consistently reported in the included studies, precluding further evaluation of their dynamic clinical utility. Therefore, although our findings support the prognostic value of FAR in PC, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Future research should aim to validate these findings through large-scale, multicenter, multi-ethnic prospective studies. In particular, standardized and clinically meaningful FAR thresholds need to be established to improve inter-study comparability and facilitate routine clinical application. Ideally, optimal cut-off values should be determined using robust statistical approaches, such as receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, and further validated in independent prospective cohorts. Beyond baseline risk stratification, future investigations should also explore the clinical value of dynamic changes in FAR during treatment, as longitudinal monitoring may provide additional insight into therapeutic response, disease progression, and survival outcomes. Moreover, integrating FAR with molecular or genomic biomarkers, as well as other clinicopathological parameters, may improve prognostic accuracy and contribute to the development of more comprehensive prediction models for pancreatic cancer. In addition, mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate the biological role of the fibrinogen–albumin axis in PC progression and to determine whether therapeutic modulation of either component may improve clinical outcomes. Ultimately, incorporating FAR into multidimensional prognostic frameworks may enhance individualized risk stratification and support more precise therapeutic decision-making in PC.

Conclusion

This analysis demonstrates that high FAR is consistently associated with poorer overall and progression-free survival in pancreatic cancer patients. FAR effectively integrates tumor-driven systemic inflammation and host nutritional status, serving as a simple, cost-effective, and reproducible prognostic biomarker. Its predictive value remains robust across treatment modalities and FAR thresholds, supporting its potential utility in clinical risk stratification and individualized management. Despite limitations related to retrospective study designs, regional patient cohorts, and heterogeneous cut-offs, FAR represents a promising tool to guide therapeutic decisions and surveillance strategies. Future prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore the mechanistic role of FAR in pancreatic tumor progression.
