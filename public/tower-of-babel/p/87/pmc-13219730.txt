Numerical and experimental investigation of the effect of geometric parameters on the acoustic performance of bio-inspired reed structures
Authors: Reta Warkina, Amanuel Borema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, rapid economic development and urbanization have led to serious environmental problems, including noise pollution. Although existing acoustic absorbers can be designed to target specific frequency ranges, those effective at low frequencies typically require large volumes. Consequently, there is a growing demand for compact absorbers capable of mitigating low-frequency noise in the range of 100–1500 Hz. Conventional liners, such as melamine foam, exhibit limited performance in this frequency band. To address these limitations, NASA patented a broadband acoustic absorber inspired by natural reeds, which mimics their geometry and sound absorption characteristics. This study investigates the influence of reed diameter, orientation angle, and spacing on the sound absorption performance of reed-inspired structures. Models with reed spacings of 6 mm, 8 mm, and 10 mm and reed angles of 45° were developed for reed diameters of 3.5 mm and 5 mm using SolidWorks. Numerical simulations were performed in COMSOL Multiphysics based on the Delany–Bazley model. The results indicate that a configuration with a 5 mm reed diameter, 8 mm spacing, and a 45° orientation achieves the highest sound absorption coefficient. Sound pressure measurements further confirmed the effectiveness of the optimized design, showing a reduction from 92.3 dB to 48.1 dB in the numerical analysis and from 91.4 dB to 57.1 dB in the experimental measurements, corresponding to an average sound pressure reduction of approximately 47.8%. Despite minor discrepancies between numerical and experimental results, the findings demonstrate that bio-inspired reed-like structures provide efficient and compact low-frequency noise attenuation. Overall, this study shows that careful geometric optimization of reed-inspired absorbers can bridge the gap between idealized numerical predictions and experimental performance, offering a promising approach for developing compact, high-efficiency solutions for low-frequency acoustic control in aerospace, automotive, industrial, and architectural applications.Keywords: Acoustic absorber, COMSOL multi-physics, Sound absorption coefficient, Sound pressure level, Delany-Bazely model, Sound dbSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The escalation of noise levels has become increasingly pronounced with ongoing urbanization and population growth. Various sources, including residential areas, nightlife venues, restaurants, aircraft operations, continuously congested traffic, and industrial activities, collectively contribute to elevated environmental noise. Today, noise pollution is regarded as one of the major global environmental challenges, primarily driven by the rapid expansion of the transportation sector1. In response, NASA is actively pursuing innovative solutions to transform aircraft systems and components rather than relying on incremental improvements2. Acoustic absorbers are now widely applied in industrial noise control, environmental acoustics, and architectural design. In spaces such as concert halls and auditoriums, bass traps are sometimes used to satisfy strict acoustic requirements and to achieve high sound quality3. Additionally, acoustic liners and noise barriers provide effective means of mitigating noise pollution generated by automobiles and aircraft. Mufflers, for example, help reduce exhaust noise from vehicles. Therefore, the incorporation of acoustic absorbers is essential for creating a pleasant and comfortable auditory environment for communities4. In the modern world, effective sound management has become increasingly important across various fields, ranging from architectural design and transportation to environmental protection and industrial processes5. Acoustic performance, defined as the ability of a structure to control and manipulate sound waves, plays a critical role in ensuring comfort, safety, and efficiency in diverse applications6 The pursuit of innovative methods to enhance acoustic performance has stimulated growing interest in bio-inspired structures. Nature has long inspired engineers and designers by providing refined solutions to complex problems through millions of years of evolution7. Bio-inspired structures emulate the forms, functions, and mechanisms of natural systems, exploiting their inherent efficiency, resilience, and adaptability.

One particularly fascinating area of bio-inspiration lies in the realm of natural reeds and their remarkable acoustic properties. Found in wetlands and marshes worldwide, natural reeds have evolved unique geometries and material properties to thrive in their environments. In addition to their ecological significance, these reeds exhibit exceptional abilities to manipulate sound waves, making them a compelling source of inspiration for acoustic engineering and design8. The slender and flexible nature of these structures enables them to effectively transmit, absorb, and scatter sound waves, rendering them exemplary models for enhancing acoustic performance within man-made systems. The increasing fascination with bio-inspired structures mirroring natural reeds arises from their capacity to potentially transform numerous engineering applications. Through the emulation of geometric characteristics, material compositions, and structural dynamics inherent in natural reeds, engineers aspire to devise innovative solutions aimed at enhancing sound transmission, reducing noise, and augmenting acoustic comfort in architectural designs, transportation vehicles, acoustic barriers, and beyond5.

Bio-inspired methods not only promise to enhance acoustic performance but also align with broader sustainability and ecological objectives. By emulating nature’s design principles, engineers can craft structures that are efficient, effective, and environmentally conscious. The slender, cylindrical structure of natural reeds represents a defining aspect of their geometry. This elongated shape facilitates efficient sound transmission, as sound waves traversing through the reed’s hollow interior encounter minimal impedance and attenuation. Additionally, the cylindrical form promotes the omnidirectional emission of sound, enabling reeds to effectively disperse sound energy throughout their surroundings3. The acoustic properties of natural reeds are not only influenced by their shape but also by their material composition. Typically composed of fibrous cellulose structures strengthened by lignin, reeds offer a balance of flexibility and rigidity. This combination is crucial for their ability to endure various environmental conditions while preserving their acoustic integrity9,10. This fibrous structure acts as a natural acoustic absorber, dampening sound vibrations as they propagate through the reed material11. Moreover, the distinctive microstructure of natural reeds, marked by hierarchical patterns of fibers and air pockets, contributes to their superior acoustic capabilities. This hierarchical arrangement allows reeds to scatter and diffuse sound waves efficiently, thereby diminishing the occurrence of standing waves and mitigating reverberation in their surroundings11.

The exceptional acoustical behavior of natural reeds is a result of the intricate interplay between their geometry and material properties. Through precise adjustments to dimensions, density, and arrangement, nature has honed the performance of reeds to serve multiple ecological purposes, such as communication, habitat protection, and environmental signaling12. Understanding and replicating these intricate design principles hold tremendous potential for engineering bio-inspired structures with tailored acoustic properties for diverse applications.

In the field of noise control, particularly in environments such as automobiles and theaters, researchers have been making significant strides in advancing both natural and synthetic polymeric fibrous materials. These materials provide numerous benefits, such as safety, ease of design, softness, and cost-effectiveness. Natural fibers, derived from animals or plants, have gained attention for their biodegradability, non-toxicity, and recyclability13. Examples of these natural materials include jute, flax, kenaf, hemp, coir fiber, wood, coconut, straw, cane, and corn husk. They hold promise for various noise control applications, offering sustainable alternatives to synthetic materials while also adhering to environmental and safety standards14. Sound energy dissipation in fibrous materials primarily happens through viscous effects resulting from friction between air molecules and pore walls, along with thermal losses due to heat transfer among the fibers. However, the intricate structure of natural fibers can lead to discrepancies between predictive models and real-world results15. For instance, Ayub et al. discovered that the Johnson-Allard equivalent fluid model is more appropriate for sound absorption materials based on coir fibers. Meanwhile, Liu et al. noted that hollow kapok fibers exhibit reduced thermal conductivity, impacting the assessment of thermal conductive effects14. To address this, they proposed a modified Biot-Allard model that considers the material’s heat conduction performance, resulting in absorption predictions consistent with experimental results.

Selecting natural fibers with appropriate physical characteristics is essential for optimizing sound absorption coefficients. For example, kapok nonwovens with smaller pore sizes and higher porosity generally exhibit superior sound absorption coefficients, particularly at lower frequencies. Furthermore, the integration of a gradient pore structure enhances sound absorption performance within lower frequency ranges. These findings emphasize the significance of understanding and leveraging the physical properties of natural fibers to enhance sound absorption capabilities across various applications16,17

The rising need for transportation that prioritizes safety, environmental sustainability, and reduced noise levels, while accommodating a growing population, highlights the importance of incorporating acoustically absorbent liners into different types of aircraft in the coming years18. With a wide range of flight vehicles currently in operation and new innovative concept aircraft and prototypes being developed annually, NASA is leading the charge in pursuing revolutionary ideas that go beyond incremental improvements, aiming to achieve transformative innovations in aircraft components and systems19.

What insights can we glean from nature to advance liner technology? Additionally, how can we design multifunctional structures that not only absorb sound but also serve other purposes like bearing loads, insulating, dampening vibrations, transferring heat, or generating lift? These inquiries are steering NASA’s endeavors towards innovative solutions that cater to the evolving needs of aviation, aiming for a quieter and more sustainable future in air transportation. Drawing inspiration from studies on noise’s environmental impact and endeavors to harness nature’s principles to mitigate aircraft noise, the researchers are leveraging advancements in manufacturing and materials, exemplified by Bishop-Moser, Spadaccini, and Andres, to develop multifunctional structures with novel properties. Principles of sound absorptive materials

Acoustic wave propagation within porous and resonant media is fundamentally governed by partial differential equations that describe the spatiotemporal evolution of pressure fluctuations. Analytical solutions to these equations are generally intractable for geometrically intricate domains. Accordingly, numerical methodologies, most notably the finite element method (FEM), are routinely employed. FEM effectuates a spatial discretization of the domain, thereby converting the continuous governing equations into a discrete algebraic system whose solution furnishes a high-fidelity approximation of the intrinsic acoustic behavior34.

Influence of reed spacing on the acoustic absorption performance for constant reed diameters

Numerical simulations were performed in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 using the Delany–Bazley empirical model to assess the effect of reed spacing on the sound absorption behavior of the proposed structure over a frequency range of 0–2000 Hz. Two constant reed diameters, 3.5 mm and 5 mm, were considered, while the spacing between adjacent reeds was varied as 6 mm, 8 mm, and 10 mm. The study aimed to investigate how geometric spacing influences the interaction between acoustic waves and the porous reed array, and consequently, the overall absorption coefficient. For each configuration, the frequency-dependent absorption coefficient was calculated and compared. The results indicate that decreasing the spacing between reeds generally enhances absorption performance, particularly in the low- to mid-frequency range, due to increased viscous and thermal losses within the narrow air gaps. In contrast, larger reed spacing reduces flow resistivity, weakening energy dissipation and lowering absorption levels. Additionally, for the same spacing, reeds with a 5 mm diameter exhibit slightly higher absorption than those with 3.5 mm, which can be attributed to the increased solid volume fraction and improved acoustic impedance matching. These findings highlight that reed spacing is a critical design parameter in optimizing the acoustic absorption characteristics of reed-inspired sound absorbers.

Conclusion

Rapid urbanization and industrial growth have intensified noise pollution, particularly at low frequencies (100–1500 Hz), where conventional absorbers require large volumes and existing materials, such as melamine foam, exhibit limited broadband performance. Inspired by natural reeds, NASA patented a broadband absorber mimicking their geometry and absorption characteristics. This study investigates compact bio-inspired reed like absorbers, analyzing the influence of geometric parameters reed diameter, spacing, and orientation on sound absorption. Three spacings (6 mm, 8 mm, and 10 mm) and two diameters (3.5 mm and 5 mm) at a 45° orientation were modeled numerically using COMSOL Multiphysics and validated experimentally through 3D-printed prototypes. Among the configurations tested, reeds with a 5 mm diameter and 8 mm spacing exhibited the highest absorption, while the 3.5 mm diameter with 6 mm spacing highlighted the effect of diameter on acoustic performance. The optimized design achieved a peak numerical absorption coefficient of 0.9874 at 750 Hz, whereas the experimental maximum reached 0.9607 around 200 Hz. While numerical simulations predicted sustained high absorption across a broad frequency range, experimental results showed a gradual decline at higher frequencies, highlighting the influence of real-world factors such as material imperfections and boundary conditions. Sound pressure measurements further confirmed the effectiveness of the optimized reeds, with reductions from 92.3 dB to 48.1 dB numerically and from 91.4 dB to 57.1 dB experimentally. Despite minor discrepancies between numerical and experimental outcomes, the bio-inspired reed-like structures provide efficient, compact low-frequency noise attenuation. Overall, these findings demonstrate that careful geometric optimization of reed-inspired absorbers can bridge the gap between idealized numerical predictions and experimental performance, offering a promising pathway for compact, high-efficiency solutions to low-frequency acoustic control in urban and industrial environments.
