Females exhibit greater cardiac volume reductions with expiratory muscle loading during submaximal exercise compared to males
Authors: Sarah A Angus, Michelle Ottlik, Connor J Doherty, Benjamin P Thompson, Jason S Au, Eric J Stöhr, Paolo B Dominelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractIntrathoracic pressure (ITP) modulates venous return, ventricular filling and ejection throughout the respiratory cycle, and its fluctuations are accentuated during exercise. We sought to determine whether ITP manipulation during submaximal exercise alters cardiac volumes differently in males and females. Young, healthy participants completed two protocols (both n = 30, 15 females): (i) inspiratory loaded breathing (75 W and 30% W max) and (ii) expiratory loaded breathing (100 W and 60% W max). We aimed to increase the inspiratory and expiratory load by +20 cmH2O oesophageal pressure (absolute submaximal trials; 75 and 100 W) and +50% oesophageal pressure swings (relative submaximal trials; 30% and 60% W max). Intrathoracic pressure was measured with a balloon catheter placed in the oesophagus, and left ventricular volumes and cardiac output were determined via echocardiography. Inspiratory loading increased oesophageal pressure swings relative to spontaneous breathing at both intensities [75 W: +24.2 ± 10.2 cmH2O (ITP = +77.3%); 30% Wmax: +14.3 ± 8.6 cmH2O (ITP = +53.1%); P < 0.0001] with no difference in stroke volume (SV) [75 W: +3.2 ± 10.1 mL (SV = +3.8%); 30% W max: +0.7 ± 9.7 mL (SV = +0.8%); P ≥ 0.100] in either sex. Expiratory loading increased oesophageal pressure swings relative to spontaneous breathing [100 W: +23.1 ± 4.1 cmH2O (ITP = +66.3%); 60% W max: +17.1 ± 8.5 cmH2O (ITP = +50.1%); P < 0.0001]. With expiratory loading, SV decreased compared to spontaneous breathing [100 W: −11.4 ± 9.7 mL (SV = −13.8%); 60% W max: −8.3 ± 9.5 mL (SV = −10.2%); P < 0.0001] with left ventricular end‐systolic volume (LVESV) decreasing in females [100 W: −6.5 ± 4.7 mL (LVESV = −13.9%); 60% W max: −6.1 ± 6.5 mL (LVESV = −13.1%); both P < 0.0006], but not in males [100 W: −1.9 ± 7.2 mL (LVESV = −2.6%); 60% W max: −0.1 ± 4.8 mL (LVESV = −0.2%); P ≥ 0.409]. Overall, exercise appears to blunt any potential cardiac volume changes with inspiratory loaded breathing, whereas expiratory loaded breathing (less negative ITP) imposes a preload limitation that the exercising heart cannot compensate, thereby unmasking a female‐specific reduction in LVESV. Key points At rest, more negative intrathoracic pressure (ITP) with inspiratory loading lowers left ventricular end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV) and stroke volume (SV). Previous work conducted at maximal exercise shows no cardiac volume changes with inspiratory loading. The mechanisms and any sex‐based differences are unknown.During rest and exercise, less negative ITP with expiratory loading reduces LVEDV, SV and cardiac output; however, sex‐specific responses remain unclear.Inspiratory loading at submaximal exercise results in no cardiac volume changes and responses were not different between males and females.Expiratory loading at submaximal exercise reduces LVEDV, SV and cardiac output in both sexes; however, females experience greater reductions in cardiac volumes and exhibit a decline in left ventricular end‐systolic volume (LVESV).During exercise, potential cardiac volume changes associated with more negative ITP (inspiratory loading) appear to be masked by the cardiovascular responses to exercise, whereas less negative ITP (expiratory loading) imposes an uncompensated preload limitation although females attempt to counter this through LVESV reductions. Introduction

Intrathoracic pressure (ITP) is the pressure within the thoracic cavity and serves as the backing pressure for many cardiopulmonary structures, including the right atrium, pulmonary vasculature and the left ventricle (Jozwiak & Teboul, 2024; Mahmood & Pinsky, 2018). As such, ITP governs several of the key pressure gradients that underlie cardiopulmonary interactions, particularly those regulating venous return and left ventricular ejection (Magder, 2016; Pinsky, 2007). Intrathoracic pressure changes during the respiratory cycle, with inspiration involving a more negative ITP and expiration requiring a less negative or more positive pressure. During exercise, the swings in ITP become greater as end‐inspiratory ITP becomes more negative, whereas end‐expiratory ITP becomes more positive (Aliverti, 2016). During inspiration, a more negative ITP enhances the pressure gradient for venous return by lowering right atrial pressure. Venous return is also augmented by activation of the skeletal muscle pump, which increases mean systemic filling pressure. However, this same negative ITP also increases left ventricular (LV) transmural pressure, effectively elevating afterload and requiring greater myocardial work to eject blood (Buda et al., 1979; Mahmood & Pinsky, 2018; Pinsky, 2005). By contrast, during expiration, when ITP becomes less negative (or more positive), the pressure gradient for venous return is reduced, which can diminish LV end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV), yet LV ejection is facilitated by the lower transmural pressure (Jozwiak & Teboul, 2024; Pinsky, 2018).

Experimental manipulations of ITP have been widely employed to isolate the direct effects of respiratory mechanics on cardiac function. Increasing the magnitude of negative ITP, via inspiratory loading or Mueller manoeuvres, has been shown at rest to augment right ventricular filling pressures and venous return up to oesophageal pressures of around −30 cmH2O (Cheyne et al., 2020; Scharf et al., 1979; Wright et al., 2023). However, these increases in right ventricular filling do not translate to higher LV stroke volume (SV) because the elevated LV transmural pressure impedes ejection and the leftward septal shift associated with right ventricular distention reduces LV compliance and filling (Cheyne et al., 2018). Consequently, LVEDV and SV decline despite enhanced venous return. A previous study conducted at maximal exercise found no change in SV or cardiac output with inspiratory loaded breathing (Harms et al., 1998).

Manipulating ITP on the expiratory side provides the opposite perturbation where ITP increases (relative to inspiration) and becomes less negative or more positive. A study conducted at both rest and exercise has shown that expiratory loading reduces SV and cardiac output (Stark‐Leyva et al., 2004). These reductions were primarily attributed to a decreased pressure gradient for venous return, leading to lower LVEDV and subsequent reductions in SV and cardiac output through series ventricular interactions; however, Stark‐Leyva et al. (2004) did not measure LV volumes.

Despite extensive investigation into the haemodynamic consequences of altered ITP, little is known about whether these cardiac effects differ between males and females. From a cardiac perspective, females have smaller heart sizes and volumes compared to males even when scaled to body surface area and lean body mass (Sandstede et al., 2000; Vasan et al., 1997). These anatomical differences lead to smaller cardiac outputs and larger ejection fractions in females (Chung et al., 2006; Rutkowski et al., 2020). From a pulmonary perspective, females have smaller lungs and airways compared to males, even when matched for height and lung size (Dominelli et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2021). As a result, during exercise, females experience higher airway resistance and greater work of breathing (W b), particularly once ventilation exceeds ∼50 L min−1 (Dominelli et al., 2015; Guenette et al., 2007; Mann et al., 2020). These characteristics may lead to larger ITP swings in females, potentially increasing their reliance on respiratory‐driven pressure gradients to assist venous return. If true, sex‐based differences in airway mechanics and respiratory muscle work could translate into distinct cardiac responses when ITP is manipulated.

Accordingly, we sought to determine whether the cardiac responses to both inspiratory (more negative) and expiratory (more positive) ITP manipulations during submaximal exercise differ between males and females. We reasoned that, because females experience greater W b during exercise, females may rely more heavily on respiratory‐driven pressure gradients to augment cardiac filling. Therefore, we hypothesized that (i) during inspiratory loading, females would exhibit a greater increase in LVEDV compared to males and (ii) during expiratory loading, females would demonstrate a greater reduction in LVEDV and SV compared to males, reflecting a heightened sensitivity of cardiac filling to respiratory pressure changes.

Methods

Ethical approval

The experimental procedures were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Board at the University of Waterloo (CREB# 46172) and the Central Ethics Committee at Leibniz University Hannover (EV LUH 07.2024, ID 270). The research methods and protocols adhere to the recommendations outlined by the Declaration of Helsinki concerning the use of human participants, except for registration in a database. All participants provided their written informed consent.

Participants

Thirty participants (n = 15 females) completed protocol 1 and an additional thirty participants (n = 15 females) completed protocol 2 (see ‘Experimental overview’ below). Participants were young (<40 years old) healthy adults who completed two distinct protocols. Demographics and baseline cardiorespiratory data are shown in Table 1. Participants were excluded if they had any known respiratory, cardiovascular or other conditions, which would impact their response to exercise. All participants engaged in >30 min of moderate intensity physical activity, three times per week. Females were excluded if they were pregnant or nursing and were tested randomly throughout their menstrual cycle because there is no difference in the ventilatory response across the menstrual cycle (MacNutt et al., 2012; Mann et al., 2024). The use of oral contraceptives was not an exclusion criterion.

Experimental overview

The present manuscript combines two data sets (protocols 1 and 2) to provide a comprehensive description of sex differences in heart–lung interactions during exercise. Both protocols involved two testing sessions each which occurred on different days separated by >48 h. Thirteen participants completed both protocols (doing all aspects), which were separated by >5 months, all other participants completed only one of the protocols (Fig. 1). The same procedures were conducted on day 1 for both protocols to determine eligibility, maximal oxygen uptake (V˙O2) and familiarization with echocardiography. Day 2 for protocol 1 consisted of four exercise trials with either an inspiratory load or spontaneous breathing. Day 2 for protocol 2 involved four exercise trials with an expiratory load or spontaneous breathing. The experimental testing visit (day 2) for protocols 1 and 2 were discreet and occurred on different days. For both protocols, exercise was performed at an absolute and relative intensity to better allow for sex‐based comparisons (Dominelli & Molgat‐Seon, 2022). The absolute exercise intensities allowed for comparisons between males and females for a similar metabolic demand, accounting for overall size differences. The relative exercise intensities allowed for comparisons between males and females for a matched relative metabolic demand, which minimizes size effects. Presenting sex‐based comparisons at both intensities provides a framework to distinguish sex‐related differences from those attributable to size.

Experimental design of each protocol and the timeline for data collection

Data collection for protocols 1 and 2 were separated by >5 months. The procedures conducted on days 1 and 2 were the same for both protocols 1 and 2. However, protocol 1 (inspiratory loading) involved four exercise trials [two loading (75 W and 30% W max) and two control trials (75 W and 30% W max)]. Protocol 2 (expiratory loading) involved four exercise trials [two loading (100 W and 60% W max) and two control trials (100 W and 60% W max)].

Peak exercise (day 1)

Participants were instrumented with an oesophageal (protocols 1 and 2) and gastric (protocol 2) balloon catheter and performed a maximal incremental exercise test to exhaustion on a semi‐recumbent cycle ergometer in the left lateral decubitus position (Ergoselect 1200; Ergoline, Blitz, Germany) to determine maximal work rate and oesophageal pressure changes. The cycle ergometer was adjusted for height and comfort for each participant and kept the same for the subsequent testing visit. All participants completed a 3‐min warm‐up prior to the maximal exercise test, which began at 60 W for males, 20 W for females with intensity increasing by 20 W min−1. Maximal exercise tests were terminated when participants failed to maintain a cadence of 60 r.p.m. despite verbal encouragement. Cardiometabolic and respiratory variables were collected as described below.

Pulmonary function

Pulmonary function measures were performed according to American Thoracic Society standards (Bhakta et al., 2023) using a calibrated whole‐body plethysmograph (Medisoft, Sorinnes, Belgium) in all participants across both protocols. Spirometry was used to determine forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), %FVC expired in FEV1 and forced expiratory flow at 25–75% of expiration. All measurements were compared to reference values to determine normal pulmonary function (Quanjer et al., 2012).

Protocol 1: experimental exercise trials (day 2)

Upon arrival, participants were positioned on the semi‐recumbent cycle ergometer and placed in the left lateral decubitus position and rested for an additional 15 min during which the sonographer performed baseline cardiac image acquisition (see ‘Transthoracic echocardiography’). After imaging, participants returned to a chair for placement of the oesophageal balloon catheters (see ‘Oesophageal and gastric pressure’). Afterwards, participants performed four discrete exercise trials, with two at an absolute intensity (75 W) and two at a relative intensity (30% of peak power output (W max) as determined on day 1). The order of exercise trials was randomized, counterbalanced and stratified by sex where both males and females have the same number of ordering exposures. Each exercise intensity had a trial where inspiratory pressure was increased, whereas the other served as a control. All exercise trials were continuous, lasting ∼10–12 min in duration. The experimental trials involved the following in sequential order. First, 3–4 min of spontaneous breathing, followed by 3–4 min of inspiratory loading (labelled as ‘INS LOAD’), followed by another 3–4 min of spontaneous breathing (labelled as ‘SPON’). The control trials at each exercise intensity were identical except they did not include a ‘INS LOAD’ condition, but rather the participant cycled continuously for 10–12 min when breathing spontaneously for the duration of the test. Participants had ∼15 min of rest between exercise trials or until their heart rate returned to within ±10 beats min−1 of baseline rest. All trials were completed at a standardized cadence of 60–70 r.p.m. to minimize movement artefact and ensure high quality cardiac image acquisition. Echocardiographic images were acquired during each condition (INS LOAD and SPON) once participants reached a steady‐state heart rate and ventilation. Although participants were at a steady‐state pre‐LOAD, ventilation and heart rate typically rose during the INS LOAD condition and stabilized post‐LOAD at levels similar to INS LOAD. Therefore, INS LOAD was compared with post‐LOAD because these stages demonstrated similar cardiopulmonary stability at the same time as differing in the ITP pattern. Thus, SPON data were collected post‐loading to match physiological conditions across stages and minimize variability. To standardize the echocardiographic assessment acquisition, the sonographer performed a similar sequence of imaging during each trial.

Protocol 2: experimental exercise trials (day 2)

Participants were positioned in the semi‐recumbent left‐lateral position on the cycle ergometer and rested quietly for ∼15 min when baseline cardiac images were acquired (see ‘Transthoracic echocardiography’). Following imaging, oesophageal and gastric balloon catheters were placed (see ‘Oesophageal and gastric pressure’). Participants then completed four exercise bouts: two at an absolute intensity (100 W) and two at a relative intensity (60% W max). Trial order was randomized and counterbalanced with stratification by sex. Within each intensity, one bout involved expiratory loading and the other served as a control condition. Expiratory loading trials consisted of 3–4 min of spontaneous breathing, 3–4 min of expiratory pressure loading (labelled as ‘EXP LOAD’) and 3–4 min of spontaneous breathing (labelled as ‘SPON’). Control trials were performed with spontaneous breathing throughout the 10–12 min exercise bout. Participants rested ∼15 min between exercise bouts or until heart rate returned to within ±10 beats min−1 of pre‐exercise values. Cycling cadence was maintained at 60–70 r.p.m. Cardiac images for both conditions (EXP LOAD and SPON) were obtained once steady‐state heart rate and ventilation were reached. As with protocol 1, ventilation and heart rate increased during loading and stabilized at a similar level during the subsequent SPON phase; therefore, SPON data were collected post‐loading to ensure comparable physiological conditions. Therefore, EXP LOAD was compared with post‐LOAD because these stages exhibited similar cardiopulmonary stability but differed in the ITP pattern. The sonographer followed an identical standardized imaging sequence to protocol 1.

Respiratory pressure manipulation

Figure 2 displays raw oesophageal pressure traces at each stage while exercising at 75 and 100 W in one male and one female participant that completed both protocols 1 and 2. To increase inspiratory and expiratory work, custom‐built respiratory resistors were placed in either the inspiratory (protocol 1) or expiratory arm (protocol 2) of the breathing circuit. The resistors functioned to reduce the radius of orifice and increase resistive W b. The respiratory resistors had resistances ranging from 6 to 14 cmH2O L−1 s−1, which were sufficient at submaximal flows to increase ITP and the W b by 50–65% above control, levels previously shown to elicit cardiac volume changes (Cheyne et al., 2016; Karam et al., 1984; Miller et al., 2006; Stark‐Leyva et al., 2004). The level of respiratory load was scaled to exercise intensity: for absolute intensities (75 and 100 W), an additional +20 cmH2O was added above each participant's measured oesophageal pressure swings (e.g. if oesophageal pressure swings were 20 cmH2O at 75 W, an additional +20 cmH2O was added for a total of 40 cmH2O). For the relative exercise intensities (30% and 60% W max), the level of respiratory load corresponded to +50% of each participant's oesophageal pressure swings at the given workload (e.g. if a participant's oesophageal pressure swings were 20 cmH2O, an additional +10 cmH2O was added for a total of 30 cmH2O). Oesophageal pressure swings were determined on day 1 during the maximal exercise test, and these values were used to calculate each participants individual LOAD condition on day 2. Oesophageal pressure swings were verified on day 2 during SPON prior to the LOAD condition to ensure the correct resistor was added during the LOAD condition.

Protocols 1 and 2 exercise intensities selection

Protocol 1 (inspiratory loading) and protocol 2 (expiratory loading) were performed at different absolute and relative exercise intensities to ensure that the respiratory loads applied (during the LOAD conditions) were comparable. At lower workloads, inspiration is active, whereas expiration remains mostly passive; thus, the expiratory loading trials required higher exercise intensities to elicit sufficient ventilation and active recruitment of the expiratory muscles. Two different loading conditions were applied during the exercise trials (absolute: +20 cmH2O, and relative: +50% of delta oesophageal pressure swings). To ensure a standardized relative (+50%) respiratory muscle challenge, we increased the expiratory loading exercise intensities to 60% W max and 100 W. An additional consideration was that both the inspiratory and expiratory loads needed to be applied at exercise intensities where the relevant respiratory muscles were already active. This ensured that the inspiratory and expiratory loads produced comparable ITP changes and avoided artificially initiating expiratory muscle recruitment at low workloads. As such, because our delta oesophageal pressure swings were similar between protocols (during SPON), this approach ensured that the absolute load applied was similar across protocols, even though the exercise intensities differed. If both protocols had been performed at the same intensity (e.g. 30% W max), the inspiratory loading trials would reflect active respiratory muscle recruitment, whereas the expiratory loading trials would not. Therefore, leading to mismatched oesophageal pressure swings and an overall respiratory load that is not similar between protocols. By conducting inspiratory loading at lower exercise intensities and expiratory loading at higher exercise intensities, we were able to achieve comparable changes in oesophageal pressure swings and ensure that the respiratory muscle loads applied (during the LOAD conditions) were equivalent between protocols despite differences in exercise intensity.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Ultrasound measurements were acquired at rest and during exercise (Vivid E95; GE Medical, Horton, Norway; Vivid S70N; GE Medical, Chicago, IL, USA) with a M5Sc‐D Phased Array Transducer 1.5–4.6‐MHz probe for 2D imaging and saved for offline analysis with commercially available software (EchoPAC; GE Medical, Horton, Norway). Echocardiographic images were obtained from five consecutive cardiac cycles during expiration, as determined by a superimposed respiratory trace, to reduce physiological variation in cardiac output across the respiratory cycle, in accordance with current guidelines (Lang et al., 2005). Although this approach minimizes beat‐to‐beat variability and improves measurement reproducibility, it does exclude potential differences that may occur during inspiration. Therefore, it is possible that subtle inspiratory‐phase effects were not captured; however, focusing on expiration is a well‐established method for standardizing cardiac measurements and minimizing confounding variation related to respiratory mechanics (Mitchell et al., 2019). Cardiac images were considered non‐analysable if image quality did not permit reliable delineation of the LV endocardial borders throughout the cardiac cycle. Images were excluded when the heart was not fully within the imaging window, when lung artefact obscured the LV free wall or septum, or when image blur prevented clear identification of end‐diastole and end‐systole. Inclusion of a cardiac image required continuous visualization of the entire left ventricle at both end‐diastole and end‐systole. During the resting cardiac scan (prior to exercise testing), a trained sonographer determined the best windows for image acquisition and labelled each window on every participant with indelible ink that lasted the duration of the experimental visit. During each exercise stage, small adjustments were made to the transducer position (from previously marked positions) to ensure optimal transducer angle and image quality. Left ventricular internal dimensions were assessed at rest in the parasternal long axis view. Pulsed Doppler recordings were performed in the apical four‐chamber view, with the sample volume at the tip of the mitral valve and analysed for LV inflow early (E) and atrial (A) waves. Beat‐by‐beat SV was determined using the velocity‐time integral method by measuring Doppler velocity within the LV outflow tract obtained from the apical five‐chamber view and LV outflow tract diameter measured from the parasternal long axis view. During both rest and exercise, images were collected in the apical two‐chamber, four‐chamber and short‐axis views at the level of the mitral valve and papillary muscle. Representative apical four‐chamber and two‐chamber images of a female and male participant exercising at 60% W max is shown in Fig. 3. During all measurements, participants maintained the same body position with their back flat against the cycle ergometer in the left lateral decubitus position with their left arm raised and resting above their head. Exercise performance was probably attenuated because of this position; however, this was not the focus of the study because we prioritized image quality at the same time as keeping participants comfortable. Left ventricular volumes including SV, LVEDV and LV end‐systolic volume (LVESV) were calculated using the Simpson's biplane method in 2D apical four‐ and two‐chamber views of the heart (Lang et al., 2015). LV SV and heart rate (from ultrasound) were multiplied to determine cardiac output. To account for sex differences in anthropometric and cardiac sizes, LV dimensions and volumes were scaled allometrically to body surface area (BSA0.5 and BSA1.5, respectively) (Dewey et al., 2008). Body surface area was determined using the Du Bois and Du Bois equation [BSA (m2)] = 0.007184 × height (cm)0.725 × weight (kg)0.425.

Respiratory flow, pressure and volume

Ventilatory variables were collected with a customized metabolic cart. Participants breathed through a customized non‐rebreathing valve (2700B; Hans Rudolph, Kansas City, MO). Inspiratory and expiratory flow was continuously measured by two independently calibrated pneumotachometers (model 3813; Hans Rudolph, Shawnee, KS, USA) located on either end of the breathing circuit. The expired pneumotach was heated to 37°C, whereas the inspired pneumotach was kept at room temperature. The measured flow from the inspiratory pneumotach was numerically integrated to determine volume, which allowed for the determination of both tidal volume and breathing frequency with their product being inspiratory ventilation. Expired ventilation was calculated from inspiratory ventilation using the Haldane transformation. Ventilation and tidal volume are expressed in body temperature pressure saturated. Mouth pressure was measured via a port in the mouthpiece connected to a calibrated differential pressure transducer (DP45‐34; Validyne Engineering, Northridge, CA, USA). The distal ends of both an oesophageal (protocols 1 and 2) and gastric (protocol 2) balloon catheter (see “Oesophageal and gastric pressure” below) were connected to separate differential pressure transducers (range = ±140 cmH2O). Mixed expired oxygen and carbon dioxide were sampled from a mixing chamber with a sample line connected to calibrated oxygen and carbon dioxide analysers (S‐3‐A/I and CD‐3Am, respectively; AEI Technologies, Bastrop, TX, USA) with the flow rate of gas sampling set to ∼175 mL min−1. Oxygen uptake (V˙O2) was determined by taking the difference between the inspired and expired oxygen using inspiratory ventilation and expiratory ventilation. Both V˙O2 and carbon dioxide output (V˙CO2) are expressed in standard pressure temperature dry. In 14 participants, V˙O2 and V˙CO2 were measured with a commercially available metabolic cart (Quark RMR‐CPET; COSMED, Blitz, Germany).

Oesophageal and gastric pressure

Participants were instrumented with an oesophageal balloon catheter in protocol 1, whereas participants were instrumented with both an oseopheageal and gastric balloon catheter in protocol 2. A topical anaesthetic (lidocaine hydrochloride; Xilocaine) was applied to the participants’ nares and nasal conchae before passing a balloon catheter (Ackrad Laboratories Inc., Cranford, NJ, USA) through the nose. One balloon was positioned in the lower third of the oesophagus, and the other balloon was position in the stomach (gastric pressure). Participants were asked to perform a brief Valsalva manoeuvre when the catheters were open to the atmosphere to empty the balloons and then 1 mL (oesophageal balloon) or 2 mL (gastric balloon) of air was administered into the balloon using a glass syringe. The validity of each balloon's position was assessed using a dynamic occlusion test with a ΔPeso:ΔPm (ratio between change in oesophageal pressure and change in mouth pressure) threshold between 0.87 and 1.13 to confirm valid balloon placement (Baydur et al., 1982) before being secured in place.

Heart rate and blood pressure

Heart rate was measured using two separate three‐lead electrocardiograms (one for ultrasound and one for the separate data acquisition system) throughout the duration of the experiment. Arterial blood pressure was obtained via photoelectric plethysmography (Human NIBP Nano Interface; ADInstruments, Colorado Springs, CO, USA). Resting measures of blood pressure were taken from an automated oscillometric sphygmomanometer placed around the upper right arm of the participant (ePM 12M; Mindray, Shenzhen, China). Blood pressure measured from the arm at rest were used to calibrate the absolute beat‐by‐beat blood pressure values from the finger during exercise. During exercise, a blood pressure cuff was placed on the participants’ middle finger of their right hand to collect beat‐by‐beat blood pressure for the duration of the exercise protocol (day 2). The physiological calibration system was turned on during exercise data collection. Prior to testing, the correct cuff size was determined using the finger cuff guide. The height correction unit was used to account for height changes of the finger and calibrated prior to use.

Data collection and analysis

Raw data were recorded at 200 Hz with a 16‐channel analogue‐to‐digital data acquisition system (PowerLab/16SP model ML 795; ADInstruments). All respiratory variables were measured continuously using open‐circuit spirometry. The mechanical W b was determined from pressure–volume loop integration using oesophageal pressure as previously described (Dominelli & Sheel, 2012). Inspiratory work was calculated using the oesophageal pressure–time product, whereas expiratory work was calculated using the gastric pressure–time product (Dominelli & Sheel, 2012). Oesophageal pressure swings were determined by calculating the difference between the peak and nadir oesophageal pressure during each breath. The W b, respiratory, heart rate and blood pressure were analysed in 30–45 s epochs at the same time the echocardiographic measurements of cardiac output were taking place.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio, version 2024.12.0+467 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Cardiovascular, ventilatory, metabolic, respiratory pressure, LV volumes indexed to BSA and control trial data were analysed using two‐way mixed factorial ANOVA with sex (male, female; between‐subjects factor) and condition (load vs. control/SPON breathing; within‐subjects factor) at each exercise intensity. Normality of residuals was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homoscedasticity with the Levene's test. When a significant sex × condition interaction was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means (EMMs; emmeans package, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). These included: (i) within‐sex comparisons of condition (SPON vs. LOAD) for males and females separately and (ii) within‐condition comparisons for sex (male vs. female) during spontaneous and loaded breathing. All post hoc tests used the Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (Figs 4, 5, 6, 7). Beat‐by‐beat changes in SV were analyse using a repeated measures one‐way ANOVA with the Greenhouse Geisser correction, followed by Bonferroni adjusted multiple comparisons.

From these models, simple slopes were estimated for each sex to determine whether the relationship between mean oesophageal pressure and each cardiac variable was significantly different from zero. The interaction term (sex × mean oesophageal pressure) was used to test whether the slopes differed significantly between males and females. All P values were derived using Satterthwaite's approximation for degrees of freedom.

Results

Participants

Demographic and baseline data are presented in Table 1, with peak exercise responses in Table 2. All participants had normal cardiovascular and pulmonary function relative to reference values. As expected, there were main effects of sex for several anthropometric, cardiac and pulmonary outcomes, with males generally larger and exhibiting greater cardiac volumes and ventilatory capacity than females (Table 1). When LV volumes were indexed to BSA1.5, the main effects of sex were no longer present (Table 1). No main effects of protocol or protocol × sex interactions were observed, indicating that the demographics of participants were not different between protocols. Similarly, during peak exercise, males had a higher V˙O2, V˙CO2, ventilation, tidal volume and work rate compared to females (Table 2). Similar to the demographics, no main effects of protocol or protocol × sex interactions were observed. Left‐lateral semi‐recumbent cycling can elicit a lower maximal V˙O2 compared to upright cycling (Astrand & Saltin, 1961) and thus the reported values are presented as V˙O2 peak. Nevertheless, all participants achieved maximal effort as indicated by respiratory exchange ratios (RER) above 1.10 during day 1 testing. Protocol 1 (inspiratory loading)

Oesophageal pressure and work of breathing

Inspiratory loading elevated mouth pressure, resulting in greater oesophageal pressure swings and higher W b (Fig. 4A and C ), accompanied by a more negative mean oesophageal pressure (Fig. 4B ). Ventilation was unchanged between conditions (Fig. 4D ). The main effects of condition were consistent across both exercise intensities (all P < 0.0001). A main effect of sex was observed at 30% W max (average absolute watts: females = 54 W; males = 80 W), with males exhibiting higher W b and ventilation compared to females (P = 0.016 and P = 0.050, respectively). No interaction effect was found for any variable.

Cardiovascular responses to inspiratory muscle loading

At 30% W max, cardiac output was higher in males than females (P = 0.003) (Fig. 5A ). SV was greater in males at both 75 W and 30% W max (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5B ), whereas females exhibited a higher heart rate at 75 W (P = 0.0002) (Fig. 5C ). LVEDV was also greater in males at both intensities (P = 0.0003) (Fig. 5D ), with LVESV higher in males at 75 W (P = 0.031) (Fig. 5E ). An interaction was observed in mean arterial pressure (MAP) where females increased MAP from SPON to INS LOAD, whereas males exhibited this increase at 30% W max (interaction P = 0.019) (Fig. 5F ). When LV volumes were allometrically scaled to BSA1.5, the previously observed main effects of sex were no longer present. No main or interaction effects were observed when LV volumes were scaled to BSA1.5 (all P ≥ 0.101) (Table 3).

Control trials

The control trials involved 10–12 min of continuous cycling with spontaneous breathing, and no loading intervention was applied. During the absolute intensity control trials (75 W), no main effects of condition or condition × sex interactions were observed for oesophageal pressure swings, mean oesophageal pressure or W b (all P ≥ 0.092). Ventilation was higher in females than males (main effect of sex, P = 0.045). SV and LVEDV were greater in males compared to females (both P = 0.001), with males also exhibiting higher LVESV (P = 0.023). Heart rate was higher in females than males (P = 0.001), whereas MAP was not different between conditions or sexes (P ≥ 0.355).

For the relative intensity control trials (30% W max), no main effects of condition or condition × sex interactions were observed for oesophageal pressure swings, mean oesophageal pressure or ventilation (all P ≥ 0.084). However, W b was higher in males than females (P = 0.042). Cardiac output, SV and LVEDV were greater in males (all P ≤ 0.001), whereas LVESV was higher in males (P = 0.050). Females demonstrated higher heart rate compared to males (P = 0.005). MAP did not differ between conditions or sexes (P ≥ 0.175). When LV volumes were allometrically scaled to BSA1.5, the previously observed main effects of sex were no longer present. No main or interaction effects were observed when LV volumes were scaled to BSA1.5 (all P ≥ 0.325).

Mixed expired gases

At the absolute intensity trial (75 W) with inspiratory loaded breathing, V˙O2 was higher during INS LOAD compared to SPON breathing (P = 0.001), with no differences observed for V˙CO2 or RER (all P ≥ 0.110). Importantly, the absolute V˙O2 at 75 W did not differ between males and females (P = 0.201). During the absolute intensity control trials, V˙O2, V˙CO2 and RER were unchanged between conditions or sexes (all P ≥ 0.085). During the relative intensity trial (30% W max) with inspiratory loaded breathing, V˙O2 was greater during INS LOAD compared to spontaneous breathing (P = 0.001) and was higher in males than females (P = 0.001). Similarly, males exhibited greater V˙CO2 than females across conditions (P = 0.001). The RER was unchanged by condition or sex (all P ≥ 0.533). For the relative intensity control trials, V˙O2 and V˙CO2 were greater in males (both P = 0.001), whereas RER did not differ between conditions or sexes (P ≥ 0.462).

Protocol 2 (expiratory loading)

Oesophageal pressure, gastric pressure and work of breathing

At 100 W, the magnitude of oesophageal pressure swings increased during expiratory loading (main effect of condition, P < 0.0001), with females exhibiting greater swings than males (main effect of sex, P = 0.003). At 60% W max (average absolute watts: females = 106 W; males = 152 W), oesophageal pressure swings were elevated during loading regardless of sex (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6A ). Mean oesophageal pressure and mean gastric pressure were more positive during EXP LOAD at both intensities (all P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6B and C ). Work of breathing increased from SPON to EXP LOAD at both 100 W and 60% W max (both P < 0.0001). At 100 W, females had higher W b than males (P = 0.035), whereas, at 60% W max, males demonstrated greater W b (P = 0.006) (Fig. 6D ). Ventilation showed a main effect of sex at both intensities, with females higher than males at 100 W and males higher than females at 60% W max (P = 0.050, P = 0.0002, respectively) (Fig. 6E ). No interaction effects were found for any variable.

Cardiovascular responses to expiratory muscle loading

Cardiac output decreased with expiratory loading at both intensities (main effect of condition, both P < 0.0001) (Fig. 7A ). SV decreased during loading, with an interaction at 100 W (SV reduced in both sexes) and at 60% W max (interaction: P = 0.050; condition and sex effects, both P ≤ 0.005) (Fig. 7B ). Heart rate demonstrated a main effect of sex at 100 W, with females higher than males (P < 0.0001). At 60% W max, heart rate decreased from SPON to EXP LOAD and was higher in females overall (both P < 0.001) (Fig. 7C ). LVEDV was reduced with loading at 100 W (condition and sex effects, both P < 0.0001) and, at 60% W max, females showed a reduction whereas males did not (interaction, P = 0.006) (Fig. 7D ). LVESV decreased only in females at both intensities (interaction, both P ≤ 0.050) (Fig. 7E ). Mean arterial pressure increased from SPON to EXP LOAD at both 100 W and 60% W max (main effect of condition, both P = 0.0003) (Fig. 7F ).

Table 3 displays allometrically scaled LV volumes to BSA1.5, which yielded similar results to absolute values; however, all main effects of sex were abolished (all P ≥ 0.168). SV index at 100 W decreased from SPON to LOAD (main effect of condition: P < 0.0001), at 60% W max, females observed a reduction from SPON to LOAD, whereas males did not (P = 0.008). The reduction in SV index observed in females was the result of a reduction in LVEDV index at both 100 W and 60% W max (P = 0.007 and P = 0.010, respectively), whereas no change was observed in males from SPON to LOAD (both P ≥ 0.129). LVESV index decreased in females from SPON to LOAD at 100 W and 60% W max (both P ≤ 0.049) with no change observed in males (both P ≥ 0.590).

Beat‐by‐beat SV

To assess the temporal onset of the cardiovascular response to expiratory loading, beat‐by‐beat SV was examined during the transition from SPON to LOAD in a subset of 10 participants. SV decreased within one to two heart beats following application of the expiratory load and remained reduced over subsequent heart beats at both the 100 W and 60% W max exercise intensities (both P < 0.0001).

Control trials

The control trials involved 10–12 min of continuous cycling with spontaneous breathing, and no loading intervention was applied. During the absolute intensity control trial (100 W), there were no main effects of condition or condition × sex interactions for oesophageal pressure, gastric pressure, W b, ventilation or metabolic variables (all P ≥ 0.089). For cardiac outcomes, males exhibited greater SV, LVEDV and LVESV compared to females (all P = 0.001), whereas females demonstrated higher heart rate (P = 0.001). Cardiac output and MAP were unchanged between conditions and sexes (all P ≥ 0.091).

During the relative intensity control trial (60% W max), no main effects of condition or condition × sex interactions were observed for mean oesophageal or gastric pressures, ventilation (all P ≥ 0.119). Males exhibited greater oesophageal pressure swings and higher W b than females (P = 0.014 and P = 0.002, respectively). For cardiovascular variables, males demonstrated greater cardiac output, SV, LVEDV and LVESV than females (all P ≤ 0.050), whereas females exhibited higher heart rates (P = 0.023). MAP did not differ between conditions or sexes (P = 0.804).

When LV volumes were allometrically scaled to BSA1.5, the previously observed main effects of sex were no longer present. No main or interaction effects were observed when LV volumes were scaled to BSA1.5 (all P ≥ 0.130).

Mixed expired gases

At 100 W, V˙O2 was higher during expiratory loaded compared to spontaneous breathing (P = 0.001), with no differences observed for V˙CO2 or RER (all P ≥ 0.202). The absolute V˙O2 demand at 100 W was not different between males and females (P = 0.110). During the absolute intensity control trials, V˙O2, V˙CO2 and RER were unchanged between conditions or sexes (all P ≥ 0.069). At 60% W max, V˙O2 was greater during expiratory loaded compared to spontaneous breathing (P = 0.001) and was higher in males than females (P = 0.001). V˙CO2 was also greater in males compared to females (P = 0.001). The RER was unaffected by condition or sex (all P ≥ 0.126). During the relative intensity control trials, V˙O2 and V˙CO2 were higher in males compared to females (both P = 0.001), with no change in RER between sexes or conditions (P ≥ 0.185).

Integrative influence of respiratory muscle loading on cardiovascular variables

Figure 8 shows the relationships between mean oesophageal pressure and cardiac output (Fig. 8A and B ) and SV (Fig. 8C and D ). At 100 W, both male and female cardiac output slopes were significantly different from zero, with the female and male slopes being different from each other suggesting females had a steeper decline compared with males (P = 0.045) (Fig. 8A ). At 60% W max, both sexes again demonstrated reductions in cardiac output, with females showing a greater decrease (P = 0.005) (Fig. 8B ). For SV, slopes at 100 W were significantly different from zero in both sexes but did not differ between them (P = 0.262) (Fig. 8C ). At 60% W max, both sexes showed reductions in SV, with a steeper slope in females (P = 0.009) (Fig. 8D ). Collectively, inspiratory loading (more negative mean oesophageal pressure) did not alter cardiac output or SV, whereas expiratory loading (increasing mean oesophageal pressure towards positive values) led to greater reductions, particularly in females.

Figure 9 presents the corresponding regressions for left ventricular volumes. At 100 W, both sexes demonstrated significant decreases in LVEDV with increasing mean oesophageal pressure (expiratory loading), with females showing a greater reduction than males (P = 0.050) (Fig. 9A ). At 60% W max, only females exhibited significant reductions in LVEDV, again with a steeper slope than males (P = 0.002) (Fig. 9B ). For LVESV, females demonstrated significant decreases with more positive oesophageal pressures at both intensities, whereas no change was observed in males (Fig. 9C and D). Overall, inspiratory loading did not affect LVEDV or LVESV, but expiratory loading reduced LVEDV in both sexes at 100 W and only in females at 60% W max. LVESV decreased with expiratory loading at both exercise intensities in females only.

Allometrically scaling LV volumes to BSA1.5 did not change the findings of Figs 8 and 9 because the same relationships were also observed when LV volumes were indexed to BSA1.5.

To integrate across protocols, Fig. 10 illustrates combined data from the 13 participants who completed both inspiratory and expiratory loading (protocols 1 and 2). No statistical analyses were conducted on this data set. However, Fig. 10 further emphasizes our claims expressed in Figs 8 and 9 where it appears that making mean oesophageal pressure more negative (as seen with inspiratory loading) had no effect on cardiac output, SV, LVEDV or LVESV. Conversely, making oesophageal pressure more positive (as seen with expiratory loading) leads to reductions in cardiac output, SV, LVEDV and LVESV. Figure 10 qualitatively suggests that expiratory loading exerts a greater influence on cardiac volumes and output than inspiratory loading (as also evidenced by Figs 8 and 9) in participants who completed both protocols. No sex‐specific comparisons were performed for Fig. 10 as a result of the unequal and unpowered sample size.

Discussion

Major findings

The present study examined potential sex differences in the cardiac response to manipulating ITP during exercise. Three key findings emerged. First, making ITP more negative from inspiratory loading did not alter cardiac volumes and no sex differences were observed. We attribute the lack of effect to the cardiovascular responses to exercise (e.g. increased central venous pressure, myocardial contractility, heart rate and blood pressure), which may have negated the influence of more negative ITP on cardiac volumes. Second, making ITP more positive via expiratory loading elicited marked changes in cardiac variables, with clear sex‐specific responses. Specifically, both sexes demonstrated reductions in cardiac output and SV, but the underlying mechanisms differed. In males, the reduction in SV was the result of a reduced LVEDV with a maintained LVESV, whereas females experienced a reduction in both LVEDV and LVESV. Third, the cardiac volume differences observed in females with expiratory loading are indicative of a true sex effect rather than a size effect. When LV volumes were scaled to BSA1.5, cardiac volume changes with expiratory loading were no longer present in males, but remained in females. Lastly, the cardiac responses to changes in ITP depend not only on the direction of the pressure change, but also may depend on the cardiovascular system's ability to override it. Although modest reductions in ITP were well tolerated, increases in ITP imposed greater mechanical and haemodynamic constraints on ventricular filling, indicating that compensatory mechanisms in females become insufficient to maintain SV under higher loading. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that sex differences in cardiac volumes during exercise are evident when ITP is increased (less negative) above normal levels.

Inspiratory loading on cardiovascular haemodynamics

The absence of a change in cardiac volumes with inspiratory loaded breathing is contrary to our hypothesis. The rationale behind cardiopulmonary interactions during inspiration is that, as ITP becomes more negative, right atrial pressure decreases and increases the pressure gradient for venous return. The elevated venous return should translate to increases in LVEDV and SV simply through series ventricular interactions (e.g. increased blood flow to the heart should cause more blood to leave the heart) (Guyton et al., 1957; Lalande et al., 2012). However, other work performed at rest suggests a different outcome of more negative ITP. Rather than augmenting LV filling, greater negative ITP reduces LVEDV and SV (Buda et al., 1979; Cheyne et al., 2018; Robotham et al., 1978; Summer et al., 1979). The reductions in LVEDV and SV arose from direct ventricular interactions and septal flattening, where increasing right ventricular filling displaces the septum leftward, impairing LV compliance and filling (Buda et al., 1979; Brinker et al., 1980; Cheyne et al., 2016, 2018; Iliceto et al., 1988; Peters et al., 1989). At the same time, the more negative ITP elevates LV transmural pressure and wall stress, increasing afterload and further impairing LV ejection (Buda et al., 1979; Condos et al., 1987; Orban et al., 2008; Scharf et al., 1979; Virolainen & Kupari, 1993). Recent work suggests that inspiratory loading generating oesophageal pressures of −20 to −25 cmH2O significantly reduce LVEDV and SV in young, healthy resting individuals, coinciding with marked leftward septal displacement (Cheyne et al., 2018). These findings suggest that RV filling may continue to increase with more negative ITP, but the resulting septal shift impairs LV filling and subsequently ejection. Moreover, investigations of the cardiac effects of performing Mueller manoeuvres to −30 cmH2O found similar reductions in LVEDV and SV (Wright et al., 2023) and are also consistent with other studies (Brinker et al., 1980; Buda et al., 1979; Iliceto et al., 1988). Collectively, these studies reinforce the idea that, although the initial expectation from inspiratory loading is an increase in venous return and LVEDV, when ITP becomes sufficiently negative, direct ventricular interactions and increased afterload dominate, resulting in net reductions in LVEDV, SV and cardiac output. Another important consideration is the influence of lung volume on the pulmonary vasculature. When lung volume increases above functional residual capacity, alveolar pressures increase and compress alveolar vessels, whereas extra‐alveolar vessels distend. Because alveolar vessel compression has the dominant effect, pulmonary vascular resistance increases substantially (Whittenberger et al., 1960), subsequently increasing right ventricular afterload. Elevated lung volumes can also store a greater portion of the pulmonary blood volume, reducing right atrial filling (Steingrub et al., 2003). Together, increased right ventricular afterload and reduced left atrial filling lead to decreases in LVEDV and SV.

The absence of cardiac volume changes with inspiratory loading might be explained by several physiological mechanisms that may have countered the expected cardiovascular consequences of more negative ITP. First, previous work has largely been conducted at rest, whereas the present study involved submaximal exercise. The enhanced cardiac function and blood flow redistribution lead to a proportional increase in venous return, SV, cardiac output and arterial pressure to meet the elevated metabolic demand of exercise. These changes most probably augmented LV function by preserving LVESV and SV despite the added inspiratory load. These mechanisms may explain why more negative ITP alters SV at rest but not during exercise (Harms et al., 1998). Importantly, the study by Harms et al. (1998) was conducted at maximal exercise, comprising a condition under which SV is probably near its physiological upper limit, thus limiting the capacity for additional modulation by inspiratory loading. As such, any inspiratory loading induced changes in ventricular filling or ejection would be expected to have minimal impact on SV under conditions of maximal exercise. Our findings align with this previous work that reported no change in SV or cardiac output during inspiratory muscle loading, although this investigation did not assess LV volumes because cardiac output was determined via the direct Fick method (Harms et al., 1998). Second, the skeletal muscle pump plays a dominant role promoting venous return during exercise by generating rhythmic increases in venous pressure that propel blood centrally. The elevated venous return and central venous pressure during exercise, combined with increases in heart rate and myocardial contractility, probably overrode or masked any additional influence of more negative ITP. Therefore, it is possible that inspiratory loading in the present study increased venous return but, because the compensatory mechanisms were already active to accommodate the elevated blood flow, these mechanisms most probably buffered any additional increase in venous return, resulting in no overall change to cardiac volumes. This concept is analogous to that of right atrial pacing, which produces little additional change in SV during maximal exercise (Munch et al., 2014). Taken together, these findings suggest that, although large sustained negative ITP in isolated conditions can reduce cardiac volumes, the dynamic environment of exercise mitigated these effects, resulting in no measurable change in LVEDV, SV or cardiac output with inspiratory loading.

We initially hypothesized that females may exhibit greater cardiovascular responses to inspiratory loading as a result of their elevated oesophageal pressures associated with higher W b and greater ventilatory demands during exercise. We reasoned that, if oesophageal pressure swings contribute to venous return, females may rely more heavily on this mechanism, potentially exaggerating sex‐specific differences in cardiac filling and output. Inspiratory loading during low‐intensity exercise increased oesophageal pressure swings and W b to a similar extent in males and females, resulting in no measurable differences in cardiac volumes or output.

Expiratory loading on cardiovascular haemodynamics

Expiratory loading led to an overall reduction in SV and cardiac output in both males and females, consistent with the expected haemodynamic consequences of elevated intrathoracic pressure. When ITP becomes less negative (more positive), right atrial pressure increases, diminishing the pressure gradient for venous return and thereby reducing LVEDV. More positive ITP can also reduce the diastolic transmural pressure gradient across the left ventricle, directly limiting LV filling and further contributing to the reduction in LVEDV. Together, these mechanisms probably both contribute to the reduction in SV and cardiac output during expiratory loading. This interpretation aligns with previous work demonstrating that expiratory loading decreases SV and cardiac output at rest and during exercise (Lalande et al., 2012; Stark‐Leyva et al., 2004; Tyberg et al., 2000; Wright et al., 2025). Previous work in exercising dogs have shown that modest increases in expiratory intrathoracic pressure can cause immediate reductions in SV and cardiac output (Miller et al., 2006). These rapid declines were attributed to the mechanical constraints on cardiac filling, including a reduction in the diastolic transmural pressure gradient and a loss of central blood volume during loaded expiration (Miller et al., 2006). Consistent with these findings, beat‐by‐beat analysis in a subset of participants in the present study found that SV decreased within the first one to two heart beats following application of the expiratory load and remained reduced over subsequent heart beats.

In the present study, the reduction in SV reflected a decrease in LVEDV in both sexes, together with a decrease in LVESV that was observed only in females. Thus, the sex‐specific differences in cardiac function during expiratory loading appear to be driven primarily by changes in LVESV in females. In the setting of reduced venous return and preload, females seem to engage compensatory mechanisms that lower LVESV in an effort to preserve SV. A potential explanation relates to intrinsic sex differences in LV twist mechanics. With reductions in preload, females exhibit greater LV twist and faster untwisting velocity than males (Williams et al., 2016). In the present study, both sexes experienced a significant reduction in LVEDV with expiratory loading; however, only females had a reduced LVESV, indicating an enhanced ability to eject blood. Moreover, the observed reduction in LVESV in females may reflect elevated LV twist or more favourable ventricular–vascular coupling because females generally have lower arterial elastance at rest and during exercise (Najjar et al., 2004), which reduces afterload and promotes LV ejection. Despite these compensations, SV still decreased in both sexes. The effect of reduced venous return and LVEDV under more positive ITP outweighed the female‐specific reductions in LVESV, resulting in an overall decline in SV. Taken together, expiratory loading unmasks sex‐specific differences in cardiac mechanics, yet the dominant effect remains the mechanical limitation of venous return imposed by more positive ITP.

Cardiac volume reductions reflect a sex effect rather than a size effect

When LV volumes were allometrically scaled to BSA1.5, the previously observed main effects of sex in the absolute data were abolished, indicating that the larger cardiac volumes in males primarily reflect differences in body size rather than intrinsic cardiac function. By contrast, the main effects of condition remained, and new interaction effects emerged, demonstrating that only females experienced significant reductions in SV index, LVEDV index and LVESV index with expiratory loading, whereas no changes were observed in males (Table 3). These findings indicate that the cardiac volume reductions observed with expiratory loading are not a size‐driven phenomenon but instead represent a true, sex‐specific physiological response. The continuation of these reductions after normalization to BSA1.5 suggests that females experience a greater relative decline in LV filling and ejection volumes than would be expected based on body size, consistent with a physiological limitation in preload reserve or ventricular compliance rather than geometric scaling effects. Thus, scaling to BSA1.5 strengthens our interpretation that expiratory loading elicits a sex difference in cardiac function rather than one attributable to size (Table 3). Moreover, consistent with the absolute LV volume findings, no changes were observed with inspiratory loading after normalization to BSA1·5 (Table 3), supporting that the observed effects are specific to increased ITP associated with expiratory loading.

Cardiovascular responses to manipulating intrathoracic pressure

A unique aspect of the present study is the integration of both inspiratory and expiratory loading protocols to examine the influence of ITP across a continuum (Figs 8 and 9). Rather than viewing inspiratory and expiratory loading as separate interventions, these conditions should be conceptualized along a continuum of oesophageal pressure. Inspiratory loading makes ITP more negative, whereas expiratory loading makes it less negative (more positive), a continuum that should be considered despite the differing exercise intensity stimulus across protocols. When oesophageal pressure became more negative (during inspiratory loading), there was no relationship between mean oesophageal pressure and cardiac volumes. We interpret this to mean that, during exercise, the effects of large negative ITP swings are probably washed out within the integrated haemodynamic adjustments that accompany exercise.

A subset of participants completed both protocols, allowing us to directly compare the influence of inspiratory and expiratory loading within the same individuals (Fig. 10). These data mirrored the regression analyses in Figs 8 and 9, reinforcing that increasing negative ITP had no influence on cardiac volumes, whereas making ITP less negative (more positive) resulted in clear reductions in cardiac output, SV, LVEDV and LVESV. This asymmetry highlights an important physiological point: during inspiratory loading, the heart is still given sufficient preload to maintain function, aided by heightened skeletal muscle pump activity during exercise. By contrast, expiratory loading exerts a more powerful limitation on venous return, such that the heart is not given the opportunity to preserve its function because right‐sided filling is directly constrained. The resulting reductions in LVEDV and SV therefore reflect a true preload limitation that cannot be compensated by exercise‐induced inotropy or chronotropy. Although the interventions used here were artificial, they accentuate mechanisms that occur during spontaneous breathing, where normal fluctuations in ITP continuously modulate venous return and cardiac function. As such, these findings highlight the central role of ITP in governing cardiopulmonary interactions and reveal that the physiological consequences are not symmetric across the ITP spectrum, with more positive pressures exerting greater constraints on cardiac function than more negative pressures.

Technical considerations

There are several technical considerations that warrant discussion. First, all trials were conducted in the left lateral decubitus position to optimize echocardiographic imaging. Although this approach enhanced image quality, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to upright exercise. Second, beat‐by‐beat blood pressure was measured using photoelectric plethysmography, a method that can be sensitive to motion artefacts and dependent on finger perfusion. To minimize error, participants were instructed to restrict upper limb movement, and their hand was supported and warmed when necessary. Third, participants could not be blinded to the intervention because the sensation of breathing through a resistor was obvious, and investigators provided verbal feedback to ensure maintenance of breathing patterns throughout the duration of the exercise trials. Fourth, end‐expiratory lung volume was not measured during the expiratory loading trials to avoid disrupting breathing patterns and altering intrathoracic pressure, which could have influenced the cardiac data. Although expiratory loading can increase end‐expiratory lung volume and thereby affect pulmonary vascular compliance and right ventricular afterload, we aimed to minimize this by instructing participants to breathe out forcefully during expiration when the load was applied. This approach reduced participant discomfort and helped preserve echocardiographic image quality because high lung volumes can degrade cardiac imaging windows and reduce heart visibility.

Perspectives

The inspiratory and expiratory loading conditions used in the present study provide insight into how ITP influences cardiac volumes during exercise. The negative ITPs elicited during inspiratory loading reflect those that are typically generated during high intensity exercise but were applied at submaximal exercise intensities to allow for an accurate assessment of ventricular volumes at the same time as maintaining high quality echocardiographic image acquisition. Likewise, the expiratory loads applied in the present study approximate the positive ITPs experienced by individuals with expiratory flow limitation, a condition in which the airways cannot generate additional expiratory flow despite increased effort, leading to sustained elevations in expiratory pressure. Taken together, the present findings provide a physiological framework for understanding how exaggerated negative and positive ITP swings, whether occurring during high intensity exercise or in individuals with expiratory flow limitation, can alter cardiac filling and ejection.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that manipulating ITP during exercise produces sex‐dependent cardiac effects. Specifically, making ITP more negative with inspiratory loading led to no measurable change in LV volumes, with no differing responses in males and females. By contrast, making ITP less negative with expiratory loading produced marked reductions in SV and cardiac output, driven primarily by a fall in LVEDV that probably reflects a reduced pressure gradient for venous return and subsequently lower preload. In both sexes with expiratory loading, LVEDV decreased; however, a reduction in LVESV occurred only in females. When LV volumes were allometrically scaled to BSA1.5, cardiac volume changes were only observed in females and not males. We attribute the female‐specific reduction in cardiac volumes (when scaled to BSA1.5) and decrease in LVESV (absolute values) as potentially arising from differences in ventricular mechanics or ventricular–vascular coupling. When considered across the spectrum of ITP manipulation, any potential cardiac volume changes with more negative shifts in ITP appear to be masked by the normal cardiovascular responses to exercise, whereas positive shifts in ITP impose a true preload limitation that cannot be fully compensated. Overall, our findings suggest a sex‐specific cardiac response when ITP is increased above normal levels during exercise and highlight the expiratory phase as a significant constraint on cardiac filling and ejection.
