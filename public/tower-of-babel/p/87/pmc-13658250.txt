Hydrophobic association and ionic coordination synergistically enhance a multifunctional hydrogel for high-performance wearable strain sensing
Authors: Jiayi Fu, ChengRui Jiang, Wanping Xiang, Guangtao Chang, Ruoxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible hydrogel-based strain sensors have attracted increasing attention for applications in wearable electronics and health monitoring due to their tissue-like mechanical properties and electrochemical responsiveness. However, most existing hydrogels suffer from mechanical brittleness, environmental instability, and humidity-sensitive conductivity, which limit their use in dynamic human motion detection. This study presents a high-performance flexible strain sensor based on PALCC hydrogel, which integrates hydrophobic association, ionic coordination, and deep eutectic solvent (DES) to enhance both mechanical and electrical properties. The hydrogel demonstrates exceptional mechanical strength (296.4 kPa), high elongation (434.6%), and stable electrical conductivity, making it ideal for wearable electronics and health monitoring applications. The synergistic interactions within the hydrogel network, including covalent bonding, ionic crosslinking, and hydrophobic associations, enable a two-stage linear strain response with outstanding durability (over 2000 cycles). Molecular dynamics simulations further elucidate the dynamic co-aggregation and strengthening mechanisms of the network. The sensor shows excellent dual-mode strain sensing under both tensile and compressive deformations, stable signal output for human motion and vocal vibration detection, and the capability to transmit information through Morse code via fingertip pressure. These results provide a promising solution for real-time health monitoring and wearable sensor applications, paving the way for future advancements in flexible strain-sensing technologies.

Introduction

In recent years, flexible hydrogel-based strain sensors have emerged as an innovative research direction in wearable electronics and intelligent healthcare due to their combination of tissue-like mechanical properties and electrochemical responsiveness1–3. With the global incidence of sports injuries and chronic musculoskeletal disorders rising year by year, the development of forward-thinking systems capable of real-time monitoring of human motion has become crucial for reducing risks, improving health management, and lowering healthcare costs4. However, existing commercial sensors often face challenges, such as mismatched rigid interfaces with biological tissues, poor environmental adaptability, and signal latency, which makes it difficult to meet the dual requirements of mechanical adaptability and high sensitivity in high-intensity motion environments5. Therefore, the development of sensors with excellent flexibility, sensitivity, and stability has become a core issue that needs to be addressed in this field6. In this context, hydrogels, with their high-water content, tunable conductivity, and favorable three-dimensional network structure, have become ideal interface materials for connecting the human body with electronic devices7–9.

Unfortunately, despite the promising application of hydrogels in flexible strain sensors, their mechanical brittleness, poor environmental stability, and the humidity sensitivity of their ionic conductive networks significantly limit their practical use in high-dynamic human motion monitoring10–12. Researchers have proposed various innovative strategies to address these issues. In terms of mechanical enhancement, dual-network interpenetrating (such as PVA-PAA13,14) and semi-interpenetrating (such as PVA-CS15,16) topological structures have significantly improved the fracture elongation and fatigue resistance of hydrogels. By introducing a secondary network layer, these hydrogels are able to maintain stability under a broader range of strains17. Salt-induced and Hofmeister effects have been shown to enhance the material toughness and self-adaptive ability in dynamic environments18,19. These effects strengthen the hydrogel performance in dynamic conditions by adjusting ionic strength and hydrogen bond density20. Additionally, the mechanical properties of hydrogels can be further enhanced by designing copolymers with hydrophobic regions21 or incorporating nanoparticles22. While these strategies have improved the mechanical robustness of hydrogels to some extent, they still face challenges such as increased rigidity due to excessive densification, which can lead to local fracture or deformation limitations23.

In terms of electrical enhancement, the incorporation of conductive fillers such as carbon nanotube (CNT)24–26 and transition metal carbides, nitrides, or carbonitrides (MXene)27,28 has significantly improved the conductivity of hydrogels, while micro-nano structural designs (such as nano-cone arrays, micro-structured surfaces, and photolithography techniques) have been employed to enhance the sensitivity of resistance change29–32. These structures optimize the electron transport pathways within the hydrogel, reduce electrical signal noise, and improve the accuracy of sensor responses. However, these conductive nanofillers are often hydrophobic and prone to aggregation due to van der Waals forces33, necessitating the use of dispersants34 or grafting treatments35. Without these, the incompatibility between the nanofillers and the hydrogel matrix would affect the long-term electrical stability and reproducibility. In terms of environmental stability, ionic liquids36, highly hygroscopic salts37,38, and organic solvents39 have been used to improve the hydrogel’s antifreeze and anti-drying properties. However, these additives often exhibit certain biological toxicity, limiting their application in biomedical and wearable devices. In recent years, deep eutectic solvents (DES), as a new type of green solvent, have gradually become a research focus. DES can maintain the ion mobility of hydrogels at low temperatures and improve their performance in extreme environments, but their effects on hydrogels still need to be optimized to ensure stability and biocompatibility during long-term use.

This work presents a simple preparation method for wearable strain/pressure hydrogel sensors based on a polyvinyl alcohol/polyacrylic acid (PVA-PAA) dual-network and hydrophobic association structure. The PVA-PAA dual-network structure provides a solid framework for the hydrogel sensor, enhancing its mechanical performance, while the hydrophobic association structure further improves the hydrogel’s compressive and tensile strength by forming microscopic hydrophobic regions. These phenomena are supported by molecular dynamics simulations. Additionally, the incorporation of chitosan (CS) enhances the hydrogel’s structural topology and flexibility, making it more suitable for wearable applications. At the same time, the DES composed of glycerol and choline chloride provides strong ionic conductivity and environmental stability. The hydrogel sensor exhibits excellent mechanical properties, conductivity, environmental stability, and high-quality sensing performance, making it suitable for human motion signal recognition and output. It can also meet the demands of long-term high-intensity motion monitoring, offering new technological insights for the further development of flexible wearable sensors.

Figure 1c and d schematically illustrates the microstructural evolution of the PALCC hydrogel during the high-temperature crosslinking process. In the initial stage, the precursor components were uniformly distributed after sufficient agitation. As the crosslinking temperature increased, PVA and PAA first formed a preliminary network through hydrogen bonding, while partial esterification between hydroxyl and carboxyl groups established stable covalent crosslinking points. Then, LA segments began to aggregate via hydrophobic associations, and electrostatic interaction between CTAB and the hydrophilic PAA chains progressively strengthened the network. Meanwhile, the amino groups (–NH2) of CS interacted with the carboxyl groups (–COOH) of PAA through ionic crosslinking or amide formation, further densifying the network structure. Ultimately, a multiscale three-dimensional network stabilized by covalent, ionic, and hydrophobic associations was established, leading to a robust gelation process. The specific intermolecular interactions within the fully crosslinked PALCC hydrogel are illustrated in Fig. 1d.

The dynamic light scattering (DLS) results show that, during the high-temperature crosslinking process, the three hydrogel systems exhibited distinctly different network evolution behaviors (Fig. 2a–c). In the PA system, the crosslinking primarily relied on hydrogen bonding interactions and polymer chain entanglement between PAA and PVA13. The particle size increased monotonically over time and exhibited a unimodal distribution, indicating that the network was composed of uniform covalently linked chain segments (Fig. 2a)40. After the addition of LA and CTAB, the PALC hydrogel exhibited the formation of stable micellar cores. The P(AA-co-LA) chains polymerized on the surface of the micelles, leading to the formation of hydrophobic micelle crosslinking points and ion association between PAA and CTAB. As a result, a multi-scale structure consisting of “covalent main network-hydrophobic micellar network-ionic composite network” was established41,42. This system showed a bimodal particle size distribution, although the peaks were narrow (Fig. 2b). The introduction of CS further enhanced the network coupling, as the strong electrostatic interactions between -NH3+/-COO- and multiple hydrogen bonds significantly improved the crosslinking density43. CS chains acted as bridging agents, promoting secondary parallel and copolymerization between the micelles. Consequently, larger micelle clusters formed in the PALCC system, confirming the occurrence of cooperative copolymerization and hierarchical crosslinking within the system (Fig. 2c).Fig. 2Molecular size changes during high-temperature crosslinking.a PA, (b) PALC, (c) PALCC.

Parameter optimization of PALCC hydrogel

To enhance the overall performance of the PALCC hydrogel, key preparation parameters were systematically optimized. First, the influence of mass ratio between PVA and AA on the mechanical and electrical properties of the hydrogels was investigated. As shown in Fig. 3a, both the tensile strength and elongation at break of the hydrogel initially increased and then decreased with the rising PVA-to-AA ratio. The optimal mechanical performance was achieved at a ratio of 2:8. Mechanism studies have shown that, an appropriate amount of PVA forms a hydrogen-bonded network through hydroxyl groups, serving as the mechanical backbone that dissipates stress through chain slippage under external loading. However, when the PVA content exceeded 30%, excessive crystallization and hydrogen bonding sites caused the network to become overly compact and rigid, while the reduced AA content weakened the dynamic reversible interactions, leading to a decline in mechanical properties. On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 3b, the electrical conductivity of PALCC hydrogels increased significantly with higher AA content. This enhancement can be attributed to the increased concentration of ionic charge carriers generated by the dissociation of carboxyl groups in AA, which facilitates the formation of continuous ionic transport pathways, thereby improving the overall conductivity44.Fig. 3Optimization of preparation parameters for PALCC hydrogels.Effects of the mass ratio of PVA to PAA on the (a) mechanical properties and (b) electrical conductivity of the hydrogels; c effect of LA content on the mechanical properties; Effects of CTAB content on the (d) mechanical properties and (e) electrical conductivity; f Effect of CS content on the mechanical properties of the hydrogels.

Optimization of preparation parameters for PALCC hydrogels.

As shown in Fig. 3c, the incorporation of LA significantly improved the mechanical performance. As a typical hydrophobic monomer, LA introduces dynamic hydrophobic associations that enhance energy dissipation and reinforce the network structure, resulting in improved strength and toughness of the hydrogel. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 3d and e, the addition of CTAB in the range of 0.2-0.4 g not only strengthened the network through hydrophobic associations but also enhanced electrical conductivity through cationic migration45. Considering both mechanical and electrical performance, 0.4 g was determined to be the optimal CTAB dosage. In addition, CS participated in the formation of hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic complexation within the PVA/AA system. At a CS content of 0.75 g, the hydrogel network became the most compact, yielding optimal tensile strength and elongation at break values of 296.4 kPa and 434.6%, respectively (Fig. 3f).

Compositional modulation of hydrogel structure and performance

To investigate the influence of hydrophobic associative components and CS on the microstructure and performance of PALCC hydrogels, three types of hydrogels—PA, PALC, and PALCC—were prepared and systematically analyzed. As shown in Fig. 4a, the cross-sectional morphology of the PA hydrogel exhibited a smooth and compact structure without distinct porous features, suggesting a limited energy dissipation mechanism. Upon the introduction of LA and CTAB, the PALC hydrogel developed a uniform three-dimensional porous network. This ordered phase-separated structure, induced by hydrophobic associations and surfactant-directed alignment, effectively facilitated stress transfer throughout the network. Notably, the incorporation of CS led to the formation of a hierarchical network structure in the PALCC hydrogel, characterized by mesopores formed through hydrophobic association microdomains and fine microcracks originating from CS chain interactions. These microcracks are attributed to the strong ionic pairing and hydrogen bonding introduced by CS, which increased network rigidity and caused greater shrinkage during freeze-drying, resulting in crack formation. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 4b) further confirmed the synergistic interactions among the hydrogel components. The characteristic peaks at 1045 cm-1 and 1637 cm-1 correspond to the C-O stretching of PVA and the C = C stretching of AA, respectively. Compared to the C-O stretching vibration peak of PVA at 1093 cm-1, the C-O peak in the three hydrogels shifts to a lower frequency. This indicates that PVA and AA form the basic network structure in the hydrogel through hydrogen bonding and ionic crosslinking. The peak at 2933 cm-1 is associated with the C-H stretching vibrations of methyl and methylene groups. The additional absorption peaks at 1190 cm-1 and 1733 cm-1 in the PALC and PALCC hydrogels are attributed to the C-O-C and C = O stretching vibrations from LA, confirming that these components introduced hydrophobic crosslinking sites and enhanced the crosslinked network structure. Moreover, the broadening of the peak in the range of 3409-3438 cm-1 can be ascribed to the disruption of the original hydrogen bonds46,47. SAXS analysis further quantified the structural order (Fig. 4c). The PA hydrogel exhibited low scattering intensity, indicating a relatively homogeneous internal structure. In contrast, the increased scattering intensity of PALC and PALCC hydrogels reflected the formation of nanoscale ordered domains. The characteristic variation observed in the PALCC scattering profile may originate from additional electron density fluctuations caused by CS incorporation, which is consistent with the polysaccharide-enhanced scattering phenomena reported by Lan et al48. In addition, SAXS analysis was employed to probe the evolution of nanoscale structural heterogeneity rather than to extract precise geometric parameters, given the multicomponent and dynamically crosslinked nature of the hydrogel network.

Excellent mechanical flexibility and resilience are crucial for flexible sensing devices to withstand repeated deformations. As shown in Fig. 4d, the PALCC hydrogel demonstrated outstanding stretchability, compressibility, and shape recovery under macroscopic mechanical tests. It could be stretched from an initial length of 30 mm to 90 mm without fracture and rapidly recovered its shape after compression. Furthermore, the hydrogel tolerated complex deformations such as bending, twisting, and folding without structural damage, exhibiting remarkable flexibility and structural stability. As shown in Fig. 4e, when employed as a flexible circuit switch, the PALCC hydrogel successfully powered an LED bulb. The circuit responded promptly to connection and disconnection states, indicating excellent electrical conductivity and stable signal response, thereby demonstrating its potential application in flexible electronic devices.

The micellar hydrophobic and ionic composite network formed by LA and CTAB significantly enhanced the mechanical strength of the PALC hydrogel, while the incorporation of CS introduced flexible polymeric segments that increased the elongation at break of the PALCC hydrogel. However, the introduction of more flexible chains also reduced the density of hydrophobic micelles, leading to a slight decrease in tensile strength. The stepwise tensile tests (10-100% strain) of PALCC hydrogels, shown in Fig. 5c, revealed pronounced hysteresis loops that expanded with increasing strain, indicating a significant energy dissipation mechanism during deformation. Interestingly, even under 100% strain, the PALCC hydrogel maintained good deformation reversibility, with a relatively small hysteresis area and no noticeable plastic collapse. This behavior demonstrates the coexistence of high elasticity and toughness, attributed to the multiscale network architecture formed through dual “chemical crosslinking” and “physical association” mechanisms. Quantitative analysis of this energy dissipation behavior (Fig. 5d) further confirmed that PALCC could effectively redistribute external stress through dynamic bond breaking/reformation and molecular chain slippage, thereby preventing irreversible structural damage. The cyclic tensile tests (Fig. 5e, f) also demonstrated the excellent fatigue resistance and reversibility of PALCC, confirming its structural robustness under repeated deformation.

Compression tests revealed that the hydrogels exhibited good compressive strength and stability under deformation. As shown in Fig. 5g, all samples displayed a nonlinear increase in stress with increasing strain, reflecting the typical deformation characteristics of soft polymer networks. The cyclic compression tests (Fig. 5h, i) further demonstrated the outstanding compressive toughness of PALCC. The dynamic breaking and reformation of hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and ionic bonds enabled efficient stress dissipation and rapid structural recovery, providing a solid foundation for its potential applications in flexible load-bearing and impact-protection systems.

Environmental stability and antibacterial properties

Stable antifreeze and anti-swelling properties are critical for the practical applications of hydrogels. As shown in Fig. 6b, when the PALCC hydrogel was immersed in deionized water, it effectively resisted swelling and maintained structural stability, with a weight change rate of approximately 2.3% after 10 days. Additionally, after 10 days of storage at room temperature, the residual mass of the PALCC hydrogel remained at around 93.6%. The sensor also exhibits excellent water retention capabilities under both high and low temperature conditions, which can be attributed to the role of DES in the system. As shown in Fig. 6c, molecular simulation of intermolecular weak interactions revealed that the strong interaction energy between H2O-DES was significantly higher than that between H2O-H2O, providing enhanced hydration49–51. Furthermore, Figure S3 further demonstrates that the DES-H2O system exhibits higher electronegativity and stronger electrostatic interactions, which is one of the key factors contributing to the hydrogel’s superior anti-drying performance.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was further employed to measure the water content and distribution within the hydrogel. The transverse relaxation time (T2) values and spectra are shown in Table S2 and Fig. 6d. Three peaks were observed within the 0.01-10,000 ms range, corresponding to the bound water (T21), semi-bound water (T22), and free water (T23). It can be seen that all three hydrogels predominantly contain bound and semi-bound water, where the T21 and T22 peaks representing the largest areas, with bound and semi-bound water being dominant in all three hydrogels, while PALC and PALCC exhibit slightly enhanced water confinement at the local scale due to the introduction of hydrophobic association and ionic interactions. This suggests that water molecules are strongly confined within the crosslinked network, with reduced mobility, this reduced mobility contributes to improved mechanical performance and environmental stability.

Figure 6 (e-f) displays the antibacterial performance of the hydrogels. Compared to the PA hydrogel as the control, both PALC and PALCC exhibited excellent antibacterial effects, with the PALCC hydrogel achieving inhibition rates of 96.87% and 97.51% against Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), respectively. This enhanced antibacterial performance is attributed to the synergistic effect of CTAB and CS within the hydrogel52. These materials can disrupt bacterial cell membranes through electrostatic interactions, thereby inhibiting bacterial growth and proliferation, showing great potential for applications in biomedical and antibacterial materials.

Strain-sensing properties

Aluminum foils were attached to both ends of the strip-shaped PALCC hydrogel to serve as electrodes, enabling direct use as a flexible strain sensor. As shown in Fig. 7a, the relative resistance change (ΔR/R0) of the PALCC hydrogel sensor exhibited a typical two-stage linear sensing behavior. In the strain range of 0-110%, the gauge factor (GF) was approximately 0.845 with a linear correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.987, while in the higher strain range of 110-249%, the GF increased markedly to 1.674 with an R2 value of 0.992. This two-stage linear sensing behavior can be attributed to the evolution of the conductive network within the hydrogel during stretching: At low strains, the hydrogel maintains a relatively stable and compact polymer network, making the movement of ions or electrons more difficult; At higher strains, the polymer network becomes more separated, facilitating greater penetration of ions and electrons along more efficient pathways, resulting in an increased GF value. Figure 7b–d display the ΔR/R0 curves of the PALCC hydrogel sensor during progressive stretching-releasing cycles. The device exhibited continuous, stable, and well-defined electrical signal outputs throughout the process. Notably, as shown in Fig. 7e, the resistance response of the hydrogel sensor was nearly unaffected by variations in stretching rate. Under identical strain conditions, the signal amplitude, waveform profile, and baseline stability remained highly consistent, demonstrating excellent anti-interference capability and dynamic stability. The current-voltage (I-V) characteristics (Fig. 7f) revealed that as the strain increased from 0 to 150%, the slope of the I-V curves gradually decreased, indicating a systematic reduction in conductivity. This behavior confirmed that the formation and rapid breaking/reconnection of conductive networks were key factors governing the sensing performance. To evaluate response stability, a 20% tensile strain was repeatedly applied and released at 10 s intervals, and the corresponding ΔR/R0 signals were recorded, as shown in Fig. 7g. The sensor displayed almost complete recovery of the resistance signal without significant hysteresis, indicating remarkable elasticity and signal reversibility. More importantly, even after 2000 tensile cycles at 100% strain, the PALCC hydrogel sensor maintained stable and repeatable resistance responses (Fig. S4). Nevertheless, slight signal drift was observed during the measurements, which can be attributed to moisture evaporation from the hydrogel matrix. To simulate the practical scenario in which sensors experience moisture loss during storage and transportation before reaching end users, the sensor was dried at room temperature for 5 days until a constant mass was achieved. After moisture equilibrium was established, the sensor displayed significantly improved signal stability. The enlarged views of the representative stages reveal only moderate baseline evolution, while the signal maintains excellent reproducibility and waveform consistency throughout the 2000 loading-unloading cycles (Fig. 7h). Application

The high-performance flexible strain sensor based on PALCC hydrogel, with its unique dual-mode response characteristics in both stretching and compression, finds applications in human kinematic detection and health monitoring. By integrating the sensor with an electrochemical signal acquisition system, an integrated platform was successfully developed to convert biomechanical motion into quantifiable electrical signals, offering an innovative technological platform for real-time health monitoring. As shown in Fig. 9a–e, during basic validation experiments, the PALCC hydrogel sensor was integrated at the finger joint, wrist joint, and back of the hand. As the joint flexion angle increased from 0° to 90°, the sensor’s ΔR exhibited a gradual increase, with a peak change of exceeding 300%. This capability highlights its suitability for applications in flexible electronics, where real-time, reliable signal transmission is essential.

Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a high-performance flexible strain sensor based on PALCC hydrogel, incorporating hydrophobic association, ionic coordination, and deep eutectic solvent (DES) to achieve enhanced mechanical, electrical, and environmental properties. The hydrogel exhibits remarkable mechanical strength, high stretchability, and excellent electrical conductivity, along with outstanding stability in both tensile and compressive strain sensing. Its dual-mode response, combined with superior cyclic durability, makes it an ideal candidate for real-time human motion detection and health monitoring applications. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations revealed the structural evolution and dynamic strengthening mechanisms of the hydrogel network, providing insight into its unique properties. The sensor demonstrated versatile functionality, including human motion detection, vocal vibration sensing, and the ability to transmit Morse code, proving its potential for integration into wearable devices and human–machine interaction systems. The findings from this study offer a promising platform for the development of future flexible, durable, and multi-functional sensors for advanced wearable technologies.

Methods

Materials

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, 1799 type, Mw ≈ 7.5×104 g mol-1, purity ≥ 98%, degree of alcoholysis 89-99%), acrylic acid (AA, AR), acetic acid (AcOH, AR), choline chloride (ChCl, EP), glycerol (Gly, AR), and N,N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA, AR) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Chitosan (CS, Mw ≈ 8.8×104 g mol-1, DD = 95%, degree of deacetylation ≥ 93%) was obtained from Shanghai D&B Biological Science and Technology Co., Ltd. Lauryl acrylate (LA, purity ≥90%), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, EP), and ammonium persulfate (APS, AR) were purchased from SJ&K Scientific.

Preparation of pre coagulation liquid

PVA Solution: 7.5 g of PVA powder was added to 42.5 g of deionized water, and the mixture was heated and stirred at 90°C for 1 h until the solution became clear, yielding a 15 wt.% PVA solution.

CS Solution: 0.2 g of CS was dissolved in a mixture of 1 g AcOH and 8.8 g deionized water. The solution was heated and stirred at 60°C for 1 h until it became clear, resulting in a 2 wt.% CS solution.

DES Solvent: 8.9 g of ChCl and 41.1 g of Gly (molar ratio 1:7) were mixed in a clean container, and the mixture was heated and stirred at 60°C for 1 h until a clear solution was obtained, forming the DES.

Preparation of hydrogel

The raw materials were mixed according to the designated ratios, followed by thorough agitation to ensure homogeneous dispersion. The mixture was then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 2 min to remove all air bubbles. Subsequently, the pre-gel solution was poured into appropriate molds and subjected to crosslinking at 60 °C for 6 h under sealed conditions, resulting in the formation of the PA, PALC, and PALCC hydrogels (the meanings of the letters, in order, are as follows: P represents PVA, A represents AA, L represents LA, the first C represents CTAB, and the second C represents CS). The detailed compositions of each hydrogel are provided in Part S1 and Table S1.
