Reconstruction of the medial patellofemoral ligament using a double-bundle semitendinosus tendon graft and a single suture anchor
Authors: Weizong Sun, Junchao Zhao, Binyang Meng, Zi Zhang, Liang Zhang, Jiangang Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe double bundle medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFL-R) is a well-established surgical technique for the management of patellar instability. While double anchor screw fixation on the patellar side is currently favored by many surgeons, this article presents a modified surgical approach involving minor technical modifications that may decrease dependence on internal implants.MethodsFrom July 2016 to August 2023, the same surgeon performed two distinct MPFL-R procedures on patients with recurrent patellar dislocation. The novel single-anchor MPFL-R technique involves fixing the inferior graft bundle into a surgically created medial bony groove of the patella using a single suture anchor, while the superior bundle is passed through the junction of the vastus medialis obliquus (VMO) tendon and the patella. The double-anchor MPFL-R technique utilizes two suture anchors placed on the patella to separately secure the inferior and superior graft bundles, following the established surgical approach. Functional outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at follow-up using the Kujala, Lysholm, and Tegner scores. Radiological outcomes, including the Insall–Salvati index, tibial tubercle–trochlear groove (TT–TG) distance, and patellar tilt angle (PTA), were also evaluated preoperatively and during follow-up.ResultsA total of 40 patients (42 knees) in the single-anchor (SA) group and 72 patients (76 knees) in the double-anchor (DA) group were included. Patient characteristics were comparable between the two groups. Preoperative clinical scores were as follows: Kujala (SA: 59.0 ± 9.2, DA: 60.1 ± 8.1), Lysholm (SA: 62.7 ± 9.1, DA: 61.6 ± 8.3), and Tegner (SA: 2.8 ± 1.0, DA: 3.1 ± 1.3). Postoperative scores improved to Kujala (SA: 88.3 ± 4.7, DA: 90.6 ± 4.8), Lysholm (SA: 90.8 ± 4.2, DA: 91.0 ± 4.9), and Tegner (SA: 4.6 ± 1.2, DA: 4.8 ± 1.5). Radiologically, preoperative patella trochlear angle (PTA) values were SA: 31.8 ± 9.0 and DA: 29.3 ± 10.2, which improved postoperatively to SA: 14.7 ± 8.6 and DA: 12.6 ± 6.3. All postoperative outcomes demonstrated significant improvement compared to preoperative values (P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed between the SA and DA groups in any outcome measure (P > 0.05).ConclusionSingle-anchor MPFL-R is a reliable technique for managing recurrent patellar dislocation, demonstrating comparable clinical and radiographic outcomes to the double-anchor approach. This method provides a feasible surgical alternative for surgeons already proficient in the double-anchor technique.

Introduction

The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary patellar restraint during the first degrees of knee flexion. Lateral patellar displacement of the patella usually damages the MPFL [1–4]. The medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFL-R) has emerged as an established surgical intervention for the management of recurrent patellofemoral instability in patients with normal lower limb alignment and insufficiency of the proximal medial stabilizers [5–8]. A variety of surgical techniques for MPFL reconstruction have been described in the literature [9–23]. Previous research has demonstrated that a double-bundle reconstruction of the MPFL is a rational approach for restoring the native ligamentous morphological and biomechanical properties [9, 24–26].

Currently, many surgeons still favor the technique of inserting two suture anchors on the medial aspect of the patella to reconstruct the double-bundle medial patellofemoral ligament [6]. This surgical technique provides reliable fixation and favorable clinical outcomes, representing a classic double-bundle reconstruction method for the MPFL. However, this technique may cause more bone damage to the patella and a greater reliance on high-value medical consumables, thereby raising the overall surgical expenses for patients. While some anchorless reconstruction techniques have effectively circumvented the aforementioned issues [3, 13–15, 20, 21], they require significant surgical technique modifications from surgeons, and in some cases, even require a process of relearning. This might not easily prompt surgeons accustomed to the double-anchor technique to make the switch. Moreover, some techniques involving the creation of a patellar bone tunnel may pose a potential risk of patellar fracture [27]. Based on this, we have proposed a novel technical improvement plan, which enables the aforementioned surgeons to make the least surgical technique changes to both minimize the impact on the patellar bone and reduce the use of implants, thereby moderately alleviating the medical burden on patients.

This study aimed to introduce a novel single-anchor double-bundle MPFL reconstruction technique and evaluate its clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that functional outcomes following single-anchor double-bundle MPFL-R would improve significantly during the follow-up period.

Methods

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tianjin University Tianjin Hospital and adhered to patient consent protocols (IRB approval number: 2026013). We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent either the single-anchor or double-anchor MPFL-R performed by the same surgeon (J. C.) between July 2016 and August 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) at least two documented dislocation episodes; (2) a minimum follow-up period of 24 months; and (3) closed femoral growth plates.

Both the single-anchor and double-anchor MPFL-R procedures are indicated under the same clinical circumstances. The surgical procedure was performed by an experienced senior surgeon (J. C.), who specializes in the management of patellar instability. The decision between single-anchor (SA) MPFL-R or double-anchor (DA) MPFL-R depended on the chosen by the patient (e.g. their economic situation), and detailed procedures were clearly introduced before the choice was made. Plain radiographs were examined to ensure that the femoral physes had been closed. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of prior knee surgery; (2) concomitant knee ligament injuries; (3) diagnosis of osteoarthritis based on clinical and radiographic evidence; and (4) concomitant systematic disease influencing the evaluation of knee function.

Surgical intervention was considered when conservative management following initial dislocation proved ineffective and recurrent dislocations occurred. Preoperative assessment included evaluation of anatomical risk factors, such as Insall-Salvati index, patellar tilt angle (PTA), tibial tubercle-trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance and trochlear dysplasia. Patient demographic data, including age, sex, affected side, and body mass index (BMI), were collected.

Surgical technique

The insertion site of the pes anserine tendon was palpated, and a 2-cm incision was made 2 cm medial to the tibial tubercle. The anserinus bursa was incised, and the semitendinosus was identified. Subsequently, the tendon was precisely released from its insertion on the tibia and bluntly dissected to define the interval between the superficial medial collateral ligament and the semitendinosus. The tendon was released using a tendon stripper and whip-stitched at both ends with NO.2 Vicryl (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA).

A 3-cm-long incision was created along the proximal half of the medial border of the patella. The superficial fascia was incised, and the medial border of the upper half of the patella and the vastus medialis obliquus (VMO) tendon was explored. The periosteum of the medial aspect of the proximal third of the patella was precisely dissected, and a 5-mm wide bony groove was created using a rongeur along the medial aspect of the patella. A 4.5-mm suture anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL) was subsequently screwed into the groove at the junction of the middle and proximal thirds of the patella.

We used a method to define the femoral tunnel point, which combines palpation, isometry tests, and intraoperative fluoroscopy. The medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle were palpated and subsequently marked on the skin. A 2-cm incision was made, and the soft tissues were dissected until the femur was reached. The medial epicondyle and the adductor tubercle were then palpated again, and a guidewire was placed at the point 1 mm posterior to the mid-point between them. Subsequently, blunt dissection was performed to create an extra-synovial tunnel between the second and third layers of the knee, extending from the patellar incision to the femoral incision, using a clamp. Then, an isometry test was conducted with the suture anchor tails and guidewire along the motion arc. Finally, intraoperative fluoroscopy was carried out to confirm that the guidewire was located within the area around Schöttle’s point [6, 28].

The semitendinosus graft was secured within the patellar bony groove using suture anchor tails, and the periosteum and soft tissues of the medial patella were sutured closed with anchor tails. The superior bundle of the graft was threaded through the junction of the VMO tendon and the patella. The two bundles of the graft were passed through the extra-synovial tunnel and twined on the guidewire. The isometry test was conducted once again. Subsequently, a 6-mm cannulated drill was passed over the guidewire to create a 30-mm-long tunnel on the medial aspect. A No.5 Ethibond passing suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) was threaded through the femoral tunnel via the beath pin. Then, the two ends of the graft were threaded through the femoral tunnel, and the knee was flexed to ensure that the graft had appropriate tension, neither excessive nor insufficient (Fig. 1a–f). The knee was positioned at 45° of flexion, and a 7*25 mm interference screw was inserted into the femoral tunnel without over-tension. An arthroscopic view was obtained (Fig. 2).

Lateral retinacular release (LRR) was performed only when the PTA exceeded 20° and could not be rectified to a minimum of 0°. A medial tibial tuberosity transfer (TTT) was conducted [29, 30] for patients who fulfilled the following criteria: ① the TT-TG distance was in excess of 20 mm; ② the sulcus angle was greater than 145°, which indicated a completely flattened trochlea (Dejour type B) or the absence of severely dysplastic medial femoral condyles (Dejour type C); and ③ the Q angle was greater than 18° for male patients and 20° for female patients. TTT and LRR procedures must be completed prior to femoral graft fixation to prevent alteration of the graft tension.

Postoperative rehabilitation

A compressive ice wrap was applied during the initial 24 h subsequent to surgery to alleviate swelling. Partial weight-bearing was permitted, and the knee was safeguarded with a long-winged knee brace for the first two weeks [29, 31], enabling a range of motion from 0° to 90°. From the third to the sixth week, the knee was non-weight-bearing during walking and standing under the protection of a brace, allowing a motion ranging from 0° to 120°. After six weeks, the patients commenced progressive activity without a brace, and participation in contact sports was permitted after six months.

Clinical and radiologic outcome assesments

All patients underwent physical examination prior to surgery. Preoperative and follow-up clinical evaluations were conducted using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), specifically the Kujala, Lysholm, and Tegner scores. Functional failures—including recurrent subluxation or dislocation, as well as patient-reported instability—were documented. Data on complications, such as infection, chronic effusion, hypoesthesia, restricted range of motion, and patellar fracture, were also collected.

Preoperative plain radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans were obtained for the majority of patients. Patellar height was evaluated on lateral radiographs using the Insall-Salvati index. On axial CT images, the TT-TG distance was measured [32], and trochlear dysplasia was graded according to the Dejour classification, as previously described [1, 33, 34]. Postoperative CT scans were performed to evaluate whether patellar position was normalized (Fig. 3). The distance from the femoral tunnel center to Schöttle’s point was measured using translucent and surface-rendered 3D-CT reconstructions (Fig. 4). The PTA was evaluated on both preoperative and postoperative CT scans using established methods [35]. All radiographic measurements were independently assessed by two experienced observers.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired sample t-tests were used for within-group comparisons, while independent sample t-tests were applied for between-group comparisons. A post hoc power analysis was conducted with G*Power software (version 3.1). Statistical significance was defined as a P-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 136 patients (142 knees) met the inclusion criteria, comprising 49 knees treated with single-anchor MPFL-R and 93 knees treated with double-anchor MPFL-R. Of these, 16 patients were excluded: 5 due to a history of prior knee surgery, 6 with concomitant ligament injuries, 4 diagnosed with osteoarthritis, and 1 with systemic conditions affecting functional evaluation. Additionally, 5 patients were lost to follow-up and 3 had incomplete clinical data, leading to their exclusion. The final cohort included 112 patients (118 knees), with 42 knees assigned to the single-anchor (SA) group and 76 to the double-anchor (DA) group. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 5. After 24 months, all included patients were successfully contacted.

Patient demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences in demographic variables or pathoanatomic risk factors between the two groups.

Clinical outcomes

Results of the clinical scores (Kujala, Lysholm, and Tegner) for the SA and DA groups are presented in Table 2. In the SA group, the mean Kujala score improved from 59.0 ± 9.2 preoperatively to 88.3 ± 4.7 postoperatively, the Lysholm score increased from 62.7 ± 9.1 to 90.8 ± 4.2, and the Tegner score rose from 2.8 ± 1.0 to 4.6 ± 1.2. Similarly, in the DA group, the Kujala score improved from 60.1 ± 8.1 to 90.6 ± 4.8, the Lysholm score from 61.6 ± 8.3 to 91.0 ± 4.9, and the Tegner score from 3.1 ± 1.3 to 4.8 ± 1.5. All postoperative scores showed significant improvement compared with preoperative values (P < 0.001), although no significant differences were observed between the SA and DA groups before surgery (P > 0.05).

Radiological outcomes

Complete imaging data from 38 knees in the SA group and 67 knees in the DA group were retrospectively reviewed. The results of the measurements are presented in Table 3.

The mean Insall-Salvati index was 1.3 ± 0.2 in the SA group and 1.2 ± 0.2 in the DA group. The mean TT–TG distance measured 18.9 ± 3.9 mm in the SA group and 19.2 ± 4.1 mm in the DA group, while the mean distance from the femoral tunnel center to Schöttle’s point was 4.1 ± 2.3 mm in the SA group and 3.9 ± 3.5 mm in the DA group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of the Insall-Salvati index, TT–TG distance, or femoral tunnel center-to-Schöttle’s point distance.

In the SA group, the PTA improved from 31.8 ± 9.0 preoperatively to 14.7 ± 8.6 postoperatively, while in the DA group, it changed from 29.3 ± 10.2 to 12.6 ± 6.3. Postoperative PTA values returned to normal levels in both groups, showing significant improvement compared with preoperative measurements (P < 0.001), although no significant differences were observed between the two groups.

Complications

No intraoperative complications occurred. No infections or chronic effusions were observed during the follow-up period. A patellar fracture occurred in a young female patient following a fall during a car accident, which took place more than one year after her initial surgery. Two patients presented with recurrent patellar dislocation. Trauma was determined to be the primary cause of redislocation. A young male sustained a knee sprain during basketball activities ten months postoperatively. Revision surgery was performed by the same surgeon, with intraoperative confirmation of laxity in the transplanted tendon. At the final follow-up, physical examination, functional outcomes, and radiographic findings had returned to optimal levels. In a separate case, a middle-aged female experienced an accidental fall while climbing stairs twenty-one months after surgery. Following self-reduction of the patella, the patient declined surgical revision. Since then, she has avoided high-impact activities and has had no further dislocations.

Six patients exhibited a positive apprehension sign with associated patellar lateralization but reported no subsequent episodes of dislocation or subluxation. Four patients demonstrated mild limitation in knee range of motion (ROM), with flexion not exceeding 110°. One reoperation was performed, consisting of arthroscopic release to address knee stiffness secondary to flexion below 90°. All five patients with limited ROM cited inadequate adherence to the rehabilitation protocol as the cause. Eleven patients reported mild anterior knee pain, and five patients reported mild hypoesthesia.

The overall complication and adverse event rates did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that the new single-anchor MPFL-R yielded clinical and radiographic outcomes comparable to the double-anchor technique in the management of recurrent patellar dislocation. Significant improvements were observed in the Kujala, Lysholm, and Tegner scores, while radiological parameters remained similar between the two groups. These findings suggest that single-anchor MPFL-R can effectively restore native ligament anatomy and represents a reliable and safe surgical option for recurrent patellar dislocation.

Currently, medial soft-tissue stabilizer reconstruction is the mainstream approach for managing recurrent patellar dislocation, with the restoration of native anatomical structures remaining a fundamental principle [36]. MPFL-R has been demonstrated to be an effective treatment approach [4, 7, 29, 34, 37, 38]. Various MPFL reconstruction techniques have been described in the literature, utilizing autografts—including semitendinosus, gracilis, patellar, and quadriceps tendons—as well as allografts and synthetic grafts [2, 22, 23, 39–42]. For many surgeons, double-bundle MPFL reconstruction using a free gracilis autograft is the preferred method [3, 28]. Several graft fixation techniques at the patella are available in MPFL-R [3, 9, 12, 14–16, 21, 43–47]. In a comparative study of five different patellar fixation methods, Lenschow et al. found that suture anchors provide adequate stiffness and maximum load capacity [48]. Given these advantages, many surgeons prefer the technique [6] of inserting two suture anchors on the medial aspect of the patella to reconstruct the double-bundle MPFL. However, this technique may cause more bone damage to the patella and a greater reliance on high-value medical consumables. This study proposed a novel graft fixation technique at the patella for double-bundle reconstruction of the MPFL. The inferior bundle was fixed within the bony groove at the medial middle and proximal third aspects of the patella, and the superior bundle was passed through the junction between the VMO and patella, which imitated the anatomical morphology of the native MPFL and optimized postoperative function and outcomes. From a biomechanical viewpoint, the double-bundle technique exhibits an angular synergistic effect that simulates the broad footprint of the MPFL in the patella, enabling a greater capacity to resist patellar dislocation at the early knee flexion angle [49]. The attachment of the superior bundle and the VMO represents a method of elastic fixation, which can compensate for the slight length mismatch of the reconstructed MPFL during mobility [35]. The bony groove enlarges the contact area, which may facilitate tendon-bone healing. In the SA group, significant improvements were observed in the Kujala, Lysholm, and Tegner scores from preoperative baselines to the final follow-up (p < 0.001). Postoperative PTA values also showed significant enhancement compared with preoperative measurements (p < 0.001). These outcomes were comparable to those obtained using the conventional technique (DA group), in which a semitendinosus graft is fixed with two suture anchors at the superomedial patellar border [7]. Therefore, the new technique demonstrates non-inferiority compared to existing approaches. Furthermore, it requires only a single anchor for patellar fixation, resulting in reduced bone damage and lower medical costs. This surgical technique shares most procedural steps with the double-anchor technique and presents relatively lower modification difficulty compared to the anchorless approach. It can serve as a viable alternative for surgeons familiar with the double-anchor technique, offering a cost-effective solution for patients concerned about treatment expenses. Ultimately, it contributes to a satisfactory outcome for both surgeons and patients.

Although non-anatomic tunnel placement does not necessarily lead to poor short-term results, the femoral tunnel position should be as anatomic as feasible to optimize graft function and outcomes. Nevertheless, this remains a challenging step, even with the radiologic landmarks defined by Schöttle [7]. A study by Ziegler et al. indicated that radiographic localization led to inaccurate MPFL femoral tunnel placement on a true lateral radiograph, especially when there was a deviation from a true lateral fluoroscopic image, which could be difficult to acquire during the operation. They recommended direct intraoperative assessment of the anatomy rather than relying solely on radiographs [50]. In this study, we combined intraoperative palpation, isometry tests, and intraoperative fluoroscopy to determine the femoral tunnel position. Postoperative imaging confirmed a satisfactory distance between the femoral tunnel center and Schöttle’s point.

The tension of the graft is also of significance. Excessive tension of the graft can notably alter patellofemoral contact pressure, which may lead to pain and arthrosis [51]. Elias et al. demonstrated that tension exceeding 10 N could induce pain, stiffness, and early medial patellofemoral osteoarthritis [52–54]. Song et al. suggested that the appropriate knee angle for graft fixation should range between 30° and 45° [55, 56]. In this study, the graft at the femoral tunnel was fixed with the knee at 45°. Based on clinical experience, the surgeon will laterally displace the patella to achieve appropriate tension before performing the final fixation. Furthermore, the motion of the knee and the positions of the patella and femur under arthroscopy were checked to prevent overtension.

Previous studies have reported patellar fractures associated with the use of transpatellar bone tunnels for graft fixation. Such tunnels act as stress risers, potentially compromising the articular surface or cortical integrity of the patella [9, 10, 57, 58]. This risk informed our decision to avoid the anchor-free technique involving a patellar bone tunnel. In our study, one patellar fracture and two recurrent patellar dislocations were all associated with traumatic causes. To date, there has been no spontaneous recurrence of patellar dislocation, which further confirms the reliability of the current surgical approach.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, nonrandomized design and relatively small sample size may have increased the risk of type II error, as no statistically significant differences were detected. Second, since the study was conducted at a single center by a single surgeon, the findings may not be generalizable to other settings. Third, as an initial objective of this novel surgical technique, it is intended to include more economically disadvantaged patients in the SA group, which may lead to broader potential implications.

Conclusion

Single-anchor MPFL-R is a reliable technique for managing recurrent patellar dislocation, demonstrating comparable clinical and radiographic outcomes to the double-anchor approach. This method provides a feasible surgical alternative for surgeons already proficient in the double-anchor technique. It also minimizes patellar bone impact and reduces implant usage, thereby helping to moderately reduce the medical burden on patients.
