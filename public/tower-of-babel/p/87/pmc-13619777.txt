Efficacy and Safety of Endoscopic Treatment for Chronic Hemorrhagic Radiation Enteropathy: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Yue Sun, Yuhang Yin, Siqi Jia, Cong Gao, Qianqian Li, Wenhui Huo, Yanhong Gao, Xiaofeng Liu, Xingshun Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy, a common complication secondary to pelvic radiotherapy, can be treated by endoscopy. However, the efficacy and safety of endoscopic treatment have not been systematically assessed.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases were searched to identify all relevant studies on endoscopic treatment for chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy. A random-effects model was employed to perform meta-analyses. Pooled rates and weighted mean difference were calculated. Heterogeneity was explored through meta-regression, leave-one-out sensitivity, and subgroup analyses.ResultsOverall, 81 studies including 2626 patients with chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy were eligible. Among them, argon plasma coagulation (APC) was performed in 2255 patients, with the rates of clinical success, endoscopic success, transfusion-free, recurrence, mild and moderate adverse events, and serious adverse events being 95%, 97%, 100%, 7%, 7%, and 0%, respectively; and the mean hemoglobin level increased by 20.61 g/L. Radiofrequency ablation was performed in 132 patients, with the rates of clinical success, endoscopic success, transfusion-free, recurrence, mild and moderate adverse events, and serious adverse events being 100%, 100%, 84%, 8%, 8% and 0%, respectively; and the mean hemoglobin level increased by 20.33 g/L. After laser treatment, cryotherapy, heater probe treatment, and bipolar electrocoagulation, the clinical success rates were 87%, 92%, 100%, and 94%, respectively; and the endoscopic success rates were 91%, 89%, 100%, and 100%, respectively.ConclusionEndoscopic treatments are often effective and safe for chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy. APC is the most widely studied choice of endoscopic treatment. In future, head-to-head comparative studies are needed to explore their benefits and risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-026-03684-3.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy is a complication of pelvic radiotherapy and is commonly characterized by persistent intestinal bleeding and anemia, which may substantially affect patients’ quality of life. Although endoscopic treatment is frequently used for this condition in clinical practice, the efficacy and safety of available endoscopic modalities have not been comprehensively and systematically assessed.Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of currently available endoscopic treatments for chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy and to summarize potential differences among major treatment modalities. What was learned from the study? In 81 studies including 2626 patients, endoscopic treatment showed favorable efficacy and safety. The clinical and endoscopic success rates of APC were 95% and 97%, respectively, while the clinical and endoscopic success rates of RFA were both 100%, without any serious adverse events for both APC and RFA. APC remains the most widely studied modality and appears to have a good safety profile, whereas RFA may be promising for symptom control and bleeding relief.However, as a result of the lack of head-to-head comparative studies, the optimal endoscopic treatment strategy remains to be defined. High-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to compare different endoscopic modalities in the future.

Introduction

Pelvic radiotherapy is an important choice of treatment for various malignant tumors, such as prostate cancer, cervical cancer, and bladder cancer, and can significantly improve the survival of patients with malignant tumors [1–3]. However, it can cause intestinal complications, such as chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy, which is typically characterized by persistent intestinal bleeding with anemia and seriously affects the patients’ life quality [4]. It occurs at least 3 months after pelvic radiotherapy. Its pathogenesis is very complex. Radiation not only directly causes DNA damage in intestinal epithelial cells and loss of crypt stem cells but also induces mucosal atrophy, small artery fibrosis, and obliterative endarteritis through the continuous action of free radicals and inflammatory mediators, leading to local ischemia and telangiectasia, which together contribute to the development of persistent bleeding [5]. In addition, immune system activation, intestinal nervous system dysregulation, and intestinal microbiota imbalance all play important roles in the disease progressions [5, 6]. These mechanisms contribute to chronic and progressive nature of this disease, which are important causes for its long-term stubbornness and difficulty to cure [5, 6].

Drug or supportive treatment is ineffective for chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy, and endoscopy has become commonly employed in clinical practice [4]. In 2019, the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) conducted a systematic review regarding endoscopic treatment for chronic hemorrhagic radiation proctopathy. Based on the quantitative efficacy meta-analysis of a few types of endoscopic treatments, clinical practice recommendations were formulated. However, only studies published before 2017 were included. Additionally, they focused on the improvement of bleeding, but lacked systematic quantitative analyses of other critical outcomes, such as change in hemoglobin, transfusion, recurrence, and adverse events. Herein, we conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis by more comprehensively reviewing the existing evidence regarding all types of endoscopic treatment for chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy.

Methods

Registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. This study was registered in PROSPERO on August 19, 2025 (registration number CRD420251128249). This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Literature Source

All published papers were searched via the PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library databases. The last search was conducted on August 21, 2025. Search items were as follows: ((radiation enteropathy [all fields]) OR (radiation proctopathy [all fields]) OR (radiation enteritis [all fields]) OR (radiation duodenitis [all fields]) OR (radiation ileitis [all fields]) OR (radiation colitis [all fields]) OR (radiation proctitis [all fields]) OR (radiation-induced intestinal damage [all fields]) OR (radiation-induced intestinal injury [all fields]) OR (radiation induced bowel disease [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation proctitis [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation enteritis [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation duodenitis [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation ileitis [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation colitis [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy [all fields]) OR (hemorrhagic radiation proctopathy [all fields]) OR (radiation-induced ulcer [all fields]) OR (radiation-induced perforation [all fields])) AND ((argon plasma coagulation [all fields]) OR (bipolar electrocoagulation [all fields]) OR (heater probe [all fields]) OR (radiofrequency ablation [all fields]) OR (Nd-YAG laser [all fields]) OR (cryotherapy [all fields]) OR (cryoablation [all fields]) OR (endoscopic therapy [all fields]) OR (endoscopic treatment [all fields])).

Study Selection

Eligible studies were screened by two researchers (YS and SJ). The following inclusion criteria were established on the basis of the PIOS principles: the subjects were patients with chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy (P); the intervention was endoscopic treatment (I); the study reported at least one predefined outcome (O), including clinical success rate, endoscopic success rate, change in hemoglobin before and after treatment, transfusion-free rate, recurrence rate, mild and moderate adverse events rate or serious adverse events rate; the study design types were single-arm studies and comparative studies including the endoscopic treatment group (S). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicates; (2) case reports or case series studies involving fewer than three patients; (3) letters, comments, or editorials; (4) reviews and/or meta-analysis; (5) guidelines, notes, or consensuses; (6) experimental or animal studies; (7) research protocols or studies with only clinical trial registration information. There were no restrictions on the publication language and date.

Data Extraction

Two researchers (YS and SJ) extracted the following data from each study: the first author, publication year, country, study design, enrollment time, publication type, the number of patients with chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy who underwent endoscopic treatment, male ratio, age, type of endoscopic treatment, number of endoscopic treatment sessions, follow-up duration, pre- and post-treatment hemoglobin level, number of patients achieving clinical success after treatment, number of patients achieving endoscopic success after treatment, number of patients requiring transfusion before treatment, number of patients no longer requiring transfusion after treatment, number of patients who developed disease recurrence after treatment, number of patients with mild and moderate adverse events, and number of patients with serious adverse events. For comparative studies, only the relevant data in patients who underwent endoscopic treatment were extracted.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included clinical success rate and endoscopic success rate. The secondary outcomes included change in hemoglobin level before and after treatment, transfusion-free rate, recurrence rate, mild and moderate adverse events rate, and serious adverse events rate.

Definitions

Because the definitions of treatment success varied across the included studies, outcome data were extracted according to the definitions used in the original articles. Most studies did not provide separate definitions of treatment success according to lesion location or follow-up duration. Study Quality

Two researchers (YS and WH) independently conducted study quality assessment using the first eight items of the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) scale, namely the clarity of study aim, continuity of included patients, prospective data collection, appropriateness of endpoint indicators, sufficiency of study duration, situation of loss to follow-up, objectivity of result evaluation, and appropriateness of statistical analysis. Each item was scored 0–2 points. Specifically, 0 points were given for items not mentioned or clearly non-compliant, 1 point was given for items mentioned but with insufficient detail or partially compliant, and 2 points were given for items fully compliant. For comparative studies, only the data of endoscopic treatment group was assessed for quality. The total score was 16 points. A score of 0–7, 8–12, and 13–16 was deemed as low, moderate, and high quality, respectively.

Statistical Analyses

Meta-analysis was performed with a random-effects model using STATA 15.1 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) and RStudio 2025.05.0 (Posit Software, PBC). Proportional meta-analysis was performed by calculating the pooled rates of each outcome and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to stabilize the variance of original proportions. Meanwhile, the change in hemoglobin level was also summarized and expressed as weighted mean differences and their 95%CIs. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Cochrane Q test and I2 statistics were employed to assess the heterogeneity, and P < 0.1 or I2 > 50% was considered a statistically significant heterogeneity. Meta-regression analyses, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses were used to explore the source of heterogeneity. Meta-regression analyses and subgroup analyses were performed using 10 covariates, namely publication year (before vs. after 2015), study design (prospective vs. retrospective), publication type (full-text vs. abstract), study quality (low vs. moderate vs. high), region (Europe vs. Asia vs. North America vs. South America vs. Oceania vs. Africa), sample size (< 10 vs. ≥ 10), mean follow-up time (≤ 6 months vs. > 6 months), mean number of treatment sessions (< 2 times vs. ≥ 2 times), male ratio (≤ 75% vs. > 75%), and mean age (< 73 years vs. ≥ 73 years). In the meta-regression analyses, variables with P < 0.05 were considered potential sources of heterogeneity. An interaction test was used to assess differences in effect sizes between subgroups, with P < 0.1 indicating statistically significant interaction. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing studies one by one from a meta-analysis and then recalculating the pooled effect size and I2 value. If the pooled effect size and I2 were not substantially changed, the statistical results would be robust. When there were ≥ 10 studies included in a meta-analysis, the Egger test was performed to evaluate the publication bias, and P < 0.1 was considered statistically significant publication bias.

Study Quality

Fifty-five and 26 studies were of moderate quality [7–53, 63, 64, 78–80, 85–87] and high quality [54–62, 65–77, 81–84] (Table S1).

Clinical Success

The pooled clinical success rates were 95% for APC (95% CI 93–97%, P < 0.001; I2 = 71.02%, P < 0.01), 100% for RFA (95% CI 98–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 6.02%, P = 0.39), 87% for laser treatment (95% CI 76–96%, P < 0.001; I2 = 43.37%, P = 0.10), 92% for cryotherapy (95% CI 76–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 20.71%, P = 0.28), 100% for heater probe treatment (95% CI 92–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.97), and 94% for BEC (95% CI 85–99%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = /), respectively (Fig. 2a). Considering that the heterogeneity was significant among studies regarding APC, meta-regression analyses (Table S2) and sensitivity analyses (Table S3) did not find sources of heterogeneity, and subgroup analyses are shown in Fig. S1. The pooled endoscopic success rates were 97% for APC (95% CI 94–99%, P < 0.001; I2 = 66.37%, P = 0.00), 100% for RFA (95% CI 100–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.72), 91% for laser treatment (95% CI 79–99%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.61), 89% for cryotherapy (95% CI 63–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 53.07%, P = 0.12), and 100% for heater probe treatment (95% CI 92–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.97), respectively; and only one study reported endoscopic success rate after BEC was 100% (Fig. 2b). Transfusion-Free

The pooled transfusion-free rates were 100% for APC (95% CI 95–100%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.96), 84% for RFA (95% CI 69–95%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.56), and 87% for laser treatment (95% CI 74–97%, P < 0.001; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.83), respectively (Fig. 3b). There was no data on cryotherapy, heater probe treatment, and BEC. There was no significant publication bias among studies regarding APC (P = 0.364).

Recurrence

The pooled recurrence rates were 7% for APC (95% CI 5–10%, P < 0.001; I2 = 24.20%, P = 0.14) and 8% for RFA (95% CI 0–29%, P = 0.14; I2 = 0.00%, P = /), respectively; and only one study reported recurrence rate after laser treatment was 71% (Fig. 3c). There was no data on cryotherapy, heater probe treatment, and BEC. There was no significant publication bias among studies regarding APC (P = 0.003).

Serious Adverse Events

The pooled rates of serious adverse events were 0% for APC (95% CI 0–0%, P = 0.38; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.95), 0% for RFA (95% CI 0–2%, P = 1.00; I2 = 0.00%, P = 1.00), 1% for laser treatment (95% CI 0–6%, P = 0.53; I2 = 34.56%, P = 0.16), 4% for cryotherapy (95% CI 0–15%, P = 0.17; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.70), 0% for heater probe treatment (95% CI 0–8%, P = 1.00; I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.97), and 1% for BEC (95% CI 0–6%, P = 0.41; I2 = 0.00%, P = /), respectively (Fig. 4b). Discussion

To our knowledge, four previous meta-analyses evaluated the efficacy and safety of several types of endoscopic treatment for chronic hemorrhagic radiation proctopathy [88–91]. By comparison, the current meta-analysis had some strengths. First, three previous meta-analyses only included patients with chronic hemorrhagic radiation proctopathy [88–90], while ours included patients with lesions in both the colon and rectum. Second, four previous meta-analyses were limited to the comparison between APC and formalin or BEC [88, 90, 91], and focused on RFA alone [89], but ours included nearly all types of endoscopic treatment. Third, four previous meta-analyses focused on the overall response, but they did not classify the treatment-related adverse events [88–91]. By comparison, ours assessed the outcomes more comprehensively. Fourth, three previous meta-analyses included three to seven studies [88, 89, 91], while ours included 81 studies. Finally, unlike previous meta-analyses [88, 89], we comprehensively assessed study quality using the MINORS scale.

Our study demonstrated that RFA achieved both clinical and endoscopic success rates of 100%, which are slightly superior to those of APC (95% and 97%). RFA can cover a larger mucosal area with consistent energy level and effectively ablate superficial vascular through uniform thermal effects, reducing both missed lesions and the risk of deep tissue thermal injury [9, 47]. APC is also a non-contact method, but its energy distribution is more dependent upon the operator’s technique and the spraying angle, which may result in “jumping” cauterization in some lesions [28, 75]. Besides, we found that the clinical and endoscopic success rates of APC might be influenced by the publication year, patients’ age, male ratio, and follow-up duration. Earlier studies focused on older patients with prostate cancer, who had more lesions confined to the anterior rectal walls mainly consisting of superficial capillary dilation with superficial ulcers [12, 75]. APC can achieve a higher hemostasis success rate in such lesions [12, 75]. However, recent studies focused on patients with gynecological malignant tumors, who receive wider radiation fields, resulting in more diffuse and deeper intestinal lesions, often accompanied by ulcers and erosions [40, 73]. In such lesions, the coverage of APC may be insufficient, resulting in limited hemostatic effect [40, 73]. During a relatively long follow-up period, patients who underwent APC exhibited sustained improvement in bleeding symptoms and mucosal appearance, confirming its long-term benefits [60].

Chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy has similar pathological features of gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE) [92, 93]. In accordance with the efficacy of endoscopic treatment for GAVE [94], our study also demonstrated that APC was superior to RFA in reducing transfusion dependence. Thus, APC may be preferred when the clinical goal is to reduce transfusion dependence.

The rates of serious adverse events of APC and RFA were both 0%, indicating that they had good safety as the first-line choices of endoscopic treatment. Moreover, the rates of mild and moderate adverse events of APC and RFA were 7% and 8%, respectively, with common manifestations, including bloating, abdominal pain, and brief minor bleeding. With more treatment sessions or longer follow-up duration, the rates of mild and moderate adverse events of RFA tended to rise, probably due to cumulative mucosal damage [9].

Laser treatment showed lower clinical and endoscopic success rates and a high recurrence rate (71%) [52]. This is probably because laser treatment performs selective cauterization of vascular ectatic areas through high-energy focusing [93, 95]. However, as a result of its great depth of tissue penetration, the propensity to cause tissue carbonization and stenosis, and considerable variation among devices and energy settings, its efficacy tends to be unstable [93, 95]. Additionally, laser treatment often struggles to cover widely dilated vascular beds, and some lesions may not be fully treated, which become a potential source of recurrence [96]. Our study demonstrated relatively low adverse events rates of laser treatment, but we should still pay attention to the risk of potential adverse events considering its mechanism of action.

We found that cryotherapy showed higher adverse event rates (27% mild and 4% serious). This may be attributed to freeze–thaw injury and reperfusion damage, which can cause mucosal necrosis, edema, and vascular fragility, thereby increasing the risk of ulcers or bleeding [58, 97]. Notably, the efficacy and safety of cryotherapy seem to vary across regions. Such a regional difference may be related to nonuniform parameters, operators’ experiences, and patients’ heterogeneity [93, 98].

Both heater probe treatment and BEC reached an endoscopic success rate of 100%. However, the number of relevant studies was limited and the sample sizes were small, so these results should be interpreted cautiously. Notably, in the only one available study on BEC, the rate of mild and moderate adverse events reached 73%, possibly due to excessive thermal damage caused by uncontrolled energy output [86].

Our study had several limitations. First, most included studies were single-arm, retrospective, and of moderate quality, and high-quality randomized controlled trials were lacking, which limits the level of evidence. Second, there was heterogeneity among studies in terms of publication year, study design, publication type, study quality, region, sample size, mean follow-up time, mean number of treatment sessions, male ratio, and mean age, which may influence the stability and generalizability of the pooled results. Third, only a few studies reported the outcomes related to cryotherapy, heater probe, and BEC, and their sample sizes were small, limiting the reliability and generalizability of our results. Finally, some studies were published only as conference abstracts without sufficient description of methods and data, probably compromising the rigor and reproducibility of our results.

Conclusion

Generally, endoscopic treatment is effective for hemostasis and improvement of anemia in patients with chronic hemorrhagic radiation enteropathy. As the most extensively studied type of endoscopic treatment, APC has a good safety profile. RFA seems be superior over APC in terms of symptom control and bleeding relief. Laser treatment has a high recurrence risk. Cryotherapy, heater probe, and BEC should be feasible as alternative or supplementary options in specific settings. As a result of the lack of head-to-head comparison, the optimal choice of endoscopic treatment could not be identified. In future, randomized controlled trials should be performed to clarify the advantages of different endoscopic treatments.
