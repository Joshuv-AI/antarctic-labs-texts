Resurgence of Human Adenovirus Infections in the Korean Military Following the COVID-19 Pandemic
Authors: Hyun-Wook Park, Eunyoung Lee, Soon-Hwan Kwon, Eunja Ko, Donghoon Kim, Bomi Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe study investigates the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of the resurgence of human adenovirus (HAdV) infections among Korean military personnel following the easing of the coronavirus disease 2019 restrictions, with a focus on serotype distribution and healthcare utilization.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective descriptive study of 450 laboratory-confirmed HAdV cases identified between January 2023 and July 2024. We retrieved data from the New Defense Medical Information System and performed serotyping on a subset of samples using real-time polymerase chain reaction and sequencing.ResultsHAdV cases increased sharply beginning in late 2023 and peaked in July 2024. Serotyping revealed a significant shift: HAdV-14 accounted for 98.8% of typed cases, replacing the historically dominant HAdV-55. Most patients presented with respiratory symptoms, although gastrointestinal symptoms were also common. Co-infections occurred in 7.3% of cases. 26.2% of patients required hospitalization, with a mean stay of 7.4 days. One patient required intensive care.ConclusionThis study highlights the resurgence and serotype shift of HAdV infections in a military population, reflecting post-pandemic viral dynamics. The findings underscore the need for continuous molecular surveillance and strengthened preparedness strategies in congregate military settings.

INTRODUCTION

Human adenoviruses (HAdVs) are non-enveloped, double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) viruses classified into seven species (A–G), encompassing numerous types, with new types continuing to emerge.1 These viruses cause a spectrum of clinical conditions, from mild respiratory infections to severe pneumonia, gastroenteritis, and conjunctivitis, depending on the host factors and virus type.23 Although HAdV infections commonly affect young children, they pose a significant threat in closed or crowded settings such as military bases, where close living quarters, stress, and field training conditions amplify transmission risk.345

In Korea, HAdV outbreaks have persisted as a significant public health challenge in military populations, with serotype 55 as a major contributor to severe respiratory diseases.6789101112 Between 2013 and 2022, HAdV emerged as the most prevalent respiratory virus in this population, accounting for 80.41% of detected respiratory viruses and contributing to pneumonia in 65.79% of cases, markedly higher than the 48.33% pneumonia rate observed in non-HAdV infections.7 Additionally, HAdV infections led to more emergency department visits, outpatient consultations, prolonged hospital stays, and extended intensive care unit (ICU) admissions compared to non-HAdV respiratory infections.7

During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, widespread mask-wearing, enhanced hygiene, and social distancing suppressed the spread of respiratory infections, including HAdV.7 However, after authorities eased these measures in 2023, civilian HAdV cases surged.7 Military settings soon experienced a similar resurgence, raising concerns about changes in HAdV epidemiology and clinical severity.

This study investigates the recent resurgence of HAdV infections in the Korean military following the easing of COVID-19 restrictions. We analyze epidemiological trends, clinical characteristics, medical utilization patterns, and serotype distribution. The findings can support outbreak prevention and control strategies in the Korean military and help guide targeted intervention measures such as vaccination programs.

METHODS

Study design and population

This retrospective descriptive study analyzed medical records of 450 laboratory-confirmed HAdV cases identified through respiratory virus polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing at Korean military hospitals between January 2023 and July 2024. We retrieved relevant clinical data, including electronic medical records and test results, through the New Defense Medical Information System, which centrally collects and manages data from all Korean military hospitals under the Armed Forces Medical Command.

Data collection and analysis

We reviewed clinical characteristics such as symptoms and treatment modalities, and health care utilization for all confirmed HAdV cases. To assess healthcare utilization, we examined medical visits that occurred within seven days of testing. We excluded cases that performed PCR testing during hospitalization to avoid misclassifying hospital admissions as directly caused by HAdV infection.

We also identified and analyzed co-infections with other respiratory viruses. The PCR test panel detects the following respiratory viruses: HAdV, human rhinovirus (HRV), human coronavirus (HCoV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), human parainfluenza virus (HPIV), human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV), human bocavirus, human enterovirus, and influenza virus (IFV). To ensure the accuracy of the analysis and maintain consistency with previous studies, we included only viruses that all respiratory virus PCR tests consistently included: HAdV, HMPV, HPIV, HRSV, and IFV.7 We diagnosed pneumonia in patients who presented clinical symptoms or physical findings typical of pneumonia when infiltrates were observed on chest X-ray or computed tomography, without distinguishing between viral and bacterial etiologies.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Laboratory procedures and serotyping

Additionally, we performed serotyping on 169 cases to identify the distribution of HAdV strains. Serotyping commenced in January 2024 following confirmation of an outbreak in early 2024; therefore, we did not analyze samples collected in 2023. For other cases in which we did not conduct serotyping, the primary reason was that the laboratory did not receive the specimens, likely due to being lost or discarded during transportation.

We extracted DNA from 200 μL of respiratory samples using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit (Cat. No. 69506; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and eluted the DNA in a final volume of 100 μL of elution buffer. For HAdV genotyping, we used real-time PCR with an HAdV-14-specific primer and/or partially amplified the hexon gene for sequencing to identify subtypes.13 We conducted real-time PCR on a GeneChecker Ultra-Fast PCR System UF-150 (Genesystem, Daejeon, Korea) using a two times (2×) PCR master mix in a 10 μL final volume. The reaction mix contained 5 pmol of each primer, 0.5 pmol of type-specific probe and 3 μL of template DNA. We used distilled water as a negative control in all experiments. We carried out the PCR assays under standard conditions for 40 cycles as follows: denaturation at 94°C for 5 seconds, annealing at 54°C for 5 seconds, and elongation at 72°C for 5 seconds.

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Armed Forces Medical Command (AFMC-2024-06-006-002) approved this study. The IRBs waived the requirement for informed consent.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics and epidemiological trends

Between January 2023 and July 2024, we identified 450 laboratory-confirmed HAdV infections among Korean military personnel out of 1,754 total respiratory virus PCR tests performed (positivity rate: 25.7%). All patients were male, and 95.3% (429 cases) were under the age of 25, reflecting the conscription-based demographic structure of active-duty recruits (Table 1). Among the 256 patients with identified rank, 96.9% (248 cases) were conscripts. Underlying conditions were rare, with only 12 cases (2.7%) of asthma and 1 case (0.2%) of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease reported.

Serotype distribution and co-infections

We conducted serotyping in 169 cases (37.6% of the total) and found that HAdV-14 had nearly replaced the historically dominant HAdV-55, accounting for 98.8% (167 cases) of all typed infections (Table 1). We primarily performed serotyping at Hongcheon Hospital in 145 of the 158 patients who presented (Supplementary Table 1). We observed no significant differences between the groups regarding age and co-infection status.

We identified co-infections with other respiratory viruses in 33 cases (7.3% of the total). HMPV was the most common co-infecting virus, found in 14 cases (42.4%), followed by HPIV (n = 12, 36.4%), HRSV (n = 5, 15.2%), IFV B (n = 2, 6.1%), and IFV A (n = 1, 3.0%).

Clinical presentation and healthcare utilization

Of the 450 patients, 445 (98.9%) were symptomatic (Table 2). Respiratory symptoms predominated, occurring in 425 cases (94.4%), and included cough (n = 368, 81.8%), sputum (n = 351, 78.0%), rhinorrhea and nasal obstruction (n = 294, 65.3%), and sore throat (n = 275, 61.1%). We observed gastrointestinal symptoms in 150 cases (33.3%), with diarrhea in 97 cases (21.6%) and nausea/vomiting in 94 cases (20.9%). Seventeen cases presented with only gastrointestinal symptoms without respiratory involvement; we did not test these with a gastrointestinal pathogen panel, raising the possibility that HAdV detection in respiratory samples could be incidental or a bystander effect in some cases. Other symptoms were present in 410 cases (91.1%), with fever and chills observed in 399 (88.7%).

Clinicians administered antibiotics in 82.2% (n = 370) of cases. Antibiotic use was mostly empirical, often prescribed for upper respiratory symptoms in outpatient settings due to limited access to military hospitals in remote units. Clinicians used antiviral medications in 1.3% (n = 6) of cases, though records did not detail specific indications. They also prescribed steroids in 6.9% (n = 31) and provided oxygen therapy in 0.7% (n = 3) of cases. Pneumonia was diagnosed in 122 patients (27.1%).

We observed outpatient visits in 240 cases (53.3%), with an average of 1.2 visits per case. Hospitalization was required in 118 cases (26.2%), with a mean duration of 7.4 days. Severe clinical outcomes were rare, with only one case requiring transfer to a civilian hospital and another requiring ICU admission.

DISCUSSION

This study found a sharp resurgence of HAdV infections in the Korean military from late 2023 to mid-2024, following the relaxation of COVID-19 preventive measures. This resurgence reflects the impact of returning to pre-pandemic social behaviors and the heightened vulnerability of military personnel living in close quarters. We also identified an unprecedented shift toward HAdV-14 dominance in serotype distribution, indicating post-pandemic changes in respiratory viral ecology. These findings highlight the need for robust surveillance systems and proactive preventive strategies in military settings.

Our analysis revealed geographic clustering of HAdV cases, with Armed Forces Hongcheon Hospital reporting the largest concentration. This pattern pointed to localized transmission hotspots rather than a uniform spread across military installations. Of the 450 cases, 140 were from units directly under Army Headquarters, including 137 from the Army Education Command. Among them, 115 cases were from the Army Consolidated Logistics School’s Transport Training Unit. Within this unit, the 1st Transport Education Regiment accounted for 70 cases, all of which received treatment at Hongcheon Hospital, and 67 of these occurred after May 2024. The 3rd Transport Education Regiment contributed 45 cases, with 5 treated at Hongcheon Hospital and 35 at Chuncheon Hospital. This pattern may stem from unit-specific environmental factors, such as high-density living conditions and shared training environments, which facilitate the spread of viruses. The Transportation Training Regiment, which reported the highest number of cases, serves as a facility where recruits undergo approximately 2–3 weeks of specialized driver training after completing basic training at the Army Training Center. Most soldiers live in large, shared quarters, sleeping closely together on bedding arrangements without bed frames, resulting in high occupant density. Furthermore, during automobile training, multiple personnel share the same equipment, which scholars have reported to increase the potential for transmission. In response, the Center implemented measures such as pre-training vehicle disinfection, enhanced personal hygiene practices after training, and isolation of symptomatic individuals. As a result, the monthly case counts decreased to 20 in August and 15 in September, and the incidence among new trainees also declined.

We interpret the sharp increase in HAdV infections as part of a broader global resurgence of respiratory viruses following the COVID-19 pandemic. Zhao et al.,14 using a global dataset, documented an unprecedented resurgence of respiratory viruses, including HAdV, after governments relaxed non-pharmaceutical interventions. Their study found that HRV, HRSV, HCoV, HAdV, and HPIV resurged earlier than other viruses and followed a consistent sequence across global locations. They attributed this consistency to virus-specific transmission factors such as immune durability, preexisting prevalence, virus–virus interactions, and inherent transmissibility.

We also observed a similar trend in Korea’s civilian population. Since April 2023, HAdV infections have surged significantly.7 An analysis of national surveillance data by Lee et al.15 reported that the HAdV detection rate peaked at 42.2% in week 34 of 2023, an increase from the pre-pandemic average of 8.2% and the pandemic period average of 6.1% (2020–2022).

Although we performed serotyping on only 37.6% of HAdV-infected cases, we observed a notable and unprecedented shift in serotype distribution, with HAdV-14 emerging as the dominant strain. Historically, HAdV-55, a recombinant of types 11 and 14,16 had predominated in HAdV infections within the Korean military.61112 HAdV-55 has been reported primarily in Korea and China and associated with severe respiratory diseases, including pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and fatalities.61012171819202122 Researchers have also recognized HAdV-14 as a strain capable of causing severe outcomes. Since 2006, multiple outbreaks at U.S. military bases have documented HAdV-14 infections resulting in fatal pneumonia.32123242526 Civilian populations in the United States and Ireland have also reported HAdV-14-related fatalities.23272829 Our study found a relatively high hospitalization rate of 26.2%, which may partly reflect conservative admission practices in military settings, where clinicians prioritize monitoring and isolation over clinical severity alone. Nevertheless, the rate still highlights the need for caution regarding HAdV-14.

Interestingly, this serotype shift in the military differs from that in the Korean civilian population, where HAdV-3 became predominant post-pandemic.15 However, recent surveillance data indicate a continuous detection of HAdV-14 in civilians since March 2024, potentially signaling an emerging overlap in viral circulation.30 While these differences do not definitively indicate that outbreaks in the military originated from the civilian population, it is possible that low-level circulation of HAdV-14 in the community contributed to its introduction and subsequent amplification within military settings. These findings underscore the need for ongoing molecular surveillance to detect emerging viral strains before they cause large-scale outbreaks.

In our study population, HAdV infection presented predominantly with respiratory symptoms, consistent with previously reported patterns of adenovirus infection. However, we also observed a relatively high prevalence of gastrointestinal symptoms. Diarrhea occurred in 21.6% of cases, and nausea or vomiting appeared in 20.9%—both higher than the previously reported rates of diarrhea (12.8–20.7%) and nausea/vomiting (17.2%) during outbreaks dominated by HAdV-55.61012

We acknowledge several limitations in interpreting our findings. First, we lacked serotype data for 62.4% of the cases, potentially biasing our understanding of serotype distribution. Second, by analyzing only newly occurring medical utilization within seven days of testing, we may have underestimated overall utilization. Third, we included only viruses consistently tested across all respiratory virus PCR panels, which may have led us to underestimate the actual prevalence of co-infections and their effect on disease severity. Despite not testing all cases, we detected HRV and HCoV in 80 (17.78%) and 17 (3.78%) cases, respectively. Fourth, because we did not include a matched comparator group of non-HAdV respiratory infections during the same period, we urge caution in interpreting the relative burden of HAdV. Fifth, we could not adequately assess detailed clinical courses, including specific indications for treatment and the presence of secondary infections, due to limited information in the medical records.

Despite these limitations, our findings offer valuable insights into the evolving epidemiology of HAdV in the Korean military following the COVID-19 pandemic. Notably, the resurgence of HAdV outbreaks that were common before the pandemic, combined with large-scale outbreaks in the military from serotypes that circulated at low levels in the civilian population, underscores the high vulnerability of military personnel and emphasizes the need for interventions like vaccinations. Currently, no HAdV vaccination program is in place in the Korean military. Given that reintroducing HAdV-4 and HAdV-7 vaccines reduced HAdV infections in the US military,31 similar strategies could be beneficial in Korea. Since the prevalent serotypes differ in the Korean military, developing a vaccine specifically targeting HAdV-14 would be particularly essential.

Future research should explore the long-term impacts of these epidemiological shifts on adenovirus transmission dynamics and disease severity. Researchers should also analyze the genomic characteristics of the currently circulating HAdV-14 strain to determine if it represents a distinct variant with altered virulence or transmissibility. Comparative studies on HAdV serotype distribution in military and civilian populations could reveal whether military outbreaks originate from civilian sources. Simultaneous analysis of serotype patterns in both populations would help clarify how the shift in dominant serotypes occurred in the military. Such research could clarify whether the serotype shift is specific to the military or part of broader ecological changes, providing insights into HAdV introduction and transmission. Researchers should also consider viral evolution, altered competitive dynamics among circulating adenovirus strains, and changes in population immunity during the COVID-19 pandemic.
