Hypusination of the translation factor eIF5A regulates mitochondrial tRNA processing to promote prostate cancer aggressiveness

ABSTRACT
Protein synthesis plays a central role in cancer development and progression. eukaryotic initiation factor 5 A (eIF5A), a translation factor activated by hypusination, is implicated in tumorigenesis, however, its mode of action is still unclear. We find that hypusinated eIF5A (eIF5Ahyp) promotes metastasis and tumor growth in prostate cancer (PCa) by supporting mitochondrial metabolism and translation. eIF5Ahyp controls the subcellular localization of Mitochondrial Ribonuclease P Protein 3 (MRPP3) mRNA encoding a protein essential for mitochondrial tRNA (mt-tRNA) maturation. We show that eIF5Ahyp regulates the nuclear export of MRPP3 mRNA, its expression, thereby promoting mt-tRNA maturation. Our findings establish that MRPP3 enhances mitochondrial metabolism and supports PCa metastasis. Importantly, its expression restores mitochondrial translation and tumor growth inhibited by the downregulation of eIF5Ahyp. Together, we uncover a critical role for eIF5Ahyp in mitochondrial protein synthesis and highlight its broader implications in coordinating the expression of nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, linking hypusination to cancer progression.

Introduction

Cancer cell growth and tumor progression are fundamentally driven by increased protein synthesis and metabolic reprogramming, two closely interconnected processes1. The translation factor eukaryotic initiation factor 5 A (eIF5A) plays a crucial role in this context, being involved in the initiation, elongation and termination of translation for a specific subset of proteins2. There are two known highly homologous (91%) isoforms of eIF5A: eIF5A and eIF5A2, encoded by two different genes located on two different chromosomes. However, eIF5A2 expression is weak in the prostate3. One of the defining features of both isoforms of eIF5A is that they undergo hypusination, a unique and highly conserved post-translational modification essential for its activity4. This modification, dependent on the polyamine spermidine, is catalyzed by two enzymes: deoxyhypusine synthase (DHPS) and the deoxyhypusine hydroxylase (DOHH).

Polyamines play an important role in cell proliferation, apoptosis, protein and nucleotide synthesis5. Previous studies, including our own, have demonstrated that polyamines are essential for metastasis in prostate cancer (PCa)6,7, and their levels are significantly elevated in PCa tissues compared to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) or normal prostate tissues7. These findings point to the polyamine/hypusination axis as a central player in PCa progression. Yet, despite the established link between hypusinated eIF5A (eIF5Ahyp) and neoplasia, the underpinning mechanisms of its pro-tumorigenic actions remained elusive. According to the GLOBOCAN study, PCa is the second most diagnosed cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer death among men worldwide8. Removal of the prostate (prostatectomy) and radiotherapy are treatments for localized tumors. For more advanced PCa, anti-androgen treatments and/or chemotherapy (Docetaxel) are prescribed. However, patients quickly develop resistance, leading to fatal outcomes. Therefore, there is a critical need to discover therapies that target new molecular pathways.

Emerging evidence suggests that tumor cells hijack the translation machinery to selectively promote the expression of oncogenic proteins, thereby fueling cancer progression9. mRNA translation is an energy-intensive process, consuming 30 to 50% of the cell’s energy, primarily supplied by mitochondria10. Recent evidence highlights eIF5A’s role in mitochondrial activity in macrophage polarization and senescence11,12. However, the implications of hypusination in mitochondrial metabolism in cancer progression have yet to be understood.

Mitochondria possess their own genome, consisting of 2 ribosomal RNAs, 22 tRNAs, and 13 encoding proteins, which are essential components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain13. The genome is transcribed as a single polycistronic unit, requiring precise post-transcriptional modifications to generate functional and mature RNAs. These processes are controlled by nuclear-encoded proteins and require accurate coordination between the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes to maintain mitochondrial function and integrity14.

In this study, we show that eIF5Ahyp mediates the nuclear-mitochondrial dialog by regulating the expression of the mitochondrial ribonuclease P protein 3 (MRPP3), a nuclear-encoded endonuclease essential for the maturation of mitochondrial tRNAs (mt-tRNA). Our data reveal that both eIF5Ahyp and its modifying enzyme DHPS are upregulated in PCa tumors, and elevated DHPS expression correlates with poor prognosis. Conversely, depletion of eIF5Ahyp suppresses PCa cell aggressiveness, reduces the growth of patient-derived tumoroids and limits metastasis in vivo. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that eIF5Ahyp regulates mitochondrial activity and the maturation of mt-tRNA through the downregulation of MRPP3. Consistent with the role of eIF5Ahyp, we identify MRPP3 as a pro-oncogenic factor that drives tumor growth and progression.

Results

eIF5Ahyp and DHPS are upregulated in human PCa

To determine the role of eIF5Ahyp in PCa, we carried out an in-depth bioinformatic analysis using several public RNA and proteomic databases and assessed hypusination in patient samples. Analysis of the CamCaP Study Group dataset (GSE70770)15, which includes benign and tumor tissues from 259 patients, revealed that the key regulators of hypusination: DHPS, DOHH and EIF5A were significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1a, b). Consistent with the major role of polyamines in PCa, genes encoding enzymes involved in polyamine metabolism, such as the ornithine decarboxylase 1 (ODC1), spermidine synthase (SRM) and spermine synthase (SMS), were also significantly upregulated in tumors (Fig. 1a, b). We further analyzed the expression of DHPS, DOHH, EIF5A and EIF5A2 using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from Heidegger et al.16. Integrated analysis of tumor and normal samples (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a–e) revealed a significant increase in the expression of EIF5A (p = 6 × 10−5), DOHH (p = 1.8 × 10−6), and DHPS (p < 2.2 × 10−16) within tumoral epithelial cells compared to normal epithelial cells (Fig. 1d). Further analysis of four databases (TCGA, Grasso, Lapointe and Varambally)17–19 showed a correlation between the expression of the genes of the hypusination module (eIF5A1, eIF5A2, DHPS and DOHH) (Supplementary Fig. 1f), however, only DHPS was consistently and significantly upregulated in PCa (Supplementary Fig. 1h). Remarkably, patients with high levels of DHPS also showed significantly higher expression of DOHH and EIF5A (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Since PCa is dependent on androgens, expression analysis of the hypusination genes and AR was performed, but no significant correlation was found between hypusination genes and AR hallmark genes signature (Supplementary Fig. 1g).

To determine if hypusination plays a role in PCa metastasis, we used Cancertool20, a web-based platform for cancer gene expression analysis. DHPS was consistently overexpressed with the progression of the disease from primary tumors to metastases (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1h). Interestingly, our analysis of lymph node involvement (N) and distant metastasis (M), using the OncoDB online database, confirmed the rise in DHPS levels as the disease progressed (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). We then analyzed the expression of the hypusination mRNAs in a database of metastatic castration prostate cancer patients (mCRPC), the most advanced form of PCa21. Our results showed a significant increase in DHPS mRNA levels in neuroendocrine prostate cancer—a rare but highly aggressive subtype of PCa compared to adenocarcinoma—whereas DOHH, EIF5A, and EIF5A2 levels remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Finally, high DHPS mRNAs levels are associated with lower overall survival in prostate cancer patients based on the TCGA database (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Altogether, our data show that DHPS overexpression is associated with metastatic PCa and poor prognosis.

We then analyzed the protein levels of DHPS, DOHH, and eIF5A in a proteomic database that includes 306 patients with adenocarcinoma22. Interestingly, the three proteins were significantly more expressed in tumoral than normal tissue (Supplementary Fig. 2f). To reinforce the relevance of our findings to human pathology, we assessed eIF5Ahyp, total eIF5A and DHPS protein levels by immunoblotting in the tumoral and adjacent normal tissues from PCa patients at our local hospital (CHU Nice) (Table 1 and Fig. 1h). Both eIF5Ahyp and DHPS were significantly elevated in tumors tissues (p = 0.0045 and p = 0.021, respectively) while eIF5A did not increase (Fig. 1i, j and Supplementary Fig. 2g). Inhibition of eIF5Ahyp suppresses tumor growth and PCa aggressiveness in different PCa models

To characterize the pro-tumorigenic role of eiF5Ahyp in PCa, we employed two approaches to inhibit eIF5Ahyp: knockdown (KD) of DHPS using IPTG-inducible shRNA and pharmacological inhibition with GC7, a competitive inhibitor of DHPS, in three PCa cell lines (LNCaP, DU145 and PC3) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Both DHPS knockdown and GC7 treatment led to a significant reduction in cell proliferation, migration and invasion, as observed in Boyden chamber assays (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). Notably, these interventions induced cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase (Supplementary Fig. 3d) without causing apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

To further validate these findings, we tested the effects of eIF5Ahyp inhibition on three patient-derived tumoroids (MSK-PCa1, MSK PCa3 and MSK-PCa4)23. We verified by cytometry and immunoblot that both GC7 and the downregulation of DHPS decreased eIF5Ahyp (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3f, g) and observed that it induced a significant decrease in tumoroid growth as well as tumoroids formation capacities (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4a). In contrast, GC7 had a minimal impact on the proliferation of organoids derived from normal prostate tissue (Supplementary Fig. 4b). To analyze the effects of the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp on cell division in 3D, we set up the fluorescence ubiquitin cell cycle indicator (FUCCI)-based system in tumoroids. This technique allows the tracking of cell division in live cells within their endogenous environment with high spatiotemporal resolution24. We observed that GC7 treatment decreased cell division and increased cell cycle arrest within the 3D tumoroids (Fig. 2c). We further evaluated the role of eIF5Ahyp in tumor growth in vivo using xenograft experiments. DU145 and PC3 cell lines, either expressing control shRNA (shCtl) or shDHPS, or treated with a vehicle or GC7 (10 mg/kg, i.p.), were implanted in mice. Both strategies with the two cell lines resulted in a significant reduction in tumor volume and weight (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4c–f). These results were validated in RM-1 cells, a mouse prostate cancer cell line, injected subcutaneously in syngeneic C57Bl6J mice (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). Altogether, our data clearly show that eIF5Ahyp is required for PCa cell proliferation and tumor growth.

To determine the effects on metastasis, we used an orthotopic xenograft model in which PC3 cells expressing luciferase were injected directly into the prostate of mice, following established protocols25,26. This model allows the cancer cells to grow within their native microenvironment, enabling the development of primary tumors and the formation of distant metastases. The injected cells were either transfected with control shRNA (shCtl) or shRNA targeting DHPS (shDHPS) to inhibit eIF5Ahyp. After 50 days, DHPS and eIF5Ahyp were significantly decreased in prostate tumors (Supplementary Fig. 4i) and tumor progression was assessed using the bioluminescent signal in animals. Remarkably, total luminescence was significantly reduced in mice injected with shDHPS cells, indicating lower tumor and metastasis burden (Fig. 2g). Further imaging of organs commonly invaded by metastatic cells (kidney, spleen, liver, lung and digestive track) revealed luminescent cells at distant sites, confirming metastatic spread (Supplementary Fig. 4j). While metastasis was observed in nearly all mice injected with shCtl cells, only 2 out of 7 mice injected with shDHPS cells developed metastases in the liver, spleen and kidney, and just 1 out of 7 showed metastatic spread to the digestive tract. Notably, none of the mice injected with shDHPS cells had lung metastases (Fig. 2h). Taken together, our results suggest that eIF5Ahyp is essential for promoting tumor growth and PCa metastasis.

eIF5Ahyp controls mitochondrial metabolism in PCa cells

Building on previous studies linking eIF5Ahyp to mitochondrial metabolism11, we performed metabolite profiling of DHPS KD, GC7 treated cells and control counterparts of DU145 cells. Of the 308 metabolites measured by LC-MS/MS, the steady-state levels of 71 metabolites showed significant changes in response to DHPS depletion (p < 0.05), with six intermediates of the TCA cycle (citrate, aconitate, α-ketoglutarate, succinate, fumarate and malate) significantly decreased upon eIF5Ahyp inhibition (Fig. 3a, b and Table 2). Consistent with the metabolic effects of DHPS silencing, a reduction in TCA cycle intermediates was also confirmed in cells treated with GC7 (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). To assess whether the activity of the TCA cycle was affected by eIF5Ahyp, we employed a stable isotope-tracing approach by using [13C6]-glucose (Fig. 3c). As expected, the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp, significatively decreased the abundance of aconitate (M + 2), α-ketoglutarate (M + 2), succinate (M + 2), fumarate (M + 2) and malate (M + 2) in both cell lines with a more pronounced effect in DU145 (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 5e). To determine if the decrease in eIF5Ahyp affects mitochondrial respiration, we measured the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) in PCa cells treated with GC7 or knockdown for DHPS. Although ECAR remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g), eIF5Ahyp inhibition impaired basal respiration, the maximum respiratory capacity and ATP production in DU145, LNCaP and PC3 cells (Fig. 3e, f, and Supplementary Fig. 5h). Similar effects were observed in patient-derived tumoroids, where inhibition of eIF5Ahyp decreased OCR without significantly affecting ECAR (Supplementary Fig. 5i, j). Importantly, in line with our previous results showing that mitochondria adapt their shape and structure in response to energetic stress27, the effect of eIF5Ahyp inhibition were not due to a decrease in mitochondrial content (Supplementary Fig. 5k), but were associated with mitochondrial fragmentation and cristae disorganization; a hallmark of energetic stress28 (Fig. 3g, h).

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that eIF5Ahyp is critical for maintaining mitochondrial metabolism and integrity in PCa cells.

eIF5Ahyp supports MRPP3 expression and mitochondrial t-RNA maturation

Given the role of eIF5Ahyp in mRNA translation, we examined whether hypusination influences protein synthesis. Using a puromycin incorporation assay, we demonstrated that both GC7 and DHPS knockdown reduced the synthesis of nascent proteins in PCa cells and patient-derived tumoroids, indicating inhibited mRNA translation (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). To further explore this, we conducted a polysome fractionation assay to examine the association of ribosomes with mRNA. Polysomes (actively translated mRNA, with two or more ribosomes, and monosomes, mRNA with one ribosome) were separated by zonal ultra-centrifugation on sucrose gradient according to their molecular weight and shape, and the different fractions were quantified as described previously29. We observed that GC7-treated cells exhibited a substantial decrease in polysomes and an increase in the 40S, 60S, and 80S ribosomal subunits, suggesting a global reduction in protein translation (Fig. 4a). A proteomic analysis identified significant downregulation of 439 proteins and upregulation of 85 proteins upon DHPS depletion in DU145 cells (Fig. 4b). Similar trends, with less protein affected, were seen in LNCaP cells, where 161 proteins were downregulated and 30 upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Pathway enrichment analysis of the downregulated proteins highlighted disrupted mitochondrial translation and function as key processes affected by eIF5Ahyp depletion (Supplementary Fig. 6e). We performed polysome sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 6f) and integrated the data with the proteomic study to identify common pathways using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). We found that 26 pathways were commonly regulated (Fig. 4c). Notably, 11 of the top 15 pathways were associated with mitochondrial metabolism, including the mitochondrial protein-containing complex (Fig. 4d). To validate the role of hypusination in mitochondrial mRNA translation, we measured mitochondrial translation in cells treated with either vehicle, GC7, or chloramphenicol, a known inhibitor of mitochondrial protein synthesis. Consistent with chloramphenicol’s effects, GC7 treatment significantly decreased mitochondrial translation (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Supporting these findings, data from the Heidegger single-cell RNA seq (scRNA-seq) patient database revealed a positive correlation between high DHPS and EIF5A expression and genes involved in mitochondrial translation in epithelial prostatic cells (Supplementary Fig. 6h–k). This establishes a clear link between eIF5Ahyp and mitochondrial translation. In agreement with these results, our proteomic analysis further identified the downregulation of the family of the Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins in DU145 and LNCaP cells upon eIF5Ahyp depletion (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 6d). PPR proteins are essential RNA-binding proteins involved in mitochondrial RNA processing and translation30. Given its essential role as a regulator of mitochondrial RNA maturation and function, both in vitro and in vivo, MRPP3 emerged as a key target31,32. MRPP3 was one of the most significantly downregulated proteins following eIF5Ahyp inhibition, with a Log2 fold change of − 2.99 in DU145 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, we found that MRPP3 protein levels significantly correlate with the expression of DHPS, DOHH, eIF5A and A2 in the Sun proteomic database that includes 306 patients (Supplementary Fig. 7b). To confirm these findings, we validated the downregulation of MRPP3 protein levels in DU145, LNCaP and the three patient-derived tumoroids following GC7 treatment or DHPS KD (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 7c). To study the role of MRPP3 in the effects of eIF5Ahyp on mitochondrial translation, we overexpressed it in cells treated with GC7 (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Interestingly, elevated MRPP3 levels rescued the downregulation of mitochondrial translation induced by the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp (Fig. 4f) demonstrating that eIF5A regulates mitochondrial translation through MRPP3. MRPP3 functions as the catalytic enzyme of the RNaseP protein complex that regulates the maturation of mitochondrial tRNA (mt-tRNA)32 (Fig. 4h). To study the function of MRPP3 in the context of low hypusination level, the amount of immature tRNA was quantified in DU145 cells and tumoroids. Consistent with the decrease of MRPP3 caused by eIF5Ahyp inhibition, we observed a 2.7- and 8.2-fold increase in unprocessed mt-tRNAVal and mt-tRNAIle, respectively, following GC7 treatment (Fig. 4i). Additionally, we sorted dividing and arrested cells from FUCCI-expressing tumoroids (Fig. 2c) and quantified the relative amount of immature tRNA. Decreased Ki67 expression in GC7-treated and arrested cells confirmed their reduced proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 7e), and these cells also exhibited increased levels of immature mt-tRNAVal and mt-tRNAIle (Fig. 4j). To independently assess tRNA maturation, we analyzed pre-RNA cleavage by RNA-seq. A defect in the processing of mt-tRNA can be detected by the analysis of the reads in the region spanning the mitochondrial tRNA and mRNA sequences, as previously described by Münch and Harper31. When tRNA processing is malfunctioning, sequence reads from the contiguous mRNA can extend into tRNA, resulting in an increase of reads. Interestingly, we observed a significant increase in the reads spanning the regions between tRNA and ND2, COX1 and COX2 mRNA, indicating an accumulation of immature mt-tRNA when eIF5A was inhibited (Fig. 4k). Altogether, our results show that eIF5Ahyp inhibition reduces MRPP3 expression and mt-tRNA maturation, resulting in the decrease of mitochondrial translation.

eIF5Ahyp controls the nuclear export of MRPP3 mRNA

We next aimed to investigate how hypusination regulates the expression of MRPP3. eIF5A is a nucleocytoplasmic shuttle protein and a RNA-binding protein33,34. Furthermore, MRPP3 mRNA contains the sequence motif AAAUGU necessary for the binding to eIF5A as described by Xu et al.35. Thus, we postulated that eIF5A binds to MRPP3 mRNA and exports it to the cytosol to be translated. Using a RIP-qPCR assay, we validated the binding of eIF5A to MRPP3 mRNA in DU145 and PC3 cells (Fig. 5a). In accordance with previous studies36, we also showed that eIF5A binds to c-MYC mRNA but not RPLP0, GAPDH mRNA. Then, we examined whether hypusination interferes with the cellular localization of eIF5A in PCa cells by immunofluorescence. Similarly to our positive control, leptomycin B, a potent inhibitor of nuclear export37, the treatment with GC7 or the KD of DHPS significantly increased the amount of eIF5A in the nucleus compared to control cells (Fig. 5b, c). This result suggests, as previous described38, that hypusination is required to target eIF5A in the cytoplasm. To demonstrate that eIF5A hypusination directly interferes with the transfer of MRPP3 mRNA in the cytosol, we performed cellular fractionation and quantified MRPP3 mRNA by q-PCR in the nuclear and cytosolic fractions of DU145 cells treated with GC7. To exclude an effect on the transcription of MRPP3, we first analyzed MRPP3 mRNA expression and showed that GC7 and leptomycin B did not affect MRPP3 mRNA levels (Fig. 5d). In accordance with the role of eIF5A in the nuclear-cytosolic transfer, GC7 led to the accumulation of MRPP3 and c-MYC mRNA in the nucleus whereas RPLP0 mRNA was not affected (Fig. 5e), showing that hypusination is required for the nuclear export of MRPP3 mRNA. Of note, leptomycin B increased MRPP3, RPLP0 and c-MYC mRNA in the nucleus and it also decreased the expression of MRPP3 and c-MYC, suggesting that nuclear export is a general mechanism that controls the expression of both proteins (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Importantly, we also observed that both immature mt-tRNAVal and mt-tRNAIle were significantly increased upon leptomycin B treatment (Supplementary Fig. 8b). MRPP3 is a pro-oncogenic protein that regulates mitochondrial function and PCa metastasis

To investigate the role of MRPP3 in PCa cell metabolism and tumorigenesis, we generated DU145 and PC3 cell lines with stable knockdown of MRPP3 (shMRPP3) (Supplementary Fig. 9a) and conducted steady-state metabolomics and Seahorse analysis to assess mitochondrial function. Similarly to the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp, the downregulation of MRPP3, significatively decreased the abundance of citrate (M + 2), α-ketoglutarate (M + 2), fumarate (M + 2) and malate (M + 2) (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 9b). In addition, silencing of MRPP3 decreased basal respiration, oxygen consumption, maximum respiratory capacities and ATP production in both cancer cell lines, indicating impaired mitochondrial function (Fig. 6b). Finally, downregulation of MRPP3 caused drastic changes in mitochondrial morphology, including increased circularity and disorganized cristae structure (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). Next, we examined whether the alterations of metabolism caused by MRPP3 silencing translated to changes in tumor growth and aggressiveness. In vitro, MRPP3 knockdown significantly reduced the migration and invasion capabilities of PC3 and DU145 cells (Supplementary Fig. 9e). Consistent with these results, MRPP3 depletion led to a significant decrease in the growth of patient-derived tumoroids, highlighting its role in supporting tumor cell viability and expansion (Fig. 6c). To determine if MRPP3 has a pro-tumoral role in vivo, we performed subcutaneous injections of PCa cells. Remarkably, MRPP3 downregulation significantly decreased the tumor growth of both DU145 and PC3 in a xenograft model and RM-1 in syngeneic mice (Fig. 6d, e and Supplementary Fig. 9f–h). Since MRPP3 expression rescued mitochondrial translation after the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp (Fig. 4f), we examined whether it could overcome the anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects of low eIF5Ahyp in vitro. Indeed, exogenous expression of MRPP3 restored the migratory and invasive capacities of DU145 treated with GC7 (Supplementary Fig. 9i, j). We next validated the contribution of MRPP3 in eIF5A effects in vivo. Notably, the expression of MRPP3 in DU145 cells, which have low levels of eIF5Ahyp, restored tumor growth in a xenograft model (Fig. 6f, g and Supplementary Fig. 10a). Similarly, MRPP3 overexpression counteracted partially the anti-tumoral effects resulting from the downregulation of DHPS in the RM-1 syngeneic model (Supplementary Fig. 10b–d) demonstrating that eIF5Ahyp plays a role in neoplasia through MRPP3. To further characterize the role of MRPP3 in PCa, we analyzed the formation of metastasis after injection of PC3-Luc cells expressing shMRPP3 in the prostate. Monitoring luminescence allowed us to track tumor spread, and inhibition of MRPP3 led to a significant reduction in total luminescence, indicating lower tumor burden (Fig. 6h). Moreover, metastasis analysis showed that only 1 out of 8 mice injected with cells depleted for MRPP3 developed metastases at two sites (spleen and digestive tract), while 100% control (shCtl)-injected mice exhibited widespread metastasis in the liver, spleen, and kidney (Fig. 6i and Supplementary Fig. 10e). To implement these results, MRPP3 expression was analyzed in clinical samples from our local cohort of PCa patients. MRPP3 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, further supporting its role as a pro-tumorigenic factor (Fig. 6j, k). Similarly, we found that MRPP3 is significantly more expressed in PCa tumors than prostate tissue from patients with Benign Prostate Hyperplasia (BPH) (Supplementary Fig. 10f). Interestingly, the analysis of Sun’s proteomic database confirmed that MRPP3 is overexpressed in tumors22 (Supplementary Fig. 10g). Of note, while we did not find a correlation between MRPP3 and DHPS or eIF5Ahyp in our cohort, Sun’s database revealed a correlation between the expression of MRPP3, DHPS, and eIF5A (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

In conclusion, our data demonstrate that MRPP3 is a protein driving the pro-oncogenic effects of eIF5A. It maintains mitochondrial function and controls tumor progression in PCa (Fig. 7). Discussion

We demonstrate here that, in addition to playing a role in PCa metastasis, eIF5Ahyp controls mt-tRNA maturation and participates in the regulation of the coordinated expression between the nuclear and mitochondrial genomes via MRPP3. Our conclusions are based on multiple sets of clinical data and experimental results obtained from established cell lines, patient-derived tumoroids, and in vivo experiments, including a syngeneic mouse model. Mechanistically, we established that eIF5Ahyp is required for the export of MRPP3 mRNA from the nucleus to the cytoplasm to be translated, highlighting a poorly known function of eIF5A. Despite the essential role played by MRPP3 in mitochondrial function, its involvement in cancer was never studied. In accordance with the role of DHPS, we demonstrated that it regulates mitochondrial metabolism, and it is a pro-oncogenic protein implicated in metastatic PCa.

Since polyamines are elevated in prostate39 the study of hypusination is highly relevant, however, overexpression of DHPS was also observed in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC)40 and pancreatic cancer41. In both studies, GC7 inhibited ESCC and pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth in xenograft models. More recently, the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp with GC7 was also shown to limit breast cancer cell invasion in the Chicken Chorioallantoic Membrane (CAM) model through the impairment of TGFβ signaling in cells harboring p53 mutations42. Finally, eIF5Ahyp has been shown to promote cell proliferation and tumor growth in colorectal cancer by controlling MYC protein elongation36. Interestingly, in the same model, the combined inhibition of polyamine synthesis and eIF5Ahyp induced a synergistic effect by inducing apoptosis43. To a lesser extent, we also observed a decrease in MYC expression upon treatment with GC7 in PCa cells. We suggest that this may be partly due to the inhibition of the nuclear export of c-MYC mRNA. Thus, although these reports show a pro-oncogenic role for eIF5Ahyp, we demonstrate that it is also implicated in the formation of metastasis. Therefore, we have identified a new therapeutic target for advanced PCa.

In contrast with these studies, Gobert et al. showed that eIF5Ahyp protects against oxidative damage and limits the risk of neoplastic lesions in colon44. They demonstrated that patients with colitis have reduced DHPS expression, and deletion of DHPS in intestinal epithelial cells leads to spontaneous inflammation and increased tumorigenesis in mice. The expression of numerous enzymes involved in the detoxification of peroxides and reactive aldehydes was decreased in DHPS KO colonic epithelial cells proving that eIF5Ahyp plays a role in the protection against oxidative damage and colon carcinogenesis and point to a cell-specific effect of eiF5A in the development of cancer. More recently, Doglioni et al. demonstrated that Aspartate promotes the N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor activity, and this is required for DOHH mRNA expression in breast cancer cells metastasis. We analyzed the expression of DOHH and NMDA (Grin2d) in primary tumors and the metastasis of PCa orthotopic xenografts and did not find significant changes in mRNA expression, suggesting that this mechanism may not be implicated in PCa.

Studies conducted by Kahana’s group revealed that GC7 inhibits both initiation and elongation, whereas shRNA targeting eIF5A specifically inhibits elongation45. This discrepancy may be due to the off-target effects of GC7. However, eIF5A has been involved in all stages of translation. It regulates initiation46 and more specifically the fidelity of start codon initiation4, but it also plays a crucial role in the elongation and termination phases2,47. In our study, we downregulated DHPS and utilized GC7. Notably, both interventions exhibited similar effects on PCa cells and tumor growth, and they similarly influenced MRPP3 expression, minimizing potential but still possible off-target effects of GC7.

Our study shows that eIF5Ahyp regulates mitochondrial activity and the TCA cycle, demonstrating that the inhibition of hypusination induces an energetic stress with a decrease in ATP production. We observed that AR-positive cells, including LNCaP, normal organoids, and MSK-PCa3 tumoroids, exhibit lower levels of translation. This confirms the findings of Liu et al., which demonstrate that AR acts as a negative regulator of protein synthesis48. Furthermore, inhibiting hypusination in these cells had a less pronounced effect on metabolism. This suggests that cells with low translation levels may be less sensitive to hypusination inhibition, an effect that impacts more specifically cell metabolism.

In the context of protective immunity, Puleston et al. demonstrated that eIF5A hypusination regulates macrophage polarization by modulating mitochondrial respiration11. They showed that the downregulation of eIF5Ahyp decreases the translation of specific proteins important for mitochondrial functions. In fact, these proteins have a mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) that confers an extra-dependency on eIF5A for translation. More recently, in senescent cells, a study showed that p53 maintains spermidine levels and high eIF5A hypusination to sustain mitochondrial protein synthesis12. Surprisingly, they found that GC7 affects protein expression in senescent cells but not in proliferating cells. Unlike our study, these results were not validated by genetic modulation of DHPS. Furthermore, the authors did not detect modification of MRPP3 expression upon GC7 treatment, clearly suggesting that eIF5Ahyp is acting differently in proliferating PCa cells. This discrepancy may be explained by the specificity of PCa cells. Indeed, the high concentration of polyamines and DHPS observed in the prostate may confer a specific mode of action of eIF5Ahyp in prostate tumors.

Besides the well-established function of eIF5A as a translation factor, it is also an RBP that acts as a nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttle protein34. We showed here that MRPP3 mRNA is a target for eIF5A. Lee et al. found that the nuclear vs cytosolic location of eIF5A is regulated by hypusination38 but also acetylation49. Consistently, we demonstrate that the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp decreases MRPP3 mRNA and eIF5A nuclear export. Other examples of eIF5A-mediated transport between the two cellular compartments have been described for different mRNA such as Nos234 or CD8350 highlighting a function for eIF5A different from its canonical role of translation factor. MRPP3 is essential for the maturation of tRNA, and it is required for mitochondrial translation. Most of the proteins required for the mitochondrial function are encoded by the nuclear genome, thus, a specific communication between mitochondria and the nucleus has been developed by cells51. Indeed, the nucleus modulates gene expression and mitochondrial function via anterograde signaling. Conversely, mitochondria regulate nuclear genes expression through the retrograde response. Here, we show that eIF5A through the regulation of the nuclear export of MRPP3 mRNA, plays a role in anterograde signaling and fine-tuning of mitochondrial function. MRPP3 is the catalytic ribonuclease component of the Mitochondrial RNaseP complex encompassing MRPP1 (TRMT10C gene), MRPP2 (HSD17B10 gene). and MRPP3 (PRORP gene) form the RNase P complex. The loss of MRPP3 in vivo has been shown to abolish mt-tRNA processing, leading to the accumulation of immature mt-tRNA and dysfunction of the mitochondrial respiratory complexes32. Of note, a recent study reports that MRPP3 is also linked to the cleavage of mt-mRNA, a function that diverges from the canonical role of the protein52. Here, we demonstrate that the MRPP3 is a pro-oncogenic and pro-metastatic protein in PCa. The role of MRPP3 in human disease is poorly known, and few reports have described a relationship between defects in mitochondrial RNA processing and pathologies. Mutations in PRORP (the gene encoding for MRPP3) were observed in some patients with insulin resistance and multi-system diseases53,54. Interestingly, a defect in mitochondrial metabolism was associated with these mutations in both pathologies. However, the mutations in PRORP induce defects in the maturation of mt-tRNA in multi-system diseases whereas the processing of pre-mt-tRNA was not altered in insulin-resistance mice suggesting another function for MRPP3.

In conclusion, our results reveal that the RBP (RNA-binding protein) function of eIF5A controls MRPP3 expression and PCa metastasis. This provides a molecular basis for developing novel therapies targeting mitochondrial function in advanced PCa, for which few therapeutic options are available.

Methods

Ethical statement

The study utilized human prostate tissue samples obtained from PCa patients who gave informed written consent before undergoing radical prostatectomy at the CHU Hospital of Nice in 2023. This research was performed in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki, and it was approved by the French national ethics committee under the CODECOH number DC2022-4966.

Mice were fed a standard chow diet (Cat#A04, Safe) and housed in a temperature-controlled room under a 12-h light–dark cycle under pathogen-free conditions. Experimental protocols were validated by the National Animal Care and Use Committee and the regional ethics commission (Ciepal Azur), and the French Ministry of Research under the reference: APAFIS#41336-20230208 100 1830 v7, following the European regulation on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes (EU directive 2010/63/EU.

Statistics, reproducibility and data analysis

All experiments were performed at least three independent times unless stated. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 9.0 using appropriate test: two-tailed Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney or ANOVA as indicated in the figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Allocation of samples and mice was random in all experiments. The investigators were not blinded during experiments outcome assessment. Not all replications were successful due to technical problems in some experiments.

Patient samples were analyzed by two pathologists to isolate the normal tumor tissue from the prostatectomy. For animal experiments, 6 to 10 mice were used as indicated in each figure legend. For the experiments with GC7, mice were randomly distributed in the control or treated group. All data are available upon request to the corresponding author.

Patients

At diagnosis, the patients’ ages ranged from 54 to 77 years, their clinical characteristics are described in Table 1.

Cell culture and cell lines

DU145 (cat# HTB-81), LNCaP (cat#CRL-1740), and PC3 (cat#CRL-1435) are human PCa cell lines purchased from ATCC. RM-1 (cat#CRL-3310) is a mouse prostate cancer cell line from ATCC. The cells are used at low passage after purchase and tested monthly for mycoplasma contamination. Cells are passed whenever they reach 70% confluence. DU145 and PC3 cells are cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco), LNCaP cells are cultured in RPMI1640 medium (Gibco). Culture medium is supplemented with 10% FBS (Dutscher) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 μg/ml respectively (Gibco). Cells are incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For the inhibition of eIF5Ahyp cells were treated with GC7 (N-guanyl-1,7-diaminoheptane synthesized by AtlanChim Pharma, Nantes, France and Sigma-Aldrich) at 40 µM for 24 h in all the experiments.

To generate cell lines and tumoroids that express IPTG-inducible shDHPS, we infected the cells with lentivirus containing plasmid pLKO-puro-IPTG-3xLacO, which holds: Clone TRCN0000330717, ref seq NM_001930 (Sigma-Aldrich) and then the cells were selected with puromycin 1 µg/ml for 1 week, to inhibit DHPS, the cells were induced with IPTG 250 µM for 5 days.

We also generated cell lines and tumoroids expressing shCtl, shMRPP3 and shDHPS by infecting them with lentiviruses containing the plasmids: (pLV[shRNA]-EGFP/Neo-U6 > Scramble_shRNA), (pLV[shRNA]-EGFP:T2A:Neo-U6>hPRORP[shRNA#1](VB900138-9190phn),(pLV[shRNA]-EGFP:T2A:Neo-U6 > hDHPS[shRNA#1] (VB900141-4950rcu), and we selected resistant clones with Geneticin 400 µg/ml (Gibco) for 3 weeks. To overexpress the MRPP3 protein, we inset the MRPP3 coding sequence (addgene plasmid #67866) into FUmGW plasmid (addgene plasmid #22479) and selected resistant cells with 300 µg/ml zeocin (Thermofisher) for 4 weeks.

Patient-derived prostate cancer tumoroids

Patient-derived tumoroid MSK-PCa1 (AR-, Syp-), MSK-PCa3 (AR + , Syp-), MSK-PCa4 (AR-, Syp + ) and normal prostate organoids (AR + , Syp-) are obtained through a collaboration with Yu Chen’s laboratory (MSKCC, New York) and were maintained in the culture medium as described previously23, the culture medium was refreshed every 3 days, and the tumoroids are passaged every 2 weeks.

To measure the size of the tumoroids, 10000 cells per well were seeded in a 24-well plate. After one week of growth, the tumoroids were treated for another week with GC7 at 80 µM, or IPTG (500 µM) to induce the expression of shDHPS, the medium containing the treatment was replaced every 3 days, and at the end of the treatment, the tumoroids are fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min on ice. The tumoroids and organoids were imaged with a microscope (EVOS), and the size was measured using Cell Profiler software. The number of tumoroids measured for Fig. 2b is as follow: for MSK-PCa1, 3474 (Ctl) and 3778 (GC7); 1477 (shDHPS-) and 1964 (shDHPS +); for MSK-PCa3, 1743 (Ctl) and 1212 (GC7); 1520 (shDHPS-) and 1890 (dhDHPS +); and for MSK-PCa4, 3,167 (Ctl) and 3,260 (GC7); 3167 (dhDHPS-) and 3260 (shDHPS +). For Fig. 6c: 1379 (Ctl) and 932 (GC7).

Invasion assay with tumoroids

To study invasion, tumoroids cultured for a two-week period were transferred into a 2 mg/ml collagen matrix at pH7. After 48 h tumoroids were fixed with 8% PFA solution on ice for 4 h, then permeabilized with a 0.5% PBS triton solution. The tumoroids are labeled with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) and phalloidin (Thermofisher) and imaged using a Nikon confocal microscope. The number of tumoroids counted for the invasion assay in Fig. 2d is as follows: 54 (Ctl) and 60 (GC7).

Cytometry analysis of tumoroids

Tumoroids were treated 24 h with 80 µM GC7 or 6 days with 500 µM IPTG to induce the shDHPS before analysis. Treated or untreated tumoroids were recovered on ice and after dissociation, fixed and permeabilized with Cytofix/cytoperm solutions (BD biosciences). After saturation with 3% BSA, the cells were labeled with primary antibody hypusine (ProteoGenix), MRPP3 (Abcam), then the corresponding secondary antibody (Thermofisher). The cells are then analyzed with the VYB cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec).

FUCCI reporter system in tumoroids

To investigate the cell cycle in tumoroids, the Fucci reporter system was generated. Tumoroides were infected with lentivirus to express the plasmid pBOB-EF1-FastFUCCI-Puro (Addgene Plasmid #86849). After selection for 1 month with puromycin 1 µg/ml, the tumoroids were treated with GC7 80 µM for 48 h, then imaged with a Nikon spinning disk microscope, or dissociated with TrypLE Express (Gibco), then analyzed through the VYB cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec) to determine the proportion of Green and mKO2 cells. We also used an Aurora cell sorter to separate green proliferative cells from red arrested cells and extracted RNA from each condition for Mt-RNA processing analysis.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted on ice using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the reverse transcription system (Promega). Then, the cDNA is diluted 100-times, and primers (Eurofins) (sequences in supplementary material) are used at a concentration of 500 nM in a final volume of 10 µL. Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermofisher) is added, and the plate was read using a StepOnePlus System (Applied Biosystems) for qPCR analysis.

Animal experiments

For xenograft experiments, PCa cells (2.106 cells) were injected subcutaneously into 5-week-old male nude mice (strain: Rj:NMRI-Foxn1nu/Foxn1nu) mice (Janvier). Mice were injected daily with 100 µL of PBS or GC7 (10 mg/mL/kg/day), and tumor size was measured twice a week. Tumor volume was determined using the following equation (π/6) × A × B² (A = larger tumor diameter, B = smaller tumor diameter), as previously described55. The maximum tumor size permitted by our ethics committee is 1 cm³; however, none of the tumors in this study reached that limit. The experiments were concluded based on predefined timelines and humane endpoints, in compliance with EU Directive 2010/63/EU. Experiments for the DU145 model were terminated after 28 to 31 days, for the PC3 model after 20 to 25 days, and for the RM-1 model after 10 days, following prior assessments of tumor growth.

For the orthotopic prostate xenograft, PC3 cells expressing the Luciferase gene and shCtl, shDHPS or shMRPP3 (7. 105 cells) were mixed with Matrigel (Corning) in a 1:1 ratio and injected into the anterior lobe of the prostate of 6-week-old male NOD-SCIDγ mice (Envigo). Abdominal incisions were reclosed with sutures. Animals were monitored once a week for 50 days using a photon imager (Biospace Optima System system). For Biospace analysis, the animals were injected with luciferin (Revvity) (1.5 mg/mouse). At the end of the experiments, the size of the tumors was monitored, prostate, lungs, kidneys, liver, spleen and digestive tract were harvested and analyzed. We decided to end the experiment after 50 days, based on our previous studies using the same model, when metastasis appeared25.

In vitro migration and invasion assays

Migration and invasion assays were carried out as previously detailed6. Cells deprived of serum for 24 h were plated onto transparent PET inserts with 8.0 µm pores (Corning). For invasion assays, Boyden chamber inserts were coated with extracellular matrix (Sigma-Aldrich) that was diluted at a ratio of 1:10. The bottom surface of the transwells was immersed in medium containing 10% FBS and fibronectin (10 µg/ml) (Santa Cruz). After 6 h for migration assays and 24 h for invasion assays, the upper surface of the Boyden chamber was gently wiped clean with a cotton swab. Cells that migrated through the insert were then fixed, stained with DAPI (2 mg/ml), imaged using an EVOS microscope (Life Technologies) and counted using ImageJ.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were cultured at a density of 2.105 cells/well in 6-well culture plates, they were trypsinized and fixed with 70% ethanol. After treatment with 100 µg/mL RNase (Invitrogen), cells were incubated with propidium iodide (32 µg/ml) for 30 min at 4 °C and analyzed using the MACSQuant® Analyzer 10 flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec).

Electron microscopy

This experiment was carried out as reported27. Cells were fixed using 1.6% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7,4) at room temperature (RT) and subsequently at 4 °C for 16 h. After washing with sodium cacodylate buffer 0.1 M (pH: 7,4), samples were processed for 1 h at RT with 1% osmium tetroxide and 1% potassium ferrocyanide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH: 7,4). Cells were then washed with distilled water, dehydrated with ethanol and embedded in epoxy resin. Thin sections of 80 µM are contrasted with lanthanides (Gadolinium 5%, Samarium 5%) and observed with a transmission electron microscope JEM1400 (JEOL) with Morada CCD camera (Olympus SIS). Mitochondrial circularity was measured and analyzed using Image J. In Fig. 3h, the number of mitochondria analyzed is as followed: Ctl (n = 295), GC7 (n = 416), shDHPS- (n = 271) and shDHPS + (n = 203).

Analysis of mitochondrial protein synthesis

Cells seeded in 150 mm dishes at 80% of confluence and treated with puromycin (20 µg/ml) for 20 min. As a positive control, cells are treated with 100 µg/ml chloramphenicol (Sigma-Aldrich), a mitochondrial translation inhibitor, for 24 h. Cells were then rinsed with PBS, scrapped and mitochondria extracted using the mitochondrial extraction kit (Thermofisher) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated mitochondria were fixed with 2% PFA and permeabilized with 0.1% saponin buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min at 4 °C. Saturation was performed using 3% FBS for 30 min. Mitochondria were then stained with anti-puromycin antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) followed by the incubation with anti-mouse secondary antibody (Thermofisher). TOM22 coupled antibody (Miltenyi) was used for additional staining. Fluorescence was analyzed using the Aurora cytometer. Anti-TOM22 coupled antibody (Miltenyi) was used for additional staining. Fluorescence was analyzed using the Spectral Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek) equipped with 5 lasers.

Protein synthesis assay

To measure protein synthesis, 2 µg/ml puromycin is added to the DU145 culture medium and 50 µg/ml puromycin is added to DU145 cells expressing plKO shdhps, for 15 min and a positive control is also performed with cycloheximide (10 µg/ml), which inhibits protein synthesis, added 20 min before the addition of puromycin. The cells were then washed with PBS on ice, and the proteins were extracted. An immunoblot was carried out by migrating 20 µg of protein, and the anti-puromycin antibody was applied (Sigma-Aldrich).

Immunofluorescence

For the immunofluorescence assay, cells were fixed with 4% PBS/PFA for 15 min and then permeabilized with 0.2% triton solution for 5 min. Cells were then saturated with 1% PBS/BSA for 45 min and incubated with primary antibody anti-eIF5A (Bdbiosciences), anti-tom20 (BD Transduction Laboratories) (1/300) overnight at 4 °C and coupled secondary antibody (1/400) (Life Technologies) for 1 h. Nuclei were labeled using Dapi (Sigma-Aldrich), and images were taken using a Nikon A1R confocal microscope. In Fig. 3b, the number of branchs analyzed is as follow: Ctl (n = 21), GC7 (n = 20), shDHPS- (n = 21) and shDHPS (n = 29. Colocalization analysis was performed using the JaCoP plugin in Fiji to assess the degree of overlap between fluorescent signals.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblots were conducted as described previously6. Cells were lyzed using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) complemented with protease inhibitors (Mini-complete, noEDTA, Roche). Protein quantification was done using a BCA assay. Proteins were denatured with Laemmli buffer, and 20 µg of protein was loaded on the SDS-PAGE gel. The proteins were then transferred overnight at 4 °C to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies anti-hypusine (ProteoGenix), anti-eIF5A (Bd Biosciences), anti-DHPS (Bethyl Laboratories), anti-ERK2 (Santa Cruz), anti-HSP90 (Santa Cruz), anti-MRPP3 (Abcam). After rinsing, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies, anti-rabbit or anti-mouse conjugated to a horseradish peroxidase (Promega). ECL (Millipore) was used for detection on a Syngene PXi imager. All uncropped immunoblots are provided in the Source Data file.

RNA-seq

Cells cultured in a 6-well plate were detached in 1X PBS, resuspended cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, then the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was collected. The sample was immediately frozen in − 80 °C and shipped to BGI (Hong Kong) along with adequate dry ice to be analyzed. The data were analyzed using different platforms. Coverage data across the tRNA region and gene body were normalized by the total number of reads, followed by conversion to Counts per Million (CPM) for each condition (Control, GC7). This normalization accounts for differences in sequencing depth, allowing for comparison across samples. The plot compares coverage trends across these genomic regions.

Polysome profiling

DU145 Cells were seeded at 106 cells/10 cm dish, treated with 40 µM CG7 48 h later (D0) during 24 h. At the end of this treatment, cells were treated 10 min with emetin (Sigma-Aldrich) 25 µg/ml, washed twice with PBS, and harvested by trypsination. Cytoplasmic lysates were prepared by incubation of cells 10 min in hypotonic buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl, 1X CompleteTM EDTA free protease inhibitor (Roche) and 10 U/mL RNAseOutTM (Invitrogen). Cells are disrupted by shaking in the presence of ceramic beads (10 sec at 4500 rpm in a Precellys Evolution homogenizer). Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 750 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and mitochondria were pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein content of the cytosolic extract was measured by Bradford assay, and samples containing 2 mg protein were loaded on top of 15–50% sucrose gradients poured using the Gradient Master (Serlabo Technologies). After ultracentrifugation at 250,000 × g during 105 min at 4 °C on a SW41 Beckman rotor, fractions of 700 µl each were collected from each gradient while 254 nm absorbance profiles were generated using an ISCO UA-6 detector.

Polysome sequencing

RNA sequencing

RNA extractions were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq6000 machine with a paired-end protocol. Libraries were prepared with the TruSeq mRNA kit.

RNA-seq Preprocessing

Raw sequencing reads were aligned on the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.8a), with the annotation of known genes from gencode v37. Gene expression levels were then quantified using Salmon (1.4.0) and the annotation of protein-coding genes from gencode v37.

Differential expression analyses & Candidates Deregulated Genes selection

Differential expression analyses were conducted on untreated and treated samples using DESeq2 (v1.40.2), with independent processing of the POLY and CYTO cohorts. Genes lacking adjusted p-values due to low coverage were excluded, and log2 fold changes were corrected using the APEGLM shrinkage method (apeglm v1.22.1). A significance threshold (adjusted p < 0.05) and a log2FC cutoff of 1 were applied for both cohorts. Genes were categorized based on significance and direction of expression changes, distinguishing shared (BOTH_DOWN/UP) or cohort-specific (CYTO_DOWN/UP or POLY_DOWN/UP) regulation.

To better identify genes with non-parallel expression changes between CYTO and POLY, we applied a three-step filtering approach. Genes significantly associated with treatment in at least one cohort were retained. A ratio of mean expression changes in POLY vs. CYTO (thresholds > 2 or < 0.5) was used to detect gene expression inflation or deflation. Finally, a t-test (p < 0.05) assessed the significance of differences in expression changes between POLY and CYTO. Genes were classified as TRANSLA when changes were driven mainly by translation, BUFFER when transcriptional changes were counteracted by translation, and STAB when expression was not statistically different between transcriptional and translational levels. All these analyses were performed in R v4.3.1.

Bioinformatics Analysis: Sequencing was performed by the Cancer Genomics Platform (CRCL), RNA-seq quality controls and pre-processing were handled on the Gilles Thomas Bioinformatics platform (CRCL-CLB). RNA-seq Analyses were conducted collaboratively between the Gilles Thomas Bioinformatics Platform and the RibosOMICS Platform (CRCL).

Proteomic analysis

The cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer, and 15 μg of protein samples were stacked on NuPAGE™ in a single band to perform trypsin digestion before mass spectrometry analysis using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) in data-independent acquisition mode (DIA). Protein identification and quantification was processed using the DIA-NN 1.8 algorithm56 and the DIAgui package (https://github.com/marseille-proteomique/DIAgui57.

Nuclear-Cytoplasmic Fractionation

The fractionation was performed as previously described58. Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and centrifuged 400rcf. They were then resuspended in 0.1% NP-40 supplemented with RNAsine (Promega), which lyses the cytoplasmic membrane, allowing the collection of the cytoplasmic fraction. The lysate was centrifuged at 10000rcf to pellet the nuclei. The supernatant, containing the cytoplasmic fraction, was collected, and the nuclei were washed three times with 0.1% NP-40. Following this, approximately 50 passes through a 30 G syringe were performed to ensure proper nuclear lysis. RNA was then isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen).

RIP-qPCR

Cells from two 10 cm dishes were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed using an RNase-free lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 5 mM NaF, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1% NP40, complemented with protease inhibitors). After 30 min of gentle agitation, the lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was measured using a BSA assay. Protein G beads (MedChemExpress) were washed twice with the lysis buffer, and 30 µl of beads were added to the lysate for 30 min at 4 °C to eliminate non-specific binding proteins. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and 1% of it was set aside as input for analysis.

eIF5A was immunoprecipitated using 10 µg of anti-eIF5A antibody (BD Biosciences) and 100 µl of Protein G beads overnight. In parallel, a control immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-IgG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) to account for non-specific binding. The next day, the beads were recovered by centrifugation and washed five times with a high-salt RNase-free buffer (50 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 5 mM NaF, 5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1% NP40, complemented with protease inhibitors). RNA was then isolated from the beads using TRIzol. The isolated RNA was subjected to RT-qPCR to analyze the enrichment of specific RNA molecules, following the protocol provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Metabolite profiling via LC-MS

Metabolomic analysis was performed as previously described6. Cells from three 10 cm dishes at 70% confluence were refreshed with serum-free medium overnight. Metabolites were extracted using 4 ml of 80% methanol. The samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 3000rcf to pellet the insoluble material. Two additional extractions were performed on the insoluble pellet using 0.5 ml of 80% methanol, followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 16,000 × g at 4 °C. The 5 ml extracted metabolites were pooled and dried under nitrogen gas using an N-EVAP system (Organomation Associates, Inc). The dry pellet was resuspended in 20 µl of HPLC-grade water for mass spectrometry, and 7 µl of the suspension was injected into a 5500 QTRAP triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB/SCIEX) coupled to a Prominence UFLC HPLC System (Shimadzu) for the analysis of 300 endogenous water-soluble metabolites via selected reaction monitoring for steady-state analysis of the samples. We used Metaboanalyst 6.0 to carry out the analyses.

Stable isotope tracing analysis

For 13C6 glucose tracing, cells were cultured in triplicates in 10 cm dish. After being washed with PBS, glucose-free DMEM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS, and containing the tracer (13C6-D-glucose) at a 25 mM final concentration (Sigma-Aldrich), was added for 2 h. Cells were washed with PBS and rapidly fixed with cold 80% methanol. The cell suspension in methanol was collected and centrifuged at 21,000 rcf for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the cell pellet resuspended with 500 µl of methanol solution. It was centrifuged again at 21,000rcf for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatants collected were dissolved in 8 M urea (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0) at 60 °C. The samples were then dried using a nitrogen gas apparatus (N-EVAP) and subjected to LC-MS analysis.

Prostate tumor database analysis

We analyzed transcriptomic data from the GSE70770 databases of normal and tumor human prostate tissue, and used Phantasus software v1.11.0 (https://artyomovlab.wustl.edu/phantasus)59 to perform differential expression, analysis of polyamine pathway genes in different normal and tumor samples. We used the CANCERTOOL Website to analyze DHPS expression in different prostate cancer databases20.

Glycolysis and mitochondria respiration

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in real time using the Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent) following the instructions of the manufacturer. Prior to the assay, cells were starved of glucose for 1 h, then glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM), 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP, 10 µM) and a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (1 µM each) were added sequentially added at specific time points during the assay. After each experiment, the protein content was quantified for standardization. No significant variation was observed in protein levels.

To measure the OCR on patients derived from tumoroids, approximately 150 tumoroids were plated in 10 µl of Matrigel matrix (Corning) per well in a 96-well plate. After 48 h, the tumoroids medium is changed to a glucose-free medium for 1 h and then injected successively to obtain a final concentration of 20 mM glucose, 4 µM oligomycin, 40 µM DNP, 4 µM Antimycin and 4 µM Rotenone.

Single-cell data analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from the GSE193337 dataset16 were analyzed with RStudio (version 4.3.1) and Seurat (version 5.0). Cell-type annotation was performed using the scType Cell Marker Database. Four patient datasets were integrated for the analysis. Epithelial cells were identified as a result of merging subclusters corresponding to club cells, basal cells, and other epithelial subtypes. A threshold for dividing the epithelial cells into DHPS-high and DHPS-low subclusters was set based on DHPS gene expression, with a cutoff of < 1.2. We have performed pathway enrichment analysis using the fgsea package.
