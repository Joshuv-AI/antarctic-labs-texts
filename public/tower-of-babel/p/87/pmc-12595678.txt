A novel RTEL1 nonsense variant in a case of familial pulmonary fibrosis: clinical description and genetic implications
Authors: Giovanni Franco, Francesca Bertola, Filippo Aloisi, Samuele Promi, Umberto Zanini, Paola Faverio, Angela Bentivegna, Raphaël Borie, Bruno Crestani, Giovanni Cazzaniga, Fabrizio Luppi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive interstitial lung disease, often linked to telomere-related gene (TRG) mutations in familial forms. Telomere biology disorders can also manifest with systemic features such as bone marrow failure and liver disease. We report a 72-year-old woman with a family history of pulmonary fibrosis and premature hair greying. The patient had a history of bronchopneumonia at age 20 and pulmonary tuberculosis at age 23. Genetic testing identified a novel heterozygous nonsense variant in Regulator of Telomere Elongation Helicase 1 (RTEL1) (NM_001283009.2:c.2962 C > T; p.Gln988Ter). This variant introduces a premature stop codon, likely leading to loss of function. The variant, absent from major population databases, was classified as “likely pathogenic” according to ACMG criteria. RTEL1 encodes a DNA helicase essential for DNA replication and telomere maintenance, and its disruption contributes to epithelial cell dysfunction in pulmonary fibrosis. This case expands the mutational and clinical spectrum of RTEL1-associated interstitial lung diseases and underscores the importance of genetic testing in patients with familial pulmonary fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03976-4.Keywords: Familial Pulmonary Fibrosis, Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis, Interstitial Lung Diseases, Regulator of Telomere Elongation Helicase 1, Telomere-related genes

Background

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), is a chronic fibrosing interstitial pneumonia of unknown cause, typically associated with worsening dyspnoea, persistent cough, impaired quality of life, and poor prognosis [1, 2]. Nearly 10% of patients with IPF have a first-degree relative affected by interstitial lung disease (ILD), supporting the hypothesis of a shared genetic predisposition [3, 4]. Most of the rare genetic variants identified to date involve telomere-related genes (TRG) [5]. These genes play a crucial role in telomere maintenance, thereby preserving chromosomal integrity during cell division [6].

Rare variants in the Regulator of Telomere Elongation Helicase 1 (RTEL1) gene have been described in patients with dyskeratosis congenita, typically presenting with bone marrow failure, mucosal fragility, pulmonary or liver fibrosis, and a heterogeneous clinical phenotype often characterized by delayed onset of non-hematological symptoms such as enetropathy, cerebellar hypoplasia and neurodevelopmental defects [7]. The most severe presentation is Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome, a severe variant of short telomere syndrome, characterized by intra-uterine growth retardation, early onset bone marrow failure, immunodeficiency, microcephaly and cerebellar hypoplasia [8]. Heterozygous RTEL1 mutations have been identified in approximately 5–9% of patients with familial forms of fibrotic ILD [9, 10], and are associated with marked heterogeneity in pulmonary phenotypes and a low prevalence of extrapulmonary manifestations [11]. Recognition of a telomere biology disorder has important therapeutic implications. Patients with telomere-related gene mutations are at increased risk for adverse effects from immunosuppressive therapies, including bone marrow suppression [12]. Furthermore, patients with telomere biology disorders are at higher risk of increase hematological complications as bone marrow failure following lung transplantation [13].

We report the case of a patient with a family history of familial pulmonary fibrosis, in whom we identified a novel RTEL1 gene variant absent from major population databases.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old woman, former smoker (5 cigarettes/day for 30 years, quit at the age of 45), with no significant occupational or environmental exposures, was referred to our ILD Center at Fondazione IRCCS San Gerardo dei Tintori, Monza, Italy, in October 2019 following incidental detection of basal interstitial abnormalities on chest High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT). Her medical history was notable for bronchopneumonia at the age of 20 and pulmonary tuberculosis at age 23. She denied any history of chronic bronchitis, childhood respiratory diseases, or symptoms suggestive of connective tissue disease. Her family history was remarkable for pulmonary fibrosis affecting two brothers, one sister, and a nephew (Fig. 1). Additionally, premature hair greying was reported in several family members.

Family pedigree of the index patient (arrowhead) showing multiple cases of pulmonary fibrosis across two generations. Affected individuals are indicated in red. The proband (female) and four other family members (two brothers, one sister, and one nephew) were diagnosed with interstitial lung disease, consistent with a familial form of pulmonary fibrosis

At baseline, the patient denied dyspnea or cough. Physical examination detected bilateral basal velcro-like crackles. Pulmonary function testing showed a preserved total lung capacity (TLC) of 87% predicted, a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 96% predicted and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) of 94% predicted, with a mild reduction in carbon monoxide diffusing capacity (DLCO) of 71% predicted. Chest HRCT showed apical pleural thickening and mild honeycombing in the lower lobes, more pronounced on the left side (Fig. 2A). Serological tests including antinuclear antibodies (ANA), extractable nuclear antigens (ENA), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and IgG specific for hypersensitivity pneumonitis resulted all negative. Hematological and liver tests were normal.

Radiological and functional follow-up of the patient. A Baseline high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) images of the chest (September 2019) showing subpleural reticulations and early honeycombing in the lower lobes, predominantly on the left. B Follow-up HRCT after 12 months (October 2020) demonstrating slight progression of fibrotic changes, with increased honeycombing in the subpleural basal regions. C HRCT performed five years after diagnosis (May 2025) showing a radiological progression. These findings are consistent with a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP). D Longitudinal pulmonary function tests over a 5-year follow-up. FVC: Forced vital capacity; TLC: total lung capacity; DLCO: diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide

Considering the family history of pulmonary fibrosis and the presence of premature greying, a multidisciplinary discussion (MDD) concluded with a diagnosis of early-stage IPF, as part of familial pulmonary fibrosis (FPF). The patient refused antifibrotic therapy. One year later, HRCT of the chest showed a mild progression of honeycombing (Fig. 2B). As a result of this radiological progression, the patient agreed to initiate an antifibrotic therapy with pirfenidone, that was well tolerated. During a 5 years follow-up, serial lung function tests showed stable FVC and DLCO (Figure D), while HRCT evidenced a progression of honeycombing (Fig. 2 C).

Considering the patient’s positive family history of pulmonary fibrosis and the presence of premature hair greying, genetic testing was proposed to investigate the presence of pathogenic variants in genes associated with familial pulmonary fibrosis. The molecular analysis was performed by a whole-exon sequencing (WES) approach in a germline contest with selective enrichment of coding regions and splice site (+/- 20nt) (parameters quality: medium coverage min. 100X, coverage > 10X min. 98%; Reference genome Hg38). Variants were filtered for a virtual panel enabling comprehensive screening of known telomere-related genes and other genes implicated in fibrotic interstitial lung diseases (Supplementary table).

The analysis revealed a novel heterozygous nonsense variant in the RTEL1 gene: NM_001283009.2:c.2962 C > T; p.(Gln988Ter), which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. This variant introduces a premature stop codon, likely leading to loss of function of the encoded helicase, which plays a key role in telomere maintenance.

The variant was not reported in major population databases including gnomAD, ClinVar, or dbSNP. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, this variant was classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ (class 4)[14].

Although family segregation and functional (iE. Telomere length) analyses have not yet been performed, the clinical phenotype and family history support its potential pathogenic role.

Discussion

IPF is a rare, irreversible, and progressive interstitial lung disease typically associated with older age, cigarette smoking, and other environmental exposures. In addition to these risk factors, both rare pathogenic mutations and common genetic variants have been implicated in the pathogenesis of IPF [15]. Nearly 10% of patients with IPF report a first-degree relative affected by ILD, supporting a genetic predisposition [3, 4].

Telomere-related genes (TRGs) are the most frequently associated with familial pulmonary fibrosis (FPF), with TERT mutations identified in approximately 15–22% of cases, followed by TERC (2–5%), RTEL1 (5–10%), and PARN (2–5%). Other less common variants have been described in genes involved in telomere maintenance and ribonucleoprotein complexes, including DKC1, TINF2, NOP10, NHP2, ACD, NAF1, ZCCHC8, RPA, and POT1[4].

We report the case of a 72-year-old woman with a positive family history of pulmonary fibrosis, in whom a novel heterozygous nonsense variant in the RTEL1 gene was identified: NM_001283009.2:c.2962 C > T; p.(Gln988Ter). RTEL1encodes a DNA helicase involved in critical cellular processes, including DNA replication, genome stability, DNA repair, and telomere maintenance. The identified variant introduces a premature termination codon, which is predicted to lead to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay or the production of a truncated, non-functional protein.

This variant is absent from major population databases, including gnomAD, ClinVar, and dbSNP, and was classified as “likely pathogenic” according to the ACMG guidelines. Although segregation analysis within the family has not yet been performed, the occurrence of pulmonary fibrosis in multiple first- and second-degree relatives strongly supports a monogenic, inherited form of the disease. Nevertheless, the possibility of a somatic RTEL1 mutation, as well as the contribution of other genetic susceptibility factors or environmental exposures, cannot be completely ruled out.

In a cohort of 35 patients, Borie et al. reported that patients carrying RTEL1 variants may present with a range of ILD phenotypes, including not only idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis IPF, which remains the most common, but also unclassifiable ILD, chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, connective tissue disease-associated ILD, and even sarcoidosis and pneumoconiosis. Interestingly, usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) was the predominant radiological pattern (82.6%), and IPF was the final diagnosis in 74% of patients. Despite the central role of RTEL1 in telomere maintenance, hematologic and hepatic abnormalities, common in carriers of TERT or TERC mutations, were less frequently observed in RTEL1-mutated individuals. The study also confirmed that RTEL1 is expressed predominantly in alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages, but not in fibroblastic foci, reinforcing the hypothesis that alveolar epithelial dysfunction plays a pivotal role in disease pathogenesis [11].

The identification of a novel, likely pathogenic RTEL1 variant in our patient, combined with a strong familial aggregation of pulmonary fibrosis, further expands the phenotypic spectrum linked to RTEL1-associated disease.

Conclusion

This case highlights a novel likely pathogenic RTEL1 variant in a patient with familial IPF and premature hair greying. Genetic analysis provided crucial insights into disease etiology, reinforcing the importance of integrating genomic tools into the diagnostic workup of familial ILD. Further studies and family-based segregation analysis are warranted to confirm the pathogenicity of this variant and to better understand the phenotypic spectrum of RTEL1-associated pulmonary fibrosis.
