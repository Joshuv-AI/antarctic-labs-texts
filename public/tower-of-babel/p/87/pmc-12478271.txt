Non-targeted metabolomics reveals substrates and products of maillard reaction related to changes of color and aroma of dried sweet potato during storage
Authors: Sihang Wang, Qingmei Bu, Ying Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDried sweet potato (SP) produced using the inverse steaming process gradually turns deep brown and develops a rich aroma during storage. Investigating these changes and exploring the contributing reaction and compounds are important to clarifying the underlying mechanisms and utilizing the pigment and aroma substances. This study mainly investigated how the Maillard reaction (MR) influences the evolution of color and aroma of dried SP during storage through physicochemical determination and non-targeted metabolomic analysis. The results indicated that MR increased the levels of browning, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, and melanoidins after a 50-day storage period. Maltose, Glu, and His were the primary substrates of MR during storage. Ten significantly upregulated volatile compounds contributed to the development of the aroma of dried SP during storage. Among them, 2-methyl-butanal (ROAV 12.68) and maltol (ROAV 3.07) were determined to be the critical aroma compounds of dried SP stored for 50 days.Keywords: Dried sweet potatoes, Reverse steaming process, Maillard reaction, Metabolomics, Browning, Flavor

Introduction

Sweet potato (SP) (Ipomoea batatas(L.) Lam.) belongs to the Convolvulaceae family and has a high yield, strong adaptability, good taste, and high nutritional value (Yuan, Rao, et al., 2024). However, storing fresh SP is difficult. To extend the shelf life of SP and improve its added value, it is processed into other products, such as fried chips, yogurt, and cakes, among which dried SP is a common product (El Sheikha & Ray, 2017; Qiu et al., 2018). The inverse steaming process is commonly used to produce dried SP and is also called the “three times steamed and dried” process, as the process of steam-drying SP is repeated three times (Fan et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2023). Dried SP produced using the inverse steaming process is sweet and chewy. During storage, SP gradually turns deep brown and develops a salty, sweet, caramel, nutty, and soy sauce aroma. However, the mechanisms underlying these changes remain unclear.

Studies have shown that the Maillard reaction (MR) occurs during the processing and storage of dried products, resulting in changes in color and aroma (Hamzaoglu et al., 2018; Michalska et al., 2016). Melanoidins, which are late-stage MR products (MRPs), confer food products with a brown color (Wang et al., 2011), modulate the release of aroma compounds (Hofmann & Schieberle, 2002), and improve physiological functions, such as antioxidant (Sharma et al., 2021) and antimicrobial activities (Peña-Correa et al., 2023). Volatile MRPs can contribute distinct aroma to food products; for example, thiol sulfides and thiophenes generated from the cysteine-xylose-glutamate reaction can create roasted or broth-like aroma (Zheng et al., 2023). 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, tetramethyl pyrazine, 3.5-(or 2) diethyl-2(or 5)-methylpyrazine, and furaneol are the characteristic aromatic volatile MRPs of chocolate, while the volatile MR compounds of durum wheat pasta are maltol, 2-furanmethanol, furfural, and benzaldehyde (Starowicz & Zielinski, 2019). Habinshuti, Mu, and Zhang (2021) reported that SP MRPs had higher quality and content of aroma compounds than its protein hydrolysates. Moreover, the aroma of the MRPs was influenced by the type of monosaccharide that reacted with SP hydrolysates (Habinshuti, Zhang, et al., 2021).

Metabolomics is a comprehensive and systematic approach for conducting a thorough and in-depth analysis of differential metabolites to evaluate changes induced by food processing and storage (Utpott et al., 2022). For example, Zheng et al. (2023) used non-targeted metabolomics to analyze the critical flavor compounds generated from the cysteine-xylose-glutamate system under different conditions, revealing that 2-methyl-3-furanthiol, 2-furanthiol, and phenylacetaldehyde contribute to distinct flavors. Keawkim and Jom (2022) performed non-targeted metabolomic analyses of roasted germinated Sacha inchi seeds, which revealed that roasting significantly decreased reducing sugar content (fructose and glucose) but increased amino acid content, suggesting the occurrence of protein degradation and the MR. Tang et al. (2025) also used non-targeted metabolomics to study the effect of MR on flavor characteristics and biological activity of Boletus edulis hydrolysates. Thus, the application of this method to analyze different substances involved in the MR will be helpful for understanding the formation of color and flavor.

To evaluate the effects of MR on the color and aroma of stored dried SP produced using the inverse steaming process, the changes in color, melanoidins, 5-HMF, and aroma were determined. Non-targeted metabolomics was conducted to identify and analyze the differential non-volatile and volatile metabolites between fresh (stored for 0 d) and stored (50 d) dried SP. This study aimed to identify the critical reaction substrates (reducing sugars and amino acids) and products involved in the MR of stored dried SP that contribute to its color and aroma. These results will be helpful for further exploration of the MR mechanism and utilization of MRPs in stored dried SP.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

SP (Yanshu 25) was purchased from Lanling County, Linyi City, Shandong Province, China. Potassium ferrocyanide, zinc acetate, sodium bisulfite, ferrous sulfate, hydrogen peroxide, and salicylic acid were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shenyang, China). Methanol, acetonitrile, acetic acid, isopropyl alcohol, and 2-Octanol were purchased from CNW Technologies, GmbH. All chemical reagents used were of analytical grade and are commercially available.

Preparation of dried SP

Following the established methods of Fan et al. (2024) and Feng et al. (2023), dried SP was prepared using an inverse steaming process. After cleaning, peeling, and cutting into strips (1.5 cm thick × 6 cm long), SP was steamed (100 °C, 15 min) using a home steamer and then dried at 35 °C for 24 h using a drying oven (DHG—9104S, Shanghai Jinghong Laboratory Instrument Co., Ltd.). Steaming and drying were repeated thrice, and the third drying step lasted until the moisture content of the SP decreased to 18.0 ± 2.0 %. Then, the dried SP was vacuum-packed and incubated at 45 °C to accelerate storage. Samples of dried SP were removed every 10 days, vacuum freeze-dried, and milled. The obtained dried SP powder was used for subsequent analyses.

Measurement of 5-HMF

The 5-HMF levels were determined following the method described by Ma et al. (2016). Briefly, 5 g of dried SP lyophilized powders was dissolved in 25 mL of sterile water, followed by addition of 0.5 mL potassium ferrocyanide and zinc acetate. Thereafter, deionized water was added to obtain a total volume of 50 mL. After mixing, the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (9000 rpm, 10 min). Two portions of 5 mL supernatant were extracted and added to 0.20 g/100 mL NaHSO3 solution and sterile water, respectively. Sensory evaluation of aroma

The sensory evaluation of the aroma of the stored dried SP was performed as described by Deng et al. (2025). Eight trained evaluators (age 18–30 years) were selected from Shenyang Agricultural University (Shenyang, China) to obtain sensory scores of the dried SP of different storage times based on five aroma attributes (sweet, caramel, soy sauce, nutty, and salty aroma) and overall aroma acceptance. The sensory evaluation was conducted as follows: a total of 2 g dried lyophilized SP powder was placed in a sealed and odorless transparent bottle and then served to evaluators at room temperature (25 °C). Different samples were distinguished by random numbers on the bottle, which were presented to the evaluators simultaneously after being shuffled. The intensities of each characteristic aroma attribute were scored on a scale of 0–5: 0 indicates none or not perceptible intensity, 3 indicates moderate intensity, and 5 indicates extremely high intensity. The sensory evaluation was carried out in an evaluation room without interference from other odors. All sensory evaluators explicitly agreed to participate in the sensory research.

E-nose analysis

The aroma characteristics of dried SP were analyzed by E-nose (PEN 3 Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) consisting of 10 different sensors, which are sensitive to different compounds: W1C is sensitive to aromatic benzene compounds; W5S detects nitrogen oxides; W3C responds to aromatic ammonia; W6S primarily detects hydrides; W5C is responsive to alkane aromatic compounds; W1S detects short-chain alkanes; W1W is sensitive to sulfides and terpenes; W2S detects alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones; W2W responds to organic sulfides and aromatic compounds; and W3S detects long-chain alkanes.

Referring to the method of Hao et al. (2025), 1.0 g of the dried lyophilized SP powder was placed in a 50 mL headspace vial and allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for 2 h. The sampling time, sensor self-cleaning and zeroing time, and analysis sampling time were set as 1, 120, 5, and 60 s, respectively. The injection flow rate was 600 mL/min. Each sample was detected in parallel three times.

Non-targeted analysis of non-volatile metabolites

Referring to the previous method with some modifications (Zhang, He, et al., 2022), lyophilized SP powder (25 mg) was extracted with 1000 μL of a methanol: acetonitrile: water (2: 2: 1, v/v) solvent mixture containing isotopic internal standards. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, homogenized for 4 min, sonicated in an ice-water bath for 5 min, and then stored at −40 °C for 1 h. Following centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was collected for instrumental analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC system equipped with a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 2.6 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.01 % acetic acid in water (Phase A) and isopropyl alcohol: acetonitrile (1: 1, v/v, Phase B). The gradient program was as follows: 0–4 min: A: B (99 %: 1 %), 4–4.5 min: A: B (1 %: 99 %), 4.5–6 min (99 %–1 %). The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min, with a sample tray temperature of 4 °C and an injection volume of 2 μL.

Mass spectrometric analysis was conducted using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 instrument under the following conditions: sheath gas flow: 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow: 15 Arb; capillary temperature: 320 °C; full MS resolution: 60,000; MS/MS resolution: 15,000; impact energies, 20/30/40; and spray voltages of 3.8 kV (positive) and − 3.4 kV (negative).

The database used for compound identification was BiotreeDB (V3.0) with a cutoff value of 0.3. Relative quantification of the compounds was performed using internal standard normalization.

Non-targeted measurement of volatile metabolites

Referring to previous methods (Yuan, Zhang, et al., 2024), with few alterations, lyophilized SP powders (500 ± 5 mg) were transferred to 20 mL headspace vials, spiked with 10 μL of 2-octanol (10 mg/L in dH2O) as an internal standard, and analyzed using an Agilent 7890 GC system coupled with a 5977 B MS detector.

SPME conditions included a 60 °C incubation temperature, 15 min preheating, 30 min incubation, and 4 min desorption.

GC analysis utilized a DB-Wax column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm) with helium carrier gas at 3 mL/min purge flow and 1 mL/min column flow. The oven temperature was set to 40 °C (4 min), ramped at 5 °C/min to 245 °C, and held for 5 min.

MS operation employed electron ionization (EI) at −70 eV, with transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole temperatures maintained at 250 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. Data acquisition in scan mode covered an m/z range of 20–400 with a 2.37 min solvent delay.

Chroma TOF 4.3 × software (LECO Corp., St Joseph, MI, USA) and the Nist database (https://www.kinetics.nist.gov/pml/atomic-spectra-database) were used for compound identification, with a match percentage exceeding 80 %. The relative contents of each volatile component were obtained by comparing the peak areas with that of the internal standard, 2-octanol.

ROAV calculation was performed to obtain the contribution of each compound to the overall aroma. Statistical analysis

All the tests were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and SPSS 23 software. One-way ANOVA was used for intergroup comparisons, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Changes in color

Changes in the color of the dried SP at different storage times is shown in Fig. 1 A-E. As the storage duration increased, the color of the dried SP changed from yellow to brown, and the brown color gradually darkened. The a* value of the dried SP gradually increased, and the b* and L* values gradually decreased. The longer the storge time, the more significant the change in ΔE, which indicates that an extended storage period has a significant darkening effect on the color of dried SP. MR is one of the reactions that can cause browning, which has been reported as the main cause of discoloration in many food products during storage, such as orange juice, dried apricots, ready-to-eat shrimps (Chen et al., 2024; Hamzaoglu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024). SP contains abundant substrates (reducing sugars and amino acids) for MR (Adu-Kwarteng et al., 2014; Mu et al., 2009). Furthermore, the moisture content of dried SP (18.0 ± 2.0 %) fell within the range (10–20 %) that been found to significantly promote the formation of brown polymers (melanoidins) through MR (Yahya et al., 2014). Thus, it is speculated that MR maybe one of the main reactions that resulted in the color change of dried SP during storage.

Changes in BD, melanoidins, and 5-HMF

BD is used to indicate the degree of brown pigment formation in the samples during storage (Yang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 2 A, BD gradually increased, indicating an increase in pigment accumulation. The 5-HMF content of dried SP increased with prolonged storage (Fig. 2 B). 5-HMF is an indicator of MR, which is mainly formed by the cracking of Amadori or Heyns compounds or degradation of 3-deoxyglucosone (Mohsin et al., 2019). Melanoidins are brown nitrogen- or oxygen-containing polymers and copolymers, and are the final products of MR (Yang et al., 2024). 5-HMF accumulates as a precursor of melanoidins, which induces browning (Paravisini & Peterson, 2018). As shown in Fig. 2 C, melanoidins content gradually increased with increasing storage time. These results are consistent with the changes in color, indicating that the occurrence of MR leads to the accumulation of melanoidins. During the storage process, BD and the contents of 5-HMF and melanoidins in dried SP exhibited an upward trend, suggesting that MR occurred during storage and contributed to the development of the brown color.

Sensory evaluation of aroma

Sensory evaluation of the aroma of dried SP stored for 0, 30, and 50 days (D0, D30, and D50) was performed to study the changes in characteristic aroma. As shown in Fig. 3, with increased storage time, the score of the overall acceptance increased, indicating that a more favorable aroma can be obtained from dried SP stored for 50 days. The score of each characteristic aroma on D0 (Fig. 3) showed that the main aroma is sweet, followed by salty and soy sauce, nutty, and caramel aroma. With increased storage period (Fig. 3), the score of soy sauce aroma increased the most, followed by those of caramel, sweet and salty, and nutty. The score of each aroma on D50 followed the same trend, indicating the generation of more volatile compounds that contribute to the soy sauce, caramel, and sweet aromas. It was reported that MRPs from sparkling wines (Charnock et al., 2022) under low temperature had caramel aroma, and the MRPs from soy protein hydrolysates (Liu et al., 2012) exhibited caramel and soy sauce aromas, indicating that MR is one of the main reactions that contribute to the aroma development of dried SP during storage.

Sensory evaluation of aroma. (A): Sensory score radar chart of dried SP during storage (0 d, 30 d, 50 d). (B): Sensory score of overall acceptance aroma.

E-nose analyses of aroma

The PCA score plot of E-nose (Fig. 4 A) was used to analyze differences in volatile compound profiles among the dried SP stored for 0, 30, and 50 days (D0, D30, and D50). As shown in Fig. 4 A. the total cumulative variance contribution rate was 99.13 % (PC1 = 83.37 %, PC2 = 15.76 %). PC1 explained 83.37 % of the variance, indicating that PC1 explains most of the variation caused by storage. Fig. 4 A indicates a large separation between the D0 and D50 groups along PC1, but an extremely small separation between D0 and D30, indicating that the aroma profile between D0 and D50 is significantly distinct, while that between D0 and D30 is similar. These results are consistent with those of the overall acceptance in sensory evaluation. Therefore, the long storage time may have contributed to the development of the aroma of dried SP.

E-nose analysis. (A): PCA score plot. (B): E-nose radar chart of dried SP during storage (0 d, 30 d, 50 d).

As shown in Fig. 4 B, the differences between the D50 group and other groups (D0 and D30) mainly resulted from the higher response values of the W1C, W5S, W6S, W5C, W1S, W1W, W2S, and W2W sensors, suggesting that, during the 50-day storage, dried SP generated more contents of aromatic benzene compounds, nitrogen oxides, hydrides, aromatic ammonia, short-chain alkanes, sulfides, terpenes, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, organic sulfides, and aromatic compounds. Asikin et al. (2014) found that changes in the concentrations of aromatic MRPs (furanone/furan) induced distinct nutty and caramel aromas in non-centrifugal cane brown sugar, suggesting that changes in furanone/furan induced by MR may have contributed to the nutty and caramel aromas D50 SP. Regarding D0 and D30 SP, there was a large overlap between them, indicating a similar profile of volatile compounds between the two, which is consistent with the results of the PCA score plot. Therefore, the development of the aroma of dried SP during storage depends on the period of storage. Herein, the longer the storage duration, the higher the quantity of aroma compounds and the richer the generated aroma, making it necessary to analyze the differential volatile compounds between D50 and D0 to further identify the compounds contributing to the aroma.

Multivariate statistical analysis

The non-targeted metabolomics approach was employed to analyze the differential non-volatile and volatile metabolites between un-stored dried SP (D0) and dried SP stored for 50 days (D50). The results of PCA of non-volatile and volatile metabolites are shown in Fig. 5 A(a) and Fig. 5 B(a), respectively. The total cumulative variance contribution rates were 71.9 % (PC1 = 54.1 %, PC2 = 17.8 %) and 79.1 % (PC1 = 67 %, PC2 = 12.1 %), respectively. Notably, the samples were clearly distinguished based on the storage time, indicating that D0 and D50 were significantly different in terms of metabolic composition.

Multivariate statistical analysis. A: Non-volatile metabolites of D0 and D50 groups. (a): PCA score plot. (b) OPLS-DA permutation Plot. B: Volatile metabolites of D0 and D50 groups. (a): PCA score plot. (b) OPLS-DA permutation Plot.

As a supervised multivariate modeling approach, OPLS-DA utilizes R2X and R2Y metrics to quantify model fit, while Q2 serves as an indicator of predictive performance. Generally, Q2 values >0.5 are typically regarded as indicative of satisfactory model performance (Peng et al., 2022). Non-volatile metabolite profiling via OPLS-DA yielded cumulative R2X, R2Y, and Q2 values of 0.557, 1.000, and 0.992, respectively, reflecting high model reliability for the D0 and D50 groups. In the volatile metabolite model, the cumulative R2X, R2Y, and Q2 values of OPLS-DA between the D0 and D50 groups were 0.816, 1, and 0.918, respectively. Both OPLS-DA models showed goodness-of-fit and high predictive ability. The OPLS-DA model's robustness was evaluated using 200 permutation tests, with validation results illustrated in Fig. 5 A(b) and B(b). The x-axis denotes sample permutation, where a value of 1.0 corresponds to the initial model's R2 and Q2 metrics. Validation results revealed that both OPLS-DA models exhibited R2 values below the 1.0 threshold, accompanied by negative Q2 values, confirming model robustness and effective overfitting prevention (Meng et al., 2025). To clearly identify the differential metabolites, the values of VIP, p, and FC were calculated to screen the metabolites, with VIP > 1, p < 0.05, and FC ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5 (Log2FC ≥ 1 or Log2FC ≤ − 1) considered significant. The non-volatile and volatile differential metabolites related to MR were analyzed, and the results are discussed in 3.6 and 3.7, respectively.

Changes in amino acid and peptide contents were observed during storage, possibly owing to protein hydrolysis and MR at the appropriate temperature (Zhao et al., 2023). The degrees of protein degradation and MR consumption may be responsible for the different trends in amino acid and peptide content. In this study, the levels of Glu and His were significantly downregulated, which may be due to their involvement in MR during storage. Zheng et al. (2021) indicated that Glu, Ser, and Asp underwent MR during SP roasting, which is similar to the results of the present study. Zhou et al. (2016) studied MR involving Glu as the substrate, and indicated that the MRPs exhibited brown color with baking and nutty aromas. Li et al. (2023) indicated that MRPs from His exhibited brown color, with burnt-sweet, toasted, nutty, and sweet aromas, indicating that MR using Glu/His as a substrate generates favorable characteristic color and aroma for food products. After storage, the content of Gln-Arg was also found significantly downregulated, indicating it probably participated MR during the storage of dried SP. Previous studies have shown that the peptides with smaller molecular weights tend to generate more volatile compounds through MR (Su et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2013). Moreover, the composition of volatile MRPs varies depending on the sequence of peptides (Nishimura, 2025).

Effects of storage on soluble sugar changes in dried SP

The D50 group exhibited downregulation and upregulation of three and eight soluble sugars, respectively, compared with those in the D0 group (Table 1 and Fig. S1). The main soluble sugars in SP were maltose, fructose, glucose, and sucrose (Zhang et al., 2023). The upregulation of arabinose, galactose, xylose, mannose, and raffinose after storage may be due to the decomposition of cell tissues caused by the thermal environment and the degradation of macromolecular sugars into small soluble sugars (Hamzaoglu et al., 2018). During storage, reducing sugars undergo MR to form MRPs, such as 5-HMF and furfural, which provide a richer aroma and color to dried SP (Hou et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. S1, after storage, the content of reducing sugars (maltose) in the dried SP showed a downward trend, possibly because of their participation in the MR with amino acids. Zheng et al. (2021) found that maltose content first increased and then decreased with an increase in roasting time, indicating that maltose was involved in the MR, which is consistent with the results of this study. A decreased content of sucrose was also observed after storage, possibly due to its hydrolysis into glucose and fructose (McDaniel et al., 2012), increasing the levels of the two monosaccharides.

Effects of storage on volatile metabolite levels in dried SP

A total of 96 volatile metabolites were detected in the D50 and D0 groups, including 12 alkenes, 20 aldehydes, 25 alcohols, 18 ketones, 4 esters, 8 furan compounds, and 9 others. As shown in Fig. 6 A-B, 48 volatile metabolites were identified as significant differential substances in dried SP, including 8 alkenes, 7 aldehydes, 8 alcohols, 11 ketones, 3 esters, 8 furan compounds, and 3 others. Among them, the D50 group exhibited significant downregulation of four metabolites and significant upregulation of 44 metabolites. Flavor is a predominant attribute of MRPs. Flavor compounds, mainly including aldehydes, ketones, and furans (Cui et al., 2021) emerge primarily during the intermediate stages of the MR process (Liu et al., 2022).

Significantly upregulated and downregulated differential volatile metabolites of stored dried SP compared with un-stored dried SP. A: The heatmap of significantly changed volatile compounds. B: The Log2FC value of significantly changed volatile compounds.

Aldehydes are usually generated from the Strecker degradation of amino acids during the MR, which contribute to the characteristic flavor (Nobis et al., 2021). Most aldehydes exhibited low odor threshold values and possessed pleasant, fruity, and nutty aromas (Chen et al., 2019). After storage, the contents of seven aldehydes were significantly enhanced. Among them, 3-methyl-butanal and 2-methyl-butanal are products of the Strecker degradation of valine, isoleucine, and leucine, which impart chocolate, malt, and bitter almond-like flavors. (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal, a fragrant compound, exhibits nutty, grassy, rose, and honey aromas and can be generated through the MR (Huang et al., 2024).

Ketones are typically formed through the Strecker breakdown of amino acids and the thermal oxidative degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (Meng et al., 2025). They have a sweet, fruity, and caramel aroma owing to the presence of short-chain carbonyls in their structures (Huang et al., 2024). In this study, a total of 9 polyunsaturated fatty acids were identified in dried SP, including linoleic acid, 9-oxo-10(E),12(E)-octadecadienoic acid, 13(S)-Hode, 13(S)-Hpotre, pinolenic acid, (10E,15Z)-9,12,13-trihydroxyoctadeca-10,15-dienoic acid, dodeca-2(E),4(E)-dienoic acid, 9Z,11E,13E-octadecatrienoic acid, and stearidonic acid. No significant downregulation of these fatty acids was observed during the storage of dried SP, which may be due to the use of vacuum packaging, light avoidance, and relatively low storage temperature (Domínguez et al., 2019; Rubio et al., 2008). Therefore, the influences of the thermal oxidation degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids could be excluded. After storage, the D50 group exhibited a significant upregulation of 10 ketones and downregulation of one ketone compared with those in the D0 group. Among the differentially upregulated ketones, 1-hydroxy-2-butanone was the most upregulated, with a Log2FC value of 13.81. 1-hydroxy-2-butanone, which provides a malty flavor, is also found in soy sauce (Wang et al., 2020). Additionally, 2-heptanone has a pear-like odor. 6-methyl-2-heptanone exhibits a lemongrass aroma. 3-penten-2-one is the primary flavor contributor in fried pomegranate seeds (Gao et al., 2023), whilst 1-hydroxy-2-propanone is the main predictor of a roasted odor (Cardinal et al., 2020).

Furans, a significant class of volatile oxygen-containing heterocyclic aromatic compounds and predominant constituents resulting from MR, are important flavor compounds formed in food products under conditions, such as roasting and pressure cooking (Van Lancker et al., 2011; Zhang, Li, et al., 2022). Furan compounds and their derivatives have a unique sweet, nutty odor and exert a profound enhancement effect on the sensory properties of food products (Dragone et al., 2009).

Thirty types of furans and their derivatives were detected before and after storage of dried SP. The generation of furans can be attributed to the low storage temperatures of the dried SP. When the MR temperature was low, furans were rapidly produced (Mottram et al., 2002). After storage, the levels of eight furans were significantly upregulated: dihydro-4-hydroxy-2(3H)-furanone, dihydro-2-methyl-3(2H)-furanone, 5-methyl-2(5H)-furanone, 2-furanmethanol, 3-methyl-2(5H)-furanone, furfural, 5,6,7,7a-tetrahydro-4,4,7a-trimethyl-(R)-2(4H)-benzofuranone, and 2(5H)-furanone. The Log2FC value of 5-methyl-2(5H)-furanone, the most upregulated furan compound, was 11.47. 2-furanmethanol, dihydro-4-hydroxy-2(5H)-furanone, and dihydro-2-methyl-3(2H)-furanone have sweet and caramel odors (Asikin et al., 2017). As a key MR intermediate, furfural derives its five‑carbon skeleton from pentose precursors through C1-C2 enolization of Amadori rearrangement products, exhibiting characteristic sweet and toasted aromatic notes (Ni et al., 2021). According to Li et al. (2020), mass spectrometry and isotope labeling results revealed that furfural is derived from xylose; therefore, it can be speculated that furfural in dried SP may come from xylose.

In addition, after storage, the maltol content of dried SP, which has a caramel flavor and is a key volatile component of soy sauce, was significantly upregulated (Gao et al., 2024). Among all the significantly upregulated volatile compounds (Fig. 6 B), maltol had the highest Log2FC value (20.94), indicating that it was the most upregulated compound and had the greatest influence on the change of the aroma of dried SP during storage. The volatile compounds in order of descending Log2FC values were: maltol, 1-hydroxy-2-butanone, dihydro-4-hydroxy-2(3H)-furanone, 5-methyl-2(5H)-furanone, (E, Z)-2,6-nonadienal, dihydro-2-methyl-3(2H)-furanone, 2-furanmethanol, 2-methyl-butanal, 2(5H)-furanone, and furfural. Combined with the description of their flavors, it is speculated that these 10 compounds were important contributors to the development of sweet, caramel, and soy sauce aromas of dried SP during 50 days of storage. Tsai et al. (2021) reported that furfural, maltol, and dihydro-2-methyl-3(2H)-furanone were the main sources of aroma in roasted SP, which is similar to the results of this study.

To further investigate the contribution of these 10 volatile compounds to the aroma characteristics of the dried SP stored for 50 days, ROAVs were calculated. Based on the reported odor thresholds of maltol (5000 μg/L), 2-furanmethanol (15,000 μg/L), 2-methyl-butanal (320 μg/L), and furfural (14,100 μg/L), respectively (Khairallah et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2020; Leffingwell Odor Threshold Database*: http://www.leffingwell.com/odorthre.htm), the ROAV was 3.07 for maltol, 0.09 for 2-furanmethanol, 12.68 for 2-methyl-butanal, and 0.26 for furfural. Generally, ROAV >1 indicates that the volatile compound has a significant contribution to the aroma characteristics of the product, while 0.1 < ROAV <1 indicates that a compound plays a coordinating role in the overall aroma (Meng et al., 2025). According to the obtained ROAV, 2-methyl-butanal, and maltol were determined to be key aroma components of the dried SP stored for 50 days, and furfural were determined to be the modified aroma components.

Conclusion

This study focused on the influence of MR on the changes in the color, aroma, and volatile and non-volatile metabolite contents of dried SP processed using the traditional reverse steaming process after accelerated storage at 45 °C for 50 days. With an increase in storage time, the values of L* and b* decreased, while those of a* and ΔE increased. Additionally, with increased storage time, BD, 5-HMF, melanoidin, and hydroxyl radical scavenging rate also increased. Moreover, during the 50-day storage of dried SP, the soy sauce, caramel, and sweet aromas developed faster than the other aromas and became the main aroma of dried SP. Non-targeted metabolomics revealed that maltose, Glu, and His may be the main reaction substrates involved in MR. The significantly upregulated aldehydes (2-methyl-butanal, (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal), ketones (1-hydroxy-2-butanone), furans (dihydro-4-hydroxy-2(3H)-furanone, dihydro-2-methyl-3(2H)-furanone, 2-furanmethanol, furfural, 2(5H)-furanone, 5-methyl-2(5H)-furanone), and maltol were speculated to be the MR volatile compounds that contribute to the caramel, sweet, and soy sauce aromas of the dried SP after storage. Among them, 2-methyl-butanal and maltol were particularly significant as major contributors to the overall aroma of the dried SP stored for 50 days. The results of this study provide a theoretical foundation for further research on the reaction pathways of MR in dried SP. In addition, future studies should focus on validating the sensory effects of the MRPs and exploring their industrial applications, such as use as natural flavor enhancers, flavor modulators, and antioxidant-rich ingredients, to enhance the functional and sensory qualities of SP-based food products.
