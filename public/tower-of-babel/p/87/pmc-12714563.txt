Nucleolar MED19 regulates 2′-O-methylation of rRNA in supporting cancer cell growth
Authors: Yang Ming, Zhao Jiamin, Peng Yu, Lu Chengjiang, Zhang Yiyang, Zhang Hanqing, Qiang Zhou, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Mediator complex is a transcriptional co-factor for RNA polymerase II (Pol II)-dependent gene expression, with MED19 serving as an integral subunit. While MED19 overexpression has been documented in diverse cancer types to promote tumor progression, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we uncover a previously unrecognized function whereby MED19 localizes to the nucleolus independently of the Mediator complex. This nucleolar targeting is mediated by a conserved poly-lysine motif at the MED19 C-terminus, which enables binding to ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and fibrillarin (FBL), a catalytic component of the 2′-O-methyltransferase complex and pre-rRNA processing factor. Mechanistically, MED19 facilitates rRNA processing and 2′-O-methylation that promotes the efficiency of internal ribosome entry site-dependent translation for a number of onco-promoting genes including c-Myc. Collectively, these findings reveal a novel Mediator-independent function of MED19 in regulating ribosome-mediated translational control, thus providing mechanistic insights into its onco-promoting role.

Introduction

The Mediator complex, a large protein complex consisting of 30 subunits, connects the enhancer and promoter and relays various transcription factors or co-factors to the basal transcription machinery for precise transcription control [1, 2]. It remains unclear whether all the 30 subunits constitute the Mediator complex with equal stoichiometric amounts, or certain subunits may possess non-canonical functions independent of the complex.

MED19 is a subunit located in the hook region of the middle module of the Mediator complex [3]. It was initially discovered in yeast where it was named ROX3 and was found to be involved in transcriptional regulation of the CYC7 gene and the yeast cell’s response to stress [4, 5]. Subsequently, ROX3 was also found during the purification of the yeast Mediator complex [6], but deletion of ROX3 in yeast did not affect cell viability [7]. In human cells, the MED19 gene was initially cloned from lung cancer cell lines and was found to be significantly associated with the proliferation and migration of lung cancer cells, leading to its naming as lung cancer metastasis-related protein 1 (LCMR1) [8, 9]. Subsequent purification studies of the Mediator complex in human cells also identified the LCMR1 protein, and through comparison with ROX3 protein of yeast, it was found that LCMR1 and ROX3 are orthologous proteins [10]. As a result, both ROX3 and LCMR1 were later renamed as MED19 [11].

Consistent with its initial discovery as a lung cancer-related protein, MED19 has been found to be widely correlated with the occurrence and development of various cancers, including liver cancer, lung cancer, gastric cancer, breast cancer, prostate cancer, osteosarcoma, melanoma, bladder cancer, colorectal cancer, laryngeal cancer, cervical cancer, and ovarian cancer [12]. However, despite extensive reports on its association with cancer phenotypes, the underlying molecular mechanism of MED19 in cancers remains elusive.

The nucleolus is the largest membraneless subcellular structure in the nucleus and responsible for the production of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and the subsequent assembly of ribosomes with 80 ribosomal proteins, which are transported from the cytoplasm to the nucleolus [13]. Within the nucleolus, clustered arrays of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) are transcribed by RNA polymerase I into 47S pre-rRNA. Three of the four rRNAs are transcribed together within the 47S transcript, which is then processed to release the mature rRNAs, including the 18S, 5.8S, and 28S rRNAs [14]. The 5S rRNA is independently transcribed by RNA polymerase III outside the nucleolus and transported into the nucleolus for participation in ribosome assembly [15]. During the processing of pre-rRNA, over 100 sites on rRNA are subjected to post-transcriptional modifications, such as 2′-O-methylation, which plays important roles in rRNA function [16]. Mature ribosomes exhibit heterogeneity rather than uniform translational activity [17]. Modifications on rRNAs modulate ribosomal activity and influence the translational preferences of specific RNAs [18]. The dynamic regulation of nucleolar activity is closely related to cell proliferation, differentiation, and stress response [19]. In rapidly proliferating cells, such as cancer cells, the number and activity of nucleoli are significantly increased to meet the demand for ribosomes during rapid cell proliferation [20].

Because the nucleolus is a membraneless dynamic structure, the recruitment of part of nucleolar proteins to the nucleolus relies on nucleolar targeting sequences, which are typically rich in positively charged amino acids such as lysine and arginine [21]. K/R-rich sequences are recruited to the nucleolus through charge interactions with negatively charged rRNAs or other nucleolar RNAs [22]. During the analysis of protein sequences of the Mediator complex subunits, we found that MED19 contains multiple poly-lysine (poly-K) patches that may represent a typical nucleolar targeting feature. Among the 30 subunits of the Mediator complex, only MED19 possesses this characteristic sequence. This suggests that MED19 may have the potential to localize to the nucleolus.

In this study, we show that MED19 can localize to the nucleolus, independent of the Mediator complex. The multiple poly-K patches in the C-terminus of MED19 mediate its nucleolar localization by binding to rRNA and BoxC/D-snoRNP (small nucleolar RNA/ribonucleoprotein complex), which is responsible for 2′-O-methylation of rRNA. Within the nucleolus, MED19 facilitates the 2′-O-methylation and processing of rRNA through its interaction with the catalytic enzyme and processing factor FBL by phase separation. Ribosomes with higher rRNA 2′-O-methylation level promote the translation of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) with internal ribosome entry site (IRES) element, such as the oncogene c-Myc. Moreover, knockdown of MED19 disrupts the maturation of 18S rRNA—a process that is also regulated by FBL. Our study provides the first evidence that MED19 is a novel nucleolar targeting and RNA-binding protein, beyond its canonical role as a subunit of the Mediator co-factor complex. The association of MED19 with the nucleolus expands our understanding of regulation of the nucleolus activity and provides a novel mechanistic explanation for the onco-promoting role of MED19 in various cancers.

Methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The HEK293T cell line, HuH7 cell line, HeLa cell line, and their derived variants were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Hyclone, #SH30243.01) with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell, #FSP500), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco, #15140122) and in high-humidity incubators at 5% (v/v) CO2 and 37°C.

Plasmids, oligonucleotides, and antibodies

All transient expression plasmids used in this paper were constructed by seamless cloning method and T4 DNA ligase–ligation-based approaches based on the backbone of the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid (Invitrogen, V790-20) using the seamless cloning kit (Beyotime, #D7010M) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The pcDNA3.1-HA-MED19 was constructed by inserting the HA tag and MED19 CDS sequence into the pcDNA3.1 plasmid. The MED19 truncation vectors were constructed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based seamless cloning method to remove the specific fragment based on the constructed pcDNA3.1-HA-MED19 plasmid. The MED19 mutation vectors were constructed by replacing the original sequence with a synthetic mutation sequence in the constructed pcDNA3.1-HA-MED19 plasmid. The MED19 stable overexpression lentivirus plasmid pLVX-Puro-MED19 was constructed by inserting the MED19 CDS sequence into the pLVX-Puro plasmid (Clontech, 632164). The MED19 stable knockdown retrovirus plasmid pSiren-RetroQ-Puro-shMED19 was constructed by inserting a specific shRNA (short hairpin RNA) sequence targeting the UTR region of MED19 mRNA into the pSIREN-RetroQ plasmid (Clontech, #631526). The plasmid pRL-TK-IRES-Firefly for IRES element translational efficiency was constructed by inserting an IRES sequence and the Firefly luciferase sequence into the 5′ end of the Rennila luciferase sequence in the original pRL-TK plasmid (Promega, #E2241). All the constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. All the oligonucleotides were synthesized by the Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Information of oligonucleotides and antibodies used in this research is listed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescence

Cells grown on the coverslips were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Then they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. Cells were then incubated with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min for permeabilization. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the cells with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibody was diluted in blocking solution and incubated with the cells overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with PBS, the secondary antibody conjugated with a fluorophore was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Coverslips were mounted on slides using a fluorescent mounting medium and sealed with nail polish. Samples were imaged using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal fluorescence microscope.

Western blot

Cell lysates were prepared using 2× SDS sample loading buffer and heated at 98°C for 10–15 min. The samples were then separated by electrophoresis on 12% or 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using wet transfer methods. The transferred membranes were blocked with TBST (Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20) buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 8) containing 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature. Antibodies were diluted in TBST buffer containing 5% BSA and incubated with the membranes overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking. The membranes were washed with TBST after incubation with the primary antibody, then incubated with the secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. The secondary antibody was diluted in TBST buffer containing 5% non-fat dry milk for 2 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent, and the signals were visualized using the Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescence imaging system.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells from a 10 cm culture dish were scraped using a cell scraper, washed once with PBS, and lysed with a mild cell lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5% Triton X-100, 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail]. The cell lysate was then sonicated using a sonicator set at 30% power for 2 min (5 s on, 5 s off). The lysate was centrifuged at 4°C and 10 000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and antibody was added, followed by incubation at 4°C on a rotating platform for 12 h. Protein G magnetic beads were then added to the mixture and incubated at 4°C with rotation. After incubation, the magnetic beads were washed with cell lysis buffer, rotating at 4°C for 5 min per wash, and this process was repeated five times. The washed beads were then resuspended in 50 µl of 4% SDS and incubated at room temperature for 10 min with gentle shaking. The supernatant, containing the eluted proteins, was collected using a magnetic stand and used for subsequent western blotting or mass spectrometry experiments.

Silica enrichment

293T cells were cultured in a 10 cm culture dish to 90% confluence. The medium was aspirated, and the culture was crosslinked by ultraviolet light (UV) at 254 nm with an energy setting of 300 mJ/cm². The cells were scraped off the dish using a cell scraper and lysed with a lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5% Triton X-100, 10 mM EDTA, 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail). The lysate was then sonicated at 40% power for 1 min (5 s on, 5 s off). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was mixed with four volumes of 6 M guanidine hydrochloride solution, followed by the addition of an equal volume of absolute ethanol, and inverted to mix thoroughly. The resulting mixture was loaded onto a silica column from an RNA extraction kit and was centrifuged at 1000 g at room temperature for 1 min. The column was then washed twice with Wash Buffer 1 (4 M guanidine hydrochloride in 80% ethanol) and twice with Wash Buffer 2 (80% ethanol in 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8) by centrifuging at 1000 × g at room temperature for 1 min. Finally, the column was centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 1 min at room temperature, and the collection tube was changed. Seventy-five microliters of ddH₂O were added to the center of the silica column, allowed to stand for 2 min, and then centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 2 min. The eluted liquid contained the total RNA-bound proteins.

CLIP-seq

The cell crosslinking and lysis steps were performed as described in the silica enrichment protocol. The supernatant of the cell lysate was incubated in the presence of HA antibody (Ab9110, Abcam) at 4°C for 12 h, followed by the addition of Protein G magnetic beads (88847, Thermo Scientific) and incubation at 4°C for 4 h. The beads were washed three times with Wash Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA) and then once with High Salt Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) and Low Salt Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA), respectively. After washing, RNase T1 (EN0541, Thermo Scientific) was added to a final concentration of 5 U/µl, and the reaction mixture was incubated at 22°C for 15 min with intermittent shaking to partially digest the RNA. Subsequently, 1 μl (5 U) alkaline phosphatase (M0525V, NEB) was added to dephosphorylate the 3′ ends of the RNA bound to MED19, followed by phosphorylation of the 5′ ends with 1 μl (10 U) PNK enzyme (M0201Vm, NEB). Then, proteinase K (EO0491, Thermo Scientific) was used to digest MED19 to release the RNA. The released RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (R4130, MagenBio), and library preparation was performed using a small RNA library preparation kit (RK20312, Abclonal). Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. For the bioinformatics analysis, first, raw sequencing reads were quality-controlled and filtered using Trimmomatic to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences [23]. The high-quality reads were then aligned to the reference genome containing the rDNA sequence using Bowtie2 [24]. Next, the binding peaks were called and identified by MACS2 [25]. The read numbers of RNA species with peaks called were calculated by the FeatureCount [26].

Mass spectrometry assay of modified nucleosides

Total cellular RNA was extracted using the Trizol method. One microgram of RNA was mixed with 1 µl of Nuclease P1 (M0660S, NEB), 1 µl of RNase A (EN0531, Thermo Scientific), and 4 µl of RNase P1 buffer in a final volume of 40 µl. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 µl of CIP (calf intestinal phosphatase) (M0525V, NEB) and 4.5 µl of CIP buffer were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for another hour. The reaction mixture was diluted to 200 µl with ddH₂O. Relative quantification of the modified nucleosides was performed using mass spectrometry.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 500 cells per well in 100 µl of DMEM culture medium. At 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h after cell seeding, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

UV cross-linking mass spectrometry

The cell crosslinking, lysis, immunoprecipitation, and wash steps were performed as described in the CLIP-seq protocol. After washing, the immunoprecipitated magnetic beads were resuspended in 50 µl of PBS buffer, followed by the addition of 1 µl RNase A (EN0531, Thermo Scientific) and 1 µl RNase T1 (EN0541, Thermo Scientific). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h with intermittent shaking to digest the RNA. The magnetic beads with digested RNA were washed once with PBS and then incubated in 50 µl of 2% SDS at room temperature for 5 min. The supernatant was collected using a magnetic stand, subjected to SDS–PAGE and run for 1 cm, followed by Coomassie staining and destaining. The stained protein band from the 1 cm gel strip was excised for mass spectrometry analysis. The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using the RNPxl module in the OpenMS software [27].

HaloTag based denaturing UV-RIP-qPCR

HaloTag-MED19 and HaloTag-GFP plasmids were transiently transfected into 293T cells. After 48 h, the cells were harvested and lysed using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5% Triton X-100, 5 mM EDTA, 1× Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, 1× RNase Inhibitor). The supernatant of the lysate was collected after centrifugation and incubated with Magne™ HaloTag® Beads (G7281, Promega) for affinity purification. During the wash steps, the beads were washed with buffers containing either 6 M guanidine hydrochloride, 10% SDS, or 8 M urea. This was followed by sequential washes with high-salt buffer (1 M NaCl) and low-salt buffer (10 mM NaCl). Subsequently, the beads were resuspended in 100 μl of 0.5% SDS, and 5 μl of proteinase K (20 mg/ml) (EO0491, Thermo Scientific) was added. The mixture was incubated at 50°C for 1 h to digest the proteins. After discarding the beads, the supernatant was purified and recovered using an RNA extraction kit (R4130, MagenBio). Finally, RT-qPCR was performed using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Q311-02, Vazyme).

Luciferase assay

The luciferase assay was performed using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System kit (E1960, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s manual. Cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and cultured until they reached ~70% confluence, when they were transfected with a luciferase reporter gene plasmid. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed by Passive Lysis Buffer. After centrifugation, the supernatants were then transferred to a white, opaque 96-well plate. The Firefly luciferase substrate was added to each well and measured. Subsequently, the Renilla luciferase substrate Stop&Glo was added to the same well and measured. The plate was immediately placed in a microplate reader (FlexStation 3, Molecular Devices) to measure the luminescence intensity.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

Recombinant His-GFP-tagged wild-type MED19 (His-GFP-MED19-WT) and a lysine-to-alanine mutant version (His-GFP-MED19-K-A), in which all lysine residues within the NoLS domain were substituted with alanine, were expressed in Escherichia coli and purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography based on the 6×His tag. Protein purity was assessed by Coomassie Blue staining. For electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) analysis, a biotin-labeled rRNA probe was chemically synthesized and incubated with the purified proteins in binding buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT), 5% glycerol) at room temperature for 30 min. An unlabeled rRNA probe was added as a competitive probe at a molar ratio of 10:1 relative to the labeled probe. The protein–RNA complexes were separated on a 7.5% native polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto a nylon membrane. Biotin-labeled RNA was detected using streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (SA-HRP) (A0305, Beyotime) and a Chemiluminescent SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS (34579, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Stable knockdown of MED19 by retrovirus

The shRNA sequence targeting the UTR region of MED19 mRNA was inserted into the pSiren plasmid using restriction enzyme digestion and ligation. The pSiren plasmid and pHelper plasmid were co-transfected into 293T cells cultured in a 10-cm dish. After 48 and 72 h, the culture medium was collected once and mixed to obtain the virus suspension. The virus was selected by centrifugation at 15 000 × g at 4°C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The 293T cells and HuH7 cells to be infected with the virus were seeded into a six-well plate. When the cells reached 80% confluence, the supernatant containing the virus suspension was added along with 10 μg/ml of polybrene and 50 µl of HEPES Buffer, pH 8. The virus infection was allowed to proceed for 24 h before the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 2 μg/ml of puromycin. The drug selection was maintained for 48 h to remove the uninfected dead cells, and the surviving cells were those with stably knocked-down MED19. Subsequently, western blot was used to assess the efficiency of the knockdown.

RiboMeth-seq

To evaluate genome-wide changes in rRNA 2′-O-methylation, RiboMeth-seq was performed as previously described [28, 29] with modifications. Total RNA was extracted from wild-type and FBL knockdown HuH7 cells and fragmented by alkaline hydrolysis through incubation with sodium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate mixture solution (pH = 11) at 90 °C for 8 min (20–80 nt). The RNA fragments were ethanol/sodium acetate precipitated. RNA libraries were constructed using Small RNA-seq Lib Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (RK20312, ABclonal) and sequenced on an Illumina platform. For data analysis, sequencing adapters were trimmed and high-quality reads were aligned to reference rRNA sequences using Bowtie2. Nucleotide-resolution coverage profiles of both 5′ and 3′ ends were generated, and the methylation scores (MethScores) were calculated to quantify the likelihood of 2′-O-methylation at each site [28, 29]. Differential methylation analysis was conducted between wild-type and MED19 knockdown conditions.

Detection of nascent proteome (AHA-based)

Cells were cultured to ∼70% confluence. After two washes with PBS, the culture medium was replaced with methionine-free high-glucose DMEM supplemented with dialyzed serum. L-azidohomoalanine-HCl (AHA) was added to a final concentration of 1 mM, and cells were incubated for an additional 30 min. Cells were then lysed in PBS containing 0.5% NP-40. For click chemistry labeling, the reaction mixture was prepared in 100 μl of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0–7.5) as follows (final concentrations indicated): 0.1 mM biotin-alkyne, 0.2 mM CuSO4, 1 mM THPTA (Tris(3-hydroxypropyltriazolyl-methyl) amine), and 5 mM sodium L-ascorbate. The components were mixed thoroughly, followed by the addition of 100 μl of clarified cell lysate. The reaction proceeded at room temperature with gentle rotation for 30 min. Finally, the reaction mixture was subjected to western blot analysis. Biotinylated proteins were detected using streptavidin–conjugated horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and an ECL chemiluminescence detection system.

RNA extraction

Cells in the six-well plate were lysed by 1 ml of Trizol (15596018CN, Thermo Scientific). After homogenization, 200 µl chloroform (C2432, Sigma–Aldrich) was added, and the samples were mixed well. After centrifugation at 12 000 × g (4°C) for 10 min, the aqueous phase was mixed with equal volume isopropanol and was incubated at 4°C for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4°C and 12 000 × g for 10 min to pellet the RNA. The pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried briefly, and resuspended in 50 µl of DEPC-treated water.

RT-qPCR

One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme #R323) kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions at 37°C for 15 min. The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was used to prepare a PCR mix with the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme #Q311-02), and real-time PCR was performed using Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 7 Pro (Thermo Fisher #A43185).

RTL-Q assay

Fifty nanograms of total RNA was mixed with 10 μM specific reverse transcription (RT) primers (Supplementary Table S1) and incubated at 70°C for 5 min, followed by chilling on ice for 3 min. Then, 200 U of MMLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, #M1701) and buffer were added, along with a final concentration of 1 μM dNTP mix and 1 μl of RNase Inhibitor (Vazyme, #R301-01). The mixture was pipetted up and down to mix thoroughly and incubated at 42°C for 1 h, followed by inactivation of the reverse transcriptase at 75°C for 15 min. The reverse-transcribed cDNA was diluted to 40 µl with ddH2O and used for real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR with the Fu-R and Fd-R primer pairs.

Northern blot assay

Total RNA (10 μg) dissolved in the formamide was separated on a 1% formaldehyde agarose gel and transferred onto a positively charged nylon membrane. Following UV crosslinking, the membrane was hybridized with biotin-labeled oligonucleotide probe (Supplementary Table S1) at 42°C overnight in a hybridization oven. Hybridized probes were detected using a streptavidin–conjugated horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and an ECL chemiluminescence detection system.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) experiments were performed as described previously [30, 31]. HuH7 cells were transfected with mEGFP-MED19 plasmids. After 48 h, the green puncta formed by mEGFP in live HuH7 cells were bleached by 488 nm laser at 80% intensity of using the Zeiss LSM 800 microscope. The fluorescence intensity in the fluorescence recovery process was observed using a Zeiss LSM 800 microscope with a 63 × oil immersion objective and recorded by the microscope camera and associated software every 10 s.

Protein preparation

pET28a-6×His-mEGFP-MED19 and pET28a-6×His-mCherry-FBL vectors were introduced into E. coli BL21(DE3). The bacteria were grown at 37°C in 2×YT culture medium with 200 rpm shaking until the optical absorbance reached 0.6. Then IPTG was added into the medium at a final concentration of 1 mM. The culture was continued for 6 h at 37°C. The cells were collected via centrifugating the culture at 6000 × g for 10 min and then sonicated in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl) containing the lysozyme (final concentration, 1 mg/ml). The cell lysate was centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was mixed with Ni-NTA beads that were pre-equilibrated by lysis buffer and incubated with rotation for 2 h. The beads were then washed by wash buffer (5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole) four times. The beads were eluted with elution buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mM imidazole, 145 mM NaCl). The purity of the eluted protein was assessed by SDS–PAGE, and the protein was stored at −20°C in aliquots.

Droplet formation assay

Purified mEGFP-MED19 and mCherry-FBL were mixed with 1% PEG8000 in the buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH = 7.5) and 150 mM NaCl. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min and then observed under Zeiss LSM 800 microscope.

Results

MED19 localizes to the nucleolus via poly-K-featured nucleolar localization sequence

By analyzing the protein sequence of MED19, we found that the C-terminal part of MED19 protein contains multiple poly-lysine (poly-K) patches, which are a typical feature of the nucleolar localization sequence (NoLS) [22] (Fig. 1A). We then used NOD [32], a NoLS prediction tool, to further evaluate the nucleolar localization possibility of MED19. MED19 was predicted to be a nucleolar protein with a potential NoLS located between amino acid 161 and 235, a region that includes multiple poly-K motifs (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, evolutionary analysis based on multiple sequence alignment revealed that the lysine-rich C-terminal sequence of MED19 is highly conserved throughout evolution, particularly in metazoans (Supplementary Fig. S1E). All the above analyses suggest that MED19 is a putative nucleolar-localized protein.

MED19 localizes to the nucleolus. (A) Protein sequence of MED19. The predicted NoLS is highlighted in blue with lysine (K) residues included marked in red. (B) The NoLS prediction of MED19 protein using the NOD web tool. The x-axis indicates the amino acid position along the MED19 protein sequence, while the y-axis shows the nucleolar localization score assigned by the NOD for each amino acid. Each blue dot represents an individual amino acid. The pink region above 0.8 represents potential NoLSs. (C) Immunofluorescence staining of MED19 using specific antibodies in various cell lines. FBL serves as a commonly used nucleolar marker, and DAPI stains nuclear DNA. Scale bar: 10 μm. (D) Immunofluorescence images of the MED23, CDK8, MED4, and MED8 subunits of the Mediator complex in HuH7 cells, using respective antibodies. Scale bar: 10 μm. (E) Schematic representation of exogenously expressed full-length MED19 and its truncated or mutated variants. HA-MED19-FL represents full-length MED19. HA-MED19-NoLS represents MED19 with the NoLS. HA-MED19-ΔNoLS represents MED19 with the NoLS deleted. HA-MED19-K-A represents MED19 with all lysine residues (K) in the NoLS mutated to alanines (A). (F) Immunofluorescence images of exogenously expressed full-length MED19 and its truncated or mutated variants in HuH7 cells, detected using an anti-HA antibody. Scale bar: 10 μm. (G) Schematic illustration of the GFP localization experiment. GFP-WT, a plasmid expressing wild-type GFP protein. GFP-19NoLS, a plasmid expressing a GFP fusion protein with the NoLS of MED19 fused to its C-terminus. (H) Co-localization results of GFP-WT and GFP-19NoLS proteins. (I) Immunofluorescence was performed using an HA-tag antibody. Three representative cells were selected for display. The HA tag was knocked into the N-terminus of the MED19 protein via gene editing technology in the HuH7 hepatocellular carcinoma cell line.​

We then performed immunofluorescence coupled with confocal microscopy using a MED19-specific antibody to examine the subcellular localization of MED19. MED19 exhibited a clustered distribution rather than a diffuse distribution in multiple cell lines, and the formed clusters showed remarkable co-localization with the nucleolar marker protein FBL [33] in terms of shape and distribution (Fig. 1C). Considering that MED19 is also a subunit of the Mediator complex, we next investigated whether MED19 localized to the nucleolus in a manner of being part of the complex. We examined the localization of four representative subunits of the Mediator complex, MED23, CDK8, MED4, and MED8, which belong to the Tail, CKM, Middle, and Head modules of the Mediator complex, respectively [3]. We observed that these subunits exhibited a predominantly diffuse nuclear distribution without apparent nucleolar localization (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1A), in stark contrast to the clustered nucleolar localization of MED19 (Fig. 1C).

To validate the predicted NoLS of MED19, we constructed several truncation and mutation variants of MED19 for immunofluorescence (Fig. 1E). The ectopically expressed full-length HA-MED19 localized to the nucleolus (Fig. 1F), consistent with immunofluorescence analysis using an anti-MED19 antibody. Interestingly, the NoLS of MED19 (HA-MED19-NoLS) is sufficient for nucleolar localization when expressed alone (Fig. 1F). In contrast, deletion of the NoLS of MED19 abolished its nucleolar localization (Fig. 1F). Moreover, when all the lysines in the NoLS of MED19 were mutated to alanines (K-A), its nucleolar localization was also lost (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S1B). Next, we fused its NoLS to GFP and examined the subcellular distribution of the fusion protein. While wild-type GFP showed diffuse localization, the NoLS-tagged GFP was strongly enriched in the nucleolus and co-localized with FBL (Fig. 1G and H; Supplementary Fig. S1F and G). These results demonstrate that MED19’s nucleolar targeting depends on its NoLS and underscore the importance of lysine-rich motifs in this process.

Since overexpression of proteins could potentially cause mis-localization artifact, we used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing to knock in an HA tag at the N-terminus of the endogenous MED19 locus (Supplementary Fig. S1H), ensuring physiological expression of HA-tagged MED19. Immunofluorescence staining with an anti-HA antibody demonstrated clear nucleolar localization of endogenously expressed HA-MED19 (Fig. 1I). Importantly, this result also corroborated the antibody specificity—a common concern in biological studies. Overall, these results provide strong evidence supporting the nucleolar targeting ability of MED19.

By analyzing the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia database, we found that the expression level of MED19 varies slightly in most sequenced cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Notably, HuH7, an adherent epithelioid cell line derived from liver cancer, ranked second in terms of MED19 expression (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Consequently, HuH7, along with the commonly used rapidly proliferating cell line 293T, was selected for subsequent experiments.

MED19 directly binds to rRNA

Based on previous studies, the positively charged NoLS is recruited to the nucleolus through binding to negatively charged nucleolar RNAs [22]. Given that MED19 also contains a lysine-rich NoLS, we speculate that MED19 is a novel RNA-binding protein. Supporting this hypothesis, a large-scale RNA-binding proteome study previously identified the poly-lysine (poly-K) motif as one of the most abundant non-classical RNA-binding motifs in the human RNA-bound proteome [34]. Furthermore, MED19 was predicted to be an RNA-binding protein by catRAPID [35], a widely used computational tool for RNA-binding protein prediction (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B).

To determine whether MED19 directly binds RNA, we performed a Silica-Enrichment assay under stringent denaturing conditions [36] (Fig. 2A). Two hundred fifty-four nanometer UV covalently cross-links proteins to their directly bound RNA, whereas protein–protein interactions are not cross-linked [37]. Under high concentrations of guanidine salt, RNA is adsorbed onto silica matrices, and proteins that are covalently cross-linked to the RNA are retained on the silica surface [38, 39, 40]. In contrast, indirect RNA-binding proteins (mediated through protein–protein interaction), as well as free proteins, are efficiently removed due to the strong denaturing properties of guanidine salt [41]. This method enables the selective enrichment of proteins that directly bind RNA, which can subsequently be analyzed by western blotting (Fig. 2A).

As positive controls, the well-characterized nucleolar RNA-binding proteins FBL and NPM1 were enriched by silica matrices (Fig. 2B and C). In contrast, tubulin and actin, which were used as negative controls, showed no enrichment under the same conditions (Fig. 2D and E). Notably, MED19 was also significantly enriched by silica material (Fig. 2F). HuR and HNRNPC, two other classical RNA-binding proteins, were also employed as positive controls and exhibited significant electrophoretic shifts indicative of covalently bound RNA (Supplementary Fig. S2C and D). However, no pronounced mobility shifts were observed for the nucleolar RNA-binding proteins FBL and NPM1, which is in agreement with the pattern seen for MED19. Exogenously expressed full-length MED19 was efficiently enriched by the silica material, whereas the truncated variant lacking the NoLS failed to be enriched (Fig. 2G). Notably, the ectopically expressed NoLS domain alone was similarly enriched, indicating its sufficiency for RNA binding (Fig. 2H). In contrast, the K-A mutation variant of MED19 failed to be enriched by silica material even after the UV cross-linking (Fig. 2I), suggesting that the positively charged lysine residues within the NoLS are essential for RNA interaction.

To further verify the RNA-binding region of MED19, live 293T cells were subjected to UV-cross-linking, followed by immunoprecipitation of MED19 from cell lysates. The immunoprecipitated MED19 was then subjected to RNase digestion, followed by mass spectrometry analysis. After RNase digestion, the remaining RNA adducts on the protein were identified as specific modifications by dedicated mass spectrometry data-processing algorithms [27], allowing for the precise mapping of RNA-binding amino acid residues of the protein (Fig. 2J). Mass spectrometry analysis revealed that lysine residues within MED19’s NoLS were predominantly involved in RNA binding, particularly those located in poly-K motifs (Fig. 2K). While lysine accounts for only 12% of the total amino acid content in MED19, it represented 96.5% of the residues identified as cross-linked to RNA by mass spectrometry (Supplementary Fig. S2F and G). This striking enrichment strongly suggests that the detected RNA–amino acid cross-links are not due to random or background interactions, but rather reflect the genuine RNA-binding properties of MED19. Similar results were obtained when the NoLS of MED19 was fused to the C-terminus of GFP (Supplementary Fig. S2E, I, and J). In this context, RNA was specifically cross-linked to the lysine residues within the NoLS, with minimal cross-linking observed in the GFP moiety (Supplementary Fig. S2E). Additionally, we observed that uridine-containing RNA fragments were cross-linked to MED19 with characteristic neutral losses of H₂O or HPO₃ (Supplementary Fig. S2H and K). These findings further support the critical and specific role of lysine residues in the RNA-binding activity of MED19’s NoLS.

Next, we employed CLIP-seq [42] to identify the RNA species directly bound to MED19 under denaturing conditions. Sequencing results showed that MED19 primarily bound to rRNA (Fig. 2L and M). Among the snoRNAs co-pulled down by MED19, ~80% were classified as the BoxC/D-type snoRNAs (Fig. 2N), implying that MED19 may play a role in regulating rRNA 2′-O-methyaltion. To further demonstrate that MED19 directly binds rRNA, we have performed a HaloTag-based denaturing UV-RIP-qPCR experiment (Supplementary Fig. S2M). HaloTag is a protein tag that can form a covalent bond with Halo-ligand magnetic beads [43]. The covalent bonds can withstand extremely stringent washing conditions. 6 M guanidine hydrochloride or 10% SDS, or 8 M urea were used in the washing steps, followed by additional high- and low-salt buffers. The results showed that under the washing of these three strong protein denaturants, compared with the negative control GFP protein, MED19 can still significantly bind to pre-rRNA (Fig. 2O). EMSA demonstrates that MED19 is capable of directly binding to rRNA in vitro; however, when all lysine residues within the NoLS domain of MED19 were mutated to alanine, the protein then lost its ability to interact with rRNA (Fig. 2P and Supplementary Fig. S2L). Collectively, these findings establish MED19 as a novel RNA-binding protein that directly interacts with rRNA through its lysine-rich NoLS domain.

MED19 interacts with BoxC/D-snoRNPs

To further explore the function of MED19 in the nucleolus, immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry [44] (IP-MS) experiments were performed to identify the interacting proteins of MED19. More than 1000 putative interaction partners were identified (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S2). Gene Ontology (GO) [45] enrichment analysis revealed that the MED19 interacting proteins are mainly involved in nucleolus- and translation-related processes (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S3A and C). Enrichment analysis based on the Reactome database [46] revealed that the interacting proteins of MED19 are primarily involved in rRNA modification and processing (Fig. 3D). Intersection of MED19-interacting proteins with nucleolar proteins annotated in the GO database showed that ~25% of the identified interactors are classified as nucleolar proteins (Fig. 3A). Functional enrichment analysis of this 25% subset yielded results highly similar to those obtained for the full set of MED19 interactors (Fig. 3C and E; Supplementary Fig. S3B and D), suggesting that the nucleolar component of the interactome largely accounts for the major biological functions associated with MED19.

Integrating the analysis of MED19-interacting proteins with its CLIP-seq data, we propose that MED19 may be involved in the regulation of rRNA 2′-O-methylation. This modification is mediated by the BoxC/D-snoRNP, which consists of a sequence-specific guiding snoRNA and four core protein components: FBL, NOP58, NOP56, and SNU13 [47]. The IP-MS analysis of MED19 also identified these four proteins (Fig. 3F), which were further confirmed by immunoprecipitation–western blot (IP-WB) (Fig. 3G). We also found that the interaction between MED19 and BoxC/D-snoRNP proteins was resistant to RNase A treatment, suggesting that the interaction is independent of RNA (Fig. 3G). Reciprocal Co-IP using an anti-FBL antibody selectively enriched MED19 over other Mediator subunits (Fig. 3H). Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in technology, we first inserted an HA tag at the N-terminus of either MED19 or MED23 in 293T cells for minimizing non-specific binding associated with different antibodies and beads. Co-IP experiments with anti-HA beads confirmed that both MED19 and MED23 were able to pull down core Mediator subunits; however, only MED19, not MED23, is able to specifically pull down snoRNP components (Fig. 3K). These results strongly indicate that MED19 associates with snoRNPs independently of the Mediator complex and localizes to the nucleolus. Importantly, the K-A mutant of MED19 abolished its interaction with Box C/D snoRNP proteins (Fig. 3I). Moreover, ectopic expression of the NoLS domain of MED19 alone was also able to pull down BoxC/D-snoRNP proteins, with interaction strength comparable to that observed with full-length MED19 (Fig. 3I). Likewise, the deletion of the N-terminal or the middle part of MED19 did not impair its ability to associate with Box C/D snoRNPs (Fig. 3J). In summary, MED19 interacts with the Box C/D snoRNP complex in the nucleolus, and this interaction is predominantly mediated by the NoLS domain.

MED19 promotes 2′-O-methylation of rRNA by enhancing the binding of FBL to pre-rRNA

Based on the above findings, we hypothesize that MED19 may play a regulatory role in rRNA 2′-O-methylation. To test this hypothesis, we first performed mass spectrometry analysis of 2′-O-methylated nucleosides and found that knocking down MED19 significantly reduced the 2′-O-methylation level of total RNA (Fig. 4A). Next, we employed RTL-Q [48, 49], a quantitative PCR-based method, to assess changes in 2′-O-methylation level at specific sites (Fig. 4B). Under low dNTP concentrations (0.5–1 μM), reverse transcriptase stalls at 2′-O-methylated sites, leading to a truncated cDNA species that terminates at the modification site. When no 2′-O-methylation is present at the corresponding position in an individual RNA molecule, full-length cDNA synthesis occurs (Fig. 4B). By designing primers flanking the target 2′-O-methylation site, the relative abundance of these cDNA forms can be quantified by qPCR to determine the methylation level at the specific modification site (Fig. 4B). We selected several well-characterized 2′-O-methylation sites on rRNA for RTL-Q analysis. The results showed that MED19 knockdown significantly decreased methylation levels at these sites (Figs 4C–E). Similar reductions were observed in HuH7 cells (Supplementary Fig. S4A). To comprehensively profile the changes in ​​2′-O-methylation​​ on rRNA, we performed ​​Ribometh-seq​​ analysis [28, 29]. The phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides in RNA undergo hydrolysis in alkaline solutions, leading to random RNA fragmentation. However, phosphodiester bonds with 2′-O-methylated nucleotides resist this chemical degradation. By aligning the ends of all sequenced fragments to the reference rRNA sequence, we can identify significantly underrepresented ends, which are then identified as 2′-O-methylation sites, and their relative methylation levels can be calculated accordingly (Fig. 4F). By performing Ribometh-seq on total RNA extracted from HuH7 cells with or without MED19 knockdown, we observed that the knockdown of MED19 led to a significant decrease in the methylation levels at numerous 2′-O-methylated sites. However, the extent of this decrease was not uniform across all sites. Some sites exhibited only a mild reduction in 2′-O-methylation, while others showed a much more pronounced decrease (Fig. 4G–I). Notably, the results of ​​RTL-Q​​ showed a ​​consistent trend​​ with those obtained from the ​​Ribometh-seq​​. We then attempted to determine the mechanism by which MED19 regulates rRNA 2′-O-methylation. Given that MED19 may modulate gene transcription, we first examined whether its knockdown affects the expression of Box C/D snoRNP proteins. Western blot analysis revealed that MED19 depletion did not alter their protein levels (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Fig. S4B), suggesting that its regulatory role in rRNA 2′-O-methylation is unlikely to stem from changes in the expression of the catalytic machinery. Next, we performed CLIP-qPCR using an FBL antibody. The results showed that MED19 knockdown reduced FBL binding to pre-rRNA, while MED19 re-expression restored the FBL-rRNA interaction (Fig. 4K and L). This indicates that in the nucleolus, MED19 may promote the binding of FBL to its catalytic substrate pre-rRNA through a certain mechanism, thereby enhancing FBL’s overall catalytic efficiency in establishing 2′-O-methylation modifications.

These findings presented herein demonstrate that MED19 modulates the interaction between fibrillarin (FBL) and its substrate pre-rRNA; however, the underlying molecular basis needs to be further defined. MED19 contains extensive intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) (Fig. 5A), a structural feature associated with the capacity to facilitate liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS). Phase separation has been shown to enhance biochemical reaction efficiency by concentrating molecular components [50–53], with prior studies demonstrating that enzymatic and regulatory proteins can leverage LLPS to augment catalytic activity [54–57]. Notably, FBL has been reported to be implicated in phase separation processes [58–60], and given the nucleolus’ crowded microenvironment [33], we hypothesize that MED19 undergoes LLPS with FBL to enhance local biochemical reactions driving rRNA 2′-O-methylation. In vitro experiments revealed that purified MED19 forms droplet-like condensates (Fig. 5B and C), and co-incubation with equimolar FBL results in joint phase separation into hybrid droplets (Fig. 5D). Live-cell imaging of mEGFP-MED19-transfected cells using FRAP revealed dynamic properties typical of liquid condensates: following photobleaching, mEGFP-MED19 fluorescence recovered within minutes (Fig. 5E and F). Given MED19’s established RNA-binding activity with pre-rRNA (Fig. 2), these data support a model whereby MED19-FBL phase separation enhances their cooperative association with pre-rRNA substrates.

MED19 promotes IRES-mediated translation of onco-promoting genes

Mature ribosomes with elevated rRNA 2′-O-methylation levels have been shown to enhance IRES-dependent translation—a cap-independent mechanism for mRNA translation initiation in transcripts harboring IRES elements within their 5′ untranslated regions (5′ UTRs) [62–65] (Fig. 6A). IRES sequences enable ribosome recruitment independently of the 5′ cap structure, a mechanism first identified in viral RNAs [66, 67]. The encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) IRES is a widely used model element in bicistronic IRES reporter systems [68]. Using the luciferase-based bicistronic assay [69] (Fig. 6B), we demonstrate that MED19 depletion significantly reduces EMCV IRES-mediated translation efficiency (Fig. 6E). Conversely, MED19 overexpression enhances EMCV IRES activity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6E). In mammalian mRNAs, the c-Myc oncogene harbors a well-characterized IRES in its 5′ UTR [70] (Fig. 6C and D). Cloning the c-Myc IRES into the bicistronic IRES reporter revealed that MED19 knockdown impairs c-Myc IRES-dependent translation (Fig. 6F). Consistently, MED19 depletion reduces c-Myc protein abundance without altering mRNA levels (Fig. 6G and H), whereas Tubulin—lacking a known IRES in its 5′ UTR—remains unaffected (Fig. 6G). As a critical regulator of cell proliferation linked to oncogenesis [71], c-Myc’s translation is thus post-transcriptionally regulated by MED19 via IRES-mediated mechanisms.

To explore the generality of this mechanism, we investigated three additional genes—XIAP [72, 73], CCND1 [74–76], and c-Fos [77]—whose translation has been previously shown to strongly depend on IRES sequences, and they are also onco-promoting genes. The IRES sequences from these genes were cloned into the IRES reporter plasmid, and luciferase assays revealed that MED19 knockdown significantly impaired translational activity of these IRES elements (Supplementary Fig. S5A). This defect was rescued by MED19 re-expression (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Western blot analysis in 293T and HuH7 cells confirmed that endogenous protein levels of XIAP, CCND1, and c-Fos were reduced upon MED19 depletion and restored by its re-expression (Supplementary Fig. S5B and C). These data indicate that, analogous to c-Myc, MED19 promotes translation of diverse IRES-containing mRNAs via enhanced rRNA 2′-O-methylation.

To validate the specificity of MED19’s role in IRES-mediated translation, we used MED23—a Mediator subunit lacking nucleolar localization (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1A)—as a negative control. The bicistronic IRES reporter harboring the c-Myc IRES was employed to assess translation efficiency following MED23 knockdown. Quantitative analysis of luciferase activity showed no significant change upon MED23 depletion (Supplementary Fig. S5D), and western blotting confirmed unchanged c-Myc protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S5E). These results demonstrate that MED19’s regulation of c-Myc IRES-dependent translation is specific and not attributable to general Mediator complex function.

Pan-cancer analysis reinforces the onco-promoting role of MED19

Comprehensive analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database [78] revealed frequent upregulation of MED19 transcripts across diverse cancer types (Fig. 7A). Survival analysis demonstrated that high MED19 expression correlates with poor prognosis in multiple malignancies (Fig. 7I and J; Supplementary Fig. S6B–E). Leveraging the DepMap database [79], which integrates genome-wide CRISPR-KO and RNAi screens across >1000 cancer cell lines, we found that genetic depletion of MED19 impairs proliferation in most cell lines (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. S6A), indicating its essential role in sustaining cancer cell growth. Functional validation in 293T and HuH7 cells using CCK8 assays confirmed that MED19 knockdown significantly reduces proliferation rates (Fig. 7C and D), consistent with database-derived insights. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of cancer cell lines and clinical tumor samples revealed a positive correlation between MED19 and c-Myc protein abundance, whereas no such correlation was observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 7E–H). This finding reinforces the post-transcriptional regulatory role of MED19 in c-Myc expression. Collectively, these integrative analyses across public datasets and experimental models corroborate MED19’s widespread onco-promoting function [12] and align with our mechanistic findings. Finally, we propose a model of the molecular mechanism for MED19-mediated onco-promoting role (Fig. 7K).

Discussion

Historically characterized as a subunit of the Mediator complex [11], our study uncovers an unexpected nucleolar localization of MED19 that is independent of the Mediator complex. Importantly, this nucleolar localization is robustly supported by multiple independent assays, including various immunofluorescence analyses (Fig. 1), diverse co-immunoprecipitation experiments (Fig. 3), MED19 CLIP-seq (Fig. 2L–N), and EMSA (Fig. 2P). Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in of an HA tag at the endogenous MED19 locus provides additional confirmation of the nucleolar localization for MED19.

Previous investigations into nucleolar protein localization have established that such targeting often requires NoLSs, which typically consist of lysine/arginine-rich motifs, including poly-lysine (poly-K) stretches [21, 22]. The positive charge of poly-K sequences enables electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged RNA backbone, thereby facilitating nucleolar recruitment of target proteins [22]. Notably, among all Mediator complex subunits, MED19 uniquely harbors a poly-K sequence. This distinctive feature confers MED19 with specialized nucleolar targeting and RNA-binding capabilities. Given that MED19 also functions as a Mediator subunit [81], future studies may address whether it contributes to the complex’s RNA-binding capacity and how such RNA-binding might be involved in regulating RNA polymerase II (Pol II)-dependent transcription.

Recent studies have revised the traditional view of rRNA 2′-O-methylation as a static modification, demonstrating its dynamic regulation across cell types and physiological states [63]. This plasticity critically influences cellular functions and fate decisions [82]; however, the molecular mechanisms governing this process remain poorly characterized.

In this study, we show that MED19 depletion reduces rRNA 2′-O-methylation levels without altering the abundance of fibrillarin (FBL), the catalytic enzyme for this modification. Instead, MED19 facilitates FBL binding to pre-rRNA substrates. We propose that MED19 promotes phase separation with FBL, enhancing their local concentration and interaction probability. Supporting this model, MED19 contains an extended IDR (Fig. 5A), a hallmark of proteins capable of forming biomolecular condensates [60]. Phase separation is known to accelerate local biochemical reactions [54], and MED19’s dual binding capacity for rRNA and FBL provides a mechanistic basis for its role in modulating 2′-O-methylation efficiency.

The biochemical mechanisms by which rRNA 2′-O-methylation enhances IRES-dependent translation remain incompletely understood. High-throughput analyses have predicted over 1000 mammalian mRNAs to contain IRES elements [83], yet only a subset—predominantly encoding cancer-related proteins—have been experimentally validated [84]. Among these, c-Myc mRNA was one of the first identified IRES-containing transcripts [70]. IRES-mediated translation provides translational flexibility critical for supporting rapid proliferation of cancer cells [66]. While MED19 is canonically recognized as a Mediator subunit regulating RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription [85], our findings uncover an unexpected role for MED19 in promoting IRES-dependent translation. This non-canonical function is enabled by MED19’s nucleolar localization and its regulation of rRNA methylation, revealing a novel mechanism through which a transcriptional regulator intersects with translational control.

Collectively, MED19 may enhance FBL activity through the formation of phase-separated condensates with FBL. As a core subunit of small nucleolar ribonucleoproteins (snoRNPs), FBL catalyzes 2′-O-methylation and participates in pre-rRNA processing [58, 86]. Given that FBL has been reported to regulate 18S rRNA processing [58], we examined the impact of MED19 on this pathway. MED19 knockdown in cells resulted in a marked reduction in mature 18S rRNA, with no effect on rDNA transcription (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). Northern blot analysis of RNA isolated from HuH7 cells further confirmed that MED19 deficiency impairs 18S pre-rRNA processing (Supplementary Fig. S7C and D).

Since dysregulated 18S rRNA processing can reduce global translation efficiency, we measured the abundance of newly synthesized proteins using an AHA-based click chemistry assay [87, 88]. This analysis demonstrated that MED19 knockdown leads to a decrease in global protein synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S7E). These observations indicate that MED19 knockdown compromises 18S rRNA maturation, which in turn reduces global translational capacity. Together, these results expand the functional significance of the MED19-FBL interaction.

Impairment of pre-rRNA processing lowers global protein synthesis rates, limiting the cellular supply of proteins. Concurrently, reduced rRNA 2′-O-methylation further impairs IRES-mediated translation. We propose that under the backdrop of overall translational suppression, the additional impairment of IRES-dependent translation may combinatorially dampen the synthesis of proteins encoded by IRES-containing oncogenes. Given that rapidly proliferating cancer cells rely on robust protein production and sufficient levels of oncoproteins (e.g. c-Myc), impairment of these two processes would restrict cancer cell proliferation. Collectively, these findings suggest that MED19 exerts its well-documented oncogenic function through this novel mechanism.

In summary, our study identifies the nucleolus as the critical subcellular compartment for MED19-mediated oncogenic activity. By establishing a novel link between a canonical transcriptional coactivator and translational regulation, these results uncover a non-canonical function of MED19—providing potential innovative strategies for cancer therapeutic intervention.
