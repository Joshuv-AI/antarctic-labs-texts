Data deficiency, taxonomic bias, and economic interests curtail insect and arachnid conservation in the United States
Authors: Wes Walsh, Laura L. Figueroa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceA comprehensive evaluation of North American insect and arachnid species assessments and US endangered species legislation shows the conservation needs of most North American insect and arachnid species are unknown, and US state and federal legal protections are minimal and taxonomically biased. Extractive industries appear to limit policy implementation while positive public attitudes toward the environment are correlated with more protected insect and arachnid species. This quantitative assessment of US insect and arachnid conservation needs highlights that scientists, government, nonprofit organizations, and community members must work collectively to increase data collection, strengthen local conservation policies, diversify the taxa receiving protections, and generate a broad base of public support to effectively protect invertebrate biodiversity and the crucial services they provide.

Biodiversity is essential for long-term ecosystem health (1). In recent decades, biodiversity loss has accelerated, with documented declines across a broad range of taxa (2–8), although discrepancies in the extent to which species are studied and assessed makes documenting biodiversity loss more difficult in some taxa than others (4, 9). For example, while 100% of described bird species have been evaluated for extinction risk by the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN), only 1% of described insects and arachnids have been similarly assessed, despite accounting for 73% of all described animal species on earth (10, 11). Discrepancies in assessment and taxonomic bias in species protections have led to conservation policies that primarily benefit large vertebrates (12), meaning vulnerable taxa that receive less widespread public support, such as insects and arachnids, may be left with few protections.

Insects and arachnids are of immense ecological, economic, and cultural importance (13–15). They provide the crucial and frequently cited ecosystem services of pollination (16), biological control (17), and nutrient cycling (14, 18), but are also bioindicators of air and water quality (19, 20), staples of science education (21), and have inspired art (22), culture (14, 15), and technology (15, 23) for millennia. Insect and arachnid biomass, abundance, and species richness have all declined in recent decades (2, 5–7). Consequently, some laws to protect arthropods have been enacted around the world, but policies tend to favor certain charismatic insects such as large, colorful butterflies and moths (24–27). Taxonomic biases in insect and arachnid conservation policies have not been systematically evaluated in the United States (24), despite being a large, biodiverse country with an existing legislative framework for protecting endangered species. Therefore, it remains unclear to what extent vulnerable insects and arachnids in the United States are unprotected, limiting action to effectively protect invertebrate biodiversity.

The federal government of the United States has, historically, had one of the world’s strongest laws for protecting vulnerable species: the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA). This law mandates implementation of a species recovery plan, continuous population monitoring, and protection from lethal and nonlethal collection and harassment for all listed species (28). The US Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) reviews species proposed for listing by USFWS staff, as well as by public petitions, to determine whether protections are warranted. Although the act stipulates decisions be based only on “the best scientific and commercial data available” (28), studies of vertebrates show bias toward “charismatic” animals (29, 30). Similarly, although the act is meant to prioritize species protections, its implementation is often shaped by opposition from extractive industries (i.e., mining, quarrying, and oil and gas extraction) that argue species protections adversely impact profits (31, 32). Industry opposition has led to stalled listing decisions (33), poorer outcomes for protected species (34), and weakened federal habitat protections (35). Given both the value of the ESA for species conservation, and known biases in vertebrate listings, it is important to understand whether this law adequately protects vulnerable insects and arachnids.

The challenges of federal species protections in the United States (35) highlight the need to also understand conservation policies at more local levels, and whether taxonomy and external interests affect these protections. Most US states have a state endangered species act (SESA), though the strength of these protections varies greatly relative to the ESA. State protections range from only weak prohibitions on the trafficking and intentional killing of protected species, to recovery planning and habitat protections, which, in a few states (namely Illinois, Massachusetts, and Wisconsin), are even stronger than those of the ESA (36–38). Unlike the federal ESA, not all SESAs consider insects and arachnids eligible for protection (24). Furthermore, few state laws require scientific backing for their listing decisions (36), which might leave states more susceptible to making listing decisions based on other factors, such as the relative charisma of a given taxa or how protections may conflict with industry interests. Understanding the factors that limit or enhance protections at the state level could enable targeted awareness campaigns, and ultimately, policy improvements.

Here, we comprehensively evaluate insect and arachnid conservation at the state and federal levels in the United States. We compiled an up-to-date list of the described insect and arachnid species in North America north of Mexico (the best comparable range available for most orders) and then evaluate the extent to which described insect and arachnid species have been assessed for extinction risk by NatureServe (NS), an independent assessment of species conservation needs in the United States and Canada. We use NS to maximize policy relevance since NS is most widely used by the US government to inform conservation decisions (39). We then assembled a list of all insect and arachnid species legally protected by US state or federal law and compared them against all US insects and arachnids on NS to determine the extent to which taxonomy and extinction risk influence protections. Finally, to understand potential impediments to enacting invertebrate conservation legislation, we tested ecological, economic, and social explanations for differences in state-level endangered species policies. Our study provides a comprehensive quantitative assessment of US invertebrate conservation legislation, outlining clear gaps in current species protections and specific barriers that must be addressed to protect vital insect and arachnid species.

Results

Conservation Assessment of Described North American Insect and Arachnid Species.

Based on our compiled list, there are 99,312 described insect and arachnid species in North America north of Mexico, the most speciose being Coleoptera (beetles; 25,160 species), Hymenoptera (bees, ants, and wasps; 18,000 species), and Diptera (flies; 17,000 species) (Fig. 1). Just 46.6% of described insects and arachnids (46,257 species) have entries on NS, the primary database informing US species protections (39) and, of the insect and arachnid species on NS, 75.3% (34,823 species) have not been assessed for extinction risk throughout their range in the United States and Canada (Figs. 1 and 2 and SI Appendix, Table S1). This means, between described species without entries and species with unassessed entries, the conservation needs of 88.5% of described insect and arachnid species are unknown. The percentage of described insects and arachnids with entries on NS varied significantly by order (Χ2= 971.32, df = 40, P < 0.001; Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S1), with 73.2% of orders having less than half of their described species on NS.

For the insects and arachnids with entries on NS, order was a significant predictor of whether species had been assessed for extinction risk throughout their range in the United States and Canada (“range wide”) (Χ2 = 14066, df = 37, P < 0.001; Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S2). The orders Ephemeroptera (mayflies), Plecoptera (stoneflies), Trichoptera (caddisflies), and Odonata (damselflies and dragonflies) were more likely to be assessed for extinction risk than others (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S2), presumably because these taxa are monitored as indicators of water quality and many are used by anglers (20). Of the insect and arachnid species on NS, 6.7% (3,093 species) are classified as critically imperiled, imperiled, or vulnerable (hereafter, collectively, “at-risk”) range wide (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S1). Within assessed insect and arachnid species, the orders Opiliones (harvestmen), Plecoptera, and Pseudoscorpiones (pseudoscorpions), were significantly more likely to be at-risk range wide than other orders (Χ2 = 1633.9, df = 24, P < 0.001; Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S3).

US Protected Insect and Arachnid Species.

Only 2.5% of the insects and arachnids in the United States considered at-risk throughout their range have protection nationwide under the federal ESA, compared to 27.7% of birds at-risk range wide in the United States (Figs. 1 and 3). As of 2024, the ESA protects 108 insect and arachnid species (Figs. 1 and 3 and SI Appendix, S2). The orders with the most federally protected species are Lepidoptera (butterflies and moths; 42 species), Coleoptera (19 species), and Diptera (15 species) (Figs. 1 and 3 and SI Appendix, S2). We identified 339 insect and arachnid species protected by SESAs, including 36 insect and arachnid species also protected federally (Figs. 1 and 3 and SI Appendix, S2). Cumulatively, state laws protect just 3.7% of US insect and arachnid species considered at-risk range wide (Fig. 3). The orders with the most state-protected species are Lepidoptera (145 species), Odonata (90 species), and Coleoptera (35 species) (Figs. 1 and 3 and SI Appendix, S2). Thus, within the small subset of US insect and arachnid species that have been assessed and are known to be at-risk range wide, we found just 5.3% are protected by any federal or state endangered species law (Fig. 3).

Within insects and arachnids, federal and state listings were taxonomically biased at the order level (federal: Χ2 = 129.96, df = 36, P < 0.001; state: Χ2 = 420.59, df = 36, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Tables S4 and S5). Under the federal ESA, odonates were more likely to be protected than coleopterans (SI Appendix, Table S4). At the state level, pairwise comparisons showed odonates were significantly more likely to be protected compared to all other insect and arachnid orders that had at least two state protected species (28 of 41 orders had no state protected species), followed by lepidopterans (SI Appendix, Table S5). Hemipterans (true bugs) were more likely than plecopterans, trichopterans, and hymenopterans to be state protected (SI Appendix, Table S5). Also, due to a disproportionate number of protected dipterans in a single genus from Hawaii (SI Appendix, Tables S6 and S7), dipterans were more likely to be protected than coleopterans, orthopterans (grasshoppers, crickets, katydids), plecopterans, and pseudoscorpions at the federal level and more likely to be protected than trichopterans at the state level (SI Appendix, Tables S4 and S5). Order was also a significant predictor of whether an insect or arachnid species protected under the federal ESA had been petitioned by the public for listing (e.g., by a nonprofit organization or independent researcher as opposed to being proposed by USFWS staff; Χ2 = 45.37, df = 10, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Specifically, all federally protected hymenopterans, plecopterans, and orthopterans were petitioned, compared to 35.7% of lepidopterans (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Of all 26,125 insect and arachnid species recorded in the United States on NS (SI Appendix, Table S8), species at greater risk of extinction range wide were more likely to receive protection at both the federal and state levels compared to species at lesser risk of extinction (extinction risk federal: Χ2 = 472.89, df = 9, P < 0.001; state: Χ2 = 369.15, df = 9, P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Tables S9 and S10). Insect and arachnid species that had been evaluated for extinction risk by NS range wide were also more likely to be protected than those that had not been evaluated (SI Appendix, Tables S9 and S10). Since most North American insects and arachnids on NS have not been evaluated for extinction risk, this means most insects and arachnids are unlikely to receive legal protection.

State Laws Protecting Insects and Arachnids.

As of 2024, 45 states maintained a legally protected state endangered species list, but just 57.7% (26 states) clearly included insects in their definition of wildlife and protected at least one (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and Dataset S1). Although state definitions of wildlife that included insects also generally applied to arachnids (Dataset S1), only five states protect an arachnid species, with each of those states protecting just one. We tested the following as predictors of state policy (SI Appendix, Table S11): number of range wide at-risk insects and arachnids occurring in the state, ecoregion richness, total state income (measured by Gross Domestic Product, GDP), percent of GDP from extractive industries (mining, quarrying, and oil and gas extraction), percentage of federally owned land, number of R1 research institutions, and prevalence of eco-centric values in the state’s population [a value orientation in which wildlife are seen as deserving rights similar to humans (40)]. The percentage of GDP from extractive industries was the only significant predictor of whether a state had a SESA (Χ2 = 10.11, df = 1, P = 0.001; Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S12), and for states that had a SESA, whether it included insects and arachnids as eligible (Χ2 = 15.43, df = 1, P < 0.001; Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Table S13). States with higher percentages of GDP from extractive industries were less likely to have a SESA (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S12), and if they did have one, were less likely to include insects and arachnids (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Table S13). For states with SESAs that have at least one insect or arachnid species protected, the percent of state residents with eco-centric values was the only significant predictor of how many total insects and arachnids the state protected (Χ2 = 7.54, df = 1, P = 0.006; Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Table S14), where a higher proportion of the state’s population holding eco-centric values corresponded with more protected insect and arachnid species in that state (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Table S14).

Discussion

Lack of Assessment Impedes Conservation of Insects and Arachnids.

Almost nothing is known about the status and conservation needs of most insects and arachnids in North America. We found that less than half of described insect and arachnid species have entries on NatureServe (NS), and of the insects and arachnids with entries, over 75% have not been evaluated for extinction risk throughout their range in the United States and Canada. Thus, the number of insect and arachnid species whose conservation needs are understood is a small and taxonomically biased fraction of the species that exist (41, 42), which directly inhibits conservation because unevaluated insects and arachnids were significantly less likely to receive protection. Moreover, population trends and life history information is still lacking for many species that have been assessed by NS, even those that have legal protection, further impeding effective conservation. Therefore, unless proactive measures are taken to document, assess, and study a greater diversity of insects and arachnids, many understudied species currently facing extinction may be lost (4, 9).

Insect and Arachnid Protections are Limited.

Of the insect and arachnid species in the United States known to be at-risk range wide, a highly conservative estimate of true conservation needs, 94.7% have no legal protection. Only 2.5% have nationwide protection through the federal ESA compared to 27.7% of range wide at-risk bird species in the United States. Protections for birds are higher due to both widespread public support (29) and a long legacy of effective collaboration between stakeholders (e.g., nonprofit organizations, hunters, and bird watchers) to monitor birds and support the passage of bird-focused conservation policies (e.g., Migratory Bird Treaty Act) (43). These efforts have successfully recovered bird species, including the bald eagle and peregrine falcon, from the brink of extinction. By comparison, insects and arachnids are very poorly protected and would likely benefit from similar campaigns to increase public support and foster collaboration among diverse stakeholders.

All state laws combined protect just 3.7% of range wide at-risk insect and arachnid species, and these laws, bound by state borders, do not necessarily protect those species throughout their US range. In addition to those insects and arachnids considered at-risk range wide, many states also protect insects and arachnids which are considered at-risk within the state but stable range wide (Figs. 1 and SI Appendix, S3 and S6). States may prioritize locally vulnerable species, believing federal agencies should protect more widely at-risk species. For US birds, this diffusion of responsibility has left some regionally and nationally at-risk birds completely unprotected (44), and the problem is amplified for insects and arachnids because so few are federally protected and not all states consider them eligible for protection. Moreover, legal protections in some states may be minimal or unenforced (36–38), and although some states include insects and arachnids in their State Wildlife Action Plans, these programs can be of limited conservation value if agencies lack the legal authority to manage invertebrates (45). Ultimately, minimal federal listings and patchy state protections leave almost every at-risk insect and arachnid in the country unprotected from decline and potential extinction.

We also found that protections for arachnids were particularly scarce, even compared to insects. There were only five state protected arachnids in the United States; 80.8% of the states that protect insects do not protect a single arachnid species, and of the 11 federally protected arachnids, nine only occur in a small region of central Texas. Thus, throughout the United States, even in areas where many insects are protected, arachnids are largely ignored, a trend also seen in European species protections (46). This is concerning because, to the extent that they have been evaluated, we found that Pseudoscorpiones (pseudoscorpions) and Opiliones (harvestmen) were two of the orders most likely to be at-risk in the United States and Canada. Many arachnids are data poor, which may partially explain the lack of protection, however, arachnids can also inspire negative emotional responses in many people, reducing public support for arachnid conservation (47). Many vulnerable pseudoscorpions and opiliones are also small, dull-colored cave dwellers, meaning, compared to large day-flying insects like butterflies and dragonflies, these arachnids may be less frequently encountered, making them both difficult to study and less known to the public. Still, arachnids are of immense ecological (14), economic (17), and scientific value (19), and campaigns to strengthen arachnid conservation and improve public perception of these taxa are urgently needed.

Insect and Arachnid Protections are Biased, Primarily Toward Odonata and Lepidoptera.

Although state and federal protections do prioritize more vulnerable insects and arachnids, both are also taxonomically biased, primarily toward Odonata (dragonflies and damselflies) and Lepidoptera (butterflies and moths). European insect protections are similarly biased toward these orders, though whether this is a product of a human preference for large, colorful, and day-flying (i.e., “charismatic”) taxa like many butterfly and dragonfly species, or because these orders are better monitored or inherently more at-risk than others is not entirely certain (25, 27). In the United States, a preference for charismatic insects seems like the most likely explanation since there are several well-monitored, highly at-risk insect orders which are poorly protected and comprised primarily of inconspicuous, less well-known species. Specifically, we found that the orders Plecoptera (stoneflies) and Trichoptera (caddisflies) have over 98% of their described species entered on NS, most likely because these orders are monitored as bioindicators of water quality and are important to anglers (20). Over 45% of assessed plecopteran and trichopteran species in the United States are considered at-risk range wide, yet, of range wide at-risk species, just 0.6% of trichopterans and 1.0% of plecopterans are protected, compared to 26.8% of odonates and 11.6% of lepidopterans. This discrepancy suggests that biases in protection are not due solely to differences in monitoring or inherent risk, but rather are shaped by a human bias toward certain taxa.

We also found order was a significant predictor of whether a federally protected insect or arachnid species was initially petitioned for listing, rather than proposed by the USFWS. In fact, all federally protected Hymenoptera (bees, ants, and wasps), Plecoptera, and Orthoptera (grasshoppers, crickets, katydids) species came from a public petition. Notably, these are orders which are overall underrepresented in federal and state protections. Public petitions are generally filed by nonprofit organizations (e.g., Xerces Society for Invertebrate Conservation). These organizations can specialize in understanding the conservation needs of diverse groups of vulnerable arthropods by working with extended networks of highly specialized academic and nonacademic researchers in a way that may not be feasible for a government agency tasked with managing the conservation needs of plants and animals (vertebrate and invertebrate alike) in aquatic and terrestrial systems throughout the country. Therefore, the petition process, by leveraging this wider pool of knowledge, may be an important means by which a wider diversity of taxa can gain protection.

We also identified some lesser instances of taxonomic bias not seen in Europe (25, 27). US protections were biased toward Diptera (flies), but this was due to a unique group of protected Hawaiian Drosophila species more than a bias toward the order itself (SI Appendix, Methods 1). Hemipterans (true bugs, such as leafhoppers and cicadas) were more likely to be state protected than plecopterans, trichopterans, and hymenopterans. The reason for this pattern is less clear; it may be a confounding effect of the relatively small number of hemipterans that have been assessed or, as has been suggested for state watchlists (24), caused by the uneven geographic distribution and involvement of taxonomic experts for some groups. Similarly, the state-level bias toward Odonata may be magnified by the geographic overlap between biodiversity hotspots of this order and states that protect insects (48). Overall, however, US insect and arachnid protections favor a few, often large and widely recognized insect taxa, meaning increased data collection will be insufficient for expanding protections to many important bioindicators, pollinators, predators, and other species without active efforts to protect a greater diversity of insects and arachnids.

Extractive Industries Limit State Protections While Eco-Centric Values Increase Listings.

As of 2024, about half the country (24 states and 66% of the United States by land area), had no state protected insect or arachnid species, meaning the federal ESA was the only legislative mechanism protecting imperiled insects and arachnids in those places. The absence of local protection is not due to an absence of local conservation need since ecological factors (e.g., number of range wide at-risk insects and arachnids and ecoregion diversity) were not significant predictors of policy. In fact, the areas of the country with the fewest protections (the south and west) are also the areas with the greatest diversity of insects (49) and arachnids (50), and, concerningly, the greatest number of at-risk insect pollinators (51). Rather, we found that the prevalence of extractive industries and human attitudes toward wildlife were the only significant predictors of state policy.

States where extractive industries were larger contributors to overall GDP were less likely to have a SESA and, if they did have one, tended to have a narrower definition of wildlife which did not include insects and arachnids. Extractive industries often oppose species protections under the federal ESA because protections can limit their access to land (31, 32). Industries may similarly oppose states enacting endangered species laws or including insects and arachnids as eligible if the state has a SESA, since this greatly expands the pool of potentially protectable species. Legislators in turn are likely reluctant to antagonize major contributors to their economy, and, as has been alleged with the ESA (32), industry lobbying may be involved. Conservationists can argue economic reasons for protecting invertebrates [e.g., insect ecosystem services are worth $57 billion annually in the United States (13)], although a purely economic argument may be insufficient justification for the protection of all vulnerable species. As such, enacting comprehensive legislation to protect insects and arachnids in the United States will likely require greater awareness, increased public pressure on companies, and oversight of the industry’s political influence.

Where state protections existed, we found that a higher prevalence of eco-centric values among residents corresponded with more protected insects and arachnids. Eco-centric values favor conservation even if it restricts human activity or economic growth (40), so where this viewpoint is more prevalent, agencies may face less opposition to conserving species, leading to more protected insects and arachnids overall. It should be noted that all states that protect at least one insect or arachnid had 1% or less of their GDP from extractive industries, which likely shapes economic and social dynamics within the state. Whereas eco-centric individuals see wildlife as deserving of rights similar to humans, people without eco-centric values often hold more utilitarian views toward wildlife and respond more favorably to managing wildlife for consumptive uses like hunting and fishing (40). This perspective is not, however, inherently at-odds with invertebrate conservation. For example, anglers benefit from aquatic insects that support fish populations (20), act as water quality bioindicators (20), and inspired modern fly-fishing (15). US bird conservation has succeeded in appealing to people who appreciate birds for both nonconsumptive and consumptive reasons (e.g., bird watchers and hunters) (43). In contrast, the correlation between eco-centric views and the number of protected insect and arachnid species suggests current invertebrate conservation messaging has either failed to resonate with diverse societal perspectives of wildlife management or has not adequately explained the utilitarian arguments for invertebrate conservation.

Conservation Implications: Urgent Need to Protect, Assess, and Value Insects and Arachnids.

The status of 88.5% of described North American insect and arachnid species is unknown, and unassessed insects and arachnids are unlikely to be legally protected in the United States. Life history studies and population monitoring are therefore urgently needed to assess conservation needs and understand ways in which taxa may be of economic, ecological, or scientific importance before they are lost. Although lack of assessment remains the first barrier to conserving most insects and arachnids, it is likely not a limiting factor for most aquatic insect orders, which are well assessed, although this has not translated to conservation action. Thus, data collection alone will be insufficient for rectifying all gaps in protections without a collective shift in what we consider worthy of conservation. As of 2024, 94.7% of insect and arachnid species known to be at-risk range wide in the United States had no legal protection, and protections were biased toward large and well-known insect orders. Federal and state agencies need to protect more insect and arachnid species and expand beyond charismatic taxa starting with vulnerable species of known ecological, economic, or scientific importance (e.g., aquatic insects used to monitor water quality), with the eventual goal of conserving biodiversity for its inherent value. Greater public involvement, potentially through a formal petition process, may be one means of diversifying protected invertebrate taxa at the state level. Current state laws are patchy and incomplete; at minimum every state should have a legal mechanism for protecting vulnerable insects and arachnids, and states should consider strengthening regional collaborations to conserve more regionally and nationally at-risk species. Finally, conservation messaging needs to appeal to more diverse viewpoints and align with local interests because broad community participation is needed to contribute to monitoring efforts and push for legislative change. Ultimately, effective US insect and arachnid conservation will require a coordinated effort between scientists, state and federal governments, nonprofit organizations, and local communities to protect, document, and value biodiversity in all forms.

Methods

Data Collection.

Described insect and arachnid species.

We compiled an up-to-date list of the number of described species for all insect and arachnid orders in North America north of Mexico as of 2024 (detailed in SI Appendix, Table S15), since this information was not available from a single primary literature source. We located at least two separate sources for each order (35 orders; for six orders we were unable to locate more than one source). Where discrepancies existed, we prioritized sources published since 2000 and counts from regularly updated online checklists maintained by museums, universities, or scientific societies; then primary literature; then books and field guides. Since counts exclusively for the United States are unavailable for most orders, we use North America north of Mexico as the geographic range for all orders (therefore, species endemic to Hawaii or US island territories are not included in described species counts since we were unable to obtain separate reliable described species counts for most orders in those regions). For the four orders of Acari (mites and ticks: Mesostigmata, Sarcoptiformes, Trombidiformes, and Ixodida) described species counts only include species from Canada because we were unable to find reliable recent counts for North America or the United States for these highly underdescribed taxa (52).

US protected insect and arachnid species.

We compiled a list of every insect and arachnid species receiving protection at the state and/or federal level in the United States. We first recorded all insects and arachnids protected under the US ESA) (1973) on the USFWS Environmental Conservation Online System (ECOS) (https://ecos.fws.gov/ecp/). On each species’ ECOS profile we also noted if they had been proposed for listing through the public petition process. We considered only petitions for the species to be listed as threatened or endangered, not petitions to designate critical habitat, change a species’ status once listed, or delist a species once listed. The ESA treats subspecies as separate entities from their parent species (i.e., parent species of a protected subspecies do not receive protection), so we similarly treat subspecies as distinct groups, but for brevity use “species” throughout to refer to each separate protected species/subspecies.

Next, we located all existing state endangered species lists by searching “endangered” on the website of the department responsible for maintaining a state’s endangered species list (see State Laws section; Dataset S1) or navigating to another source when indicated by law. We recorded all insects or arachnids assigned the state status “threatened” or “endangered” under a SESA. “Species of concern” and other such categories were not included since these classifications varied by state and generally did not confer legal protection.

NatureServe (NS).

The nonprofit NatureServe (NS) maintains a biodiversity database (https://explorer.natureserve.org/) with records of species in the United States and Canada, and assigns them a numeric extinction risk from 1 (“Critically imperiled”) to 5 (“Secure”) across the species’ range in the United States and Canada (called “global” scale by NS but referred to here as “range wide” for clarity), as well as at the state and national scales. Unless otherwise stated, we used NS range wide rank in all analyses because very few insects and arachnids had national or state level rankings. Species without a numeric extinction risk may be considered “unrankable” (U) due to poor or conflicting information or simply “not yet ranked” (NR); we refer to both collectively as “not assessed”. Nonnumeric ranks also exist for extinct (X) and extirpated or potentially extinct (H) species which we treat collectively as “extinct”. We chose NS over a database like the IUCN Red List to maximize policy relevance since NS had assessed more species of interest in this study and is more widely used by the US government (39). During analysis, we used two datasets: “NatureServe North America” and “NatureServe United States” (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Methods 2).

NS North America.

This dataset was used to examine the extent to which described insect and arachnid species in North America north of Mexico had been entered on NS and evaluated for extinction risk range wide. It included insect and arachnid species entries in Canada and the United States excluding Hawaii, since Hawaii falls outside the “North America north of Mexico” range of described species counts. This dataset also did not include any subspecies since subspecies were not included in described species counts.

NS United States.

This dataset was used when examining US state and federal species protections. It included all insect and arachnid species confirmed in the United States, including Hawaii because the federal ESA and Hawaii SESA both protect species occurring in that state. We included subspecies in this dataset because the ESA and most SESAs treat subspecies as separate entities from parent species. In addition to analyzing the effect of range wide extinction risk on insect and arachnid species protections, we also specifically looked at all insect and arachnid species considered at-risk or extirpated at the state level in each state (SI Appendix, Methods 3). From this same geographic range (United States including Hawaii), we also looked at all bird species and subspecies entries on NS so we could compare rates of federal protections for birds, insects, and arachnids (SI Appendix, Methods 4). We used birds because they have a long history of legal protection in the United States., are well assessed, and receive broad public support and funding (12, 29, 43, 47). Thus, they provide a gauge of what might be considered relatively strong US protections.

State Laws.

We examined ecological and socioeconomic explanations for differences in state legal protections for endangered insects and arachnids, focusing on SESAs. SESAs are legally binding and therefore less vulnerable to changes in funding and administrations (37, 45), compared to other important but voluntary programs to conserve species (e.g., State Wildlife Action Plans). We identified states that maintain lists of legally protected endangered species and citations for each law [Dataset S1; (38)]. In June 2024, we located the cited law on the most recent legislative session of each state legislature’s website. To ensure we found all relevant laws, on each website we also searched for the phrases “endangered” and “wildlife” and reviewed all titles and chapters with the phrases “Hunting and Fishing”, “Wildlife”, and “Conservation”. We recorded the definition of “wildlife” used in law(s) and/or regulation(s) concerning endangered species, as well as the entity responsible for maintaining the state’s endangered species list (Dataset S1). States were classified as “inclusive” if their definition of wildlife explicitly included insects and arachnids and/or they had at least one such state-protected species, “exclusive” if their definition of wildlife did not include these groups, or “ambiguous” if their definition of wildlife could be interpreted as including insects/arachnids (e.g., contained the phrase “all other animals”) but the groups were not mentioned explicitly, and no insect or arachnid species was state-protected (Dataset S1). Additional details in SI Appendix, Methods 5.

Statistical Analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software v4.3.2 (53), and all R packages used are cited in SI Appendix, Table S16.

Described Insect and Arachnid Species and NS Assessment.

For each order we calculated the percentage of described insect and arachnid species with entries on NS in North America north of Mexico and compared percentages across orders using a chi-squared test. We also tested whether taxonomic order predicted an insect or arachnid species’ likelihood of being assessed (not ranked U or NR) range wide, and for assessed species, the likelihood of being at-risk (ranked 1, 2, or 3) range wide. For both responses, we fit a binomial regression model where the predictor was taxonomic order and ran post hoc pairwise comparisons on order.

Taxonomic Bias.

We looked at potential order-level taxonomic biases for all insects and arachnids protected somewhere in the United States (and family level bias for 7 orders, see SI Appendix, Methods 6). We fit two binomial regression models: one assessing state-level protection, and the other federal protection. In each model, the explanatory variables were the taxonomic level of interest and the species’ extinction risk on NS. For these models we used species’ range wide extinction risk, except for the 0.2% of species which had been assessed nationally but not range wide (for those we used US national risk) to minimize the number of unassessed species (SI Appendix, Methods 7). We ran post hoc pairwise comparisons on all models. We also reran the state level model after excluding the disproportionate number of dragonflies and damselflies only protected by the state of Ohio (SI Appendix, Methods 8), and both models after accounting for a large group of flies (Diptera) in a single genus from Hawaii (SI Appendix, Methods 1). We also looked at order as a predictor of whether a federally protected insect or arachnid species was initially proposed for listing through the public petition process. We did not conduct this analysis for state protected insect and arachnid species since most states do not have a petition process (36), and no state provided information about how individual protected species were selected. For this analysis, we fit a binomial regression model using order as a predictor of being petitioned.

State Laws.

To analyze predictors of state conservation policy we created global models with all explanatory variables, then used model diagnostics and Akaike Information Criterion corrected (AICc) to identify the model of best fit. We report the results of the top performing model and present any models within 2 AICc of the top performing model (SI Appendix, Tables S12–14). The following explanatory variables were included in our global models (see SI Appendix, Table S11 for data sources and further rationales): number of range wide at-risk insects and arachnids occurring in the state, ecoregion richness, total state income (measured by Gross Domestic Product, GDP), number of R1 research institutions, percentage of federally owned land, percent of a GDP from extractive industries (mining, quarrying, and oil and gas extraction), and prevalence of eco-centric values in state residents. We used range wide at-risk insects and arachnids to reflect local conservation need because very few insects and arachnids had state-level assessments and the number of state-assessed insects and arachnids varied widely by state. Ecoregion richness was used to reflect differences in the number of ecosystems between states. We also included total state GDP and R1 research institutions to reflect differences in economic and academic resources states might have available for conservation, and we included percentage of federally owned land to reflect differences in the governing bodies responsible for managing state land. We chose to include extractive industry GDP given the documented conflict between these industries and federal species protections (31–33, 35). Extractive industry GDP data were not available for Rhode Island and Delaware, so these states were excluded from models that included extractive industry GDP. We also included the prevalence of eco-centric values to capture local value orientations toward wildlife. This eco-centric values metric comes from a 2018 survey of 43,949 individuals across all 50 states in which respondents were categorized based on their attitudes toward conservation issues (40). Eco-centric individuals saw wildlife as deserving rights similar to humans and supported conserving wildlife for nonutilitarian purposes (40).

We first used a binomial regression to test predictors of whether a state kept a list of legally protected endangered species. We removed total GDP due to collinearity (VIF > 2) with range wide at-risk insects and arachnids and removed R1 research institutions for collinearity with ecoregion richness. Range wide at-risk species and eco-centric values were colinear, but we were particularly interested in both ecological vulnerability and social values as drivers of policy, so we created two models, one with at-risk species and the remaining predictors, and one with eco-centric values and the remaining predictors. We then compared the AICc of reduced versions of both models against each other.

Next, using a binomial regression, we looked at predictors of whether states with SESAs included insects and arachnids. Here, “ambiguous” states were grouped with states that explicitly exclude insects and arachnids since no state in either group protects any of these species. We removed total GDP due to collinearity with range wide at-risk insects and arachnids and log-transformed the percent of federal land to meet model assumptions.

Finally, for states with at least one insect or arachnid listed, we tested whether our predictors explained variation in the total number of protected insect and arachnid species. We used a negative binomial generalized linear model, an approach appropriate for overdispersed count data. Ecoregion richness and eco-centric values were collinear, but we were interested in the influence of both ecology and attitudes on protections, so we created two separate models. One contained ecoregion richness and the remaining explanatory variables. The second contained eco-centric values and all remaining explanatory variables except R1 institutions and extractive industry GDP, both removed due to collinearity with eco-centric values. We then compared all reduced versions of each model against each other using AICc.
