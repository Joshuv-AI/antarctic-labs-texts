Spatial distribution: Effect of oxygen availability on gut microbiota adhesion and colonization
Authors: Ruixue Ding, Xiaotong Sun, Yinan Wang, Binbin Tu, Yue Liang, Qi Lin, Fuqiang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in host digestion and immune regulation; however, most existing research relies on fecal sampling, leaving uncertainty regarding whether fecal microbiota can accurately reflect the symbiotic microbial components at distinct gut sites. To address this gap, this study employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to systematically analyze the microbial composition of three spatial compartments in the rat colon: luminal contents, mucus layer, and epithelial tissue. The results revealed notable differences in microbial communities across the three compartments. The luminal contents, which were closely associated with the fecal microbiota, exhibited high microbial abundance, encompassing gram-positive bacteria, gram-negative bacteria, aerobic bacteria, and obligate anaerobes. By contrast, the mucus layer and epithelial tissue were predominantly enriched in gram-negative bacteria and obligate anaerobes, with a marked scarcity of aerobic bacteria. Further analysis indicated that the ACE and Chao1 indices were significantly higher in mucus samples than in luminal samples, whereas tissue samples showed reduced richness but relatively stable diversity. Additionally, Proteobacteria were rarely detected in the lumen but were significantly enriched in the mucus and tissue. These findings suggest that the oxygen content in the intestinal lumen is significantly higher than that of the host-derived mucus layer and epithelial tissue and that this oxygen gradient drives the spatial partitioning of the gut microbiota. Collectively, this study clarifies the spatial distribution characteristics of the gut microbiota and highlights oxygen availability as a key factor in regulating microbial adhesion and colonization.

1. Introduction

The gut microbiota constitutes a complex and dynamic ecosystem that is pivotal to host physiology through its involvement in nutrient digestion, metabolic regulation, immune homeostasis, and defense against intestinal pathogens [1–4]. Commensal microbial communities are not merely passive inhabitants of the gastrointestinal tract; they actively modulate host cellular functions and systemic health, rendering them a central focus in modern microbiology and biomedicine [5,6]. Despite substantial advances in gut microbiome research, the vast majority of studies rely on fecal sampling, and the extent to which the symbiotic microbial components of distinct gut niches are accurately reflected by fecal microbiota remains poorly defined [4,7]. This disconnect is particularly problematic given that spatially distinct compartments along the longitudinal and transverse axes of the gut are inhabited by intestinal bacteria, with each compartment characterized by unique micro-environmental conditions [8,9].

The intestinal mucus layer, a gel-like structure composed primarily of mucin, functions as a critical physical barrier that separates the luminal contents from the epithelial surface [10,11]. This barrier is not homogeneous; the outer mucus layer is relatively porous and colonized by mucus-degrading bacteria, whereas the inner layer is denser and has traditionally been considered sterile in healthy individuals, although recent evidence suggests that this layer can be penetrated by select bacteria that interact with epithelial cells and crypts [12,13]. Moreover, the small intestinal mucus layer is thin, nonadherent, and permeable to bacterial particles, whereas the colonic mucus layer forms a thicker, stratified structure [14,15]. These spatial differences in mucus architecture, coupled with variations in nutrient availability, pH, and immune signaling, contribute to the formation of niche-specific microbial communities characterized by distinct functional profiles [16,17]. However, existing studies on gut microbial biogeography have predominantly focused on Western populations, with limited data from Asia, Africa, and South America [18,19], leading to an incomplete understanding of global microbial spatial dynamics.

Oxygen availability is a key yet underexplored environmental factor that influences microbial distribution. Low, but detectable, oxygen levels are present in the intestinal lumen, whereas the mucus layer and epithelial tissue are characterized by near-anaerobic conditions [17,20]. A correlation between intraluminal oxygen gradients and the radial partitioning of the gut microbiota in humans and mice has been suggested in previous studies [17], and Proteobacteria have been linked to mucosal niches [16,21]. However, systematic comparisons of microbial communities across the three core colonic compartments are lacking, and the role of oxygen in driving microbial adhesion and colonization remains to be validated. Additionally, although mucus barrier function is regulated by specific microbes (including Akkermansia muciniphila) [9,22–24], the mechanisms through which barrier integrity and host–microbe crosstalk are influenced by spatial microbial heterogeneity remain unclear.

In this study, we analyzed the spatial distribution of gut bacteria in the luminal intestinal contents, outer mucus layer, and colonic tissue. These spatial differences reflect distinct bacterial genomic repertoires and colonization strategies, providing a more systematic basis for investigating microbial distribution patterns and host–microbe interactions in the intestine.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Culture and grouping of rats

SPF male Wistar rats were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. After 8 weeks of feeding, the body mass of the rats reached 200–300 g. Following CO2 anesthesia, the rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The rats were transferred to an anaerobic, aseptic, constant-temperature super clean workbench. A 75% ethanol solution was used to wipe the abdomen of the rats, and sterilized surgical scissors were used to open the abdominal cavity, after which the intestinal tract was quickly and completely separated. All experimental animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee for the Experimental Use of Animals at Shenyang Agricultural University (License No. SYXK (Liao) 2022-0001).

2.2. Spatial distribution of the colon lumen, mucus, and tissue

First, dissection was initiated at the cecal junction, and a 1-cm segment of the proximal colon was sampled. The segment was opened longitudinally, and the contents were gently removed with forceps. Approximately 100 mg of luminal contents were collected as the “lumen” samples. The remaining cavity contents were removed, and the remaining tissue was placed in 100 mL of PBS and washed by vigorous shaking with clamps for 20 s. Subsequently, excess buffer was removed, and the mucus was scraped from the tissue surface using a sterile cell scraper (BD Falcon). The obtained samples were labeled as “mucus.” The remaining tissues were collected as “tissue” samples. The sampling process is illustrated in Fig. 1a. All intestinal samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

2.3. 16S rRNA gene sequencing

The rat colon samples (three groups of five rats each) were processed immediately. DNA extraction from mucosal, tissue, and luminal samples was performed according to the method described by Ding et al. [19]. Briefly, the V3–V4 variable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified, and library sequencing was performed using the Illumina MiSeq platform with a 300-bp paired-end sequencing kit.

2.4. 16S rRNA gene sequence bioinformatic processing

Sequencing of the library was done using the Illumina Miseq platform, the resulting files were demultiplexed using QIIME 1.9.0. Oerational taxonomic units (OTUs) (97% identity) were performed using USEARCH software. Alpha and beta diversities were generated in QIIME (v 1.9.0) and distinguished at the OTU level [25].

2.5. Statistical analysis

The relative abundances of genera and phyla in all samples were used to determine the microbial composition, abundance, and diversity of the intestinal samples [26]. The core bacterial communities in the colonic microbiome were determined using the Shanghai Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com). Linear discriminant analysis combined with LEfSe analysis was used to identify group differences and specific bacteria at the taxonomic level [27].

3. Results and discussion

3.1. Spatial distribution and composition analysis of commensal bacteria during colon colonization

To explore the spatial structure of intestinal microbial distribution in the colon, a schematic of vertical dissection of the proximal (ascending) colon was constructed (Fig. 1). Quality control and bacterial diversity estimation for each group in high-throughput sequencing are presented in Table 1. Significantly higher ACE and Chao1 indices were observed in mucus samples compared to lumen samples, which further confirms that the mucus layer serves as a favorable microenvironment for microbial adhesion and colonization. This finding is consistent with the study by Jakobsson et al. [7], who reported that the composition of the gut microbiota shapes the colon mucus barrier, and the mucus layer in turn provides a physical scaffold and nutrient source for microbial colonization. Notably, the Shannon diversity index was most prominent in mucus samples, showing an exponential increase that warrants further investigation. One plausible explanation is that the mucus layer, as an intermediate compartment between the lumen and tissue, integrates both luminal-derived nutrients and host-derived factors, creating a heterogeneous microenvironment that supports the coexistence of diverse microbial taxa [12,13]. In contrast, tissue samples exhibited a significant decrease in species richness but a steady increase in community diversity, which may be attributed to the stringent selection pressure imposed by the epithelial barrier and near-anaerobic conditions, allowing only highly adapted and specialized microbes to colonize [5,8].

Principal component analysis (PCA) and beta diversity NMDS analysis were performed on Wistar rat intestinal microbiota samples at the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) level to contextualize the results within existing human [28] and rat [29] microbiota studies (Fig. 2). The two-dimensional PCA model (F1 and F2) explained 66.59% of the total variance, with F1 and F2 accounting for 44.41% and 22.18%, respectively. Samples were clustered into three distinct groups (lumen, mucus, and tissue), with five biological replicates in each group showing high consistency. This reproducibility aligns with the findings of Mark Welch et al. [30], who demonstrated consistent spatial organization of the gut microbiota in gnotobiotic mice, supporting the reliability of our compartment-specific sampling strategy. In the NMDS analysis, a small number of S1 tissue and S3 mucus samples overlapped, which may be associated with the fluidity of the mucus layer and dynamic microbial colonization [7]. As reported by Yasuda et al. [16], the small intestinal mucus layer is non-adherent and permeable to bacterial particles, and similar permeability may exist in the colonic mucus layer, allowing occasional microbial migration between the mucus and tissue compartments. Additionally, individual differences in microbial communities cannot be excluded, which may contribute to the partial overlap observed [28].

3.2. Analysis of microbial community distribution and adhesive colonization of the colonic gut microbiota

At the phylum level, the three compartments shared the same dominant phyla but differed in their relative abundances (Fig. 3a). Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria were the dominant phyla, with Firmicutes accounting for over 50%, a finding consistent with those of previous studies on intestinal microbiota in humans and rhesus monkeys [6,31]. The relative abundance of Firmicutes was lower in mucus and tissue samples than in lumen samples, likely because of the direct connection between the luminal contents and cecum, which maintains a microbial composition similar to that of feces [4,7]. By contrast, mucus and tissue samples were enriched in gram-negative bacteria with lipopoly-saccharide-containing outer membranes, such as Proteobacteria [20]. This enrichment is consistent with the findings of Shin et al. [20], who identified Proteobacteria as a microbial signature of gut dysbiosis. However, our study further specifies their spatial localization in mucosal and tissue compartments, highlighting their potential role in host–microbe crosstalk at the epithelial interface.

At the genus level, the species diversity of the lumen, mucus, and tissue samples was similar, but the richness of bacterial genera gradually decreased with the progression of adhesion and colonization from the lumen to the mucus and then to the tissue (Fig. 3b). Among the 23 identified genera, Romboutsia, Muribaculaceae, Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium accounted for the largest proportions of the colonic microbiota. Mucus samples were dominated by gram-negative bacteria (18/23) and facultative anaerobes (13/23), whereas lumen samples were predominantly gram-positive bacteria. Lactobacillus, Muribaculaceae, and Akkermansia were particularly abundant in mucus samples. These genera utilize sugars to produce acidic metabolites, reduce intestinal pH, and improve the microbial microenvironment [32,33], and their enrichment in the mucus layer may be mediated by their ability to metabolize mucin glycans and tolerate low oxygen levels [9,23]. For example, Akkermansia muciniphila, a key mucin-degrading bacterium, contributes to gut homeostasis by regulating mucus barrier function and producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [23,24,34,35], and its high abundance in mucus samples further supports the role of the mucus layer as a niche for mucin-degrading microbes.

Ternary plot analysis based on OTU data (Fig. 4) revealed that Romboutsia ilealis, A. muciniphila, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, and Lactobacillus johnsonii were the most abundant species in all three compartments. Firmicutes accounted for over 47% of the total abundance, followed by Verrucomicrobiota, which was mainly composed of o__Verrucomicrobiales and s__Akkermansia muciniphila. Notably, Verrucomicrobiota reached 23% in the lumen but significantly decreased to 14.1% and 10.4% in mucus and tissue samples, respectively. This distribution pattern may be explained by the fact that A. muciniphila degrades mucin in the mucus layer, and its growth may be limited because of near-anaerobic conditions and reduced nutrient availability in deeper tissue compartments [35,36]. Additionally, the fermentation products of A. muciniphila can serve as energy sources for other gut microbes [37,38], potentially supporting the growth of obligate anaerobes in the mucus and tissue layers, which may explain the inverse relation between the abundance of Verrucomicrobiota and enrichment of strict anaerobes in these compartments.

Most strikingly, Proteobacteria were extremely rare in the lumen (1.09%) but were significantly enriched in mucus (5.59%) and tissue (2.54%) samples. The enrichment of Proteobacteria in mucosal and tissue compartments may be driven by their ability to utilize oxygen more efficiently than obligate anaerobes in the low-oxygen microenvironment of the mucus layer [17,21]. As reported by Albenberg et al. [17], intraluminal oxygen gra2dients correlate with the radial partitioning of intestinal microbiota, with facultative anaerobes (including Proteobacteria) occupying the mucosal niche, where oxygen levels are slightly higher than those in tissue but lower than those in the lumen. Moreover, the outer membrane of Proteobacteria may facilitate their adhesion to epithelial cells and evasion of host immune surveillance [20,40], further promoting their colonization of mucus and tissue compartments. The co-occurrence network diagram (Fig. S1) showed that species with an abundance of more than 300 exhibited significant co-occurrence relationships across the three compartments, suggesting potential synergistic or competitive interactions among microbial taxa. However, the nonrandom assembly of intestinal microbiota in these compartments remains unclear and requires further investigation [41].

3.3. Differential analysis of the spatial distribution of core functional microbes in the colon

To elucidate the functional roles of the microbiota at distinct biogeographic sites, the PICRUSt10 program was used to generate microbial COG profiles and correlate functional features with key enzymes in the gut samples (Fig. S2). LEfSe analysis (linear discriminant analysis score ≥2) identified significantly differential functions between sites, including amino acid transport and metabolism, translation, transcription, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, energy production and conversion, and signal transduction mechanisms (Fig. 5). These functional characteristics indicate vigorous metabolic activity in the microbiota of all three compartments, reflecting their specialized roles in nutrient utilization, host signaling, and immune modulation [5,6].

At the taxonomic level, LEfSe analysis revealed distinct and differentially enriched bacteria across the three compartments (Fig. 5). The lumen was enriched with diverse taxa, including Coriobacteria (class to order, family Eggerthellaceae), Bacteroidales (family to genus), Burkholderia (family to genus), and Veillonellales (order to genus). These taxa include gram-positive, gram-negative, aerobic, and obligate anaerobic bacteria, consistent with the higher oxygen levels in the lumen that support the growth of a broader range of microbial species [7,17]. By contrast, the mucus samples were enriched in only two taxa: Peptococcales (order to genus) and Morganellaceae (family to genus). Peptococcales belong to the family Peptococcaceae of the order Clostridiales and are strict gram-positive anaerobes [42,43]. Their enrichment in the mucus layer may be attributed to their ability to ferment SCFAs and other metabolites produced by mucin-degrading bacteria (Akkermansia), as well as their tolerance to low oxygen levels [44]. Morganellaceae, a family of gram-negative facultative anaerobes, may benefit from intermediate oxygen concentrations in the mucus layer and their ability to adhere to mucins [40]. Tissue samples were enriched in four taxa: the Clostridia-vadinBB60-group (order to genus), subgroup-2 (order to genus), Burkholderiales (order, family Alcaligenaceae, genus Achromobacter), and Lachnospirales (order to genus, family Defluviitaleaceae to genus). These taxa are predominantly gram-negative and strictly anaerobic, consistent with near-anaerobic conditions in the tissue compartment [5,17]. Clostridia- vadinBB60-group and Lachnospirales are known to produce SCFAs (including butyrate), which play crucial roles in regulating intestinal epithelial cell proliferation, maintaining barrier integrity, and modulating host immune responses [44–46]. Burkholderiales (including Achromobacter) are gram-negative obligate anaerobes that interact with epithelial cells to regulate cytokine production and immune homeostasis [40]. The enrichment of these strict anaerobes in tissue samples highlights the strong selection pressure imposed by oxygen availability and host epithelial barriers, which restrict the colonization of highly adapted taxa [5,8].

The differential enrichment of bacterial taxa across the three compartments strongly supports the role of oxygen availability as a key driver of spatial microbial partitioning. The lumen, which has high oxygen levels (derived from luminal contents and epithelial respiration [17]), supports the growth of diverse microbes. By contrast, the mucus layer and tissue, characterized by near-anaerobic conditions [17,20], select gram-negative bacteria and obligate anaerobes that lack oxygen-dependent metabolic pathways and can tolerate oxidative stress. This observation is consistent with those of previous studies in humans and mice [17,21], which reported a correlation between oxygen gradients and radial microbial partitioning. For example, Stearns et al. [21] found that facultative anaerobic Proteobacteria were more abundant in mucosal niches, whereas obligate anaerobes dominated deeper tissue compartments. Our study extends these findings by systematically comparing three distinct compartments (lumen, mucus, and tissue) and identifying specific taxa associated with each niche, providing a more detailed mechanistic understanding of oxygen-driven microbial colonization.

Furthermore, the spatial distribution of microbiota has important implications for host health and disease. The enrichment of Proteobacteria in mucus and tissue samples is noteworthy because this phylum is often associated with gut dysbiosis and inflammatory bowel disease [20,47]. Darfeuille- Michaud et al. [48] reported a high prevalence of adherent-invasive Escherichia coli (a member of the Proteobacteria phylum) in the ileal mucosa of patients with Crohn’s disease, suggesting that mucosal colonization by specific Proteobacterial taxa may contribute to intestinal inflammation. By contrast, the enrichment of SCFA-producing strict anaerobes (including Clostridia and Lachnospirales) in tissue samples may have protective effects, as SCFAs provide energy for epithelial cells and suppress proinflammatory cytokine production [44–46]. These findings highlight the importance of spatial microbial distribution in maintaining gut homeostasis and suggest that disruptions in oxygen gradients may alter microbial colonization patterns and contribute to disease pathogenesis [17,31].

4. Conclusions

This study identified oxygen availability as a key driver of the spatial partitioning of gut microbiota across the colon lumen, mucus layer, and epithelial tissue, advancing our understanding of intestinal microbial niche adaptation. Oxygen gradients select for distinct communities, with diverse taxa in the oxygen-rich lumen and gram-negative obligate anaerobes in near-anaerobic mucus and tissue. This indicates that stool samples fail to capture mucosal- and tissue-associated microbial signatures that directly modulate intestinal barrier integrity and metabolic homeostasis. The enrichment of dysbiosis- linked Proteobacteria in mucosal and tissue compartments highlights the relevance of spatial microbial misalignment in gut disease pathogenesis, underscoring the necessity of integrating spatial context into dysregulation studies. Future studies should validate these observations in human cohorts, incorporate direct oxygen measurements, and apply multiomics approaches to elucidate the functional mechanisms of oxygen-driven microbial adaptation. Collectively, this work emphasizes that a spatial perspective is indispensable for the accurate characterization of host–microbe crosstalk, laying a foundation for targeted microbiome-based strategies in disease prevention and management.
