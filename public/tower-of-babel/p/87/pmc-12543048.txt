COL6A1, LAPTM5, and ZFAND2A as Crucial Biomolecules Driving Immunoregulation in Human Nucleus Pulposus Degeneration
Authors: Ze‐Yu Lu, Peng‐Bo Chen, Qing‐Yin Xu, Muradil Mardan, Meng‐Jia You, Bo Li, Sheng‐Dan Jiang, Lei‐Sheng Jiang, Xin‐Feng Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a major contributor to chronic low back pain, driven by complex interactions among immune cells and various molecular mechanisms. In this study, we integrated single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐Seq), transcriptomics, and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the immune mechanisms and identify key biomolecules involved in IVDD. Single‐cell analysis of nucleus pulposus (NP) samples from four patients with disc degeneration revealed 20 distinct clusters, which were categorized into four primary cell types: blood cells, chondrocytes, endothelial cells, and stromal cells. These cell types were shown to play crucial roles in the immune microenvironment, as evidenced by ligand‐receptor interaction analysis, which delineated potential intercellular communication networks. By combining eQTL (expression Quantitative Trait Loci) data with FinnGen datasets in MR analysis, we identified five key genes (CD83, COL6A1, LAPTM5, NPC2, ZFAND2A) significantly associated with IVDD risk. The expression of these genes in NP tissues showed strong correlations with immune cell infiltration. Protein‐level validation confirmed the upregulation of COL6A1, LAPTM5, and ZFAND2A in degenerative NP tissues. Functional enrichment analysis revealed their involvement in multiple immune‐related pathways. Additionally, co‐expression analysis identified relationships between these key genes and critical immune genes, such as CD14 and CD274. This study leverages multi‐omics integration to uncover the immune regulatory mechanisms underlying IVDD, providing valuable insights into the role of biomolecules in the pathogenesis of disc degeneration and offering potential for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) significantly compromises physical health and quality of life while imposing a substantial societal and economic burden [1]. As a multifactorial condition, the etiology of LBP encompasses biological, psychological, and social factors [1, 2]. Among its various causes, intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is widely recognized as a critical contributor [3, 4].

The intervertebral disc (IVD) consists of three main components: the nucleus pulposus at the center, the surrounding annulus fibrosus, and two cartilaginous endplates connecting adjacent vertebral bodies [5]. While IVDD was traditionally attributed to a combination of epigenetic, metabolic, and environmental factors, growing evidence highlights genetic predisposition as a primary determinant [6, 7, 8]. Research indicates that the heritability of progressive disc degeneration over a 5 year period is estimated to range from 47% to 66%, with severe IVDD heritability reaching 64% in the lumbar spine and 79% in the cervical spine [9, 10]. Aging, injury, and chronic overuse of the spine induce a cascade of molecular, cellular, and structural changes in IVD tissues. These alterations result in the gradual loss of normal spinal structure and function, culminating in pain and disability [11].

The nucleus pulposus (NP), a critical component of the IVD, comprises 70%–90% water content, 35%–65% proteoglycans, and 5%–20% type II collagen by dry weight [12]. During maturation, large vacuolated notochordal cells within the NP transform to smaller fibrochondrocyte‐like cells [13, 14]. NP cells (NPCs) are pivotal to the pathogenesis of IVDD, orchestrating key cellular and molecular processes [15], and recent advances have revealed the immune system's central role in these processes. Degenerated NPCs are predominantly classified into inflammatory, endoplasmic reticulum stress‐related, and fibrocartilaginous subtypes. Additionally, interactions between macrophages and progenitor NPCs, as well as macrophage polarization into M1 and M2 subtypes, significantly influence cellular metabolism [16]. The accumulation of macrophages and T cells further drives stromal cells toward a calcified phenotype [17]. Therefore, understanding the immune interactions within the NP and identifying specific molecular markers and signaling pathways associated with degeneration are critical for elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying IVDD.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a method based on Mendel's laws of inheritance that utilizes single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to investigate causal relationships between exposures and clinical outcomes. By leveraging the random allocation of genetic variants, MR mitigates confounding and reverse causation, offering robust causal inferences [18]. In this study, SNPs associated with gene expression changes (expression quantitative trait loci, eQTL) serve as proxies for long‐term exposures [19, 20]. The FinnGen database provides associations between these genetic variants and IVDD outcomes. By integrating SNP‐gene expression and SNP‐disease association data, MR enables the identification of causal links between exposures and outcomes [21].

This study aims to integrate scRNA‐Seq, transcriptomic, and MR analyses, with experimental validation, to investigate the immune regulatory mechanisms and pathways involved in IVDD. In doing so, the study seeks to provide novel insights into the molecular and cellular processes underlying IVDD, potentially paving the way for the development of more effective diagnostic tools and therapeutic strategies.

Single‐Cell Analysis

Single‐cell RNA data were analyzed with the Seurat package, applying the following filtering criteria: nFeature_RNA > 200, nFeature_RNA < 5000, and percent.mt < 20%. The data were then normalized, scaled, and processed through principal component analysis (PCA), followed by Harmony integration. The ideal number of principal components (PCs) was selected using the ElbowPlot method. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used to visualize the spatial distribution of cell clusters. Clusters were annotated based on biological relevance, and cell types associated with disease processes were identified. Marker genes for each cluster were extracted using the FindAllMarkers function from the single‐cell dataset. Genes with p_val_adj < 0.05 and |avg_log2FC| > 0.585 were chosen as signature markers for each subtype.

Ligand‐Receptor Interaction Analysis

CellPhoneDB (database version: 4.0, PMID: 35794482) is an open‐access database of receptor‐ligand interactions [17]. It includes ligands and receptors, each with subunit structures representing heteromeric complexes. Integrated with resources like UniProt, Ensembl, PDB, and IUPHAR, CellPhoneDB contains 1885 protein interactions. This integration enables an in‐depth exploration of intercellular communication, providing insights into the interaction networks among various cell types. Using the statistical analysis tool of CellPhoneDB, we assessed the significance of ligand‐receptor interactions in the context of single‐cell expression profiles. We randomized cell cluster labels 1000 times to compute the mean receptor expression within each group and the mean ligand expression in its interacting counterpart. This process produced a null (Bernoulli or two‐point) distribution for every receptor‐ligand pair across comparisons between the two cell types. Subsequently, we highlighted selected ligand‐receptor pairs to illustrate key interaction relationships.

Gene Function Enrichment Analysis

To thoroughly investigate the functional associations within the gene sets, functional annotation was carried out using the Metascape database (www.metascape.org). Specific genes underwent Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses. Criteria for statistical significance were set at a minimum overlap ≥ 3 and p ≤ 0.01.

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

The FinnGen database offers extensive summary statistics from numerous GWAS studies. Based on the outcome IDs obtained from the FinnGen database, corresponding GWAS summary statistics (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) were retrieved and combined with eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium to perform causal inference analyses. SNPs meeting the genome‐wide significance threshold for each gene (p < 1e‐8) across all loci were selected as potential instrumental variables (IVs), and linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNPs was assessed. Among SNPs with R2 < 0.001 (clumping window size = 10,000 kb), only those with p’ < 5e‐5 were retained. Four statistical methods were applied to evaluate causal relationships: Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW), MR Egger, Weighted Median, and Weighted Mode. If only one SNP was associated with a causal relationship, the Wald ratio method was used. This analysis provided overall estimates of how cis‐ and some trans‐gene expression in peripheral blood influences disc degeneration. Heterogeneity (Cochran's IVW Q test) and gene diversity tests were employed to validate and further examine the causal relationships.

Clinical Tissue Samples

The study protocol involving human specimens was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No. XHEC‐NSFC‐2021‐159). NP tissues were collected from elderly patients (two males and three females, aged 70–80 years, mean age 73.6 years) with degenerative lumbar disc disease (Pfirrmann grade ≥ IV) during surgery and classified as degenerative tissues [24]. Similarly, NP tissues from younger patients (three males and two females, aged 20–40 years, mean age 30.0 years) undergoing surgery (Pfirrmann grade ≤ III) were designated as relatively healthy tissues. NP tissue samples were dissected using a scalpel and allocated for cell culture, Western blotting, and immunohistochemical staining experiments.

Protein Extraction and Western Blotting

Fresh NP tissue was finely minced into small fragments. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime), containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X‐100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, sodium orthovanadate, sodium fluoride, EDTA, and leupeptin, supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1045, Beyotime). The components were thoroughly mixed immediately before use. Lysis buffer was added at a ratio of 200 μL per 20 mg of tissue. After complete lysis, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. SDS‐PAGE Protein Loading Buffer (20315ES, Yeasen) was added to the protein samples, followed by thorough mixing and boiling at 100°C for 5 min.

Protein samples underwent SDS‐PAGE separation and were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membranes were incubated for 15 min with a rapid blocking buffer (PS108P, EpiZyme), followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with specific primary antibodies [25]. The primary antibodies included CD83 (AF5233, Affinity), COL6A1 (17023–1‐AP, Proteintech), LAPTM5 (A17995, ABclonal), NPC2 (19888–1‐AP, Proteintech), ZFAND2A (43205, SAB), and GAPDH (10494–1‐AP, Proteintech). After three 5 min washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using the Ultrahypersensitive ECL chemiluminescence kit (P0018AS, Beyotime).

Immunohistochemical Staining

NP tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5‐μm‐thick sections. For immunohistochemical staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, such as CD83 (AF5233, Affinity), COL6A1 (17023–1‐AP, Proteintech), LAPTM5 (DF15005, Affinity), NPC2 (19888–1‐AP, Proteintech), and ZFAND2A (43205, SAB). Immunoreactivity was visualized using the horseradish peroxidase‐streptavidin detection system (Dako, Glostrup, Sweden). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Immune Cell Infiltration Analysis

The single‐sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) method is commonly employed to assess immune cell populations within the microenvironment. In this study, ssGSEA was used to analyze expression profiles and estimate the relative abundance of the 29 immune cell types, including B cells, NK cells, and T cells.

Gene Set Variation Analysis

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) is an unsupervised, non‐parametric approach designed to assess gene set enrichment in transcriptomic data. It generates overall scores for predefined gene sets, converting gene‐level expression variations into pathway‐level differences, thus providing insights into the biological functions of the samples. In this study, gene sets were sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (MsigDB) (v7.0), and the GSVA algorithm was used to calculate scores for each gene set, allowing the assessment of potential biological function changes across different samples.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Patients were categorized into high and low expression groups based on gene expression levels, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify pathway variations between them. Background gene sets, annotated with version 7.0 from the MsigDB database, were used to define subtype‐specific pathways for differential expression analysis. Pathway variations among subtypes were assessed, and gene sets with significant enrichment (adjusted p < 0.05) were ranked based on their consistency scores. GSEA is widely employed to explore the relationship between disease classification and biological relevance.

Human Nucleus Pulposus Cell Isolation and Culture

NP tissues were collected and immediately placed in PBS for transfer to a sterile bench. The tissues were washed three times with sterile PBS and then digested with type II collagenase (0.2%, Sigma‐Aldrich) at 37°C for 3–4 h. The digestion solution was supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) and 0.1% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). After enzymatic digestion, the tissue fragments were suspended in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Within approximately 5 days of incubation at 37°C, NP cells migrated out of the tissue pieces and were designated as passage 0 (P0). When P0 cells reached 80%–90% confluence, they were digested with Trypsin–EDTA for 3 min at 37°C, resuspended, and subcultured at appropriate densities. Cells within low passages (≤ 4) were used for subsequent experiments. Morphological characteristics of NPCs remained stable between P1 and P4.

Plasmid Transfection

For plasmid transfection, constructs—pcDNA3.1, pcDNA3.1‐COL6A1, pcDNA3.1‐LAPTM5, or pcDNA3.1‐ZFAND2A (GenePharma, Shanghai, China)—were mixed with serum‐free medium and P3000. This mixture was then combined with Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) prepared in an equal volume of serum‐free medium, incubated, and subsequently added to the cells. Cells were cultured for 72 h before further experiments.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

RNA extraction from cultured cells was performed using TRIzol reagent (R0016, Beyotime). Briefly, cells were washed twice with 1 × PBS in 6‐well plates. Then, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent was added to each well and incubated for 5 min at room temperature to lyse the cells. Subsequently, 200 μL of chloroform was added, the mixture was vortexed thoroughly, and incubated on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, the aqueous phase was carefully transferred to a new tube and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol. The mixture was inverted several times to ensure thorough mixing, and then incubated for 10 min. RNA was precipitated by centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol by centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 5 min each. RNA was then air‐dried and resuspended in DEPC‐treated water, and RNA concentration was measured. cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A, Takara). Quantitative PCR was conducted with the SYBR Green Kit Master Mix (11202ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The ABI 7500 Sequencing Detection System was used to analyze amplification products, with gene expression levels normalized against glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) [26]. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Regulatory Network Analysis of Key Genes

In this study, transcription factors were predicted using the R package RcisTarget, which performs motif‐based analysis. The normalized enrichment score (NES) of each motif reflects its enrichment within the gene set. Additional annotations were inferred based on motif similarity and gene sequences, supplementing the original database annotations. The first step in estimating motif overexpression within a gene set is to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) for each motif based on the gene set's recovery curve. The NES for each motif was then computed as derived from the AUC values across all motifs in the set. Gene–motif rankings were performed using the RcisTarget.hg19.motifDBs.cisbpOnly.500 bp file.

Statistical Analysis

Reliable MR analysis relies on three primary assumptions: (1) Relevance Assumption (the instrumental variable (IV) is strongly associated with the exposure but not directly with the outcome); (2) Independence Assumption (the IV is not related to any confounders); and (3) Exclusivity Assumption (the IV affects the outcome only through the exposure, with pleiotropy considered if it influences the outcome via other pathways) [27, 28]. All bioinformatics analyses were conducted with R software (version 4.3.0).

Experimental validations were conducted with at least three independent replicates. Two‐tailed statistical tests were applied, and a p value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical significance is denoted as ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, and *p < 0.05.

Results

Processing and Analysis of NP‐Related Single‐Cell Data

In this study, we analyzed scRNA‐Seq data from NP samples obtained from four patients with disc degeneration. To ensure data quality, cells were filtered using the criteria: nFeature_RNA > 200, nFeature_RNA < 5000, and percent.mt < 20%. After filtering, 15 505 cells were retained for subsequent analysis (Figure S1A,B). The gene expression profiles of these cells were visualized, and the 10 genes with the highest normalized variance were identified (Figure S1C). Principal component analysis (PCA) identified a notable batch effect among the samples (Figure S1D). To address this, we applied Harmony analysis for batch effect correction (Figure S1E). The ElbowPlot method determined the optimal number of principal components (PCs) as 30 (Figure S1F). Using the UMAP dimensionality reduction technique, the cells were clustered into 20 distinct groups (Figure 1A).

(B) Cell type annotation of clusters into blood cells, chondrocytes, endothelial cells, and stromal cells based on marker genes. (C) Predicted ligand‐receptor interactions between cell types, as determined by CellPhoneDB. (D) Stromal cells exhibited the most abundant potential interactions with other cell types. (E) Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of stromal cell marker genes via Metascape.

To further investigate the biological relevance of these clusters, we annotated each cluster based on known marker genes (Figure S1G). As a result, the 20 clusters were classified into four cell types: blood cells, chondrocytes, endothelial cells, and stromal cells (Figure 1B). We then employed CellPhoneDB to analyze the ligand‐receptor interactions among these cell types, revealing numerous potential interactions between stromal cells, chondrocytes, and other cell types (Figure 1C). Analysis of ligand‐receptor gene pairs revealed that stromal cells had the highest potential for interaction with other cell types (Figure 1D). Based on these findings, we selected marker genes from stromal cells as candidate genes for subsequent analysis. Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of the stromal cell marker genes was conducted. Metascape database analysis showed that these marker genes were primarily involved in key pathways, such as the extracellular matrix, secretory granule lumen, positive regulation of immune response, and TNF signaling pathway (Figure 1E).

MR Analysis Identifies Key Genes Associated With Disc Degeneration

To pinpoint key genes involved in disc degeneration, we performed MR analysis using summary statistics from a cohort of 336439 IVDD‐related samples (Controls: 294770; Cases: 41669), with the outcome ID being finngen_R10_M13_INTERVERTEB. Using the extract_instruments and extract_outcome_data methods, we identified eight genes whose eQTLs exhibited significant causal relationships with disc degeneration (p < 0.05). These genes were ATP13A3, BMP2, CD83, COL6A1, HLA‐DQA1, LAPTM5, NPC2, and ZFAND2A (Figure 2A–H).

MR analysis of genes associated with disc degeneration using integrated eQTL and FinnGen datasets. (A–H) Causal relationships between the eQTLs of individual genes (ATP13A3, BMP2, CD83, COL6A1, HLA‐DQA1, LAPTM5, NPC2, and ZFAND2A) and the risk of disc degeneration. (I–M) Leave‐one‐out analysis was performed separately for the MR studies of CD83, COL6A1, LAPTM5, NPC2, and ZFAND2A.

The analysis revealed that ATP13A3 (OR: 0.909, 95% CI: 0.832–0.993, p = 0.034) and HLA‐DQA1 (OR: 0.918, 95% CI: 0.882–0.955, p = 2.75e‐5) may be associated with low risk of disc degeneration, whereas CD83 (OR: 1.053, 95% CI: 1.010–1.097, p = 0.013), COL6A1 (OR: 1.053, 95% CI: 1.005–1.103, p = 0.028), LAPTM5 (OR: 1.036, 95% CI: 1.005–1.069, p = 0.022), NPC2 (OR: 1.041, 95% CI: 1.000–1.084, p = 0.048), ZFAND2A (OR: 1.047, 95% CI: 1.017–1.079, p = 0.001), and BMP2 (OR: 1.061, 95% CI: 1.010–1.116, p = 0.018) were associated with high risk of disc degeneration.

To assess the reliability of these causal relationships, we conducted heterogeneity and genetic pleiotropy analyses. The heterogeneity analysis showed no significant heterogeneity for the MR studies of each of the eight genes (Q_pval > 0.05) (Figure S2A–H). Given that ATP13A3, BMP2, and HLA‐DQA1 had a limited number of SNPs, we performed a sensitivity analysis for CD83, COL6A1, LAPTM5, NPC2, and ZFAND2A, respectively, using the leave‐one‐out method. The results showed that excluding any single SNP from the gene did not significantly impact the overall causal estimates, supporting the robustness of these findings. Consequently, these five genes were selected for further investigation (Figure 2I–M).

To explore the expression of these key genes in NP tissue, we performed Western blotting and immunohistochemical staining. The results indicated that CD83 expression in degenerated NP tissue showed a tendency toward an increase; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3A,B). COL6A1, LAPTM5, and ZFAND2A showed significantly higher expression in degenerated tissue (Figure 3C–F,I,J). Surprisingly, NPC2 expression was lower in degenerated NP tissue, possibly reflecting variations in gene expression between tissue and blood samples (Figure 3G,H).

Relationship Between Key Genes and Immune Infiltration in NP Degeneration

The IVD immune microenvironment consists of immune cells, extracellular matrix components, growth factors, and inflammatory mediators, all of which play a crucial role in disease pathogenesis and treatment response. This study examined the link between key genes and immune infiltration to explore potential molecular mechanisms driving NP degeneration progression.

We illustrated the proportions of immune cell types and their intercorrelations across the samples (Figure 4A,B). Notable findings showed marked differences between the control and disease groups in B_cells, Parainflammation, Th1_cells, Th2_cells, and Type_II_IFN_Response (Figure 4C). Further analysis explored the relationship between key genes and immune cell infiltration, revealing strong associations. ZFAND2A exhibited a strong negative association with B_cells and Cytolytic_activity; LAPTM5 was positively correlated with Macrophages and TIL but negatively correlated with aDCs; COL6A1 exhibited positive correlations with T_cell_co‐inhibition and HLA but negative correlations with Th2_cells and Type_II_IFN_Response (Figure 4D).

Furthermore, we retrieved data from the TISIDB database to assess the relationships between these three genes and immune factors such as immune regulators, chemokines, and cell receptors (Figure 4E–I). These results indicate that the key genes are strongly associated with immune cell infiltration and may play a critical role in modulating the immune microenvironment in the context of disc degeneration.

Exploring Specific Signaling Mechanisms Related to Key Genes

We explored the signaling pathways involving the three key genes to clarify their roles in disc degeneration progression. GSVA analysis revealed that high COL6A1 expression was linked to pathways including PEROXISOME and ANGIOGENESIS (Figure 5A). Similarly, high LAPTM5 expression was enriched in signaling pathways such as NOTCH_SIGNALING and WNT_BETA_CATENIN_SIGNALING (Figure 5B). Finally, high ZFAND2A expression was significantly associated with pathways such as REACTIVE_OXYGEN_SPECIES_PATHWAY and GLYCOLYSIS (Figure 5C).

RNA‐Seq‐based pathway enrichment and functional analysis of key genes in primary NPCs. (A–C) GSVA analysis showing pathways enriched for high and low expression of COL6A1, LAPTM5, or ZFAND2A. (D–F) GSEA analysis further reveals key pathways associated with these genes. (G–I) Overexpression of COL6A1, LAPTM5, or ZFAND2A in primary NPCs altered mRNA levels of extracellular matrix, cell survival, and catabolic markers.

Further GSEA analysis provided additional insights: COL6A1 was involved in pathways including ECM–receptor interaction, Aldosterone synthesis and secretion, and Neutrophil extracellular trap formation (Figure 5D). LAPTM5 was associated with the Relaxin signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, and NF–kappa B signaling pathway (Figure 5E). Lastly, ZFAND2A was linked to pathways including Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, Proteasome, and Protein export (Figure 5F). These findings suggest that the identified key genes may play critical roles in IVDD progression through their involvement in these specific signaling pathways.

To further validate the functional relevance of these genes, we conducted overexpression experiments in NPCs (Figure S3A–C). Overexpression of COL6A1 or ZFAND2A markedly reduced the mRNA levels of AGGRECAN, COL2A1, and BCL2 compared with the control group (Figure 5G,I). LAPTM5 overexpression significantly increased ADAMTS5 and MMP3 expression while concomitantly decreasing AGGRECAN expression (Figure 5H). Collectively, these results suggest that COL6A1 and ZFAND2A suppress extracellular matrix synthesis and cell survival, whereas LAPTM5 enhances catabolic and degradative processes in NPCs, thereby reinforcing their causal roles in IVDD pathogenesis.

Discussion

This study offers a detailed exploration of the mechanisms underlying disc degeneration, with a particular focus on the role of immune regulation in the NP. By integrating scRNA‐Seq, transcriptomic, and MR analyses, we identified key genes associated with disc degeneration and uncovered their relationships with the immune microenvironment. These findings provide novel insights into the pathophysiology of disc degeneration and highlight potential targets for intervention.

Using scRNA‐Seq analysis, we identified distinct cell types within NP samples, as well as potential ligand‐receptor interactions between stromal cells, chondrocytes, and other cell populations. Interestingly, the presence of blood cell types in the degenerated discs may be attributed to the infiltration of vascularized granulation tissue, a process reported in previous studies [34, 35]. These immune cells likely play a pivotal role in modulating the immune microenvironment within the disc, underscoring their importance in the degeneration process. Furthermore, marker genes from stromal cells were enriched in critical biological pathways, such as the extracellular matrix and positive regulation of immune response, suggesting these pathways as promising therapeutic targets for IVDD.

Through MR analysis, we identified five key genes significantly associated with IVDD risk: CD83, COL6A1, LAPTM5, NPC2, and ZFAND2A. Expression levels of these genes in the eQTL database showed a strong association with increased IVDD risk, providing a basis for blood‐based diagnosis of IVDD. Experimental validation using Western blotting and immunohistochemistry confirmed that COL6A1, LAPTM5, and ZFAND2A were significantly upregulated in degenerated NP tissues compared to relatively healthy samples.

Functional analysis further revealed their roles in immune responses and cellular signaling, findings consistent with existing literature. These results emphasize the complex interplay between gene expression and immune cell infiltration, which likely contributes to IVDD progression [17]. In addition, GSVA and GSEA analyses demonstrated that these key genes participate in critical signaling pathways, particularly Notch signaling and reactive oxygen species pathway, which are closely linked to immune regulation and can influence cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, suggesting they play a central role in IVDD pathogenesis.

From a therapeutic perspective, the genes identified in this study may offer promising targets for future intervention strategies in IVDD. For instance, COL6A1, although an essential component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), was observed to be significantly upregulated in degenerative discs, as demonstrated by our experimental validation. Its aberrant expression may contribute to pathological ECM accumulation or fibrosis [36]; thus, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating or suppressing its overexpression may help restore ECM homeostasis and prevent further structural deterioration. LAPTM5 is associated with lysosomal degradation and immune activation [37]. Its expression was also elevated in degenerative tissues, suggesting that inhibiting its expression or activity could alleviate inflammatory responses in the disc microenvironment. Additionally, ZFAND2A plays a critical role in proteasome‐mediated protein turnover and stress responses [38]. Its elevated expression in degenerative tissues may exacerbate stress‐related cellular damage and degeneration. Therefore, targeting the aberrant expression or function of ZFAND2A may help alleviate stress‐induced apoptosis, making it a promising therapeutic target for disc degeneration. These insights open avenues for the development of RNA‐based therapeutics, small‐molecule inhibitors, or gene‐editing technologies to modulate the function of these genes. While further in vivo validation and functional studies are warranted, our findings lay a solid foundation for the future development of targeted molecular therapies in IVDD.

One of the most intriguing findings of this study is the identification of a complex transcriptional regulatory network involving key genes, mediated by multiple transcription factors. These findings underscore the intricate regulatory mechanisms of gene expression in IVDD, offering promising directions for future research into the epigenetic and transcriptional regulation of this disease.

Although this study provides valuable insights, there are several limitations to consider. First, the FinnGen database primarily includes genetic data from European populations, particularly Finnish individuals, which may restrict the applicability of our findings to other ethnic groups [39]. However, despite this population‐specific origin, we believe the findings hold significant potential relevance for non‐European populations. The key genes identified in this study are likely to exert similar biological functions across diverse ethnic backgrounds. Validating the roles of these genes in non‐European cohorts could further elucidate shared genetic susceptibilities and support the identification of universally relevant biomarkers. Furthermore, such cross‐ethnic investigations may provide essential data to guide the development of globally applicable diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for disc degeneration. Second, the eQTL data utilized were derived from whole blood samples, and as such, the causal relationships identified may not directly reflect gene expression patterns in IVDs but rather represent systemic expression trends [21, 40]. Consequently, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to the context of IVDD. To address this limitation, we performed experimental validations to provide a more accurate reflection of gene expression in NP tissue. Third, the experimental validation of key genes was conducted on a relatively small number of clinical samples. This is primarily due to the challenge of obtaining fresh and relatively healthy NP tissue. Future studies with larger sample sizes and multicenter collaborations are warranted to validate and extend our current findings. Lastly, addressing the limitation that the experimental validations in this study were performed on whole NP tissues without specific cell subset isolation, future research should further investigate the expression patterns and functional roles of these key genes across distinct NP cell subpopulations. Cell type‐specific experimental models may provide deeper insights into the context‐dependent mechanisms of these genes within the cellular microenvironment. Such studies would not only enhance our understanding of NP cellular heterogeneity but also facilitate the identification of more precise therapeutic targets.

In conclusion, this study provides new perspectives on the pathogenesis of IVDD and identifies promising molecular targets for future diagnosis and therapeutic strategies. A more comprehensive understanding of the functions of these key genes and their roles within the immune microenvironment could pave the way for the development of more effective and precise interventions for this prevalent health issue.
