Beyond difficile: three novel toxin B-producing Clostridioides species from human patients with diarrhea
Authors: Daniela Aguilar, Thomas Riedel, Sandra Maaß, Anke Trautwein-Schult, Meina Neumann-Schaal, Sarah Kirstein, Isabel Schober, Boyke Bunk, Luis Acuña-Amador, Diana López-Ureña, Carlos Quesada-Gómez, Dörte Becher, Jörg Overmann, César Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClostridioides difficile infection (CDI), primarily mediated by toxin B (TcdB), is a leading cause of healthcare-associated and community-acquired diarrhea worldwide. Although this genus exhibits substantial genomic diversity, functional studies have focused mainly on a limited set of isolates, leaving other genomospecies understudied in this regard. Here, we characterized five isolates derived from five distinct patients with suspected CDI cases that tested negative for the tcdC PaLoc marker. Integrated genomic, proteomic, and phenotypic analyses confirm that these strains constitute three novel toxin-producing species and their distinct carbon utilization profiles, exoproteomes, and spore protein repertoires suggest niche specialization within the human gut ecosystem. These species harbour monotoxin PaLoc architectures encoding TcdB7 or TcdB11, located on either chromosomal or extrachromosomal elements, which frequently co-occur with binary toxin loci. Functional assays confirmed secretion of active TcdB, cytotoxicity in mammalian cells, and moderate enterotoxicity in a murine ileal loop model, although virulence was attenuated relative to C. difficile R20291 from Clade 2. Collectively, these findings expand the taxonomy of toxigenic Clostridioides, highlight the dynamic evolution of TcdB-mediated pathogenicity, and emphasize the importance of refining diagnostic workflows and surveillance strategies to address emerging diarrheal diseases. The three novel species were designated as Clostridioides cryptodifficilis sp. nov., Clostridioides divergens sp. nov., and Clostridioides subdifficilis sp. nov.KEYWORDS: C. difficile, novel Clostridioides species, Clostridioides taxonomy, comparative genomics, pathogenesis, TcdB

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile is a major bacterial pathogen with significant implications for both human and animal health, causing various gastrointestinal diseases. Its pathogenicity is driven by a combination of toxin production, metabolic adaptability, and intricate interactions with the gut microbiota and microenvironment [1].

Among its virulence factors, toxin B (TcdB) plays a central role in the onset and progression of C. difficile infection (CDI) [2]. Encoded within a chromosomal region known as the pathogenicity locus (PaLoc), this highly variable toxin exhibits 12 subtypes, each with distinct functional characteristics [3]. The PaLoc also comprises tcdA (responsible for encoding toxin A or its fragments), tcdR (a regulator of toxin expression), tcdE (a holin-like protein that plays a role in toxin secretion), tcdC (another regulator of toxin production), and tcdL, which is a remnant of an endolysin from a phage holin/endolysin module [1,4]. In addition to the toxins encoded by the PaLoc, certain C. difficile strains carry the binary toxin CDT (Clostridium difficile transferase), an exotoxin that ADP-ribosylates actin and is associated with increased adherence, colonization, and disease severity, although it is found in a separate chromosomal location [1].

In addition to its toxin diversity, C. difficile exhibits remarkably dynamic epidemiology. Over the past few decades, ribotypes such as 017, 027, and 078 have spread globally, contributing to increased CDI severity, recurrence, and mortality [5]. These epidemic strains likely evolved in response to selective pressures imposed by antibiotic use and human activity [6], and new toxigenic lineages continue to emerge.

Contrary to the long-held view that CDI is primarily a nosocomial infection, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) studies suggest that hospital transmission may be less widespread than previously believed [7]. Instead, mathematical models indicate that the ongoing spread of C. difficile involves asymptomatic carriers in the community and extra-clinical reservoirs [8–10]. Consistently, this bacterial species has been recovered from farm animals, wildlife, food, soil, sediments, and water sources [11], emphasizing the need for ongoing surveillance and more comprehensive public health strategies to mitigate CDI.

The remarkable genetic heterogeneity of C. difficile has been extensively characterized through multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and WGS analyses [12]. Currently, sequenced strains are grouped into five MLST clades (Clades 1–5), alongside several deeply divergent lineages commonly referred to as cryptic clades. These cryptic lineages possess highly atypical PaLoc structures and frequently evade standard molecular diagnostics due to their unique tcdB sequences and divergent TcdB epitopes [13–15, unpublished data]. Furthermore, their genomes share an average nucleotide identity (ANI) of less than 95% with that of C. difficile sensu stricto, supporting their delineation as distinct species [15].

To date, the virulence potential and clinical relevance of these cryptic lineages remain largely unresolved, underscoring a critical gap in our understanding of Clostridioides pathogenesis beyond the canonical species. To bridge this gap, we conducted an extensive taxonomic and functional examination of five isolates from human patients with diarrhea, which exhibited atypical diagnostic features. By employing a multidisciplinary strategy that combined WGS, proteomics, chemotaxonomy, catabolic profile analysis, and biomodel experimentation, we present clear evidence for the classification of three novel Clostridioides species that produce active TcdB and cause disease. These results enhance our understanding of the diversification and broader taxonomy of this clinically significant genus, underscoring the need for more comprehensive diagnostic methods to effectively detect and manage emerging toxigenic species.

Methods

Bacterial strains

This study was performed using isolates from the Anaerobic Bacteriology Research Laboratory (LIBA: Laboratorio de Investigación en Bacteriología Anaerobia) collection at the University of Costa Rica (UCR), along with reference C. difficile strains from the Leibniz Institute DSMZ culture collection (Supplementary Table 1). The LIBA isolates were cultivated from 2005 to 2018 from distinct pediatric and adult human patients with a clinical diagnosis of CDI using Cycloserine-Cefoxitin Fructose Agar (CCFA) plates inoculated with ethanol-shocked stool samples. These bacteria yielded positive PCR amplification for the tpi gene and exhibited colony and microscopic characteristics typical of C. difficile, appearing as Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacteria with subterminal spores, (Supplementary Figure 1). Nonetheless, they drew our attention because they consistently yielded negative PCR results for tcdC. All LIBA isolates were successfully cultivated under standard conditions established for C. difficile (37°C, selective or supplemented media, anaerobic atmosphere), confirming their adaptation to identical experimental parameters. The LIBA isolates originated from routine diagnostic work and were irreversibly de-identified before analysis. Because the study did not include patient-identifiable information, the UCR Bioethics Committee designated it as non-human-subjects research and waived the requirement for informed consent (Resolution CEC-74-2021). All bacterial strains were preserved at −80°C in glycerol stocks. The type strains reported here were deposited in the DSMZ and JCM culture collections (see below).

Sequencing and automated annotation

Hybrid assemblies were generated using Illumina and PacBio Sequel IIe reads. To this end, genomic DNA (gDNA) for paired-end short-read sequencing was obtained with the DNEasy Ultraclean Microbial kit (Qiagen) from biomass collected from trypticase soy broth tubes inoculated to match the turbidity of a McFarland standard 3 and incubated anaerobically for 48 h at 35°C. In turn, gDNA for PacBio Sequel IIe sequencing was obtained using either the MasterPure Gram-Positive DNA Purification kit (Epicentre) with 20 µM EDTA added to the eluate to prevent degradation, or the Nanobind CBB kit (PacificBiosciences). Sequencing by synthesis on Illumina instruments was outsourced to MicrobesNG (United Kingdom), MiGS (USA), SeqCenter LLC (USA), and SeqCoast (USA). PacBio sequencing was performed at the DSMZ using a Sequel II platform. PacBio contigs were polished by mapping of Illumina reads using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner v0.7.12-r1039 [16] and identification and correction of sequence deviations by VarScan v2.3.6 [17], applying the “mpileup2cns” command with minimum coverage of 10, at least six supporting reads, minimum read base quality of 20, a variant allele frequency threshold of 0.8, and a p-value of 0.01. If necessary, the resulting chromosomal and extrachromosomal contigs were manually circularized and shifted to dnaA, rep, or soj as the start point. For automated annotation, we used Prokka v1.14.6 [18]. Genome features were summarized using GenoVi [19].

Bioinformatic analyses for bacterial identification, taxonomic placement, and typing

To verify the preliminary identification of the LIBA strains as C. difficile and evaluate their diversity, we performed rMLST (ribosomal Multilocus sequence Typing) and MLST analyses on their hybrid assemblies using the ribosomal MLST webserver [20] and FastMLST tool [21], respectively.

Taxonomic placement of isolates at the genus level was performed using complementary phylogenomic and protein-level similarity approaches. An initial phylogenomic reconstruction based on conserved bacterial core genes was performed with UBCG2 (Up-to-date Bacterial Core Gene set 2), employing a curated reference panel of type strain genomes representing Clostridioides and phylogenetically related genera within the Peptostreptococcaceae family. This reference dataset comprised Clostridioides difficile DSM 1296T (NZ_CP011968.1), Metaclostridioides mangenotii DSM 1289T (JAGGJX010000001.1), Asaccharospora irregularis DSM 2635T (FQWX01000001.1), Peptostreptococcus anaerobius DSM 2949T (AMEL01000001.1), Paraclostridium bifermentans DSM 14991T (CP079737.1), Peptoclostridium litorale DSM 5388T (JJMM01000001.1), Romboutsia ilealis DSM 25109T (LN555523.1), and Terrisporobacter glycolicus DSM 1288T (CP117523.1). We also calculated pairwise protein-level relatedness using EzAAI (Easy Average Amino acid Identity) to determine average amino acid identity (AAI) values between LIBA genomes and type strain references. In this regard, AAI was selected over ANI for genus-level taxonomic assessment, as amino acid sequences exhibit greater evolutionary conservation than nucleotide sequences and provide more reliable phylogenetic signal for deeper taxonomic relationships beyond the species level.

Comprehensive taxonomic validation at the species level was performed using the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) [22], applying established genomic thresholds for bacterial species delimitation: ANI values ≤95%, 16S rRNA gene sequence divergence ≥3%, and dDDH values <70% to define distinct species boundaries. Within the TYGS framework, identification of the closest type strain genomes was achieved through a dual screening strategy combining MASH-based prescreening with 16S rRNA gene-based analysis. 16S rRNA gene sequences were extracted using RNAmmer and matched against the TYGS 16S database via BLAST search. The highest-scoring matches were subsequently subjected to refined distance estimation using the Genome Blast Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) method under the coverage algorithm with distance formula d5. For phylogenomic inference within TYGS, pairwise genome distances were calculated from whole-proteome datasets and 16S rRNA gene sequences using GBDP under the trimming algorithm with distance formula d5, incorporating 100 pseudo-bootstrap replicates for statistical support. Digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) values and associated confidence intervals were estimated using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) version 4.0 with recommended settings, emphasizing the d4 formula for robust species-level taxonomic circumscription.

Finally, to establish the phylogenetic placement of the LIBA isolates relative to the C. difficile reference strains 630 (accession NZ_CP010905), R20291 (accession NZ_CP029423), M68 (accession NC_017175), M120 (accession NZ_CP068555), or strains indicated in Supplementary Table 1, we aligned concatenated MLST alleles and variable-split k-mers identified with ska2 [23] to generate PhyML [24] dendrograms. These phylogenies were analyzed with the Species Delimitation plugin in Geneious (Biomatters) to compute: (i) the mean probability that an unknown sequence falls within or as sister to a reference clade under the least stringent placement criterion, reflecting assignment success based on intra- versus inter-clade distances (Liberal P ID), and (ii) the probability that two candidate species appear reciprocally monophyletic by chance under a neutral coalescent model (Rosenberg’s P(A|B)), with low values indicating non-random monophyly. These complementary metrics quantify the reliability of the assignments and the statistical support for species-level clusters.

Pangenome analyses

To illustrate differences between the core and accessory genomes of the LIBA isolates and reference C. difficile strains, we ran PPanGGoLiN v. 2.2.0 [25] on three genome datasets composed of (i) LIBA strains alone, (ii) C. difficile reference strains, and (iii) LIBA strains in combination with C. difficile reference strains. This software classifies gene families into persistent (present in almost all genomes), shell (present at intermediate frequencies), or cloud (present at low frequencies) partitions, provides estimates of genomic fluidity to quantify genomic dissimilarity, and identifies conserved gene modules within the modules.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-ToF MS)

Species-level identification and clustering of the bacterial isolates were performed using MALDI-ToF MS, which discriminates taxa based on ribosomal protein and other high-molecular-weight biomolecule fingerprints [26]. To this end, exponentially growing cultures in brain-heart infusion medium broth (BHI) at 37°C were harvested and processed as described by Schumann and Maier [26]. Mass spectra were acquired on a Microflex equipment (Bruker Daltonics) and analyzed with MBT Compass Explorer v4.1 and FlexAnalysis v3.4 (Bruker Daltonics).

Fatty acid analyses

We determined the cellular fatty acid profiles of the LIBA isolates from stationary phase cells in BHI. The combination of incubation time and media ensures comparability, as different media and growth conditions significantly affect fatty acid profiles, which stabilize with increasing culture age [27]. Fatty acids were analyzed after conversion to fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). Saponification, methylation, and extraction were performed according to the protocol described by Sasser [28]. FAMEs mixtures were separated by gas chromatography (GC) and quantified using a flame ionization detector. In subsequent analyses, FAMEs were identified by GC-MS on an Agilent GC-MS 7000D system based on the procedure published by Vieira et al. [29] using retention time and mass spectra. To resolve the position of the single double bonds and determine branched-chain fatty acid positions, cyclo-positions, and multiple double bonds, FAMEs were derivatized to dimethyl disulfide adducts [30] or 4,4-dimethyloxazoline derivatives [31]. The relative abundances of the detected FAMEs were compared and visualized using MeV [32].

Fermentation profiles

Volatile fermentation products (VFP) were extracted from culture supernatants after 24 h of incubation in BHI at 37°C and quantified as described by Neumann-Schaal et al. [33], using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled to a 5977A mass selective detector equipped with a high-efficiency source and integrated with a Gerstel RTC autosampler system. VFPs were quantified relative to the internal standard o-cresol, to which all compound abundances were normalized. Data visualization was conducted using MeV [32].

Carbon source usage

Carbon-source utilization profiles were determined with PM1 and PM2 Phenotype MicroArray plates (Biolog) containing 190 carbon substrates. To ensure the availability of auxiliary nutrients, each well was supplemented with 1/10 strength C. difficile minimal medium (0.1X CDMM). Pre-cultures were grown anaerobically on BHI agar plates for 24 h at 37°C. Biomass was harvested inside an anaerobic chamber, resuspended in 1/10 CDMM, and adjusted to an initial optical density of 0.1 at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀). Aliquots of 100 µL of this suspension were dispensed into every well of the PM plates, which were then incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 48 h. Growth was monitored turbidimetrically (OD₆₀₀) with a multimode microplate reader (Spark, Tecan). Three biological replicates were performed. For each plate, the background was corrected by subtracting the median OD₆₀₀ of the non-responsive wells. Differences in mean carbon utilization between C. difficile and the novel Clostridioides sp. were evaluated independently for every substrate using Welch’s t-tests, and log₂ fold changes (log₂FC) were calculated to quantify the effect size. A substrate was classified as a differential hit if it met both a p-value below 0.05 and a log₂FC > |1|.

Proteomics

To elucidate functional differences between the novel Clostridioides species and C. difficile and to identify potential leads that could inform the development of improved diagnostic tools, proteins were extracted from the supernatant of stationary cells (exoproteome) or mature spores (spore proteome) and analyzed by shotgun MS. Three biological replicates were examined for each sample type.

For exoproteome analyses, LIBA and reference strains were cultured in BHI to the stationary phase. Cells were removed from culture supernatants by centrifugation and filtration through 0.2 µm membranes. Proteins in cell-free supernatants were enriched by precipitation with pre-hydrolyzed Strata Clean resin (Agilent) as described previously [34]. Resin-bound proteins were washed and electroeluted by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then digested in-gel with trypsin, eluted in water, and purified with C18 tips (Pierce) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To prepare spores, 1/1000 dilutions of cultures in BHI were streaked on BHI agar plates. After 7 d of incubation at 37°C under anaerobiosis, biomass was harvested with ice-cold water, and vegetative cells were removed by density gradient centrifugation in 1x PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and one volume of 100% Histodenz (Sigma). Afterwards, the spore fractions were washed with 1x PBS containing 1% BSA. Before usage, the spores were allowed to mature for 14 d at 4°C. The abundance and purity of spore preparations were controlled using Schaeffer and Fulton staining. To analyze the spore proteome, 1 × 108 spores were heated for 10 min at 95°C in two volumes of 2x Laemmli buffer and loaded onto 10% SDS-gels. Separated proteins were in-gel digested with trypsin and eluted in water, as described elsewhere [34]. Peptides were purified as described above using C18 tips (Pierce).

For Liquid Chromatography (LC)-MS/MS analyses, peptides were separated by reversed-phase column chromatography using an EASY nLC II instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and self-packed columns (OD 360 μm, ID 100 μm, length 20 cm) filled with 3 µm diameter C18 particles (Dr. Maisch). Following loading with 0.1% acetic acid in water, the peptides were separated by applying a binary nonlinear gradient with acetonitrile in 0.1% acetic acid for 180 min. The LC was coupled to a LTQ Orbitrap Velos-Pro mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) with a spray voltage of 2.6 kV. After a survey scan in the Orbitrap (r = 30,000), MS/MS data were recorded for the 20 most intense precursor ions in the linear ion trap. Singly charged ions were not considered in the MS/MS analysis. The lock-mass option was enabled in all analyses.

Raw MS/MS data were searched against a customized protein database comprising all predicted amino acid sequences from the genomes of the LIBA isolates, the reference C. difficile strains listed in Supplementary Table 1, and C. difficile Semix9 – a cryptic clade C-III environmental isolate with a fully resolved genome that carries a CDT locus on an episomal extrachromosomal element resembling a bacteriophage-plasmid hybrid [35]. This custom database was demultiplexed using CD-Hit v4.8.1 [36] by applying a “global sequence identity” threshold of 0.95, resulting in a final database containing 14843 entries. The protein clusters included in this non-redundant database are available in Supplementary Table 2.

Protein identification by database search was performed with MaxQuant v2.2.2.0 [37], which included common laboratory contaminants and reversed sequences for false discovery rate (FDR) estimation. Search parameters were configured for trypsin/P-specific digestion with up to two missed cleavages, and methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications. Matching between runs was enabled with default settings. Both protein and peptide-spectrum match (PSM) FDRs were controlled at 1% (FDR = 0.01). Protein identification required a minimum of two unique peptides. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was performed using the MaxLFQ algorithm, with a minimum ratio count of 2 and quantification based exclusively on unique peptides [38]. Intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) values were calculated to estimate semi-absolute protein abundances [39]. Quantitative values were consolidated into a single entry for orthologous protein clusters containing one gene per strain to facilitate inter-strain comparisons (Supplementary Table 3). The localization of the annotated protein sequences was predicted using PSORTb 3.0 [40]. Protein clusters were functionally annotated by assigning Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) categories using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.9 [41].

Protein biomarker discovery

We used three methods to distinguish protein markers specific to LIBA strains or C. difficile reference strains: (i) comparative analysis of their predicted proteomes, (ii) identification of uniquely expressed proteins, and (iii) detection of proteins with differential abundance. These analyses used the non-redundant database described above, refined by removing protein functions shared by multiple proteins in a single strain. Protein abundance levels were compared using Student's t-tests, with a p-value threshold of 0.01. To be considered differentially abundant, a log₂FC greater than |0.8| was required.

TcdB detection and functional characterization

Toxin loci and their accompanying flanking genes were extracted from annotation files using Artemis v.18.0.0 [42] and then aligned and compared visually using clinker v.0.0.30 [43]. TcdB subtypes were identified using the BLAST algorithm in Diffbase [3,44].

To confirm the activity of TcdB among the LIBA isolates, cell-free supernatants obtained using BHI following published protocols [45] were added to HeLa and 3T3 cell monolayers grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco). Cells were monitored by optical microscopy for 20 h for the appearance of cytopathic effects (CPE) using a Lionheart FX automated microscope (BioTek) in the bright field channel. As a control, polyclonal TcdB/TcdA antiserum (TechLab) was used to neutralize TcdB effects. The Clade 2 C. difficile strain R20291 was used as a TcdB positive control in this series of experiments.

For TcdB immunodetection, the exoproteome preparation protocol described above was modified as follows: strains were grown in parallel in BHI under anaerobiosis and sampled at equivalent points of their growth curves, corresponding to comparable cell densities. Under these match conditions, differences in recovered extracellular protein were interpreted as reflecting variation in secretion rather than differences in biomass. The resulting concentrated protein preparations were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to membranes for western blotting (WB) using an anti-TcdB monoclonal antibody (2CV, tgcBIOMICS). A total-protein gel was run in parallel to document the overall protein content of each supernatant preparation.

To evaluate the virulence of the LIBA isolates, we tested them in a murine ileal loop model. Briefly, four-week-old Hsd:ICR mice, weighing approximately 20 g, were inoculated with 0.1 mL of bacterial supernatant into ligated ileal loops, following previously established protocols [45]. Four hours post-inoculation, animals were euthanized, and data were collected. Tissue damage severity was assessed by measuring the loop weight-to-length ratio (mg/cm), myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity (U/100 mg), and concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in tissue homogenates. Five mice per strain, including controls, were analyzed. Data were used to classify the inflammatory response into four severity categories (1–4), based on quartiles (0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1.00). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Committee for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals at UCR (approval number: CICUA-73-2023).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for the first-line drugs metronidazole and vancomycin were determined using E-test strips on Brucella Blood Agar plates, incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 48 h. Interpretations followed the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [46]. For metronidazole, susceptibility breakpoints were defined as susceptible (MIC ≤ 8 μg/mL), intermediate (MIC =16 μg/mL), and resistant (MIC ≥ 32 μg/mL). Due to the absence of CLSI clinical breakpoints for C. difficile, vancomycin susceptibility was interpreted using an epidemiological cutoff value of ≤ 2 μg/mL.

Data availability

Hybrid genome assemblies for the LIBA isolates, together with their PGAP annotations, were deposited in GenBank under BioProject PRJNA1297032 (BioSamples SAMN50205303-SAMN50205307, accessions: CP196987-CP197006). Additionally, the Prokka annotations used in the study are available on Figshare (DOI: ). The MS proteomics data were uploaded to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride) under dataset accession numbers PXD067952 (exoproteome) and PXD066931 (spore proteome). All other supporting datasets and detailed results are provided in Supplementary Tables and Figures (see accompanying listing).

Results

The LIBA isolates represent three novel Clostridioides species

The genomes of the LIBA isolates consist of single, circular chromosomes ranging from 4.17 to 4.35 Mb, with GC contents varying from 28.6% to 28.8%. Additionally, each isolate contains at least two extrachromosomal elements that predominantly encode hypothetical proteins, but exhibit features reminiscent of phages (60–133 kb, 26.2–28.6% GC) or plasmids (141–201 kb, 24.5–25.3% GC) (Table 1).

All phylogenomic and protein-level similarity analyses were concordant in placing the LIBA isolates within the genus Clostridioides and supporting their recognition as three novel species. In the UBCG2 core-genome phylogeny, all five LIBA genomes clustered within the Clostridioides clade alongside C. difficile and were clearly differentiated from neighboring genera within the peptostreptococcal lineage, including Metaclostridioides, Peptostreptococcus, Peptoclostridium, Paraclostridium, Romboutsia, Asaccharospora, and Terrisporobacter (Figure 1(a)). The AAI protein-level similarity matrix corroborated this taxonomic placement, with all five LIBA isolates exhibiting their highest amino acid identity values to C. difficile DSM 1296T among the type strain references (AAI = 93–93.6%), while corresponding values to neighboring genera were substantially lower, demonstrating clear genus-level demarcation (Figure 1(b)). The TYGS analyses, employing both whole-proteome GBDP (Figure 1(c)) and 16S rRNA gene GBDP approaches (Figure 1(d)), independently confirmed this assignment. In both TYGS phylogenetic reconstructions, the LIBA genomes formed a monophyletic group within Clostridioides while remaining phylogenetically distinct from all other genera examined.

Collectively, these complementary taxonomic approaches provide robust evidence for assignment of the LIBA isolates to the genus Clostridioides as three distinct genomic groups: group 1 (LIBA-7678), group 2 (LIBA-6622), and group 3 (LIBA-2809, LIBA-6822, and LIBA-7602).

Phenotypic analyses confirm species-level differentiation from C. difficile

MALDI-ToF spectra aligned with the WGS findings, distinguishing the LIBA isolates from reference C. difficile strains across Clades 1 through 5 (Figure 3). Specific peaks at m/z 4867 and 4826 were unique to C. difficile and not present in the LIBA isolates (Supplementary Figure 2). Conversely, peaks at m/z 4510 and 4638, m/z 4817 and 4872, and m/z 4811 and 4873 were distinctive to groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively (Supplementary Figure 2). While the clearly differentiated profiles in the MALDI-ToF fingerprints show promise for clinical diagnostic applications, their consistency must be validated in the future using a larger set of isolates.

MALDI-ToF data differentiate C. difficile strains from Clades 1–5 (cyan) from the LIBA isolates (red, with cryptic clade designations in Roman numerals).

The cellular fatty acid profiles aided in assigning the LIBA isolates to the genus Clostridioides [27,47] but did not categorically differentiate them from C. difficile (Figure 4(a), Supplementary Table 4). A similar conclusion was drawn after examination of VFP profiles (Figure 4(b), Supplementary Table 5), which displayed a high abundance of branched-chain fermentation products from both reductive (isocaproate) and oxidative (isovalerate, isobutyrate, and 2-methylbutyrate) Stickland pathways, underscoring the reliance of these bacteria on branched-chain amino acid-based Stickland reactions for energy metabolism [48]. The LIBA isolates exhibited slight variances from C. difficile in metabolites outside the primary Stickland pathways, including alcohols and p-cresol; nonetheless, these differences were not statistically significant.

Hierarchical clustering of the LIBA isolates and reference C. difficile strains from Clades 1–5 based on the Spearman’s correlation of their cellular fatty acid profiles (a) and volatile fermentation products (b). VFP data were normalized to the median for each detected compound across three biological replicates.

A multivariate ordination method revealed differences in the carbon-substrate utilization profiles of the LIBA isolates and C. difficile, suggesting niche-specific adaptations and confirming functional divergence (Supplementary Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 6A). Multiple carbon sources emerged as significant differentiators between these two groups of bacteria, with most compounds meeting the selected statistical thresholds being utilized more extensively by C. difficile. Ordered from the largest to the smallest difference, these include D-trehalose, D-melezitose, maltose, fumaric acid, uridine, inosine, L-threonine, chondroitin sulfate C, β-D-lactose, succinic acid, D-melibiose, N-acetyl-D-glucosaminitol, β-keto-valeric acid, and acetoacetic acid (Supplementary Table 6B). Conversely, the LIBA isolates exhibited significantly higher utilization for 2,3-butandione and glycyl-L-proline (Supplementary Table 6B).

Altogether, this bulk of genomic and phenotypic data categorically confirm that LIBA-7678 (previously Clade C-I), LIBA-6622 (previously Clade C-VII), LIBA-2809, LIBA-6822, and LIBA-7602 (previously Clade C-V) represent new species, which we designate as Clostridioides divergens sp. nov., Clostridioides subdifficilis sp. nov., and Clostridioides cryptodifficilis sp. nov., respectively. Strains LIBA-7678T, LIBA-6622T, and LIBA-2809T were selected as their respective type strains based on their earlier isolation dates (Supplementary Table 1).

Omics insights into the novel Clostridioides species reveal signature sequences as well as structural and functional differences

Consistent with their classification in the genus Clostridioides and human origin, the novel species and C. difficile showed comparable total counts of genes (18,397 vs. 20,745) and gene families (4,563 vs. 5,459), as well as similar sizes and fluidity of persistent (65–68%, 0.104–0.135), shell (0–4%, 0–0.002), and cloud genome components (28–35%, 0.757–0.808), number of genomic plasticity regions (143–144) and COG gene classifications (Supplementary Tables 7A and 8). When all these genomes were combined, the resulting persistent genome size decreased to 47%, the cloud genome size increased to 45%, and the number of regions of genomic plasticity increased almost 3-fold (Supplementary Table 7A), indicating that they and C. difficile share a genomic backbone on which species-specific genes are inserted. Indeed, we identified conserved gene modules unique to all newly characterized Clostridioides species (n = 19), C. difficile (n = 7), C. divergens sp. nov., and C. cryptodifficilis sp. nov. (n = 4), C. subdifficilis sp. nov. and C. cryptodifficilis sp. nov. (n = 7), and C. cryptodifficilis sp. nov. (n = 8) (Supplementary Table 7B–G). Further analysis of these gene modules can reveal ecological and physiological differences and provide methods to distinguish the novel Clostridioides species using molecular techniques commonly employed in clinical labs, such as PCR.

Regarding proteomics, the exoproteome samples contained 1,610 protein clusters representing 828 distinct functions (Supplementary Table 3A). Almost three-quarters of these proteins were assigned a function, with the largest functional groups involved in energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism, translation, and cell envelope functions (Supplementary Figure 4). The exoproteomes of the novel species and C. difficile differed in several respects, with the latter group exhibiting higher protein mass dedicated to energy production (30.7 vs. 23.7%), carbohydrate metabolism (4.5 vs. 3.5%), and lipid metabolism (4.1% vs. 3.1%) (Figure 5, left panel). On the other hand, a higher protein mass was attributed to cell envelope biogenesis (7.1 vs. 5.0%) and inorganic ion transport and metabolism (1.8 vs. 0.6%) in the novel Clostridioides species than in the reference C. difficile strains (Figure 5, left panel).

Number of protein clusters, localization and function of proteins detected in the exoproteomes (left panel) and mature spore proteomes (right panel) of the novel Clostridioides species (left side of each pair) and reference C. difficile strains representing clades 1–5 (right side in each pair).

The spore proteomes, instead, included 1,665 proteins, representing 872 protein functions, predominantly comprising proteins involved in energy and amino acid metabolism, as well as translation (Supplementary Table 3B). This dataset also revealed significant species-specific variations, such as fewer extracellular or cell wall-associated proteins in C. difficile (0.2%) compared to the novel Clostridioides species (2.1%) and a predominance of proteins involved in translation in C. difficile (22.8%) compared to energy production (24.2%) in the other Clostridioides species (Figure 5, right panel).

Our multiphasic biomarker discovery approach yielded an initial pool of 876 predicted candidates (Supplementary Table 9A), which was subsequently refined to 330 biomarker candidates (Supplementary Table 9B). While many of these proteins are classified as hypothetical, others are recognized as ribosomal and metabolic proteins. Examples include the glutathione-binding protein GsiB, 2-dehydropantoate 2-reductase, 4-hydroxy-3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl diphosphate synthase (flavodoxin), cyclohexadienyl dehydrogenase, the cysteine protease StiP, D-threo-3-hydroxyaspartate dehydratase, and UDP-N-acetylmuramoylalanine-D-glutamate ligase, among many others.

The novel Clostridioides species show diverse toxin genotypes and clinically relevant phenotypes

Since the newly identified species were recovered from stool samples of patients with diarrhea, we examined their genomes for the presence of tcdB genes. The C. divergens sp. nov. strain LIBA-7678T and the C. cryptodifficilis sp. nov. strains LIBA-2809T, LIBA-6822, and LIBA-7602 contain TcdB7 on a set of potential conjugative plasmids located within pathogenicity loci that do not have tcdA and tcdC. These loci are next to CDT genes and flanked by genes encoding a transposase and an integrase (Supplementary Figure 5). In contrast, the C. subdifficilis sp. nov. strain LIBA-6622T entirely lacks CDT loci but carries a chromosomally encoded TcdB11 that is not surrounded by recognizable mobile genetic elements (Supplementary Figure 5).

Considering the essential role of TcdB in CDI pathogenesis, we evaluated whether a subset of strains representing the three new Clostridioides species secretes an active TcdB by assessing the effect of cell-free supernatants on cultured HeLa and 3T3 cells (Figure 6(A)). Supernatants from strains LIBA-7678T (C. divergens sp. nov., TcdB7) and LIBA-6822 (C. cryptodifficilis sp. nov., TcdB7) induced a Paeniclostridium sordellii-like cytopathic effect (Figure 6(A)). On the other hand, cells intoxicated with LIBA-6622T (C. subdifficilis sp. nov., TcdB11) supernatants showed a classical CPE characterized by the formation of protrusions and cell rounding (Figure 6(A)). A polyclonal antibody effectively neutralized both CPE types, confirming that TcdB was responsible for the observed morphological changes (data not shown). The detected CPE type corresponded with the expected effect of each toxin, as predicted by their sequence clustering (Figure 6(B)).

Cytotoxicity titres in HeLa and 3T3 cells showed cell line-dependent differences in potency among the supernatants (Figure 6(C and D)). HeLa cells were more sensitive to TcdB11 from strain LIBA-6622T, whereas TcdB7 from strain LIBA-7678T induced more pronounced rounding in 3T3 cells than the supernatants from strains LIBA-6622T and LIBA-6822. Because all TcdB7-containing strains share the same monotoxin PaLoc (Supplementary Figure 5), these differences may variation in toxin production, secretion, stability, or the contribution of additional secreted factors in LIBA-7678T supernatants, requiring further investigation.

To further assess biological effects in a tissue context, we used the mouse ileal loop model, which captures the combined activity of secreted products rather than serving as a direct measure of strain pathogenicity. C. difficile R20291 caused significant fluid buildup and tissue swelling, whereas the LIBA strain supernatants produced weaker effects (Figure 7(A)). A composite severity score incorporating weight-to-length ratio, MPO activity, and proinflammatory cytokine levels indicated that LIBA-6822 induced the strongest tissue response among the novel species, although this remained lower than that induced by C. difficile (Figure 7(B)). Histopathological analysis similarly showed extensive epithelial damage, hemorrhage, and inflammatory infiltration in loops exposed to C. difficile R20291 supernatants, whereas the LIBA strains produced milder lesions, with LIBA-6822 causing moderate epithelial injury and neutrophilic infiltration (Figure 7(C)). Together, these findings show that the novel species secrete factors capable of inducing tissue responses, although their overall effects in this model were attenuated relative to R20291.

To examine whether differences in biological activity might be associated with differences in toxin production, we analyzed culture supernatants by WB after growth under matched conditions. Supernatants were collected at equivalent points of the growth curves from cultures with comparable cell numbers, such that differences in extracellular protein concentration reflected genuine differences in secreted protein output. Under these conditions, a clear TcdB-immunoreactive band was detected in the positive control strain C. difficile R20291, whereas no corresponding band was detected in the LIBA strains despite the presence of extracellular protein (Supplementary Figure 6). These data suggest that the novel strains produced lower amounts of immunodetectable TcdB relative to strain R20291 under the conditions tested. This interpretation is consistent with the strain-dependent variation observed in the cell-based assays and the attenuated effects of LIBA supernatants in the ileal loop model, although reduced antibody recognition of divergent toxins cannot be excluded.

The novel Clostridioides species are susceptible to first-line antibiotics for CDI

Given the human origin of the new Clostridioides species, we evaluated their resistance to first-line antibiotics for CDI. All isolates exhibited vancomycin and metronidazole MICs below 0.75 and 0.5 µg/ml, respectively. Hence, they were categorized as susceptible to both antibiotics.

Description of Clostridioides divergens sp. nov.

Clostridioides divergens (di.ver’gens. L. pres. part. divergens, separating from one another, diverging, referring to the phylogenetic distance from C. difficile and other related species). Cells are Gram-stain-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming rods. The colony and microscopic morphologies are similar to those of C. difficile. The genomic content of the type strain includes a single circular chromosome of 4.2 Mb with a G + C content of 28.7 mol%. The type strain harbours a monotoxin, PaLoc, located on a plasmid rather than the chromosome. This PaLoc encodes TcdB7 and causes a Clostridium sordellii-like cell rounding cytopathic effect. LIBA-7678T displays a fatty acids profile dominated by saturated components, with major constituents hexadecanoic acid (C16:0, ∼17.6%), octadecanoic acid (C18:0, ∼14.6%), and additionally hexadecanal dimethyl acetal (C16:0 DMA, ∼9.6%) deriving from vinyl ethers. After growth in BHI, fermentation end products are enriched in acetate and butyrate, with virtually no production of medium-chain alcohols. Relative to reference C. difficile strains, LIBA-7678 T shows limited carbohydrate utilization, with markedly reduced or absent use of D-trehalose, D-melezitose, and D-cellobiose, and a greater reliance on amino-acid-derived substrates consistent with Stickland metabolism. Preferentially utilized carbon sources include N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, glucose, fructose, tagatose, mannose, and the polyols mannitol and sorbitol. The type strain is LIBA-7678T (=DSM 113161T, JCM 38292T) and was isolated from the stool of a Costa Rican patient with diarrhea.

Description of Clostridioides cryptodifficilis sp. nov.

Clostridioides cryptodifficilis (cryp.to.dif.fi’ci.lis. Gr. adj. kryptos, hidden, secret; N.L. masc. adj. difficilis, the specific epithet of C. difficile; N.L. masc. adj. cryptodifficilis, the hidden difficile, referring to its cryptic phylogenetic nature). Cells are Gram-stain-positive, anaerobic, and form endospores. Morphological characteristics are consistent with those of C. difficile. The genome comprises a 4.30 Mb chromosome with a G + C content of 28.8 mol% and includes a large 143 kb plasmid harbouring a monotoxin PaLoc with tcdB7, as well as other extrachromosomal elements. It exhibits a fatty acids profile dominated by saturated fatty acids, primarily hexadecanoic acid (C16:0) and octadecanoic acid (C18:0), together with characteristic clostridial iso-branched and dimethyl acetal derivatives, the latter deriving from vinyl ethers. During growth in BHI medium, fermentation end products are enriched in branched-chain volatile fatty acids, including isocaproate, isovalerate, and 2-methylbutyrate, along with substantial amounts of acetate and valerate. The strain also produces large quantities of p-cresol, reaching levels comparable to or exceeding those of reference C. difficile strains. In terms of carbon utilization, it preferentially metabolizes simple monosaccharides such as tagatose, glucose, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, and fructose, as well as selected β-glucosides and polyols including arbutin, salicin, sorbitol, and mannitol. However, its overall metabolic profile is considerably less saccharolytic than that of C. difficile, showing minimal or no growth on several carbohydrates typically exploited by C. difficile, notably D-trehalose, D-melezitose, D-cellobiose, mannitol, sorbitol, and arabitol. This species demonstrates enhanced utilization of amino-acid-derived substrates and small organic acids, including L-leucine, N-acetyl-β-D-mannosamine, capric acid, multiple dipeptides, and D-serine. The type strain is LIBA-2809T (DSM 113163T, JCM 38291T), isolated from the stool of a Costa Rican patient with diarrhea; additional strains include LIBA-6822 and LIBA-7602.

Description of Clostridioides subdifficilis sp. nov.

Clostridioides subdifficilis (sub.dif.fi’ci.lis. L. prep. sub, under, close to; N.L. masc. adj. difficilis, the specific epithet of C. difficile; N.L. masc. adj. subdifficilis, close to difficile, referring to its phylogenetic position relative to C. difficile). Cells stain Gram-positive, are anaerobic, and form endospores. The species exhibits colony and cellular morphology typical of the C. difficile. The genome of the type strain, LIBA-6622T, consists of a 4.17 Mb chromosome with a G + C content of 28.6 mol%, with several smaller extrachromosomal elements. C. subdifficilis is genomically highly divergent from the other species in the genus. Its chromosome harbours a monotoxin PaLoc that lacks flanking mobile genetic elements and encodes the highly divergent TcdB11 subtype. Strain supernatants induce a classical cytopathic effect. Cells exhibit a fatty acids profile dominated by saturated fatty acids, with major constituents hexadecanoic acid (C16:0) and octadecanoic acid (C18:0), together with characteristic clostridial iso-branched and dimethyl acetal derivatives, the latter deriving from vinyl ethers. During growth in BHI broth, the fermentation profile is enriched in short-chain, branched-chain volatile fatty acids, notably isocaproate, isovalerate, and 2-methylbutyrate, consistent with Stickland amino-acid fermentation; acetate and valerate are also prominent, whereas alcohols are minor. This species produces moderate levels of p-cresol, albeit lower than those of LIBA-2809. Carbon utilization is narrow and comparatively carbohydrate-poor relative to C. difficile: it preferentially metabolizes a restricted subset of simple carbohydrates, with prominent signals for selected monosaccharides and a few β-glucosides/polyols. It utilizes L-leucine, L-isoleucine, and dipeptides such as glycyl-L-proline more efficiently than C. difficile, and displays elevated use of tagatose, fructose, glucosamine, and small organic acids. Conversely, it exhibits minimal or no growth on hallmark C. difficile substrates, including D-trehalose, D-melezitose, arbutin, salicin, D-ribose, and D-arabitol. The type strain is LIBA-6622T (=DSM 113156T, JCM38293T), isolated from the stool of a Costa Rican patient with diarrhea.

Discussion

Our data support the recognition of three novel Clostridioides species, each defined by distinct genomic, proteomic, and metabolic signatures. These findings expand the known diversity within this clinically relevant genus and expose limitations of routine culture and diagnostics for cryptic lineages [49,50].

The emergence of previously unrecognized enteric pathogens mirrors trends in other taxa (e.g. re-classification within Escherichia and Aeromonas), where atypical virulence profiles and fastidious growth have masked etiologic roles [51,52]. Two processes plausibly underlie speciation within Clostridioides. First, we observe pronounced genomic plasticity, including species-specific gene clusters linked to mobile elements. Second, divergent metabolic capacities indicate ecological specialization shaped by resource availability and microbial competition [53]. Although C. difficile constitutes only part of the TcdB-producing Clostridioides radiation, it appears particularly adapted to human colonization via efficient utilization of branched-chain amino acids and selected carbohydrates [54,55], together with specific TcdB subtypes [56]. By contrast, the novel species combine alternative toxin architectures and trophic strategies; notably, we detect tcdB and CDT carried on putative conjugative plasmids in some strains, a configuration compatible with co-transfer and coordinated regulation that could facilitate adaptation [35].

Proteomic profiles of spore preparations were enriched for cell-envelope and exported proteins, features that may enhance resilience and transmission in nutrient-limited or extra-host settings [57]. Consistent with their fermentation chemistry, we detected usage of substrates such as glycyl-L-proline and 2,3-butanedione, compatible with Stickland metabolism and auxiliary redox balancing via butanediol pathways, which can mitigate acid stress [48]. While mechanistic linkage requires additional targeted experiments, the convergence of proteomic and metabolomic data suggests coordinated ecological strategies.

All three species share physiological traits indicative of intestinal adaptation, including strict anaerobiosis, sporulation, amino-acid–based energy metabolism, and production of short- and branched-chain volatile fatty acids. Despite this shared background, they also show metabolic divergence. C. divergens retains moderate saccharolytic potential, consistent with a facultatively proteolytic–saccharolytic lifestyle. C. cryptodifficilis is strongly proteolytic and produces large amounts of p-cresol, a phenolic antimicrobial compound that may confer a competitive advantage during dysbiosis. C. subdifficilis exhibits the narrowest carbohydrate repertoire and the strongest amino-acid dependency, supporting specialization for late-stage fermentation in protein-rich, low-sugar niches (e.g. decaying host material or post-inflammatory residues). Together, these patterns indicate a progressive shift from mixed sugar–amino-acid metabolism (C. divergens) toward strict amino-acid fermentation (C. subdifficilis), consistent with adaptive radiation into distinct trophic niches within the human gut.

Under matched growth conditions and equivalent cell numbers, the absence of a detectable western blot signal in LIBA supernatants, in contrast to R20291, suggests reduced production of immunodetectable TcdB by the novel strains, although lower antibody recognition of their divergent toxin variants cannot be ruled out. Supernatants from the new species were consistently less cytotoxic than those from C. difficile, which could reflect differences in toxin expression, secretion, or stability [58,59], and suggests pathogenic strategies that rely on lower toxin activity in combination with other colonization factors.

We identified secreted and surface-associated proteins as candidate diagnostic biomarkers. Their reproducible detection under standard laboratory conditions argues for feasibility in immunoassays and MS-based workflows, with potential utility where molecular diagnostics are limited.

Limitations include the modest number of isolates, single-centre origin, and restricted virulence testing. Broader geographic sampling, external validation with independent collections, longitudinal epidemiology, and mechanistic studies (e.g. germ-free or antibiotic-treated models, human organoids) are needed to refine host range and clinical significance.

Our findings also suggest substantial genetic variability and raise the possibility of additional, yet-undiscovered toxigenic lineages. Expanded metagenomic surveys, along with targeted enrichment strategies, may improve detection and characterization of these elusive taxa.

In sum, our polyphasic analysis reshapes the taxonomic and functional landscape of Clostridioides, revealing a more diverse and dynamic genus than previously recognized. Incorporating species diversity beyond C. difficile into diagnostic frameworks and surveillance will improve attribution of toxin-mediated diarrheal disease and inform prevention and therapy.
