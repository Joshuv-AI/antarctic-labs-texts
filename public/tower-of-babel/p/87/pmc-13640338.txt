How do media compositions, pretreatments, and genotypes affect haploidization in sweet watermelon (Citrullus lanatus var. lanatus) ovary culture?
Authors: Deniz Metin, Ertan Sait Kurtar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, consumer demands are rapidly and continuously evolving, which accelerates the development and release of new cultivars. This dynamic process necessitates the integration of biotechnological approaches that can shorten the duration of plant breeding programs. Among these, the doubled haploid (DH) technique, which enables the rapid production of completely homozygous lines in F1 hybrid breeding programs, has been widely and successfully applied in many plant species. Unfertilized ovary culture is one such method and represents a prominent approach for haploid plant production, particularly in the Cucurbitaceae family. However, reports on its successful application in watermelon remain limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of culture media composition, pretreatments, and genotype on haploid plant production via ovary culture in watermelon. Although gynogenic embryos and in vitro plantlets were successfully obtained from all tested cultivars, the frequency of haploid induction was influenced by the composition of the culture media, pretreatment conditions, and genotype. The highest haploid induction frequency was achieved using culture media supplemented with a GPT mixture (500 mg L⁻¹ L-glutamine, 250 mg L⁻¹ L-proline, and 0.5 mg L⁻¹ thiamine). Among the tested media, M8 (MS + GPT + 1 mg/l 2,4-D + 1 mg/L BAP + 7 g/L Agar + 30 g/L sucrose + pH:5.8) was identified as the most effective, as it supported haploid plant production across all cultivars. Ploidy analysis of 94 regenerated plants revealed that 82 were haploid (n = 11), 9 were diploid (2n = 22), and 3 were mixoploid (n + 2n). Among the cultivars, Pastel F1 produced the highest number of haploid plants (52), followed by Paskal F1 (14), Crimson Sweet (10), and Crimson Tide F1 (6).Keywords: Watermelon, Ovary culture, Haploidization

Background

Citrullus lanatus var. lanatus, a member of the Cucurbitaceae family, is the second most produced vegetable crop worldwide after tomatoes, with China ranking first at 61.5 million tons [1]. Watermelon is widely preferred due to its high water content and rich composition of sugars, vitamins, antioxidants, and other bioactive compounds.

F1 hybrid watermelon cultivars are favored for their high yield, superior quality, and resistance to diseases and pests, while also meeting consumer demands for nutritional value, taste, and flavor. In recent years, doubled-haploid (DH) technology has been increasingly utilized in the development of F1 hybrid vegetable cultivars, including melon, cucumber, pepper, and eggplant [2–5]. This technique significantly shortens the breeding cycle to as little as two years, enhances breeding efficiency by reducing population size, and lowers the overall cost of cultivar development. However, the results obtained from the technique vary depending on the species and even the genotypes; some resistant species and genotypes may not respond. On the other hand, some of the obtained DH lines may not possess the desired agronomic characteristics. In today’s context, where both producer and consumer demands are rapidly evolving, the ability to quickly release new F1 hybrid varieties has become essential. Consequently, the importance of DH technology in modern plant breeding has increased substantially [6].

Classical breeding procedures in the Cucurbitaceae family are time-consuming due to their predominantly open-pollinated nature. In contrast, haploidization techniques have been successfully applied in crops such as melon, cucumber, and summer squash as an alternative to conventional breeding methods. The main objective of doubled haploid (DH) technology is to produce haploid (n) plants through androgenesis, gynogenesis, or parthenogenesis, and subsequently develop fully homozygous doubled haploid lines. In open-pollinated species, obtaining pure lines through traditional breeding requires 8–10 generations; however, DH technology significantly reduces this period to approximately 1–2 years. As a result, F1 hybrid cultivars with desirable agronomic traits can be developed and released in a much shorter time.

Considering species within the Cucurbitaceae family, the irradiated pollen technique has been successfully applied in Cucumis melo, Citrullus lanatus, and Cucurbita pepo, while ovary culture is commonly used in Cucumis sativus for double-haploid production. In addition, several factors, including genotype, cultivation conditions for donor plants, seasonal variations, hormone combinations, pre-treatments, incubation conditions, and culture media composition, significantly influence gynogenic response and haploidy frequency in Cucurbitaceae [7–11]. However, only a limited number of studies have addressed ovary culture in watermelon, and an efficient, large-scale protocol for haploid plant production has not yet been established [12–18].

The study aims to determine the effectiveness of the dihaploidization process, specifically focusing on media compositions, pretreatments, and genotypes in producing pure lines of watermelon through unfertilized ovary culture. The ultimate goal is to reduce dependency on external seed suppliers by developing high-yielding, high-quality domestic cultivars that are resistant to diseases and pests. Additionally, the study seeks to contribute to the development of new, superior F1 hybrid watermelon cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Four commercial watermelon cultivars (Pastel F1, Paskal F1, Crimson Sweet, and Crimson Tide F1) were planted as donors. Pastel F1 was obtained from Petektar Seed Company, Paskal F1 from Fito Seed Company, Crimson Sweet from Asgen Seed Company, and Crimson Tide F1 from Syngenta Seed Company.

Experimental area and crop management practices

The experiment was conducted under controlled conditions in a plastic-covered heated greenhouse, a growth chamber, and a tissue culture laboratory in the 2024 spring season (March to June). Seeds were sown in 140 cm³ cell trays filled with peat moss. Planting rows were covered with black plastic mulch, and 40 seedlings from each cultivar were transplanted at a spacing of 60 × 90 cm. Based on soil analysis, fertilizers were applied via drip irrigation throughout the growing season at rates of 8.5 kg da− 1 nitrogen (N), 9.5 kg da− 1 potassium (K), and 5.5 kg da− 1 phosphorus (P) [19]. Plants were regularly pruned to promote the development of fresh female flower buds. Disease and pest control were managed through the application of systemic fungicides and pesticides.

Preparation of ovaries

Female flower buds were collected from cultivars one day before anthesis between 07:00 and 09:00 AM and immediately transferred to the laboratory. The ovaries were first rinsed under running tap water for 15 min, then surface-sterilized in 15% commercial bleach solution (containing ~ 5% NaOCl) for 20 min. Following sterilization, the ovaries were rinsed three times with sterile distilled water for 5 min each. Excess surface moisture was removed by placing the ovaries on sterile paper towels.

Media compositions and treatments

The composition of culture media and treatment regimes was optimized based on previously established protocols for watermelon [12–18, 20, 21], cucumber [10, 22], winter squash [23], pumpkin, and summer squash [12, 24–26]. To enhance embryogenic response, in addition to conventionally utilized basal media, Murashige and Skoog medium (MS) [27] was supplemented with a GPT additive complex consisting of 500 mg L⁻¹ L-glutamine, 250 mg L⁻¹ L-proline, and 0.5 mg L⁻¹ thiamine. This modification was informed by our preliminary experimental data demonstrating improved embryogenic competence. Treatment conditions were further refined while retaining the core compositional framework of previously reported media formulations (Table 1).

Ovary culture

The induction media were sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C and 1.1 atm for 15 min. Following cooling, 8 mL aliquots of the medium were aseptically dispensed into sterile disposable petri dishes (6 × 1.5 cm) under a laminar flow cabinet. Ovaries were sectioned into slices approximately 1–2 mm in thickness. Subsequently, five to eight ovary segments were placed in each petri dish. The dishes were then sealed with sterile stretch film to prevent contamination.

Induction of callogenesis, embryogenesis, and plantlet formation

Heat shock was applied to the cultures as a pretreatment at 35 °C for 2 or 4 days in the dark. Subsequently, the cultures were transferred to dark or light conditions for varying days. At the end of these periods, ovary slices were incubated in the growth chamber at 26 ± 1 ◦ C with a light intensity of 22 µmol m -2 s -1 under a 16 h/8 h photoperiod (Table 1; Fig. 1). Once the ovary slices had differentiated (Fig. 2A, B) and developed into embryogenic calli (Fig. 2C, D), they were transferred to 300 ml culture vessels containing 40 ml of medium every 3 weeks. Subcultures were maintained in the same growth chamber conditions. For plantlet induction, the cultures were refreshed three times at three-week intervals (Fig. 2E, F).

Transformation into plants and ploidy analysis

The plantlets, approximately 1 cm in length, were first transferred to culture tubes containing 10 ml of regeneration media (Fig. 3A). As they developed roots and grew longer, they were transferred to larger culture vessels so they could continue growing into complete plants (Fig. 3B and C). Once the plants were well developed, they underwent acclimatization following the method described by Kurtar and Balkaya [23] (Fig. 3D and E).

Root and shoot development (A and B); complete plants (C); acclimatization (D and E)

The ploidy level was determined using chloroplast counts in stomatal guard cells and flow cytometry (FCM). To assess chloroplast number, the third and fourth leaves from the shoot apex were examined [9].

For FCM analysis, around 50 mg of fresh leaves of watermelon seedlings and 50 mg of the fresh leaves of rapeseed seedlings (the internal reference standard) were placed in a Petri dish containing 1.5 mL of a seed isolation liquid placed above an ice-filled box. The leaf tissue was chopped into small strips using a razor blade. Each seed sample solution was passed through a nylon filter and stained with 5 µL of the 1 mg/L prepared propidium iodide stock solution. Nuclear DNA content in samples was analyzed with a Cell Lab Quanta SC flow cytometer (Beckman-Coulter). The ploidy levels of watermelons were determined by comparing the nuclear DNA content in samples with that of the internal reference. For chloroplast numbers, counts were performed on 10 stomata in three different areas of each of the two leaves per plant.

Statistical evaluations

Because the number of explants and replicates used in the study was unequal, statistical analysis could not be performed; therefore, evaluations were based solely on the averages of the obtained data. The following parameters were assessed: cultured ovary slices (CS), differentiated ovary slices (DS), differentiation rate (DR, %), callus-forming ovary slices (CFS), callus formation rate (CFR, %), calli with embryo-like structures (CES), embryogenic callus rate (ECR, %), regenerated plantlets (RP), regeneration rate (RR, %), and haploidization efficiency (HE; haploid plant per ovary slices).

Results

Induction of callus, differentiation, maturation, and embryogenesis

During the third and fourth weeks of culture, differentiation and color changes were observed in the ovary slices across all cultivars and media. In contrast, the control treatment (M0) showed a low differentiated ovary slices (DR) of 25.0%. Variations were detected among cultivars and induction media in terms of callus-forming ovary slices (CFS) and callus formation rate (CFR, %). The CFR ranged from 27.8% (Pastel F1 in M4) to 100%. However, no callogenic response was observed in M5, M6, and M7 for Crimson Tide F1, and in M7 for Crimson Sweet (Table 2). Additionally, although some ovary slices in the control (M0) showed differentiation, they did not develop into a callus.

Relatively higher CFS values were detected in the cultivars Pastel F1 in M3 (19), Paskal F1 in M1 and M5 (15 and 16), Crimson Sweet in M10 and M1 (19 and 21), and Crimson Tide F1 in M4 and M2 (15 and 19); however, these calli did not produce CES. Embryo development and plantlet formation

From 673 cultured ovary slices, a total of 517 calli were obtained; however, only 46 of these developed into embryogenic calli. The calli exhibiting embryogenic structures were transferred to embryo development and plantlet formation media between the 11th and 13th weeks of culture. Plantlets subsequently emerged from the embryogenic calli during the 14th to 16th weeks (Table 1). Although responses varied among cultivars, regenerated plantlets (RP) were observed on M2, M7, M8, M9, and M10 media.

All regenerated plantlets were obtained from media supplemented with the GPT mixture (500 mg L⁻¹ L-glutamine, 250 mg L⁻¹ L-proline, and 0.5 mg L⁻¹ thiamine). Among the tested media, M8 supported regeneration in all varieties and proved to be the most productive medium. In total, the Pastel F1 cultivar showed the highest regeneration efficiency, producing 56 plants from 12 embryogenic calli. In contrast, Crimson Tide F1 exhibited the lowest response, with only 9 plants regenerated from 8 embryogenic calli. Overall, a total of 94 plants were obtained from 46 embryogenic calli.

Transformation into plants and ploidy analysis

In ovary culture, most of the plantlets transformed into mature plants. The plants that reached approximately 1 cm in length were cultured in regeneration media. The plantlets turned into complete plants within 2 or 4 weeks of culture. A total of 97 plantlets underwent the acclimatization process; 94 of these were successfully adapted to external conditions and used for ploidy analysis. The survival rate following acclimatization was 97%. The ploidy status of plants was determined by both counting the chloroplast number of guard cells and flow cytometry (FCM). While haploid (n = 11) plants have 6–8 chloroplasts in the stomata of guard cells, it was 10–12 chloroplasts in diploid (2n = 22) plants. Both haploid and diploid stomatal structures have been observed in mixoploid (n + 2n) plants (Fig. 4). The FCM histograms of diploid and haploid plants are presented in Fig. 5.

Ploidy analysis of 94 plants revealed that 82 were haploid, 9 were diploid, and 3 were mixoploid. Among the genotypes, Pastel F1 produced the highest number of haploid plants (52), followed by Paskal F1 (14), Crimson Sweet (10), and Crimson Tide F1 (6) (Table 2). Haploidy efficiency (HE), calculated based on the number of cultured ovaries, varied notably among genotypes. The highest HE was observed in Pastel F1 (0.96), whereas the lowest was recorded in Crimson Tide F1 (0.09). Haploid plants were successfully regenerated from M7, M8, and M9 media, with M8 proving to be the most productive. Examination of the media compositions revealed that all effective media contained a combination of GPT along with varying concentrations of 2,4-D and BAP.

Discussion

Ovary culture represents a valuable approach within the double-haploidization (DH) process, offering a time-efficient strategy for the development of F1 hybrid vegetable cultivars. Considerable progress has been achieved in optimizing ovary culture protocols for several cucurbit species, including Cucumis melo L., Cucumis sativus L., Cucurbita pepo L., Cucurbita maxima Duch., and Cucurbita moschata Duch. However, in sweet watermelon (Citrullus lanatus var. lanatus), the outcomes remain variable and insufficiently characterized. Although Kara et al. [18] reported the successful induction of haploid plants via ovary culture in watermelon, the absence of a standardized and genotype-independent protocol constitutes a major limitation. This gap has provided the primary rationale for the present study. Therefore, the effectiveness of different medium compositions and pretreatments on DH production in watermelon via ovary culture was investigated. Although limited studies have been conducted on ovary culture in sweet watermelon, our findings contribute to the understanding of this process in other species of the Cucurbitaceae family.

The induction of embryogenesis and plant regeneration in ovary culture was critically dependent on both genotype and culture medium composition. Considering the average values, different responses were observed among the tested media, with some formulations promoting high embryogenic competence, while others exhibited minimal or no morphogenic response. Callus induction and subsequent differentiation rates differed markedly across cultivars, indicating strong genotype-dependent effects. Furthermore, the media formulations demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in their capacity to support embryogenesis and plantlet regeneration, with certain treatments resulting in comparatively low frequencies of embryo formation and shoot initiation. These outcomes suggest that embryogenic induction and plantlet development are highly regulated by the qualitative and quantitative balance of nutrients and plant growth regulators within the culture medium.

MS [27] media supplemented with different amounts of 2,4-D, NAA, BAP, KN, and TDZ [11, 14–18, 29, 30], putrescine, and spermidine [31] have been used for the induction of gynogenic response in the watermelon. Furthermore, transcriptome-based studies have revealed that heat shock promotes the development of the unfertilized ovules by inducing the biosynthesis of amino acids, suggesting the need to add certain amino acids to the growing medium in watermelon ovary cultures [32]. Indeed, promising results have been reported in cucurbit ovary culture using amino acids such as proline, serine, and glutamine [33]. It has been shown that amino acids are the precursors of protein biosynthesis, that heat shock can activate the ABA signaling pathway, and that it can promote the accumulation of storage substances such as amino acids and unsaturated fatty acids in ovary culture [34].

Although all media influenced the differentiation of cultured ovaries, callogenesis did not occur at a comparable rate across treatments. Our findings revealed that the responses to callogenesis and embryogenesis varied among the culture media and genotypes tested. While some culture media and genotypes exhibited favorable responses, others showed little or no response under the experimental conditions. Callus formation was observed in all media for the Pastel F1 and Pascal F1 cultivars; however, it was absent in Crimson Sweet on M7 and in Crimson Tide F1 on M5, M6, and M7. Embryogenic capacity rate (ECR, %) and regeneration potential (RP) were identified as key indicators of ovary culture fecundity. Regarding embryogenesis, only media M2 and M8 induced embryonic structures in all cultivars. The highest embryogenic capacity (36.6%) was recorded in M9 media for the Crimson Sweet cultivar. Despite these findings, no relationship was detected between the embryogenic callus ratio and the number of regenerated plants.

Although embryogenic calli were successfully obtained, plantlet regeneration was not achieved in the M1 medium. In this study, embryo development and plantlet regeneration were achieved in all culture media supplemented with the GPT mixture. In the Crimson Sweet cultivar, only 11 plants were regenerated from the M9 medium, despite it producing the highest number of embryogenic structures. In contrast, the Pastel F1 cultivar showed the highest regeneration efficiency, with 1, 24 and 31 plantlets regenerated from the M10, M7 and M8 media, which produced 1, 3 and 5 embryogenic calli, respectively. Notably, M8 was the only medium that enabled the production of haploid plants across all varieties.

When comparing cultivars, Pastel F1 showed high fecundity, whereas Crimson Tide F1 exhibited a lower regeneration rate and was more recalcitrant. These findings are consistent with previous reports in Citrullus lanatus [12, 17–20, 21, 30–32, 35–38]. The influence of genotype and induction medium on ovary culture in Cucurbitaceae has also been documented in cucumber [10, 21, 29, 39], melon [7, 40, 41], summer squash [42], and winter squash [9, 25, 28].

The number of guard cells and FCM analyses were used to determine the ploidy status, and these techniques were found to be effective in watermelon. Thus, the counting of chloroplast numbers was a quick, reliable, and efficient method. The results of the ploidy analyses, diploid, haploid, and mixoploid plants, were determined. Diploid plants can form diploid ovary tissues or undergo spontaneous double-haploidization. Spontaneous double-haploids are valuable for the dihaploidisation process because they do not need chromosome doubling [9, 29]. The production of mixoploid plants that subsequently spontaneously doubled into double-haploid (2n) lines in dihaploidization breeding was found to be noteworthy.

The present study reports a significant advancement in the production of haploid plants via ovary culture in watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) cultivars. The regenerated haploid plants constitute valuable genetic resources for future breeding programs, particularly for the development of superior F1 hybrid cultivars. Molecular analyses are required to determine whether plants identified as diploid are spontaneous doubled haploids (DHs) or originate from diploid tissues. Diploid plants confirmed as DHs through these analyses can be directly incorporated into breeding programs. Furthermore, the findings indicate that the supplementation of the induction medium with specific amino acids, namely proline and glutamine, in combination with thiamine, enhances the frequency of haploid induction in watermelon ovary cultures. These results highlight the potential of optimizing culture media composition to improve the efficiency of haploidization techniques in watermelon breeding.

Conclusions

Doubled haploid (DH) technology is used to rapidly produce homozygous, fertile lines, particularly in open-pollinated watermelon. This study reports the successful induction of plantlets through ovary culture in watermelon, a species considered recalcitrant to such techniques. The results showed that the success of ovary culture is highly dependent on both genotype and the composition of the culture medium. Notably, media supplemented with GPT enhanced callus formation (callogenesis), embryo development (embryogenesis), and plantlet regeneration. In conclusion, future studies should focus on optimizing induction media by supplementing them with polyamines (spermidine, spermine, and putrescine), selected amino acids (glutamine, proline, arginine, alanine, and glycine), and novel plant growth regulators such as brassinosteroids, jasmonates, karrikinolide, triacontanol, and meta-topolin at varying concentrations. Additionally, factors including the growing season and culture conditions of donor plants, ovary pretreatments, and subculture frequency should be systematically evaluated to enhance gynogenic responses in recalcitrant watermelon lines.
