Outcomes of patients with rectal cancer and pathological complete response as well as patients with tumor cell dedifferentiation. Two prognostic different groups of patients responding to pre-operative oncological therapy
Authors: Tomáš Řezáč, Pavel Zbořil, Dušan Klos, Radek Vrba, Petr Špička, Lubomír Starý
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMultimodal treatment represents the standard of care for rectal adenocarcinoma. A subset of patients achieves a pathological complete response (pCR), while a different subset does not. While pCR is associated with improved overall survival (OS), non-responders suffer from not being treated in a way tailored to their needs and have worse overall survival.PurposeTo evaluate the factors responsible for pCR and tumor cell dedifferentiation. Additionally, the prognosis of patients with pCR and tumor cell dedifferentiation and the clinical impact of dedifferentiation.ResultsThe factor responsible for pCR was clinically negative lymph node status (cN0). The incidence of dedifferentiation of tumor cells was statistically significantly higher in the group of patients who were not treated with FOLFOX/FOLFIRI chemotherapy (p = 0.033), had cN1 lymph node status (p = 0.010), and primary tumor grade 2 (p < 0.0001). The clinical impact demonstrated an association between dedifferentiation and more advanced pT (p = 0.026) and pTNM stage (p = 0.035). Parameters identified as significant negative predictors of survival were: generalization (p = 0.01, HR 1.959, 95%CI 1.171–3.276), tumor budding (p = 0.001, HR 3.391, 95%CI 1.611–7.136), perineural spread (p = 0.015, HR 2.056, 95%CI 1.150–3. 676), number of positive lymph nodes (p = 0.047, HR 1.100, 95%CI 1.001–1.210), definitive pN (pN1 p = 0.003, HR 2.008, 95%CI 1.268–3.180), and stage III and IV (p = 0.037, HR 2.050, 95%CI 1.044–4.025, p = 0.001, HR 5.482, 95%CI 2.063–14.56).ConclusionThe genetics and biological characteristics of cancer cells make rectal cancer more challenging to treat. This shows why patients need personalized therapy.Keywords: neoadjuvant therapy, pathologic complete responce, cell dedifferentiation, rectal neoplasm - prognosis

Introduction

Several predictive factors for pathological complete response (pCR) have been identified in recent studies. These include circumferential tumor extent rate (≤ 2/3 cycle vs. > 2/3 cycle, P < 0.001, OR = 4.834, 95% CI: 2.309–10.121). Furthermore, the carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level was identified as a significant predictor of pathological complete response (pCR) in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer. Specifically, a CEA level ≤ 5 was associated with a higher pCR rate than a CEA level > 5 (P=0.033, OR = 1.537, 95% CI: 1.035–2.281). Furthermore, the interval between the end of chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and surgery was identified as a significant predictor of pCR. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that an interval of ≥ 8 weeks before surgery (P=0.031, OR = 2.412, 95% CI: 1.086–5.358) was a predictive factor [1]. Additionally, the new predictive models of molecular biomarkers (p53, p21, Ki-67, and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)) have been demonstrated to be correlated with pCR for locally advanced rectal cancer, thereby reinforcing the predictive efficacy of tumor response for preoperative chemoradiotherapy [2]. Even a genotype comprising 15 genes for predicting pCR had been established with whole exome sequencing, demonstrating superior sensitivity and specificity in pCR prediction compared to other clinical features [3]. A review of the available literature did not reveal a correlation between tumor dedifferentiation and rectal adenocarcinoma following radiotherapy.

Dedifferentiation is a crucial mechanism of tumor plasticity, allowing cancer cells to become more adaptable, reverting to a more immature, stem-like state, increasing their ability to proliferate, invade, resist drugs, and evade the immune system. This process involves changes in gene expression, leading to high plasticity, tumor heterogeneity, and a more aggressive phenotype, contributing to treatment resistance and metastasis.

The primary aim of our study was to evaluate the factors responsible for pCR and tumor cell dedifferentiation, i.e., the change in glandular differentiation in tumors before and after cancer treatment. The secondary objective was to assess the prognosis of patients with pCR and tumor cell dedifferentiation and the clinical impact of dedifferentiation.

Method

The study design

A retrospective analysis was conducted using our computerized institutional registry. Inclusion criteria: patients who underwent elective curative rectal resection for a tumor after neoadjuvant radiochemotherapy at University Hospital Olomouc, a complex oncological center, from January 2015 to December 2020. These patients were followed up in our hospital, either in the surgical or oncological department.

Exclusion criteria: no preoperative radiochemotherapy, postoperative radiochemotherapy, immunotherapy, no primary tumor grade assessment, rectal tumor more than 12 cm from the anal verge, generalization of the disease, local surgery, amputation, presence of an anastomotic leak, and only colo/ileostomy creation.

Subsequently, the medical records of all patients who met the inclusion criteria were subjected to a comprehensive review, after which their data was uploaded into an Open Science Framework.

Settings

A five-year retrospective study was conducted on prospectively registered consecutive patients who underwent elective rectal resection following neoadjuvant radiochemotherapy.

Participants

The study followed the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The patients were duly informed and consented to the utilization of their data.

Procedure

Experienced coloproctologists performed surgical procedures with over a decade of experience in colorectal cancer surgery. The surgical procedure was an anterior low resection with a stapled anastomosis, with or without a protective loop ileostomy.

The cancer treatment regimen comprised a combination of radiotherapy and chemotherapy. The radiotherapy consisted of a long course (45 Gy plus 5.4 Gy, 28 fractions, 1.8 Gy per fraction) or a short course (5 × 5 Gy). Chemotherapy consisted of a regimen with 5-fluorouracil or FOLFOX/FOLFIRI (5-FU, oxaliplatin, and leucovorin) administered intravenously or capecitabine at 1650 mg/m2 for ambulatory treatment.

Primary histology was obtained by biopsy during endoscopy as part of the staging of the disease. Dedifferentiation was assessed as the change in glandular differentiation in tumors by comparing primary and definitive histology.

Variables

The following clinical data were collected:

The patient’s background was recorded, including age, sex, distance of the tumor, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, presence of other tumors, autoimmune disease, hypertension, cardiac, thyroid gland, pulmonary, kidney, and hematological disease, date of primary operation, date of death, length of hospitalization.

Tumor background: clinical TNM, pathological TNM, comparison of TNM stage, type of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, change in tumor grading, tumor regression grade by Mandard, presence of tumor mutation, recurrence, and generalization, presence of a pathological complete response, number and lymphatic node positivity.

Statistical methods

IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 statistical software (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) was used for data analysis. Quantitative parameters were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare qualitative parameters. Survival analysis was performed through Kaplan–Meier statistics. Logistic regressions correlated each variable with the outcomes defined as overall survival. Variables considered clinically relevant were selected for multivariable logistic regression. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All tests were done at a 0.05 level of significance.

Main results

A pathological complete response was observed in 12 patients (11.43%) within our cohort. All patients exhibited a primary grade of cT3, while five demonstrated a primary grade of cN1. In the subsequent follow-up period, three patients exhibited disease recurrence. Two cases involved local recurrence, one of which was managed with amputation, while the other patient declined amputation. The third case involved generalized disease (25.0%). In comparison, in the rest of the cohort the recurrence rate was 29.0%, 7 patients had locoregional recurrence and 20 had generalization.

Discussion

In patients with adenocarcinoma of the rectum who undergo neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and surgical resection, pCR is associated with excellent long-term DFS and OS, allowing a significant number of patients to survive for a considerable period of time (often more than 10 years) without evidence of disease recurrence. Sell [4] reported a total of 13.5% of patients in his series who achieved pCR; these results were virtually identical to those observed in our study. However, recurrences occurred in only 2% of patients (one patient had distant metastases), which is in sharp contrast to our cohort, where recurrences occurred in 25% of patients (three of 12 patients), and over a shorter time period. We have no satisfactory explanation for this difference, although all resections had tumor-negative margins, and tumor generalization was excluded before surgery and cancer treatment.

Further elucidation may be provided by Chen’s [5] work.

He proposed that routine histopathology may be inadequate for the detection of residual tumor cells. Selected patients who had a pCR on histology underwent DNA extraction from these samples, which were then analysed for the presence of tumor-specific p53 and K-ras mutations using standard and highly sensitive polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based techniques. The authors identified molecular evidence of residual tumor cells in two of the 26 tumor specimens that had previously been reported to demonstrate a histological complete response.

A number of studies have now identified positive predictive factors for pCR, as well as clinically relevant factors associated with pCR.

Quing in his multivariate analysis revealed that circumferential tumor extent rate (≤ 2/3cycle vs. > 2/ 3 cycle, p < 0.001, OR = 4.834, 95% CI: 2.309–10.121), carcinoembryonic antigen level (both ≤ 5 vs. pre > 5 and post ≤ 5 vs. both > 5, p=0.033, OR = 1.537, 95% CI: 1.035–2.281), and interval time between the end of chemoradiotherapy and surgery (p = 0.31, OR = 2.412, 95% CI: 1.086–5.358) were predictive factors for pCR [1]. T2 clinical classification and N0 statement before treatment were independent predictive factors of pathologic complete response (p < 0.001 and p = 0.001).

As the clinical factors associated with a complete tumor response, the following were identified: tumor mobility in digital rectal examination (P = 0.047), limited parietal invasion (P = 0.001), clinically negative lymph node (P < 0.001) and a circumferential extent greater than 50% (P = 0.001).

Conversely, a T4 classification and an endoscopic tumor size exceeding 6 cm were associated with a lack of response to treatment (P = 0.049 and P = 0.017, respectively). In the context of a multivariate analysis, a delayed surgical procedure after a period of 12 weeks was also identified as a factor associated with a lack of response to treatment (P = 0.001) [6].

In our setup, only clinically negative lymph node status was confirmed as a positive clinical factor associated with pCR (p = 0.018). None of the other parameters analyzed reached statistical significance, age (p = 0.613), body mass index (p = 0.316), autoimmune disease (p = 1.0), smoking status (p = 1.0), alcohol consumption (p = 0.283), clinical T status (p = 0.392), or grade level (p = 0.360). Type of radiotherapy (RT) or chemotherapy was also without significant results, short-course RT (p = 0.507), long-course RT (p = 0.750), chemotherapy with 5-fluorouracil only (p = 0.209), capecitabine (p = 0.766), and FOLFOX (leucovorin+fluorouracil+oxaliplatin, p = 0.687). Since the time interval between the end of radiochemotherapy and definitive surgery does not exceed 2 months and is currently regularly around 1 month, we did not include this parameter in the statistical evaluation.

Conversely, the genetic and biological behaviour of cancer cells, which are not affected by standard preoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy, represents a further area of investigation. A topic that has been the subject of considerable debate in recent times and is becoming increasingly prominent is that of targeted preoperative therapy.

For example, we see that not all cancer cells respond the same to chemotherapy. Yoon described the clinical influence of cancer stem cells (CSCs) on residual disease after preoperative chemoradiotherapy in patients with rectal cancer. To identify CSCs, immunohistochemistry was performed using markers CD44 and aldehyde dehydrogenase 1. ALDH1 and CD44 positivity was found in 80.0% (n = 116) and 47.6% (n = 69), respectively; ALDH1 positivity showed weakly positive correlation with CD44 (rs = 0.269, P = 0.002). ALDH1 and CD44 positivity was related to lower tumor regression grade (TRG) (P = 0.009 and 0.003, respectively). In univariate analysis, ALDH1 positivity was associated with short recurrence-free survival (RFS) (P = 0.005) and rectal cancer specific survival (RCSS) (P = 0.043). In multivariate analysis, ALDH1 positivity was an independent prognostic factor for poor RFS (P = 0.039; hazard ratio = 2.997; 95% confidence interval = 1.059–8.478), but not RCSS (P = 0.571). The expression of ALDH1 assessment independently predicts RFS in patients with residual disease after CRT [7].

Mare and Saigusa [8, 9] obtained similar results. The expression of CD133 was found to be associated with a poorer response.

Furthermore, the issue of tumor cell plasticity, which has been relatively under-discussed in the literature, was the focus of our study. Radiotherapy as a basic treatment modality for rectal tumors can achieve a complete response (11.3% in our series), but on the other hand, it induces changes that lead to more aggressive tumor cells, resulting in a worse grade for 34 patients (32.4%). It is clear that this is a more complex question, but the numbers in our series are more than interesting.

In essence, dedifferentiation, transdifferentiation, and reprogramming represent routes to regeneration [10].

The objective of regenerative medicine is to replace lost or damaged cells. This can be achieved through the utilisation of dedifferentiation, transdifferentiation, or reprogramming processes. Recent advances have demonstrated that the introduction of a specific set of genes can not only reprogram a fully differentiated cell to regain pluripotency, but can also induce the cell to proliferate, or alternatively, switch to a different cell type.

One perspective is the cancer stem cell hypothesis [11], which posits that solid tumors are hierarchically organised with a small number of cancer stem cells that regulate tumor growth, repopulation following damage and metastasis. Subsequently, these cells give rise to differentiated progeny that lack the aforementioned features. The model predicts that for a cure to be achieved, it is necessary to eliminate all cancer stem cells. Conversely, the discovery that non-stem cancer cells display a notable capacity for plasticity, enabling them to reacquire the characteristics of cancer stem cells, particularly in the context of radiotherapy, represents a significant challenge to the cancer stem cell hypothesis.

The initial reports have indicated that radiation has a preferential effect on the killing of non-tumorigenic cells, thereby enriching for CSCs.

The clinical application of radiation therapy in daily fractions of 2 Gy from different directions with the tumor mass at the centre effectively controls tumor growth while sparing normal tissues. Additionally, stromal cells situated within or in close proximity to the tumor, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts, are subjected to substantial radiation doses, which can influence their interactions with cancer cells. While the precise molecular pathways remain to be elucidated, the majority of existing literature collectively indicates that cancer-associated fibroblasts acquire a pro-malignant phenotype following radiation exposure [12, 13].

The modification of the tumor stroma by therapeutic intervention may prove to be a pivotal factor in determining the extent of phenotypic plasticity exhibited by cancer cells in response to specific therapeutic modalities. Ionising radiation generates waves of primary and secondary reactive oxygen species in cells, with the latter persisting for weeks and months after the irradiation event. This may also explain the presence of recurrences in patients with pathological complete responses, in our setup, three patients experienced disease recurrence. Two cases involved local recurrence, the third involved generalized disease (25.0%). Regarding clinical complete response (cCR) and recurrence, available data suggest that patients have a significantly lower risk (≤ 10%) recurrence after 2 years of achieving cCR following nCRT [14]. According to others, this risk is up to 31%. More than half of these recurrences develop within 12 months of follow-up [15].

Late responses to radiation treatment have been demonstrated to affect lipids, proteins and, most importantly, DNA [16]. Such occurrences give rise to a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, characterised by increased expression of TNFα, pro-inflammatory interleukins and interferon-γ [23], which consequently activates downstream STAT3 signalling. This is known to promote self-renewal in embryonic stem cells [17].

The existence of a subpopulation of cancer cells within a tumor that exhibits the functional properties of cancer stem cells has significant implications for the clinical treatment of cancer. The demonstrated inherent chemo- and radioresistance of CSCs, which are responsible for disease relapse, a poorer survival rate and therapeutic resistance to conventional chemo- and radiotherapies, represents a significant challenge to the curability of patients.

Nevertheless, the clinical impact of tumor dedifferentiation in our study demonstrated a correlation with definitive pT (higher grades correlate with higher pT grades) and TNM stage. No association was observed between mutations and overall survival. The results are likely to be influenced by the small number of patients in the cohort, and further investigation would be a valuable contribution to the field.

In conclusion, the issue of the genetic and biological characteristics of the tumor is becoming increasingly prominent in current research, with the potential to significantly influence treatment outcomes. Firstly, in a positive manner, with the same treatment, a pathological complete response is achieved, secondly, in a negative manner, due to plasticity and changes in biological properties, a significant negative impact on overall survival is observed. Limitations of the study include its retrospective design, data heterogeneity, and the small number of participants. In addition, the single-center nature of the research and the fact that patients came from a single geographic region are potential limitations that may contribute to study bias.

Conclusion

The issue of treating rectal cancer seems simple due to the existence of clear guidelines and recommended treatments that are consistent with the stage of the disease. However, closer examination shows that the issue is more complex, with multiple variables that significantly affect the targeted condition. One of these factors is the plasticity of tumour cells, i.e., their ability to change and evolve, which can significantly affect patient´s prognosis.
