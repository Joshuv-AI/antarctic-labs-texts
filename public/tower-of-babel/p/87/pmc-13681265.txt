“Why do I need to meet with a mental health professional?”: a class format as a novel approach to delivering gamete recipient education
Authors: Shelby Alsup, Julia Conant, Julia Woodward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of donor gametes has increased substantially in recent decades. In response to the psychosocial and both short- and long-term implications of third-party reproduction, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) recommends psychoeducational counseling for recipient parents. Yet mental health referrals are often experienced as anxiety-provoking or discriminatory, particularly among LGBTQ+ individuals and those pursuing solo parenting, reflecting longstanding heteronormative bias in reproductive medicine. The ASRM has called for fertility care models that move beyond mere acceptance toward affirming, patient-centered care. This review describes over 15 years of experience implementing a psychoeducational, class-based model for gamete recipients in an academic medical center and, more recently, a private multisite fertility clinic. We present preliminary findings, highlight patient-centered benefits, outline practical implementation strategies, and discuss limitations of this approach. Preliminary quality improvement data were drawn from a 1-year survey of patients at a private multisite fertility practice who were eligible to complete either a class-based consultation with an embedded clinical psychologist or a traditional individual consultation. Overall, the majority of patients opted into the class format and found the class moderately to extremely helpful. Primary reasons for opting into the class included reduced financial burden, interest in community-building, and the perceptions of greater inclusivity. To better align required psychoeducational counseling with principles of inclusivity and patient-centered care, alternative models are needed. A class-based mental health consultation offers a practical and scalable approach that delivers essential education while reducing fears of judgment, fostering community, and improving access to care. Ongoing research is examining the impact of this model on patient knowledge, confidence, and the perceived inclusivity of care across diverse family types.

Introduction: how typical psychoeducational consultation may fail gamete recipients

Because the use of gamete donation is becoming an increasingly common form of family building, consideration of the short- and long-term implications of donor conception has become more critical (1, 2, 3). In 1993, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) issued its first guidance on psychological counseling for recipient parents, recommending that they undergo both psychological evaluation and implications counseling (4). In 2004, ASRM guidelines evolved in two significant ways: they eliminated the recommendation for psychological evaluation of gamete recipients and moved to recommending strongly that patients receive educational implications counseling from a qualified mental health professional (MHP). Given the breadth of issues about which prospective recipient parents must become informed, the latest ASRM guidelines continue to strongly recommend psychoeducational implications counseling for patients using donor gametes (5). Concerns central to parenting a donor-conceived child include coping with the need to use donor gametes; handling queries from family and friends; the necessity of reporting a child’s birth to the gamete bank to ensure limits to the number of families created by the same donor; when and how to begin discussions with the child about their origin story; managing contact between other recipient families who used the same donor or genetically related individuals who grew up in different families; and mechanisms for accessing updated medical information about the donor in the future. Current data suggests that recipient parents desire ongoing support and connection over time (6). Similarly, donor-conceived persons (DCPs) themselves overwhelmingly advocate for psychoeducational consultation as a requirement for recipient parents to provide them with support and critical education about the likely psychosocial needs of their future children (7).

The most common approach currently used by clinics following ASRM guidelines is to refer gamete recipients for an individual consultation with a qualified MHP. These clinical encounters should not involve psychotherapeutic evaluation or treatment, but rather provision of education and support resources. However, patients frequently describe this referral as anxiety-provoking, fearing evaluation of their fitness to parent or scrutiny of their psychological adjustment. This negative reaction is even more pronounced for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer or questioning patients (LGBTQ+) (8, 9) and unmarried patients pursuing solo parenthood (10, 11). Consequently, anecdotal evidence suggests that some clinics, influenced by the confluence of negative patient feedback and a desire for speed and efficiency, have made the MHP psychoeducational consult optional or eliminated it. This shift has led to many recipients opting out of meeting with an MHP before initiating donor conception. As a result, a cohort of recipients is entering into parenthood inadequately informed about issues central to parenting a donor-conceived child. These knowledge gaps can contribute to later patient frustration with the fertility clinic for failing to prepare them for the realities of raising a donor-conceived child.

An elegant and practical solution to this dilemma is to offer the MHP consult for gamete recipients in a class format. The class format can improve access to critical education while minimizing patient concerns about being judged or evaluated by an MHP. Interestingly, a similar approach was evaluated in Europe more than two decades ago, with recipient parents opting into a class out of a desire for more information about family-building with donor gametes and a greater sense of community (12). After the class, patients reported greater confidence in their decision to use donor gametes, which positively influenced their intention to disclose donor origins to their future child (13). Our goal in this review is to share our experience using the class format over the past 15 years in an academic medical center and more recently in a private multisite clinic setting. We will describe potential benefits, particularly for LBGTQ+ and solo patients, share practical implementation strategies, and discuss limitations.

The complex landscape of donor conception

Pursuing donor conception has long-term implications both for parents and their donor-conceived children (2). Recipient parents represent diverse family types, including cisgender heterosexual couples, LGBTQ+ couples, and single people of all gender identities and sexual orientations. Choice to use donor gametes may be driven by an infertility diagnosis, biological need associated with family type, or the desire to avoid passing on a genetic condition. Current data in the United States suggests that the largest proportion of donor sperm is being purchased by LGBTQ+ couples and single people rather than heterosexual couples facing male-factor infertility (14). Anecdotally, LGBTQ+ patients frequently report prior familiarity with donor conception, express feeling uncomfortable or offended by the perceived evaluative nature of the MHP consult, or state that they have already engaged in extensive research related to third-party reproduction given their long-standing awareness of the need for donor gametes.

Given that the mental health consultation recommendation risks disproportionately triggering bias concerns among LGBTQ+ patients, an understanding of the history of discrimination against this group, as well as against single patients, is essential. Until 1987, the field of psychiatry classified homosexuality as a mental health disorder (15). The heteronormative bias within the field of reproductive medicine and the history of discrimination against groups with minoritized identities seeking fertility care is well-documented (9, 16). Early practice in reproductive medicine offered fertility treatment to heterosexual couples only; single people or those in LGBTQ+ relationships were explicitly excluded from care. This history of discrimination contextualizes the mistrust often experienced by these patients and highlights the risk of nonaffirming or inadvertently harmful interactions during fertility treatment (16). The psychoeducational recommendation may activate fears about discrimination based on sexual orientation and may be perceived as a macro or microaggression. In addition, the recommendation may be seen to represent societal norms that have long promoted a two-parent ideal and thus perpetuate stigma against single people seeking to become parents (10, 11). However, even cisgender, heterosexual patients needing donor gametes may experience concern about referral for the psychoeducational consult, believing erroneously that it serves an evaluative or gate-keeping function.

In recognition of concerns about discrimination against minoritized populations, ASRM issued a Committee Opinion in 2024 stating, “It is not enough to just ‘accept’ LGBTQIA+ patients for evaluation and treatment. It is incumbent upon fertility clinics and agencies to ensure that our services are oriented toward the needs of LGBTQIA+ people (17).” Moving toward a more inclusive, affirming model of care better supports patients with diverse family forms and honors patients’ lived experiences (18, 19, 20). Holley and Pasch’s (8) call to action for providers recommends modifying the required treatment steps in third-party family building to better meet the needs of patients with diverse identities.

A novel approach to gamete recipient education

Providing information to recipients of donor gametes in a class format is one such modification of traditional fertility protocols. Classes combine structured didactic education supported by a slide presentation with opportunities for participant discussion, reflection, and sharing as desired. Psychoeducational consultation must be done by a qualified MHP (21) and typically addresses topics such as donor screening protocols; potential donor selection criteria; how, when, and why to share the conception story with the future child; initial formation of positive and affirming family narratives; and expectations for navigating potential relationships with the donor or genetic half-siblings. Counseling also serves to explore recent research findings from the voices of donors, recipients, and DCPs and educate prospective parents about the ways in which norms continue to evolve.

Because the educational needs of recipients vary in specific ways based on family type, classes should be tailored to family type and offered separately. For example, cis-gender/heterosexual sperm recipients may be interested in a more detailed discussion of how to manage grief regarding the loss of genetic connection than same-sex female couples who always knew they would need donor sperm. For queer couples, relevant content may include navigating parenting within a heteronormative landscape and treatment planning decisions such as who will contribute genetic material and/or who will carry the pregnancy. It is critical to recognize that LGBTQIA + patients are likely to arrive at the class with a strong foundational knowledge of the gamete recipiency landscape. At the same time, a qualified MHP can provide expertise in developmental and family psychology frameworks as well as translation of experiential perspectives into clinically informed guidance regarding identity development, family relationships, and long-term psychosocial outcomes. By drawing from a review of international advocacy and scholarship in third-party reproduction, class facilitation by an MHP also broadens the data set available to patients, moving from their individual network to the global community of recipient parents, DCPs, clinicians, and researchers. Options for different class types may include: same-sex female couples using donor sperm (including queer, nonbinary, and trans parents); solo parents by choice or circumstance using donor sperm (including queer, nonbinary, and trans parents); cisgender heterosexual couples using donor eggs or; and cisgender heterosexual couples using donor sperm etc.

Patients should receive guidance about their options for completing the psychoeducational consult, including the choice between a class and an individual consult. Embedding ‟choice” into clinical practice reflects core principles of trauma-informed, inclusive care, wherein patient agency is recognized as fundamental to inclusive and responsive service delivery (22). Because patients can attend the class without being required to provide personal information, it allows the provision of key information and psychosocial informed consent without risking exposure to bias, judgment, or discrimination.

The class may be delivered in person, but with the rise of telemedicine post-COVID, we now deliver classes virtually unless requested otherwise. During class sessions, active participation (e.g., camera on, inviting participants to unmute and provide feedback or ask questions as desired) is encouraged to promote engagement and help establish a safe learning environment. Depending on class size, participants are invited to introduce themselves (and their partner, if applicable), typically sharing name and pronouns, their path to parenthood so far, and/or extent of prior research into gamete recipiency (e.g., “Are you at the beginning of this process or have you done extensive research or selected a donor already?”). The degree of discussion is driven by the class members themselves, some classes involve minimal conversation, and some reflect significant openness, sharing, and a sense of community. Participants lacking a supportive community of others pursuing a similar family-building pathway can also be offered a mechanism to share contact information at the conclusion of the session, if desired. Although used by individuals in each family type, we have found this option to be particularly welcomed by prospective solo parents. After the session, MHPs should provide participants with an after-visit summary of material covered and tailored resources related to family type. Any mental health or other clinical concerns that arose during class should be addressed by the MHP through direct, individualized follow-up to ensure appropriate support and referral. Benefits of the class format

The class model outlined confers a number of potential benefits for patients, clinics, and MHPs alike. Preliminary quality improvement data on potential benefits for fertility patients comes from a survey of patients who opted into the class with an embedded clinical psychologist at a multisite private fertility practice; the alternative was completing a 1:1 consult with an embedded psychologist. Classes offered were tailored to either nonsperm producing same-sex female, nonbinary, transgender, and queer patients or solo patients.

Preliminary analysis conducted over a 1-year period (2025) showed that 75% of eligible patients (N = 196) accessing in-house psychological services opted for the group option instead of individual (1:1) sessions. Patients who chose 1:1 sessions most commonly cited scheduling misalignment with their treatment timeline or a preference to discuss more unique or complex needs (e.g., relationship or family complexity). Additionally, one site within the multisite clinic was not routinely offering the group option in early 2025, which may have limited patient awareness of the class and influenced opt-in rates. Because the cost of class sessions is lower (e.g., $150 for a 90-minute session) than that of individual sessions ($495 for a 75-minute session), the class format reduced financial burden and improved access to care. Patients’ primary reasons for opting into the class in order of ranked preference were: reducing financial barriers; interest in building community and meeting others on a similar family-building path; and viewing the class as more inclusive. Eighty-one percent of all patients rated the class as moderately to extremely helpful. The underlying goals of this model, including increasing inclusivity, fostering community, and reducing financial burden, are supported by these initial findings. Larger studies are underway to assess the impact of the class format on variables such as patients’ knowledge of third-party reproduction, confidence with parenting via donor conception, experience of receiving inclusive, affirming care, and enhanced sense of community, among others. Additionally, future research should examine the unique responses from other family types and/or identities (e.g., heterosexual couples using donor sperm/egg).

The class format can also offer benefits to clinics and MHPs in private practice. The MHPs may have concerns about the economic impact of group consultations; however, the group consultation model can present a meaningful economic opportunity rather than an economic risk for MHPs. A provider billing 10 patients at a private-pay group rate within a single session can generate comparable or greater revenue than a single individual consultation, while simultaneously freeing the appointments that would otherwise have been occupied by those patients individually. Those slots can then be allocated to higher-reimbursement individual therapy or consultation services, effectively increasing overall clinical productivity without extending provider hours. For providers in private practice who maintain a steady referral pipeline (e.g., through partnership arrangements with fertility clinics), this model may function as a sustainable revenue generator rather than a financial compromise. Classes can also reduce bottlenecks in care generated by a large number of gamete recipients being referred for this time-intensive consult. When classes are delivered at regular intervals based on patient volume, patients can be seen quickly and efficiently, a key element in patient satisfaction with care. The MHPs providing comprehensive recipient counseling can teach detailed, lengthy content to more patients without increasing burnout and fatigue. Providers using insurance-based models may be able to bill for these services using group health and behavior intervention CPT codes.

Limitations or challenges of the class format

Although the class format offers multiple potential benefits, it has several limitations. First, it is not suitable for all patients. Individualized consultation is more appropriate for patients using a directed donor, needing decisional support about donor selection (e.g. directed vs. nonidentified donor), or using more than one type of third-party arrangement (e.g., a nonidentified donor and a gestational carrier). Individualized consultation is also more appropriate for those who need an interpreter, a private space to discuss personal treatment-related questions or disclosure concerns, or support for mental health issues during assisted reproduction technology or pregnancy.

Additionally, the class format introduces several operational challenges. First, as with all video visits, patients must be advised to attend from a private location to protect confidentiality. They are encouraged to turn on their camera to support connection, participation, and effective facilitation; microphones may remain muted. Patients consent to bear responsibility for protecting the privacy of other class members, and the limits to which their own privacy can be guaranteed, given the group context. The patient health information (PHI) routinely disclosed by joining a class is first names only, and the awareness that participants are planning to be gamete donor recipients. Patients are counseled and consent that, should they choose to reveal additional personal information during the class, confidentiality cannot be guaranteed. Because patients join via an encrypted video platform, recording features are disabled.

The MHPs also must ensure that individual participants do not dominate the discussion or reveal excessively personal details. Especially when class size becomes large (e.g., more than ∼10 participants), meaningful dialogue can be limited. Because the MHP must simultaneously present didactic material and monitor the virtual room, individualized, real-time observation and assessment of participants’ responses in large classes is limited. As a result, the provider obtains less information about the history or treatment-related needs of individual patients, unless the patient raises them. Although this may be viewed as a limitation, it may be viewed alternatively as enhancing equity, given that fertility patients using their own gametes do not routinely meet with an MHP. It may also be regarded as an acceptable consequence in service of the goals of reducing stigma, broadening access, and decreasing financial burden.

Finally, facilitation of a group service delivery model may not be a fit for all MHPs. Some providers may prefer the depth and personalization of individual consultations, feel less confident in their training or perceived skillset for group facilitation, or simply find that public speaking and managing the dynamics of larger groups are not well aligned with their personal or professional competencies.

We are currently comparing the experience of small class formats (e.g., a maximum of 3 individuals or couples) with larger classes (up to 10 individuals or couples). A consistent clinical norm across chronic illness, behavioral medicine, and cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) group interventions suggests including approximately 6–10 participants per group, with some structured CBT groups extending to approximately 10–12 participants. These limited group sizes support the ability for participants to interact with each other (23). Thus far, we have found that smaller classes are characterized by greater patient engagement and sharing, whereas larger classes allow more rapid scheduling and efficient throughput. Along with evaluation of the class format as an alternative to individual consultation, optimal class size is an empirical question requiring additional study.

Conclusion

Fertility centers currently face a conundrum: how do we properly educate gamete recipients and follow ASRM recommendations for mental health consultation without sacrificing efficiency or generating patient dissatisfaction? Offering the ASRM-recommended mental health consultation for gamete recipients in a class format rather than through an individual meeting is a novel approach for addressing this problem. The class format offers affirming care, may mitigate concerns about evaluation and discrimination, and reduces cost, while ensuring that proper patient education and informed consent are achieved. Formal research is now underway to evaluate these potential benefits. As the needs of patients building their families through donor conception have evolved, so too must the field of reproductive medicine evolve in how we prepare and support these patients.
