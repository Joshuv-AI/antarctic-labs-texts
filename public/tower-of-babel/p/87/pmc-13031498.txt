Demography and behavioral ecology of the Indian crested porcupine (Hystrix indica) in Punjab
Authors: Jiakai Liu, Zhenming Zhang, Muhammad Amjad Yaqoob, Muhammad Husnain Khan, Shahid Hafeez, Fahad Rasheed, Junaid Naseer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the population density, spatial and temporal distribution, courtship behavior, reproductive ecology, and parental care dynamics of Indian crested porcupines (Hystrix indica) across diverse habitats in Punjab, Pakistan, from 2019 to 2021. The median population density across surveyed sites was 3 individuals/km², with higher densities in irrigated forest plantations and sub-mountainous regions compared to agricultural and desert ecosystems. Temporal analysis revealed significant seasonal variation, with peak activity in summer (4.98 sightings/km²) and the lowest in fall (2.04 sightings/km²). Reproductive activity was year-round, with peaks in February (singletons) and July (twins/triplets), likely linked to resource availability. Courtship behavior analysis revealed significant positive correlation between mounting and copulation, with mounting activity peaking in February–April. Parental care behaviors were predominantly performed by both males and females together, followed by females alone, males alone, sub-adults/juveniles, and non-descript members, with significant variability across families (P < 0.001). These findings highlight the influence of seasonal and social factors on porcupine behavior, emphasizing the species’ adaptability to diverse ecological conditions and the collaborative nature of parental care in porcupine families.Keywords: Population distribution, Reproductive behaviour, Social ecology, CopulationSubject terms: Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

The Indian crested porcupine (Hystrix indica) is a distinctive rodent species native to the Indian subcontinent. Its distribution extends from southern Asia and the Middle East, spanning countries from India and Nepal to Iran, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and as far west as Israel1,2. In Pakistan, the species is predominantly found in the northern and central regions of Punjab3. Renowned for its ecological adaptability, H. indica thrives in variety of habitats, including arid deserts, dry thorn forests, and agricultural landscapes3,4. This adaptability is further evidenced in Punjab, where the species occupies environments ranging from arid zones to cultivated areas5. Its ecological significance is underscored by its dual role as primary herbivore and soil modifier, influencing ecosystem dynamics through foraging and burrowing activities6.

The species is morphologically distinguished by its crest of long, stiff quills on the back and tail, which serve as a primary defense mechanism against predators6. These quills, which are modified hairs, can be erected in defensive posture to deter threats. H. indica exhibits nocturnal foraging behavior, strategy that minimizes predation risk and optimizes resource exploitation7. The selection of foraging sites and burrow locations is influenced by ecological factors such as vegetation density, soil composition, and proximity to water sources8. Despite its ecological importance, comprehensive studies on H. indica remain limited, particularly regarding its population dynamics, social structure, and reproductive biology9.

H. indica exhibits social structure organized around small family groups, typically consisting of 1–4 individuals, with monogamy as the predominant mating system10. These family units share common burrow system, which provides stable environment for rearing offspring. Social interactions within these groups are complex, involving vocalizations, scent marking, and physical behaviors that facilitate group cohesion and communication11.

Reproductive patterns in H. indica exhibit regional variability, with some populations displaying seasonal breeding and others reproducing year-round1. Reproductive success is influenced by factors such as maternal health, food availability, and environmental conditions12. In captivity, copulation occurs irrespective of estrus, with frequent mounting observed before and after births, even in the presence of offspring13–16. Gestation lasts approximately three months15,17,18, with both wild and captive populations typically producing 1–2 litters annually, each consisting of 1–3 porcupettes2,15,17,19,20. Captive individuals have occasionally produced litters of four18. In the wild, porcupette presence in burrows is observed year-round, with peaks in February and October2.

Parental care in H. indica is biparental, with both males and females contributing equally to offspring rearing15,21,22. Sub-adults remain with the family group for 1–2 years, dispersing upon reaching sexual maturity15,18. While lactation in captivity lasts 40–50 days, with weaning beginning around three months17,18, the duration of lactation and weaning in wild populations remains undocumented.

This study investigates the population dynamics and reproductive ecology of H. indica in Punjab, with focus on spatial and temporal variations in population density, courtship behavior, and parental care. The specific objectives are: Assess variations in population density across different habitats; Analyze mounting and copulation sequences to determine their independence and role in reproductive success; Identify breeding periods and evaluate potential seasonality in births; Evaluate the involvement of sub-adults in alloparenting, either independently or alongside parents.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

All methods involving animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal research23. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (AEC) of the Department of Forestry and Range Management, Faculty of Agriculture, University of Agriculture, Faisalabad. All procedures involving animals, including field surveys, trapping, and behavioral monitoring, were conducted in accordance with the Punjab Wildlife Protection, Preservation, Conservation, and Management Act (1974). The necessary permissions were obtained under Permit No. 68. All methods involving human participants were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Department of Forestry and Range Management, Faculty of Agriculture, University of Agriculture. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardian(s) prior to their inclusion in the study.

Site selection and geographic scope

This study investigated the distribution of the H. indica across Punjab, Pakistan (27.5°–34.1° N, 69.5°–75.2° E), focusing on four ecological zones: the Rainfed Pothohar Belt (Sub-Mountainous tract), Irrigated Forest Plantations, Link Canal Embankments, and Desert ecosystems. Sites were selected based on habitat diversity, porcupine activity, and environmental conditions, ensuring representative sample of Punjab’s diverse landscape, which includes fertile plains, semi-arid regions, and sparsely populated deserts. GIS tools were used to map survey locations, burrow sites, and observational points, while necessary permits were obtained from authorities (Table 1). The study aimed to assess habitat preferences and distribution patterns in response to environmental and anthropogenic factors.

Climate and topography

The study regions exhibit distinct climatic and topographic characteristics, creating diverse ecological conditions for the Indian crested porcupine. The Rainfed Pothohar Belt in northern Punjab features a semi-arid climate with dry, rocky, and undulating terrain at elevations of 200–1,000 m above sea level. In contrast, the Irrigated Forest Plantations of central Punjab, such as Kundian and Shorkot, are situated at lower elevations (150–300 m) and benefit from extensive irrigation, resulting in temperate microclimate (25–40 °C) and flat to gently undulating landscapes with organized tree plantations. The Desert Ecosystem in the Thal region is characterized by an arid climate, with minimal annual precipitation (< 75 mm), extreme temperatures (15–45 °C), and sandy dunes with sparse vegetation. Conversely, the Embankments of Link Canals, such as the Qadirabad-Ballokey canal area, experience moderated climate (20–40 °C) due to irrigation, providing suitable denning sites amidst cultivated lands. This variation in climate and topography across the study regions supports wide range of ecological conditions for porcupine habitation.

Study period

The study was conducted from January 2019 to December 2021, with the entire study period divided into four distinct seasons: Spring (February–April), Summer (May–August), Fall (September–October), and Winter (November–January). This seasonal division allowed for comprehensive assessment of temporal variations in the observed parameters.

Population distribution and density

To identify probable habitats of H. indica, local residents, hunters, and outdoor enthusiasts were interviewed for anecdotal evidence of sightings, while wildlife experts and prior scientific reports were consulted. Twelve sites were selected (Table 1), and systematic field surveys were conducted by dividing each habitat into 16 quadrants, with one quadrant randomly surveyed monthly during nocturnal hours using visual sightings and spoor tracking. Night vision goggles, laser-guided range finders, and high-intensity torches were employed for transect surveillance. Porcupines were categorized as male, female, or juvenile based on physical characteristics such as size and tail length. Presence was confirmed through distinctive signs, including quills, droppings (brown, cylindrical, 1–2 cm long), gnaw marks, tracks, and burrows (rocky crevices or hollow logs). Population density was calculated for each site, with variations analyzed across location, season, month, and demographics. Additionally, camera traps (Num’axes PIE1009) equipped with passive infrared sensors (PIR) were deployed near burrow entrances and along movement corridors, remaining operational for 4–6 weeks per site. Breeding sites and birthing burrows were prioritized, and both camera traps and direct observations were used to document mating and reproductive. Approximately 70% of population and behavioral data were derived from direct nocturnal visual surveys and spoor-based field observations, while camera trap data contributed approximately 30% of the overall analytical dataset, primarily supporting detection probability modelling and validation of reproductive and behavioral events. This integrated approach provided comprehensive insights into the species’ distribution, behavior, and ecology.

Trapping and capturing

Porcupines were captured using wire mesh cages (110 × 42 × 42 cm) with double entrances, baited with corn and potatoes24. Upon capture, individuals were anesthetized with tiletamine-zolazepam (Zoletil®) at dosage of 4–6 mg/kg, weighed, sexed, and marked for identification. Marking methods included colored adhesive tapes applied to quills and non-toxic, water-based white paint sprayed on the crest or tail, or combination of both. The adhesive tapes, naturally shed with quills, remained visible for up to a year, while the paint persisted for approximately 20 days under dry conditions.

Behavioral ecology and reproductive dynamics

Behavioral patterns, including social bonding, family structure, aggression, courtship, mounting behavior, and successful copulation, were systematically categorized and analyzed. Mounting was scored when one individual assumed dorsal-climbing position without evidence of insemination (< 10 s). Copulation was scored only upon observation of intromission, rhythmic thrusting, and terminal behavioral climax (ejaculatory pause), lasting ≥ 10 s. Pregnant females from selected family groups were closely monitored to document gestation duration, birthing burrow conditions, and neonatal development. Data on offspring growth rates, weaning, and survival were also recorded. Seasonal patterns were evaluated to assess their impact on reproductive cycles, enabling the identification of variations in birth rates and reproductive success across different study sites. This approach provided critical insights into the species’ behavioral ecology and reproductive dynamics.

Statistical analysis

Porcupine population density and the effects of location, month, season, and habitat on population dynamics were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Demographic variables were compared within groups using two-way ANOVA. The frequency of single versus multiple mountings was assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Trends in Porcupette births (2019–2021) and parental care among porcupine family (clan) members were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. All statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism (Version 8.4.3), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Population density

The median density of Indian crested porcupines across all surveillance sites and seasons were 3 (0–9 range) individuals/km². Sites 1–4 (Changa Manga Forest Plantation, Lal Suhanra National Park, Kundian Forest Plantation, and Daphar and Pakhowal Forest Plantation), characterized by irrigated forest plantations, and were surveyed in 2019. Sites 5–8 (Shorkot Forest Plantation, Cheechawatni Forest Plantation, Kathar, and Kheri Murat and Kali Dauli), encompassing irrigated forest plantations and sub-mountainous tracts, were surveyed in 2020. Sites 9–12 (Rakh Goharwala, Rakh Choubara, Peri-urban Faisalabad, and Qadirabad), representing desert ecosystems, agricultural areas, and link canal ecosystems, were surveyed in 2021. No significant difference in porcupine sightings was observed across the three years (P = 0.331). However, a relatively higher mean density (3.875 individuals/km²) was recorded in 2020 compared to other years.

Spatial variation

Porcupine sightings did not exhibit significant variation across the surveyed sites during the study period (Fig. 1A). The mean sightings ranged from 2.58 ± 0.79 at Changa Manga Forest Plantation to 4.67 ± 3.03 at Shorkot Forest Plantation. Notably, both sites are classified as irrigated forest plantations, suggesting broad distribution of porcupines across similar habitat types. The effect of habitat type on porcupine sightings was also non-significant (P = 0.870). However, the highest mean porcupine sightings per km² were recorded in agricultural areas (4.17 ± 1.95 per km2; Fig. 1B).

Temporal variation

Porcupine density (sightings/Km2) exhibited significant temporal variation across the study period (P < 0.001). Monthly averages ranged from 1.75 in December to 7.25 in June (Fig. 2A), with June showing significantly higher sightings compared to other months (P < 0.001). Intermediate densities were recorded in February (4.25), May (5.67), July (4.75), and January (5.25). These patterns suggest seasonal fluctuations in porcupine activity or visibility, potentially influenced by environmental factors.

Temporal variation in porcupine density (sightings/km²) across (A) monthly and (B) seasonal scales (P < 0.05). Significant differences (P < 0.05) among groups are indicated in GP style: 0.1234 (ns), 0.0332 (*), 0.0021 (**), 0.0002 (***), < 0.0001 (****).

Seasonal analysis further revealed significant differences in porcupine density (P < 0.001), with the highest mean sightings during summer (4.98) and the lowest during fall (2.04) (Fig. 2B). Spring and winter exhibited intermediate densities, with no significant difference between them. The observed variability highlights the influence of seasonal factors on porcupine activity, with summer showing the greatest variability in sightings.

Demographic variation

The mean number of males sighted per season was highest during summer (1.23 ± 0.51) and lowest during fall (0.50 ± 0.50) (Fig. 3). Similarly, female sightings were highest in summer (1.75 ± 0.90) and lowest in fall (0.79 ± 0.41). The male-to-female ratio remained relatively balanced across seasons, with minor fluctuations. Notably, female sightings were significantly higher than male sightings only during the summer season (females: 1.75 ± 0.90; males: 1.23 ± 0.51). These findings suggest seasonal influences on porcupine activity, with summer exhibiting the most pronounced demographic differences.

Courtship behavior

The analysis revealed that mounting were frequent across porcupine pairs, while copulation were rare. Pairs 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9, and 10 exhibited 32, 24, 27, 18, 23, 36, and 33 mounting, respectively, but no copulation were recorded. In contrast, pairs 2, 5, 6, 11, and 12 displayed 45, 40, 48, 37, and 44 mounting, respectively, with each pair recording one copulation (Table 2). A Spearman correlation test indicated significant positive relationship between mounting and copulation (r = 0.8569, P = 0.0025), suggesting that increased mounting frequency was associated with higher likelihood of copulation (Fig. 4A).

In contrast, the lowest frequency of mounting behavior was observed in January (1.2 ± 0.1) and September (1.3 ± 0.2), with mean ranks of 33.9 and 36.7, respectively, equating to 14 and 15 annual events. Additionally, year-wise analysis revealed no significant variation in mounting across 2019, 2020, and 2021 (P = 0.416; Fig. 5B).

Reproductive ecology

Birthing and the number of porcupettes were monitored in twelve porcupine families via camera recordings from 2019 to 2021. Over the study period, 21 birthing were recorded, resulting in the birth of 33 porcupettes. These included 9 single births and 12 twin births. Reproductive patterns varied among families, with some producing only single births, others only twin births, and combination of both in some cases (Table 3).

Parental care

This study assessed the distribution of parental care and alloparenting behaviors among different members of porcupine families, including males, females, both males and females together, sub adults/juveniles, and non-descript members, during the observation period from 2019 to 2021. A total of 3,982 parental care events were documented, revealing significant variability across families and notable differences in the allocation of these behaviors among family members (P < 0.001). The highest frequency of parental care was observed in cases where both males and females participated jointly, followed by females alone, males alone, sub-adults/juveniles, and non-descript members, respectively (Fig. 6). These findings underscore the variability in parental care roles, highlighting the significant involvement of specific family members as alloparents within porcupine social structures.

Discussion

This study provides analysis of the ecology and behavior of the Indian crested porcupine in Punjab, Pakistan, focusing on population distribution, habitat preferences, activity patterns, and reproductive biology. The findings highlight the species’ adaptability to diverse environments. H. indica was found to be relatively common in Punjab, with distribution pattern consistent with its reported expansion in Northeast India3,6,25. The species exhibited preference for irrigated forest plantations and sub-mountainous regions over agricultural and desert ecosystems, likely due to the abundance of preferred food resources such as bulbs, rhizomes, and tubers26. This habitat selection may also reflect avoidance of poaching and resource scarcity in agricultural and desert areas, respectively. While higher densities reported in grasslands and semi-arid ecosystems27, the current study observed the highest mean sighting values in sub-mountainous regions and irrigated forests, suggesting regional variability in habitat use. Predation dynamics in Punjab were less documented compared to other regions, where larger carnivores such as tigers and leopards pose significant threats10. The absence of such predators in Punjab may contribute to the species’ relatively stable population.

H. indica exhibited predominantly nocturnal activity, with 80% of activity occurring between 8:00 p.m. and 5:00 a.m., peaking between 11:00 p.m. and 2:00 a.m. This pattern aligns with antipredator behavior, as reduced activity during moonlit nights suggests avoidance of predation risk8. In contrast, H. cristata in Tuscany displayed more varied activity, including crepuscular peaks and less pronounced moonlight avoidance28. These differences underscore species-specific adaptations to local environmental and predation pressures. The nocturnal behavior of H. indica in Punjab likely optimizes foraging efficiency during cooler periods while minimizing predator encounters27.

The study revealed year-round reproductive activity in H. indica, with peaks in February and July. The February peak, characterized by singletons, may reflect strategy to ensure offspring survival during harsh winter conditions, while the July peak, with higher incidences of twins and triplets, coincides with increased food availability following monsoon rains6,29. This reproductive flexibility aligns with findings from other regions, where seasonal resource availability influences breeding patterns2.

Parental care in H. indica was predominantly biparental, with both parents contributing to porcupettes rearing. Male involvement, observed in varying degrees across families (e.g., 15.73% in Family 1 and 18.42% in Family 3), was critical for porcupettes survival and development, reinforcing pair bonds and stabilizing social structures2,30. This biparental care, uncommon among mammals, highlights the species’ complex social dynamics and the importance of extended parental investment. Beyond direct parental care, alloparenting by juveniles and other family members appears to enhance porcupette survival. This pattern is consistent with observations in several other rodent species, including the Mongolian gerbil31, Prairie vole32, Pine vole33, Mandarin vole34, and the African striped mouse35.

The expansion of H. indica’s range in Punjab underscore its adaptability but also highlight vulnerabilities. Habitat fragmentation, poaching, and human-wildlife conflict pose significant threats, contributing to population declines11,27. Conservation strategies must prioritize habitat preservation, particularly grasslands and sub-mountainous regions, alongside measures to mitigate agricultural conflicts and reduce poaching pressures. Such efforts are essential to safeguard H. indica and its ecological role in the region.

Study limitations and methodological mitigations

This study, while providing novel insights into the ecology and behaviour of the Indian crested porcupine (Hystrix indica) across Punjab, was subject to certain limitations inherent to field-based wildlife research. Several methodological adjustments were thereby also incorporated to minimize their potential effects on the results. Owing to the nocturnal characteristics of study specie, terrain, vegetation cover, and variable environmental conditions detection was considerably constrained. To mitigate imperfect detectability, the study integrated nocturnal visual surveys with spoor-based evidence, targeted camera trapping, and occupancy-based detection probability modelling. The insurmountable logistics required for sampling entire regions of Punjab, limited complete spatial representation for porcupine population. However, this issue was addressed by selecting multiple sites within each habitat type and applying standardized survey protocols across all locations, enhancing comparability and allowing robust habitat-level inferences while limiting site-specific bias. Seasonal variations in weather and porcupine activity influenced detectability and sampling effort. To reduce temporal bias, surveys were conducted across all four seasons over three consecutive years, enabling aggregation of multi-season data and minimizing the influence of short-term climatic anomalies on population and behavioral trends. Behavioral and reproductive analyses required regular videographic monitoring thus requiring investigation to be conducted for specifically marked individuals. This data subset was further reduced burrow abandonment and data loss. To offset this limitation, only complete and high-confidence records were included, and non-parametric statistical tests were employed to ensure reliable inferences despite reduced sample size. The combined use of long-term sampling, detection correction, multi-method data collection, and conservative statistical analyses substantially reduced their influence on key findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study provide critical insights into the reproductive ecology and behavior of H. indica in Punjab, Pakistan. The species demonstrates adaptability to diverse habitats, with preference for irrigated forest plantations and sub-mountainous regions, likely due to resource availability and reduced predation risks. Seasonal and temporal variations in activity and reproductive patterns reflect the species’ ability to optimize survival strategies in response to environmental conditions. The biparental care system and year-round reproductive activity highlight the species’ complex social structure and resilience, yet these traits also make it vulnerable to anthropogenic pressures. This study underscores the need for integrated conservation efforts to ensure the persistence of this ecologically significant species.
