Confirming Dissociated Pulmonary Vein Activity after Pulmonary Vein Isolation While Still in Atrial Fibrillation: Are We Scooping the Moon in the Water?
Authors: Simon Salim, Ying-Chieh Liao, Shih-Ann Chen
Published: 2026

Since clinical electrophysiology has emerged as a distinct service alongside advances in mapping and ablation techniques, considerable progress has been made in AF management. Initially, ablation attempts were focused on the right atrium; however, limited efficacy coupled with high recurrence prompted a shift towards left atrial (LA) ablation.1

The seminal work of Haïssaguerre et al. in identifying triggers inside the pulmonary veins (PVs), followed by reports by Hsieh et al. and Chen et al. that focal ablation of PV yields AF elimination, serves as a foundation for targeting PVs.2–4 Pappone et al. were the first to champion circumferential ablation of PV ostia.5 In patients with non-paroxysmal AF, more complex pathophysiology might play a role in addition to the triggers in PVs. Multiple reports have demonstrated diverse ablation strategies aimed at the non-PV arrhythmogenic substrate in addition to effective PV isolation (PVI).6

It is now a widely accepted concept among electrophysiologists that a good PVI remains the cornerstone of the AF ablation procedure, even in non-paroxysmal AF. In cases where AF persists despite PV ablation, further ablation and mapping strategies, such as the optimal ablation targets, procedure endpoint and the decision of cardioversion, remain uncertain. Several works, including the recent study by Kong et al., are attempting to identify best practices in non-paroxysmal AF ablation.7

Within the PV, immediately following PVI, electrical activity may persist inside the PV, which is termed “dissociated pulmonary vein activity”.8 It can manifest as silent, isolated ectopic activity, an isolated rhythm and, although infrequently, as fibrillatory PV activity despite complete PVI. In most situations, the slower electrical firing within the PVs usually represents the complete electrical dissociation between the veins and the rest of the atrium. In contrast, fibrillatory activities existing both inside and outside the ablation line become a big challenge to access PVI.

In the study by Kong et al, the authors used the term ‘ongoing fibrillation in isolated pulmonary vein’ (OFIP) and tried to differentiate it from residual PV potentials from incomplete PVI without converting AF to sinus rhythm.7

As compared with restoring sinus rhythm by cardioversion, we favour continuation of ablation until the cessation of AF in non-paroxysmal AF cases to elucidate the non-PV target.9 Thus, an optimal algorithm to confirm the conduction block between the fibrillatory LA and PV is important in this context.

The four observations by Kong et al. have differing importance for predicting complete PVI in the presence of ongoing fibrillation in both the LA and PV.7 The first observation was when spontaneous disappearance of PV potentials occurred either during ablation or catheter bumping. The manifestation was considered highly indicative of achieving complete PVI. The occurrence rate was 5/563 non-paroxysmal AF ablations and all of them had confirmed PVI in sinus rhythm.

Although it is logical to take it at face value, this is not a novel concept. Readers should also have differentials in mind, as ablation energy and catheter bumping can occasionally result in an intermittent block without sustained PVI. Given the inherent low incidence rate of both OFIP and this manifestation, we should be cautious about interpreting the findings of this study. A more rigorous approach after observing OFIP within a PV would be confirming PVI during sinus rhythm with adenosine administration or longer waiting time, as this PV would be more likely to play an important role in AF maintenance. Regardless, the present study provided an important observation for the highly predictive value of the distinct phenomenon.

The remaining three ‘suspected criteria’ were worthy of discussion. With higher incidence rates, it should be emphasised that the three suspected criteria mentioned in the study should be taken together because of their low specificity. Firstly, bipolar voltage reduction for >80% from its initial value was practical as an indicator of PVI. A bipolar voltage reduction of >80% after ablation indeed predicts effective lesion formation. However, bipolar amplitude depends partly on activation vector, so that even in sinus rhythm, differential pacing to simulate different patterns of wave propagation might yield different ‘low voltage zones’, even in the healthy tissue due to electrode positioning.10 The change in the wave propagation in AF was more challenging; one can argue that ablation can change the wave behaviour such that we can observe a decrease in the bipolar voltage without a good ablation lesion. The type and orientation of the catheters, the size and orientation of the veins and the wave-front propagation from the LA (including the position and magnitude of low-voltage zone), etc. will probably play a role in the sensitivity or specificity of the criterion.

The second criterion was the local activation time from within the PV into the antrum during cases with regularisation. This is logical for identifying the arrhythmia origin and clues regarding electrical propagation by activation timing. However, this observation only suggests some degree of entrance block and might be falsely positive if significant conduction delay is present. There is still a possibility that apparent dissociated activity with different timing might only represent a very slow conduction through a damaged but not completely blocked tissue. Furthermore, PV to LA conduction may still exist and we cannot confirm exit block when there is still ongoing AF outside of PVs. Even a true entrance block in this fashion does not eliminate the possibility of dormant conduction, which could be unveiled by adenosine or early reconnection following a 20-minute waiting period.

The last criterion was the absence of alteration in PV potential during linear ablation atthe inter-venous carina. Carina is indeed one ofthe most important anatomical regions in PVI. Its complex structure and variability render it a necessary portion to be noted in every PVI attempt. However, this criterion will be true if carina is the only possible gap left. In the presence of multiple gaps between LA and PV, the PV activity may be dominantly driven through these connections and shows no change by carina ablation. Moreover, one might not know for certain that previous ablation in the carina is enough, or that the next lesion was needed in the carina when both sides have ongoing fibrillatory waves.

With the criteria above, the authors organised an appropriate algorithm to predict OFIP among fibrillatory PVs. Given a lower incidence of OFIP, a clinical impasse occurs during AF ablation, but this is rarely studied. This work is highly impressive as the first large-scale study in this setting and has genuine clinical implications. However, the limitation is also obvious that one-third of patients meeting all three suspected criteria still need additional gap ablation in the study. On the right-hand side of the algorithm, PVI was evaluated after additional ablation, so the reported 100% sensitivity was not purely the algorithm sensitivity; rather, it reflects the combined strategy of applying the three criteria together with additional ablation when the criteria were not fulfilled. In order to assess the false negative of the algorithm, it might be more reasonable to confirm PVI before gap ablation if the OFIPs do not fulfil any of the three suspected criteria.

Triggers outside of PVs, rotational or fractionated signals in LA and epicardial PV-LA connections all push the electrophysiologists to ablate the non-PV targets.11 However, PVs themselves have complex arrhythmogenicity that contributes to AF initiation and perpetuation. Despite the growing knowledge regarding the critical role of PV, the pathophysiology of AF is not yet completely understood.12 In most cases, especially for paroxysmal AF, PVI remains the cornerstone of the ablative procedure. As electrophysiologists, we should already have a clear strategy prior to the ablation procedure, whether non-PV targets will be pursued, whether we require mapping during AF or during sinus, among other considerations. This study provides a valuable tool in the strategy for non-paroxysmal AF ablation, guiding us to start looking for non-pulmonary vein targets or to continue looking for and ablating potential pulmonary vein gaps. Another critical factor in predicting complete PVI prior to cardioversion is the potential for acute AF recurrence resulting from incomplete PVI, coupled with the geometric shifting that may occur during cardioversion, which might render further ablation less precise.

There are several other points worth mentioning from reading this article. We are reminded that in some patients (up to 3.5% as indicated in their article, which is one in a little less than every 30 patients) can have fibrillation within the pulmonary veins even though good PVI was achieved. When we start looking for a phenomenon, we may be surprised to find some are not as uncommon as initially thought. Regarding the carina’s role in PVI, this study acted as a reminder that one should not overlook the carina even after a circumferential linear lesion has been made around PV ostium. It is worth emphasising to readers that PVs with activity, especially those that exhibit persistent fibrillation, necessitate additional attention to ensure their isolation. We commend the authors for their diligent work in elucidating the phenomena, while simultaneously urging readers to consider it within a broader context.
