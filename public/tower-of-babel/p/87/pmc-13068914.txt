Physical characterization of cotoneaster seeds to improve sorting efficiency
Authors: Zdzisław Kaliniewicz, Piotr Markowski, Andrzej Anders, Dariusz Choszcz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shrubs are an important component of tree stands, particularly in species-poor habitats. Ornamental shrubs have considerable visual and esthetic appeal and are often planted in the vicinity of industrial sites. Flowering shrubs of the genus Cotoneaster belong to this category of plants. The physical attributes of cotoneaster seeds have never been investigated in the literature. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the basic aerodynamic, geometric, frictional, and mass properties of the seeds of selected cotoneaster species and to determine the correlations between these parameters to facilitate seed sorting operations. Physical properties of seeds of five species of cotoneaster (Diels’, Hjelmqvist’s, hollyberry, showy, and wall) were determined. The strength of the correlations between seed mass and the remaining seed parameters was evaluated. Showy cotoneaster produced the largest seeds and Diel’s cotoneaster the smallest. Seed mass was most highly correlated with terminal velocity in Diels’, Hjelmqvist’s, and wall cotoneaster seeds, whereas the mass of hollyberry and showy cotoneaster seeds was bound by the strongest correlation with seed thickness. These observations indicate that cotoneaster seeds should be divided into uniform fractions with the use of pneumatic separators or mesh sieves with longitudinal openings. These devices can decrease the coefficient of variation of seed mass by around 5% to around 62% in different seed fractions. The variations in seed mass were most effectively reduced in showy cotoneaster (by approx. 57% on average) and least effectively reduced in hollyberry cotoneaster (by approx. 13% on average).

Introduction

Plant communities tend to have a layered design. In most cases, vegetation is organized into the following layers: canopy (full-grown trees), shrubs, herbs, and groundcover. Herbs and groundcover are generally referred to as the undergrowth, whereas shrubs constitute the midstory layer. According to estimates, shrubs cover around 45% of total land area in the world1–3. They are an important component of tree stands, particularly in species-poor habitats. Shrubs play vital biological roles and impact the microclimate along the boundaries of tree stands, and they protect trees against the damaging effects of strong winds. Shrubs are also a source of food for forest fauna. In clear-felled forests, shrubs can be used to protect the seedlings of shade-tolerant forest tree species4.

The esthetic appeal of ornamental shrubs has been recognized since the ancient times. Shrubs have long been appreciated for their diverse forms, sizes, colors, and shapes. For this reason, they are widely used in urban planning to improve the local climate, promote public health, and enhance urban landscapes. Shrubs are also planted for decorative purposes in household gardens, parks, green squares, and allotments1,5–7.

Cotoneasters (Cotoneaster Medik.) are ornamental shrubs of the rose family (Rosaceae). Depending on the classification system, the genus Cotoneaster comprises between 50 and 400 species. Most cotoneasters are shrubs or small trees with a height up to 5 m. Cotoneasters are most widespread in the temperate regions of Northern Asia, as well as in China and the Himalayas, and they are generally distributed at altitudes below 4000 m a.s.l. In natural habitats, cotoneasters grow in rocky areas, canyons, and forest meadows in the mountains, where they prefer sunny or partially shaded areas. Cotoneasters thrive on most soil types, excluding marshy and waterlogged soils8–12.

Cotoneasters have straight, protruding, and creeping stems that often produce rooted plantlets. The stems are round and devoid of thorns, which distinguishes them from closely related hawthorn (Crataegus L.) species. Young stems are covered with hairs, but they become hairless as they mature. The stems are red-brown or purple-black, and they play an ornamental role even in winter. Cotoneasters blossom in May and June, and their flowers are a source of nectar for pollinators. The flowers are uniform in size and color, with a diameter of 1.0–1.5 cm, and pure white or pink color. Brightly colored fruit (pomes) are the most visually appealing feature of cotoneaster shrubs. Pomes have a length of 5 to 15 mm, and a spherical, oval, ellipsoid, or cylindrical shape. Ripe pomes are usually bright red, sometimes yellowish, brown, black or orange. Each pome contains 2 to 5 pyrenes (seeds) enclosed by the hardened inner wall of the carpel8,9,13–15.

The characteristic shape or color of cotoneaster shrubs can be effectively preserved through vegetative propagation of cuttings obtained from semi-lignified shoots16,17. In mass production, cotoneasters can also be propagated generatively through seeds because these shrubs reproduce by apomixis, resulting in genetically identical offspring. For this purpose, pomes are macerated to remove the fleshy mesocarp, and the seeds are then naturally dried before storage, processing, and dressing. The seeds of some cotoneaster species exhibit dormancy, including external (seed coat) and internal (embryo) dormancy. Therefore, before sowing, cotoneaster seeds have to be subjected to mechanical and chemical scarification, followed by hot-cold stratification, to break their dormancy18–22.

Before breaking their dormancy, most cotoneaster seeds should be first sorted to remove impurities and obtain uniform seed fractions with similar germination speed23,24. Seed sorting facilitates care and management practices in nurseries because sorted seeds are sown separately to promote uniform seedling emergence and growth, which is an important consideration when seeds are sown with a mechanical drill. According to research25–27, seeds should be sorted into fractions based on their mass, a physical attribute that exerts the greatest influence on germination efficiency and the germination speed. However, seed sorting is not an easy process because individual seeds often differ in mass and basic geometric parameters24,28,29. To improve the effectiveness of seed sorting, physical attributes that are strongly correlated with seed mass should be identified and applied in the separation process, preferably with the use of conventional seed cleaning machines. The variations in the basic physical characteristics of cotoneaster seeds and the correlations between these parameters have never been examined in the literature. These parameters play an important role not only in seed separation, but also in other seed processing treatments. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the seeds of selected cotoneaster species and to determine the correlations between the basic physical properties of seeds to improve the effectiveness of seed sorting operations.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The study involved the seeds of five most popular cotoneaster species in Poland: Diels’ cotoneaster (Cotoneaster dielsianus E.Pritz. ex Diels), Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster (Cotoneaster hjelmquisetii Flinck & B.Hylmö), hollyberry cotoneaster (Cotoneaster bullatus Bois), showy cotoneaster (Cotoneaster multiflorus Bunge), and wall cotoneaster (Cotoneaster horizontalis Decne.) (Fig. 1). Seeds were supplied by Dendrona (Pęcice, Poland), which produces and distributes the seeds of forest trees and shrubs in compliance with seed certification and seed trade regulations. Each sample contained 200 g of seeds. Impurities were removed manually, and only nondamaged seeds of the principal species were retained. The seeds were brought to the equilibrium moisture content by storing them in air-permeable polypropylene bags at room temperature (approx. 22 ± 2 °C) and a relative air humidity of 55 ± 5% for one month. Around 100 seeds of each cotoneaster species were used in the experiment. The experimental samples were acquired by halving and randomly selecting one of the halves for further division24,28. The resulting specimens were composed of the following number of seeds: Diels’ cotoneaster—118, Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster—123, hollyberry cotoneaster—122, showy cotoneaster—119, and wall cotoneaster—121.

Physical properties

The terminal velocity (v), length (L), width (W), thickness (T), mass (m), and angle of external friction (γ) were determined for each seed. The measurement procedures and the accuracy of each measurement were described previously by Kaliniewicz et al.29,30. The measurements were conducted using the following devices: Petkus K-293 air separator (Petkus Technologie GmbH, Wutha-Farnroda, Germany), MWM 2325 workshop microscope (PZO, Warsaw, Poland), thickness gauge, WAA 100/C/2 laboratory weighing scale (Radwag, Radom, Poland), and a steel friction plate with porosity Ra = 0.46 μm mounted to a moving inclined plane. The angle of external friction was calculated based on the average value of two measurements that were conducted by placing the seed in a parallel and perpendicular position to the inclined plane.

The seeds of each cotoneaster species were divided into three size classes: small (class 1—seeds with the smallest mass), medium (class 2—seeds with average mass), and large (class 3—seeds with the largest mass). The boundaries of each size class were determined by rounding off seed mass to the nearest 0.5 mg. The sorting process was planned on the assumption that each seed sample would be divided into two, three, or four size fractions.

Statistical analysis

The results were processed statistically in Statistica PL v. 13.3 (StatSoft Poland Ltd., Cracow, Poland). The mean values and standard deviations of the examined parameters were determined using descriptive statistics. The differences in the parameters of seeds belonging to the same species were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Homogeneous groups were identified by Tukey’s test. Linear correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the strength of the relationships between the studied parameters. Statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results and discussion

Experimental material

The standard error of the estimate was calculated based on the standard deviation of the analyzed seed attributes, the size of seed samples, and the corresponding values of Student’s t-distribution (1.9805–118 seeds, 1.9803–119 seeds, 1.9799–121 seeds, 1.9798–122 seeds, 1.9796–123 seeds). These calculations revealed that the standard error of the estimate did not exceed: 0.2 m s−1 for terminal velocity, 0.2 mm for basic geometric parameters (length, width, and thickness), 0.5° for the angle of external friction, and 0.6 mg for mass (3.7 mg in showy cotoneaster).

The volumetric coefficient k of seeds, measured with a pycnometer, was as follows: 0.549 for Diels’ cotoneaster, 0.434 for Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster, 0.428 for hollyberry cotoneaster, 0.482 for showy cotoneaster, and 0.446 for wall cotoneaster. The seed volume calculated on the assumption that the seeds of the analyzed cotoneaster species have an ellipsoid shape differed from the volume determined based on the volumetric coefficient k by around 5% (Diels’ cotoneaster) to around 22% (hollyberry cotoneaster) on average.

Diels’ cotoneaster produced the smallest seeds and showy cotoneaster the largest. The following cotoneaster species formed homogeneous groups based on seed parameters: seed length—Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster and wall cotoneaster; seed thickness—Diels’ cotoneaster and Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster; Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster and wall cotoneaster, hollyberry cotoneaster and wall cotoneaster; seed mass—Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster, hollyberry cotoneaster, and wall cotoneaster; seed geometric mean diameter—Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster and hollyberry cotoneaster. Diels’ cotoneaster seeds are similar to common juniper seeds in terms of geometric mean diameter30 and to broadleaf lilac seeds in terms of average seed mass29. In terms of average mass, the seeds of Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster and hollyberry cotoneaster resemble Amur lilac seeds, and the seeds of wall cotoneaster resemble Peking lilac seeds29.

The studied cotoneaster species, excluding showy cotoneaster, produce flat seeds (Fig. 1) that can be considered flat particles, which explains the high values of the angle of external friction, ranging from 33.5° in hollyberry cotoneaster to 39.4° in wall cotoneaster. In turn, showy cotoneaster seeds have a circular cross-section, which is why these seeds were characterized by the lowest angle of external friction at 21.3° on average.

The sphericity index of cotoneaster seeds ranged from 0.60 (hollyberry cotoneaster) to 0.82 (showy cotoneaster), while Diels’ cotoneaster, Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster, and wall cotoneaster seeds formed a homogenous group based on this parameter. The average values of aspect ratios ranged from 0.40 (T/L—Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster) to 0.85 (T/W—showy cotoneaster); the seeds of all examined species could not be included in one homogeneous group based on any of these indicators. Similarly, the seeds of the studied species did not form a single homogeneous group based on the ratios of their mass to dimensions (m/T, m/W, m/L, m/D), or density. This observation points to considerable variations in the shape of seeds of different cotoneaster species, and it suggests that a universal method for sorting seeds of Cotoneaster spp. cannot be developed.

In conclusion, the analysis of physical attributes revealed the greatest similarities between Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster and hollyberry cotoneaster seeds and between Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster and wall cotoneaster seeds (which formed homogeneous groups based on 7 parameters). No significant similarities in physical properties were found between Diels’ cotoneaster and showy cotoneaster seeds or between hollyberry cotoneaster and showy cotoneaster seeds. Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster seeds were most similar (18 similarities), whereas showy cotoneaster seeds were least similar (2 similarities) to the seeds of the remaining cotoneaster species.

Correlations between seed mass and other physical properties

As previously mentioned, seed mass exerts a considerable influence on germination capacity and the germination speed. Therefore, physical attributes that are most strongly correlated with seed mass should be identified to increase the effectiveness of seed sorting operations. The results of the correlation analysis are presented in Table 2. The absolute values of the correlation coefficient ranged from 0.013 (seed mass vs. seed length in Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster) to 0.958 (seed mass vs. seed thickness in showy cotoneaster). Practically significant values of the correlation coefficient (above 0.4) were observed in 15 out of 25 comparisons. Seed mass was most strongly correlated with terminal velocity in three species (Diels’, Hjelmqvist’s, and wall cotoneasters), whereas the strongest correlation between seed mass and seed thickness was noted in the remaining species (hollyberry and showy cotoneasters). In all cotoneaster species, seed mass was significantly correlated with seed thickness only. This observation suggests that cotoneaster seeds should be separated with the use of mesh sieves with longitudinal openings. Seed thickness was found to be the primary distinguishing feature during seed separation processes in many species of forest trees and shrubs, including broadleaf lilac29. Cotoneaster seeds can also be sorted based on their terminal velocity in a pneumatic separator. The pneumatic separator has been also recommended for sorting the seeds of snowball, sweet, and Sargent viburnums28, as well as Amur, Hungarian, and Peking lilacs29.

Recommendations for seed sorting

The studied cotoneaster species differed considerably in seed dimensions and seed mass; therefore, the analyzed seeds were divided into three size classes with different boundary values for each class (Table 3). Class 1 (smallest seeds) was composed of seeds weighing less than 6.0 mg (Diels’ cotoneaster), 9.0 mg (Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster), 9.5 mg (hollyberry cotoneaster), 12.0 mg (wall cotoneaster), and 35 mg (showy cotoneaster). Class 3 (largest seeds) contained seeds weighing more than 7.5 mg, 11.5 mg, 11.5 mg, 15.0 mg, and 64.0 mg, respectively. The percentage distribution of seeds belonging to different size classes ranged from 28.1% (class 1, wall cotoneaster) to 39.7% (class 2, wall cotoneaster).

When seeds were divided into two, three, or four fractions based on their terminal velocity (Table 4), the coefficient of variation of seed mass decreased in the vast majority of cases (42 out of 45 variants). This observation implies that sorting operations based on terminal velocity will produce more uniform seed fractions. These fractions can be sown separately in forest nurseries to achieve even seedling emergence25–27 and facilitate care and management practices. A comparison of the coefficients of variation of seed mass in each fraction demonstrated that the seeds of Diels’, Hjelmqvist’s, and wall cotoneasters should be sorted with the use of pneumatic separators, whereas the seeds of hollyberry and showy cotoneasters would be more effectively separated on mesh sieves with longitudinal openings. If seeds are separated into three fractions (the most common approach in practice), the coefficient of variation of seed mass would decrease by 36.2% (Diels’ cotoneaster), 31.8% (Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster), 13.4% (hollyberry cotoneaster), 53.5% (showy cotoneaster), and 16.8% (wall cotoneaster) on average. In Diels’, Hjelmqvist’s, and wall cotoneasters, the airflow rate should be set to approximately 5.3 m s−1, 5.8 m s−1, and 7.4 m s−1, respectively to separate seeds with the lowest terminal velocity, and to approximately 6.3 m s−1, 6.9 m s−1, and 8.0 m s−1, respectively, to separate seeds with average and highest terminal velocity (Table 4). In turn, hollyberry and showy cotoneaster should be sorted with the use of mesh sieves with longitudinal openings and aperture size of 2.0 mm and 4.4 mm, respectively, to separate the thickest seeds, and 1.8 mm and 3.1 mm, respectively, to separate the thinnest seeds (Table 5). In a sieve grain cleaner, the hopper should move in reciprocating motion to ensure consistent seed flow.

Conclusions

The seeds of the analyzed cotoneaster species differ in shape and dimensions. Showy cotoneaster produces the largest seeds, whereas Diels’ cotoneaster is characterized by the smallest seeds. The seeds of the examined cotoneaster species were arranged in the following ascending order based on the average values of geometric mean diameter, mass, and volume: Diels’ cotoneaster, hollyberry cotoneaster, Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster, wall cotoneaster, and showy cotoneaster. In terms of basic physical attributes, showy cotoneaster seeds differed most considerably from the remaining cotoneaster species, whereas Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster seeds were most similar to the remaining cotoneaster species.

Seed mass was bound by the strongest correlation with terminal velocity in Diels’, Hjelmqvist’s, and wall cotoneasters, and with seed thickness in hollyberry and showy cotoneasters. Therefore, these seeds should be sorted with the use of pneumatic separators or mesh sieves with longitudinal openings.

When terminal velocity or seed thickness are applied as the primary distinguishing feature, the coefficient of variation of seed mass in each fraction will decrease by around 5% to around 51% on average if seeds are divided into two fractions, by around 9% to around 54% on average if seeds are divided into three fractions, and by around 6% to around 62% on average if seeds are divided into four fractions. The proposed approach will lead to the most uniform distribution of seed mass in showy cotoneaster, followed by Diels’ cotoneaster, Hjelmqvist’s cotoneaster, and wall cotoneaster, and least uniform distribution of seed mass in hollyberry cotoneaster.
