Associations between active and passive social media use and anxious affect among adolescents around the world
Authors: Jean M Twenge, Lindsey Rodriguez, Tamun Hanjra, Shanting Chen, Alana Rio, Alexis Diomino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious research has provided conflicting answers about whether the active use of social media, rather than just passive use, is linked to poor mental health. In addition, few studies have examined associations between social media use and mental health in international samples. Among adolescents aged 15–16 from 47 countries around the world (N = 143,203), both active and passive social media use were associated with higher levels of anxious affect, though most associations with active use reversed or became non-significant when passive use was included in a multilevel model. Among girls across all regions, heavy passive social media users (7 + hours a day) were 88% more likely than light passive users (less than an hour a day) to strongly agree they got nervous easily; heavy active users were 64% more likely than light active users to strongly agree. Among boys, heavy passive social media users were 73% more likely than light passive users to strongly agree they worry about many things; heavy active users were 60% more likely than light active users to strongly agree. The associations appeared across all world regions, with effect sizes especially large in Central/Eastern Europe. Mean differences were larger among girls than boys, but relative risk elevations were larger among boys, likely due to lower base rates. Multilevel models identified significant interactions by gender. Thus, passive SMU (and sometimes active SMU) is associated with anxious affect, especially for girls.Keywords: Mental health, Adolescents, Social media, Anxiety, Global

The recent global expansion of social media use (SMU) has raised concerns about its impact on mental health, particularly among adolescents. Nearly half (46%) of U.S. teens say they are online "almost constantly," up from 24% in 2014 [22]. Most 13- to 17-year-olds report high daily use for YouTube (73% daily; 15% almost constantly), TikTok (57% daily; 16% almost constantly), Instagram (50% daily; 12% almost constantly), and Snapchat (48% daily; 13% almost constantly [22]. Additionally, adolescents’ mental health is deteriorating at an alarming rate: from 2011 to 2021, major depressive episodes more than doubled [32], and mental health–related emergency department visits surged by 56% in the U.S. [3].

Previous work has generally shown that heavy SMU is associated with psychological distress and depression [8], [9], [14], [19], [24], [27]. However, the way people use social media differs. As a result, researchers have also focused on whether consumption is active (e.g., interacting with others via posting updates, sharing links, commenting on others’ content, sending direct messages) or passive (e.g., scrolling through feeds, reading comments, or discussions). Research on the active-passive dichotomy has found that passive SMU is significantly associated with increased depressive symptoms and low well-being, while active SMU has either no significant association or a negative association with depression and/or low well-being [6], [7], [20], [31]. Studies suggest that passive SMU may lead to decreased subjective well-being by inducing envy as well as upward social comparison [6], [7], [29], while active SMU may lead to increased subjective well-being by enabling social support and connection [18], [31].

Thorisdottir et al [26]. found that passive SMU was linked to increased symptoms of anxiety and depressed mood among adolescents, while active SMU was associated with a reduction in these symptoms, even after controlling for total time spent on social media. When young adults were randomly assigned to use Facebook either actively or passively, those who engaged passively experienced decreases in affective well-being over time [30]. This association held after controlling for active Facebook use, other social networking site use, and direct social interactions, and was driven by passive social media use leading to increased envy. Another study found that individuals who used social media more actively reported lower depression scores while those who used social media more passively reported higher depression scores [6].

Gender

Across several studies, associations between digital or social media use and mental health issues were stronger for girls than boys [10], [25], [28]. Other work has shown that girls who used social media heavily at age 10 reported poorer mental health outcomes years later, an association not present for boys [4].

Current Research

Compared to depression or well-being outcomes, relatively few studies have examined SMU and anxious affect. Even fewer have examined associations between SMU and affect in international datasets that collect nationally representative samples from many countries. In this research, we examine how active and passive SMU are associated with anxious affect in a large, representative international dataset. We compare associations between time spent on social media and anxious affect across active vs. passive social media use, world regions, and gender. We hypothesized that both passive and active SMU would be linked to anxious affect in youth but that associations between passive use and anxious affect would be larger than associations with active use. Additionally, we hypothesized that SMU will have a larger association with anxious affect among girls, based on past research focusing on depressive symptoms and unhappiness [15], [28]. As so little research has compared associations between SMU and mental health across world regions, we do not advance specific hypotheses about region as a moderator but do examine it.

Method

Participants and procedure

Participants were students completing the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) survey, which obtains nationally representative samples of 15- and 16-year-olds enrolled in school in countries around the world. Schools were required to have at least an 80% response rate for student participants. The survey was administered in the same language used for instruction in the school [21]. We drew from the 2022 administration of the PISA dataset (N = 143,203 for the items of interest). The data are publicly available at http://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/. The original data collection was approved by the OCED ethics committee under the Declaration of Helsinki standards and all participants gave their informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study.

Measures

Social media use (SMU)

Students were asked: “During a typical weekday, how much time do you spend doing the following leisure activities?” These included a measure of passive social media use [“Browse social networks (e.g., Instagram, Facebook)”] and a measure of active social media use [“Communicate and share digital content on social networks or any communication platform (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, emails, chat)”]. Response choices were: no time at all (abbreviated as none), less than one hour, between 1 and 3 h a day (abbreviated as 1–2 h), more than 3 h and up to 5 h (abbreviated as 3–4 h), more than 5 h and up to 7 h (abbreviated as 5–6 h), and more than 7 h a day.

Anxious affect

Students were asked to what extent they agreed with two statements measuring anxious affect: “I get nervous easily” and “I worry about many things.” Response options included strongly disagree (1), disagree (2), neither agree nor disagree (3), agree (4), and strongly agree (5). We were unable to combine the two items as the OECD PISA survey asked participants a random selection of questions, thus cutting sample sizes more than in half when items are combined. In lieu of this, we examined each item separately.

Grouping by cultural regions

The social media and anxious affect questions were asked in 48 countries, which we grouped by geographic and cultural regions: Western Europe (i.e., Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Germany, Iceland, Italy, Malta, Spain, Switzerland), Central and Eastern Europe (i.e., Albania, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czech Republic, Estonia, Georgia, Greece, Hungary, Kazakhstan, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, Slovak Republic, Slovenia, Ukraine), English-speaking (i.e., Australia, Ireland, United Kingdom), the Middle East and North Africa (i.e., Jordan, Morocco, Saudi Arabia, Türkiye), Latin America (i.e., Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, Panama, Uruguay), and Asia (i.e., Brunei, Chinese Taipei [Taiwan], Hong Kong, South Korea, Macao, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand).

Analysis plan

For descriptive statistics, we report both mean scores and the percentage who indicated they strongly agreed with the anxious affect items for all categories of active and passive SMU. We also present these statistics separately for girls and boys. Analyses are weighted by the analysis weight and controlled for the economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) index, the PISA measure of socioeconomic status (SES [1]; ). We compared heavy users (more than 7 h a day) to light users (less than an hour a day) and non-users (no time at all) using the effect size d (difference in standard deviations [5]) for anxious affect item means and relative risk (RR) for the percentage who strongly agreed with each anxious affect item. For both statistics, 95% confidence intervals are reported; 95% CIs for ds that do not include zero are statistically significant, and 95% CIs for RR’s that do not include 1 are statistically significant.

We also performed multilevel analyses to explore interaction effects for type of SMU (active vs. passive) and gender. Models were fit in R version 4.5.1 [23] using lme4::lmer [2], [11]. Each model included a random intercept for country to account for PISA’s clustered sampling design and the shared contextual factors of students nested within the same country [16].

Each outcome (nervousness and worry) was modeled in separate models resulting in two main-effect models. The main-effect model entered both passive and active SMU simultaneously as predictors, alongside gender and SES as covariates. The interaction model added passive SMU-by-gender and active SMU-by gender interaction terms tested in separate models to avoid multicollinearity between the two interaction terms (passive and active hours correlate at r = 0.63). If the interaction terms were significant, we then estimated simple slopes for each gender and formal tests of the female-versus-male slope difference were estimated using emmeans [12] and the interactions package [13]; significant interactions were visualized using interaction plots.

Active versus passive SMU and anxious affect across all regions

In data combined across all regions, both active and passive SMU were associated with anxious affect. Overall, anxious affect was lowest among light users of social media and highest among heavy users. Among girls across all regions, mean scores on the nervousness item were d = .38 higher among heavy passive users than light passive users, and d = .27 higher among heavy active users compared to light active users (see Table 1). Heavy passive social media users were 88% more likely than light passive users to strongly agree they got nervous easily (see Table 3; RR=1.88); heavy active users were 64% more likely than light active users to strongly agree (see Table 3; RR=1.64). Findings were similar for the item measuring worry, though with somewhat smaller effect sizes. Mean scores on the worry item were d = .34 higher among heavy passive users than light passive users, and d = .24 higher among heavy active users (see Table 2). Heavy passive social media users were 59% more likely than light passive users to strongly agree they worry about many things (see Table 4; RR=1.59); heavy active users were 59% more likely than light active users to strongly agree (see Table 4: RR=1.59). The dose-response curve for passive use was more linear, with each uptick in social media use beyond less than an hour linked to more nervousness and worry (see Fig. 1). Comparisons between heavy use and non-use were also significant, though with lower effect sizes than comparisons between heavy use and light use.

Among boys across all regions, mean scores on the nervousness item were d = .20 higher among heavy passive users than light passive users, and d = .13 higher among heavy active users compared to light active users (see Table 1). Findings were similar for the item measuring worry, though with somewhat smaller effect sizes. Mean scores on the worry item were d = .18 higher among heavy passive users than light users (see Table 2), and d = .07 higher among heavy active users (see Table 2). Heavy passive social media users were 73% more likely than light passive users to strongly agree they worry about many things (see Table 4; RR=1.73); heavy active users were 60% more likely than light active users to strongly agree (see Table 4; RR=1.60). The dose-response curve was somewhat curvilinear for boys, with higher levels of anxious affect among non-users, the lowest levels among light users, and by far the highest levels among heavy users of social media (see Fig. 2).

Associations within regions

In nearly all comparisons within regions, heavy users of social media reported more anxious affect than light or non-users (see Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4). The association between passive SMU and nervousness was especially strong in Central/Eastern Europe (for example, d =.51 and RR=2.24 for heavy vs. light use among girls) and in the Middle East (d =.52, RR=2.19). For worrying about many things, ds for girls’ passive use were between.18 and.51 in Central/Eastern Europe, English-speaking countries, the Middle East, Latin America, and Asia. Among boys, the association between passive social media use and nervousness was largest in Central/Eastern Europe (d =.29, RR=2.71).

Associations by gender

When comparing means, the association between SMU and anxious affect was generally larger for girls than for boys (see Table 1, Table 2). However, relative risk (RR) comparisons of heavy vs. light or non-users were generally larger among boys than girls, likely because base rates for anxious affect were lower for boys (see Table 3, Table 4).

The dose-response curve was also shaped differently for girls compared to boys. Especially for passive use, anxious affect increased fairly steadily with each additional hour of SMU among girls, while among boys, anxious affect rose more slowly after SMU beyond 1–2 h and then spiked upward among the heaviest users (those using social media 7 or more hours a day; see Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Multilevel models

As a first step, we conducted intraclass correlation (ICC) analyses to determine the proportion of variation in nervousness and worry attributable to between-country differences. ICCs ranged from 0.051 to 0.056 across study variables, indicating that approximately 5 – 6% of the total variance in anxious affect was between countries, with the remaining 94–95% occurring within countries at the student level. Although between-country variance was modest, random intercepts for country were retained in all models given PISA’s clustered sampling design, in which students are nested within schools within countries, and to ensure that the reported associations reflect within-country patterns [16].

Mutually adjusted multilevel models examining main effects revealed that passive SMU was significantly and positively associated with both nervousness and worry when active SMU was included in the model (see Table 6). Each additional hour of daily passive SMU was associated with more nervousness (b = 0.041, SE = 0.002, t = 23.62, p < 0.001), and more worry (b = 0.037, SE = 0.002, t = 23.26, p < 0.001). In contrast, active SMU was significantly and negatively associated with worry when passive SMU was included in the model (b = −0.004, SE = 0.002, t = −2.65, p = 0.008), indicating that higher levels of active SMU were associated with slightly lower worry scores. Active SMU showed no significant independent association with nervousness (b = 0.003, SE = 0.002, t = 1.61, p = 0.107).

Discussion

Among adolescent boys and girls around the world, heavy social media use, both active and passive, was consistently linked to anxious affect compared to light or no use. In contrast to some previous research, associations with anxious affect appeared for both types of use. However, most associations with active SMU became non-significant or reversed when passive SMU was also included in the model; the exception was active SMU and nervousness among girls, which was still significant.

Heavier passive and active SMU were linked with anxious affect for both boys and girls. However, the dose-response curve was more linear for girls than for boys, with girls showing greater risk of anxious affect with each level of social media use after 1–2 h a day and boys showing a shallower curve with a more sudden increase in risk at the heaviest amounts of use (7 + hours a day). This pattern, with a shallower dose-response curve among boys, is consistent with previous research [10]. In addition, multilevel models demonstrated significant interaction by gender for passive SMU for both worry and nervousness and for active SMU and nervousness.

Results were relatively consistent across world regions, suggesting that the association between SMU and anxious affect exists globally. Particularly large effect sizes were seen in Central/Eastern Europe.

Implications

There are important implications of this work for researchers and policymakers. These results show that heavy SMU is linked with anxious affect for both active and passive use, but that the association between active SMU and anxious affect is in many cases due to its co-occurrence with passive use. However, active SMU was still linked to greater feelings of nervousness among girls. Future studies should investigate the specific psychological mechanisms underlying the adverse effects of active and passive consumption, such as upward social comparison, fear of missing out, and reduced time spent with friends in real life. The observed gender and regional differences also highlight the need for culturally sensitive and demographically specific research designs to explore unique risk factors and protective mechanisms. The presence of the association between heavy SMU and anxious affect across many world regions suggests the need for tailored, region-specific interventions that consider local cultural norms and digital media landscapes.

Strengths and limitations

The large, representative international dataset is a clear strength of this study, allowing a view of associations between social media use and anxious affect across many nations. Additionally, our study distinctly leverages a dataset that quantifies hours spent in passive versus active SMU, rather than the more general measures of SMU common in other large samples. Through more granular accounting of SMU time, we describe a dose-response relationship with active vs. passive SMU and anxious affect.

Our findings are subject to several limitations that offer valuable directions for future work. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data prevents us from establishing causality. Separating the results by sex and controlling for socioeconomic status does account for two important confounders. However, this analysis cannot determine whether anxious affect leads to heavier SMU or vice versa. Longitudinal and experimental studies will be helpful in disentangling this temporal relationship [17]. It is possible that SMU and mental health symptomatology interact bidirectionally, as found by one recent systematic review [14]. For example, individuals with greater depressive or anxious symptoms may engage in SMU more often due to depressive features like anhedonia. Additionally, SMU may contribute to depressive or anxious symptomatology through unwanted upward social comparison or dissatisfaction with levels of social support.

Second, the self-reported nature of SMU may be susceptible to recall bias and social desirability. Future work will benefit from objective measures of SMU such as smartphone passive sensing of time spent on different apps and a more multidimensional measure of active and passive SMU. Relatedly, the categorization of SMU into “active” and “passive” types is a simplification of a more complex reality. Future research should explore a wider array of nuanced activities on social media to capture its multidimensional nature [33], such as posting, commenting, direct messaging, or engaging with different types of content (e.g., learning something vs. watching for entertainment). Additionally, the PISA SMU items use broad time intervals, and treating the recoded midpoint-hours variable as pseudo-continuous in the multilevel models introduces assumptions about the spacing between categories that cannot be fully verified. In particular, the open-ended upper category (more than 7 h per day) precludes precise characterization of associations at the highest levels of use. Future research using SMU measures with finer-grained time intervals or ecological momentary assessment (EMA) would better characterize exposure-response patterns across the full range of daily use.

Third, the measure of active SMU included emails and chat, so it is not a dedicated measure of active SMU. However, it is likely that the vast majority of time spent on these activities involved social media platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, and Snapchat; the high correlation between active and passive SMU (r = .63) supports this assertion. Fourth, although we controlled for socioeconomic status and analyzed data separately by gender, other possible confounds (such as family structure) were not measured in the dataset. Fifth, PISA’s planned missingness design precluded examining anxious affect as a scale and limited us to examining single item measures of anxiety, which may limit construct reliability and validity.

Sixth, full OECD-recommended replicate-weight variance estimation using PISA’s 80 balanced repeated replication (BRR) weights was not computationally feasible within the multilevel modeling framework used here. Final student weights were applied as precision weights, an approach that has been shown to yield equivalent point estimates with slightly conservative standard errors [16]; significance tests are therefore biased towards Type II rather than Type I error. Readers should nonetheless interpret reported standard errors with this constraint in mind, and future work with greater computational resources may benefit from full BRR variance estimation.

Finally, while this study highlighted regional differences, further investigation should attempt to identify the specific cultural, social, and economic factors within each region that contribute to the variation in the association between SMU and anxious affect. A deeper understanding of these factors can better inform targeted interventions and policies across different global contexts.

The findings of the current study provide a broader perspective on the relationship between social media use and anxious affect, demonstrating that the link appears for both active and passive use, across world regions, and among both boys and girls, though passive use and use among girls is more strongly linked to anxious affect.
