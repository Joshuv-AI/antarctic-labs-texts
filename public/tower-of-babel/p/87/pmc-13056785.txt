Foot-and-mouth disease virus-like particle mRNA vaccine induces strong humoral and cellular immunity
Authors: Haiyun Liu, Yun Zhang, Suyu Mu, Fengping Hou, LiXin Jiang, Zi Wang, Hu Dong, Muhammad Muntazir Mehdi, Huichen Guo, Shiqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is a major animal infectious disease that has garnered significant international attention. Currently, conventional inactivated vaccines and virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines primarily induce protective immune responses through the generation of neutralizing antibodies. However, these vaccines exhibit limited activation of cellular immunity and offer short-term immune persistence. Furthermore, inactivated vaccines raise certain biosafety concerns. In this study, an FMD mRNA vaccine capable of self-assembling into non-infectious VLP was developed. Immunization of mice demonstrated that this VLP mRNA vaccine not only elicited robust humoral immune responses with long-lasting antibody production but also effectively activated cellular immunity. In guinea pig challenge experiments, it provided immune protection comparable to that of traditional inactivated vaccines. The dual immune mechanism—simultaneously activating both humoral and cellular immunity—overcomes the limitations of traditional vaccines, making this VLP mRNA vaccine an innovative candidate for FMD control and providing a technical foundation for the development of VLP mRNA vaccines for other animal diseases.Key points• A novel FMD mRNA vaccine that self-assembles into VLP was successfully developed.• The FMD VLP mRNA vaccine effectively stimulates both humoral and cellular immune responses.• The FMD VLP mRNA vaccine provides protective efficacy in guinea pig challenge models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13812-2.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth Disease (FMD) is a highly contagious viral disease primarily affecting even-toed ungulates, caused by the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV). Upon infection, blisters appear on the skin and mucous membranes, with the most typical clinical symptoms occurring in the mouth and hooves. FMDV belongs to the family of non-enveloped, single-stranded positive-sense RNA viruses, comprising seven serotypes (A, O, C, SAT1, SAT2, SAT3, and Asia1), each of which lacks cross-protection. The virus is widespread across many regions globally, characterized by rapid transmission and high incidence, posing significant threats to livestock health and substantially affecting international trade in animal-derived products (Abousenna et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2024).

Currently, the primary approach for preventing and controlling FMD is the use of inactivated vaccines. These vaccines preserve the full antigenicity of the virus and provide effective immunity. However, they have several drawbacks, including weak cellular immune responses, limited duration of protection, the necessity for multiple doses, and potential biosafety concerns (Cao et al. 2016). In recent years, virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines have emerged as an innovative alternative (Mignaqui et al. 2019). VLPs, which mimic the structure of natural virus particles but lack viral genetic material, are safe and effective at presenting antigenic epitopes. They do not induce the production of non-structural protein antibodies, which allows for distinguishing between vaccinated and wild-type infected animals. Despite these advantages, VLP vaccines often suffer from insufficient cellular immune responses and generally require adjuvants to enhance immunogenicity (Mignaqui et al. 2020). VLP for FMD can be self-assembled in host cells by co-expressing the viral P1 precursor protein and 3 C protease, forming particles that resemble the natural virus structure and are capable of eliciting immune responses (Kristensen et al. 2022).

mRNA, as an emerging therapeutic platform, has become a focal point of research (Sahin et al. 2014). mRNA vaccines function by entering host cells and initiating transient translation in the cytoplasm without integrating into the host genome, eliminating risks related to genomic alteration. Additionally, mRNA possesses inherent adjuvant properties that can effectively activate both innate and adaptive immune responses, including T-cell and B-cell immunity (Wei et al. 2024). The use of pseudouridine-modified mRNA further reduces the risk of excessive immune reactions, thereby enhancing safety and boosting antigen production (Holtkamp et al. 2006). Due to the benefits of efficient in vitro synthesis and simplified production processes, mRNA vaccines are increasingly recognized for their potential in rapid disease prevention and control (Maruggi et al. 2019). Thus, developing mRNA vaccines for FMD is of significant importance.

In this study, we focus on the O-type FMDV strain O/BY/CHA/2010 and present the design and development of a novel VLP mRNA vaccine. By employing chimeric mRNA synthesis, we achieved co-expression of the FMDV P1 precursor protein and the low-cytotoxicity 3 C protease (L127P mutant), enabling the self-assembly of VLP displaying natural antigenic epitopes within host cells. Immunization of mice demonstrated that the VLP mRNA vaccine induced robust and long-lasting humoral immunity, effectively activated both T-cell and B-cell responses, and provided protective efficacy comparable to traditional inactivated vaccines in guinea pig challenge experiments. This study offers a significant theoretical foundation and experimental reference for the design of FMDV mRNA vaccines, with promising applications in the field of disease prevention.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of FMDV VLP mRNA

The sequence of the FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine, P12A3C, corresponds to the FMDV strain O/BY/CHA/2010 (GenBank accession number: JN998085.1). In this sequence, L127 of the 3 C protease was mutated to P127 to reduce the cytotoxicity of the 3 C protease (Puckette et al. 2017). The codon-optimized sequence was inserted between the 5' and 3' untranslated regions (UTRs), and a 100-nucleotide poly(A) tail with a 20% C-modified structure was added to the 3' end to enhance mRNA translation efficiency (Li et al. 2022). The plasmid was linearized using the restriction endonuclease SapI (Beyotime, D6590M) and served as a template for in vitro synthesis of the pseudouridine-modified target mRNA (mP12A3C) via the T7 Co-transcription RNA Synthesis Kit (Synthgene, C30112). This modification helps to avoid excessive immune activation and enhances antigen production (Andries et al. 2015).

Preparation of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)

The LNP-mRNA formulations were prepared using a novel microfluidic method by following Cheng et al. (Cheng et al. 2020). The LNP components, including SM102, DSPC, cholesterol (Chol), and DMG-PEG, were dissolved in ethanol at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5. The mP12A3C mRNA was dissolved in citrate buffer (20 mM, pH 4.0). The lipids and mRNA solutions were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:3 using a microfluidic mixer (Rapid Nanomedicine System, INano E). The resulting LNP-mP12A3C complexes were dialyzed against RNase-free phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) for 24 h to remove residual ethanol. Subsequently, the complexes were filtered through a 0.22 µm filter. The particle size and zeta potential of the LNP-mP12A3C complexes were measured using a Malvern instrument (Malvern, UK), and their morphology was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi, HT7700) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) was assessed using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R11490). Aliquots of LNP-mP12A3C were stored at 4℃ for several weeks; during this period, the particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and EE% were periodically monitored.

Western blot(WB)

BHK-21 cells were transfected with LNP-mP12A3C for 24 h, after which the cells were washed with PBS and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013C). The whole cell lysate was mixed with 5 × loading buffer and boiled for 10 min before separation on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel. Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane using a Bio-Rad semi-dry transfer apparatus and blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h. The membrane was then washed three times with PBST and incubated with a pig anti-FMDV polyclonal antibody (dilution ratio 1:1000) at room temperature for 1 h. After another three washes with PBST, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-labeled goat anti-pig secondary antibody (Bioss, bs-0309Rs) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the membrane was washed three times with PBST, and the protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence system (CLINX, China).

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

After transfection of BHK-21 cells with LNP-mP12A3C for 24 h, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100. The cells were blocked with 5% BSA at 37°C for 1 h and then incubated with VHH antibody (07-His) specific to FMDV empty capsids at 37°C for 1 h. After five washes with PBST, cells were incubated with anti-His-FITC secondary antibody (Abcam, ab3554) at 37°C for 1 h. Following five additional washes with PBST, the cells were stained with DAPI for 10 min, then washed three times with PBST. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM980 + Airyscan2).

Mouse experiment

Six- to eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 10) to assess the immunogenicity of the FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine. The experimental group received 10 µg of LNP-mP12A3C via intramuscular injection, while the control group was injected with an equal volume of empty liposomes as a negative control. At designated time points, the mice were anesthetized, and blood was collected from the orbital sinus. The serum was separated by centrifugation and analyzed for binding and neutralizing antibodies. To evaluate cellular immune responses, the mice were euthanized on days 10 and 14 post-primary immunization, and their lymph nodes and spleens were collected to prepare single-cell suspensions for further T and B cell functional assays.

Detection of specific antibodies

FMDV-specific antibodies were detected using a commercial liquid-phase blocking ELISA kit (Lanwei, SWK.FMDV.01) produced by Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Serum samples were serially diluted two-fold in U-shaped bottom plates and mixed with an equal volume of FMDV O/BY/2010 antigen, incubated overnight at 4°C. Meanwhile, 96-well plates were coated with rabbit anti-FMDV IgG (5 µg/mL) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The plates were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS at 37°C for 1 h. The serum-antigen mixture (50 µL) was transferred to the coated ELISA plates and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The plates were washed five times with PBST and then incubated with guinea pig anti-FMDV antibody (1:1000 dilution) at 37°C for 1 h. After washing, 100 µL of HRP-labeled anti-guinea pig antibody (1:3000 dilution) was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After additional washing, 100 µL of TMB substrate solution was added and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 µL of 2 M sulfuric acid, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (Bio-Rad, USA).

Neutralizing antibody assay

Serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min and serially diluted in two-fold intervals. The diluted serum was mixed with 100 TCID50 of FMDV O/BY/2010 and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The serum-virus mixture was then transferred to 96-well plates seeded with confluent BHK-21 cells. After 1 h of incubation at 37°C, the supernatant was removed, and fresh DMEM medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum was added. After 72 h, cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed, and the neutralizing antibody titer was calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

ELISpot

To assess the immune response of specific T and B lymphocytes, an enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay was performed to measure the secretion of IFN-γ (Dakewe, 2,210,005), IL-2 (Mabtech, 3441-4HPW-2), and IL-4 (Dakewe, 2,210,402). Splenic lymphocyte suspensions (2 × 105 cells per well) were stimulated with FMDV VLP and seeded into 96-well plates pre-coated with anti-IFN-γ, anti-IL-2, and anti-IL-4 antibodies (Dakewe, China). Phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) was used as a positive control, while unstimulated cells served as a negative control. After incubation at 37℃ with 5% CO2 for 24 h, the plates were washed five times with 1× wash buffer. Biotinylated detection antibodies (diluted 1:100) were then added and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. Following another five washes, streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase conjugate (diluted 1:100) was added and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. The plates were washed five times again, and fresh substrate solution was added for color development. The reaction was terminated by thorough rinsing with deionized water. Spots were quantified using an ELISpot reader system (AE.L.VIS, Hamburg, Germany), and the data were analyzed using ELISpot analysis software.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions of lymphocytes were washed with PBS, resuspended in complete medium, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/mL. For intracellular cytokine staining (ICS), 1 × 106 cells per sample were stimulated with FMDV VLP (10 µg/mL) at 37°C for 2 h, followed by incubation with brefeldin A (Invitrogen; 1:1000 dilution) for an additional 4 h at 37°C. Cells were then washed and stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Green Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by surface and intracellular staining according to standard procedures. Th1 cells were defined as CD3⁺ CD4⁺/CD8⁺ IFN-γ⁺ IL-2⁺ cells. For the phenotypic analysis of Tfh, GC B, and memory T-cell subsets, cells were stained directly ex vivo without antigen-specific restimulation or brefeldin A treatment. Tfh cells were identified as CD3⁺ CD4⁺ CXCR5⁺ PD-1⁺ cells, GC B cells as CD3⁻ CD19⁺ FAS⁺ GL7⁺ cells, Tcm cells as CD3⁺ CD4⁺/CD8⁺ CD44⁺ CD62L⁺ cells, and Tem cells as CD3⁺ CD4⁺/CD8⁺ CD44⁺ CD62L⁻ cells. After staining, samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and at least 50,000 events were collected for each sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software. Detailed gating strategies and antibody information are provided in Supplementary Figure S5.

Ab-secreting cells (ASC) ELISpot

To measure antigen-specific B cells, ELISpot plates (Mabtech, 3654-TP-10) were coated with 50 μg/mL of FMDV VLP overnight at 4°C. Mouse splenic lymphocytes were pre-incubated with IL-2 and R848 for 3 days at 37°C and 5% CO₂. Biotinylated anti-IgG antibody was added, followed by a streptavidin-peroxidase conjugation reaction (Mabtech, 3825-2H). Substrate solution was added for spot development, and color development was stopped by thorough rinsing with deionized water. Spots were quantified using an ELISpot scanner (AE.L.VIS, Hamburg, Germany).

Guinea pig FMDV challenge experiment

Guinea pigs (300–400 g) were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 5 per group) to assess the protective efficacy of the FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine. The commercial O-type FMDV inactivated vaccine (500 μL per animal) served as the positive control, blank lipid nanoparticles (equal volume) as the negative control, and LNP-mP12A3C (20 μg per animal) as the experimental group. All animals were intramuscularly injected at weeks 0 and 3. Blood samples were collected at predetermined time points, centrifuged to obtain serum, and analyzed for binding and neutralizing antibodies. Four weeks post-immunization, guinea pigs were challenged in the left hind footpads with 100 50% median infective doses (100 ID50) of FMDV (0.2 mL). Clinical symptoms were monitored daily. Animals with no lesions were considered fully protected. Blood was collected daily for viral load monitoring. Six days post-challenge, guinea pigs were euthanized, and heart, lung, and spleen tissue samples were collected, homogenized, and subjected to RNA extraction. Viral RNA copy numbers were detected by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Some lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and sent to Servicebio® (Wuhan, China) for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess histopathological changes.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.) and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group differences were assessed using either unpaired t-tests or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***) or P < 0.0001 (****).

Results

Construction and characterization of FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine

The mRNA vaccine for FMDV using the P12A3C construct was designed, and the mRNA expressed the target proteins exactly and self-assembled into VLP (Fig. 1A). The codon-optimized and pseudouridine-modified mP12A3C was synthesized through in vitro transcription. Subsequently, mature LNP technology was employed to encapsulate mP12A3C. The encapsulated product (LNP-mP12A3C) exhibited an average particle size of 83.95 ± 7.60 nm with a narrow distribution and a PDI of 0.081 ± 0.011, indicating uniform particle size distribution, excellent monodispersity, and an encapsulation efficiency of up to 99.71% (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed that the synthesized lipid nanoparticles had an actual diameter of approximately 80 nm (Fig. 1C), which is conducive to endocytosis. Following incubation of LNP-mP12A3C with BHK-21 cells for 24 h, cell lysates were collected and subjected to WB analysis, revealing successful expression of FMDV-specific structural proteins VP0, VP1, and VP3 (Fig. 1D). The encapsulated formulation was stored at 4°C for up to 26 weeks, and its physicochemical and biological stability were evaluated at regular intervals. As shown in Figure S1A, the particle size of the lipid nanoparticles remained highly stable over the entire storage period, with no obvious change observed, while the polydispersity index (PDI) was consistently maintained below 0.2. Denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis further showed that, after disruption with 0.5% Triton X-100, the mRNA released from LNP-mP12A3C displayed a band corresponding to the expected theoretical size (Figure S1B), indicating that the integrity of the encapsulated mRNA was well preserved during storage. Moreover, western blot analysis demonstrated that LNP-mP12A3C retained its capacity to express the target protein after storage at 4°C for 26 weeks (Figure S1C). Taken together, these results demonstrate that LNP-mP12A3C possesses excellent storage stability at 4°C. To further verify VLP formation, we purified the supernatant from transfected cells via sucrose density gradient centrifugation and successfully isolated FMDV VLP. Dynamic light scattering revealed their average size to be approximately 25 nm, consistent with observations made by transmission electron microscopy, indicating that the nanoparticles were similar to the previously reported FMDV virus-like particles(Guo et al. 2013) (Fig. 1E). Denaturing and non-denaturing WB analyses further confirmed the presence of macromolecular complexes composed of FMDV-specific structural proteins VP0, VP1, and VP3 (Fig. S1D). The FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine induces robust humoral immunity and Th1-biased cellular immunity in BALB/c mice

Subsequently, the immunogenicity of the LNP-mP12A3C vaccine candidate in BALB/c mice was evaluated according to the immunization schedule depicted in Fig. 2A. Sera analysis revealed detectable levels of specific antibodies one week after primary immunization (1:16), with antibody titers rapidly increasing and stabilizing following booster immunization (Fig. 2B). Neutralizing antibody levels peaked during the second week following the booster (1:512) (Fig. 2C), demonstrating that the VLP-forming mRNA vaccine effectively mimicked the antigenic properties of the authentic virus. To quantify antigen-specific cellular immune responses, ELISpot assays were performed to measure cytokine-secreting spleen lymphocytes of each group (n = 3). The results revealed that the LNP-mP12A3C vaccine group elicited significantly higher levels of IFN-γ (Fig. 2D) and IL-2 (Fig. 2E), with minimal IL-4 secretion observed (Fig. 2F), suggesting a dominant Th1-type immune response. These findings are consistent with previous reports on mRNA vaccines (Pan et al. 2023; Sahin et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2025). Intracellular cytokine staining further corroborated this result, demonstrating markedly increased expression of Th1-associated antiviral cytokines IFN-γ (Figs. S2A and 2C) and IL-2 (Figs. FMDV VLP mRNA vaccines induce robust memory cell immunity in BALB/c mice

We further analyzed the proportions of central memory T cells (Tcm; CD3+CD4+/CD8+CD44+CD62L+) and effector memory T cells (Tem; CD3+CD4+/CD8+CD44+CD62L−) in splenic lymphocytes two weeks after the second immunization with the LNP-mP12A3C vaccine. Compared with the LNP group, mice vaccinated with LNP-mP12A3C exhibited a significantly enhanced activation of both CD4+ and CD8+ Tcm populations (Fig. 3A and C), as well as Tem populations (Fig. 3B and D). In addition, the frequencies of germinal center (GC) B cells and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells in the draining lymph nodes were assessed on day 10 post-immunization. The results showed that both GC B cells and Tfh cells were markedly increased in the LNP-mP12A3C group compared with the control group (Fig. 3E and F), indicating that this vaccine effectively promotes germinal center responses. Furthermore, antibody-secreting B cells in splenic lymphocytes were evaluated 18 weeks after immunization (Fig.3G). The LNP-mP12A3C-vaccinated mice exhibited a significantly higher number of antigen-specific memory B cells than those in the LNP group. The FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine induced a strong humoral immune response in guinea pigs

The immunogenicity of the LNP-mP12A3C vaccine was further assessed in guinea pigs, a model organism for FMDV, with a commercial inactivated vaccine used as the positive control. The vaccination and challenge schedule is depicted in Fig. 4A. After the primary immunization, the LNP-mP12A3C vaccine induced lower antibody levels compared to the commercial inactivated vaccine. However, two weeks following the booster immunization, antibody levels in the LNP-mP12A3C group increased rapidly and reached levels comparable to those induced by the commercial inactivated vaccine, with no significant difference observed (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the neutralizing antibody levels in the LNP-mP12A3C group were significantly higher than those in the inactivated vaccine group at this time point (Fig. 4C). The FMDV VLP mRNA vaccine provides protection in guinea pigs

To determine whether the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C vaccine confers protective immunity, guinea pigs were challenged with FMDV one week after the booster immunization. Anticoagulated blood was collected daily for six days post-challenge, and RNA was extracted to monitor the viral load. The results showed that viral load in the LNP group peaked on day 2 post-challenge and then gradually declined each day. In contrast, viral loads in both the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C and inactivated vaccine groups remained consistently low throughout the observation period, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 5A). Footpad lesions were monitored and recorded daily. Guinea pigs in the LNP group developed typical FMD symptoms, such as footpad blisters, the earliest among all groups. In contrast, no typical blisters were observed in the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C or inactivated vaccine groups during the entire observation period. Mild redness and swelling were noted only during the first three days post-challenge, resulting in a 100% protection rate (Fig. 5B). After the completion of the challenge study, heart, lung, and spleen tissues were collected for quantitative analysis of viral RNA levels. Viral RNA in the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C and inactivated vaccine groups was significantly lower than that in the LNP group (Fig. 5C). Lung tissues were subjected to H&E staining, and histopathological examination revealed lymphocyte infiltration and hemorrhage in the lungs of the LNP group. However, lung tissues from the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C and inactivated vaccine groups exhibited minimal pathological changes and appeared largely normal. Discussion

FMDV is a notifiable animal pathogen under the World Organisation for Animal Health (OIE) and is classified as a Category A animal infectious disease in China. Outbreaks of FMD cause severe economic losses to the livestock industry and profoundly disrupt domestic and international trade, earning the disease the designation of a “political and economic disease” (Humphreys et al. 2025; Zewdie et al. 2023). Vaccination remains the most effective and practical strategy for FMD control (Elrashedy et al. 2025; Mohamadin et al. 2025). Although widely used inactivated vaccines and VLP vaccines are capable of inducing protective neutralizing antibodies, each platform has inherent limitations. Inactivated vaccines carry biosafety risks during large-scale production, while VLP vaccines, despite avoiding the use of live virus, share a major drawback with inactivated vaccines—namely, relatively weak cellular immune responses and a dependence on oil-based adjuvants to enhance immunogenicity. Oil adjuvants, however, may induce local adverse reactions such as fibrosis, granuloma formation, or abscesses, limiting their long-term application in veterinary practice (Chen et al. 2021). In contrast, mRNA vaccines offer distinct advantages, including high biosafety, flexible antigen design, and intrinsic immunostimulatory properties that obviate the need for conventional adjuvants (Aleem et al. 2024).

Nanoparticle-forming vaccines are particularly attractive because they can mimic the native structure of viral particles, present conformational epitopes in their natural context, and efficiently activate both T- and B-cell responses. In addition, they overcome the low antigen density typically associated with subunit vaccines, thereby inducing robust humoral immunity (Sun et al. 2025; Wenjun Wang et al. 2021). In recent years, rapid progress in nanoparticle-based protein vaccines has significantly advanced the prevention and treatment of infectious diseases in both humans and animals (Abbas et al. 2025; Z. Liu et al. 2014; W. Wang et al. 2019). However, the development of nanoparticle-forming mRNA vaccines remains relatively limited (Fougeroux et al. 2025; J. Liu et al. 2024). In this study, we designed an mRNA vaccine based on the FMDV P1 precursor protein and the proteolytic activity of the 3 C protease, enabling in vivo self-assembly of VLP. Following encapsulation in lipid nanoparticles and administration to mice, this vaccine elicited strong humoral immune responses and efficiently activated cellular immunity. Importantly, in guinea pig challenge experiments, it conferred protective efficacy comparable to that of a commercial inactivated vaccine.

Previous studies have demonstrated that particles with diameters ranging from 20 to 200 nm exhibit prolonged circulation times, efficiently drain into lymph nodes, and effectively activate dendritic cells (Link et al. 2012; Manolova et al. 2008). Such nanoparticles markedly enhance both T cell–mediated cellular immunity and B cell–mediated humoral immunity. In the present study, self-assembled nanoparticles expressed by LNP-mP12A3C–transfected cells were successfully purified using sucrose density gradient centrifugation and comprehensively characterized. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed uniform, hollow nanoparticle structures, while non-denaturing Western blotting confirmed the formation of FMDV-specific macromolecular complexes. Furthermore, antibodies specific to FMDV VLP verified the efficient intracellular assembly of VLP driven by LNP-mP12A3C. To confirm VLP formation in vivo, muscle tissue at the injection site and inguinal lymph nodes were collected from immunized mice at 8-, 12-, and 24-h post-immunization for immunofluorescence analysis. Green fluorescence was detected in muscle tissue as early as 8 h post-injection, indicating rapid VLP formation, and signals appeared in lymph nodes by 12 h (Fig. S4). These results demonstrate that the FMDV mRNA P12A3C construct enables efficient VLP self-assembly both in vitro and in vivo, and that the assembled particles are rapidly captured by antigen-presenting cells and transported to draining lymph nodes for effective antigen presentation.

The induction of immunological memory is essential for rapid pathogen clearance and long-term protection (Pardi et al. 2018). Conventional vaccine platforms, including inactivated and protein subunit vaccines, often elicit suboptimal cellular immune responses and show limited antibody durability, raising concerns regarding their capacity to establish long-lasting immune memory. In this study, the FMD LNP-mP12A3C vaccine effectively activated both central memory T cells (Tcm) and effector memory T cells (Tem), providing a solid immunological basis for rapid recall responses upon pathogen exposure. In addition, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells are critical for germinal center (GC) formation and the generation of memory B cells. Tfh cells promote GC B-cell activation and affinity maturation, thereby enhancing the magnitude and durability of neutralizing antibody responses (Bai et al. 2022). Our results showed that the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C vaccine robustly induced Tfh cells and GC B cells in mouse lymph nodes, facilitating the generation of long-lived, high-affinity neutralizing antibodies and sustained immune protection. Consistent with these findings, antibody levels at weeks 17 and 18 post-immunization were comparable to those observed at week 5 (Fig. S3), highlighting the advantage of VLP-forming mRNA vaccines in promoting GC responses, memory B-cell formation, and long-term antibody maintenance.

Although the FMDV LNP-mP12A3C vaccine elicited strong humoral and cellular immune responses overall, guinea pig immunization data showed that antibody levels following primary immunization were slightly lower than those induced by the traditional inactivated vaccine. Notably, after booster immunization, the LNP-mP12A3C vaccine rapidly induced high titers of specific and neutralizing antibodies, and by two weeks post-boost, neutralizing antibody levels exceeded those of the inactivated vaccine group. These findings indicate that the VLP-forming LNP-mP12A3C vaccine not only induces a potent humoral immune response but also provides superior protective immunity upon boosting. Consistent with this conclusion, the guinea pig challenge study demonstrated a 100% protection rate in the LNP-mP12A3C–vaccinated group, further confirming the robust and reliable protective efficacy of this novel mRNA vaccine platform.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, although this study demonstrated the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of the vaccine in mouse and guinea pig models, these results cannot be directly extrapolated to pigs—the natural host for FMDV. Given that pigs are a primary host for FMDV, future research should focus on evaluating the vaccine's efficacy in pigs to confirm its immunogenicity and protective effects in the natural host. Additionally, clinical application studies should include dose optimization to ensure the vaccine induces an optimal immune response while minimizing potential adverse effects. To further improve the practical utility of the vaccine, we should also explore strategies such as optimizing the lyophilization process or incorporating suitable stabilizers (e.g., trehalose, sucrose, or specific amino acids) into the lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulation to enhance room-temperature stability (Cerav et al. 2026). Secondly, although this study focused on the mRNA vaccine capable of forming virus-like particle (VLP), the precise origin of the observed immune response has not been fully established. Ideally, to attribute the observed immunogenicity specifically to VLP assembly, future studies should include appropriate mechanistic controls—such as non-assembling mutant constructs (e.g., 3C-deficient or assembly-defective capsids) and/or antigen-matched protein or VLP controls (Kunishima et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2026). Such experimental designs will help clarify the role of VLP self-assembly in inducing the immune response. Lastly, although we assessed IL-4 as a marker for Th2-type immune responses in this study, reliance on a single immune marker is insufficient for a comprehensive evaluation. A more complete assessment of the Th1/Th2 immune balance requires broader cytokine profiling. For example, measuring additional Th2 cytokines (e.g., IL-5, IL-13, and IL-10) (Rauch et al. 2021) and serum immunoglobulin subtypes (e.g., the IgG1/IgG2a ratio) (Kunishima et al. 2025) will provide a more thorough characterization of the immune response and further support our conclusion that the Th1-type response predominates.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully integrates the advantages of VLP and mRNA to develop an FMDV mRNA vaccine capable of self-assembling into VLP. In mice, this vaccine effectively stimulates both T-cell and B-cell immune responses and induces long-lasting immune memory. It demonstrates robust immunoprotective efficacy in guinea pig models, making it a promising new candidate for the prevention and control of foot-and-mouth disease. Moreover, this VLP-based self-assembling mRNA vaccine platform not only provides valuable insights into the development of FMDV vaccines but also lays a solid foundation for advancing mRNA vaccine technologies for other animal diseases.
