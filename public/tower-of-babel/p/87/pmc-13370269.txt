Ligand isomerism in coordination cages: to interlock or not to interlock
Authors: Moumita Sarkar, Ayan Ghosh, Shobhana Krishnaswamy, Dillip Kumar Chand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The combination of Pd(ii) with a set of three regioisomeric neutral bis-monodentate ligands in DMSO medium, in separate pots, yielded Pd2L4-type cages, depicting ligand isomerism in coordination cages (isomer-1, -2 and -3). These tetra-cationic cages, having spacious cavities and wide windows, exhibited differential supramolecular behaviours in the solution state upon aging in terms of remaining intact as a Pd2L4-type cage or undergoing the interlocking of a pair to form a (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cage. One of the isomeric cages (isomer-3) could form the corresponding (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cage when parameters like counter anion, concentration, and time were optimized. However, isomer-1 and -2 remained intact as Pd2L4-type cages irrespective of parameter variation. For isomer-3, the counter anion of choice either exclusively favoured (for NO3−) or largely favoured (for BF4− or ClO4−) interlocking or arrested the cage (for OTf− or OTs−) with no interlocking. The compatibility of selected anions with the newly created pair of peripheral cavities of the interlocked cage and other supramolecular interactions could overcome the entropy penalty of interlocking. Additional special features of the interlocked cage include triply interlocked architecture instead of quadruply; a relatively longer central cavity (1.8 times that of the peripheral cavities) instead of being equal sized with the peripheral cavities; and the presence of solvent molecules in the central cavity of the interlocked cage instead of accommodating counter anions.

Introduction

Mechanically interlocked molecules such as catenanes,1–16 interlocked cages,17,18 rotaxanes,19,20 pretzelanes,21 and knots22 are fascinating supramolecular architectures in terms of their structural sophistication and diverse functions.22 Molecules having a pair of mechanically interlocked macromonocyclic rings, macropolycyclic cages, or combinations thereof belong to the extended family consisting of 2-catenanes and related interlocked cages.1–18 The interlocked components in 2-catenane and related systems can be as varied as follows: (i) both can be covalent organic rings/cages, (ii) both can be coordination rings/cages, or (iii) one can be a covalent organic ring/cage and the other a coordination ring/cage.10,11,16,23

The Fujita group introduced self-assembled coordination ring/cage-based 2-catenane and related interlocked systems for which they employed cis-protected Pd(ii) and suitable neutral bis-monodentate/tris-monodentate ligands.24,25 A family of 2-catenanes and related interlocked molecules having (Pd2L′2LaLb)2- and (Pd3L′3LcLd)2-type formulations could be prepared, where L′ stands for a cis-protecting group around Pd(ii).16,24–27 The bis-monodentate ligands La and Lb may be the same or different, as is the case for the tris-monodentate ligands Lc and Ld; however, the charges are not shown in the formula.16,17,25,26 Later, the Kuroda group carried out the complexation of Pd(ii) with a bis-monodentate ligand, L, which afforded another variety of self-assembled quadruply interlocked (Pd2L4)2-type cage (Fig. 1A).28 At the onset, the Pd2L4-type macrotricyclic coordination cage was formed, but two units of the cage interlocked upon aging to form a 2-catenane-like interlocked cage. A few examples of quadruply interlocked (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cages have been reported since then.29–38

A schematic depiction of (A) Pd2L4-type cages and corresponding “quadruply interlocked” (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cages;29–38 (B) a unique Pd2L4-type cage and corresponding “triply interlocked” unusual (Pd2L4)2-type system;43 and (C) a family of Pd2L4-type “isomeric cages” and the phenomenon of exclusive interlocking in the case of one of the isomers to form an unusual “triply interlocked” (Pd2L4)2-type system versus no interlocking and the retention of the Pd2L4 architecture in the other isomers (this work).

Structural evaluation of Kuroda's quadruply interlocked cages reveals that all four ligand strands of a given Pd2L4 unit pass through the four windows of the other Pd2L4 unit and vice versa, in such a manner that each window accommodates one ligand strand only, as shown in Fig. 1A. All four metal centres are disposed almost collinearly in such molecules. Three well-separated cavities of nearly equal size are created in the interlocked cage, of which one is a centrally located inner cavity and the other two are peripherally located outer cavities. It has been observed that counter anions played the role of templates and remained trapped in these three cavities.28,29,31–33,36,37 It may be noted that counter anions that are a reasonable match in size with the cavities are required for the interlocking of Pd2L4 units, but that alone is not a sufficient condition. The replacement of bound anions, usually the anions present in the outer cavities, with other externally added anions has also been reported.29,30,33,34 The sizes of the cavities are modulated as a result of the anion exchange. This is feasible whenever the modulated cavities have higher affinity towards the incoming anions as compared to that of the pre-modulated cavities towards the outgoing anion. There are only a few examples of (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cages,29–38 as compared to Pd2L4-type cages.39–41 The interlocking of Pd2L4-type cages requires spacious cavities and wide windows, as reinforced by ligand design as well as fitting anions.38,42

The Clever group has recently reported a unique (Pd2L4)2 system where two units of a Pd2L4 type cage are triply interlocked43 (Fig. 1B), in contrast to the usual quadruply interlocked architecture (Fig. 1A). The inner cavity of this triply interlocked cage is slightly longer than the outer cavities (1.2 times the outer cavities). The longer cavity is connected with each of the shorter cavities via a narrow intramolecular trench-like space.

We are interested in exploring ligand isomerism, with a focus on self-assembled Pd2L4-type cages.41,44,45 Ligand isomerism in coordination cages is represented by isomeric cages that are prepared from a common metal component but with isomeric ligands. In the present work (Fig. 1C), we disclose the differential interlocking abilities of a family of three isomeric Pd2L4-type coordination cages (isomer-1 to -3) in DMSO medium. One of the isomers (isomer-3) preferred interlocking upon aging to form a (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cage in an exclusive manner but in the presence of a selected counter anion. The other two isomers completely avoided interlocking, irrespective of counter anion, thereby retaining the Pd2L4 architecture. These isomeric cages are prepared separately by the complexation of Pd(ii) with a set of regioisomeric bis-monodentate ligands. The additional special features of our interlocked cage include the triply interlocked architecture, relatively longer inner cavity (1.8 times the outer cavities), and the presence of solvent molecules in the inner cavity of the interlocked cage instead of counter anions.

Results and discussion

We previously reported a family of Pd2L4-type isomeric coordination cages,44 namely [Pd2(L1)4](NO3)4, 1·4NO3; [Pd2(L2)4](NO3)4, 2·4NO3; and [Pd2(L3)4](NO3)4, 3·4NO3, as shown in Fig. 2A in schematic format (anions are not shown). These first-generation cages exemplify the concept of ligand isomerism. The cages are prepared by the complexation of Pd(NO3)2 with the 3-pyridyl-appended isomeric bis-monodentate ligands L1, L2 and L3. These ligands are isomeric, as qualified by the presence of an isomeric central spacer involving ortho-, meta- or para-phenylene moieties. Each central spacer is connected to the 3-pyridyl units at each end by urea functionality. A comparison of the properties of the isomeric cages with respect to the adsorption of CO2 showed differential abilities, as controlled by the emerging behaviours of the cages.

Design and synthesis of the isomeric ligands

For the present work, we have modified the ligands L1, L2 and L3 to prepare a new set of isomeric ligands that are longer and wider, but the relative directions of their coordination vectors are unchanged when a comparison is made for specific conformations. These new ligands L4, L5 and L6 (Fig. 2B) are crafted with additional para-phenylene units between the urea and terminal 3-pyridyl moieties at each end of the ligands L1, L2 and L3. Complexation of Pd(NO3)2 with the ligands L4, L5 and L6 should produce a set of second-generation Pd2L4-type isomeric coordination cages, i.e. [Pd2(L4)4](NO3)4, 4·4NO3; [Pd2(L5)4](NO3)4, 5·4NO3; and [Pd2(L6)4](NO3)4, 6·4NO3 (Fig. 2B, anions are not shown). The targeted set of isomeric coordination cages would have spacious cavities and wider windows as compared to the reported first-generation cages. Thus, the new cages might favour the interlocking phenomenon to afford 2-catenane-like interlocked cages under the optimized conditions. Probing the interlocking and exploring the similarities/differences between the behaviours of the three isomeric cages were our primary objectives. The ligands L4, L5 and L6 were synthesized by multiple steps, where the final step in each case involved the condensation of 4-(pyridine-3-yl)benzoyl azide with appropriate diaminobenzenes (see SI, Scheme S2). The characterization of ligands L4, L5 and L6 was carried out through NMR spectroscopy and ESI-MS techniques (see SI, Fig. S10–S33). The peaks in the 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 3) were assigned by analyzing the corresponding COSY spectra. However, NOESY spectral analysis was required for the assignment of urea NH protons (see SI, Fig. S15, S22, and S29).

Complexation of Pd(NO3)2 with isomeric ligands: Pd2L4-type isomeric cages

Complexation of Pd(NO3)2 with the bis-monodentate ligands L4, L5 and L6 was carried out separately at a 1 : 2 ratio (at a 5 mM concentration with respect to Pd(ii)) (see SI, Scheme S3) to prepare a series of isomeric Pd2L4-type coordination cages 4·4NO3, 5·4NO3 and 6·4NO3 (Fig. 2). The complexation reactions were carried out in DMSO-d6 medium by stirring the reaction mixture for 15 minutes at room temperature. A comparison of the 1H NMR spectra of the free ligands with the corresponding complexes revealed the usual complexation-induced shift of the signals, as shown in Fig. 3. The Pd2L4-type complexes were characterized by various spectroscopic techniques including 1H NMR, 13C NMR, COSY and HSQC methods (see SI, Fig. S34–42 and S44–S55). The signals observed in the 1H and 13C NMR spectra of complexes were assigned by analysing COSY and HSQC spectra, whereas the identification of NH protons of urea moieties required the NOESY data of the compounds (see SI, Fig. S39, S47 and S54). The Pd2L4-type molecular composition of the cages was supported by ESI-MS data, where the peaks at m/z = 553.6495, 553.6423 and 553.6501 are in good agreement with the calculated isotopic pattern of the corresponding polycationic species [M-4NO3]4+ generated by the loss of counter anions from the cages 4·4NO3, 5·4NO3, and 6·4NO3, respectively (see SI, Fig. S64–S69).

As per the crystal structures of the cages (discussed below), the bound ligand strands have C2-symmetry in the cationic cages [Pd2(L4)4]4+ and [Pd2(L6)4]4+ where the urea oxygens are syn to each other; however, C2-symmetry is not there for the bound ligand strands in the case of [Pd2(L5)4]4+ where the urea oxygens are anti to each other (Fig. 2B and C). The conformations of the bound ligands L4, L5 and L6 in the corresponding Pd2L4-type cages can be named as anti–syn–anti, anti–anti–syn (or syn–anti–anti) and syn–syn–syn, respectively (SI, Fig. S1). The same ligand conformations and comparable 1H NMR spectral behaviour were observed in our first-generation coordination cages,44i.e. [Pd2(L1)4]4+, [Pd2(L2)4]4+ and [Pd2(L3)4]4+. Alternate planar conformations of the ligands suitable for the construction of Pd2L4-type cages are also possible. Thus, we don't rule out the possibility of other conformations or mixed conformations in the architecture of [Pd2(L5)4]4+ in the solution state. In fact, crystallization of different conformational isomers of cationic [Pd2(L)4]4+- and neutral [(Cu2)2(L)4]-type coordination cages has been achieved by our group46 and the Bloch group,47 respectively, by altering the counter anion and/or co-solvent during crystallization. Keeping in mind the crystal structures, there should be one set of peaks in the 1H NMR spectrum of [Pd2(L4)4]4+ as well as that of [Pd2(L6)4]4+, but two set of peaks could be expected for [Pd2(L5)4]4+. We observed single sets of peaks for all three cages when 1H NMR spectra were recorded in DMSO-d6. The single set of peaks for [Pd2(L5)4]4+ corresponding to eleven types of protons (nine signals due to some overlaps) is attributed to fast exchange of the ligand conformations within the cage architecture. In principle, it should be possible to arrest the ligand conformation of [Pd2(L5)4]4+ in the solution state by lowering the temperature so as to observe two set of peaks, i.e. for twenty types of protons, in line with the crystal structure; in fact, more than two sets of peaks could also be expected due to the fact that four orientational isomers (see SI, Fig. S43) could be imagined for the cage considering the absence of C2-symmetry for the bound ligand strands. We employed CD3CN : DMSO-d6 as solvent to depress the freezing temperature so that we could record NMR spectra at lower temperature. Due to solubility issues, the maximum proportion of CD3CN was limited to 25%. The concentration of the cage was maintained at 5 mM with respect to Pd(ii) for consistency. Single sets of sharp peaks are observed at 297, 282, and 273 K in CD3CN : DMSO-d6 (1 : 3), indicating fast conformational exchange (see SI, Fig. S43). The signals were broadened upon further lowering of the temperature to 263 K, indicating slow conformational exchange even at such a low temperature, though decreased solubility of the cage and solvent freezing could also be probable reasons for the signal broadening.

Ligand isomerism in Pd2L4-type cages: to interlock or not to interlock

While the 1H NMR spectra of 4·4NO3 and 5·4NO3 confirmed the presence of single discrete species in each case, we recognized some unassignable additional peaks in the spectrum of 6·4NO3 (Fig. 3), where the concentration is 5 mM with respect to Pd(ii) for all three cages. This observation indicated the coexistence of a non-negligible amount of some other species along with 6·4NO3 in the solution state. The proportion of coexisting species in dynamic equilibrium often varies with the variation of one or more parameters like (i) time, (ii) concentration, and (iii) reaction temperature. Additionally, counter anions are also known to play a role in product selectivity. No further changes in 1H NMR spectra were observed upon leaving the samples prepared at 5 mM concentration with respect to Pd(ii) to stand, indicating that time has no influence at this concentration.

The samples prepared at a 10 mM concentration with respect to Pd(ii) revealed new developments. The samples containing 4·4NO3 and 5·4NO3 are found to be unchanged upon an increase in concentration, i.e. behaviour is independent of concentration (see SI, Fig. S73 and S74), whereas considerable changes were observed in the case of the sample containing 6·4NO3 with the progress of time in such a manner that we concluded that 6·4NO3 is a kinetic product at this higher concentration. The peaks due to the binuclear complex 6·4NO3 diminished with time and then disappeared, while several new peaks evolved concomitantly (Fig. 4) (see SI, Fig. S75–S77). Some of these new peaks grew initially and then diminished until they disappeared completely, indicating the formation of at least another kinetic product, P, during the course of reorganization. Remaining of the new peaks persisted at the cost of all other peaks (Fig. 4). All of the persisting peaks could be assigned to a single molecule, i.e. a (Pd2L4)2-type triply interlocked unusual cage, [Pd2(L6)4]2(NO3)8 (6′·8NO3) (Fig. 4), as discussed below. Exclusive formation of this thermodynamic product required approximately 30 days at room temperature, but it could be achieved within 15 days at 70 °C. Thus, time, concentration and temperature favoured the formation of 6′·8NO3.

(B) 500 MHz 1H NMR spectra (DMSO-d6, 298 K) for (i) L6; (ii)/(iii) a mixture containing 6·4NO3 and other products, including P and 6′·8NO3, obtained by mixing Pd(NO3)2 with L6 at room temperature, after 15 min and 1 h, and (iv) exclusively formed 6′·8NO3 obtained after 30 days. (Note: concentration with respect to Pd(ii) = 10 mM in (A) and (B).).

While ligand L6 and the Pd2L4-type complex 6·4NO3 displayed one set of peaks in the 1H NMR spectrum of each compound, the thermodynamic product showed four sets of peaks. ESI-MS studies suggested the formation of the Pd4L8-type complex [Pd4(L6)8](NO3)8, 6′·8NO3. The peak at m/z = 923.0266 for [M-5NO3]5+ is in good agreement with the calculated isotopic pattern (see SI, Fig. S70–S72). The DOSY NMR spectrum of the sample showed a single band with a diffusion coefficient of 6.185 × 10−11 m2 s−1 (see SI, Fig. S63). Four possible architectures are known for a complex with Pd4L8 composition obtained from Pd(ii) and bis-monodentate ligands. The architectures are: (1) a molecular square (with each arm having two ligand strands),48 (2) a tetrahedron (with two diagonally opposite arms with two ligand strands and remaining four arms with one ligand strand each),49 (3) a pair of quadruply interlocked cages,28–38 and (4) a pair of triply interlocked cages.43 Among these four architectures, the triply interlocked cages correspond to four sets of signals in the 1H NMR spectrum.43 Thus, we propose the formation of the triply interlocked (Pd2L4)2-type cage [Pd2(L6)4]2(NO3)8, 6′·8NO3. The interlocked cage formation is attributed to dimerization of 6·4NO3 in a triply interlocking fashion which grew as a function of time. It is reasonable to expect that a few units of nitrate anions are trapped in the cavities of the interlocked cage.

The 1H NMR spectrum of an individual Pd2L4-type cage44 prepared from a C2-symmetric ligand exhibits one set of signals, whereas quadruply interlocked (Pd2L4)2-type cages28–38 show two sets of signals due to the loss of symmetry in the bound ligand, as attributed to the different local environment of each half. Thus, the ligand moieties of the interlocked cage are demarcated using two different colours (see Fig. 1A) to depict two different environments for the otherwise symmetrical ligands. Notably, for the triply interlocked (Pd2L4)2-type cage43 there are four sets of signals in the 1H NMR spectrum due to a further loss of symmetry (see Fig. 1B); thus, the ligands are indicated using four different colours in Fig. 1B. The two-ligand strands of a given Pd2L4 unit (see the two pink-blue strands in Fig. 1B) that are cis to each other pass through a common window offered by the other Pd2L4 unit (see the window created by the two green-yellow ligands in Fig. 1B) and vice versa. However, the remaining two ligand strands pass through one window that is offered by the partner Pd2L4 and vice versa. Thus, one of the windows of both Pd2L4 units is left unused (see the window created by the two pink-blue ligands shown in Fig. 1B). The doubly penetrated window and the unused window of a given cage are located diagonally opposite to each other. All four metal centres of the interlocked cage are not colinear, as the Pd2L4 units are parallelly displaced away from each other.

Role of the counter anion: to favor interlocking or to arrest the cage

Complexation of Pd(X)2 (where X− = NO3−, BF4−, ClO4−, OTf−, or OTs−) with the ligands L4, L5 and L6 was performed in DMSO-d6 at a 10 mM concentration with respect to Pd(ii) at room temperature. The complexation reactions were monitored by the 1H NMR method. On the basis of the 1H NMR spectral study, we propose the formation of the Pd2L4-type cages [Pd2(L4)4](X)4 (4·4X) and [Pd2(L5)4](X)4 (5·4X) upon the use of ligands L4 and L5, as concluded from the single set of peaks in their spectra. There was no signature of interlocking in these cases. However, a comparison of the peak positions among the complexes with a given ligand but with different counter anions confirmed that the spectra do not exactly match with each other. Thus, the interaction of anions, in exo/endohedral manners, with the Pd2L4-type cages obtained from L4 and L5 is proposed (see SI, Fig. S78 and S79).

However, anions played a significant role in determining whether there was interlocking or no interlocking in the cases of complexes obtained using ligand L6. As discussed earlier, the complexation of Pd(NO3)2 with L6 produces [Pd2(L6)4](NO3)4, 6·4NO3, as the kinetic product but [Pd2(L6)4]2(NO3)8, 6′·8NO3, as the exclusive thermodynamic product. The complexation of Pd(BF4)2 and Pd(ClO4)2 separately with L6 initially resulted in their corresponding Pd2L4-type complexes [Pd2(L6)4](BF4)4 (6·4BF4) and [Pd2(L6)4](ClO4)4 (6·4ClO4) as major products (see SI, Fig. S80). Subsequently, the proportion of these Pd2L4-type complexes diminished but not completely, whereas the interlocked versions [Pd2(L6)4]2(BF4)8 (6′·8BF4) and [Pd2(L6)4]2(ClO4)8 (6′·8ClO4) evolved as the major products (∼80%) upon heating the reaction mixtures at 70 °C for 24 h. No significant changes could be detected upon further heating the reaction mixtures at 70 °C for 12 days (see SI, Fig. S81 and S82). Thus, a dynamic equilibrium of cage and interlocked-cage products is proposed for these anions, unlike the exclusive interlocking when the counter anion is NO3−.

1H NMR spectra of the cages [Pd2(L4)4](OTs)4 (4·4OTs) and [Pd2(L5)4](OTs)4 (5·4OTs) exhibited distinct chemical shift values for certain protons (Hb in 4·4OTs and Ha in 5·4OTs) as compared to the corresponding signals of the cages having other counter anions, i.e.4·4X and 5·4X, respectively. Also, complexation of Pd(OTf)2 and Pd(OTs)2 separately with L6 resulted in the Pd2L4-type complexes [Pd2(L6)4](OTf)4 (6·4OTf) and [Pd2(L6)4](OTs)4 (6·4OTs); no signature from interlocking was observed in the samples of 6·4OTf and 6·4OTs even after heating at 70 °C for 15 days. The lack of interlocking can be related to the well-known binding of sulfonate anions (OTf− and OTs−) with Pd2L4-type cages via endo-/exohedral modes, which would hinder interlocking.50–54 It has also been established that relatively bigger sulfonate anions such as OTf− and OTs− are unsuitable for templating the smaller-sized cavity that must be created upon the interlocking of two units of a Pd2L4-type cage.55

We examined the effect of OTf− and OTs− on the NO3−-bound interlocked cage 6′·8NO3 by mixing tetrabutylammonium triflate (TBAOTf) and tetrabutylammonium tosylate (TBAOTs), in separate experiments, with the interlocked cage in DMSO-d6. The 1H NMR spectrum of 6'·8NO3 remained unchanged even after the addition of 8 equivalents of TBAOTf or TBAOTs (SI, Fig. S83 and S84). However, upon the addition of 4 equivalents of TBANO3 to a DMSO-d6 solution of the representative cage [Pd2(L6)4](OTf)4, 6·4OTf, a new set of minor signals emerged, which, upon aging at room temperature for 30 days, yielded a nitrate-encapsulated interlocked cage, [(NO3)(DMSO) ⊂ Pd2(L6)4]26+ (see SI, Fig. S85). This observation suggests that the NO3−-templated interlocked cage (6'·8NO3) is a thermodynamically stable structure and it does not undergo disassembly or conversion to the corresponding Pd2L4 cage in the presence of OTf− or OTs−.

Crystal structures of the Pd2L4-type cages

It may be noted that Pd2L4-type cages such as [Pd2(L4)4](X)4 (4·4X) and [Pd2(L5)4](X)4 (5·4X) could be obtained exclusively irrespective of the counter anion (X− = NO3−, BF4−, ClO4−, OTf−, or OTs−). Thus, the ligands L4 and L5 are well suited for the construction of cages but not for interlocked cages. In the case of the ligand L6, cage/interlocked-cage formation is anion dependent. Among the counter anions used, only OTf− and OTs− are found suitable for the making of Pd2L4-type cages, i.e. [Pd2(L6)4](X)4, 6·4X. We attempted to crystallize the above-mentioned complexes for structure determination.

Single crystals of the complexes 4·4ClO4, 5·4OTs, and 6·4OTs, suitable for X-ray diffraction studies, were obtained from their DMSO solutions by the slow vapor diffusion of ethyl acetate. The structures of the cages are shown in Fig. 2C, and details are provided in SI Section S5. For simplicity of discussion, the cationic parts of the crystalline complexes grown from 4·4ClO4, 5·4OTs, and 6·4OTs are designated as [4]4+, [5]4+, and [6]4+, respectively. The cages are constructed with two Pd(ii) centers that are bridged by four bis-monodentate ligands. The coordination vectors of the two pyridine units of a bound ligand strand are parallel to each other as a geometrical requirement of the cage structure. The disposition of urea moieties in the ligand backbone controls the relative positions of the coordination vectors. In a given ligand strand, both the urea oxygen atoms are exohedrally pointed in [4]4+ and endohedrally pointed in [6]4+; the scenario is mixed in the case of [5]4+ where the disposition of one oxygen is exohedral and the other oxygen is endohedral. The urea hydrogen atoms are located on the opposite side (anti) to the oxygen atoms. The non-bonded distances between the two metal centers of the cages are found to be approximately 16.4, 20.6 and 22.3 Å for [4]4+, [5]4+, and [6]4+, respectively. The cages are oval-shaped, where a pair of bound ligand strands that are located trans to each other, along with the coordinated metal centers, describes an ellipse. The intermetallic distance of a given cage can be considered as the length of the major axis of the ellipse. The approximate ratio of the major axes of the three cages is 1.00 : 1.25 : 1.36. Since the shapes are not geometrically perfect, it is not appropriate to give the length of the minor axes of the ellipses. The crudely approximated lengths of the minor axes are, however, around 16, 13 and 11 Å for [4]4+, [5]4+, and [6]4+, respectively. Thus, the shape of [4]4+ can be considered closer to spherical.

Crystal structures of the triply interlocked (Pd2L4)2-type cage

Some of the cages from the family of [Pd2(L6)4](X)4, 6·4X, favored interlocking depending upon the counter anion. Parameters like time, concentration and temperature could be adjusted to make the interlocked cage formation faster. Particularly, the use of NO3− as a counter anion resulted in the exclusive formation of an interlocked cage, i.e. [Pd2(L6)4]2(NO3)8, 6′·8NO3. Single crystals of this interlocked cage were obtained by the slow vapor diffusion of ethyl acetate into a DMSO solution of the sample. The crystal structure of the interlocked cage revealed the existence of two Pd2L4 units (Fig. 5 and 6) that are triply interlocked. The meaning of triply interlocked is described in the Introduction section (and also depicted in Fig. 1 and 4). Each interlocked Pd2L4 unit exhibits a nonbonded Pd–Pd distance of 21.9 Å, which is slightly shorter than a not-interlocked Pd2L4 unit (22.3 Å for 6·4OTs), though having another counter anion. A cage unit is horizontally/parallelly displaced by approximately 3 Å from the other unit, as estimated from the distance between imaginary Pd–Pd axes that are parallel to each other.

The crystal structure of [(EtOAc)(DMSO)(NO3) ⊂ Pd2(L6)4]2(NO3)6, 6′·8NO3·solvent, highlighting the nitrate-bound outer cavities, DMSO-bound inner cavity, and EtOAc-bound intramolecular trenches (hydrogen, exohedral counter anions and solvent molecules are not shown for clarity).

Four different views (space-filled models) of the interlocked cage 6′·8NO3·solvent to depict the triply interlocked architecture (hydrogen, counter anions and solvent molecules are not shown for clarity).

There are two relatively shorter outer cavities and one relatively longer inner cavity in the interlocked cage. Each of the three cavities is bordered by a pair of parallelly displaced Pd(ii)-based coordination square planes of different Pd2L4 units, vertically separated by approximately 7.7 Å for each of the shorter cavities and 14.2 Å for the longer cavity (1.8 times longer). The cavities are slightly tilted in view of the displaced cage units, where the non-bonded distances between bordering Pd(ii) are approximately 8.2 Å for the shorter cavities and 14.5 Å for the longer cavity. The shorter cavities accommodate one nitrate ion in each whereas the longer inner cavity accommodates two DMSO molecules. The longer cavity is connected to the two shorter cavities via two trench-like intramolecular spaces. These trenches accommodate ethyl acetate molecules, with one unit in each. The encapsulated species are held through multiple supramolecular interactions provided by the cage units. Four different views of the interlocked cage are given in Fig. 6 to clearly depict the triply interlocked architecture. One of the interlocked cages of 6′·8NO3 is labelled as cage I and the other as cage II. The figure shows one ligand strand of cage I passing through a window of cage II (Fig. 6A), two ligand strands of cage I passing through a window of cage II (Fig. 6B), one ligand strand of cage I passing through a window of cage II (Fig. 6C), and no ligand strands of cage I passing through a window of cage II (Fig. 6D).

Supramolecular interaction driven interlocking

An obvious curiosity is to seek the reason behind the interlocking of a pair of Pd2L4-type cages to form the corresponding entropically unfavorable (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cage. However, it is not easy to offer a precise response, though the thermodynamic stability of the interlocked cage as compared to the two units of the participating cage is the ultimate reason. The Hiraoka group has proposed the anion matching index (AMI) as a parameter to predict the suitability of certain anions for templating the cavities of (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cages.38 Although the AMI can be considered as vital, it is not the only parameter that would guarantee the formation of an interlocked cage. The stability of interlocked architectures is governed by a complex interplay of factors, including ligand design (length and steric hindrance), solvent effects, concentration, temperature, the shape/size of counter anions, and favourable noncovalent interactions between the partner cages to compensate for the entropic penalty of interlocking.18,31,37

The binding of a fitting counter anion in the cavity of a tetra-cationic Pd2L4-type cage by electrostatic interaction with the metal center and H-bonding with electron-deficient H atoms located at the inner wall of the cage is a usual phenomenon.39,44,56,57 In contrast, the reported44 crystal structure of [Pd2(L3)4](NO3)4, 3·4NO3, shows that the counter anions (NO3−) are present outside the cavity. There are eight urea moieties with sixteen urea protons in a cage of 3·4NO3 that are exohedrally pointed and suitable H-bond donors for binding the counter anions (NO3−) outside the cavity. Further, the endohedrally disposed urea O atoms near the metal center discourage anion binding due to repulsion. We assume that for the cage [Pd2(L6)4](NO3)4, 6·4NO3, a similar exohedral preference might ensue for the counter anion (NO3−); however, since the urea O atoms are away from the metal centre, an endohedral binding preference is also possible. The cavity size is probably bigger and less-fitting size-wise for holding NO3− tightly. However, the interlocking of two Pd2L4 units created smaller-sized outer cavities that accommodated NO3− with a good fit, thereby contributing to the thermodynamic stability. Besides, there are other weak supramolecular forces observed in the crystal structure in favor of interlocking. As shown in Fig. S90, electron-deficient pyridine rings (see the orange-colored half-strand) and the carbonyl oxygen of a nearby urea moiety (see the green-colored half-strand) are engaged in lone pair…π (lp–π) interactions that are located at a distance of 3.39 Å (two such cases are observed). Attractive lp–π interaction is a well-recognized supramolecular force,58 and interaction between the lone pair of carbonyl oxygen and an aromatic center in certain protein structures has been documented.59 Two of the H atoms of the electron-deficient phenyl-pyridine moieties (orange-colored half-strand) and urea oxygen atoms (pink-colored half-strand) are also engaged in H-bonding (2.67 and 3.01 Å), as shown in Fig. S90 (two such cases). We propose that the interactions observed in the solid state of the interlocked cage should be possible in the solution state as well. A few more examples of such lp–π interactions and H-bonding interactions are likely in the solution state due to conformational movements, though the distances in the solid state are seemingly not favorable in the frozen structures. Besides, the presence of solvent molecules in the inner cavity and intramolecular trenches, having weak interactions that include the participation of the inner walls of cavities, also provides stability to the interlocked cage. In contrast, there is no endohedral oxygen in [Pd2(L4)4]4+; also, only half of the urea oxygens are endohedral in [Pd2(L5)4]4+. Probably, the weak supramolecular interaction in these cases is not enough to overcome the entropy loss if interlocking were to happen. However, the reason behind the triple interlocking is still a big curiosity for us.

Conclusions

We have designed and synthesized a series of regioisomeric bis-monodentate neutral ligands and their corresponding Pd2L4-type tetra-cationic coordination cages to depict ligand isomerism. The second-generation isomeric cages were designed to have spacious cavities and wide windows in anticipation of the self-assembly of the molecules to form interlocked cages under optimized conditions. Each of the ligand strands has two urea units, thus there are eight urea units in a Pd2L4-type cage. It was observed that the endohedral disposition of all eight urea O atoms observed in one of the isomeric cages (isomer-3) favored the formation of a (Pd2L4)2-type interlocked cage that is equipped with a central cavity and two peripheral cavities. In fact, a special type of triply interlocked cage was formed instead of the usual quadruply interlocked cage, whereupon two additional trench-like spaces evolved in the interlocked architecture. Such dimerization happened upon aging a solution of the Pd2L4-type cage; however, the presence of a selected counter anion (NO3−) at the required concentration (10 mM with respect to Pd(ii)) was necessary. The loss of entropy due to dimerization is compensated by the stability achieved by binding specific anions in the peripheral cavities of the interlocked cage and solvent molecules in the central cavity and intramolecular trenches. In the case of some other counter anions (BF4− or ClO4−), an equilibrium mixture containing a major proportion of interlocked cages and a minor amount of non-interlocked cages was observed. Anions like OTf− and OTs− didn't allow dimerization at all by blocking the cavity of the cage molecules. The other two isomeric cages (isomer-1 and -2) remained as such, irrespective of counter anion, concentration and reaction time. In isomer-1 and -2, the dispositions of the urea O atoms are not-at-all endohedral and partially endohedral, respectively, thereby discouraging interlocked cage formation. The specific finding of the present work is the importance of ligand isomerism in coordination cages for the retention of the cage or the dimerization of a pair of cages under optimized conditions. Our work proposes isomerism as a new parameter for consideration during the design and synthesis of mechanically interlocked molecules or if there is a need to suppress mechanical interlocking.
