inhibitory effect of hyperin on Staphylococcus aureus pathogenicity though interactions with sortase A and sortase B
Authors: Ting Wu, Tingzheng Xia, Fadi Cao, Bin Liu, Fan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSortase A (SrtA) and Sortase B (SrtB) promote S. aureus adhesion by anchoring a variety of virulence proteins to the surface of the cell wall, which in turn promotes the formation of bacterial biofilms and triggers cytotoxicity and inflammation. Therefore, inhibiting the function of SrtA and SrtB to block the anchoring of virulence proteins is a promising mechanism to alleviate the pathogenicity of S. aureus. In this study, we found that a natural compound named hyperin occupied the active pockets of SrtA and SrtB and inhibited their transpeptide capacity, which resulted in a reduction in bacterial biofilm formation and the adhesion of bacteria to lung epithelial cells. Hyperin did not have antimicrobial properties or cytotoxicity at the test concentrations but decreased the cytotoxicity and inflammation of cells induced by S. aureus USA300. In vivo, hyperin significantly alleviated the tissue damage and melanin formation of infected G. mellonella (G. mellonella) and improved the survival rate of the hyperin-treated group. In a mouse pneumonia model, hyperin improved the survival rate of mice infected with S. aureus USA300, and the bacterial burden and inflammatory factor levels decreased significantly. Taken together, our results confirm that hyperin is a promising reliable natural compound for combating diseases caused by S. aureus infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23458-1.Keywords: Natural products, Anti-infection, Hyperin, Inflammation, SorstaseSubject terms: Antimicrobials, Microbiology, Bacteria, Bacterial pathogenesis, Bacterial toxins

Introduction

Multiple mechanisms are involved in Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) infection1, such as those involving the well-known sortase family that anchors virulence proteins by recognizing and cleaving specific motifs2, and the notorious secretion of exotoxin α-hemolysin (αHL), and β-lactamase, which triggers bacterial resistance. αHL helps S. aureus bind to host cell membrane-specific receptors to form oligomers, piercing the cell membrane and interfering with the normal function of cells, thus promoting the development of S. aureus pneumonia3, endocarditis and embolization4. S. aureus β-lactamase hydrolyses the amide ring of β-lactam antibiotics, resulting in the reduction or even loss of the killing effect of β-lactam antibiotics on S. aureus5,6. These complex pathogenic mechanisms make the prevention and control of S. aureus infection difficult, therefore, the development of new anti-infective strategies and anti-infective agents is necessary.

The sortase family is a class of membrane proteins expressed by S. aureus and some other gram-positive pathogens, which contains multiple members, classified as A, B, C, etc7,8. S. aureus SrtA and SrtB are the two most studied members; SrtA recognizes proteins containing LPXTG motifs (X can be any residue), cleaves polypeptides between the threonine (T) and the glycine (G) of the motif and anchors these virulence proteins to cell wall covalently9. There are more than twenty proteins that contain LPXTG motifs expressed by S. aureus and they increase the pathogenicity of S. aureus through multiple mechanisms, such as fibronectin binding protein A and B (FnBPA and FnBPB), which are involved in the adhesion of S. aureus to target cells10–12; clumping Factor A (ClfA) and B (ClfB), which promote the adhesion, immune evasion and colonization of S. aureus to the host13–15; iron-regulated surface protein A (IsdA) and B (IsdB), which help S. aureus capture and transport iron ions16–18; and others mechanisms, including those that promote biofilm and persister formation, triggers inflammation19,20. When srtA is mutated, S. aureus displays defects in the expression of surface proteins21, and the pathogenicity of S. aureus to laboratory animals is significantly decreased21. S. aureus SrtB recognizes and cleaves NPQTN motifs. In S. aureus, the iron-regulated surface protein C (IsdC), which is involved mainly in the transport of iron ions, has been identified as containing this motif22. It has also been reported that S. aureus SrtB contributes to the promotion of S. aureus infections by mechanisms involving inflammation and persistence22–25. The mechanisms described above suggest that SrtA and SrtB could be potential targets for combating S. aureus infection.

Hyperin is a flavonol glycoside compound that widely exists in Hypericaceae, Rosaceae, Platycodon, Leguminosae and other plants, and it has many physiological properties26, such as anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer27, liver-protective properties28; as a result, hyperin exerts protective effects against nonalcoholic fatty liver disease29 and other diseases30. However, whether hyperin reduces the pathogenicity of S. aureus through antivirulence activity has not been reported. This study revealed that hyperin competitively occupied the active sites of S. aureus SrtA and SrtB, reducing the transpeptidization activity of these two enzymes, thus decreasing the formation of S. aureus biofilms and adhesion to lung epithelial cells, and alleviating the cytotoxicity and inflammatory response triggered by S. aureus. In vivo, hyperin had a protective effect in Galleria mellonella and mouse pneumonia models. However, hyperin did not have an anti-αHL ability or synergistic effects with ampicillin (Amp).

Methodology

Strains, growth and the reagents

S. aureus USA300 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium. LB was obtained from Qingdao Hopebio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Hyperin was obtained from CHENGDU HERBPURIFY Co., Ltd. Dabcyl-QALPETGEE-Edans for SrtA and Dabcyl-QANPQTNEE-Edans for SrtB were purchased from GL Biochem (Shanghai) Ltd. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and foetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Protein expression

A pET-28a vector that had been inserted into the genes of SrtA and SrtB of S. aureus separately was constructed and stored in our laboratory. Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) was cultured in LB medium and the expression of the target proteins was induced by the addition of 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at 18 °C for 18 h. Bacteria were harvested and lysed, the supernatant was obtained and incubated with Ni-NTA His-Tag Purification Agarose (Sangon Biothech) for 1 h. Then the target proteins were harvested by washing with 200 mM imidazole (Sigma).

Transpeptidase activity assay

The transpeptidase activity assay was performed according to methods described previously with some modifications31,32. Purified SrtA or SrtB protein (2 µM) was coincubated with different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, 60, 120 µM) for 30 min at 37 °C. The substrate peptides with fluorophores of SrtA or SrtB (8 µM) were subsequently added to each well and coincubated in dark for 1 h. The inhibitory effect of hyperin against the transpeptide ability of SrtA or SrtB was analysed by measuring the fluorescence intensity of each sample (excitation wavelength 350, emission wavelength 520). Transpeptidase activity was calculated based on the formula as follows: (Fsamples/F0) ×100, where F0 represent the fluorescence intensity of a sample that did not receive hyperin treatment and Fsamples represents the fluorescence intensity of a sample treated with different concentrations of hyperin.

Computational biology

The initial structures of SrtA and SrtB were obtained from RCSB Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) with PDB ID of 1T2W or 1QWZ. The 3D structure of hyperin (CAS number: 482-36−0) was obtained from PubChem. AutoDock Vina33 was used to perform the calculations. The complex of SrtA or SrtB with hyperin was used to perform molecular dynamics simulations by using GROMACS 2020.634 as described in the referring methods35,36.

Site-directed mutagenesis of residues

Site-directed mutagenesis of residues was carried out according to a previously described method with some modifications37. The primers that used for this assay were generated by using Gene Runner software and are shown in Table 1. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed based on the primers and the pET-28a plasmids in which S. aureus srtA or srtB gene was inserted. The PCR products were treated with DpnI enzyme at 37 °C for two hours and they were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). The purification of the mutant proteins also followed the method described above.

Surface plasmon resonance assay (SPR)

Nitrilotriacetic acid (CM5) was used as the sensor chip, which was constructed on the basis of a Biacore X100 system (GE Healthcare). First, the His antibody was arranged on the sensor chips, after which the target proteins (SrtA or SrtB or their mutants) were captured by the His antibody, after which buffer containing various concentrations of hyperin was added (10 µL per min). Finally, the data were harvested and analyzed.

Antimicrobial activity experiment

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of hyperin, Amp or their combination against S. aureus USA300 was determined on the basis of the methods described in the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). In brief, serious concentrations of hyperin (2.85–365 µM), Amp (2.85–365 µM) or Amp combined with hyperin (60 µM) were prepared based on doubling dilution, after which S. aureus USA300 was added to each well at a final concentration of 5 × 105 colony-forming units/mL (CFUs/mL). The samples were cultured at 37 ℃ for 24 h, and the MICs of hyperin and Amp combined with or without hyperin were determined by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm. The growth curve assay was carried out according to methods described previously38, S. aureus USA300 (the absorbance at 600 nm was approximately 0.3) was cocultured with different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, 60, 120 µM) at 37℃ with shaking. Samples were harvested at the specified time points, after which the absorbance at 600 nm was measured.

Biofilm assay

Overnight-cultured S. aureus USA300 was diluted to new fresh LB medium (1:100) with a bacterial density of approximately 3 × 107 CFUs/mL. One hundred microlitres of the sample was transferred into a clean 96-well plate, after which 100 µL of fresh LB medium supplemented with different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, 60 µM) was added to each well, and the cells were cultured at 37 ℃ for 24 h. After the medium that contained the plankton was removed, the samples were washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After drying, the samples were stained with 0.2% crystal violet solution for 15 min. The absorbance value at 570 nm was detected after the samples were treated with 35% glacial acetic acid31,39.

Cytotoxicity detection

Cytotoxicity detection was carried out according to a previously described method with some modifications40. A549 lung cancer epithelial cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and cultured overnight. The next day, free DMEM that supplemented with S. aureus USA300 and various concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, 60 µM) was used to replace the medium, and the multiplicity of infection (MOI) was 60. After six hours of co-culturing, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for five minutes, after which an equal volume of the supernatant was transferred to another clean 96-well plate and an equal volume of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) agent (Beyotime) was added to each well. After being incubated for 30 min in the dark, the absorbance was detected at 490 nm to determine the inhibitory effect of hyperin on S. aureus cytotoxicity. For the live/dead cell staining assay, after the cells were cultured with S. aureus USA300 and hyperin for six hours, they were stained with live/dead agents (Beyotime) for 30 min in the dark after the cultural medium was removed, and the cells were subsequently washed with sterile PBS. Images were obtained with a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, IX83). The cytotoxicity of hyperin at the tested concentrations was determined by detecting LDH levels released into the medium when cells were treated with hyperin.

Bacterial adhesion cell experiments

Pneumonia is the most common disease caused by S. aureus, and lung epithelial A549 cells have been widely used as a model cell line to investigate the inhibitory effects of inhibitors on bacterial adhesion. Therefore, in this study, A549 cells were used to perform adhesion experiments. The bacterial adhesion cell assay was performed based on the methods described previously with some modifications41,42. Overnight cultural A549 cells (in 24 cell plate, 1 × 105 cells/well) were treated with S. aureus USA300 (MOI = 30) and different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, or 60 µM) for 1.5 h. Afterwards the culture medium was removed, and the cells were harvested after being washed with PBS three times. Equal volume samples were used to count the number of colonies by plating onto LB agar medium and culturing them overnight at 37℃.

Western blot assay

S. aureus was cultured with different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, or 60 µM) for 8 h, after which the bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (12000 rpm, 2 min) and SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added. The samples were incubated at 100 °C for 10 min. The proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane, after which the expression level of SrtA and the levels of clfA (a bacterial surface protein anchored by SrtA) were detected by using specific antibodies. ClfA antibody (Cusabio, CSB-PA692021ZA01FLB, 1:1000), and SrtA antibody (cusabio, CSB-EP3093FLF, 1:3000). ICDH was used as the internal control.

Cytokine assays

The Cytokine assay was performed based on a previously described method with some modifications43. J774a.1 mouse macrophages cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS were plated into 6-well cell culture plates (2 × 106 cells/well) and cultured overnight. The next day, fresh DMEM containing S. aureus USA300 (MOI = 5) and different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, or 60 µM) were used to replace the medium, and the cells were cultured for 5 h. The supernatant was harvested after centrifugation for 5 min (12000 rpm 4 ℃). The levels of interleukin 8 (IL-8), interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) were detected with an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Sangon Biotech).

Haemolytic activity test

S. aureus USA300 was cultured overnight and diluted to LB medium (1:100), after which different concentrations of hyperin (0, 30, or 60 µM) were added to each sample, and the samples were cultured at 37 ℃ for eight hours with shaking. The culture supernatant was obtained after centrifugation for five minutes (12000 rpm, 4 ℃). After being filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millex), 10 µL of the sample was added to 500 µL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing sterile defibrillated sheep blood (at a final concentration of 2.5%) and incubated at 37 ℃ for 10 min. After the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for one minute, an equal volume of the sample was used to determine the absorbance value at 543 nm to analyse the haemolytic capacity of the different samples. Sterile defibrinated sheep blood treated with sterile water or sterile PBS was defined as the positive control (PC) and negative control (NC).

G. mellonella infection model

The infection model was constructed based on the methods reported previously with some modifications44–46. In the logarithmic growth phase, S. aureus USA300 was suspended in sterile PBS and injected into the G. mellonella through the left foot (10 µL, 1 × 105 CFUs), and 30 min after the infection, hyperin (10 µL) was injected into the G. mellonella (50 mg/kg) with the same method. The infection group and the control group were given equal volumes of solvent and sterile PBS treatment, and nine G. mellonella were assigned to each group. The survival of the G. mellonella was observed every 12 h, and the protective effect of hyperin against S. aureus USA300 infection was analysed. Pathological lesions were observed in samples from the different groups after they were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE).

Mouse pneumonia model

The BALB/c mice (female, 6–8 weeks, approximately 20 g) were purchased from the laboratory animal centre of Jilin University. All the animal tests performed in this study followed the regulations of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Jilin University and all the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the ARRIVE guidelines. The experimental protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Jilin University. The mice were housed at a suitable temperature, and they could obtain food and water freely.

Overnight-cultured S. aureus USA300 was diluted into fresh LB medium and cultured until the absorbance value of at 600 nm reached 0.6. Afterwards, the bacteria were harvested and washed with sterile PBS. The bacterial suspension (50 µL) was dropped into the left nasal cavity to construct a pneumonia model (2 × 108 CFUs/mouse). The infected mice were randomly divided into two groups (ten mice/group). Two hours after infection, the first group was treated with hyperin (100 mg/kg) and the second group (the infection group) was treated with an equal volume of solvent without hyperin at an interval of 12 h. The control group received an equal volume of sterile PBS. The survival of the mice was monitored every 12 h, and the protective effect of hyperin on the mice infected with S. aureus USA300 was evaluated. To determine the bacterial burden and inflammation levels, 1 × 108 CFUs of S. aureus USA300 was administered to each mouse, using the same method as described above, with five mice assigned to each group. After treatment for 48 h, the mice were anesthetized by injecting pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Alveolar lavage fluid and lung tissue were harvested, the cytokine levels in the alveolar lavage fluid were detected by ELISA, and the bacterial load in the lungs was determined by plating the samples onto the LB agar medium and culturing them overnight at 37 ℃.

Statistical methods

Means with standard deviations (SDs) were used to analyse the experimental data from three independent tests. Unpaired t tests, which were embedded into GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 were used to analyse the significant differences between the data. The difference was defined as significant when p ≤ 0.05. The difference in the survival rate for the G. mellonella and mice was analysed with the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test in GraphPad Prism 9.5.0.

Results

Hyperin inhibits the transpeptidase function of SrtA by competing with its active site

The molecular structure of hyperin is shown in Fig. 1a. In the SrtA activity inhibition experiment, the inhibition was defined as 0 when hyperin was not added; however, the inhibitory effects decreased with increasing hyperin concentration. Specifically, the inhibition was 24.26%, 49.6% and 79.14%, when the concentration of hyperin was 30, 60 or 120 µM, respectively (Fig. 1b). These results suggest that hyperin inhibited the activity of SrtA in a dose-dependent manner. To explore the interactive mechanism between hyperin and SrtA, molecular docking was carried out using 1T2W as the initial structure, and it was found that hyperin bound to the active pocket where the substrate of Srt A (green) is located, the affinity between hyperin and SrtA was − 8.5 kcal/mol (Fig. 1c). To verify the reliability of the binding, a kinetic simulation of 50 ns was carried out. The superposed conformation from the different moments (Fig. 1d) and the distance between SrtA and hyperin against the simulation (Fig. 1e) suggested that the binding of hyperin to the active site is reliable. The results described above indicate that hyperin inhibited the function of SrtA by competing with its active site.

Pro163 and Ile199 are critical for the binding of SrtA to Hyperin

We predicted the potential binding sites of SrtA to LPETG and hyperin and revealed that multiple residues near the active site, such as Glu105, Ser116, Gln172, Thr180 and Leu169, were involved in the interactions between SrtA and its substrate LPETG and its inhibitor hyperin (Fig. 2a and b). However, Arg197, the key active site of SrtA, formed hydrogen bonds (Hbonds) with the Glu of LPETG (Fig. 2a), and Pro163 and Ile199 were involved only in the interaction between SrtA and hyperin (Fig. 2b). The RMSD fluctuation of SrtA indicated that it maintained a stable configuration during the simulation (Fig. 2c). In-depth analysis revealed that more than 30 pairs of Hbonds were detected in the last 10 ns of the equilibrium trajectory (Fig. 2d), but the duration of most of them were short, with only four of them having higher occupancy (Fig. 2d and e). The residues involved in the formation of these four pairs of Hbonds were Asp165, Glu105 and Glu108, the O5, O6, and O11 atoms in hyperin were engaged in the formation of these Hbonds, and their occupancies were 37.8%, 47.6%, 39.8% and 49.3%, respectively (Fig. 2f). The results of the binding free energy analysis revealed that the total binding free energy between SrtA and hyperin was − 32.46 kJ/mol, which was mainly composed of electrostatic (ele) force (−130.07 kJ/mol), van der Waals (vdw) force (−109.99 kJ/mol) and solvation (sol) forces (181.78 kJ/mol) (Fig. 2g). Decomposition of the residue energy revealed that Pro163, Ile199 and Ile182 contributed more binding free energy with the values of −10.68 kJ/mol, −6.14 kJ/mol and − 6.27 kJ/mol, respectively, and that the key active residue Arg197 contributed more ele force (−4.35 kJ/mol) (Fig. 2h). To confirm the critical binding residues of SrtA, Pro163 and Ile199 were mutated to alanine (A), after which the inhibitory effect of hyperin on these mutants decreased significantly (Fig. 2i). These results suggest that successful binding between SrtA and hyperin requires the combined action of Pro163 and Ile199.

Hyperin binds to the active pocket of SrtB to suppress its activity

SrtB (PDB ID:1QWZ) with its inhibitor (MET) located at its active pocket (Fig. 3a) was used as the initial structure to analyse the interaction model with hyperin. The results indicated that hyperin also binds to the active pocket of SrtB (Fig. 3b). Arg233, Cys223, Glu224, Asn180 and Asn92 are involved in the binding of SrtB and MET (Fig. 3c). Many residues are engaged in the binding of SrtB and hyperin, such as Tyr227, Asp225, Thr91; however, Arg233, Cys223, Glu224, Asn180 and Asn92 are not involved. In addition, some Hbonds were found in the analysis (Fig. 3d). RMSD analysis revealed that SrtB and hyperin maintained a stable configuration in the simulation experiment (Fig. 3e). The overlapping trajectories at different times (Fig. 3f) and the distance between hyperin and SrtB (Fig. 3g) also confirmed that hyperin remained in the active pocket throughout the simulation process. The inhibitory effects of hyperin on SrtB transpeptidization confirmed the reliability of the docking and simulation results, as the inhibition of SrtB activity gradually increased with increasing hyperin concentration (Fig. 3h). These results indicate that hyperin also could bind to SrtB to affect its activity.

Arg233 is important for promoting the binding of SrtB to Hyperin

The total binding free energy between SrtB and hyperin was − 42.02 kJ/mol, which mainly included ele force (−72.05 kJ/mol), vdw force (−118.48 kJ/mol) and sol force (133.29 kJ/mol) (Fig. 4a). Arg233, Leu96 and Asn180 in SrtB contributed the majority of the energy; more precisely, Arg233 contributed the majority of the ele force (−15.97 kJ/mol) and vdw force (−5.76 kJ/mol), and Asn180, Thr177 and Lys178 contributed the majority of the vdw force, with the values of −10.53 kJ/mol, −5.89 kJ/mol and − 6.63 kJ/mol, respectively (Fig. 4b). These results were also confirmed by the closer distance between these residues and hyperin (Fig. 4c). In the last 10 ns, three pairs of Hbonds were stable, with the occupancies of 85.6%, 75.1% and 94.4% respectively; Glu224 and Arg233 in SrtB, and the O1 and O2 atoms of hyperin engaged in the formation of these Hbonds (Fig. 4d and e; Table 2). Arg233 was identified as the critical residue for promoting SrtB and hyperin binding, as the binding free energy (Fig. 4f) and the inhibitory effect of hyperin against the mutant protein (R233A) decreased significantly (Fig. 4g). Taken together, these findings suggest that the interaction between hyperin and SrtB involves in Hbonds, ele force, and vdw force and that Arg233 is important for this binding.

The binding between SrtA, SrtB and Hyperin was confirmed by SPR

For further confirmation, an SPR assay was carried out to confirm the binding of SrtA, SrtB and hyperin. The dissociation constant (kD) of SrtA and hyperin was 1.951 × 10−5 M (Fig. 5a) which was obtained based on the signal response (Fig. 5b) when various concentrations of hyperin were present. With respect to SrtB, when the buffer contained serious concentrations of hyperin, the signal response increased gradually to obtain a kD value of 1.980 × 10−4 M (Fig. 5c and d). These results confirm the binding of SrtA, SrtB and hyperin, which verifies the reliability of the molecular docking and molecular simulation results.

Hyperin inhibits the formation of biofilms and the adhesion of S. aureus to A549 cells

The absorbance value of S. aureus USA300 at 600 nm was similar when the bacteria were treated with various concentrations of hyperin (Fig. 6a), suggesting that hyperin does not affect the growth of S. aureus USA300 in the tested concentration range. The formation of S. aureus biofilms was defined as 100 when the sample did not receive the hyperin treatment. The biofilms formation decreased to 70.91% and 12.63% when the samples were treated with 30 or 60 µM hyperin, respectively (Fig. 6b). Similarly, when hyperin was used to treat A549 cells infected with S. aureus, the percentage of S. aureus adherent to the cells decreased to 77.00% and 20.24% compared with that in the group that did not receive hyperin treatment (Fig. 6c). These results suggest that hyperin reduced the formation of biofilms and the adhesion of S. aureus USA300 to A549 cells by blocking the function of SrtA.

Hyperin protects A549 cells from S. aureus USA300 cytotoxicity

A549 human lung epithelial cells showed similar viability when they were treated with different concentrations of hyperin (Fig. 6d). The LDH level detected in the supernatant of the A549 cell culture treated with only S. aureus USA300 was defined as 100. When the samples were treated with different concentrations of hyperin, the LDH level in the supernatant decreased to 56.44% and 36.67%, respectively (Fig. 6e). This result was confirmed by the staining of live/dead cells, as the red fluorescence that represents the dead cells are dominated in the S. aureus USA300 treatment group, while the red fluorescence representing dead cells gradually decreased and the green fluorescence representing live cells gradually increased after the samples received different concentrations of hyperin (Fig. 6f). Taken together, these findings suggest that hyperin does not have a cytotoxic effect at the test concentrations but can protect human lung epithelial cells from the cytotoxic effect induced by S. aureus.

Hyperin does not affect the expression of SrtA but reduces the levels of ClfA on the cell surface

To explore whether hyperin affects the expression of SrtA, Western blot assays were performed, and the results revealed that the expression levels of SrtA in S. aureus USA300 did not significantly differ between bacteria treated with or without hyperin (Fig. 6g and h). Clumping Factor A (ClfA) is a virulence factor that is involved in the pathogenicity of S. aureus and can be anchored to the cell surface by SrtA. The levels of ClfA decreased significantly when S. aureus was treated with different concentrations of hyperin (Fig. 6g and i). These results indicate that hyperin does not affect the expression of SrtA in S. aureus USA300 but does inhibit the function of SrtA through direct steric hindrance, which results in a reduction of ClfA on the bacterial cell wall surface.

Hyperin reduces Proinflammatory factor levels

The levels of IL-8, IL-1β and IL-6 in J774 cells infected with S. aureus USA300 reached 459.55, 615.01 and 141.31 pg/mL, respectively, but the levels of these cytokines decreased gradually with the increasing hyperin concentration (Fig. 7a–c). These results indicate that hyperin alleviated the inflammation induced by S. aureus in J774 cells.

The amount of haemolysin released from the S. aureus USA300 culture supernatant that did not receive hyperin treatment was defined as 100. These results confirm that hyperin does not affect the function of αHL and does not cooperate with Amp.

Hyperin protects G. mellonella and mice from S. aureus USA300 infection

In the infection group, G. mellonella died after 24 h of infection, and the survival rate for this group was 22.22%. In the hyperin treatment group, the death of G. mellonella was delayed by 12 h, and the survival rate increased to 55.56% (Fig. 8a). The infected G. mellonella turned black, their bodies were filled with melanin, and their tissues were disrupted completely, However, these symptoms were significantly relieved in the group that received the hyperin treatment (Fig. 8b and c). In the S. aureus USA300 mouse pneumonia model, after the hypericin treatment, the survival rate of the infected mice increased from 0 to 36.67%, and the death time was also delayed by 12 h (Fig. 9a). The bacterial burden in the lungs of the hypericin-treated group was significantly lower than that in the lungs of the infected group (Fig. 9b). The levels of IL-8 in the mouse alveolar lavage fluid from the infection and hyperin treatment groups were 756.87 pg/mL and 252.53 pg/mL respectively (Fig. 9c). Similarly, after the hyperin treatment, the amounts of IL-1β and IL-6 decreased by 504.21 pg/mL and 264.00 pg/mL, respectively (Fig. 9d and e). These results confirm that hyperin can have protective effects in S. aureus USA300 mouse pneumonia models.

Discussion

Some crystal structures of the S. aureus SrtA protein with or without ligands have been reported46–48. Zong and its colleagues reported the crystal structures of S. aureus SrtA (PDB ID: 1T2W) and SrtB (PDB ID: 1QWZ) with their respective substrates or inhibitors, His120, Cys184 and Arg197 were identified as the critical active sites of SrtA, Cys223 and Arg233 in SrtB were important on fixing its substrate peptides. In addition, some other residues in SrtA or SrtB around the active pocket interact with ligands or substrate peptides49,50. These two reports clarify the transpeptidization mechanism of S. aureus SrtA and SrtB and provide an important foundation for the development of subsequent inhibitors. Here, we carried out docking calculations between hyperin and SrtA or SrtB by using 1T2W and 1WQZ as the initial structure and reported that hyperin occupied the active site of the native substrates directly. The binding free energy was involved in the ele force, vdw force and sol force. We discovered that Pro163 and Ile199 in SrtA and Arg233 in SrtB are important for binding with hyperin. More importantly, the binding between hyperin and SrtA or SrtB was confirmed by an SPR assay, and after binding, the activity of the cleavage substrates of SrtA and SrtB decreased significantly.

As described above, many aspects of S. aureus infection are related to virulence proteins that anchored by sortases to the cell surface, such as early adhesion and colonization, biofilm formation51, cytotoxicity and inflammation13. Therefore, we examined the effects of hyperin on these indices and revealed that hyperin does not have anti-S. aureus effects but doe reduce biofilm formation, the adhesion of S. aureus to lung epithelial cells, and the cytotoxicity and inflammatory response of host cells induced by S. aureus. In vivo, hyperin increased the survival rate and alleviated the symptoms of S. aureus-infected G. mellonella. In a mouse pneumonia model, hyperin improved the survival rate, reduced the colonization of S. aureus in the lungs, and significantly alleviated the lung inflammatory response. These results confirm the reliability of the inhibitory effect of hyperin against S. aureus SrtA and SrtB activities at the experimental level, although several other potential mechanisms have not been fully explored.

αHL and β-lactamase also play important roles in promoting S. aureus infection or triggering drug resistance52,53. Here, we tested the effects of hyperin on these two aspects; however, we did not observe positive feedback because hyperin did not affect the haemolytic activity of the S. aureus culture supernatant and did not have synergistic effects on Amp activity.

Although some natural inhibitors of SrtA have been reported, these identified compounds are usually focused on two virulence factors simultaneously, αHL and SrtA, and αHL is the primary target54,55. M2-conotoxin and contryphan-R have been reported to be inhibitors of SrtA and SrtB56, but this study focused only on molecular docking. The role of hyperin in fighting bacterial infections such as S. aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae has also been reported57,58. In this study, the interaction mechanism between hyperin and SrtA or SrtB in S. aureus was elucidated on the basis of computational biology, and the effects of hyperin on S. aureus pathogenicity both in vivo and in vitro were confirmed at the experimental level. Because hyperin is a widespread natural active ingredient, it is easy to access; additionally, it is noncytotoxic. These properties make hyperin promising for future applications.

Conclusion

Hyperin bound to the bioactive sites of S. aureus SrtA and SrtB, occupied the active pocket, and inhibited the biological functions of these two enzymes. Experimental verification revealed that hyperin had no cytotoxic effect and did not affect the growth of S. aureus, but significantly reduced S. aureus biofilm formation and the adhesion of this pathogen to lung epithelial cells. In addition, hyperin alleviated the cytotoxicity and inflammation mediated by S. aureus, improved the survival rate of G. mellonella and mice infected with S. aureus, alleviated the pathological damage to S. aureus-infected G. mellonella, and reduced bacterial colonization and the inflammatory response in the lungs of S. aureus infected mice. These results suggest that hyperin can be developed as an anti-S. aureus infection drug.
