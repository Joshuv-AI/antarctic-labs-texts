Background

Stroke, a prevalent neurological disease, profoundly impacts patients’ lives, health, and well-being. As the second leading cause of death and a primary contributor to disability worldwide, it imposes substantial burdens on global healthcare systems [1]. Ischemic stroke constitutes approximately 80% of all stroke cases [2]. Survivors frequently endure long-term sequelae including motor dysfunction, visual impairment, cognitive-communication deficits, emotional disturbances, sexual dysfunction, and sleep disorders [3]. Research has demonstrated that post-stroke insomnia affects 75–95% of stroke patients globally [4], typically emerging during ischemic stroke recovery yet remaining underrecognized clinically. Australian cohort data revealed 29.8% of patients experience poor sleep quality [5], while Brazilian studies reported 70.6% prevalence within the first post-stroke year [6].

The fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) defines insomnia as a persistent condition characterized by difficulty falling asleep, maintaining sleep, or early morning awakenings with inability to resume sleep. These symptoms must persist at least three nights per week for three months, resulting in clinically significant distress or functional impairment [7]. As a prevalent post-stroke complication, insomnia involves multifactorial and incompletely understood pathophysiology, with key contributors including structural damage through ischemic injury to thalamic sleep-wake regulatory centers [8], psychological comorbidities such as post-stroke anxiety and depression that frequently co-occur with and exacerbate sleep disturbances [9], and systemic complications from cerebrovascular sequelae (e.g., cardiopulmonary dysfunction, post-stroke epilepsy, and infections) that contribute to sleep fragmentation [10]. Neurochemical alterations including nocturnal melatonin deficiency, GABAergic dysfunction, and reduced antioxidant capacity have also been implicated [11]. Emerging evidence further highlights gut-brain axis dysregulation, demonstrating that gut microbiota and their metabolites influence insomnia pathogenesis through neuromodulation [12], blood-brain barrier disruption [13], and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysregulation [14].

The evaluation of insomnia presents significant clinical challenges due to the inherently subjective nature of its symptoms, which cannot be adequately assessed through routine medical examinations. As a result, there was a generally low awareness and screening rate for post-stroke insomnia, consequently elevating risks of adverse outcomes and stroke recurrence [15]. Insomnia-associated factors encompass sociodemographic characteristics, clinical parameters, and psychological comorbidities including depression and anxiety among ISC patients [16]. The pathogenesis of insomnia in this population results from complex interactions among clinical, psychological, and social determinants, though the precise mechanistic pathways and their relative contributions remain poorly understood. Consequently, These gaps underscore the critical need for developing predictive models enabling early identification and targeted screening of high-risk patients.

Currently, Sleep assessment in ISC patients primarily relies on either subjective or objective measurement tools, including traditional logistic regression analysis [17]. With the advent of the big data era, data mining technologies have assumed increasingly prominent roles in medical research. Decision tree models, in particular, have been widely applied in studies concerning stroke, infections, frailty, pressure injuries, and mild cognitive impairment [18–20]. The decision tree model for insomnia risk in ISC patients was constructed through the classification and regression tree (CART) algorithm in machine learning. This algorithm is capable of analyzing both categorical and continuous variables, effectively utilizing all available sample data, managing high-dimensional data, screening important variables, and achieving relatively high prediction accuracy [21].

In our study, we identified the key predictive variables for insomnia in ISC patients. Using the CART algorithm, we were able to clearly present the contribution values and thresholds of these variables. This allowed doctors and nurses to formulate a decision-making path for assessing insomnia risk, which can serve as a valuable reference for the clinical management of post-stroke insomnia.

Methods

Study design and patients

This clinical investigation study was conducted following the ethical standards of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Changchun University of Chinese Medicine and received approval from the Ethics Committee in August 2024.

Initial predictors

The literature review has identified twenty-two factors associated with insomnia in patients with ischemic stroke, encompassing (a) gender; (b) age; (c) marital status; (d) educational level; (e) insurance type; (f) monthly income; (g) FMA score; (h) stroke location; (i) stroke severity; (j) body mass index; (k) ADL; (l) hypertension; (m) diabetes mellitus; (n) coronary heart disease; (0) arrhythmia; (p) hyperlipidemia; (q) smoking; (r) alcohol; (s) fatigue; (t) depression; (u) anxiety; (v) social support.

Study criteria

Participants must meet specific diagnostic criteria: stroke diagnosis must adhere to the ‘American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) Guidelines’ [22]. Chronic insomnia diagnosis must align with ‘The European Insomnia Guideline’ [23]. Inclusion criteria: (a) aged 40–75; (b) in the recovery phase; (c) experiencing their first disease; (d) capable of independently completing questionnaires and scales with adequate behavioral and language skills. Exclusion criteria: (a) impaired consciousness, cognition, or communication post-ischemic stroke; (b) pre-existing insomnia diagnoses or treatments; (c) current or prior use of sleep medications; (d) severe complications from other systems like heart, liver, lung, or kidney.

Data collection

During hospitalization, demographic information such as age, sex, height, weight, marital status, insurance type, smoking and drinking habits, and clinical comorbidities were recorded. Additionally, data on stroke location, degree of neurological impairment, motor balance scores for upper and lower limbs, and daily activity scores were documented for each patient. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was employed to assess the sleep quality. This instrument comprises 18 items organized into 7 components, each rated on a scale of 0–3, resulting in a total score range of to 21, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87 [24]. A PSQI score exceeding 7 indicated poor sleep quality (insomnia), while a score of 7 or lower denoted good sleep quality (non-insomnia). The Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) was was utilized to evaluate post-stroke fatigue levels in patients, consisting of 9 items rated on a scale of 1 to 7, where higher scores indicate more severe fatigue. The FSS exhibits strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.84) [25]. Anxiety and depression levels were evaluated using the Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), respectively [26, 27]. Each scale comprises 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 to 4, with certain items requiring reverse scoring. Participants scoring 50 or above on the SAS were categorized as experiencing anxiety symptoms, while those scoring 53 or higher on the SDS were identified as displaying depressive symptoms. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the SAS and SDS were 0.83 and 0.87, respectively. Social support was assessed through the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), which includes 10 items measuring objective support, subjective support, and the utilization of social support. The Cronbach’s α coefficient exceeded 0.7, indicating good internal consistency. Higher total scores on the SSRS indicate greater levels of social support [28].

Statistical analysis and sample size calculating

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 24.0 software. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations for normally distributed data, and as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for skewed data. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions. Group comparisons for normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneous variances were performed using a two-sample t-test. For skewed continuous variables, the rank sum test was utilized, and the Chi-squared test was applied for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05.

The model construction in this study employed the CART algorithm, a decision tree model introduced by Breiman and colleagues in 1984 [29]. This algorithm is designed to handle both classification and regression tasks by minimizing the Gini coefficient or squared error to split the root node into two child nodes. This recursive splitting process continues until further division is not feasible. To identify significant variables, univariate analysis was initially conducted, and variables showing statistical significance were chosen as initial factors for variable screening. Subsequently, decision tree algorithms were utilized to uncover underlying data patterns and determine optimal split conditions or cutoff values for each node based on Gini coefficient calculations. A total of 6 outlier data points were discovered in this study, with no missing values. The proportion of outliers was 1%, distributed across the NIHSS and BI variables. The outliers were replaced with the mean or median values to eliminate their potential interference with the analysis results. The model’s parameter settings included a random seed number of 90, a complexity parameter of 0.001, a minimum sample split of 3, and a maxdepth of 6. The model was implemented using R version 4.2.2 and R Studio.

Model verification utilized 10-fold cross-validation. Internal and external validation employed a confusion matrix, ROC and DCA curve. The model’s calibration performance was assessed using H-L test, calibration curve, and Brier Score. Adhering to the events per variable (EPV) rule with an EPV of 10, and factoring in a 5% loss during investigation, 623 samples were necessary for this study.

The prevalence of insomnia and univariate analyses of the potential predictive factors among ISC patients

The evaluation results indicated that 235 participants (37.72%) had insomnia, while 388 participants (62.28%) did not. The PSQI score for the insomnia group was 9, with an interquartile range of 8 to 11. Univariate analysis results are presented in Table 1. Variables significantly associated with insomnia (p < 0.05) and incorporated decision tree algorithm analysis were BMI, NIHSS, FSS, SAS, SDS, and SSRS.

Construction of predictive decision tree model of insomnia

The dataset, consisting of 623 samples, was divided into a training set and a validation set in a 7:3 ratio, with 436 samples allocated to the training set. Variables including BMI, NIHSS, FSS, SAS, SDS, and SSRS were incorporated into the model. The score distribution within the decision tree structure was illustrated in Fig. 1, showing a total of 24 nodes and a depth of 6. Each variable’s contribution to the model was ranked as follows: SAS = 113.47, SSRS = 85.35, FSS = 63.96, SDS = 63.03, BMI = 17.70, and NIHSS = 6.73 (Fig. 2). The analysis identified the SAS score as having the highest influence on insomnia risk, whereas the NIHSS had the lowest. Furthermore, Fig. 1 showed the SAS score at the root node of the tree, underscoring that anxiety was the most significant factor in predicting insomnia among ISC patients.

The analysis demonstrates that the decision paths align with the decision tree model structure. The model first stratifies patients using anxiety scores (SAS ≥ 40) as the primary splitting node, dividing them into high-anxiety and low-anxiety subgroups. Among high-anxiety patients (SAS ≥ 40), those with poor social support (SSRS < 44%) exhibit dramatically elevated insomnia risk (88%), which further increases to 90% when combined with high fatigue levels (FSS ≥ 21). Conversely, high-anxiety patients with adequate social support (SSRS ≥ 44) maintain relatively low insomnia probability (29%). In the low-anxiety cohort (SAS < 40), diminished social support (SSRS < 38) corresponds to 67% insomnia risk, whereas patients with robust social support and low depression scores (SDS < 45) demonstrate significantly reduced risk. The color gradient of decision nodes visually represents risk stratification, with darker hues indicating higher insomnia probability and lighter shades denoting lower risk.

Internal validation of a decision tree model for insomnia risk

In the verification dataset containing 187 cases (Fig. 3), the confusion matrix analysis showed that the model correctly predicted a total of 165 cases. Specifically, it predicted 73 cases of insomnia, of which 62 were actual cases of insomnia. Similarly, it predicted 114 cases as non-insomnia, correctly identifying 103 non-insomnia cases Based on these values, the model’s accuracy rate (ACC) on the test set was 90.4%, indicating a relatively high prediction accuracy.

The results of the H-L test showed a result with χ2 = 9.36 and p = 0.404. Furthermore, the calibration curve exhibited close alignment with the diagonal reference line (Fig.7), indicating relatively stable calibration of the model even after integrating internal validation data.

Clinical decision curves for the training and validation sets

The 10-fold cross-validation analysis indicated that setting the complexity parameter (cp.) to 0.001 effectively managed model complexity. The root mean square error (RMSE) values for the training and validation datasets were 0.29 and 0.30, respectively, suggesting minimal overfitting. The coefficient of determination (R2) values were 0.63 and 0.60, approximating 0.6, indicating that the model accounted for around 60% of the variance, denoting moderate predictive capability. The mean absolute error (MAE) values were 0.14 and 0.15, reinforcing the model’s reliability and predictive precision. Furthermore, the Brier scores were 0.06 (training set) and 0.08 (validation set), indicating a small disparity between predicted and actual results. In contrast, the Brier scores for the Treat-all baseline strategy were notably higher (0.23 and 0.25, respectively), underscoring the superior performance of the model. The Brier Skill Score (BSS) results demonstrated substantial improvement in predictive accuracy over the Treat-all baseline, with values of 0.76 (training set) and 0.70 (validation set), representing over 70% enhancement. This results further underscored the model’s generalizability.

The decision curve analysis revealed that the model demonstrated clinically useful predictive performance for insomnia risk in ISC patients across a wide probability threshold range of 10 − 90%, with particularly favorable clinical benefit rates observed between 10 − 50% thresholds, achieving 27 − 33% in the training cohort and 34 − 42% in the validation cohort (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This study examined the independent predictors of insomnia in ISC patients. The results indicated a high prevalence of insomnia, with anxiety levels, severity of depression, adequacy of social support, BMI, intensity of fatigue, and severity of stroke emerging as statistically significant predictors.

Stroke commonly triggers negative emotions in patients [30], leading to post-stroke mental health issues such as impaired attention, memory deficits, apathy, and depression [9, 31]. Previous research has highlighted post-stroke depression as a major contributor to insomnia [32]. Our findings corroborate this notion, underscoring the role of negative emotions as crucial factors. The decision tree model identified anxiety scores as the primary determinant, delineating three critical thresholds (37, 40, and 46). Patients with SAS scores below 37 exhibited a mere 2% incidence of insomnia, whereas higher scores were associated with a substantially elevated risk. This relationship likely stems from anxiety-induced dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system, leading to emotional instability, tension, and fear, all of which significantly impact sleep quality [33]. These findings imply that addressing anxiety is crucial for managing insomnia in this demographic. Likewise, significant sleep disturbances were evident in individuals with high levels of depressive symptoms (SDS scores ≥ 45). The contemporary medical field increasingly acknowledges the impact of psychological factors on patient recovery. Therefore, healthcare professionals must prioritize not only physical healing but also the holistic management of negative emotions to enhance the rehabilitation process.

Psychosocial factors are now recognized as key modifiable components. However, limited research has systematically explored the influence of protective psychosocial factors on sleep quality in stroke patients [34, 35]. Stroke survivors often rely on substantial external support, both emotional and material, to adapt to daily challenges and navigate life changes. Consequently, our study identified social support as a crucial predictive factor for insomnia, ranking second only to anxiety in significance. The model identified four distinct thresholds (38, 41, 44, and 46 points) on the SSRS, where scores ≥ 46 were linked to a mere 5% incidence of insomnia. Notably, increased social support significantly decreased the likelihood of insomnia even when anxiety levels were similar. This positive impact is likely attributed to a support network involving family, caregivers, and friends, which promotes a sense of security, reduces hypervigilance, and ultimately enhances sleep quality [36]. These results, in line with previous studies, indicate that strong social support not only reduces the risk of insomnia but also enhances functional recovery in patients with ischemic stroke [37]. Healthcare professionals should implement an integrated care strategy that addresses both physical rehabilitation and psychosocial well-being in stroke patients. This approach should involve establishing a therapeutic environment based on respect and encouragement to encourage active patient involvement, while continuously monitoring mental health status to guide personalized psychological interventions. Furthermore, there should be a focus on expanding health education programs to provide evidence-based information to stroke patients and their caregivers, aiming to minimize maladaptive coping strategies. Ultimately, the development of cost-effective rehabilitation nursing protocols should be tailored to individual patients’ clinical characteristics and socioeconomic backgrounds.

Post-stroke fatigue presents complex clinical obstacles, impacting rehabilitation compliance, motivation, emotional well-being, social interaction, and goal-oriented activities [38]. Our analysis pinpointed a crucial FSS score threshold, revealing that patients with similar anxiety levels and social support can lower their risk of insomnia by managing fatigue symptoms. This finding suggests a direct influence of fatigue on sleep quality, possibly mediated by daytime sleepiness induced by fatigue [39]. Consequently, fatigue emerges as a key predictor in our model for assessing insomnia risk. These results support prior research by Choi-Kwon and Kim (2011) that highlighted the bidirectional relationship between sleep and fatigue, particularly in elderly individuals with ischemic stroke [40]. Healthcare practitioners can address various factors to mitigate patient fatigue. This includes creating a comfortable and tranquil rest environment, minimizing interventions during sleep, establishing healthy lifestyle routines with family involvement, promoting appropriate physical activity, and reducing daytime bed rest duration. Nevertheless, the complex interplay between fatigue severity, insomnia, and mediating variables requires further rigorous investigation to elucidate their synergistic effects in ISC patients.

BMI and NIHSS were incorporated as objective predictors in the decision tree model for insomnia risk stratification. Previous studies have shown that elevated BMI is linked to increased susceptibility to insomnia among ISC patients [41]. Furthermore, higher BMI is positively correlated with overweight and obesity, which in turn increases insomnia risk [42]. Our model’s decision paths (5, 6, 13, and 14) quantitatively confirmed that optimized management of BMI significantly reduces the risk of insomnia. Concurrently, neurological deficit severity could significantly affect sleep quality [17, 43], possibly due to irreversible nerve cell damage leading to the release of harmful substances that disrupt the sleep-wake cycle [3]. To minimize the influence of severe cognitive-communicative impairments, only ischemic stroke patients with NIHSS scores < 15 were included in the study. Importantly, NIHSS scores ≥ 3 were linked to a 55% increase in the risk of insomnia, highlighting the clinical relevance of this threshold despite its relatively modest predictive value in the model. These results emphasize the need for further research to understand the interactions between stroke severity and sleep quality.

Internal validation confirmed the model’s favorable predictive accuracy and calibration. The study also assessed the model’s generalizability. External validation showed a decrease in both model stability and accuracy, likely due to variations in data distribution and sample characteristics. These results imply that although the model shows promise, its transferability to different contexts should be approached with care. Furthermore, decision curve analysis demonstrated that the model incorporating anxiety, social support, fatigue, depression, BMI, and stroke severity exhibited favorable clinical utility. This model enables quantitative assessment of insomnia risk in ISC patients, effectively identifying low-risk individuals to avoid unnecessary pharmacological interventions, while facilitating comprehensive evaluation and tailored management for high-risk patients to optimize rehabilitation outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study introduced a novel application of the CART algorithm to develop a decision tree model, departing from the prevailing logistic regression methods in existing literature. By utilizing this machine learning approach, we broadened the investigative scope concerning insomnia in ISC patients. Our findings highlighted anxiety levels as the most reliable predictor, challenging conventional notions of stroke prognosis while aligning with emerging global trends in post-stroke mental health research. Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, future studies should integrate objective biomarkers such as serological profiles, gut microbiota, and metabolic signatures to enhance insomnia prediction. Secondly, although our sample size allowed for the identification of initial risk factors, larger cohorts are necessary to detect more subtle associations. Methodologically, the use of univariate feature selection may have introduced bias by overlooking covariate interactions and underlying data distributions; future research will incorporate recursive feature elimination or LASSO regression for optimizing high-dimensional data. While externally validated, the restriction to a single-region data source limits geographical generalizability, underscoring the need for multinational datasets to bolster clinical applicability. Addressing these considerations will enhance the model’s reliability, quantify its therapeutic efficacy, and establish a robust reference framework for subsequent investigations.

Implications for practice and policy

This research highlights the importance of establishing comprehensive prevention and treatment protocols for post-stroke insomnia by including key risk factors such as anxiety, depression, fatigue, and insufficient social support in early screening protocols. The constructed decision tree model showcases a hierarchical predictive framework with adaptable risk stratification capabilities. Through the integration of diverse data sources, it offers a visual roadmap for categorization of post-stroke insomnia risk, empowering healthcare providers to promptly identify high-risk groups. We advocate for the integration of standardized mental health evaluations and evidence-based sleep management techniques into standard stroke rehabilitation procedures, alongside the enhancement of specialized training initiatives for healthcare practitioners. Simultaneously, policymakers should support the establishment of community-based support systems to optimize sleep health care for stroke survivors. These collaborative measures are poised to uphold emotional well-being, enhance sleep quality, and ultimately elevate overall recovery outcomes within this patient cohort.

Conclusions

The decision tree model created in this study to forecast the risk of insomnia in patients with ischemic stroke demonstrated moderate discriminative capacity, predictive precision, and clinical benefit. This study introduces the first prediction model tailored and validated specifically for insomnia in ischemic stroke patients. The nomogram developed can serve as a practical and individualized predictive instrument to enhance clinical decision-making processes. In practical settings, this nomogram can complement existing sleep assessment tools to comprehensively assess patients’ sleep quality. While internal and external validations have affirmed the model’s efficacy in predicting insomnia risk in ischemic stroke patients, its generalizability is deemed moderate. Subsequent research endeavors should prioritize refining data processing techniques, optimizing feature selection, and executing multicenter external validations to bolster the model’s clinical utility.
