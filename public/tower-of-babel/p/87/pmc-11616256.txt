Accelerating Oral Wound Healing Using Bilayer Biomaterial Delivery of FTY720 Immunotherapy
Authors: Afra I Toma, Daniel Shah, Daniela Roth, Jeremie Oliver Piña, Lauren Hymel, Thomas Turner, Archana Kamalakar, Ken Liu, Perry Bartsch, Leon Jacobs, Rena D'Souza, Dennis Liotta, Edward Botchwey, Nick J Willett, Steven L Goudy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrofacial clefts are the most common congenital craniofacial anomaly. Adverse healing following cleft palate repair can lead to oronasal fistula (ONF), a persistent connection between the oral and nasal cavities. Although human allograft tissues are currently the gold standard for ONF repair, these methods carry risks of infection and rejection, often requiring surgical revision. Immunoregenerative therapies present a novel alternative approach to harness the body's immune response and enhance the wound healing environment. An FDA‐approved immunomodulatory drug, FTY720, is repurposed to reduce lymphocyte egress and induce immune cell fate switching toward pro‐regenerative phenotypes. In this study, a bilayer biomaterial system is engineered using Tegaderm to secure and control the delivery of FTY720‐nanofiber scaffolds (FTY720‐NF). The release kinetics of the bilayer FTY720‐NF is optimized to maintain drug release for up to 7 days, ensuring safe transdermal absorption and tissue biodistribution. The comprehensive immunophenotyping results demonstrate a regenerative state transition in hybrid immune cells recruited to the wound site. Further, histological evaluations reveal a significant ONF closure in mice by day 7 following bilayer FTY720‐NF implantation. These findings demonstrate the utility of immunomodulatory strategies for oral wound healing, better positing the field to develop more efficacious treatment options in pediatric patients.Keywords: biomaterial, drug delivery, FTY720, oral wound healing, tissue engineering

Introduction

Cleft lip or palate is among the most common congenital anomalies, affecting 1 in 940 live births.[ 1 ] Lip repair surgery is typically required by 6 months and palate surgery before 12 months of age.[ 2 ]However, adverse healing following repair can result in oronasal fistula (ONF), an abnormal connection between the oral and nasal cavities.[ 3 , 4 ] Fistula formation can cause food to lodge in the ONF, leading to bacterial accumulation and mucosal inflammation. Persistent inflammation at the repair site may impair healing, increasing ONF occurrences and leading to infection, flap necrosis, hematoma formation, inadequate occlusion, and biomechanical tension.[ 5 , 6 ] ONF formation can impair the child's ability to eat and develop proper speech, affecting the overall quality of life; hence, it is imperative to advance clinical therapeutics for the treatment of ONF.

The oral cavity poses a distinct challenge for biomaterial scaffold development owing to complications from host bacteria, saliva, and continuous biomechanical forces. The current gold standard for treating severe mucosal wounds involves the use of commercially available dermal grafts when the local tissue available for autografts is insufficient. However, dermal grafts are an off‐label treatment without FDA approval, specifically for mucosal replacement, and only serve as structural barriers without functional capabilities.[ 7 , 8 ] Therefore, there is a need for immunomodulatory therapy that prevents dermal graft rejection by leveraging the oral wound healing cascade, reduces wound persistence, and can be approved by the FDA for mucosal tissue repair.

FTY720 (Fingolimod) is an FDA‐approved immunomodulatory drug prescribed once daily for relapsing multiple sclerosis (MS).[ 9 ] It functions as a small molecule receptor agonist for Sphingosine‐1‐Phosphate (S1P), a bioactive sphingolipid in endothelial cells, crucial for cell survival, trafficking, apoptosis suppression, and nuclear signaling pathways.[ 10 , 11 ] In vivo, FTY720 is converted to its bioactive form by sphingosine kinase‐2 (SPHK2) and binds extracellularly with G‐protein‐coupled receptors S1PR1,3‐5 with higher affinity than S1P.[ 12 ] Upon binding, FTY720‐Phosphate (FTY720P) internalizes the receptor, activating intracellular targets and simultaneously degrading S1PR, preventing lymphocyte egress from secondary lymphoid organs and driving immune cell fate switching toward a pro‐regenerative state.[ 13 , 14 ] By innovating FTY720 as an immunoregenerative candidate for ONF treatment, we fabricated a nanofiber (NF) scaffold loaded with FTY720 to serve as a 3D template and direct oral tissue regeneration as a local, transmucosal drug delivery system. Preliminary results from our lab reported that FTY720‐nanofiber scaffolds (FTY720‐NF) selectively recruited pro‐regenerative monocytes and altered gene expression of pro‐regenerative cytokines and transcription factors.[ 15 ] While promising, we recognize crucial limitations in drug delivery characterization and immunophenotyping methods in our previous research that needs to be addressed to improve FTY720‐NF treatment as a clinically translatable immunotherapy for ONF healing.

In this work, we continued our development of a novel, pro‐regenerative immunotherapeutic strategy for oral wound healing by engineering a bilayer FTY720‐NF system to locally modulate the oral immune response following cleft palate repair to reduce post‐operative ONF occurrence. To scale the approach and ensure consistent, sustained FTY720 delivery, we 1) evaluated the drug dose response, release kinetics, and biodistribution of FTY720 both in vitro and in vivo; 2) developed novel material strategies using a Tegaderm (TD) bilayer adhesive system to enhance the retention of the scaffold in the local ONF site; and 3) examined the function of pro‐regenerative immune cells that contribute to successful tissue closure and ONF healing. Herein, we demonstrate how FTY720‐NF acts as a novel form of pro‐regenerative immunotherapy for oral wound healing, paving the way for safe and efficacious treatment options for patients with cleft palate who often experience postoperative oral wound healing complications.

Results

FTY720‐NF Delivery System was Characterized, and Release Kinetics PrDrug Release for 7 Days

To develop an effective FTY720 delivery system capable of sustained release for up to 7 days, we examined the release kinetics and drug release capacity of FTY720‐NF fabricated using poly(lactide‐co‐glycolide acid (PLGA) and polycaprolactone (PCL). We employed high‐performance liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS) to characterize FTY720‐NF release kinetics and efficiency from NF scaffolds (≈0.6 mg wt.) at three drug:polymer weight ratios: blank nanofibers (blank‐NF), 1:120, and 1:60 FTY720‐NF (Figure 1C–E). Our data revealed that 1:60 ratio scaffolds cumulatively released 5.48µg ± 0.28µg FTY720 with 85.32% release efficiency over 7 d. In comparison, 1:120 ratio scaffolds released 1.33 µg ± 0.19 µg FTY720 with 75.23% release efficiency over 48 h. The 1:60 wt. ratio was selected for all subsequent characterization and in vivo studies because of its significantly enhanced drug release compared with the 1:120 ratio (P = 0.0048). Statistical analysis was performed using ordinary one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test, and data are presented as mean ± SEM with significance at P < 0.05. n = 3–5 scaffolds per drug loading group.

We evaluated the morphology and stiffness of the control and FTY720‐NF scaffolds using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) for fabrication consistency and reproducibility (Figure 1F–I). SEM micrographs showed significant differences (p < 0.001) in NF width across blank‐NF, 1:120 FTY720‐NF, and 1:60 FTY720‐NF treatment groups. Despite differences in NF diameter between blank‐NF and 1:60 FTY720‐NF, AFM was used to quantitatively assess scaffold topography and stiffness. AFM images indicated lighter regions for greater height and stiffness in both blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF. Histograms of Young's moduli (MPa) were derived from multiple contact points on blank and 1:60 FTY720‐NF. Results showed Young's moduli for blank‐NF (713.92kPa ± 495.60kPa) and FTY720‐NF (731.79kPa ± 664.81kPa). Previous NMR characterization of polymer–drug–solvent interactions in a similar system did not reveal significant residual chloroform or solvent interactions with the drug or polymer, and no associated toxicities were observed.[ 16 ] To further ensure biosafety, we conducted nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) on both blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF scaffolds to measure the amount of residual volatile solvents in the final products (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The NMR spectra revealed that there were trace amounts of residual chloroform in blank‐NF (0.06% wt chloroform:PCL) and FTY720‐NF (0.11% wt. chloroform:PCL), while methanol was undetectable in both scaffolds when compared to PCL weight. These comparable scaffold profiles confirmed that the fabrication process was safe and reproducible, with no significant impact of drug incorporation on scaffold properties.

Bilayer FTY720‐NF Delivery Accelerated ONF Wound Closure by D7

We hypothesized that fixing FTY720‐NF to the oral mucosa would enhance transdermal drug delivery and locally modulate S1P receptors (Figure 2A).[ 15 ] Using an established in vivo ONF surgery model, we evaluated various adhesive methods to secure FTY720‐NF to the injury site (Figure 2B; Figure S2A, Supporting Information). 3M Vetbond and Oasis LiquiVet 8‐second Rapid Tissue Adhesive adhered the scaffold to the tissue for over 3 days post‐implantation. However, an LC/MS study showed no FTY720 release in vitro when the scaffold adhered directly to the tissue glue, likely due to glue infiltration into the nanofibers, hindering drug diffusion (Figure S2B, Supporting Information). 3M Nexcare Tegaderm (TD) is a transparent polyurethane film that is ubiquitously used in clinical settings to protect intravenous catheter sites and wounds. TD is commercially available as a sterile, semi‐impermeable thin film that is occlusive to liquid, bacteria, and small molecules yet can easily exchange water vapor, oxygen, and carbon dioxide. TD was layered above the FTY720‐NF implanted at the injury site and thinly coated the TD with sterile tissue glue, securing the scaffold to the oral mucosa (Figure 2C,D). This bilayer adhesive system ensured scaffold attachment for up to 24 h, and the TD bilayer acted as a barrier and helped maintain a moist environment for wound healing.

All in vivo surgeries used 4–6 week‐old C57BL/6 mice, modeling the human pediatric age range of 6 months to 11.5 years.[ 17 ] Bilayer scaffolds, either blank‐NF or FTY720‐NF, were implanted at the midline ONF injury on D0. Delivering FTY720‐NF during oral surgery palatoplasty is clinically relevant to pediatric patients with ONF as it modulates the healing process early to reduce repeat fistula formation. Endoscopic images of wound closure were taken at D1, 3, 5, and 7, and injury size was measured using ImageJ software (Figure 2E). Endoscopic measurements showed significantly improved wound closure in FTY720‐NF treated mice by D7 compared to control groups (blank‐NF, p = 0.001 and injured mice, p < 0.001). No significant difference was found between blank‐NF and injury‐only mice, indicating NF alone does not promote significant ONF closure without FTY720. These results demonstrate that FTY720‐NF treatment accelerated healing with full ONF closure by D7. Mice locally injected with FTY720, serving as a non‐vehicle control, showed persistent ONF on D7 (Figure S2C, Supporting Information). Data are presented as mean wound size ± SEM, and significant differences (p < 0.05) were determined using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison test. n = 9–11 animals per treatment group.

Mice Treated with FTY720‐NF Displayed Acute Lymphopenia at 24 H

FTY720 primarily affects lymphocytes, causing reversible transient lymphopenia.[ 18 ] To assess FTY720's systemic effect on circulating lymphocytes, blood was collected from mice at 24 h and analyzed using flow cytometry to quantify immune cell counts (Figure 2F). CD45+ leukocytes were gated for innate and adaptive immune cell subsets in the blood samples. Innate immune cells were gated as neutrophils, macrophages and dendritic, while adaptive immune cells were classified as T cells, cytotoxic T cells, and B cells. Significant differences were evaluated using a two‐tail, unpaired t‐test, and were presented as mean counts ± SEM with significance at p < 0.05. n = 5–6 mice per treatment group.

Analysis of the CD11b+ myeloid cell population revealed no significant difference in blood cell counts for Ly6G+ neutrophils, MerTK− monocytes, and CD11c+ dendritic cells between treatment groups. These findings suggest that FTY720‐NF did not affect blood myeloid cells at 24 h post‐treatment but may influence myeloid functions through fate switching, cell survival, or cytokine production. In contrast, FTY720‐NF‐treated mice showed significantly lower counts of circulating CD3+ T cells (p = 0.015), CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (p = 0.006), and CD19+ B cells (p = 0.022) at 24 h. These data confirmed acute lymphopenia in FTY720‐NF mice at 24 h, indicating that the scaffold‐adhesive system facilitated transmucosal drug absorption at the local implantation site and effectively modulated lymphocyte migration.

The effects of FTY720‐NF on myeloid cells were evaluated using a flow panel of CD45+ leukocytes, and leukocyte‐specific receptor CD11b+ was used to quantify monocyte/macrophages, granulocytes, and natural killer cells (Figure 3A; Figure S4, Supporting Information). Neutrophils were identified by Ly6G+, with CD62L further distinguishing fresh versus aged neutrophils.[ 19 , 20 ] Macrophages were characterized by CD64+MerTK+ markers, with CD86+ indicating pro‐inflammatory M1‐like polarization and CD206+ indicating alternatively activated M2‐like macrophages. The MerTK−Ly6C+ antigen differentiated classical Ly6Chi from non‐classical Ly6Clo monocytes in murine palatal tissue.[ 21 ]

To define heterogeneity from flow cytometry data, we generated spanning‐tree progression analysis of density‐normalized events (SPADE) dendrograms for myeloid subsets (e.g., Ly6G+ neutrophils) from mice treated with either blank‐NF or FTY720‐NF at all timepoints. SPADE is a dimensionality reduction technique that visualizes a 2D spanning tree organized into nodes representing cell clusters based on surface marker similarities. Solid black lines indicate an inferred pseudotime trajectory to elucidate the temporal kinetics of biological processes, such as cellular phenotype transitions.[ 21 , 22 ] For simplicity, all flow files from each condition and timepoint were overlaid onto a single dendrogram and node/cluster data was exported to further make inference on differences between treatment groups and timepoints.

On D1, FTY720‐NF‐treated mice show significantly lower neutrophil percentages compared to blank‐NF mice (p < 0.001) (Figure 3B). FTY720‐NF mice have higher CD62L+ fresh neutrophil percentages (p = 0.006) and decreased CD62L− aged neutrophil recruitment (p = 0.001) on D1. The neutrophil SPADE dendrograms are color‐coded into three Ly6G+ neutrophil subpopulations (fresh, intermediate, and aged); outputs provide the subtype frequency for each condition and timepoint. Marker expression from each SPADE cluster is displayed in a heatmap, and pseudotime projection suggests a transition from fresh neutrophils at D1 to intermediate and aged (Figure S5A,B, Supporting Information). This supports prior data indicating that neutrophils extravasate early in the healing cascade, contributing to pathogen debridement as part of innate immunity.[ 23 , 24 ] After an injury, newly released neutrophils migrate from bone marrow to tissue, undergoing phenotypic and morphological changes every 4 h, while aged circulating neutrophils are eliminated by phagocytic macrophages at the end of the resting period.[ 20 , 25 , 26 ] The SPADE diagram also shows a population of Ly6C+CD11c+ neutrophils. Ly6C is rapidly upregulated on neutrophil surfaces upon activation and is expressed at 10–100‐fold higher levels compared to lymphocytes.[ 27 , 28 ] CD11c is also expressed by neutrophils, with elevated levels associated with increased microvascular sequestration, infiltration into inflammatory sites, and playing a role in effector functions such as bacterial clearance.[ 29 ] However, as the focus of this study is on the effects of FTY720‐NF on ONF healing, we do not further characterize the functional significance of this neutrophil subset.

FTY720‐NF treated mice showed a significantly lower percentage of monocytes on D1 compared to blank‐NF (p = 0.002) (Figure 3C), with no significant differences in monocyte subsets between treatment groups (Figure S4C, Supporting Information). The monocyte SPADE dendrogram clustered into three marked trajectories of Ly6Clo anti‐inflammatory, intermediate Ly6C, and inflammatory Ly6Chi nodes, and dendrogram outputs revealed marker expression as shown in the heatmap. As shown in the subtype frequency plots, monocytes present in the palate at D1 were clustered in nodes with an inflammatory phenotype and transitioned to anti‐inflammatory monocytes by D7 (Figure S5C,D, Supporting Information). Macrophages were significantly higher in FTY720‐NF treated mice on D3 than in blank‐NF mice (p = 0.004) (Figure 3D), with no significant difference in M1‐like and M2‐like polarization (Figure S4D, Supporting Information). Macrophage SPADE dendrograms and heatmaps displayed distinct clusters of unknown, M1‐like, M2‐like, or hybrid macrophages. The count and frequency of macrophage subtypes suggested a possible transition from unpolarized to M1‐like and/or M2‐like phenotypes (Figure S6A,B, Supporting Information). These data indicated that FTY720‐NF may promote a pro‐regenerative immune cell fate switch following ONF injury, with early‐stage inflammatory myeloid cells transitioning toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype by D7.

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was constructed using OMIQ.ai with flow cytometry data from all CD11b+ myeloid cells from blank‐NF or FTY720‐NF treated mice, aggregated across all timepoints (Figure 3E). UMAPs visually represent single cells based on their surface marker profiles, with proximity indicating the cellular phenotypes. Surface marker expression on UMAP ranges from dark violet (low) to yellow (high), with each myeloid cell event depicted as a dot. Phenotypic cell density between blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF treated mice is shown from blue (low density) to red (high density). On D1, Ly6G+ neutrophil expression was higher in the blank‐NF group than in the FTY720‐NF. On D3, MerTK+ expression was higher in the FTY720‐NF group, indicating that FTY720 treatment modulated myeloid cell recruitment and expression during the early healing stages.

FTY720‐NF Modulated Lymphocyte Recruitment During ONF Healing in Spatiotemporally Overlapping Stages

Lymphocyte recruitment post‐FTY720‐NF treatment was analyzed in palate tissue using a lymphoid cell flow panel gated by CD45+CD11b− markers. CD3, a T cell receptor complex produced by thymocytes, differentiated into CD4+ T helper (Th) and CD8+ T cytotoxic cells (TC). Antibodies against adhesion molecule CD44, a hyaluronan receptor, categorized activated Th cells into CD44−CD62L+ naïve (TN), central memory (TCM), and CD44+CD62L− effector memory (TEM) cells.[ 30 ] B cells, precursors to antibody‐secreting cells, were identified by surface marker CD19+.[ 31 ] NK1.1+ is a C‐type lectin‐like receptor and an early marker in natural killer (NK) cell development. CD11c+ is a marker for classical dendritic cells (DCs), expressed in both CD11b+ and CD11b− lineages.[ 32 ] SPADE and UMAPs for CD11b− cells from blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF treated mice across all timepoints were generated as previously described.

FTY720‐NF treatment significantly reduced DCs compared to blank‐NF on Days 1 and 3 post‐ONF injury (CD11b−: D1, p = 0.012 and D3, p = 0.015) (Figure 4B) and DC (CD11b+: D1, p = 0.004) (Figure S4E, Supporting Information). The DC SPADE dendrogram revealed two clusters of CD11c+ cells, denoting both CD11b− lymphoid and CD11b+ myeloid lineages, as studied by frequency of subtype and marker expression (Figure S7A,B, Supporting Information). DC SPADE dendrograms revealed FTY720‐NF may have altered DC lineage, leading to higher percentages of lymphoid‐derived DCs. By Day 5, FTY720‐NF significantly increased CD11b− NK percentage (p = 0.003) with no significant difference to CD11b+ NK cells (Figure 4C; Figure S4F, Supporting Information). FTY720‐NF reduced CD11b−CD3+T cells (p = 0.013) and Th cells (p = 0.010) with no significant difference in TC cell percentage and CD11b+CD3+ T cells (Figure 4D; Figure S4F, Supporting Information). FTY720‐NF upregulated TCM (D1, p = 0.005; D7, p = 0.026) and downregulated TEM (D1, p = 0.05) within Th subtypes (Figure S4G, Supporting Information). By D7, FTY720‐NF produced higher percentage of CD11b−CD19+ B cells (p < 0.001) and T cells (p = 0.042) compared to blank‐NF (Figure 4D,E). In contrast, CD11b+CD19+ B cells were significantly elevated following FTY720‐NF treatment on D1. It can be acknowledged that NK and T cells also expressed CD11c; however, this particular subset of CD11b+CD11c+ T and NK cells was not further evaluated in the context of oral tissue healing following FTY720‐NF treatment.

The T cell SPADE dendrogram is color depicted into subpopulations of CD3+ cells containing TC and Th. Dendrogram outputs show frequency of subtypes and marker expression for unknown, TN, TCM, and TEM cell phenotypes (Figure S7C,D, Supporting Information). From right to left, pseudotime projection of SPADE revealed a phenotypic transition from TC cells at D1 to TCM by D7 following ONF injury. Prior studies have shown that T cells fate switch to a state favoring egress over retention by upregulating S1PR1, so our data validates that a decrease in T cell percentage following treatment with FTY720 was due to T cells dependence on S1PR1‐mediated egress.[ 33 , 34 ] UMAP displayed higher CD3+ T, Th, and TC cell density on Days 1 and 3, with increased NK cell expression in the following days (Figure 4F).

FTY720‐NF Regulated Excessive Stromal Cell Recruitment in Mice Compared to Control

During wound repair, the extracellular matrix (ECM) is regenerated by stromal cells through the production of granulation tissue, vascularization, and fibroblast differentiation. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) at the ONF site are identified using a CD90+ antibody, while other stromal cells are classified as CD45− and negative for other markers. CD31 or PECAM marks endothelial cells, which are essential for angiogenesis and neovascularization during the proliferative phase. As new blood vessels and granulation tissue form, CD326+ EpCAM or keratinocytes promote cell proliferation and migration. Fibroblasts, marked by fibroblast activation protein alpha (FAPα), construct new ECM to support cell ingrowth and provide blood vessels for oxygen and nutrient supply.[ 35 ] We perform SPADE and UMAPs for all CD45− stromal cells from blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF treated mice across all time points as previously described.

FTY720‐NF treatment significantly reduced stromal cell recruitment at most time points compared to blank‐NF. Mice treated with FTY720‐NF showed notably lower MSC recruitment on D1 (p = 0.030) and D5 (p = 0.032) (Figure 5A). EpCAM levels significantly decreased on D5 and D7 (p = 0.049) in FTY720‐NF treated mice (Figure 5B), and fibroblast expression was significantly lower on D7 (p = 0.027) compared to blank‐NF (Figure 5C). No significant difference in PECAM recruitment was observed between the groups across all time points (Figure 5D). The stromal cell SPADE dendrogram showed clustering of fibroblasts and epithelial cells on the left, with hyaluronate receptor and MSC on the right. Unidentified nodes of CD45− stromal cell lineage and their contribution to oral wound healing is a potential avenue for future studies. UMAP analysis of CD45− non‐leukocytic events revealed no qualitative shift in stromal cell expression between treatments. It is possible that FTY720‐NF may help regulate excessive stromal cell presence at the wound site compared to blank‐NF. Elevated fibroblast and keratinocyte levels can correlate with chronic inflammation, hindering wound closure, as activated keratinocytes release cytokines and chemokines to transmit regulatory signals to cells of innate and adaptive immunity. Dysregulation of keratinocytes could lead to abnormal inflammatory mediator expression, akin to chronic inflammation pathogenesis, highlighting the benefit of FTY720‐NF in reducing excessive stromal cell recruitment.[ 36 ]

Local Delivery of FTY720 Sustained Safe, Efficacious Levels for Tissue Biodistribution

FTY720 was phosphorylated in vivo to its active metabolite, FTY720P, which bound to S1P receptors. This reaction could be reversible or reach equilibrium with a long elimination half‐life of ≈7 days.[ 37 , 38 ] FTY720 and FTY720P levels were measured in the palate, liver, and at 2 H, 6 H, 1 d, 2 d, 3 d, 5 d, and 7 d post‐TD bilayer scaffold implantation (Figure 6A). Due to burst release and rapid initial tissue absorption, higher concentrations of FTY720 were found in the palatal tissue (15.52µg ± 12.93) at 2 h compared to FTY720P (0.01µg ± 0.01). FTY720 levels rapidly decreased to 0.95 µg by 24 h in the palate, while the liver reached maximum FTY720P concentration (25.44 ng ± 5.27) at 24 h. The serum concentration of FTY720 was below detectable levels during LC/MS analysis, suggesting local action in the palate tissue. A pharmacology review by Novartis Pharmaceuticals indicated that a single oral dose of 50 mg kg−1 FTY720 was lethal in mice, which equated to ≈1 mg FTY720 in a 20 g mouse. FTY720 was well tolerated up to 5mg kg−1 per day, and the no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level (NOAEL) was 0.5 mg kg−1 per day.[ 39 ] The toxicology report confirmed that the delivery of FTY720‐NF was significantly below the toxic levels and near the NOAEL in mice. Our results showed that low‐dose FTY720 delivery was sufficient to induce a wound healing response in the oral palate without detectable systemic absorption.

Pentachrome Staining Revealed Complete ONF Closure and Collagen Alignment at Wound Edges Following FTY720‐NF Treatment

FTY720‐NF and blank‐NF‐treated murine skulls were collected at D1, D3, and D7 for histological analysis (Figure 6B). A modified Pentachrome stain was used to examine morphology and collagen fiber orientation in the coronal plane. The oral mucosa typically consists of distinct layers: epithelium, lamina propria (reticular layer, submucosa), and periosteum‐lined bone. On D1, both treated and blank‐NF mice exhibited prominent, irregular midline defects. The ONF wound edge disrupted the epithelial and submucosal layers. By D3, wound healing was evident in both groups, with squamous epithelium migrating over the wound edge. However, FTY720‐NF mice showed greater medial extension of the epithelium over the exposed submucosa, achieving a complete seal and resolution of the ONF by D7. In contrast, blank‐NF mice demonstrated inadequate epithelial extension to reach the midline.

Polarized light imaging of the ONF wound edge showed collagen fiber formation and organization (Figure 6C). At D1, the disrupted oral mucosa's collagen fibers are detached and disorganized at the medial reticular layer, with minimal submucosal collagen bundling. By D3, blank‐NF mice exhibit reattached collagen fibers oriented in various directions toward the underlying bone, ahead of the squamous epithelium edge. In contrast, FTY720‐NF treated epithelium advances more evenly medially, forming a cap around the lamina propria edge. Collagen fibers are organized into distinct layers, with clear bundling and horizontal alignment under 90° polarization.

Discussion

Pediatric patients face ONF, necessitating repeated, often unsuccessful surgeries to repair the cleft palate. Standard allograft treatments primarily act as membrane barriers without promoting functional tissue regeneration or modulating the immune system toward a pro‐regenerative oral environment. Current oral wound therapies have significant limitations, including 1) the absence of an FDA‐approved mucosal graft, 2) lack of immunomodulatory interventions via pro‐regenerative immune cell recruitment, and 3) functional barriers that ensure successful wound closure. To address these issues, we developed a TD bilayer biomaterial system for controlled delivery of the FDA‐approved drug FTY720 and enhanced oral wound healing following ONF injury. This bilayer system, using occlusive TD wound dressing, sustained local FTY720 release with safe tissue biodistribution, significantly enhancing ONF healing, wound closure, and immune response modulation to drive tissue regeneration. Our FTY720‐NF drug delivery system sustained release for up to 7 days in vitro and maintained local, 8–24‐h transdermal drug release in vivo. We extensively characterized local oral immune responses to FTY720‐NF, identifying a transition in myeloid and lymphoid immune cell subsets from inflammatory to pro‐regenerative phenotypes, facilitating complete wound healing by D7.

Our comprehensive immunophenotyping data align with the known oral wound healing cascade, enhancing the understanding of FTY720's role in resolving oral wounds post‐ONF injury. FTY720‐NF delivery significantly reduces neutrophil and monocyte infiltration in the palate on D1 post‐injury, likely due to FTY720's restriction of neutrophil and monocyte egress from the bone marrow via S1PR1/4 and S1PR3, respectively.[ 40 , 41 ] Neutrophil and monocyte SPADE dendrogram outputs provide insight into pseudotime shift in immune cell state, indicating that FTY720‐NF can aid in transitioning Ly6Ghi neutrophils to a CD62Lint state and Ly6C monocytes to Ly6Cint and Ly6Clo phenotypes. Previous studies indicate neutrophils circulate for 5–9 h before reaching tissues and survive for 8–16 h before phagocytosis.[ 42 ] More recent research suggests an average circulatory neutrophil lifespan of 5.4 days in humans, indicating a functional role in later wound healing stages.[ 43 ] However, to our knowledge, neutrophil survival and aging timelines in the context of FTY720 and oral wound healing remain unclear. FTY720‐NF treated mice present an influx of MerTK+ macrophages on D3. Macrophages are widely known to facilitate the maintenance and resolution of inflammation by downregulating inflammatory factors and upregulating anti‐inflammatory growth factors that send out activation signals to lymphoid and stromal cells.[ 44 ] Previous studies have shown that S1P conventionally promotes CD86+ M1‐like macrophage polarization through SIPR3, but as an S1P‐antagonist, FTY720 attenuates CD206+ M2‐like macrophage polarization instead.[ 40 ] Although no significant changes in FTY720‐mediated macrophage polarization were observed in flow cytometric analyses, marker expression from the SPADE dendrogram suggested polarization to M1‐, M2‐, or hybrid M1/M2‐like phenotypes. It is possible that the hybrid macrophage phenotype helps to resolve inflammation, which in turn, helps to drive ONF repair, similar to a study where S1PR modulators induced hybrid macrophage polarization in a murine volumetric muscle loss model.[ 21 ] In follow‐up studies, we will continue to explore the role of FTY720 in modulating macrophages in the context of ONF healing and its role as lipid and gene mediators.

Our quantitative data suggest that FTY720 treatment does not uniformly affect all lymphocytes. Evidence from DC SPADE dendrograms indicates that the lineage of DCs influences ONF healing. Prior studies have shown that classical DCs can be lymphoid CD11b−CD8+ or myeloid CD11b+ derived and such sublineage allow DCs to form a unique transcriptional entity.[ 45 ] CD8+ DCs specialize in developing Th responses and phagocytosing dead cells, while CD11b+ DCs, controlled by granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), are specialized in presenting major histocompatibility complex class II.[ 46 ] Our dendrograms show upregulation in lymphoid‐derived DCs, suggesting that ONF healing correlates with CD8+ DC differentiation. These lymphoid‐derived DCs are stimulated by cytokine FMS‐related tyrosine kinase 3 ligand rather than GM‐CSF, influencing the development of inflammatory DCs[ 47 ] In addition, we observe significant decreases in CD3+ T and CD4+ Th cells at D5 and D7, aligning with studies showing that FTY720 suppresses T‐cell egress from lymph nodes via S1PR1 to prevent hyper‐activation of lymphoid cells and reduce a pro‐inflammatory response.[ 48 , 49 ] Notably, the B and NK cell percentages increase after post‐FTY720 treatment, despite being modulated by S1PR1 and S1PR4‐5. Similar effects of FTY720 on B cells are observed in a mouse model of MS, indicating increased B cell production may be due to less reliance on S1P1‐mediated egress compared to T cells and relative resistance to FTY720‐induced apoptosis.[ 50 , 51 ] We acknowledge limitations within our gating strategy to delineate immune cell classes using CD11b as a myeloid marker and in future studies, lymphocytes can be gated out from lineage positive cells from the CD45+ gate. We also note that the inclusion of two independent variables (Time and ± FTY720) in a SPADE dendrogram is a less conventional approach as the tree itself does not represent differences between conditions. However, our team and other researchers have successfully utilized the overlaid tree as a template/map to extract data for comparing various conditions.[ 21 , 22 , 24 ] Based on the outputs from each overlaid SPADE dendrograms, we calculate cell frequency and marker expression, and then use cell clusters to represent the different phenotypes observed under the given condition.

Our previous study demonstrates that FTY720‐NF enhances oral wound closure but does not assess stromal cell functions in tissue regeneration.[ 15 ] Unlike prior studies on scaffold delivery systems for pre‐clinical oral wound models, we provide quantitative and qualitative analyses of stromal cell functions with FTY720‐NF in ONF healing.[ 4 , 52 , 53 , 54 ] The crosstalk between fibroblasts and leukocytes forms a powerful cell‐intrinsic amplification loop that can give rise to pathological overproduction of fibroblasts, which have been found to be central drivers of fibrotic conditions in nonhealing wounds due to the release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.[ 55 ] This autocrine loop results in pathological behavior from overproduction of pro‐inflammatory factors, as seen in chronic diabetic foot ulcers.[ 56 ] Incorporating FTY720‐NF at the wound site reduces aberrant stromal cell infiltration, preventing excessive fibroblast, MSC, and keratinocyte production, which is detrimental to wound closure, as shown in control mice. Pentachrome histology reveals robust, full‐thickness ONF wound closure by D7 with TD bilayer delivery of FTY720‐NF, demonstrating organized collagen fibers and medial epithelium advancement. Local delivery of FTY720 causes systemic lymphopenia, indicating S1P1 receptor antagonism. Residual FTY720 is undetectable in blood by LC/MS, likely due to low release concentrations. These findings suggest that even low tissue concentrations of FTY720 can induce systemic effects through S1P1 receptor internalization and degradation on lymphocytes, consistent with previous reports on FTY720P functional antagonism of S1P1 at low concentrations (nm).[ 51 ] Further studies are needed to quantify the tissue concentrations of FTY720 and FTY720P achieved with local delivery and to determine the minimum local concentrations required to modulate lymphocytes. These findings provide critical translational support for the further development of immunomodulatory therapeutics for oral wound healing, potentially offering greater opportunities for FDA‐approved treatment options for mucosal tissue repair.

Experimental Section

FTY720 Scaffold Nanofiber Fabrication and Electrospinning

FTY720‐loaded and unloaded electrospun NFs were fabricated using a 1:1 weight ratio of 75:25 poly(lactide‐co‐glycolide acid) (Sigma–Aldrich #719927) and polycaprolactone (Sigma–Aldrich #440744) dissolved in a 1:3 Methanol:Chloroform solvent. FTY720 (US Pharmacopeia #1270480) drug was dissolved in 1:3 Methanol:Chloroform solvent and thoroughly mixed with polymer blend solution under constant sonication and shaking. 2 mL drug:polymer solution was electrospun at the rate of 1 mL h−1 at 19 kV using a Harvard Apparatus PHD 2000 infusion pump and HV Power Supply (Gamma High Voltage Research #ES30P‐5W/DAM). NF sheets were lipolyzed overnight and stored at −80 °C until use.

In Vivo ONF Injury Murine Model and Scaffold Implantation

C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratory #000664), aged 4–6 weeks, were anesthetized with a Ketamine (100 mg kg−1): Xylazine (10 mg kg−1) cocktail intraperitoneally, and Buprenorphine SR (1.0 mg kg−1) was administered subcutaneously as an analgesic. A 1.5 mm full thickness ONF injury was created between the 3rd and 4th rugae in the hard palate mucosa using a high temperature cauterizer (Bovie Medical Corporation). 1.5 mm diameter Blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF scaffolds were implanted immediately above the ONF site and secured using Tegaderm bilayer adhesive methods. ONF healing was monitored using an endoscope (SKOR). At the terminal timepoint, mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation and cervical dislocation. Hard palate mucosa was excised using a scalpel with #11 straight, angled blade (Integra Miltex #4‐111), and blood was collected via cardiac puncture in K2 EDTA‐coated tubes (BD #367841). All procedures were performed in compliance with Emory University's Department of Animal Research and Institutional Care and Use Committee (PROTO201700263).

FTY720‐NF Release Kinetics and Drug Loading Efficiency

A 3 mm diameter NF scaffold was created using a biopsy punch for scaffold characterization purposes. NF loaded with FTY720 was immersed in simulated body fluid with 5% fatty acid‐free bovine serum albumin in phosphate buffered saline. At specified timepoints (0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 3 d, 5 d, and 7 d), the releasate was collected and replaced. The remaining scaffold was then dissolved in a 1:3 methanol:chloroform solution to determine the total drug content. Equal volumes of 100% Acetonitrile (Sigma–Aldrich #34851) were added, and the relative abundance of FTY720 was measured using LC/MS courtesy of Dr. Dennis Liotta's Mass spectrometry facilities at Emory University Department of Chemistry.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Electrospun NF loaded with or without FTY720 was cut into 3 mm diameters scaffolds using a biopsy punch. Scaffolds were sputter coated with gold for 30 s, and SEM images of blank‐NF, 5 mg, and 10 mg scaffolds were taken at 300×, 1000×, and 3000× zoom at 15kV using Emory University's Robert P. Apkarian Integrated Electron Microscopy Core.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

Electrospun NF loaded with or without FTY720 was cut into 8 mm diameters scaffolds using a biopsy punch as AFM characterization required a larger surface area. For stiffness measurement, 5.24 µm Si beads were attached to tipless silicon cantilevers using two‐part epoxy and dried for at least 24 h. The thermal method was used to calibrate the cantilever spring constant immediately prior to use by indenting a glass substrate and performing a Lorentzian fit to the thermal spectrum. Topological images and stiffness characterizations were obtained from 80 × 80 µm2 force maps with 96 × 96 force curves per image. All the force–distance measurements were performed with 2 µm s−1 approach velocity using a 500 nN force trigger. Force–distance plots were transformed into force‐indentation depth plots, then 10–100% indentation depth was used to evaluate Young's modulus using a Hertzian contact model. All AFM measurements were performed by Dr. Alan Lui courtesy of Dr. Todd Sulchek's Lab at Georgia Tech's Department of Mechanical Engineering.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)

NMR spectra (1H) were obtained using a Bruker AVANCE III spectrometer (Figure S1, Supporting Information). NMR samples (10 mg of blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF) were prepared and processed in deuterated acetone (CD3)2CO using the residual solvent peak ((CD3)2CO: 1H = 2.05 ppm) as an internal reference unless otherwise specified. NMR data were reported to include chemical shifts (δ) reported in ppm, multiplicities indicated as s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), q (quartet), m (multiplet), br (broad), or app (apparent), coupling constants (J) reported in Hz, and integration normalized to 1 atom (H), except when relative ratios of monomers were explicitly shown. NMR analysis was performed by Dr. Leon Jacobs courtesy of Dr. Dennis Liotta's Lab at Emory's Department of Chemistry.

Endoscope Images and Wound Closure Analysis

Following ONF injury, palatal images were taken by an endoscope (KARL STORZ) at Level 3 brightness on all timepoints. The oral palate was measured to be ≈4.5 mm in length between the side molars. Images and injury sizes were analyzed using ImageJ. Percent Healed Area was measured using the following formula: Percent Healed Area = D1Area(mm2)−D7Area(mm2)D1Area(mm2).

Palate Tissue Staining and Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometric studies and analyses involved n = 9–12 C57BL/6J mice per experimental group (Figure S2D, Supporting Information). Fresh palatal mucosa was harvested and digested in a solution containing Collagenase II 5500 U mL−1 (Thermofisher #17101015) and Dispase II 2.5 U mL−1 (Sigma #D4693‐1G) for 60–80 min at 37 °C. Single cells were washed in ice‐cold PBS, centrifuged for 5 min, and the cell pellet was saved. Blood from each mouse was processed using red blood cell lysis buffer and filtered to obtain a leukocyte pellet. Cells were incubated in mouse Fc block solution (BD Biosciences #101301) and stained with an optimized antibody panel (Figure S2E, Supporting Information). Fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, cells were stored in FACS buffer at 4 °C overnight until flow cytometry. Flow runs were conducted using a five‐Laser Cytek Aurora, courtesy of Emory Pediatrics/Winship Flow Cytometry core, with the gating strategy detailed in OMIQ.ai (Figure S3, Supporting Information).

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)

UMAP was conducted using OMIQ.ai software, a nonlinear dimensionality reduction algorithm capable of embedding high‐dimensional data into 2D or 3D. Cells were visualized in a scatter plot, with similarity indicated by proximity. Each flow cytometry sample was pre‐gated to select immune cell classes (myeloid, lymphoid, stromal, and neutrophils); then, imported into OMIQ.ai. UMAP parameters were set at n_neighbor = 15 and min_distance = 0.4. UMAP projections were created for cellular markers of interest, and samples from specific treatment groups or timepoints were visualized by overlaying scatterplots.

Spanning‐Tree Progression Analysis of Density‐Normalized Events (SPADE)

SPADE was generated using MATLAB, with the source code accessible at http://pengqiu.gatech.edu/software/SPADE/. Node size and color correlated with cell count and median marker expression. SPADE generated the tree through density‐dependent downsampling, agglomerative clustering, cluster linking via a minimum spanning‐tree algorithm, and user‐driven upsampling. The SPADE tree was created by exporting compensated, pre‐gated cellular subsets (e.g., Ly6G+ neutrophils) with the parameters: ignore compensation, no transformation, neighborhood size 5, local density approximation factor 1.5, max cells in pooled downsampled data 50 000, target density 20 000 cells, and 100 desired clusters.

SPADE Dendrogram Outputs and Pseudo‐Time Projections

SPADE dendrogram outputs include cell frequency from individual FCS files and heatmaps constructed from marker expression within each node clusters. Pseudo‐time projections, indicated by arrows, were manually determined by overlaying cell frequency from individual FCS files in each dendrogram, enabling pseudo‐transient trajectory mapping for each experimental timepoint. Heatmaps were constructed by calculating the z‐scores of each fluorescence intensity for every surface marker across all dendrogram annotations/nodes. Each row of the heatmap corresponded to a cellular surface marker, and each column was associated with one dendrogram node. The expression level of each surface marker ranged from high (dark red) to low (dark blue). SPADE analysis was conducted under the guidance of Dr. Peng Qiu from Georgia Tech's Department of Biomedical Engineering.

Histology and Imaging

Mouse heads were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin wax for coronal sectioning. 7–10 µm sections were mounted on charged glass slides for further histochemical staining with a modified pentachrome method. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded ethanol washes to water, followed by pre‐treatment with 6% nitric acid and sequential staining with 0.1% toluidine blue (EMS #26074‐15) and picrosirius red (Abcam #ab246832). Slides were then dehydrated and cover‐slipped with toluene‐based mounting medium for imaging. Brightfield images were captured with an AxioScan.Z1 slide scanner (Zeiss) equipped with a Plan‐apochromat 40X/0.95 objective. Darkfield polarized transmission images were captured with a 10X/0.3NA objective using an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus) equipped with a condenser with NA adjusted to 0.3, a rotatable sample stage, and an Infinity 2C color camera (Luminera). The rotatable polarizer and polarizer‐analyzer were crossed at 90° through the microscope light path.

Biodistribution

Samples of palate, liver, and blood were collected from FTY720‐treated mice (n = 3) at various intervals: 2H, 6H, 1d, 2d, 3d, 5d, and 7d. 50µL of serum was mixed with 100µL of ice‐cold acetonitrile containing an internal standard (d5‐ethoxycoumarin), vortexed, and centrifuged to pellet proteins. Tissue samples were extracted using ice‐cold acetonitrile with d5‐ethoxycoumarin based on wet tissue weights. Liver samples required 9 µL of acetonitrile per mg of tissue, while palate samples needed 24 µL per mg. Tissue samples were homogenized with ceramic beads using a Lysera homogenizer (Biotage) at 4 °C. Supernatants were transferred to LC‐MS autosampler vials for analysis. Calibration curves were generated by adding known concentrations of FTY720 and FTY720P to matrix‐matched control serum, palate, or liver samples. Analysis was performed using an Agilent 1260 LC coupled to a 6460C Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism, presenting data as mean ± SEM unless noted otherwise. An a priori power analysis determined the necessary sample size for mouse studies, aiming for a power of 0.8 and α of 0.05, with wound size as the primary outcome over 7 days. One‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test, compared in vitro FTY720‐NF release (n = 3–5 scaffolds) and in vivo wound closure and blood immune cell counts (n = 9–11 and n = 5–6 mice, respectively). For comprehensive flow analyses, differences between blank‐NF and FTY720‐NF treatments at individual time points were assessed using a parametric, unpaired t‐test (n = 11–12 mice). Normality was confirmed by visual assessment of Q–Q plots, and heteroskedasticity was determined by visual assessment of studentized residual plot. Statistical significance was determined using α = 0.05. All statistical analyses were confirmed and validated by Biostatisticians in collaboration with Emory University's Pediatric Biostatistics Core.
