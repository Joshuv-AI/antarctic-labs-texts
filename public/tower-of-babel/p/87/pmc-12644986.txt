Assessing glymphatic-associated fluid dynamics in psychiatric disorders: evidence from neuroimaging - a review
Authors: Xinlan Zhang, Liana Hatoum, Jia Ying, Chuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough the glymphatic system has been extensively investigated in neurodegenerative diseases, its potential role in psychiatric disorders is only beginning to be recognized. Conditions such as major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder frequently exhibit physiological disturbances, including disrupted sleep, neuroinflammation, vascular impairment, and altered astrocytic function, that may modulate glymphatic transport. Recent neuroimaging studies have started to explore these associations. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been used to quantify perivascular space enlargement in depression and schizophrenia. Diffusion-based approaches, including low-b-value diffusion and the diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, have been used to estimate perivascular diffusivity, with some studies linking these measures to symptom severity. Functional MRI metrics such as blood oxygen level-dependent–cerebrospinal fluid (BOLD–CSF) coupling have provided indirect markers of CSF pulsatility, revealing associations with sleep quality and cognition. Positron emission tomography (PET) has been investigated for assessing CSF tracer dynamics and targeting neuroinflammation. While these imaging results are promising, they are often indirect, methodologically heterogeneous, and derived from small samples. This review examines current evidence on glymphatic alterations in psychiatric conditions, describes shared and disorder-specific mechanisms, and assesses how complementary MRI and PET approaches can provide a more integrated understanding of glymphatic function. It also addresses methodological challenges, identifies research gaps, and discusses opportunities to incorporate glymphatic imaging into psychiatric diagnostics, prognosis, and treatment monitoring. The article is intended for researchers and clinicians in psychiatry, neurology, and neuroimaging who are interested in the translational potential of glymphatic research.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the glymphatic system has become a central model for understanding how cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exchanges with interstitial fluid (ISF) and facilitates metabolic waste clearance in the brain. The term “glymphatic” was first introduced by Iliff et al. (2012), reflecting the system’s dependence on glial cells and its functional similarity to the peripheral lymphatic system . First identified in rodent studies, this perivascular transport pathway is mainly driven by arterial pulsatility (Iliff et al., 2013b; Mestre et al., 2018b) and supported by astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) water channels (Mestre et al., 2018a; Peng et al., 2023). Since its initial discovery, accumulating evidence from human neuroimaging and histological research has confirmed the presence of glymphatic-like mechanisms in the human brain. As research has advanced, its implications for neurodegenerative diseases have been increasingly recognized, particularly in relation to impaired clearance of neurotoxic proteins such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau (Kent et al., 2020).

More recently, there is growing interest in whether similar glymphatic impairments might play a role in psychiatric disorders. Historically, psychiatric disorders have been viewed primarily through neurotransmitter (Teleanu et al., 2022) and synaptic dysfunction (Howes et al., 2025). However, these conditions are now recognized to involve broader systemic changes, including chronic inflammation (Bauer and Teixeira, 2019), sleep and circadian disruption (Wulff et al., 2010; Meyer et al., 2024), and vascular dysregulation (Schaare et al., 2023). These features are known to influence glymphatic function, suggesting that impaired fluid transport and waste clearance may represent a shared mechanism across mental illnesses.

Despite the overlaps, systematic evaluation of glymphatic system abnormalities in psychiatric populations remains limited. Individual studies have investigated glymphatic proxies such as perivascular space (PVS) morphology, diffusion-based indices (e.g. ALPS), dynamic coupling of neural activity with CSF flow using functional magentic resonance imaging (fMRI), and ventricular tracer clearance using PET. However, an integrated review that connects these findings across different psychiatric diagnoses is still lacking.

In this article, we first outline the biology and physiology of the glymphatic system, then review evidence for its dysfunction in specific psychiatric disorders, and finally discuss implications for imaging, diagnosis, and treatment strategy.

Overview of the glymphatic system

Concept of the glymphatic system

Historically, the central nervous system (CNS) was believed to lack a conventional lymphatic drainage system due to the blood–brain barrier’s (BBB) restrictive nature and absence of lymphatic vessels within brain parenchyma (Weller et al., 2009). Waste clearance was thought to rely exclusively on intracellular degradation such as autophagy and ubiquitination (Labbadia and Morimoto, 2015; Hjerpe et al., 2016; Thibaudeau et al., 2018), extracellular proteolysis (Yin et al., 2006), or passive diffusion across the BBB into the bloodstream (Abbott et al., 2010).

This prevailing view was challenged by Iliff et al. (2012), who used in vivo two-photon microscopy and fluorescent tracers in rodents to reveal a previously unrecognized perivascular fluid transport pathway. It was termed the “glymphatic system,” reflecting its dependence on glial cells (“gly-”) and its functional analogy to the lymphatic system (“-mphatic”). This pathway facilitates the exchange of CSF and ISF along the PVS, which is mediated by AQP4 water channels highly expressed on astrocytic endfeet surrounding cerebral vessels (Jessen et al., 2015).

In 2013, the same group further employed dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) with intrathecal contrast agent administration to demonstrate that the glymphatic system is brain-wide and follows the cerebral arterial vasculature (Iliff et al., 2013a). Subsequent research confirmed similar anatomical structures and functional features in humans, lending support to the existence of a human glymphatic system (Ringstad et al., 2017; Yamamoto et al., 2024).

While evidence for glymphatic pathways in humans continues to grow, most mechanistic insight still comes from rodent models. Human data remain largely indirect and rely on surrogate imaging markers rather than direct visualization of ISF movement.

Physiology of the glymphatic system

The glymphatic system is a specialized fluid transport network that maintains cerebral homeostasis (Benveniste et al., 2020), removes metabolic waste (Benveniste et al., 2019), and supports brain metabolism (Jessen et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 1, it operates through bulk flow of CSF into the brain parenchyma along periarterial spaces, exchange with ISF, and subsequent clearance along perivenous routes toward lymphatic drainage sites (Iliff et al., 2012, 2013a; Aspelund et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2017; Bedussi et al., 2018).

Glymphatic flow begins with CSF entry from the subarachnoid space into periarterial spaces surrounding large leptomeningeal arteries on the brain surface (Iliff et al., 2012). Propelled primarily by arterial pulsations (Iliff et al., 2013b; Mestre et al., 2018b), the fluid advances along these conduits into deeper brain regions through Virchow–Robin spaces that envelop penetrating arteries (Mestre et al., 2017). These channels are bounded by astrocytic endfeet enriched with AQP4 water channels (Badaut et al., 2002; Iadecola and Nedergaard, 2007; Mathiisen et al., 2010), enabling CSF to pass into the interstitium. Within the parenchyma, CSF mixes with ISF, facilitating the removal of metabolic by-products such as Aβ (Tarasoff-Conway et al., 2015) and tau (Harrison et al., 2020), and aiding in the distribution of essential metabolites including lipids (Achariyar et al., 2016) and glucose (Lundgaard et al., 2015).

PVS serve as the main conduits of glymphatic transport (Mestre et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023b; Yamamoto et al., 2024). Their size and patency are influenced by vascular compliance and astrocytic activity. Changes in vascular tone, such as those occurring in hypertension or ischemia, can alter PVS geometry and fluid flux (Mestre et al., 2018b, 2020; Rosic et al., 2019). The polarized localization of AQP4 at perivascular endfeet is equally essential; loss or mislocalization of AQP4 markedly reduces glymphatic efficiency and has been linked to impaired clearance in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders (Kress et al., 2014; Z. Xu et al., 2015; Mestre et al., 2018a; Ohene et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2019; Harrison et al., 2020; Gomolka et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2023).

perineural pathways that carry CSF along cranial and spinal nerves, most prominently the olfactory nerve, into lymphatic vessels of the nasal mucosa and onward to cervical lymph nodes (Bradbury and Westrop, 1983; Lundgaard et al., 2017);

dural lymphatic vessels within the dura mater that collect fluid from the subarachnoid and PVS and drain to deep cervical lymph nodes via skull base foramina (Ahn et al., 2019; Aspelund et al., 2015; Louveau et al., 2015; Raper et al., 2016); and

This pathway is related to, but distinct from, bulk CSF circulation, which primarily occurs within the ventricles and subarachnoid cisterns without direct exchange with the interstitial compartment. Both systems are interconnected and together influence overall glymphatic function.

Pathological alterations in psychiatric disorders relevant to glymphatic function

Accumulating evidence indicates that psychiatric disorders involve pathological changes such as BBB disruption, PVS enlargement, and AQP4 dysregulation. Alterations in these core components of the glymphatic system (vascular interface, perivascular conduits, and astroglial water channels) are likely to impair glymphatic transport.

In conditions such as schizophrenia, major depressive disorder (MDD), and bipolar disorder, microvascular endothelial deficits contribute to BBB dysfunction (Lizano et al., 2023), with tight-junction disruption as a consistent feature (Greene et al., 2020). In MDD, oxidative stress, endothelial nitric oxide synthase uncoupling, and reduced nitric oxide levels are linked to neurovascular dysfunction and increased BBB permeability (Najjar et al., 2013). Cerebral microvascular dysfunction has also been associated with late-life depression risk (van Agtmaal et al., 2017). Because BBB breakdown is associated with reduced vascular compliance and pulsatility (Konig et al., 2025), these changes may weaken the hemodynamic forces that support glymphatic transport.

PVS enlargement is another recurrent finding in psychiatric disorders and may indicate underlying glymphatic dysfunction (Chen et al., 2024; Sotgiu et al., 2025). As critical conduits for CSF–ISF exchange, enlarged PVS can paradoxically impede CSF influx, thereby disrupting solute transport and impairing waste clearance. In parallel, AQP4 variants have been linked to schizophrenia risk (Wu et al., 2018, 2020), while stress and depression models consistently show reduced AQP4 expression and loss of perivascular polarization (Liu et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2019), which may further disrupt CSF influx and clearance of neurotoxic proteins.

Together, these pathological features suggest that glymphatic dysfunction is not merely a secondary phenomenon but may represent a mechanistic contributor to psychiatric illness.

Having described pathological alterations in psychiatric disorders that may implicate glymphatic dysfunction, we next outline the general functions of the glymphatic system that may be directly relevant to these disorders.

Physiological roles of the glymphatic system and psychiatric relevance

The glymphatic system is thought to contribute to CNS homeostasis through coordinated waste clearance, immune regulation, and nutrient distribution, although the relative importance of these roles in humans remains under active investigation. These processes may be particularly relevant for psychiatric disorders, where metabolic, immune, and vascular disturbances are often observed.

Experimental and imaging studies suggest that one of its main functions is the removal of metabolic by-products, including neurotoxic proteins such as Aβ, tau, and α-synuclein (Lopes et al., 2025), as well as ions and metabolites such as potassium (Monai et al., 2019) and lactate (Lundgaard et al., 2017). Reduced clearance efficiency has been proposed as a potential contributor to neuroinflammation and altered neuronal signaling, mechanisms implicated in mood and psychotic disorders (Da Mesquita et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2016; X. Liu et al., 2021; Rasmussen et al., 2020).

The system may also participate in immune surveillance by facilitating the movement of CNS-derived antigens toward peripheral lymphatic structures, where they can interact with immune cells (Kim and Kipnis, 2025). Such pathways could influence neuroimmune activity, which appears altered in conditions marked by chronic inflammation, such as depression or schizophrenia (Najjar et al., 2013). In addition, glymphatic transport might distribute and remove inflammatory mediators, potentially affecting CNS inflammatory cascades that exacerbate psychiatric symptoms (Mogensen et al., 2021).

Another proposed function is the distribution of nutrients and signaling molecules, including glucose (Lundgaard et al., 2015), lipids (Achariyar et al., 2016; Rangroo Thrane et al., 2013; Tsujita et al., 2024), and amino acids (Jessen et al., 2015; Nycz and Mandera, 2021). If impaired, these processes could influence neuronal metabolism and synaptic plasticity—factors important for cognitive and emotional regulation.

Several features common to psychiatric illness align with factors thought to modulate glymphatic transport. Sleep disruption (Chong et al., 2022; Eugene and Masiak, 2015; Que et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2022), chronic stress (Gu et al., 2022; Ivanovska et al., 2023), systemic inflammation (Mogensen et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2023), and vascular risk factors (Ang et al., 2024) may each impair CSF–ISF exchange through effects on arterial pulsatility, AQP4 polarization, or PVS integrity. These overlapping influences raise the possibility that impaired glymphatic function contributes to cognitive and affective symptoms across diagnostic boundaries. While this hypothesis remains to be tested in large-scale longitudinal studies, clarifying these links could reveal novel prevention and treatment targets.

Understanding these potential functions requires considering the physiological mechanisms that drive glymphatic flow, which are discussed in the next section.

Mechanisms regulating glymphatic transport

Multiple physiological factors are believed to influence glymphatic transport. Arterial pulsatility, generated by the cardiac cycle, acts as a primary mechanical driver, propelling CSF along periarterial spaces (Iliff et al., 2013b; Mestre et al., 2018b). Respiratory movements may also play a role by shifting CSF through perivascular and subarachnoid routes, aided by pressure changes in the thoracic cavity (Dreha-Kulaczewski et al., 2015). The choroid plexus continuously produces CSF, creating a pressure that directs flow from the ventricles to the subarachnoid space (Jessen et al., 2015)

Sleep is a strong modulator in experimental models, with slow-wave sleep linked to greater interstitial space volume and higher glymphatic influx compared with wakefulness (Hablitz et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2013). In humans, sleep-related changes in CSF–ISF exchange have been observed, but the extent and timing remain uncertain (Fultz et al., 2019). Circadian influences are plausible, although direct evidence in humans is limited.

Astrocytic AQP4 polarization at perivascular endfeet is thought to be critical for efficient water flux. Aging (Kress et al., 2014), neuroinflammation (Fukuda and Badaut, 2012), and metabolic stress (Wei et al., 2019) can alter AQP4 localization and reduce transport efficiency. Vascular health—including arterial stiffness, endothelial function, and microvascular integrity—may further modulate flow, potentially linking systemic cardiovascular risk to impaired brain clearance (Ang et al., 2024). Additional systemic factors such as chronic stress, obesity, and hypertension may also impair clearance by promoting endothelial dysfunction, altering arterial pulsatility, and increasing BBB permeability (Gędek et al., 2023).

Because these mechanisms are sensitive to systemic and environmental influences, their disruption may be particularly relevant to psychiatric populations in which sleep disturbance, inflammation, vascular pathology, and metabolic risk factors are common. Importantly, current imaging approaches capture only indirect surrogate markers of glymphatic function [e.g. diffusion MRI, blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD)–CSF coupling, tracer clearance with positron emission tomography (PET)], and none provide a specific readout of transport impairment. Additional modulatory factors: sex differences and pharmacological influences

Sex differences in glymphatic function

Although sex-related effects on glymphatic transport remain uncertain, animal data have not shown consistent sex differences in bulk glymphatic tracer influx (Giannetto et al., 2020). In large cohorts of healthy C57BL/6 mice, investigators reported higher CSF production in females than in male mice, but this might not translate into greater glymphatic transport (G. Liu et al., 2020). Estrogen and ovarian hormones may influence astrocytic AQP4 expression and vascular properties in other contexts, yet a direct hormonal effect on glymphatic flow has not been demonstrated (Mathias et al., 2025). Human MRI studies of PVS have reported variable findings, with some showing higher PVS burden in males and others reporting no difference (Lara et al., 2022; Piantino et al., 2020); these results are frequently confounded by sleep and vascular risk factors (Ramirez et al., 2021). Given established sex differences in psychiatric epidemiology, clarifying whether sex exerts a biologically meaningful influence on glymphatic function requires standardized imaging and rigorous control of confounders.

Pharmacological modulation of glymphatic transport

Experimental evidence suggests that some psychiatric drugs may influence glymphatic-related mechanisms, though direct demonstrations in humans are rare. In a chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) mouse model, fluoxetine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), reversed glymphatic impairment, restoring perivascular AQP4 localization and improving tracer clearance (Xia et al., 2017). Another study found that citalopram acutely reduced CSF Aβ production in healthy adults and decreased plaque deposition in mice (Sheline et al., 2014). The most direct human evidence comes from a randomized controlled trial in cognitively normal older adults, where escitalopram, an SSRI, significantly reduced CSF Aβ42 concentrations compared with placebo (Sheline et al., 2020). These findings are consistent with enhanced brain solute clearance, although glymphatic imaging was not performed.

Additional preclinical work links stress and sleep pathways to transport. Mifepristone, a glucocorticoid receptor (GR) antagonist, rescued CUMS-induced loss of AQP4 polarization and improved tracer-defined transport in mice (Wei et al., 2019). In rodent studies, during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, infraslow norepinephrine oscillations drive slow vasomotion, which promotes glymphatic fluid flow, but this process can be disrupted by the sedative zolpidem (Hauglund et al., 2025). While antagonizing norepinephrine receptors during wakefulness reduces PVS volume, it also facilitates glymphatic transport (Xie et al., 2013). Melatonin has also been shown to improve AQP4 polarity and increase tracer-defined transport in both sleep-restriction and chronic stress mouse models. These effects were absent in AQP4 knockout mice, supporting a mechanistic dependence on astrocytic AQP4 (Sun et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2023a).

Lithium, a mood stabilizer widely used in bipolar disorder, has been reported to attenuate stress-induced BBB disruption and to affect hippocampal AQP4 in rats (Taler et al., 2021). It has also been shown to influence circadian regulation in the choroid plexus in rats (Liška et al., 2023), but no studies have directly examined its effects on glymphatic transport.

Among anti-inflammatory agents, minocycline and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) reduce neuroinflammation in preclinical models, but direct restoration of glymphatic clearance has not been demonstrated. In contrast, omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) improved glymphatic transport and reduced Aβ deposition in mice (Ren et al., 2017; E. Zhang et al., 2020), suggesting that some anti-inflammatory interventions may have the potential to enhance waste clearance.

Glymphatic dysfunction in psychiatric disorders

Over recent years, a growing number of studies have applied neuroimaging techniques to investigate glymphatic function in psychiatric populations. These approaches range from structural MRI, which assesses PVS morphology (Yu et al., 2022), to diffusion-based measures such as the DTI-ALPS index that estimates perivascular diffusivity (Taoka et al., 2017), and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), which evaluates BOLD–CSF coupling (Fultz et al., 2019) . In a smaller number of investigations, PET imaging has been applied to study CSF tracer clearance processes and related neuroinflammatory changes (Brown et al., 2024; Leon et al., 2017; Okazawa et al., 2024; Schubert et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2025).

Although these methods offer only indirect proxies rather than direct visualization of glymphatic flow, they provide useful tools to study clearance pathways in psychiatric illness. Before reviewing disorder-specific findings, we note that the imaging methods applied to study glymphatic function (e.g. diffusion MRI, rs-fMRI, PET) are described in detail in Section "Imaging glymphatic function". Readers seeking methodological context may wish to consult those subsections before proceeding.

Schizophrenia

Glymphatic system dysfunction in schizophrenia may reflect overlapping abnormality disturbances in sleep regulation, immune activation, and astrocytic signaling. Neuroimmune dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a core feature of schizophrenia (Müller et al., 2009; Tomasik et al., 2016), involving glial activation, cytokine imbalance, and compromised BBB integrity. Schizophrenia also presents systemic features similar to those in neurodegenerative conditions, including early cognitive deterioration (McCutcheon et al., 2023), metabolic imbalance (De Hert et al., 2009; Helaly and Ghorab, 2023), and reduced life expectancy, with symptoms often emerging during the prodromal phase (Peritogiannis et al., 2022).

Recent neuroimaging studies have used various proxies to assess brain fluid dynamics in schizophrenia. Several DTI-ALPS studies have revealed reduced ALPS indices in schizophrenia patients, particularly in those with pronounced negative symptoms, suggesting impaired perivascular diffusivity (Abdolizadeh et al., 2024; Barlattani et al., 2025; Korann et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2024). Investigations of rs-fMRI have identified weaker and more delayed global BOLD–CSF coupling, reflecting disrupted interactions between neural activity, vascular pulsatility, and CSF flow (Hua et al., 2025); these changes have been linked to sleep disturbances and cognitive deficits.

The coefficient of variation of the BOLD signal (CVBOLD), a marker of physiological pulsatility, is elevated in individuals at risk for psychosis, particularly in periventricular white matter and CSF regions (Saarinen et al., 2020). Enlarged choroid plexus(Brunner et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2024; Y.-F. Zhou et al., 2020), a structure central to CSF production, has been associated with reduced glymphatic clearance, heightened inflammation, and greater symptom severity in psychosis, suggesting a neurodevelopmental contribution to clearance dysfunction (Y. Li et al., 2023; Y. Xu et al., 2024).

At the genetic level, single-nucleotide polymorphisms within the AQP4 gene, particularly in untranslated regulatory regions, have been linked to schizophrenia risk (Y.-F. Wu et al., 2018, 2020). These variants may disrupt AQP4 water channel function, contributing to inefficient CSF-ISF exchange and prolonged inflammatory exposure.

Major depressive disorder

In MDD, glymphatic impairment may result from chronic stress, neuroinflammation, sleep disturbances, and astrocytic AQP4 mislocalization. MDD is increasingly viewed as a systemic brain disorder involving neuroinflammatory responses (Troubat et al., 2021), cognitive deficits (Lam et al., 2014), and disrupted sleep architecture (H. Fang et al., 2019; Murphy and Peterson, 2015; Nutt et al., 2008), all of which relate to glymphatic activity.

Preclinical studies employing CUMS paradigms have consistently demonstrated compromised glymphatic circulation in depressive-like mice (X. Liu et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2017). These impairments involve downregulated AQP4 expression and mislocalization along perivascular endfeet, particularly in the cortex and hippocampus. AQP4 dysfunction hinders CSF tracer influx into the brain parenchyma and promotes the accumulation of Aβ, potentially contributing to the elevated Alzheimer’s disease risk observed in MDD (Xia et al., 2017). Administration of fluoxetine (Xia et al., 2017) or omega-3 PUFA supplements (X. Liu et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2017; Zailani et al., 2024) restored AQP4 expression and reversed both glymphatic dysfunction and behavioral deficits.

Mechanistically, chronic stress appears to suppress AQP4 expression through GR signaling. Activation of GR via dexamethasone mimicked CUMS-induced glymphatic suppression, while blockade with mifepristone partially rescued AQP4 levels and tracer influx (Wei et al., 2019). Iron overload has also been shown to exacerbate glymphatic dysfunction and depressive-like behaviors in stressed mice (S. Liang et al., 2020), supporting the notion that multiple stress-related factors can impair brain fluid homeostasis.

Human neuroimaging studies have provided converging evidence for glymphatic alterations in MDD. DTI-ALPS imaging consistently shows reduced ALPS indices in MDD, indicating compromised perivascular diffusion, with reductions correlating with greater depressive and anxiety symptoms, lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores, and elevated markers of inflammation and oxidative stress (Bao et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2025; Q. Liang et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; C. Yang et al., 2024). Resting-state fMRI studies also report weakened global BOLD–CSF coupling, closely associated with sleep disturbances (Y. Zhang et al., 2024). Importantly, a recent fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET study measuring ventricular CSF tracer clearance found that lower efflux was significantly associated with more severe depressive symptoms, further supporting the link between impaired CSF-mediated waste removal and MDD symptomatology (Zhou et al., 2025).

Bipolar disorder

Glymphatic function in bipolar disorder (BPD) appears to be strongly influenced by circadian rhythm disruption and sleep fragmentation, which are core features of the disorder. Circadian rhythm disturbances and irregular sleep architecture are common in BPD and are known modulators of glymphatic activity (Harvey, 2008; Tonon et al., 2024). Animal studies show that chronic rhythm disruption and sleep deprivation reduce glymphatic tracer influx (X. Li et al., 2023).

A recent DTI-ALPS study in BPD patients revealed significantly decreased indices compared with healthy controls, suggesting attenuated glymphatic transport efficiency (Kikuta et al., 2025). These reductions were more pronounced in patients with longer illness duration or more frequent mood episodes. Another study found no group-level difference in ALPS indices between BPD and controls, but lower values were linked to longer illness duration, which suggests progressive dysfunction (Ueda et al., 2024).

Lithium treatment modulates circadian gene expression in the choroid plexus, a critical component of the glymphatic system, by delaying and prolonging the molecular clock rhythm (Liška et al., 2023). This may help realign glymphatic activity with the sleep–wake cycle. Current imaging studies in BPD remain limited, and the specificity of ALPS reductions to glymphatic dysfunction, rather than broader white matter changes, is still debated.

Post-traumatic stress disorder and anxiety disorders

Glymphatic disruption in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and anxiety disorders may reflect chronic stress-induced astrocytic remodeling combined with sleep fragmentation. Astrocyte dysfunction is well established in stress-related disorders, with chronic stress altering both the expression and localization of AQP4 water channels (Bollinger et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2019). These changes disrupt perivascular fluid exchange and impair glymphatic function.

One study reported significantly lower ALPS indices in PTSD patients compared to healthy controls. These reductions correlated with cognitive deficits and greater symptom severity, with sleep quality partially mediating the association (M.-L. Wang et al., 2025). Another investigation found that lower ALPS values were strongly associated with increased cortical mean diffusivity in entorhinal and parahippocampal regions, which are regions vulnerable to neurodegeneration (Shao et al., 2024).

In generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), rs-fMRI showed decreased coupling between global BOLD signals and CSF inflow (Qian et al., 2025). This reduction was associated with poorer cognitive performance, suggesting that altered glymphatic activity may contribute to cognitive impairment in anxiety. While these results are promising, ALPS and BOLD–CSF coupling measurements can be influenced by factors such as white matter microstructure and vascular function (Ringstad, 2024), and should be interpreted cautiously rather than as direct measures of glymphatic activity.

Neurodevelopmental disorders

Glymphatic disturbances in neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), may arise from early-life sleep disruption and atypical astrocytic or vascular development (Abbasian et al., 2025; Bruni et al., 2025; Davoudi et al., 2023; Dury et al., 2025). Children with these conditions often exhibit delayed sleep onset, frequent night awakenings, shortened total sleep time, and altered melatonin secretion, all of which may interfere with glymphatic activity.

Diffusion-based imaging studies report reduced ALPS indices in children with ASD, indicating impaired perivascular diffusivity (X. Li et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2025; M. Wang et al., 2025). Region-specific PVS enlargement, particularly in prefrontal white matter, has been linked to language impairments and stereotyped behaviors (Sotgiu et al., 2025).

Similar abnormalities are observed in ADHD, with reduced ALPS indices and enlarged PVS correlating with inattention and visual memory deficits, especially in the right hemisphere (Y. Chen et al., 2024; Y. Fang et al., 2025). Although these findings suggest glymphatic alterations in neurodevelopmental disorders, the interpretation of such metrics in developing brains remains uncertain because normal developmental changes in white matter and vascular structure can also influence these measures.

Sleep disorders with psychiatric comorbidity

Sleep is critical for glymphatic function, primarily by enhancing CSF–ISF exchange during NREM slow-wave sleep. Disruption of this process in sleep disorders may impair brain waste clearance, and contributes to cognitive and emotional symptoms across psychiatric conditions (Chong et al., 2022; Logan and McClung, 2019; Yan et al., 2021).

In obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), ALPS indices are consistently reduced compared to healthy controls and show a negative correlation with apnea severity and oxygen desaturation levels (Lee et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2024; Nepozitek et al., 2025). Similar reductions are seen in individuals with general sleep disruption, where lower ALPS values are linked to poorer Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores (Saito et al., 2023). In line with this, CSF velocity in the aqueduct tends to decrease following sleep deprivation compared to normal sleep, although the difference has not reached statistical significance (Pedersen et al., 2023). In narcolepsy, group-level differences in ALPS are less pronounced, but values correlate with polysomnographic measures such as wake after sleep onset and REM sleep proportions (Gumeler et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024).

OSA patients also exhibit enlarged PVS and altered free water content, which may normalize after surgical intervention (Lin et al., 2024). Some studies, however, report decreased ALPS indices postoperatively alongside cognitive decline, suggesting that structural improvements do not always translate to better glymphatic function (Roy et al., 2025). Overall, these findings support a mechanistic link between sleep disorders and reduced brain clearance, although the contribution of non-glymphatic vascular or metabolic factors also requires consideration.

Substance use disorders

Substance use, including alcohol, sedatives, stimulants, cannabis, and opioids, can disrupt glymphatic function through glial activation, vascular dysfunction, and altered AQP4 expression. These effects can contribute to neurotoxicity and cognitive decline.

Neuroimaging studies of alcohol-related brain injury report enlarged PVS in the frontal cortex and basal ganglia, correlating with elevated lipid levels and suggest impaired clearance (H. Liu et al., 2024). Lower ALPS indices are also observed in alcohol use disorder, with values linked to poorer cognitive performance (Dai et al., 2024). Animal models confirm that chronic alcohol exposure induces reactive astrogliosis, disrupts AQP4 localization, and reduces glymphatic transport (Lundgaard et al., 2018), while acute alcohol intake reduces vascular pulsatility (Q. Liu et al., 2020).

Other substances may affect glymphatic function through different mechanisms. In benzodiazepine use disorder, enlarged choroid plexus volume correlates with lower ALPS indices (Y. Zhou et al., 2024). Recreational marijuana use shows consistent ALPS changes, but lower indices are associated with earlier onset, reduced working memory, and lower fluid intelligence (Andica et al., 2024). Methamphetamine dependence significantly reduces ALPS values, especially in the right hemisphere, and is associated with impaired cerebral blood flow and BBB integrity (Cheng et al., 2025). In heroin-dependent individuals receiving methadone maintenance, ALPS indices increase compared to healthy controls, possibly reflecting compensatory responses to prior opioid-induced injury (L. Wang et al., 2023).

Preclinical studies further demonstrate that cocaine impairs glymphatic function through astrogliosis, reduced vascular pulsatility, and loss of AQP4 polarity (W. Chen et al., 2020), while ketamine has been linked to cognitive deficits via downregulation of AQP4 expression and inhibition of glymphatic circulation (X. Wu et al., 2024).

Shared mechanistic pathways in psychiatric glymphatic dysfunction

Across psychiatric disorders, several biological mechanisms appear to converge in disrupting glymphatic function (shown in Table 2): (i) sleep disruption reduces the interstitial expansion that occurs during slow-wave sleep, limiting glymphatic clearance; (ii) neuroimmune activation triggers astrocytic reactivity, alters cytokine profiles, and promotes perivascular inflammation; and (iii) AQP4 mislocalization from perivascular endfeet to other astrocytic domains reduces water flux into the interstitium, impairing both inflow and clearance.

MRI-based methods

MRI has become an essential tool for investigating glymphatic function, allowing whole-brain assessment of CSF dynamics, tracer movement, and fluid exchange pathways with high spatial and temporal resolution. MRI provides the advantage of avoiding ionizing radiation, making it safer for longitudinal monitoring of CSF dynamics and glymphatic function across both human and animal models.

However, MRI-based methods primarily provide indirect evidence of CSF–ISF exchange. They rely on proxies such as diffusion metrics, fluid motion, structural features, or physiological coupling patterns, rather than direct visualization. Interpretation therefore requires caution, as these measures may be influenced by unrelated processes such as inflammation, edema, vascular alterations, or atrophy.

Despite these challenges, advanced MRI techniques are advancing our understanding of the glymphatic system and its role in brain health and disease.

Diffusion MRI

Diffusion MRI, particularly diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), non-invasively estimates water diffusivity along the PVS. The ALPS index compares diffusivity parallel versus perpendicular to the PVS, where a high ALPS index is interpreted as reflecting efficient clearance of waste solutes via the glymphatic system (Taoka et al., 2017). It has been used in studies of depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and PTSD, frequently showing reduced perivascular diffusion consistent with impaired glymphatic activity. However, ALPS indices are affected by changes in radial diffusivity and white matter integrity (Schilling et al., 2025; Wright et al., 2024), raising concerns about specificity.

Low-b-value diffusion protocols, especially when combined with cardiac gating or phase averaging, improve the characterization of fluid movement within the PVS (Naganawa et al., 2024; Taoka et al., 2017). Unlike conventional DTI acquisitions, which are optimized for probing water diffusion in tissue microstructure, low-b-value approaches better capture the incoherent motion of free or loosely bound water, including the fast-moving components of CSF and ISF (Boyd et al., 2024). Cardiac gating further refines these measurements by synchronizing data acquisition with the cardiac cycle, reducing motion-related variability and enabling detection of subtle, pulsation-driven fluid displacements. Phase averaging reduces physiological noise, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio for slow oscillatory water motion. Together, these refinements allow better isolation of the vascular-pulsatile and respiration-coupled components of perivascular fluid transport. This may increase sensitivity to early or subtle glymphatic alterations in psychiatric populations that may be undetectable using standard diffusion imaging techniques.

Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI

DCE-MRI assesses glymphatic transport by tracking contrast distribution over time. In preclinical studies, intrathecal injection of gadolinium-based agents revealed macroscopic CSF movement aligned with arterial input and dependent on AQP4 (Iliff et al., 2013a; L. Yang et al., 2013). In humans, intrathecal DCE-MRI demonstrated age-related reductions in tracer influx and slower clearance in regions such as the basal ganglia and medial temporal lobe (Ringstad et al., 2018). However, large-molecule contrast agents do not fully mimic the transport of physiological solutes, and the invasive nature of intrathecal administration limits clinical application.

To address these limitations, intravenous DCE-MRI has been explored. Although only a small fraction of contrast enters the CSF, delayed imaging detects enhancement in ventricular spaces and the PVS (Deike-Hofmann et al., 2019; Richmond et al., 2023). For example, PVS enhancement up to 4 h following injection has been reported, including in patients with renal impairment (Naganawa et al., 2017). While less robust than intrathecal delivery, intravenous DCE-MRI is less invasive and may be more feasible for longitudinal or interventional studies in psychiatric populations.

Resting-state fMRI: global BOLD–CSF coupling

Resting-state fMRI assesses glymphatic-related activity by examining the temporal correlation between global low-frequency BOLD fluctuations and CSF signal changes, usually in the ventricles or basal cisterns. This coupling reflects neurovascular–CSF interactions, particularly during sleep or quiet rest (Fultz et al., 2019). Reduced coupling has been linked to cognitive decline (Han et al., 2021), disrupted sleep (Zhao et al., 2025), and inflammation, features common in psychiatric disorders. In depression, schizophrenia, and GAD, altered coupling patterns correlate with symptom severity and cognitive deficits, making it a promising non-invasive biomarker.

Phase-contrast MRI

Phase-contrast MRI (PC-MRI) quantifies fluid velocity by encoding motion-related phase shifts in the MR signal. While traditionally used to evaluate vascular flow, PC-MRI is now widely used to assess CSF flow velocity and volume in the cerebral aqueduct, ventricles, and subarachnoid space (Yoshida et al., 2009). Time-resolved evaluation of CSF flow shows that physiological oscillations, particularly cardiac and respiratory cycles (Yildiz et al., 2017), drive CSF movement and are believed to facilitate solute transport and waste clearance within the CNS.

PC-MRI acquisition methods range from 2D single-slice imaging to advanced 4D flow MRI (Rivera-Rivera et al., 2024), with gated sequences enabling quantification of CSF stroke volume, peak velocity, and net flow. These parameters demonstrated altered CSF dynamics in psychiatric conditions, including wider systolic pulses and greater diastolic flow in treatment-resistant depression (Naish et al., 2006), as well as reduced peak velocity and forward volume in schizophrenia (Kartalcı et al., 2021), findings consistent with impaired glymphatic transport.

In addition, PC-MRI has been used to investigate how physiological factors such as body posture (Muccio et al., 2021) and sleep states (Alperin and Burman, 2024) influence CSF flow, indirectly reflecting changes in glymphatic efficiency. When combined with structural MRI, PC-MRI localizes CSF flow abnormalities related to glymphatic dysfunction in both neurodegenerative and psychiatric conditions.

PET-based imaging approaches

PET tracks radiolabeled tracer kinetics with high sensitivity, allowing real-time assessment of solute transport and clearance in CSF and brain parenchyma. Unlike MRI, which often uses structural or diffusion-based proxies, PET can directly monitor radiolabeled tracer kinetics or detect molecular targets such as neuroinflammation and protein aggregation. Interpretation, however, must consider tracer-specific uptake and metabolism.

Dynamic PET for CSF clearance kinetics

Small-molecule tracers such as [¹¹C]-butanol have been applied to study solute exchange across ventricular and perivascular boundaries. Although not a direct measure of glymphatic flow, its small size and high lipid solubility enable rapid diffusion across membranes, making it useful for modeling passive transport and clearance dynamics (Mehta et al., 2024).

Extending beyond non-specific tracers, dynamic Aβ and tau PET has also been used to probe CSF clearance. Studies with [¹⁸F]-THK5117, a tracer for tau pathology, and [¹¹C]-Pittsburgh compound B ([¹¹C]-PiB) showed reduced ventricular clearance linked to amyloid burden and impaired kinetics across Alzheimer’s disease (Leon et al., 2017; Schubert et al., 2019).

More recently, dynamic [¹⁸F]-FDG PET, a tool traditionally used for measuring glucose metabolism, has been adapted to assess ventricular CSF clearance. In this approach, early-phase time–activity curves from ventricular CSF regions are modeled to estimate the efflux rate constant, which reflects how quickly FDG clears from the CSF compartment (Zhou et al., 2025). Because FDG also undergoes tissue uptake via glucose transporters and is intracellularly trapped, it is essential to account for tissue uptake in interpreting CSF tracer clearance metrics. Despite this complexity, [¹⁸F]-FDG PET provides an accessible and clinically available tool to probe glymphatic-related ventricular CSF clearance, particularly when combined with structural MRI to control for ventricular anatomy and periventricular metabolism.

PET tracers targeting neuroinflammation and protein aggregation

PET tracers developed to target neuroinflammation provide an additional perspective on glymphatic function. The most widely used include ligands for 18-kDa translocator protein (TSPO; e.g. [¹¹C]PK11195, [¹⁸F]DPA-714), as well as more selective probes for astrocytic and microglial activity such as monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B; [¹¹C]SL25.1188), cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1; [¹¹C]PS13), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; [¹¹C]MC1) (Meyer et al., 2020). These tracers visualize cellular activation states rather than solute flow, yet the glymphatic–meningeal lymphatic network is a principal route for cytokine and immune-cell trafficking (Jain et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2023). Elevated or persistent inflammatory signals on PET may therefore serve as an indirect of impaired clearance or barrier dysfunction at perivascular interfaces.

Although traditionally applied to neurodegeneration, protein aggregation tracers such as Aβ and tau PET ([¹¹C]-PiB, [¹⁸F]-THK series, [¹⁸F]-flortaucipir) highlight how impaired clearance contributes to psychiatric comorbidity risk. While these scans quantify deposition rather than CSF–ISF exchange, the glymphatic system is central to removing extracellular Aβ and soluble tau. Abnormal retention or propagation patterns observed with these tracers can thus provide indirect evidence of reduced glymphatic efficiency, especially in disorders where psychiatric and cognitive symptoms overlap with neurodegeneration (Huang et al., 2024; Kamagata et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2025).

Other imaging methods

Beyond PET and MRI, several imaging-based approaches provide additional insight into glymphatic dynamics, primarily in preclinical models.

Optical imaging

Two-photon microscopy offers micron-scale spatial resolution for visualizing CSF–ISF exchange, tracer kinetics, and AQP4-dependent water transport in rodent models (Bèchet et al., 2020; Iliff et al., 2013b). This approach was central to the foundational observations of periarterial influx, parenchymal mixing, and perivenous efflux. It also allows real-time tracking of solute movement during different physiological states such as sleep versus wakefulness. Despite its unmatched resolution, optical imaging requires invasive cranial windows and fluorescent tracer administration, which preclude application in humans. Its main role remains in preclinical mechanistic studies, where it guides hypotheses for translational imaging in psychiatric disorders.

Near-infrared spectroscopy

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) measures changes in light absorption related to hemoglobin oxygenation, offering a non-invasive means to monitor superficial cortical hemodynamics and, indirectly, associated fluid shifts. In neonates and infants, where skull thickness is minimal, NIRS has been applied to study sleep-associated vascular and fluid dynamics, potentially linked to glymphatic activity (Pierro et al., 2012; Pizza et al., 2010; Yoon et al., 2025). Its limited penetration depth (∼1–2 cm) and low spatial specificity restrict its utility in adult populations. However, portable NIRS devices may allow longitudinal monitoring of sleep–wake physiology in psychiatric patients, complementing other measures of glymphatic function.

Ultrasound-induced modulation

Low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) modulates vascular pulsatility and transiently alters BBB permeability, especially when paired with intravenously administered microbubbles. In rodent models, such stimulation has enhanced the influx and clearance of tracers through glymphatic pathways, potentially by amplifying perivascular fluid flow(Hsiao et al., 2025; C. Wu et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025). While primarily explored for drug delivery, LIFU raises the possibility of therapeutic modulation of glymphatic transport in diseases with impaired clearance, including psychiatric disorders with suspected neuroinflammatory components. Translation to humans will require careful safety evaluation, particularly regarding repeated exposures and off-target effects.

Non-imaging methods

Several non-imaging strategies provide complementary or indirect assessments of glymphatic function.

CSF biomarkers

Serial lumbar punctures or Ommaya reservoir sampling enable temporal tracking of solute concentrations such as Aβ, tau, lactate, or inflammatory mediators, serving as biochemical surrogates of clearance efficiency (Chong et al., 2022). For example, delayed reduction in CSF Aβ after sleep deprivation or systemic inflammation may indicate impaired glymphatic clearance. While highly specific to CSF composition, such sampling provides only localized and temporally discrete data, limiting inference about whole-brain transport.

CSF-to-blood clearance assays

These approaches quantify how rapidly intrathecally administered tracers (e.g. radiolabeled albumin or gadolinium chelates) appear in the peripheral blood (Hovd et al., 2022). Faster appearance suggests more efficient clearance from the CSF compartment into systemic circulation. Although conceptually straightforward, these assays require invasive administration, carry procedural risk, and provide limited anatomical resolution, making them less suitable for large-scale psychiatric research. They may, however, be valuable in proof-of-concept studies or as adjuncts to imaging-based methods.

Electrophysiological proxies

Electroencephalogram (EEG) slow-wave activity is linked with greater CSF–ISF exchange in both animal and human studies, probably through its association with reduced norepinephrine tone, increased interstitial space volume, and enhanced perivascular flow (Hablitz et al., 2019, 2020; Jiang-Xie et al., 2024). In psychiatric populations, where sleep architecture is frequently altered, reduced slow-wave activity could represent a physiological correlate of impaired glymphatic function. However, EEG remains an indirect measure and cannot localize clearance deficits. Combining electrophysiological data with imaging or biomarker assessments can strengthen inferences about the role of sleep physiology in glymphatic regulation.

Low-b-value diffusion MRI

Sensitive to slow, incoherent water movement and bulk CSF flow, this approach provides a promising surrogate for glymphatic transport within perivascular and ventricular compartments. It is fully compatible with standard clinical MRI protocols and scalable for large studies. If existing diffusion datasets include low-b shells (b ≤ 500 s/mm²), retrospective analysis is feasible; although optimal results require acquisition with specific parameters such as cardiac gating or phase averaging. While it remains an indirect measure and may be influenced by vascular and inflammatory factors, it is particularly valuable for studies examining sleep, vascular risk, or metabolic health in relation to psychiatric symptoms.

Resting-state fMRI coupling analyses

Metrics such as global BOLD–CSF coupling provide indirect but physiologically meaningful indices of CSF pulsatility, which are linked to sleep-dependent clearance. Because rs-fMRI is already widely collected in psychiatric cohorts, previously acquired datasets, if they include sufficient scan duration (≥ 8–10 min) and appropriate preprocessing, can be reanalyzed for BOLD–CSF coupling. Emerging studies in depression and schizophrenia have reported associations with symptom severity and sleep disturbances. While temporal resolution limits mechanistic interpretation, the method’s availability and reproducibility make it highly suitable for longitudinal or developmental work.

Structural MRI with PVS quantification

PVS enlargement on T2-weighted MRI is linked to impaired clearance, neuroinflammation, and vascular pathology—key contributors to psychiatric morbidity. This measure can be extracted from standard clinical or research T2-weighted scans, allowing retrospective analysis in existing datasets. Although PVS burden is not specific to glymphatic dysfunction, it provides a non-invasive anatomical marker that is increasingly relevant for psychiatric phenotyping, especially in aging, cognitive impairment, or neuroinflammation-focused studies.

Dynamic PET imaging

Tracers such as [¹⁸F]-FDG have been applied to assess CSF efflux from the ventricular system, which may serve as an indirect indicator of glymphatic clearance. PET can also characterize associated neuroinflammatory and metabolic processes. While full dynamic framing from injection onward provides the richest data, prior work has demonstrated that clearance estimation is still feasible using the slope from a 30–60 min post-injection window (Zhou et al., 2025), making it possible to adapt certain existing PET datasets for retrospective analysis if these frames are available or listmode data were stored. Because many legacy scans omit either early or late dynamic frames, most high-quality glymphatic PET studies will still require new acquisitions optimized for clearance modeling. Although cost and radiation exposure limit routine use, dynamic PET is particularly valuable for mechanistic or treatment–response studies in targeted psychiatric populations.

Clinical translation and therapeutic implications

Growing evidence linking glymphatic dysfunction to psychiatric disorders suggests that glymphatic imaging could become an important component of clinical assessment. Although these approaches remain in the early stages of development, non-invasive techniques such as DTI-ALPS, rs-fMRI-derived BOLD–CSF coupling, and dynamic PET-based CSF clearance measurements show potential as physiological biomarkers of impaired brain waste removal. By capturing fluid transport dynamics not addressed by conventional neurochemical or structural assessments, these markers may help define physiologically distinct subtypes, stratify risk for progression, and provide tools for monitoring treatment response. This may be particularly valuable in psychiatric conditions where neuroinflammation, sleep disruption, or cognitive decline are prominent.

As reviewed in Section "Pharmacological modulation of glymphatic transport", preclinical studies and early human data suggest that glymphatic transport can be influenced by specific psychotropic and anti-inflammatory interventions. The most direct human evidence comes from SSRI trials showing reduced CSF Aβ concentrations, while agents such as melatonin and omega-3 PUFAs have shown glymphatic benefits in animal models (Y. Chen et al., 2025; X. Liu et al., 2020; Sheline et al., 2020). These examples highlight the potential for repurposing existing medications, and for developing novel agents targeting astrocytic AQP4 polarity, vascular function, or neuroimmune signaling.

Beyond pharmacology, strategies that promote restorative sleep, stabilize circadian rhythms, or modulate noradrenergic tone can enhance glymphatic function. In animal models, lowering norepinephrine during sleep or sedation increases interstitial space volume and accelerates solute clearance (Xie et al., 2013). Clinically, this suggests opportunities for integrating sleep-focused behavioral therapies, chronotherapy, or selected sedatives into treatment strategies, alongside management of vascular and metabolic risk factors.

Advancing our understanding of glymphatic physiology in mental illness may shift psychiatric care toward a model integrating metabolic, vascular, and immune perspectives into a unified framework for brain health. By combining mechanistic imaging biomarkers with targeted therapeutic approaches, future clinical strategies could more precisely identify, monitor, and treat the physiological underpinnings of psychiatric disorders.

Major gaps and future directions

Research on glymphatic function in psychiatric disorders is gaining momentum, but remains in its early stages. Much of the current evidence is indirect, and key questions remain unanswered (Fig. 4).

One major priority is to develop more direct and validated biomarkers of glymphatic function. Current methods such as low-b-value diffusion MRI, BOLD–CSF coupling, and PET-based clearance modeling capture correlates of fluid transport but lack specification and consistency for clinical translation. Critically, most MRI-derived metrics are region-agnostic: they infer CSF bulk flow or water diffusively along deep perivascular pathways and cannot directly quantify exchange within specific nuclei, cortical layers, or deep gray structures. Limitations in spatial resolution and modeling assumptions further confound attribution at the scale of penetrating arterioles and micro-PVS. Future work should prioritize regionally resolved assays, harmonize acquisition or analysis pipelines, physiological monitoring, and cross-site calibration to improve reliability and enable clinical translation.

Another critical gap involves understanding the direction of observed associations. Glymphatic dysfunction has been linked to sleep disruption, neuroinflammation, and cognitive deficits across several psychiatric conditions. However, it remains unclear whether impaired clearance contributes to these disorders or represents a downstream effect. Longitudinal and interventional studies, such as testing whether modulating noradrenergic tone improves both clearance and symptoms, are needed to determine whether restoring glymphatic function can yield meaningful clinical benefits.

A further challenge is to identify which aspects of glymphatic impairment are shared across psychiatric disorders and which are condition-specific. Features such as astrocytic dysfunction, AQP4 mislocalization, and chronic immune activation are common in preclinical models, but their relative importance in humans and in specific diagnoses remains uncertain. Large, multimodal studies with deep clinical phenotyping may clarify mechanistic subtypes that span traditional diagnostic categories.

The developmental trajectory of the glymphatic system is another area that remains largely unexplored. Many psychiatric disorders emerge early in life, yet little is known about how fluid clearance systems mature or how early disruptions might influence long-term risk. Understanding these patterns could inform early detection and prevention strategies.

There is also growing interest in therapeutic strategies targeting the glymphatic system. Preclinical studies show that agents such as melatonin and omega-3 PUFAs can improve glymphatic transport, and human studies of SSRIs suggest enhanced solute clearance via CSF sampling, although direct imaging has yet to be conducted. Interventions that promote sleep quality and circadian rhythm integrity, support metabolic health, or reduce inflammation, particularly with approaches that normalize astrocytic and vascular function, remain promising but are largely untested in psychiatric populations. Future treatment studies should incorporate glymphatic imaging as an outcome measure to assess whether such interventions improve both brain clearance and psychiatric symptoms.

Recent AI methods are beginning to dismantle long-standing bottlenecks in glymphatic imaging. Notably, F. Chen et al. (2025) applied a deep learning model for highly accurate, automated segmentation of enlarged PVS alongside DTI–ALPS analysis—showing reliable quantification of glymphatic impairment in Alzheimer’s disease (Dice > 0.90). Fully automated DTI–ALPS pipelines have also been validated for inter-observer reliability, achieving near-perfect reproducibility (ICC > 0.99) in clinical datasets (Oura et al., 2025). These AI advances enable large-scale retrospective analyses that would have previously been impractical. Embedding such tools in multimodal pipelines could greatly accelerate psychiatric glymphatic research and trial-ready biomarker development.

Finally, methodological consistency and cross-disciplinary collaboration remain essential. Many current studies suffer from small sample sizes, variable imaging methods, and limited clinical characterization. Coordinated efforts using hybrid PET/MRI systems, harmonized analysis pipelines, and integration of behavioral, biological, and imaging data are critical for building a robust evidence base.
