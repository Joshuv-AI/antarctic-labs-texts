Magnetic continuum soft robot-driven precise delivery of prodrug nanoassemblies for gastric cancer chemo-immunotherapy
Authors: Yuequan Wang, Hao Ye, Denis von Arx, Yukang Li, Yifan Wang, Alexandre Mesot, Carlos Franco, Xiang-Zhong Chen, Yuting Wang, Shenwu Zhang, Salvador Pané, Meng Niu, Bradley J. Nelson, Cong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Local precise drug delivery is conducive to improving therapeutic efficacy and minimizing off-target toxicity. Current local delivery approaches are focused mostly on superficial or postoperative tumor lesions, due to the challenges posed by the inaccessibility of deep-seated tumors. Herein, we report a magnetic continuum soft robot capable of non-invasive and site-specific delivery of prodrug nanoassemblies-loaded hydrogel. The nanoassemblies are co-assembled from redox-responsive docetaxel prodrug and oxaliplatin prodrug, and subsequently embedded into a hydrogel matrix. The hydrogel precursor and crosslinker are synchronously delivered using the soft robot under magnetic guidance and in situ crosslinked at the gastric cancer lesions, forming a drug depot for sustained release and long-lasting treatment. As the hydrogel gradually degrades, the nanoassemblies are internalized by tumor cells. The redox response ability enables them to be selectively activated within tumor cells to trigger the release of docetaxel and oxaliplatin, exerting a synergistic anti-tumor effect. We find that the combination effectively induces immunogenic cell death of gastric tumor, enhancing antitumor immune responses. This strategy offers an intelligent and controllable integration platform for precise drug delivery and combined chemo-immunotherapy.

Introduction

Localized drug delivery offers the advantage of concentrating therapeutics at tumor sites, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity by reducing overall drug exposure in circulation [[1], [2], [3]]. This unique approach of drug delivery not only maximizes drug bioavailability at the tumor but also mitigates severe toxic side effects commonly encountered in systemic chemotherapy [4,5]. Such a drug delivery modality is expected to not only exert potent anticancer outcomes but also improve the life quality of patients with cancers in the clinical practice. Despite being proposed over six decades ago, however, the clinical translation and application of localized anticancer drug delivery have not been an easy path and has found limited clinical application. Even to this day, this therapy is only applicable for the treatment of superficial tumors or as adjunctive therapy following surgical resection, such as head and neck cancer, breast cancer, and skin cancer [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Currently, achieving precise drug delivery and treatment for digestive tract tumors remains a clinical challenge, including gastrointestinal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and related peritoneal metastases. Therefore, it is a major clinical need to develop minimally invasive intrabody delivery devices or drug-administration strategies. Such systems must overcome anatomical constraints and enable precise delivery of antineoplastic drugs into hard-to-reach tissue regions, on the premise of ensuring safety, controllability and compatibility with clinical procedures.

Localized drug administration is generally achieved through minimally invasive interventions that introduce therapeutics into the body’s natural conduits—such as vascular, gastrointestinal, or urinary tracts—using endoscopic or catheter-based delivery systems [10]. However, conventional devices employed clinically are often composed of relatively rigid tendon-like or coaxial tube structures designed to ensure the transmission of mechanical force and torque from the operator’s hand to the distal tip [11]. While this design supports effective manipulation over short distances, it compromises maneuverability and safety when navigating tortuous or narrow anatomical pathways, such as those encountered in many deep-seated organs and tissues. Furthermore, these stiff instruments pose a significant risk of mechanical injury to fragile surrounding tissues during insertion or operation, limiting their applicability in precise localized delivery [12,13]. To overcome these inherent limitations, continuum soft robots constructed from flexible, biocompatible materials have emerged as a transformative technology [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. These robots exhibit compliance and adaptability that better match the mechanical properties of biological tissues, significantly reducing the risks of trauma. Leveraging advanced magnetic actuation strategies, external magnetic fields can remotely control embedded micro-magnets within the robot, enabling highly precise and responsive navigation even through complex and convoluted channels [19]. Rational design of magnetic continuum soft robots not only facilitates deep access to otherwise unreachable tumor sites but also enables minimally invasive and accurate drug delivery, thus representing a promising platform for next-generation localized cancer therapies.

Another major challenge commonly encountered in localized tumor therapy lies in how to maintain long retention and sustained drug release at the target sites [[20], [21], [22]]. Traditional intraluminal drug delivery often suffers from rapid clearance, resulting in low and uncontrollable drug accumulation within tumors and consequently suboptimal efficacy and uncontrollable off-target toxicity [23,24]. Injectable hydrogels with intrinsic adhesiveness provide a promising solution by forming in situ depots that enable long-term retention and sustained release of anticancer drugs [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. In particular, in situ crosslinkable hydrogels can be administered via injection or catheter as precursors, which polymerize within the body under enzymatic, photothermal, thermal or chemical stimuli to form three-dimensional gel networks [[29], [30], [31]]. Such a design is especially suitable for precise intratumoral drug delivery. Despite the high therapeutic potential of localized delivery, conventional chemotherapy still carries the risk of damaging adjacent healthy tissues. In recent decades, a variety of stimuli-responsive prodrugs of anticancer agents have been developed that are able to selectively release active drugs within the tumor microenvironment characterized by low pH, high redox gradients, and overexpressed enzymes [32,33]. Moreover, increasing evidence shows that certain chemotherapeutic drugs effectively induce immunogenic cell death, thereby activating antitumor immunity and reversing immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments [34,35]. This important discovery has established a bridge between traditional chemotherapy and emerging immunotherapy, leading to the emergence of a promising chemo-immunotherapy. Chemo-immunotherapy has shown remarkable clinical promise, which is expected to improve progression-free and overall survival across various cancers by eliciting durable, tumor-specific immune memory that prevents metastasis and relapse [[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. More importantly, this provides a solution to the problem that local medication is ineffective in dealing with tumor metastasis.

In this study, we presented a minimally invasive integration strategy for precise intratumoral delivery and localized chemo-immunotherapy using a magnetically actuated continuum soft robot (Fig. 1). First, we designed and synthesized a disulfide bond-bridged lipophilic docetaxel prodrug (DSSO) and a redox-activatable tetra-valent oxaliplatin prodrug (PtD). Docetaxel and oxaliplatin represent a clinically validated chemotherapeutic combination for the treatment of gastric cancer. DSSO and PtD could co-assemble into uniform and stable nanoparticles through a simple one-step nano-precipitation method. The hybrid prodrug nanoassemblies were subsequently loaded into an injectable and in situ crosslinkable hydrogel matrix. The hydrogel precursor solution, together with the crosslinker and prodrug nanoassemblies, was synchronously and accurately delivered to the tumor site by soft robotic devices under external magnetic guidance. Upon reaching the target and mixing these components, in situ gelation formed a localized drug depot, which enabled prolonged retention of the nanoassemblies. As the hydrogel gradually degraded, prodrug nanoassemblies were internalized by tumor cells and activated in the presence of high intracellular redox stimuli, triggering release of docetaxel and oxaliplatin. This not only amplified local synergistic chemotherapy but also efficiently induced immunogenic cell death (ICD). Finally, it exerted robust antitumor responses in a tumor-bearing mouse model and rabbit gastric cancer model with good safety. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to develop an integrated therapeutic system by integrating magnetic continuum soft robot with prodrug nanoassembly technology, which overcomes the limitations of conventional delivery tools and offers a powerful platform for non-invasive, site-specific chemoimmunotherapy, with particular promise for the treatment of deep-seated and surgically inaccessible tumors.Fig. 1Magnetic continuum soft robots-driven precise delivery of prodrug nanoassemblies for gastric cancer chemoimmunotherapy. Materials and methods

Materials

Docetaxel, oxaliplatin, palmitic acid, decanol and 2,2′-disulfanediyldiacetic acid were purchased from Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China). RPMI-1640 cell culture medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). DSPE-MPEG₂K was supplied by AVT (Shanghai) Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Hoechst 33342 and MTT were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). TNF-α and IFN-γ ELISA kits were purchased from NeoBioscience Technology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Anti-CD11c, anti-CD80, anti-CD86, anti-CD8, anti-CD3 and anti-CD4 antibodies were acquired from Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The HMGB1 ELISA kit was purchased from Shanghai Jianglai Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Glass-bottom cell culture dishes were obtained from Wuxi NEST Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuxi, China).

Synthesis of DSSP

DSSP was prepared via a three-step esterification. First, palmitic acid (1 mmol) was esterified with ethylene glycol (20 ml) in the presence of p-toluenesulfonic acid (TsOH, 0.2 mmol) at 110 °C under nitrogen. Palmitic acid, dissolved in methylbenzene (5 ml), was added dropwise, and the reaction proceeded for 2 h. The mixture was extracted with methylbenzene, washed with saturated NaHCO3, evaporated, and purified by silica gel chromatography (n-hexane/ethyl acetate = 20:1, 0.1% acetic acid) to yield palmitic acid glycol ester. Second, palmitic acid glycol ester (1 mmol) was coupled with 2,2′-disulfanediyldiacetic acid (1 mmol) in DCM (10 ml) using DMAP (0.1 mmol) as a catalyst at room temperature for 12 h under nitrogen. The product was purified by silica gel chromatography (cyclohexane/acetone = 15:1). Finally, the intermediate (1 mmol) was activated with EDCI (2 mmol), HOBt (2 mmol) and DMAP (0.2 mmol) in DCM (10 ml) under ice-bath conditions for 1 h, followed by the addition of docetaxel (1 mmol) and stirring for 48 h at room temperature. The solvent was removed, and the crude product was purified by preparative liquid chromatography (100% acetonitrile) to obtain DSSP. The structure was confirmed by FT-ICR-MS (Bruker) and 1H NMR (400 MHz, Bruker).

Synthesis of PtD

Oxaliplatin (1 mmol) was dissolved in H2O (10 ml) at 50 °C, followed by the addition of 30% H2O2 (1 ml) and stirring for 3 h. The solution was cooled to 4 °C overnight to recrystallize Pt (IV)-2OH, which was collected, washed with ice-cold ethanol and diethyl ether, and vacuum-dried to yield a white powder. Pt (IV)-2OH (1 mmol) was dispersed in DMF (20 ml) at 35 °C, and a DMF solution of succinic anhydride (2 mmol) and DMAP (0.2 mmol) was added. After 12 h of stirring, EDCI (2 mmol) was introduced to activate the carboxyl groups for 20 min, followed by the addition of decanol (4 mmol) and stirring overnight. The mixture was filtered, concentrated to ∼2 ml, and precipitated in diethyl ether (50 ml). The precipitate was collected, washed and vacuum-dried. The crude product was purified by preparative liquid chromatography (acetonitrile: H2O = 80:20) to yield PtD, whose structure was confirmed by FT-ICR-MS (Bruker) and 1H NMR (400 MHz, Bruker).

Fabrication and characterization of binary prodrug nanoassemblies

PEGylated binary prodrug nanoassemblies (D/P NAs) were prepared as follows: PtD (0.74 mg), DSSP (0.26 mg) and DSPE-MPEG2k (0.25 mg) were completely dissolved in absolute ethanol and then injected into 2 ml deionized water under stirring. Ethanol was subsequently removed by rotary evaporation at 30 °C, yielding PEGylated binary nanoassemblies at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml.

Single-component nanoassemblies were fabricated using the same procedure. Specifically, PtD NAs were obtained by dissolving PtD (1.0 mg) and DSPE-MPEG2k (0.25 mg) in absolute ethanol, followed by injection into 2 ml deionized water and ethanol removal under the same conditions. Similarly, DSSP NAs were prepared by dissolving DSSP (1.0 mg) with DSPE-MPEG2k (0.25 mg) in ethanol, injecting into water, and removing ethanol by rotary evaporation at 30 °C.

For in vivo fluorescence tracking, DiR-labeled prodrug nanoassemblies were prepared by dissolving DiR (0.1 mg), PtD (0.74 mg), DSSP (0.26 mg) and DSPE-MPEG2k (0.25 mg) in ethanol, followed by slow dropwise addition into deionized water with stirring. Ethanol was removed under the same conditions as above to obtain the labeled formulations.

The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential of all nanoassemblies were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK). Morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Japan). TEM specimens were prepared by dropping the nanoassemblies onto a 300-mesh carbon-coated copper grid, air-drying at room temperature, and staining with 2% phosphotungstic acid prior to imaging.

Colloidal stability

The in vitro stability of binary nanoassemblies in aqueous environments was evaluated by tracking temporal changes in particle size. In brief, 1 ml PEGylated binary nanoassemblies was added to 20 ml either phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) or PBS supplemented with 10% FBS to mimic physiological and serum-containing conditions. The dispersions were incubated at 37 °C under gentle shaking. At specific time intervals, samples were withdrawn and the hydrodynamic diameters of the nanoassemblies were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK). Changes in particle size over time were used to assess the colloidal stability of the formulations.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking studies were performed to investigate the intermolecular interactions between DSSP and PtD. Initially, two prodrug monomer structures were built using GaussView 5 software. Each structure was subsequently optimized at the HF/6–31G* level using the Gaussian 09 program to obtain energetically stable conformations. These optimized geometries were then subjected to molecular docking simulations individually using AutoDock. The resulting docking calculations provided the binding energies and allowed evaluation of the potential interactions between the prodrug molecules.

Drug release profiles

The release behavior of DSSP and PtD prodrugs in the presence or absence of DTT was investigated using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Briefly, 200 nM of each prodrug was added to 30 ml PBS containing 30% anhydrous ethanol, with different concentrations of DTT. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking. At predetermined time points, 0.5 ml samples were collected and analyzed by HPLC to determine the amount of parent drug released from the prodrugs. The HPLC analysis was performed following the method previously established in our group’s studies [[43], [44], [45]].

Formation of NAs-loaded hydrogel in vitro

NAs-loaded hydrogel (NAs@Gel) was prepared by mixing the NAs with a sodium alginate (SA) solution, followed by stirring at room temperature until a homogeneous solution was obtained. To mimic in vivo gelation, Ca²⁺ (20 mM) was added to the NAs@SA solution in a glass vial, which immediately induced gelation. The injectability of the NAs@SA solution was evaluated by loading it into a syringe and injecting it into a Ca²⁺ solution, resulting in the formation of line-shaped hydrogel structures.

The microstructure of the hydrogels was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) after freeze-drying the gels overnight. Additionally, the rheological properties were characterized using a rheometer, measuring the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) over a range of strain values to assess gel strength and viscoelastic behavior.

Retention performance of NAs@Gel in vivo

In vivo retention of NAs@Gel was evaluated using BALB/c mice. Mice were subcutaneously injected with either D/P/DiR NAs@Gel or DiR solution. The fluorescence signal of DiR was monitored at various time points using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS), with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 740 nm and 790 nm, respectively. This approach allowed the assessment of the hydrogel’s ability to sustain localized retention of the nanoassemblies compared to the free dye solution.

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxic effects of NAs on murine gastric cancer MFC cells were determined using the MTT assay. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³ cells/well and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 12 h. After removing the medium, fresh culture medium containing NAs was added, and cells were incubated for an additional 48 h. Subsequently, 25 µl MTT solution (5 mg/ml) was introduced into each well and incubated for 4 h. The medium was then replaced with 200 µl DMSO to dissolve the formazan crystals, followed by shaking for 10 min. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

CRT exposure

CRT surface exposure was evaluated to confirm immunogenic cell death in MFC cells via immunofluorescence and flow cytometry. For immunofluorescence analysis, MFC cells were seeded in glass bottom cell culture dishes and allowed to adhere for 12 h. Cells were then treated with various formulations (100 nM DSSP or 400 nM PtD) for 12 h, washed twice with PBS, and incubated with a primary anti-CRT antibody for 1 h, followed by FITC-conjugated secondary antibody staining for 30 min. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342, and fluorescence images were acquired using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

For quantitative analysis, MFC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated under the same conditions. Cells were collected, washed with PBS, and sequentially incubated with primary anti-CRT antibody and FITC-conjugated secondary antibody. After two final PBS washes, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the proportion of CRT-positive cells.

HMGB1 release and ATP secretion

HMGB1 secretion and ATP secretion from MFC cells were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit. Briefly, MFC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 12 h. Cells were then exposed to different formulations (100 nM DSSP or 400 nM PtD) for an additional 12 h. The culture supernatants were collected, and extracellular HMGB1 levels and ATP secretion were measured according to the ELISA kit protocol provided by the manufacturer.

DCs mature

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were obtained from mice and cultured for 7 d in the presence of IL-4 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). Mature BMDCs were then co-incubated for 12 h with MFC cells pre-treated with different formulations. After incubation, cells were collected, stained with anti-CD11c (FITC), anti-CD80 (PE) and anti-CD86 (APC), and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess maturation status.

Animals

Female BALB/c mice and Japanese white rabbits were obtained from the Animal Center of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University. All experimental procedures complied with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University and were approved by the university’s Animal Ethics Committee.

Antitumor efficacy

MFC tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were randomized into four treatment groups: PBS, DSSP NAs@Gel, PtD NAs@Gel and D/P NAs@Gel, once the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³. Formulations were administered via intratumoral injection, with NAs@SA and Ca²⁺ solution co-delivered at the injection site to induce in situ gelation. Drug doses were equivalent to 10 mg/kg oxaliplatin or 3.5 mg/kg docetaxel. Treatments were conducted four times at 4-d intervals. Tumor progression was monitored by measuring tumor dimensions and body weight every 2 d

In vivo immune responses

Following the final treatment cycle, tumors were excised for analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Briefly, excised tumor tissues were minced into small fragments and subjected to enzymatic digestion in 1 mg/ml collagenase I solution at 37 °C for 30 min in a shaking water bath. The digested tissues were mechanically dissociated using the rubber end of a syringe plunger, and the resulting cell suspension was passed through nylon mesh filters. Erythrocytes were removed by treatment with red blood cell lysis buffer, after which the remaining cells were washed and resuspended in PBS. For quantification of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells (CD3⁺ CD8⁺) and CD4⁺ T cells (CD3⁺ CD4⁺) within tumors, cells were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated anti-CD3-FITC and either anti-CD8-PerCP-Cy5.5 or anti-CD4-APC antibodies, following the manufacturer’s protocols. To assess DC maturation (CD11c⁺ CD86⁺ CD80⁺) in tumor-draining lymph nodes, the same tissue dissociation and preparation steps were applied. The resulting single-cell suspensions were stained with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD86-APC and anti-CD80-PE antibodies. All immune cell populations were analyzed via flow cytometry (FCM). For cytokine profiling, serum was collected from treated mice, diluted appropriately, and analyzed for TNF-α and IFN-γ levels using commercial ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Robotic magnetic navigation platform and magnetic endoscope

The robotic magnetic navigation system (MNS) employed in this study was derived from the Navion platform, incorporating three high-power-density, liquid-cooled electromagnetic coils to enable precise magnetic field control. The magnetic endoscope had an outer diameter of 4.25 mm and a total length of 1.25 m. Its distal portion consisted of an 85 mm long articulated segment embedded with permanent magnets, terminating in a tip equipped with a miniature camera (OVM6946, OmniVision), integrated illumination, a flushing channel for lens cleaning, and a working channel. In addition, a dual-channel magnetic catheter was embedded within the endoscope, allowing simultaneous delivery of the NAs@SA gel matrix and the CaCl₂ crosslinking solution, enabling in situ crosslinking at the catheter tip. Illumination was provided by acrylic optical fibers connected proximally to a medical white-light source (Visera Elite II OTV-S300, Olympus). The 1.2 mm-diameter working channel could also be utilized for gastric insufflation when required.

Magnetically guided gastric tumor treatment

An orthotopic VX2 rabbit gastric cancer model was established to evaluate the precision delivery of hydrogel via magnetic guidance. The VX2 tumors were surgically implanted into the gastric wall of rabbits, and tumor growth was monitored using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Approximately 12 d after implantation, the tumor volume reached ∼500 mm³, at which point animals were randomly divided into two groups: non-treatment control and treatment (n = 5). The success rate of model establishment was approximately 60%. Drug administration was performed twice at 4-d intervals. For the treatment group, a soft robotic endoscope under magnetic guidance was used to deliver the hydrogel for in situ crosslinking. Drug doses were equivalent to 10 mg/kg oxaliplatin or 3.5 mg/kg docetaxel. Prior to treatment, rabbits were fasted for 12 h to ensure gastric emptying. During the procedure, animals were anesthetized and positioned on the robotic magnetic navigation platform, with conventional X-ray imaging employed to provide additional visual feedback for tracking the location of the magnetic endoscope and catheter. The magnetically guided catheter was navigated to the tumor site, where D/P NAs@SA and CaCl₂ solution were co-injected to achieve in situ gel formation. After treatment, tumor tissues were collected for histological evaluation, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and TUNEL assays, to assess tumor apoptosis and necrosis.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) when appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structure identification of prodrugs

To reduce the non-specific toxicity of chemotherapeutic agents and enable selective activation within tumor cells, we synthesized two reduction-responsive prodrugs of docetaxel and oxaliplatin. As illustrated in Fig. S1, the docetaxel prodrug (DSSP) was obtained by conjugating palmitic acid to docetaxel via a disulfide-containing linker. This disulfide bond serves as a redox-sensitive moiety that can be cleaved in the tumor intracellular environment, which is characterized by elevated glutathione (GSH) levels, thereby enabling selective release of the active drug. In parallel, a reduction-activatable oxaliplatin prodrug (PtD) was synthesized by directly coupling decanol to the oxaliplatin (Fig. S2). Given the inherent reduction sensitivity of the Pt (IV) prodrug, this structural design ensures that PtD remains inert in circulation but can be reduced to its cytotoxic oxaliplatin form within tumor cells. The chemical structures of both DSSP and PtD were thoroughly confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy and MS, validating the successful synthesis of the targeted prodrugs (Figs. S3-S6).

Fabrication and characterization of binary prodrug nanoassemblies

Docetaxel and oxaliplatin are widely used in clinical combination regimens for the treatment of gastric cancer. However, in current practice, these agents are often administered sequentially rather than simultaneously to mitigate overlapping toxicities, which may inadvertently compromise their synergistic therapeutic potential. To address this limitation, a prodrug-based strategy was adopted to minimize the nonspecific systemic toxicity and enable synchronized co-delivery of both drugs for enhanced combination efficacy. By conjugating hydrophobic alkyl chains to each chemotherapeutic agent, the resulting prodrugs exhibited increased hydrophobicity and altered molecular geometry, which facilitated their spontaneous self-assembly into nanoassemblies in aqueous environments. Such structural modifications facilitated the formation of nanostructures without relying on additional carrier materials. Furthermore, PEGylation was used to stabilize the prodrug nanoassemblies within the hydrogel matrix for local intratumoral administration. We first evaluated the individual self-assembly capabilities of DSSP and PtD using a one-step nanoprecipitation method. As shown in Figs. 2A-2C and Table S1, both prodrugs successfully self-assembled into uniform nanoassemblies with average diameters of approximately 90 nm for DSSP and 160 nm for PtD, respectively. TEM confirmed their well-defined spherical morphology (Fig. S7). To realize synchronized co-delivery, a hybrid nanoassembly system composed of both DSSP and PtD was further constructed. The molar ratio of DSSP to PtD (1:4) was determined based on the clinically used dosage ratio. Interestingly, co-assembly of the two prodrugs yielded stable and homogeneous nanoassemblies, referred to as D/P NAs, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of around 120 nm (Fig. 2D). TEM analysis further revealed their uniform spherical nanostructure, similar to the single-drug assemblies (Fig. S7). Zeta potential measurements indicated that all three of nanoassemblies, including DSSP NAs, PtD NAs and D/P NAs, exhibited negative surface charges around −25 mV (Fig. 2E), which is favorable for reducing protein adsorption and improving blood stability. Notably, the three NAs demonstrated excellent colloidal stability in both PBS (pH 7.4), FBS-containing PBS and SA solution, as shown in Figs. 2F and S8. This excellent stability was crucial for maintaining integrity during the gelation process and enhancing the cellular uptake efficiency of the NAs. Collectively, these results demonstrate that both individual and co-assembled prodrug nanoassemblies can be reliably fabricated via a simple and scalable approach. The co-assembled D/P NAs, in particular, provide a promising platform for simultaneous delivery of synergistic chemotherapeutics while minimizing systemic toxicity, laying the groundwork for subsequent intratumoral hydrogel encapsulation and localized chemoimmunotherapy.

Exploration of nanoassembly mechanism and drug release profiles

To further elucidate the driving forces behind the co-assembly of the DSSP and PtD prodrugs, as well as their drug release profiles, a series of molecular and physicochemical analyses were conducted. Molecular docking simulations were first employed to investigate the non-covalent interactions responsible for the formation of the binary nanoassemblies. As shown in Fig. 2G, the results revealed that the nanoassembly process was primarily governed by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. To experimentally validate these predicted intermolecular forces, we introduced a set of force-disrupting agents, including sodium chloride (NaCl), urea and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), which are known to selectively interfere with electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic forces, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 2H, incubation with NaCl had a negligible effect on the particle size of D/P NAs, indicating that electrostatic interactions played an insignificant role in the assembly process. In contrast, co-incubation with urea led to a modest increase in particle size, suggesting that hydrogen bonding contributed to the structural stabilization of the nanoassemblies. Notably, treatment with SDS caused a pronounced increase in the particle size, strongly implicating hydrophobic interactions as the dominant force driving the co-assembly of DSSP and PtD. These findings support a co-assembly mechanism primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions between the alkyl-modified prodrugs, with additional stabilization via hydrogen bonding. Such a mechanism facilitates the formation of stable, uniform nanostructures under physiological conditions, enabling carrier-free drug delivery.

In terms of drug release behavior, the prodrug nanoassemblies were designed to remain stable and selectively disassemble within the unique intracellular microenvironment of tumor cells. Both DSSP and PtD are redox-responsive, containing disulfide or platinum (IV) linkers that can be cleaved in the presence of elevated intracellular GSH levels. This redox sensitivity enables tumor-specific activation and controlled release of active docetaxel and oxaliplatin at the intracellular level. As shown in Fig. 2I-2J, accelerated drug release was observed under reducing conditions mimicking the tumor cytosol (1 and 10 mM GSH), while negligible release occurred in normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4, no GSH), confirming the stimuli-responsive behavior of the nanoassemblies. Collectively, these results highlight the robust self-assembly capacity and redox-triggered drug release properties of the D/P NAs, which are essential for achieving synchronized, tumor-specific delivery of both chemotherapeutic agents with minimized systemic toxicity.

Construction and characterization of prodrug nanoassemlies-loaded hydrogel

To construct a biocompatible and injectable localized drug delivery platform, SA was selected as the hydrogel matrix due to its excellent biodegradability, quick gelation process, and proven clinical safety. SA is globally recognized as a highly safe food additive and dietary fiber supplement [46]. Since there are no specific enzymes capable of degrading alginate in the human gastrointestinal tract, it undergoes only limited breakdown by intestinal microbiota. The majority of the material passes through the digestive system and is excreted in the feces without metabolic participation or accumulation in the body. The gelation mechanism relies on ionic crosslinking between Ca2+ and the carboxylate groups (–COO⁻) on SA chains. As shown in Fig. 3A, upon addition of Ca2+, the SA solution immediately transformed into a hydrogel, confirming the rapid gelation behavior. To encapsulate the prodrug nanoassemblies, D/P NAs were loaded into the SA matrix, forming a homogeneous D/P NAs@SA precursor solution. Upon contact with Ca2+, this solution rapidly underwent ionic crosslinking, resulting in the formation of a stable hydrogel (D/P NAs@Gel), indicating its potential for in situ gelation at tumor sites (Fig. 3B). To facilitate visual tracking and demonstrate injectability, DiR, a near-infrared fluorescent dye, was introduced into the D/P NAs. As shown in Fig. 3C, the D/P/DiR@SA precursor could be smoothly extruded from a syringe and immediately formed a self-supporting hydrogel when injected into Ca2+ solution. The mechanical properties of the resulting hydrogel were evaluated using oscillatory rheological analysis. As shown in Fig. 3D-3E, both the blank SA hydrogel and D/P NAs@Gel exhibited significantly higher Gʹ than Gʺ over a range of strain values, indicating a stable gel-like behavior and well-formed crosslinked network structure. The encapsulation of D/P NAs did not compromise the structural integrity of the hydrogel. Further morphological characterization was performed using SEM. As shown in Figs. 3F and S9, the lyophilized blank SA hydrogel and D/P NAs@Gel revealed a porous three-dimensional structure with interconnected networks ranging from several to tens of micrometers. High-magnification SEM images confirmed that the nanoassemblies were successfully embedded within the hydrogel walls, suggesting efficient encapsulation and potential for sustained release (Fig. S10). Furthermore, the release behavior of NAs from the hydrogel was investigated. The D/P NAs@Gel exhibited a gradual release profile over 72 h, with a cumulative release of over 50% from the hydrogel (Fig. S11), indicating its potential as a sustained drug depot.

In addition to structural properties, the adhesive capability of D/P NAs@Gel was evaluated on various substrates. As shown in Fig. 3G, the hydrogel exhibited strong adhesion to a variety of surfaces, including glass, stainless steel, plastics, human skin, and even the complex surface of mouse stomach tissue, supporting its suitability for gastrointestinal or surgical application scenarios. To assess the retention capability of the hydrogel in vivo, DiR-labeled D/P NAs were encapsulated in the SA gel and subcutaneously injected into tumor-bearing mice. As demonstrated in Fig. 3H, free DiR-labeled D/P NAs exhibited rapid signal decay within 24 h, while the DiR signal in the D/P/DiR NAs@Gel group remained detectable up to 8 d post-injection, indicating significantly prolonged retention at the tumor site due to hydrogel entrapment. This sustained retention is crucial for maximizing local drug exposure and enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Together, these results demonstrate that the D/P NAs-loaded alginate hydrogel can serve as an injectable, adhesive and in situ gellable delivery depot with sustained retention properties, key features that support its application in localized chemoimmunotherapy.

In vitro cytotoxicity and ICD induction

We next evaluated the in vitro cytotoxicity of the nanoassemblies against MFC cells using the MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 4A and Table S3, the co-assembled D/P NAs exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to DSSP NAs or PtD NAs alone. This suggested that the synchronized delivery of both prodrugs within a nanoplatform could potentiate synergistic cytotoxic effects, thereby enhancing the overall therapeutic efficacy. We further evaluated the cytotoxicity of the three nanoassemblies toward normal 3T3 cells. Docetaxel and oxaliplatin are widely used chemotherapeutic agents in clinical oncology. Recent studies have demonstrated that, beyond direct cytotoxicity, both agents are capable of inducing ICD, a form of regulated cell death that stimulates antitumor immune responses. ICD is typically characterized by the release or exposure of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT) exposure on the cell membrane and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release into the extracellular space [47,48]. These DAMPs act as immunological “danger signals”: CRT serves as an “eat me” signal that facilitates phagocytosis by dendritic cells (DCs), while HMGB1 acts as a “find me” signal that promotes DC recruitment and activation. To assess whether D/P NAs could enhance ICD, we analyzed hallmark DAMP expression in MFC cells following treatment with various nanoformulations. As shown in Figs. 4B-4E and S13, both DSSP NAs and PtD NAs induced CRT exposure, HMGB1 release and ATP secretion to a certain extent, confirming their ICD-inducing capability. However, D/P NAs induced a markedly stronger ICD response than either monotherapy formulation, likely due to the synergistic effects of the dual agents.

We then investigated whether this enhanced ICD translated into increased maturation of DCs. BMDCs were harvested from BALB/c mice and co-cultured with MFC cells that had been pre-treated with different nanoassemblies. The maturation status of BMDCs was evaluated by flow cytometry, assessing the expression of co-stimulatory markers CD80 and CD86 on CD11c⁺ cells. As illustrated in Figs. 4F and S14, treatment with either DSSP NAs or PtD NAs moderately promoted DC maturation. Notably, MFC cells treated with D/P NAs induced a significantly higher proportion of mature BMDCs, indicating superior immunostimulatory potential. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the co-delivery of DSSP and PtD via nanoassemblies not only amplifies direct cytotoxicity against tumor cells but also robustly stimulates ICD and subsequent DC activation, laying a solid foundation for potentiating antitumor immune responses.

Furthermore, their incorporation into a SA-based hydrogel afforded an injectable and adhesive delivery system with in situ gelation and prolonged tumor retention. These features collectively supported the potential of D/P NAs@Gel as a versatile drug delivery platform for localized chemo-immunotherapy. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted in vivo studies using a murine MFC tumor model. BALB/c mice bearing established MFC tumors were randomly divided into four treatment groups: PBS, DSSP NAs@Gel, PtD NAs@Gel and D/P NAs@Gel. Treatment with DSSP NAs@Gel or PtD NAs@Gel moderately slowed tumor progression, reflecting the limited efficacy of monotherapy. Histological analyses provided further support for the enhanced antitumor effects. H&E staining of tumor tissues revealed extensive cellular damage and necrosis in tumors treated with D/P NAs@Gel (Fig. 5F). Additionally, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assays indicated widespread apoptosis in the same group (Fig. 5G), further corroborating the strong pro-apoptotic effect of the hybrid nanomedicine.

Antitumor mechanisms and immune responses in vivo

Given the pronounced tumor inhibition observed with D/P NAs@Gel, we sought to gain deeper insights into the underlying immunological mechanisms responsible for its therapeutic efficacy. As previously established, both docetaxel and oxaliplatin possess the dual capabilities of direct tumor cytotoxicity and the induction of ICD, a process that converts dying cancer cells into a source of tumor antigens and danger signals. In particular, our in vitro studies demonstrated that the co-delivery of docetaxel and oxaliplatin via D/P NAs effectively stimulated hallmark features of ICD, such as CRT surface exposure and HMGB1 release, thereby promoting DCs maturation. To validate whether similar immune activation occurred in vivo, we first performed immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections to evaluate CRT exposure. As shown in Fig. S17, the D/P NAs@Gel group exhibited markedly higher CRT signal intensity compared to the other treatment groups, indicating a potent ICD induction at the tumor site. CRT exposure serves as a crucial “eat-me” signal that facilitates antigen uptake by DCs, which is essential for initiating adaptive immune responses.

Next, we examined DCs maturation within tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that mice treated with D/P NAs@Gel exhibited a dramatic elevation in the population of CD11c⁺CD86⁺CD80⁺ mature DCs compared to all control groups (Figs. 5H and S18). Quantitatively, this group showed nearly a 10-fold increase in mature DCs over the PBS-treated group, confirming that D/P NAs@Gel strongly enhanced local immune activation. Importantly, neither DSSP NAs@Gel nor PtD NAs@Gel alone elicited such a robust response, underscoring the synergistic effect of co-delivery. Since DCs maturation is pivotal for T cell priming, we subsequently analyzed T cell infiltration in tumor tissues to assess adaptive immune activation. Flow cytometry demonstrated significant increases in both CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes and CD4⁺ helper T cells following D/P NAs@Gel treatment (Figs. 5I-5J and S19-S20). Furthermore, we quantified the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the tumor microenvironment. ELISA results revealed substantial upregulation of TNF-α and IFN-γ in the D/P NAs@Gel group (Fig. S21), indicating effective immune activation and a shift toward an immunostimulatory tumor milieu.

Taken together, these results provide compelling evidence that D/P NAs@Gel effectively triggers a cascade of immunological events in vivo, beginning with ICD induction, followed by DC maturation, T cell recruitment and cytokine secretion—culminating in a potent chemo-immunotherapeutic antitumor effect. This multifaceted mechanism underscores the therapeutic promise of this hydrogel-based prodrug nanoplatform for combination cancer therapy.

Magnetically guided gastric tumor treatment

Building upon the promising in vitro and in vivo outcomes of D/P NAs@Gel in inducing robust cytotoxic and immune responses, we further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of this drug delivery platform in a deep-seated gastric tumor model established in rabbits. Given the anatomical complexity and limited accessibility of intragastric lesions, conventional local delivery strategies often fall short in achieving precise and sustained drug accumulation at the tumor site. To overcome these limitations, we integrated the D/P NAs-loaded in situ-forming hydrogel with a magnetically actuated continuum soft robotic system, enabling non-invasive, site-specific delivery of therapeutics directly into gastric tumors.

As previously developed by our group [49,50], the robotic magnetic navigation system consists of six high-power electromagnets, each comprising copper wire windings around an iron core (Fig. S22). The windings are designed with small interstitial gaps to facilitate airflow, thereby enabling efficient air cooling and limiting thermal accumulation during operation. These electromagnets are mounted onto iron yokes and arranged with an inter-magnet spacing of 14 cm to ensure optimal magnetic field distribution and workspace accessibility. As shown in Fig. S23, the system is capable of generating a maximum magnetic field at the center of the workspace in various directions without causing thermal overload of the electromagnets. The platform reliably produces a magnetic field strength of up to 25 mT in any arbitrary direction. For the navigation experiments, a field strength of 20 mT was employed to ensure safe and stable operation over extended periods. In combination with a magnetic endoscope, this platform enables high-precision, non-invasive imaging and facilitates new minimally invasive surgical procedures. To assess the feasibility of the magnetic navigation system, we conducted experiments using a simulated stomach model. Initially, the endoscope was advanced through the stomach and navigated into the duodenum via the pylorus. After being retracted into the stomach, a retroflexion maneuver was performed to visualize the cardia region. The navigation process was monitored externally using a camera system, while real-time intragastric visualization was achieved via the endoscope’s onboard camera. We further customized a dual-lumen microcatheter embedded within the magnetic endoscope to enable real-time, visualized and targeted drug delivery. The injectable hydrogel precursors, comprising D/P NAs@SA and a calcium-based crosslinker, were separately loaded into the two lumens of the microcatheter. Under external magnetic actuation, the soft robot was navigated through the upper gastrointestinal tract and precisely positioned at the tumor site in the stomach. Upon synchronized release of the hydrogel components, rapid in situ gelation occurred, forming a localized hydrogel depot at the tumor interface. This depot facilitated sustained retention and controlled release of the prodrug nanoassemblies, thereby enhancing localized chemotherapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic exposure. To validate this strategy, an orthotopic gastric cancer model was established in rabbits using VX2 tumors, and magnetically guided hydrogel administration was performed under clinically translatable conditions. Throughout the procedure, real-time monitoring was achievable via an X-ray imaging system (Fig. 6B), ensuring precise localization of the soft robotic device. When tumor volumes reached approximately 500 mm³, treatment was initiated, with two administrations performed at 4-day intervals. As shown in Figs. 6C and S24, tumors treated with D/P NAs@Gel exhibited minimal growth, whereas those in the untreated control group demonstrated rapid progression. Furthermore, histological analysis by H&E and TUNEL staining revealed extensive apoptosis and tissue damage in tumors from the treated group (Fig. 6D-6E), indicating the potent therapeutic efficacy of the magnetically guided chemo-immunotherapy platform.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a redox-responsive nanoassemblies (D/P NAs) co-assembled from docetaxel and oxaliplatin prodrugs, which were subsequently incorporated into an injectable, in situ crosslinkable alginate hydrogel (D/P NAs@Gel). This platform demonstrated significant synergistic cytotoxicity, potent induction of ICD, and robust dendritic cell maturation both in vitro and in vivo. When administered intratumorally, D/P NAs@Gel exhibited effective tumor suppression with minimal systemic toxicity, highlighting its potential as a localized chemo-immunotherapeutic system. To further address the challenge of precise and minimally invasive drug delivery in deep-seated tumors, we coupled this therapeutic platform with a magnetically actuated continuum soft robot capable of navigating the gastrointestinal tract under real-time imaging. This integrated system successfully achieved targeted hydrogel delivery to orthotopic gastric tumors in rabbits, followed by sustained in situ drug release and pronounced tumor regression. Overall, this work presents a versatile and clinically translatable strategy that combines nanomedicine, hydrogel engineering and magnetic soft robotics for non-invasive, site-specific chemo-immunotherapy. The integration of programmable navigation with localized therapeutic delivery not only overcomes anatomical limitations of conventional intratumoral injection but also opens new avenues for precision treatment of inaccessible solid tumors.

In addition, in comparison with other localized chemo-immunotherapy approaches, such as microneedle patches or injectable smart hydrogels, our magnetic continuum soft robot-assisted strategy offers several unique advantages. Microneedle-based systems are limited primarily to superficial lesions and often cannot reach deep-seated tumors, while conventional injectable smart hydrogels rely on passive administration and lack precise site-specific control. In contrast, the magnetically actuated soft robot enables minimally invasive, targeted delivery of hydrogel-encapsulated prodrug nanoassemblies to deep and anatomically complex tumor sites, overcoming accessibility limitations. Furthermore, this approach allows synchronized in situ crosslinking of the hydrogel depot and co-delivery of multiple prodrugs, ensuring stable drug ratios, sustained release, and enhanced intracellular uptake. Collectively, the combination of robotic navigation with redox-responsive prodrug nanoassemblies not only achieves precise local chemotherapy but also amplifies immunogenic cell death, highlighting the potential of this platform as a next-generation localized chemo-immunotherapy strategy.
