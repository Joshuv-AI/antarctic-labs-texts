Predicting abnormal birth weight and identifying associated factors using machine learning in the Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System, Ethiopia
Authors: Sington Abdeta, Ousmane Diop, Steve Cygu, Aboubacry Drame, Reinpeter Momanyi, Belayneh Endalamaw Dejene, Mulugeta Tadele, Yordanos Sintayehu, Miranda Barasa, Mirkuzie Woldie, Tsinuel Girma, Rosa Tsegaye, Agnes Kiragga, Bethlehem Adnew, Rawleigh Howe, Merga Dheresa, Alemseged Abdisa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBirth weight is a reliable indicator of intrauterine growth and an important predictor of neonatal survival, growth, and long-term development. Globally, approximately 15.5% of live births are low birth weight, and nearly 10% are macrosomic (high birth weight), with a substantial proportion of these cases occurring in sub-Saharan Africa. Birth weights outside the normal range of 2,500-4,000 g are considered abnormal and are associated with increased risks of neonatal and maternal complications. Ethiopia is similarly affected by the growing burden of abnormal birth weight.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study design using secondary HDSS data collected from 2015 to 2022 was employed. This design was considered appropriate because the study aimed to develop machine learning models using routinely collected surveillance data to predict abnormal birth weight and identify associated factors, rather than to establish causal relationships. All singleton births were included, and those with missing birth weight data were excluded. Six machine learning algorithms identified from the literature were built and compared to identify the best-performing model for predicting abnormal birth weight. Prior observational studies and expert opinion were used to select the candidate features for all models. The synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) was used to manage the imbalance in the dataset. The dataset was split into training (80%, n = 9,242) and testing (20%, n = 2,311) subsets for model development and evaluation. Hyper-parametric tuning was performed using grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation to optimize model performance and reduce over-fitting. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), accuracy, precision, F1-score, and Kappa. Feature importance analysis was done using Shapley Additive explanation (SHAP) values.ResultsThe Descriptive analysis of 11,553 singleton births showed that 10.78% of the newborns had high birth weight (HBW) and 9.28% had low birth weight (LBW). The eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model performed best by achieving an AUC of 0.835, an accuracy of 0.72, a precision of 0.67, an F1-score of 0.63, a recall of 0.54, and a kappa of 0.52 for abnormal birth weight prediction. The feature importance analysis showed that the top predictors for the low birth weight (LBW) include maternal educational status, age at first delivery, and antenatal care (ANC) visit, while high birth weight (HBW) was strongly predicted by antenatal care (ANC) visit, maternal literacy status, age at first delivery, and maternal education.ConclusionMachine learning models showed moderate performance in predicting abnormal birth weight using HDSS surveillance data. Maternal educational characteristics, age at first delivery, and ANC utilization were identified as important predictive features. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because the model identified predictive associations rather than causal relationships. Further studies incorporating additional maternal clinical and nutritional variables, as well as external validation datasets, are recommended to improve predictive performance and generalizability.Keywords: Prediction, Machine learning, Abnormal birth weight, Associated factors, Hararghe HDSS, Ethiopia

Introduction

Intrauterine growth and development are among the most critical and sensitive processes during human development, and any deviations can lead to immediate and long-lasting significant impacts on the newborn [1]. In many developing nations, birth weight is commonly used to evaluate intrauterine growth [2]. An infant’s birth weight serves as a reliable measure of intrauterine growth. It acts as a crucial predictor of the newborn’s long-term survival, continued growth, and overall physical and psychological development [3].

Low birth weight (LBW) is defined as a birth weight of less than 2500 g. It is related to factors like poor maternal nutrition during pregnancy, intrauterine infections, and lack of antenatal care visits [1, 4]. High birth weight (HBW) or Macrosomia is defined as a weight of more than 4000 g, which is often linked to maternal obesity, pregnancy beyond 40 weeks, and changes in the mother’s lifestyle during pregnancy [5]. Newborns who weigh 2500 to 4000 g just after delivery are considered to have a normal birth weight, and birth weights outside the 2,500-4,000 g range are considered abnormal [6, 7].

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that globally, out of 139 million live births, more than 20 million babies with lower than normal birth weight are born each year, constituting 15.5% of all live births, and of whom nearly 95.6% are in developing countries [8].

Several studies in Sub-Saharan Africa have shown that the region accounts for the majority of LBW cases in the world. Socio-demographic factors, including young maternal age, primiparity, low educational level, and poor maternal nutritional status both before and during pregnancy, significantly contribute to low birth weight [9, 10]. Additionally, interventions such as focused antenatal care and nutritional counseling during the prenatal and perinatal periods are essential to prevent LBW [11]. On the other hand, few studies have shown that multiparity and maternal obesity are associated with high birth weight [12, 13].

In Ethiopia, despite ongoing maternal and child health interventions, abnormal birth weight (LBW and Macrosomia) remains a major public health problem. The country is among the nations with a high neonatal mortality due to factors associated with abnormal birth weight, especially LBW, which are one of the critical issues that cause newborns to be susceptible to short-term and long-term health consequences and mortality [14]. Macrosomia, or high birth weight, is increasingly becoming an important but often under-recognized public health concern in Ethiopia. While most previous studies and prediction models have primarily focused on low birth weight, high birth weight is also associated with serious maternal and neonatal complications, including obstructed labor, previous history of macrosomia, and increased future risk of obesity and metabolic disorders [15]. Emerging nutritional and lifestyle transitions in low- and middle-income countries may further contribute to the growing burden of macrosomia [16, 17].

Despite evidence that abnormal birth weight contributes substantially to perinatal morbidity and mortality, and despite extensive studies conducted in developed countries, limited studies in Sub-Saharan Africa have simultaneously examined both low birth weight and high birth weight. In Ethiopia, most previous studies have mainly relied on conventional statistical approaches, which may not adequately capture the complex and non-linear relationships among maternal, neonatal, and environmental factors associated with birth weight outcomes. In addition, evidence on explainable machine learning approaches for abnormal birth weight prediction using population-based surveillance data remains scarce in low-resource settings. The identification of important predictive features may help healthcare professionals and policymakers prioritize targeted maternal and child health interventions. With the increasing availability of routine health and demographic surveillance data in Ethiopia, there is an opportunity to apply modern machine learning techniques for abnormal birth weight prediction. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and compare multiple machine learning models for predicting abnormal birth weight and identifying associated factors using data from the Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System, Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using secondary data extracted from the Hararghe Health and Demographic Surveillance System (HDSS) under Haramaya University. This study design was considered appropriate because the study aimed to develop machine learning models using routinely collected surveillance data to predict abnormal birth weight, rather than to establish causal relationships. The Hararghe HDSS was established in 2007 to collect data on population health, demographic events, and related factors to inform evidence-based public health research, policy, and interventions in Ethiopia. It has three HDSS sites: Harar HDSS, Kersa HDSS, and Haramaya HDSS. In the Hararghe HDSS, data are collected through face-to-face interviews using the Open Data Kit (ODK) application, focusing on major variables such as socio-demographic characteristics, birth, death, and other health determinants. Data quality is maintained through standardized data collection procedures, routine supervision, consistency checks, and centralized database management. The study population includes all singleton live births recorded in the Hararghe HDSS during the study period (2015–2022). Newborns with missing birth weight measurements were excluded from the analysis. The outcome variable is abnormal birth weight classified as low LBW or HBW, and the features include socio-demographic, maternal, and fetal factors.

Data extraction and preprocessing

A total of 13,323 live births initially met the inclusion criteria and were selected. 1,770 newborns with missing birth weight data were excluded, leaving 11,553 observations in the final sample. Before analysis, records with missing birth weight values were assessed for completeness and consistency across key socio-demographic and maternal variables. Because birth weight was the primary outcome variable, observations lacking this information could not be included in the analysis. We compared available baseline characteristics between included and excluded observations and found no substantial systematic differences across major demographic variables, suggesting that exclusion was unlikely to introduce major selection bias. Important data sets, including birth weight, maternal, environmental, and other neonatal factors registered between 2015 and 2022, were extracted from the Hararghe HDSS database. The three HDSS sites follow standardized surveillance procedures under a unified system; the population was assumed to be broadly comparable across sites; however, residual site-level heterogeneity may still exist and was not explicitly modeled. The raw data, obtained from the Hararghe HDSS database, were first cleaned and preprocessed using the No-Code Codex Platform (https://doi.org/10.2196/preprints.67836), App version: v.26.01.1, developed by the African Population and Health Research Center (APHRC). The platform automates many of the data pre-processing and analysis efforts. Data preprocessing, which involved handling missing values with an appropriate method for each data component and encoding categorical variables, was performed.

Feature engineering included recategorizing birth weight into three outcome groups: low birth weight (LBW), normal birth weight, and high birth weight (HBW). Maternal age at first birth and age at first delivery were grouped into four categories: teenager, young adult, adult, and older adult. Gravidity was categorized as primigravida, multigravida, and grand multigravida. Similarly, the total number of children was grouped into few (1–3), moderate (4–6), and many (> 6). These categorizations were performed to improve interpretability and align with clinically and epidemiologically relevant maternal risk classifications, although some loss of information from continuous variables is acknowledged. Most of the data preprocessing was automated by the platform. Prior observational and ML-based studies and expert opinion were used to select the candidate features for all models.

Data analysis

An initial exploratory data analysis was conducted to understand variable distributions and relationships. The dataset was split into training (80%, n = 9,242) and testing (20%, n = 2,311) subsets for model development and evaluation. Six supervised machine learning algorithms, such as the Random Forest classifier, XGBoost classifier, LASSO, Ridge, K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), and Multinomial logistic regression, and an ensemble of them were developed and compared. The ensemble model combined predictions from selected high-performing and methodologically diverse base learners using a soft voting aggregation strategy, where the final predicted birth weight class was determined based on the averaged class probabilities generated by the constituent models. These machine learning algorithms were selected because they represent widely used, computationally efficient, and interpretable approaches commonly applied in public health prediction studies. The selected models include both linear and nonlinear learners, allowing comparison between simpler interpretable approaches and more complex ensemble methods. Even though advanced algorithms may yield further predictive gains, our objective was to evaluate algorithms that balance predictive performance, computational feasibility, and applicability in low-resource settings. Hyper-parameter tuning combined with 10-fold cross-validation was performed to ensure reliable performance estimates and minimize over-fitting.

Hyperparameter tuning was performed automatically using Grid Search integrated within the analytical platform. The platform evaluated multiple parameter combinations for each model and selected the configuration with the best predictive performance. However, detailed parameter search ranges and final optimized values were not retained by the platform.

Before applying the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), the birth weight categories were imbalanced, with 80.00% (9,235) of newborns classified as normal birth weight, 10.78% (1,245) as high birth weight (HBW), and 9.28% (1,073) as low birth weight (LBW). To address this imbalance and improve model learning, SMOTE was applied. Following oversampling, each birth weight category was balanced to 8000 observations.

Model performance was evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, Cohen’s kappa, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC). Precision measures the proportion of correctly predicted positive cases, while the F1-score represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced assessment of model performance, particularly for imbalanced datasets. Therefore, the F1-score is often preferred over accuracy alone for model evaluation [18]. AUROC was selected as the primary performance metric because it is widely used to assess the discriminatory ability of machine learning models. Multiclass AUROC was calculated using the macro-average one-versus-rest approach.

Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, which quantify the contribution of each feature to the prediction of LBW, normal birth weight, or HBW. SHAP provides consistent and interpretable explanations by estimating the impact of individual features on model predictions. However, SHAP values indicate predictive importance rather than causal relationships. SHAP summary plots and feature-specific SHAP value plots were used to evaluate both the cumulative and feature-level contributions of predictor variables.

Results

Of the 11,553 live singleton births, 53.03% (6,127) were male. 10.78% (1,245) of newborns had HBW, and 9.28% (1,073) had LBW, while 80.00% (9,235) had normal birth weight.

Male newborns were more likely to be high birth weight or macrosomia (711, 11.6%) than female newborns (534, 9.8%), whereas female newborns were slightly more likely to be low birth weight (560, 10.3%) than male newborns (513, 8.4%) (p < 0.001). 627 (15.2%) of high birth weight babies were born to mothers who could read and write, significantly more than the 616 (8.3%) low birth weight babies born to mothers who could neither read nor write (P = 0.01).

Unlike term births, which were primarily of normal birth weight (8,904, 80.5%), post-term births showed a higher proportion of abnormal birth weight, with 62 (16.9%) low birth weight and 51 (13.9%) high birth weight. Pre-term births had the highest proportion of low birth weight, with 29 (23.8%), and a lower proportion of high birth weight, with 16 (13.1%) (P < 0.001). Multigravida mothers had 649 (11.6%) high birth weight deliveries, compared with 206 (11.8%) primigravida and 390 (9.3%) grand multigravida (P < 0.001). High birth weight was found in 438 (12.3%) of births from wealthy households and 340 (8.6%) from poor households, according to the household wealth index (P < 0.001). Multiple machine learning algorithms were evaluated to assess predictive performance and robustness. Model discrimination was assessed using the macro-averaged area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (macro-AUROC) to account for the multiclass nature of the outcome. Additional evaluation metrics included accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Cohen’s kappa. Among the evaluated algorithms, the XGBoost model achieved the highest performance with an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.827–0.829), accuracy of 0.72, precision of 0.67, F1-score of 0.63, recall of 0.54, and kappa of 0.52. This was followed by the Ensemble model with an AUC of 0.811 (95% CI: 0.810–0.812), Random Forest with 0.767 (95% CI: 0.766–0.768), Ridge with 0.660 (95% CI: 0.658–0.661), LASSO with 0.658 (95% CI: 0.657–0.660), Multinomial Logistic Regression with 0.656 (95% CI: 0.655–0.658), and K-Nearest Neighbors with 0.603 (95% CI: 0.601–0.604). Overall, the XGBoost model demonstrated comparatively better discriminatory performance than the other evaluated models (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Prevalence of abnormal birth weight

The study revealed that 10.78% (1,245) of newborns had HBW, and 9.28% (1,073) had LBW, while the remainder had normal birth weight. The higher proportion of high birth weight (HBW) compared with low birth weight (LBW) may reflect an ongoing epidemiological and nutritional transition in the study setting. While LBW has traditionally been the main concern in low-income countries, improvements in maternal health services and antenatal care may have contributed to a relative reduction in LBW, alongside a gradual increase in HBW [19, 20]. This pattern may also suggest an emerging dual burden of abnormal birth weight, where undernutrition-related LBW and overnutrition-related macrosomia coexist [19]. Although this finding should be interpreted as an association within the study data, it highlights the possibility that HBW is becoming an increasingly relevant public health issue in settings such as Ethiopia, warranting broader predictive and preventive approaches.

Performance of the machine learning model

In recent years, healthcare data have been growing rapidly in both quantity and dimension, making machine learning algorithms the preferred approaches for handling these increasingly large and complex datasets [21]. In this study, machine learning models were applied to predict abnormal birth weight and to identify important features among newborns in Hararghe HDSS. Our comparison of six supervised ML algorithms showed that the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model outperformed the others in predicting ABW. XGBoost estimates feature importance using internal importance scores, allowing clear identification of influential predictors. Prior studies that have applied machine learning models to predict ABW have reported encouraging performance. Ranjibar et al. [22] demonstrated that an XGBoost model achieved strong diagnostic metrics, including an accuracy of 0.79, a precision of 0.87, a recall of 0.69, and an F1 score of 0.77. Similarly, Desiani et al. [23] reported excellent performance of the naïve Bayes model, with an accuracy of 0.85 and a recall of 0.72. Despite these favorable results, both studies were limited by a small sample size. In our study, the XGBoost model achieved the highest performance with an Area under the Curve (AUC) of 0.83, an accuracy of 0.72, a precision of 0.67, an F1-score of 0.63, and a kappa of 0.52. These findings support the utility of machine learning approaches for predicting abnormal birth weight. This finding aligns with previous literature suggesting that ML algorithms are increasingly used methods for handling the rapidly growing quantity and dimension of healthcare data, and is relatively higher than the performance of the same model in the study done by [24], which had an AUC of 0.79.

Feature importance and factors associated with abnormal birth weight

The SHAP beeswarm analysis provides a granular view of how socio-demographic, maternal, and fetal factors drive the model’s predictions for abnormal birth weight. The strength of the XGBoost model enabled a detailed exploration of the predictors of ABW, addressing the second research question regarding the main factors associated with ABW. The analysis identified several predictive features associated with each abnormal birth weight category.

The model identifies maternal education as a strong predictor of low birth weight, with having no formal education shifting the prediction towards a positive LBW outcome. Other studies revealed, in regions like sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, the lack of formal education emerges as the most significant predictive factor for LBW [22, 25, 26]. This finding is in line with a study conducted in Ethiopia, which ranks maternal education among the top features for predicting low birth weight newborns [26]. This may be explained by the fact that education often serves as a proxy for maternal nutritional knowledge and healthcare utilization, as women with lower levels of education less frequently access micronutrient supplementation and sufficient calorie intake needed for healthy fetal development during pregnancy [26, 27].

The wealth index of the household is also revealed to have contributed to abnormal birth weight, with a wealth status favoring HBW and the poor one positively predicting LBW. This reflects a nutritional transition observed in developing economies where increased socioeconomic status is associated with more sedentary lifestyles [28, 29]. In machine learning studies utilizing Gradient Boosting and Random Forest on datasets from public hospitals in Ethiopia, researchers have highlighted that as household income increases, the risk of metabolic complications such as gestational diabetes also rises, pushing SHAP values toward the HBW end [30]. Antenatal Care (ANC) showed contrasting effects on the prediction of LBW and macrosomia. The protective effect of ANC against LBW is a standard clinical expectation, where regular visits allow for the early detection of intrauterine growth restriction and are probably due to the nutritional supplements and the identification of other modifiable risk factors, as revealed by [31, 32]. However, the positive association between ANC attendance and HBW may reflect improved maternal nutrition, increased fetal growth monitoring, or identification of pregnancies already at risk for macrosomia, which in some cases can lead to fetal overgrowth [33]. Macrosomia can also be linked to the metabolic and clinical factors identified during ANC follow-up, making ANC a screening modality rather than a cause of macrosomia [34].

The findings from this study, as evidenced by many other previous studies, showed that gestational age and fetal sex are among the top predictors of abnormal birth weight. Preterm birth was identified as a major contributor to low birth weight [35]. also revealed consistent results and stated that the finding is biologically plausible, as the third trimester represents a critical period for accelerated fetal growth, tissue maturation, and fat deposition. When pregnancy is shortened, the fetus is deprived of this vital growth window, resulting in reduced birth weight [36]. In contrast, the model identified the male sex as a significant predictor of HBW, a “sexual dimorphism” in fetal growth. This finding is roughly in line with [37]. This may be related to the fact that male fetuses typically exhibit different placental growth strategies and higher metabolic rates, leading to increased weight at birth [24, 38]. Furthermore, the models show that young and teenage maternal ages [26] and adverse previous outcomes (miscarriage) increase the likelihood of LBW while negatively predicting macrosomia. This is consistent with the finding of Akhtar et al. (2025), which may reflect physiological constraints or maternal nutritional “depletion”, affecting fetal growth potential in subsequent pregnancies [39].

Strengths and limitations of the study

Limitations of the study

Although we utilized a large dataset encompassing several maternal and neonatal factors, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study used retrospective cross-sectional HDSS data; therefore, causal relationships between the identified predictive features and abnormal birth weight cannot be established. The machine learning models identify predictive associations rather than causal determinants. Second, although a relatively large dataset containing multiple maternal and neonatal variables was utilized, important predictors such as maternal body mass index (BMI), maternal comorbidities, and maternal nutritional status during pregnancy, pregnancy-related complications, and behavioral factors were not available in the HDSS dataset. The absence of these variables may have reduced the models’ predictive performance and limited the identification of additional important features associated with abnormal birth weight. Although the HDSS sites follow standardized procedures, residual site-level heterogeneity may still exist and was not explicitly modeled. Although SMOTE was applied to address class imbalance, synthetic oversampling may introduce potential bias and may not fully represent the true population distribution. Fifth, some continuous variables were categorized during preprocessing, which may have resulted in partial loss of predictive information. Despite important measures taken, some degree of selection bias cannot be fully ruled out after excluding records with missing birth weight data. In addition, detailed parameter search ranges and final optimized values were not retained by the platform, which may affect reproducibility. The models demonstrated moderate predictive performance, particularly in recall, suggesting that some abnormal birth weight cases may still be misclassified as normal birth weight. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered as a standalone clinical diagnostic tool.

Furthermore, the study did not include external validation using independent datasets outside the Hararghe HDSS sites, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations and settings.

Conclusion and future direction

This study demonstrated the potential of machine learning approaches for predicting abnormal birth weight using HDSS data in a resource-limited setting. Among the evaluated models, XGBoost showed comparatively better predictive performance, achieving a macro-averaged AUC of 0.83, and identified several important factors associated with abnormal birth weight prediction, including maternal education, literacy status, antenatal care attendance, gestational age, maternal age at first delivery, household wealth index, gravidity, and newborn sex. These findings suggest that maternal socioeconomic, demographic, and pregnancy-related characteristics substantially contribute to abnormal birth weight patterns in the study setting. However, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory predictive associations rather than causal evidence or standalone clinical decision-making tools. Although the model demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance, misclassification of some abnormal birth weight cases indicates that further refinement is required before clinical implementation.

Future studies should incorporate prospective study designs, additional maternal and clinical variables such as maternal BMI, nutritional status, and pregnancy-related comorbidities, as well as external validation using independent datasets. Further research should also assess fairness, implementation feasibility, and integration into maternal and child health systems to improve predictive performance, generalizability, and practical applicability in low-resource settings.

Strengths of the study

This study has several strengths. First, it utilized a relatively large population-based HDSS dataset collected over eight years, which improved the robustness and representativeness of the findings within the study setting. Second, unlike many previous Ethiopian studies that focused only on low birth weight, this study simultaneously predicted both low birth weight and high birth weight, providing a broader understanding of abnormal birth weight patterns. Third, the study applied multiple machine learning algorithms and compared their predictive performance using several evaluation metrics. In addition, the use of explainable artificial intelligence through SHAP analysis improved model interpretability by identifying the relative contribution of important predictive features. Finally, the study used routinely collected surveillance data, highlighting the potential applicability of machine learning approaches in resource-limited public health settings.
