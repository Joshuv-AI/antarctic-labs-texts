Ultrahigh-throughput screening of heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus based on fluorescence-activated droplet sorting
Authors: Xin Cheng, Zerui Wang, Yadong Liu, Liyan Ma, Zhiyi Wang, Yuxin Qiao, Wenbin Du, Shuilong Guo, Dongwei Chen, Jianrong Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (hVISA) is associated with suboptimal glycopeptide treatment outcomes. However, conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing fails to distinguish hVISA from vancomycin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (VSSA), potentially delaying effective therapy. The gold standard population analysis profiling with area under the curve (PAP-AUC) method is labor-intensive and time-consuming, necessitating rapid detection alternatives.MethodsThis study introduces a microfluidic platform based on fluorescence-activated droplet sorting (FADS) for the rapid detection and isolation of hVISA. Through systematic optimization of key parameters such as droplet generation conditions, bacterial suspension concentration, fluorescence probe selection, fluorescence duration, and vancomycin screening concentration, we established a rapid hVISA screening system. The efficacy of this system was evaluated using the hVISA standard strain Mu3, followed by validation with 15 clinical hVISA isolates. Genomic analysis further elucidated the genetic basis of drug resistance in hVISA strains.ResultsWe developed a high-throughput detection platform for hVISA by integrating a microfluidic system with FADS. The selected fluorescent probe exhibited detectable fluorescence signals as early as 6 h post-cultivation, with stability maintained for at least 1 week. Background

Staphylococcus aureus remains a major and versatile pathogen responsible for a wide range of infections, from abscesses and osteomyelitis to bacteremia [1]. The emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), which exhibits resistance to nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, has further complicated treatment and poses a significant challenge for clinicians. Vancomycin consequently becomes the last-line therapy for MRSA [2]. Moreover, the abuse of antibiotics has led to the emergence of heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (hVISA), associated with persistent infection, treatment failure, and prolonged hospitalization [3]. However, it remains a challenge to accurately identify hVISA.

Compared to other vancomycin-insensitive S. aureus, including vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA) and vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA), hVISA is not rare. The prevalence of hVISA among MRSA strains exhibits significant variation, ranging from 0 to 93.0%, attributed to differences in experimental methods and geographical distributions [4]. Conventional susceptibility testing methods, including agar dilution or broth microdilution, cannot reliably differentiate hVISA from vancomycin-sensitive S. aureus (VSSA). This is partly because a small proportion of VISA strains within the hVISA population grow slowly, allowing faster-growing VSSA to dominate in mixed cultures over short periods. Alternative screening methods, including brain–heart infusion agar with 4 μg/mL vancomycin (BHIV4), or 5 μg/mL teicoplanin (BHIT5), and macro E-test (MET), despite their operational simplicity, exhibit limitations in sensitivity and specificity compared to the gold standard population analysis profiling with area under the curve (PAP-AUC) method [5]. However, PAP-AUC requires counting and analyzing bacterial colonies grown on plates with varying vancomycin concentrations over 48 h, rendering it too cumbersome and complex for routine clinical use. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need for a rapid and accurate detection method to identify hVISA strains and prevent antibiotic abuse.

To address the limitations of existing screening methods, this study innovatively employs microfluidic technology. Microfluidics enables single-cell culture and analysis, offering new possibilities for microbiome research [6]. The system can efficiently generate thousands to millions of water-in-oil (w/o) droplets ranging from picoliters to nanoliters in volume. Bacteria within a colony are randomly encapsulated into these droplets following a Poisson distribution. Each droplet serves as a micro-culture chamber, facilitating bacterial growth and product accumulation throughout the screening process. This approach eliminates the competition for resources and inhibition among microorganisms that are typically observed in conventional methods [7]. In this study, a microfluidic system was employed to separate bacteria with different susceptibility in the hVISA community and culture them individually in distinct droplets without cross-interference. The microbial communities within a single droplet are homogeneous, facilitating the isolation of bacteria with heterogeneously mediated characteristics (Fig. 1).

Another key advantage of microfluidic technology is its capacity to enrich target bacterial strains through advanced screening techniques. High-throughput screening methods have become increasingly sophisticated and widely adopted for efficient microorganism screening [8]. Among these, fluorescence-activated droplet sorting (FADS) is particularly prominent [9, 10]. In FADS, droplets are rapidly and sequentially scanned by a laser, with the fluorescence signal measured by a photomultiplier tube (PMT), enabling a throughput of several thousand droplets per second. Fluorescent markers are essential for bacterial growth detection, with resazurin and nucleic acid probes being the most widely used. Bacterial metabolites convert non-fluorescent resazurin into resorufin, which is an effective marker for identifying droplets containing actively growing bacteria [11]. However, resorufin is prone to leaking from droplets over time, limiting the incubation period to just a few hours. In this study, we selected FRET-based RNA probes to monitor bacterial growth [12]. These probes, along with bacteria and antibiotics, are encapsulated within nanoliter-scale droplets and incubated under specific antibiotic concentrations. As bacteria propagate, RNases released by the bacteria cleave the FRET-based probes, leading to an increase in fluorescence intensity. Therefore, fluorescent droplets containing growing bacteria can be distinguished from those without cells.

Limited studies have explored the application of microfluidic systems for screening of drug-resistant bacteria. Liu et al. [13] developed a microdroplet platform for high-throughput evaluation and isolation of fusidic acid-resistant Escherichia coli in a label-free manner. Another study focused on screening fast- and slow-growing bacteria based solely on their growth characteristics [14]. To our knowledge, it represents the first application of a microfluidic platform for studying hVISA. Initially, high-throughput droplet generation enables single-droplet encapsulation, enabling subsequent screening and enrichment of bacterial populations from the sensitive community based on their differential susceptibility to specific drug concentrations. This process results in the formation of a distinct bacterial community.

Methods

Bacteria culture and sample preparation

Bacterial strains were prepared following protocols from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The standard strains Mu3 (ATCC700698; hVISA) and Mu50 (ATCC700699; VISA) were kindly provided by Dr. Yuanyuan Dai from the Provincial Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Other hVISA strains used in this study were obtained from our previous research [5]. Strains ATCC29213, ATCC25923, ATCC43300, and RN4220 were maintained in our laboratory. Vancomycin hydrochloride (Lot NO.: 130,360) was purchased from National Institutes for Food and Drug Control. Prior to testing, the frozen bacterial stocks were cultured overnight at 37 °C with constant agitation (∼200 rpm). Subsequently, the overnight culture was diluted 50-fold in fresh medium and incubated with shaking for 2 h at 37 °C to prepare the bacterial inoculum for further experimentation.

Microfluidic devices’ fabrication and operations

Microfluidic devices were fabricated from PDMS using soft lithography. A silicon wafer was coated with SU-8 photoresist and patterned through UV exposure. PDMS was mixed with a curing agent (10% w/w), poured over the SU-8 mold, degassed, and cured at 65 °C. After peeling off, input/output ports were punched, and the PDMS was bonded to a glass slide via plasma treatment. A hydrophobic coating was applied with Aquapel and Novec 7500 oil. Fluid flow was controlled using syringe pumps with Gastight glass syringes (250/500 μL). Droplets were generated in the flow-focusing device following procedures as previously described [15, 16].

The compact optical system for FADS

The FADS optical system includes a detection module for measuring fluorescence of picoliter droplets, a high-voltage module, an Edgertronic camera, and a power supply [10]. It integrates a high-power laser, photomultiplier tubes, and an analog-to-digital circuit. Data is analyzed using LabVIEW and Matlab, with histograms setting sorting thresholds.

Bacterial encapsulation and cultivation in droplets

The standard strain Mu3 was utilized to evaluate bacterial encapsulation. The bacterial suspension incubated overnight in BHI medium was further cultured in fresh medium for 2 h. It was then diluted to concentrations of 109, 108, 107, and 106 CFU/mL. Droplets were generated using EvaGreen® droplet generation oil (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) as the continuous oil phase. A microfluidic system with an oil-to-bacterial suspension ratio of 2:1 was used to encapsulate the bacteria into droplets, and the percentage of positive droplets was determined after 24 h of incubation. Based on the Poisson distribution formula (ProbabilityX=k=fλ;k=e-λλkk!), the optimal bacterial concentration for picodroplet generation was selected. After incubation, bacterial growth within the droplets was confirmed using inverted fluorescence microscopy (Eclipse Ti; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Different droplet sizes

Droplet generation was studied across four microchannel depths (20 μm, 25 μm, 30 μm, 35 μm). Droplet size, generation rate, stability (fusion/fragmentation), and coefficient of variation (CV) were evaluated at 0 h, 12 h, and 7 days.

Bacterial growth and fluorescence stability

The fluorescent probe is crucial for research and detection applications. This study evaluates the performance and suitability of the FRET-based fluorescent RNA probe reported in the literature [12]. Bacteria in the logarithmic phase (106 CFU/mL) were mixed with a fluorescent probe, and absorbance (600 nm) and fluorescence (490 nm excitation, 520 nm emission) were measured using an EnSpire Plate Reader at 37 °C every 25 min for 12 h. The stability of the probe was assessed by encapsulating bacteria and the probe in droplets, incubating, and measuring fluorescence at multiple time points (0–7 days). Fluorescence intensities of positive and negative droplets were analyzed to evaluate the probe’s stability over time.

Droplet sorting

Droplet sorting was performed as previously described with minor modifications [14, 17]. The droplet sorting chip uses electrodes and outlet channels to sort droplets based on fluorescence signals. The sorting threshold was set based on the fluorescence intensity of droplets in preliminary experiments of each batch of droplet sorting, which clearly distinguishes negative and positive droplets. Positive droplets are sorted by applying high-voltage pulses, while negative droplets flow into the waste channel. Data acquisition and real-time analysis are managed using LabVIEW software and a DAQ card.

Determination of drug screening concentrations and specificity validation

To investigate the effects of vancomycin on the hVISA strain Mu3, we added different concentrations of vancomycin (0, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 μg/mL) into the culture medium, with bacteria-free medium serving as a negative control. The inhibition of bacterial growth was assessed by randomly counting positive droplets in three fields of view. Furthermore, the specificity of the selected drug screening concentrations was validated using standard strains Mu50, Mu3, ATCC29213, ATCC25923, ATCC43300, and RN4220 from the same batch.

E-test

Droplets with fluorescence intensities above the sorting threshold were collected into an Eppendorf tube and demulsified with 1H,1H,2H,2H-Perfluoro-1-octanol (PFO, Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology, China). The recovered microbial cells were then cultivated on BHI agar plates containing 4 μg/mL vancomycin [9]. Vancomycin E-test strips were carried out on regular Mueller–Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) inoculated with a 0.5 McFarland standard bacterial suspension. An appropriate amount of bacterial suspension was spread evenly on the surface of the MH agar plate and placed for 5–10 min to absorb the bacterial solution. The E-test strips containing gradient concentrations of antibiotics were gently stuck using sterile tweezers on the surface of the agar, with the gradient end of the strips labeled towards the high concentration area to ensure that the test strips were in full contact with the agar and to avoid bubbles. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined as the corresponding concentration value on the test strip at the observing position of the intersection of the inhibition ellipse ring and the test strip and compared between the original strain and the screened strain.

Genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from each strain using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified DNA was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration was quantified using a Thermo Scientific Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq PE150 at Beijing Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and index codes were assigned to identify sequences from each sample. The raw sequencing data were processed through three assembly software tools, and the results were integrated using CISA software. The assembly characterized by the fewest scaffolds was selected for further analysis. The functional effects of whole-genome single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPome) were predicted using SnpEff (v.4.3 T), a comprehensive toolbox for genomic variant annotation and functional effect prediction [18, 19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphing were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8. Statistical significance was assessed using either an unpaired t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a P-value threshold of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Droplet formation and process optimization

Initially, we utilized microfluidic chips with different channel depths to generate droplets, and all chip designs successfully produced droplets in a stable manner (Fig. 2A–P, Additional file 2). Notably, shallower channels resulted in smaller droplet diameters, with mean diameters of 35.56 ± 2.02 μm, 38.16 ± 1.10 μm, 43.64 ± 0.73 μm, and 51.73 ± 1.09 μm for channel depths of 20 μm, 25 μm, 30 μm, and 35 μm, respectively (Fig. 2M). In terms of generation rate, microfluidic chips with channel depths of 20 μm and 25 μm produced droplets of comparable volumes, with generation rates of 5897.3/s and 4772.9/s, respectively. While microfluidic chips with channel depths of 30 μm had a generation rate of 3192.2/s, channels with a depth of 35 μm had a generation rate of 1915.8/s (Fig. 2N). After a 7-day incubation, the stability of droplets was found to be volume-dependent, with larger droplets being more susceptible to fragmentation and coalescence (Fig. 2O). The coefficient of variation (CV) of droplet diameters was also monitored at 0 h, 12 h, and 7 days. It was observed that the CV generally increased over time, particularly in the droplets generated by microfluidic chips with channel depths of 30 μm and 35 μm. Interestingly, in the droplets generated by microfluidic chips with channel depths of 20 μm and 25 μm, the CV at 7 days was lower than at 12 h, suggesting that smaller droplets tended to shrink as incubation time increased. These findings indicate that channel depths of 20 μm and 25 μm resulted in a higher droplet generation rate and better stability, rendering them more suitable for further studies in this system.

To facilitate single-cell level analysis, it is crucial to encapsulate bacteria individually within droplets. We evaluated the impact of different bacterial concentrations (109, 108, 107, and 106 CFU/mL) on droplet encapsulation efficiency (Fig. 2). Poisson distribution analysis revealed that a bacterial concentration of 106 CFU/mL (λ = 0.51) was optimal for our requirements. At this concentration, 60% of droplets were empty, and 30.65% contained a single bacterium. Although this dilution concentration does not achieve complete encapsulation of individual bacteria, there may be a small number of cases where multiple bacteria are encapsulated together; subsequent treatment with antibiotics will inhibit the growth of sensitive bacteria, thus ensuring the selection of resistant strains.

Validation of fluorescent probe

For subsequent sorting, the selection of appropriate fluorescent probes is critical for monitoring bacterial growth. The principle underlying these probes is illustrated in Fig. 3A. RNA probes, which are based on Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET), consist of short oligoribonucleotides labeled with a fluorophore at the 5′ end and a quencher at the 3′ end. When the fluorophore and quencher are in close proximity, the fluorescence is typically quenched. However, upon cleavage of the RNA probe by RNases produced by growing bacteria, the fluorophore and quencher become spatially separated, resulting in an increase in fluorescence. Additionally, enzyme-linked assay results confirmed that fluorescence increases in conjunction with bacterial growth, thereby validating the reliability of the probes (Fig. 3B). Fluorescence stability validation revealed that bacterial fluorescence began to rise after 4 h, stabilized at 12 h, and remained consistent up to 7 days (Fig. 3C–D). Moreover, the fluorescence intensity ratio between positive and negative droplets peaked at 6.27 after 24 h (Fig. 3E–F). To reduce the incubation time, a 12-h period was established as the standard incubation time, during which the fluorescence intensity of positive droplets was significantly higher than that of negative droplets, providing a robust basis for sorting.

Determination of drug screening concentrations and specificity assessment

To assess the inhibitory effect of the drug on sensitive bacteria within the hVISA population and to enhance the screening of resistant strains, we cultured standard hVISA strains in media containing varying concentrations of vancomycin for up to 12 h. As shown in Fig. 4, an increase in vancomycin concentration correlated with a decrease in the number of positive droplets. Specifically, at 4 μg/mL (VAN4), the number of positive droplets was significantly lower than that at VAN3 (P = 0.0128), but not significantly different from VAN6 (P = 0.0668). Based on these findings, we selected VAN4 as the optimal concentration for drug screening.

Additionally, we evaluated the specificity of VAN4 against VISA (Mu50), hVISA (Mu3), and VSSA (ATCC29213, ATCC25923, ATCC43300, and RN4220) strains (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Our observations indicated that VAN4 partially inhibited the hVISA strain but had no inhibitory effect on the VISA strain. In contrast, VAN4 fully inhibited all tested VSSA strains. These results demonstrate that VAN4 can effectively differentiate between sensitive and resistant strains, making it a suitable concentration for screening resistant strains within the hVISA population.

Screening and testing of standard strain

We evaluated the performance of the system using the standard hVISA strain Mu3, following the optimized parameters for droplet encapsulation, incubation, and sorting. Droplets were generated on a microfluidic chip with a channel depth of 20 µm (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A) and contained vancomycin at a final concentration of 4 µg/mL, fluorescent probes, bacteria at a final concentration of 106 CFU/mL, and BHI medium. After 12 h of incubation, the droplets were introduced into the sorting chip (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B) for FADS sorting.

A time-lapse fluorescence sequence of Mu3 screening and sorting is shown in Fig. 5A and Additional file 3. Three bacterial states were observed within the droplets: blank, suppression, and growth. Blank and suppression droplets showed low fluorescence intensity, directing them to the waste channel. In contrast, droplets containing bacteria in the growth state exhibited higher fluorescence intensity, exceeding the predefined sorting threshold. This triggered the voltage amplifier to generate dielectrophoresis, driving the target droplets into the sorting channel. The fluorescence signal distribution of the droplets was processed to generate a histogram (Fig. 5B), which distinctly categorized the droplets into three groups: noise (black), negative droplets (blue) consisting of blank and suppressed bacteria, and positive droplets (red) representing bacteria in the growth state. In addition to the screening of the standard hVISA strain Mu3, we have also performed the screening of VISA strain Mu50, clinical hVISA isolate C1, and VSSA strain ATCC 29213 (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). The result showed that the hVISA-selected population (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A and C) forms three subgroups: positive droplets, negative droplets, and empty droplets, based on their time-lapse fluorescence signal. In contrast, the VISA (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B) or VSSA (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D) population only show two groups.

Screening and analysis of standard hVISA strain Mu3. A Time-lapse fluorescence sequence of standard hVISA strain Mu3 screening over 1000 ms. B Histogram of droplets fluorescence signals for Mu3 sorting. C Increase in MIC of the microbial community to vancomycin after sorting process, as determined by E-test

After sorting, the MIC for the standard strain increased from 2 to 3 µg/mL (Fig. 5C), demonstrating the system’s capability to successfully identify and isolate drug-resistant populations within hVISA. The procedure was completed in approximately 12 h, significantly faster than the gold standard PAP-AUC, which requires 48 h. Moreover, our system not only identified resistant strains but also sorted them, offering a distinct advantage over the PAP-AUC method, which is limited to identification.

Validation of clinical isolates

To further validate the reliability of our system, we conducted additional screening on previously isolated clinical strains [5] (Fig. 6A). These isolates had been confirmed as hVISA by the gold standard PAP-AUC method (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). The isolates belonged to five distinct subtypes: ST239-t030, ST59-t437, ST1097-t2270, ST5-t570, and ST5-t2460 (Additional file 1: Table S1). After sorting, we selected a single colony with morphology similar to the original isolate for growth in BHI V4 (BHI containing 4 µg/mL vancomycin) to perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). The results revealed an increased resistance population, with the MIC exceeding that of the initial culture (Fig. 6B, Additional file 1: Table S1). This confirmed that the system could effectively identify and isolate resistant bacterial populations from clinical isolates.

Comparative genomic analysis

In order to further elucidate the genetic differences between the strains before and after screening, we conducted comparative genomic analysis on 8 pairs of bacteria selected based on their observed drug sensitivity changes (Fig. 6C). Our analysis identified a total of 12 genes (accC, atl, prs, stp, walk, yjbM, addA, sdrE, trpC, glnR, agrB, and ydjI) with non-synonymous mutations and insertions across 17 loci (Additional file 1: Table S2) [20–31].

Discussion

Identifying hVISA is challenging due to its superficial vancomycin sensitivity and low prevalence of resistant strains. This study used a microfluidic droplet system to isolate individual hVISA bacteria, eliminating competition between sensitive and resistant strains. When exposed to vancomycin, sensitive strains were inhibited, while resistant strains proliferated and released RNases, triggering fluorescence signals. The FADS system efficiently detected and sorted resistant strains, reducing detection time to 6 h, a significant improvement over the 48-h PAP-AUC method. The system is reliable, efficient, and cost-effective, enhancing therapeutic outcomes and reducing labor costs.

FADS is a widely utilized labeled sorting method [32]. In this study, the system comprises three main components: droplet encapsulation, screening of positive droplets following incubation of bacteria with probes, and data analysis. Droplet encapsulation and subsequent screening of positive droplets primarily rely on the design of the droplet generation chip and the surface tension generated by Novec 7500 fluorinated oil within the liquid system. Our previous studies have comprehensively established the feasibility and effectiveness of this droplet encapsulation system [9, 10].

Droplet encapsulation is key for isolating individual hVISA bacteria, ensuring clear differentiation between sensitive and intermediate strains. In this study, we explored how channel depth (20–35 μm) affects droplet size, throughput, fluorescence intensity, and stability in an oil–water system. As channel depth decreased, droplets became smaller and more stable, without reducing fluorescence intensity. This suggests that smaller droplets can maintain high throughput while potentially limiting space for bacterial growth, which is crucial for effectively isolating and identifying hVISA strains.

The selection of probes is crucial for the FADS system screening. The FRET-based RNA probe used in this study is designed to be cleaved by RNases, which are essential enzymes broadly expressed across bacterial species [12]; therefore, it is not specific to Staphylococcus aureus. In this study, we used this probe for the differentiation of different populations of Staphylococcus aureus without regard to species specificity. If it is required to differentiate multiple bacteria at the same time, it is necessary to design a specific probe for a specific group of bacteria for the test. Further analysis revealed that fluorescence signals could be clearly detected as early as 4 h post-inoculation; however, at this stage, the fluorescence intensity varied significantly, reflecting inconsistent bacterial growth rates. Between 6 and 12 h, the fluorescence signals gradually stabilized. However, after 2 days, an increase in the number of false-positive droplets was observed. Therefore, the optimal screening time is recommended to be within the range of 6 h and 2 days. Considering the fluorescence signal intensity and screening efficiency, 12 h was determined to be the optimal screening time.

To effectively identify the intermediate-resistant components in hVISA, it is crucial to select an appropriate vancomycin concentration that inhibits drug-sensitive bacteria without affecting drug-intermediate strains. In this study, vancomycin concentrations ranging from 0 to 8 μg/mL were evaluated on standard hVISA strains. The results indicated that a vancomycin concentration of 4 μg/mL was optimal, as it not only achieved the desired effect but also corresponded to the lower boundary of the MIC (4–8 μg/mL) for VISA strains [33]. Further analysis indicated that this concentration had a negligible impact on the VISA standard strain Mu50, but was sufficient to completely inhibit vancomycin-sensitive strains.

Upon establishing the system, we effectively applied this method to identify a more resistant population within the Mu3 strain, demonstrating the feasibility of our approach. Further validation using clinical isolates of hVISA revealed a spectrum of increased MIC values within this population. Notably, significant changes in SNPs and InDels were observed before and after screening, potentially correlating with the observed rise in vancomycin MIC. Genes such as walK [24, 34], stp [23], prs [35], trpC [28], and atl [23, 36] have been previously associated with vancomycin resistance in the literature, while accC, yjbM, addA, sdrE, glnR, agrB, and ydgI were newly identified in our study, highlighting the complexity of hVISA/VISA formation. Both walK and stp have been confirmed to be related to vancomycin resistance [23, 34, 37]. WalK is a key component of the two-component regulatory system WalK/WalR, which regulates genes involved in autolysis, biofilm formation, and cell wall metabolism [23, 34]. Meanwhile, stp plays a role in virulence gene regulation [23].

Although the system effectively differentiates between positive and negative droplets, it still has inherent limitations [38]. Primarily, this method is sensitive to fluctuations in fluid flow and microchannel resistance. Issues such as oversized pulses from the pump, droplet aggregation, and channel deposits can divert unselected droplets into the collection channel, affecting sorting stability. Enhancing throughput and reducing sorting time could help mitigate these problems. A further limitation is that current sorting devices can only sort into two channels, restricting their potential for multiplex sorting in diverse screening applications.

In addition to system-related factors, bacterial growth in drug solutions is a critical consideration. In drug-containing droplets, some bacteria may initially be inhibited but could potentially recover and resume growth under certain conditions, particularly for strains close to the MIC. Therefore, it is recommended to use antibiotic-containing plates for screening rather than standard agar plates. Additionally, bacteria may experience induction in the drug environment, which is often reversible; once the drug pressure is removed, the MIC can revert to its original state [39]. Minimizing the incubation time can significantly reduce this effect.

Conclusions

In summary, this microfluidic platform provides a rapid method for screening and isolating drug-resistant strains, as validated with clinical isolates. The significance of this work is underscored by its capacity to facilitate the early identification and isolation of hVISA, often referred to as a “hidden killer.” This advancement supports the investigation into the mechanisms underlying hVISA development, helps reduce antibiotic abuse, and ultimately benefits patients afflicted with this resistant strain.
