Priorities for medication management information resources for people with dementia and carers: a community-driven approach using a modified Delphi method
Authors: Jacqueline Wesson, Amanda J Cross, Karen Watson, Natali Jokanovic, Carl Schneider, Ardalan Mirzaei, Joanne Chung Man Lo, Mouna Sawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManaging medications safely can be challenging for people with dementia and carers living in the community and medication errors can be a source of preventable harm. Resources to support people in medication management must address their information needs and prioritise these alongside those of broader stakeholders.ObjectiveWe aimed to generate content statements for inclusion in tailored medication management literacy resources for people with dementia and carers.Design and MethodsUsing a community-based participatory research approach, we established a Medication Management Guidance Partnership: collaboration between the research team, research advisory group, and partner organisations. A mixed-method approach generated 49 statements (Phase 1) then we conducted an online modified Delphi process to gain consensus (Rounds 1 and 2) and prioritise statement order (Round 3) for inclusion in resources (Phase 2). Primary criterion for consensus required ≥80% of participants rating statements as important (≥7 on 9-point Likert-type scale). Secondary criteria assessed response variability, and statements were required to meet all criteria for inclusion.ResultsPeople with dementia (n = 5), carers (n = 5), healthcare professionals and/or national consumer organisation representatives (n = 13) reached consensus on 44 statements across six information domains. ‘Information about decision-making’ was ranked highest, followed by ‘general question prompts’, ‘information about common medications’, ‘addressing complexities,’ ‘getting guidance in different languages’ and ‘additional supports.’ConclusionOur ‘menu’ of statements about medication management priorities, endorsed by end-users, can be used to guide development of resources to improve medication management and potentially reduce medication-related harm in this population.

Background

People with dementia have almost three times more comorbidities than people without dementia, leading to polypharmacy and complex medication regimens [1, 2]. Managing medications safely can be challenging: factors such as multiple medication schedules, limited adherence support and use of high-risk drugs such as psychotropic medications, may increase the risk of preventable medication-related harm including hospital admission and mortality [3, 4]. The World Health Organisation and the Australian Government have highlighted that strategies to mitigate medication-related harm include raising public awareness and increasing medication literacy [5, 6].

Medication literacy is intrinsically linked to increased patient participation in decision-making [6]. Decision-making related to medication management is a complex process and often clinician-led [7]. A scoping review and environmental scan of available hardcopy and on-line resources to improve medication literacy identified no codesigned resources that provided guidance for this population [8]. Also, evidence on the content for medication management resources to improve medication literacy in this population is limited [9], especially in relation to medication-management strategies to support cognitive changes [10]. This is despite their expressed desire for tailored medication resources [11, 12] and advocacy for adopting a rights-based approach [13].

Codesigning medication management literacy resources with people with dementia and their carers across all stages of research is an approach that allows for their needs and preferences to be addressed [12, 14, 15]. Such an approach should also include healthcare professionals who play key roles in prescribing and medication management. While health services research has increasingly included people with lived experience in design of interventions [16], little is known about preferences and needs of people with dementia and their carers when it comes to medication management literacy resources. Further, information needs differ across care settings, and most people with dementia live in the community [17], so resources should be tailored to this setting.

There is growing evidence that tools and resources that provide information on all aspects of medication management may improve medication safety [18, 19], and empower people to be involved in decision-making. There is currently no agreement on content to be included in medication literacy tools and resources, especially from the perspective of those with dementia and carers. Using our community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach [20, 21], we aimed to generate, gain consensus and prioritise content for inclusion in medication management literacy resources for this population in the community.

Methods

This study is part of a larger project aiming to co-design medication management resources for people with dementia and carers. Previously, we identified and validated a list of priorities for medication management literacy resources across community settings in a scoping review [8] and focus group/interview study [21]. The current modified Delphi study used mixed methods, was guided by McGrattan et al 2019 [22] and reported accordingly [23](See Supplementary Data Table 1). In Phase 1, we generated statements based on our previous findings. In Phase 2, using the Delphi process (April–August 2024) we obtained consensus then prioritised statements to determine what content should be included in resources (see Fig. 1).

We used a CBPR approach [20] to establish a Medication Management Guidance Partnership (MMGP) at the inception of the larger project: a collaboration between the research team, the Research Advisory Group (RAG), and partner organisations. The MMGP (n = 22) was formed using professional networks, inviting representatives from healthcare and dementia organisations, peak bodies, advocacy groups and carer support groups. It included people with dementia, carers (non-paid family members/friends or neighbours providing support to a person with dementia), healthcare professionals, and/or dementia researchers [24]. Within the MMGP, the RAG comprised five people with lived/living experiences of dementia: people with dementia (n = 2) and carers (n = 3). Ethical approval was obtained from The University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee [Approval number 2023/HE000492].

Phase 1: Statement generation

A list of general themes were collated, based on the scoping review [8] and data from our focus groups and interviews [21]. The research team (JW, AC, KW, NJ, JL, MS) tabulated this data, grouped themes into broad topic areas, or information domains, and then formulated practical, action-based statements for the Delphi process. Each domain was described to provide context before asking participants whether the statements were important to be included in medication management resources. Draft versions of the Delphi survey were reviewed by three members of the MMGP—one person with dementia, one carer and one healthcare professional, with revisions made accordingly.

Phase 2: Modified Delphi procedure

Recruitment

Participants were recruited across Australia using purposive sampling from our MMGP, and to increase the sample size of people with lived/living experience, we used a snowballing and word-of-mouth approach to reach consumer advocacy groups and professional networks. Participants were eligible if they were ≥ 18 years of age, had relevant experience or knowledge of medication management and could give informed consent. In addition, experience managing medications was required of lived/living experience participants: either their own medications (people with dementia), or someone else’s (unpaid carers). We aimed to recruit approximately 15–20 participants to obtain heterogenous opinions and expertise across all stakeholders [25, 26], and to account for attrition between rounds [27]. Potential participants were invited to participate in this voluntary study by email, and dementia-friendly versions were included as appropriate.

Modified Delphi processes

A modified Delphi process was essential to systematically elicit the views of all stakeholders, weighting views of people with lived/living experience equally with those of healthcare professionals. We modified the Delphi process by using Phase 1 data to generate initial content [26], and provided longer response times within and between rounds to allow for additional reminders to be sent [28, 29].

For Phase 2, the online survey presented participants with a list of content statements, and importance for inclusion in a medication management resource was rated (Fig. 1), using a bipolar 9-point Likert-type scale (9 = very important and 1 = very unimportant). Links were sent via email to surveys using the REDCap electronic data capture tool [30, 31] (Version 15.3.3, Vanderbilt University, USA) (Rounds 1 and 2) and Qualtrics web-based survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA) (Round 3) hosted by The University of Sydney.

Participants rated statements across six information domains (Rounds 1 and 2), then prioritised both the order of domains and statements within each domain in Round 3. This step was incorporated to account for likely limits on length [32], so it was important to elicit participants’ highest-rated information needs. Participants provided comments and suggested additional statements for consideration at each round. The number of rounds was based on a pragmatic decision according to degree of consensus after each round versus potential response exhaustion [28].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond WA, USA) characterising participant demographics and the distribution of stakeholders. We used quantitative consensus criteria combined with qualitative review by the research team to capture more nuanced results and feedback after each round [27].

Individual responses were summed to determine the statements’ overall rating. An a priori primary threshold for consensus was set at ≥80% agreement (rating of ≥7) between participants as a cut-point for statements to be included [33]. Secondary measures were added to further explore variability, or dispersion of responses for each statement, and account for outliers in data. The interquartile range (IQR) assessed absolute variability within the response distribution (cut-point at ≤1.5) [34]. The coefficient of quartile variation (CQV) explored the spread of quartiles, providing a robust, standardised, unit-less measure that enables comparison with other similar populations (cut-point at ≤10%) [35, 36].

Statements were excluded if they did not meet all three criteria for consensus. Statements were sent to Round 2 for clarification if criteria were met but participant comments indicated a need for revision. Comments and feedback about included statements were recorded verbatim and reviewed by the multi-disciplinary research team and revised statements were included at Round 2, along with suggestions for new statements. Statements that met all criteria at the end of Round 2 were included in the prioritisation round (Round 3) and statements were ranked in order of importance based on the median ratings for each domain, as well as the order of domains overall.

Results

Phase 1: Statement generation

The scoping review highlighted that key medication information (about purpose/directions), medication effects and indications, how progression of dementia impacts medication prescribing, and safe, appropriate administration were priorities [8]. The qualitative study extended these findings, adding that question prompts, information about informed consent and how to ask about strategies to address challenges should be included [21]. Phase 2: Modified Delphi study

Participant demographics

Invitations were sent to 28 people and 23 participated in Round 1 (82.1% response rate), comprising 10 people with lived/living experience (5 in each group) and 4 healthcare professionals (geriatrician, pharmacist and nurses (n = 2)), 3 dementia researchers (sociologist, psychologist, and nurse) and six participants who were clinician researchers (nurse, psychogeriatrician, and pharmacists (n = 4)). Of these 23 participants, 21 contributed to Round 2 and 20 to Round 3, indicating a high retention rate between rounds (91.3%; 95.1% respectively). See Table 2.

Statements rated in Delphi process

Of the original 49 statements, 25 (51%) were included without further change, and 13 (26.5%) were excluded. Based on participant feedback, 11 (22.5%) were sent for further refinement at Round 2, and an additional 17 new statements were generated (Fig. 1).

At Round 2, 28 statements were rated across the same six domains. Nineteen (68%) were included without further change (of which 8/17 (47%) were new statements generated at Round 1), and 9 (32%) were excluded. This resulted in a total of 44 statements across six domains for final inclusion in the resource (Table 1).

The domain addressing ‘medication information and general question prompts’ changed the most over Rounds 1–2, with 18 statements considered by participants in total, and 10 statements meeting final consensus (Tables 3). Excluded statements (see Supplementary Data Table 2) reflected potentially duplicated sentiment and/or general medication management issues, such as ‘use of medication delivery devices’, and ‘questions about storage of medication’.

Half the original statements (n = 12) regarding ‘information about decision-making’ generated from our previous works reached consensus without change. The other half, along with four new statements were reviewed and endorsed at Round 2, resulting in no statements being excluded. This domain resulted in the largest number of statements overall (n= 16).

The domain addressing ‘information about common medications’ showed much change: 11 statements were rated across Rounds 1–2 but only two statements reached consensus. Statements related to dementia drugs (memantine and cholinesterase inhibitors) and anti-psychotics were endorsed, while those related to other medication classes commonly used in dementia care were excluded, including the new monoclonal antibody drugs, pain medications, non-prescription and complementary medicines. This domain also showed greatest variation in responses, with approximately half the statements meeting the primary criterion but not meeting both secondary criteria.

Finally, two domains were relatively stable across rounds, with minor changes: the finding ‘additional supports’ domain and ‘addressing complexities in medication management’. For example, the latter had only one original (n = 6) and one new statement (n = 2) excluded: ‘what will happen if medication is not taken on a single occasion’ and ‘information about practical adherence strategies’, the latter meeting primary but not secondary criteria.

Results of Round 3 showed that the order of domains should be changed so that ‘information about decision-making’ was prioritised over other medication-specific domains, such as the ‘general information prompts’ and ‘information about common medications’ (Table 3). Statements within each domain were also prioritised in order (Table 4) indicating areas of medication management that should be preferentially included, providing our final ‘menu’ of statements. For example, the most important statements about decision-making addressed goals of care, decision-making rights, the notion of ‘presumed capacity,’ understanding changes in capacity over time and giving informed consent.

Participant comments highlighted practical considerations with medication management and associated information needs (see Supplementary Data Table 3). For example, participants indicated it was not necessary to include information about medications that ‘might’ be used. Similarly, the statement ‘how to get information in a preferred language’ was endorsed over ‘the right to have information in a preferred language’, suggesting that more practical, action-based statements were preferred.

Discussion

Our study has addressed a significant knowledge gap, identifying priorities for information about medication management to be included in resources for people with dementia and their carers. Sixty-six statements were considered across rounds one and two, and we arrived at a ‘menu’ of 44 statements across six ‘information’ domains, highlighting significant information needs of our stakeholders. These statements were ranked in order of priority by all stakeholders involved in prescribing, dispensing, managing or taking medications, and this menu of items can now be used as a foundation for co-designed medication management resources.

Endorsed statements reflected both real-life issues experienced by participants as well as similar themes raised in the literature [8, 15, 21], notably the two highest ranked domains: ‘information about decision-making’; and ‘medication information and general question prompts’. Decision-making rights, goals of care, presumed capacity, as well as informed consent, were considered important inclusions at the top of the resource, consistent with information needs identified by Reeve and colleagues [15] and this ‘decision-making’ domain contained most statements. Its size and position indicated participants’ value decision-making and highlights the need to improve poor participation rates in medication management decisions [10]. Resources including this type of information align with recent focus on human rights in aged and dementia care [37, 38], also reflected in the ‘Statement of Rights’ in the new Aged Care Act [39], where autonomy in decisions about care is fundamental.

The second-ranked domain, ‘general information and question prompts’ was also the second largest domain, indicating that basic medication management education such as how to take medication and how to ask about side effects is important to stakeholders. It is unclear whether participants endorsed this domain because gaps exist or because they wanted to ensure essential information was covered. However, basic medication literacy is important, is important in proactively preventing medication-related harm [9] and when combined with the decision-making domain, a stronger foundation for reducing medication-related harm can be established.

An unexpected result was the lack of consensus regarding information about common medications. Only two medication classes were endorsed in the domain ‘information about common medications’: ‘dementia’ drugs for symptomatic management of cognitive symptoms, and antipsychotics, despite another eight medication classes being presented. Contrary to research indicating broader information was wanted or needed [4, 21]. The lack of endorsement for the statement relating to newer medications was also surprising (‘information about new monoclonal antibody drugs used to remove plaques…’) but may be related to the timing of our study, when evidence for the use of these medications was limited and none of were approved for use in Australia.

Specifically, endorsement of the antipsychotic statement likely reflects evidence of this medication class being used as chemical restraint for people with dementia experiencing changed behaviour [40]. Consistent with previous findings [21], ‘question prompts’ about non-medication options was endorsed, aligning with clinical guidelines recommending non-pharmacological interventions as first-line management [41, 42]. This shift away from medication use might partially explain why the statement about opioids for pain relief was not endorsed, despite pain being a frequently cited factor related to changed behaviour [43]. Further, the statement about identifying ‘issues such as pain…, when the person’s communication skills or understanding has declined’ received 100% endorsement, suggesting support for a more nuanced approach to pain management over high-risk medications.

Polypharmacy and complexities in medication management regimes are often cited reasons for medication-related harm [1, 10] and half of the statements in this ‘information about complexities’ domain in our final menu relate to changed behaviours as well as those discussed above, reflecting the strong relationship between medication and changed behaviour. For example, asking about strategies to manage when a person may not want to take a dose without covert methods that override their decision-making rights, such as concealing medication in food or drinks reflects the real-life dilemmas that carers face and our findings again endorse a rights-based approach to supporting behavioural changes.

Our co-design methodology embedded the voice of people with lived/living experience, reflecting unique experiences and expertise across all stakeholders equally, and resulting in practical, relevant results. Statements were presented in lay language, and MMGP enabled material that was co-created with medication users, prescribers and other key stakeholders. The online Delphi method allowed participants to respond without influence of others, and at their own pace [29]. While over half of the original statements were included without alteration, the inclusion of an additional 17 new statements after Round 1 based on participant comments revealed gaps beyond the literature, mainly related to decision-making—highlighting the strong preference for people with living experience to direct their own care. These findings demonstrate the value of a robust, participatory process aligned with CBPR principles [20], ensuring relevance, specificity and acceptability of content of medication management resources.

Strengths and limitations

Several strengths are noted in terms of our participatory approach and methodology. Response rates were high across all rounds, and may reflect the importance of establishing stakeholder partnerships prior to commencement of the study to generate high interest and commitment to the content area [28] and supportive processes.

Our consensus criteria were strict, employing a measure of agreement, but also measures of response variability that were internally (IQR) and externally focused (CQV), with the latter minimising the impact of outliers and enabling generalisation of our results to other similar populations [29, 33]. However, some statements that met the primary criterion, but not secondary measures, were excluded. We therefore suggest that users of our menu tailor and test information needs to local settings and populations so as not to exclude statements that may be appropriate. Further, limitations on length of a printed resource meant that reducing the number of statements was pragmatic, so other formats, such as digital platforms may support inclusion of more information. Our sample may also be considered a limitation: findings based on participants living in eastern Australia may not be generalisable internationally, however inclusion of the CQV was to support some broader interpretation, and our study aims were contextualised by the Australian jurisdiction.

Conclusion

Our study provides a minimum set or ‘menu’ of statements addressing information needs to support people living with dementia and their carers to manage their own medications safely. Consensus statements related to information about decision-making; general question prompts about medication and information about addressing complexities were prioritised by our participants. Our findings can guide development of user-centred, practical tools to improve medication literacy, increase choice and participation in decision-making and reduce medication-related harm in people living with dementia in the community.
