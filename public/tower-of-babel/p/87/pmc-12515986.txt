Flexible tactile sensors based on gold nanoparticles-precipitated carbon nanotubes with low contact resistance and high sensitivity
Authors: Sangjun Sim, Euichul Chung, Yunsung Kang, Kyubin Bae, Jongbaeg Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Flexible tactile sensors are receiving considerable interest due to their potential in diverse fields, including physiological monitoring and wearable electronics. Despite numerous studies to broaden their practical use, it remains difficult to simultaneously attain high sensitivity and a wide-range pressure detection. In this study, we have fabricated a tactile sensor with highly porous three-dimensional conductive architecture based on carbon nanotubes (CNTs) functionalized with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). The zero-dimensional AuNPs, directly precipitated onto the CNT surface, exerted minimal effect on the sensor’s initial resistance. Upon applying pressure to the tactile sensor, the contact resistance among the AuNPs-precipitated CNTs changes significantly, resulting in a high sensitivity of 23.23 kPa–1 in the low-pressure range (0.05–500 kPa) and 11.06 kPa–1 in the high-pressure range (500–1125 kPa). The sensor also exhibits outstanding sensing characteristics, including low hysteresis and excellent repeatability. Leveraging these advantages, the sensor has successfully detected pulse wave signals, neck/jaw muscle movements, and walking motions, confirming its practical applicability in wearable healthcare technologies.

Flexible tactile sensors have attracted growing interest for use in various fields, such as physiological sensing, human-machine interfaces, and robotic systems1–7. Accordingly, various tactile sensors operate using diverse sensing mechanisms, such as resistive8,9, piezoelectric10,11, triboelectric12,13, and capacitive14,15, have been developed for utilization in the aforementioned applications. Among these, contact-resistive-type tactile sensors are widely used because they have a simple design and can easily obtain signals16–19. In these sensors, the structure and material of the sensing element are important because the contact resistance’s change of the sensing element is directly related to the sensitivity and pressure-detection range. Hence, sensing element layers with various microstructures, such as pyramids20,21, domes22,23, pillars24,25, and hierarchical structures26,27 have been proposed. These microstructures induce large changes in the contact area induced by applied pressure, which contribute to enhanced sensitivity of the tactile sensor. Furthermore, numerous nanomaterials have been used as conductive materials while simultaneously maintaining mechanical flexibility, including zero-dimensional (0D) structures such as metal nanoparticles and carbon black28,29, one-dimensional (1D) structures such as metallic nanowires and carbon nanotubes (CNTs)21,30,31, and two-dimensional structures such as MXene and graphene32,33. As an example, Zhu et al. demonstrated tactile sensors based on a micro-pyramidal-structured elastomer covered with functionalized gold nanowires31. The contact region between the microstructure and the electrodes could easily increase under even small pressure inputs. Consequently, this sensor achieved a high sensitivity of 23 kPa–1, and a tiny heart rate waveform was successfully measured. However, the pressure range maintaining high sensitivity is limited to 0.6 kPa, because the micro-pyramidal structure reaches deformation saturation even under low pressure.

Alternatively, three-dimensional (3D) porous architectures possess a large surface area that allows for substantial variation in contact area under applied force, making it an attractive candidate for tactile sensing applications34–42. In particular, 3D porous structures can effectively disperse the applied pressure, thereby gradually expanding the contact interface even at elevated pressure levels. This 3D structure enables a wider sensing range than that of conventional microstructure. Furthermore, various conductive nanomaterials can be easily deposited on 3D porous surfaces through a simple solution-based process (e.g., the dip-coating method)43–50. For instance, Iglio et al. demonstrated a flexible tactile sensor by coating CNT ink onto a porous structure49. The sensor showed a broad pressure range from 0 to 50 kPa and a low detection limit of 20 Pa. In addition, a sensing material with higher stiffness can further widen the sensing range by sustaining deformation even under high pressure. Lü et al. demonstrated a flexible tactile sensor constructed with a porous framework integrated with reduced graphene oxide (rGO), which exhibited a wide sensing range from 0.02 to 1194 kPa and maintained performance over 10,000 loading cycles with negligible hysteresis48. However, these tactile sensors showed a low sensitivity of below 0.1 kPa–1, limiting their use in various applications, such as biomedical sensing and robotic systems. One strategy to enhance the sensitivity is to use two or more nanomaterials as functionalized materials for 3D porous structures51–54. The synergistic effect of utilizing nanomaterials with different characteristics can enhance the tactile sensor’s sensitivity. As a representative case, Zhang et al. developed a flexible tactile sensor by combining CNTs with 1D geometry and silver nanoparticles with 0D geometry54. The fabricated sensor exhibited a considerably enhanced sensitivity of 9.08 kPa–1. However, the highly sensitive region was limited to a specific pressure range from 11 to 61.81 kPa. Hence, it remains difficult to design tactile sensors that maintain high sensitivity over an extended range of applied pressure.

Here, we demonstrate a flexible sensitive tactile sensor utilizing a 3D porous framework, integrated with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs)-decorated CNTs through a scalable fabrication process. The AuNPs were directly precipitated onto the CNT surface via “wet chemical precipitation”55 and coated on a highly porous polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) structure by the facile “dipping and drying” method45,47. Compared to previously reported flexible tactile sensors that rely on complex microfabrication techniques such as laser carving56, multilayer casting57,58, or cleanroom-based lithography59, our sensor offers a distinct advantage in fabrication simplicity and scalability (Table S1, Supplementary information). The use of a solution-based dip-coating process with AuNP-decorated CNTs enables low-cost, large-area production suitable for practical wearable applications. In particular, the electrical interaction between the directly precipitated AuNPs and the CNTs was stronger than that between the coated AuNPs and the CNTs (i.e., low contact resistance between precipitated AuNPs and CNTs)60, extremely increasing the current under applied pressure. The tactile sensor exhibited excellent sensitivity, reaching 23.23 kPa–1 in the low-pressure range (0.05–500 kPa) and 11.06 kPa–1 in the high-pressure range (500–1125 kPa). It also showed rapid response and recovery time (96/96 ms), minimal detection threshold (50 Pa), repeatability (1000 cycles), reproducibility, and low hysteresis ( < 1.5%). These excellent characteristics originated from the AuNPs directly precipitated on the CNTs with low contact resistance, which are functional materials for pressure-sensing elements. In addition, we experimentally verified the effect of the concentration of the decorated AuNPs (i.e., directly precipitated AuNPs) on the sensitivity of the sensor. Our sensor exhibits a high sensitivity and broad sensing range, successfully demonstrating its practical utilization in wearable healthcare technologies ranging from heart rate monitoring to foot pressure measurement with a single device.

Results

Fabrication of a tactile sensor utilizing 3D porous structure

Figure 1a indicates the overall fabrication process of the conductive sponge (i.e., 3D porous structure)-based flexible tactile sensor (see Fig. S1, Supplementary information). First, AuNPs-precipitated CNT ink was prepared by directly precipitating AuNPs on the surface of sodium-citrate-functionalized CNTs using the “wet chemical precipitation” process55. The concentration of the decorated AuNPs was controlled by varying the amount of the HAuCl4 solution. A highly porous PDMS sponge was fabricated by curing a mixture of uncured PDMS and NaCl templates and leaching NaCl from the PDMS/NaCl composites through the infiltration process61. Then, the PDMS sponge was coated with the fabricated AuNPs–CNTs ink via a “dipping and drying” process45,47, forming the conductive network. The functional materials (i.e., directly precipitated AuNPs on CNTs) were easily infiltrated in the porous PDMS structure through oxygen plasma treatment, and thus, conductive pathways were stably formed62. Finally, copper electrodes were attached to the upper and lower sides of the conductive sponge, forming a sandwich-like structure. The optical images of the fabricated AuNPs–CNTs-based conductive sponge are illustrated in Fig. 1b. In the loading state, the conductive sponge was significantly compressed owing to the highly porous ( ≈ 80.8%) feature of the fabricated PDMS sponge (see Fig. S2, Supplementary information). The porous structure of the AuNPs–CNTs-coated sponge was verified through surface analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). To further validate the structural design of the porous sponge-based sensor support the design, we measured the stress–strain curve of the porous sponge (Fig. S3, Supplementary information), which revealed a nonlinear response with a sharp increase in stress at higher strains. As depicted in Fig. 1c, AuNPs-precipitated CNTs were uniformly coated on the PDMS sponge. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis further verified the presence of gold and carbon elements in the AuNPs–CNT-integrated PDMS (see Fig. S4, Supplementary information for the EDS spectra of the conductive sponge surface). We further analyzed the dimensional distribution of the sponge micropores ( ≈ 150 μm) and precipitated AuNPs ( ≈ 50 nm) using ImageJ software (see Fig. S5, Supplementary information for size distribution analysis). To further support the electronic interaction between AuNPs and CNTs, Raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were performed. As shown in Fig. S6 in Supplementary information, the XPS spectrum of AuNPs–CNTs reveals clear Au 4 f peaks, confirming the successful deposition of AuNPs on the CNT surface63. Tactile Sensing Performances

Figure 2a shows the schematics of the pressure-detecting mechanism of the directly precipitated AuNPs on the CNTs-coated sponge sensor. During unloading, the electrical path was primarily established between the electrodes through the electrically interconnected CNTs on the sponge, resulting in a low current output. The individually isolated AuNPs with 0D geometry contributed minimally to initial current response in the CNTs. When pressure was applied, the contact points among the AuNPs-precipitated CNTs increased, thereby forming conductive pathways. We conducted a more detailed analysis of the performance characteristics depending on the presence or absence of gold nanoparticles in the following section. In particular, the contact resistance between the directly precipitated AuNPs and the CNTs was low, leading to a relatively significant change in the current compared with that in a mixture of AuNPs and CNTs54. The pressure-sensing characterization of the tactile sensor, which is based on a sponge coated with AuNPs-precipitated CNTs, was tested by measuring how the current changed when pressure was applied (see Fig. S8, Supplementary information regarding the details of experimental setup). The current–voltage (I–V) characteristics under various pressure intensities (1–1000 kPa) is illustrated in Fig. 2b. The linear I–V behavior confirms that Ohmic contact is sustained between the electrodes and the sponge structure under compression. Increasing the pressure from 0.05 to 1125 kPa caused the resistance to decrease significantly, from 178 kΩ to 9.7 Ω. As shown in Fig. 2c, we evaluated the normalized current change (ΔI/I0) of the AuNPs-decorated CNTs-based porous tactile sensor by loading and unloading the pressure under a bias voltage (0.1 V). This low bias voltage was intentionally chosen to minimize power consumption and Joule heating, ensuring stable and safe operation for wearable and skin-interfacing applications65. Here, the sensitivity (S) was determined using the equation S = (ΔI/I0)/P, with P representing applied pressure, I0 the initial current, and ΔI the change in current. The developed tactile sensor exhibited a sensitivity of 23.23 kPa–1 in the low-pressure range (0.05–500 kPa, R2 = 0.9943) and 11.06 kPa–1 in the high-pressure range (500–1125 kPa, R2 = 0.9738). The high sensitivity of the sensor is attributed to the substantial rise in contact interfaces among the functional materials (i.e., AuNPs–CNTs) with the applied pressure. Additionally, AuNPs were decorated on the surface of CNT with strong electrical interactions, leading to a large current change with increasing contact points60. Furthermore, the device showed a low hysteresis of 1.5% compared to the normalized current change (ΔI/I0) under compression and recovery cycles. The reversible output current signal under compression and recovery cycles is attributed to the fast recovery of the sponge structure enabled by its elastic characteristics. A comparative analysis of sensitivity and pressure range between the proposed device and earlier reported flexible tactile sensors is presented in Fig. 2d, where these parameters serve as representative indicators of sensing performance36,39–42,45,66–68 (see Table S2, Supplementary Information). Compared to previously reported tactile sensors, our device exhibited a relatively higher sensitivity over an extended pressure detection range. Although certain sensors showed higher sensitivity compared to our sensor39,45,68, the sensing range was less than 100 kPa. Also, a tactile sensor with a broader sensing range than that of our sensor exists40, but its sensitivity was below 0.1 kPa–1. Furthermore, the normalized current change as a function of the compression level was evaluated, as illustrated in Fig. S9 in the Supplementary information. The measurement results clearly show how the sensor operates, with the current change in the sensor increasing when the sensor is compressed by up to 75%. The sensor’s transient response under cyclic loading and unloading conditions at low pressures (1, 3, 5, and 7 kPa) and high pressures (100, 500, and 900 kPa) is presented in Fig. 2e. We determined the response/relaxation times of the sensor to be 96/96 ms, respectively (Fig. 2f). The device exhibited an instantaneous response to the applied pressure, with a response time comparable to that of recently developed tactile sensors69,70. The detection limit was 50 Pa (Fig. S10, Supplementary information), indicating that the highly porous PDMS structure could be easily deformed. To evaluate the sensor’s mechanical durability, we monitored its normalized current after 1000 loading cycles under pressures ranging from 0.05 to 1125 kPa. The variation was 4.9%, confirming the high repeatability of the sensor (Fig. 2g). We also performed a repeated loading and unloading test for 1000 cycles at three different pressure levels (50 kPa, 170 kPa, and 1000 kPa) to evaluate the sensor’s durability across a wide operational range. The device exhibited stable and consistent signal responses with negligible degradation under all conditions, confirming its high mechanical and electrical robustness (see Fig. S11 and Fig. S12, Supplementary information). In addition, we fabricated three sensors using the same preparation method to investigate reproducibility, as demonstrated in Fig. S13 in the Supplementary information. To quantitatively evaluate device-to-device variation, we also included the mean and standard deviation of the normalized current responses at representative pressures (125, 625, and 1125 kPa) (Table S3 in the Supplementary information). Although a slight differences in sensitivity were observed, all three sensors achieved extremely high sensitivity (>10 kPa–1) across an extended pressure range (>1000 kPa). This result indicates that our approach, which is based on a scalable fabrication process using sponges coated with AuNPs-precipitated CNTs as pressure-sensitive materials, offers notable advantages for achieving reproducibility.

Discussion

Characterization of the functional materials

The high sensitivity originates from the AuNPs directly precipitated on the CNTs with low contact resistance. The precipitation of AuNPs on the CNT surface introduces Lennard-Jones interactions, and a lower Fermi level compared to that of pristine CNTs, thereby improving charge carrier concentration and conductivity60. We experimentally studied the effect of the presence of AuNPs and the formation method of AuNPs on the pressure-sensing characterization. Specifically, we fabricated three types of tactile sensors based on conductive sponges using different functional materials (directly precipitated AuNPs on CNT surfaces, CNTs, and a mixture of AuNPs and CNTs) and characterized them.

We evaluated the relative current change of the three types of tactile sensors with applied pressure (Fig. 3a). The sensitivities of the tactile sensor with directly precipitated AuNPs on the CNT surface, CNTs, and a mixture of AuNPs and CNTs were 18.02, 2.82, and 1.24 kPa–1, respectively (Fig. 3b). Standard deviation–based error bars derived from three replicate measurements were included. The detailed experimental results of the AuNPs-precipitated CNTs-based tactile sensor are presented in Figs. 1 and 2. A CNTs-coated sponge-based tactile sensor was prepared through a dip-coating process in a CNT solution. The density of CNTs was the same as that in the precipitated AuNPs–CNT solution (experimental group). Figure 3c shows an SEM image of the tactile sensor with a CNTs-coated PDMS sponge. Upon applying pressure to the sensor, the pores of the sponge became closer, leading to CNT-to-CNT contacts and a corresponding increase in current. However, the CNT-to-CNT junction had a high contact resistance60, which limited the current change, resulting in a lower sensitivity (2.82 kPa–1) than that of the tactile sensor with precipitated AuNPs on the CNTs. A tactile sensor with a mixture of AuNPs and CNTs as functional materials also showed a relatively low sensitivity (1.24 kPa–1). This is attributed to the aggregation of AuNPs in the mixture of AuNPs and CNTs. The aggregated AuNPs interfere with the increase in contact between CNT strands under applied pressure, resulting in low sensitivity. We fabricated the sensor using the same process (i.e., dipping and drying process) applied to the fabrication of CNTs-based tactile sensor. The device was fabricated by dipping it in an AuNPs–CNT solution in which the AuNP and CNT ink solutions were mixed. The densities of the individually mixed CNTs and AuNPs in the solution were the same as that in the directly precipitated AuNPs–CNT solution. The SEM images of the tactile sensor with mixed AuNPs and CNTs are presented in Fig. 3d. Even if an AuNP solution with high conductivity is mixed with the CNT ink and used as a functional material, the AuNPs are electrically weakly formed on the CNT surface, leading to a high contact resistance between the AuNPs and the CNTs60. We analyzed the contact resistance between the nanomaterials within the micropores to clarify the effect of the presence and formation method of AuNPs on the tactile sensor’s sensitivity (Fig. S14 in Supplementary information). The sensor initially formed several conductive paths along the aggregated AuNPs, exhibiting a relatively low initial resistance ( ≈ 71 kΩ), with the least change in current when pressure is exerted (see Fig. S15 in the Supplementary information). Therefore, it showed the lowest sensitivity among all the sensors. Although we have not independently separated the effects of internal and contact resistance, our experimental results showing a relatively low initial resistance and higher ΔI/I0 under constant pressure loading compared to CNT-only samples suggest that AuNP decoration contributes to reducing overall resistance.Fig. 3Characteristic evaluation of tactile sensors with different functional materials.a Sensitivities of the sensors consisting of three functional materials (directly precipitated AuNPs on the CNT surface, only CNTs, and a mixture of AuNPs and CNTs) b Sensitivities of these tactile sensors. c, d SEM images of the tactile sensors with three functional materials (directly precipitated AuNPs on the CNT surface, only CNTs, and a mixture of AuNPs and CNTs). Characterization of the concentration of the precipitated AuNPs

We fabricated three types of sensors with different AuNP concentrations while maintaining the concentration of CNTs to investigate the effect of the AuNPs concentration decorated on the CNT surface. The concentration of the decorated AuNPs was adjusted by controlling the weight ratio of the CNTs and the HAuCl4 solution to obtain low-density AuNPs (weight ratio of CNT and HAuCl4 = 1:3.8), medium-density AuNPs (weight ratio of CNT and HAuCl4 = 1:3.9), and high-density AuNPs (weight ratio of CNT and HAuCl4 = 1:4.0). Although the weight ratio of CNTs to HAuCl4 changed only slightly from 1:3.8 to 1:4.0, SEM image analysis revealed a sharp increase in the density of deposited AuNPs, from 0.96% to 7.29% (Fig. S16). To confirm the reproducibility of this nonlinear trend, we analyzed four independently prepared medium-density samples (CNT:HAuCl4 = 1:3.9) using ImageJ program. SEM image binarization (threshold = 180) revealed consistent AuNP coverage of 2.41% ± 0.46% (Fig. S17 and Table S4, Supplementary information), demonstrating reliable and repeatable deposition under identical conditions. This nonlinear response is attributed to the fact that even a small change in HAuCl4 concentration significantly affects nanoparticle formation, including nucleation site density and particle size71. In determining the optimal weight ratio between CNTs and HAuCl4, preliminary experiments were conducted to find the optimal composition ratio among CNTs, sodium citrate, and HAuCl4 (Table S5, Supplementary information). The upper limit of the weight ratio was set based on the electromechanical performance (i.e., sensitivity) of the fabricated sensor. Conversely, the lower limit of the weight ratio was determined based on the minimum AuNP precipitation necessary for effective seed formation, as confirmed by SEM imaging and EDS analysis after fabrication. While this also influences the sensor’s sensitivity, all three types of sensors demonstrated high sensitivity exceeding 5 kPa–1 and a broad detection range beyond 1000 kPa.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the normalized current change was measured by applying pressure to each sensor. The sensitivities of the sensors with low-, medium-, and high-density AuNPs were 11.24, 18.02, and 6.78 kPa–1, respectively. Standard deviation–based error bars derived from three replicate measurements were included. For the device utilizing low-density AuNPs as the functional materials, the initial current path was formed by following the CNT strands coated onto the PDMS surface. When the pressure was applied, the CNT-to-CNT, CNT-to-AuNP, and AuNP-to-AuNP contacts increased, similar to that in the sensor with medium-density AuNPs and CNTs, increasing the current paths. However, under high applied pressure exceeding 200 kPa, the change in current was limited because there were insufficient decorated AuNPs with high conductivity to achieve sufficient conductive networks. According to Fig. 4b, the resistance of the sensor with low-density AuNPs and CNTs at a pressure of 800 kPa was relatively high ( ≈ 18.4 Ω) compared with the resistances of the sensors with medium-density AuNPs and CNTs ( ≈ 12.1 Ω) and high-density AuNPs and CNTs ( ≈ 11.8 Ω). The tactile sensor with medium-density AuNPs and CNTs as functional materials had a higher sensitivity than the other sensors because the current networks increased significantly across a broad pressure range, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The tactile sensor incorporating high-density AuNPs showed significant changes in the contact area, even at a pressure of 200 kPa. However, the change in current was limited at high pressures, and thus, the sensor showed a low sensitivity at high pressures. The resistance of the sensor with high-density AuNPs under 200 kPa of applied pressure was approximately 25.2 Ω, which is relatively low compared with the resistance of the sensor with medium-density AuNPs and CNTs ( ≈ 41.7 Ω). The correlation between the density of precipitated AuNPs on the CNT surface and its electro-mechanical performance originates from (I) strong interfacial transport of AuNPs-CNT with minimal contact resistance, and (II) an increased availability of electron transport pathways (Fig. S18, Supplementary information). The strong interfacial electrical coupling between CNTs and AuNPs, supported by density functional theory simulations, is attributed to Lennard-Jones interactions from precipitated AuNPs on the CNT surface, which contribute to increased charge carrier concentration and lower contact resistance60. Additionally, the precipitation of AuNPs on the surface of CNTs induce p-type doping by shifting the Fermi level, due to the higher work function of Au (∅Au) (5.0–5.47 eV)72 compared to that of MWCNTs (∅CNT) (4.3–4.9 eV)73. This shift promotes electron transfer from the CNTs to the AuNPs and thus, an optimal amount of AuNPs enhances sensor performance (i.e., sensitivity) by increasing electron transfer pathways and reducing in effective interfacial resistance. However, an excessive amount of AuNPs on the surface of CNTs can lead to faster saturation of contact points even under low-pressure, which limits the sensor’s sensitivity over a wide pressure range. The SEM images of the conductive sponge surfaces with low-, medium-, and high-density AuNPs decorated on CNTs, respectively, are shown in Fig. 4c–e. Application for the wearable healthcare device

The tactile sensor composed of a sponge coated with AuNPs–CNTs exhibits both excellent sensitivity and a broad pressure-detection range, along with a scalable fabrication process. This makes it highly applicable for detecting a wide pressure, ranging from subtle physiological pressure to large-scale body motions with high pressure. Therefore, we attached tactile sensors to a subject’s body to precisely measure the signals in these applications (Fig. 5a). Consequently, the fabricated device successfully detected both the low pressure of a heart pulse and the high pressure of a footstep. First, our sensor was mounted to the wrist of a subject, and the normalized current change was measured to detect real-time arterial pulses (Fig. 5b). Considering that arterial pulse pressure typically ranges from approximately 5300 to 8000 Pa74, the fabricated sensor demonstrates a linear response with a sensitivity of approximately 23.23 kPa–1 within a pressure range of 50 Pa to 500 kPa. The sensor showed repeated arterial pulse waves, distinguishing the percussion (P1), tidal (P2), and diastolic (P3) peaks. The average pulse rate was calculated to be 86.68 BPM with a standard deviation of 7.25 BPM, which falls within the normal range for healthy adult males. The average radial augmentation index was calculated to be 0.71 with a standard deviation of 0.12. These results indicate a slightly above-average vascular age for the 31-year-old volunteer75. Despite some indistinguishable arterial pulse peaks, which may be attributed to signal noise and the relatively short duration of each pulse76, this wearable health monitoring demonstration is still meaningful as the sensor achieves high sensitivity over a broad pressure range, including the low pressure range required to detect even minimal pulses (5300 Pa to 8000 Pa). Figure 5c shows our sensor for monitoring the movement of neck muscles. By attaching the sensor to a subject’s neck, we monitored the repetitive patterns of change in the current upon the subject drinking water. In measuring swallowing activity, the peak ΔI/I0 values are affected by factors such as sensor placement, the sex of the participants, and the conformability of the sensor77. The observed peak increase of approximately 300% during swallowing falls within the range reported in recent studies using flexible tactile sensors, which have recorded peak values between 50% and 400%50,78,79. These results could provide helpful information for diagnosing dysphonia and vocal cord damage80. As shown in Fig. 5d, our sensor successfully detected cheek muscle movement according to the degree of chewing intensity. To quantitatively evaluate the sensor’s ability to differentiate masticatory effort, we conducted additional measurements with 10 replicates each during the chewing of soft and hard food. The average peak normalized current (max ΔI/I0) and standard deviation values were 1.91 ± 0.62 for soft food and 7.81 ± 0.62 for hard food, indicating that the current response during hard chewing was approximately 4.08 times greater than that of soft chewing. The correlation between the obtained current and jaw movement can be further utilized in diagnosing temporomandibular disorders81. Furthermore, our sensor is applicable for monitoring physical walking motions that require a wide pressure-detection range. Figure 5e shows the individual motions of taking off (state 1) and stepping on (state 2). We attached two tactile sensors to the front and back of a subject’s knee and placed the other sensor on foot to measure the walking motion of the subject in real time. Figure 5f shows the real-time change in the current obtained from the three individual sensors, where two separate walking steps can be distinguished. As the subject performed the motion of stepping on (state 2), the current signals obtained from the sensor placed on the foot (sensor 3) significantly increased, whereas the signal from the sensors attached to the knee decreased. However, when the feet of the subject were off the ground (state 1), the maximum peak current value from the sensors attached to the knee (sensors 1 and 2) was observed when the knees were bent inward. During gait analysis, sensor 3 exhibits a normalized current of up to approximately 4000, corresponding to an estimated pressure of around 170 kPa. Although sensor placement, foot size, and subject weight contribute to variability in pressure distribution and the corresponding signals, this measurement aligns well with values reported in recent wearable pressure sensors. Conclusion

A flexible tactile sensor incorporating a 3D porous structure coated with AuNPs-precipitated CNTs was successfully developed. As external pressure was applied, the contact points among the AuNPs-precipitated CNTs increased, leading to an increase in current. Particularly, the current could be extremely increased owing to the low contact resistance among the functional materials (i.e., AuNPs-precipitated CNTs). The sensor demonstrated a high sensitivity, achieving 23.23 kPa–1 in the low-pressure range (0.05–500 kPa), and 11.06 kPa–1 in the high-pressure range (500–1125 kPa). Additionally, the device exhibited superior performance characteristics, including a rapid response, high repeatability, and reproducibility. The device was characterized by varying the formation of the functional layers and the concentration of the decorated AuNPs. Using this sensor, we successfully monitored physiological signals and human motions, indicating its potential for integration into wearable electronic systems.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of the AuNPs-precipitated CNT Ink

We prepared 2 wt% multiwalled CNTs (MWCNTs) (diameter: 50 nm, length: ≈5 µm, Applied Carbon Nano Technology Co.) ink dispersed in deionized (DI) water. The MWCNT ink was mixed with a 1 wt% sodium citrate tribasic solution (Duksan Co.) and sonicated the solution for 10 min using an ultrasonic bath. Following the dispersion process of the CNT ink in sodium citrate solution, hydrogen tetrachloroaurate (III) tetrahydrate (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Co.) was added to the sodium-citrate-treated CNT solution at 150 °C while stirring at 80 rpm. AuNPs were precipitated from the hydrogen tetrachloroaurate (III) complex using sodium citrate. The contact resistance between the precipitated AuNPs and the CNTs was low owing to the strong electrical interactions between them60. Moreover, we controlled the concentration of AuNPs using the amount of hydrogen tetrachloroaurate (III) tetrahydrate solution71.

Fabrication of tactile sensor based on AuNPs–CNTs-coated porous PDMS

Sodium chloride (NaCl) powder was blended with uncured PDMS at a 6:1 weight ratio. Then, the mixed NaCl–PDMS composite was poured into a mold and subsequently cured at 90 °C for 1 h. After then, NaCl–PDMS was demolded from the mold and cut into the dimensions of 8 mm × 8 mm × 3 mm with a razor blade. The infiltration process was followed by magnetic stirring for 2 h in DI water to leach the NaCl powder; thus, a porous PDMS structure was successfully fabricated61. The porous PDMS structure was then treated with oxygen plasma treatment was applied at 100 W and 50 sccm for 5 min to allow the functional material solution to be easily immersed in it62. Finally, the porous PDMS was dipped into the fabricated AuNPs-precipitated CNT ink and dried at 90 °C for 2 h.

Characterization of the tactile sensors

The morphological features of the tactile sensors were investigated, and the presence of precipitated AuNPs was confirmed using SEM and EDS techniques (IT-500HR, JEOL). The sizes of the micropores and precipitated AuNPs were analyzed using ImageJ software. The sensing performance was assessed using a motorized z-axis push–pull system (KMX-E1000N, MAS UTM) coupled with a DTG-10 force sensor (Digitech). The electrical responses, specifically normalized current variations, were recorded using a precision source meter (2614B, Keithley).
