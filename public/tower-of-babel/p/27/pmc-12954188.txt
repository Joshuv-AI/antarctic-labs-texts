Characterization of a UQCRC1 variant in a patient with progressive weakness, pain and sleep issues reveals a functional mitochondrial defect restored by mitochondrial transplantation
Authors: Gerardo G. Piroli, Rebecca Myers, Lynda Holloway, Andrew Hayek, Ellen Linebaugh, Julie R. Jones, Cindy Skinner, Steven A. Skinner, Norma Frizzell, Richard Steet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary mitochondrial defects underlie the heterogeneity of many rare inherited disorders. Pathogenic variants that disrupt the function of the multi-subunit protein complexes of the mitochondrial respiratory chain contribute to a range of neurological phenotypes and other clinical manifestations. These variants are also thought to contribute to the onset and progression of numerous more common neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease. Here we describe an individual affected with progressive muscle weakness and pain harboring a paternally inherited missense variant in UQCRC1, encoding a subunit of Complex III. Biochemical characterization of cells from the proband and his father demonstrated normal steady-state levels of UQCRC1 and UQCRC2 protein. Functional assessment of mitochondrial respiration in lymphoblasts and fibroblasts, however, showed a clear deficit in respiratory parameters in the proband, with a more attenuated response in the father. Lastly, we demonstrate that healthy mitochondria isolated from HEK293 cells can be transferred to the patient lymphoblasts, restoring basal mitochondrial respiration and ATP production. Perspectives on the contribution of this variant to the patient phenotypes, and the potential of mitochondrial transplantation and different compounds as treatment modalities for patients with primary mitochondrial deficits, is discussed.

Introduction

Rare inherited mitochondrial disorders represent a heterogeneous group of genetic conditions characterized by defects in nuclear or mitochondrial-encoded proteins that maintain the architecture and function of mitochondria [1]. The majority of the genes harboring the disease-causing mutations encode the supercomplex component proteins of the electron transport chain (ETC) necessary for oxygen consumption and ATP production. These disorders are predominantly characterized by neurological and sensory manifestations, although the involvement of other systems is common. Genomic sequencing of undiagnosed cases often uncovers variants of unclear clinical significance in genes that encode subunits of all of the major ETC complexes [2].

Variants in genes (UQCRC1 and UQCRC2) encoding two subunits of Complex III have been described in human patients [3], [4]. UQCRC1 encodes the ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein I involved in the transfer of electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c that helps establish the proton gradient needed for ATP synthesis. Variants in UQCRC1 cause autosomal dominant parkinsonism with polyneuropathy [3]. Biallelic UQCRC2 variants have also been described in patients with Complex III deficiency [4]. Clinical manifestations vary for these gene variants, ranging from liver failure and hypoglycemia to severe encephalomyopathy. Genomic sequencing of affected individuals will continue identifying variants in both UQCRC1 and UQCRC2, with many likely classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Likewise, the complete range of phenotypic outcomes associated with these gene variants is not fully defined.

UQCRC1 variants have been identified in individuals with pediatric speech and language deficits [3]. Increased expression of this gene is also found in different cancers [5], [6]. Several studies have characterized UQCRC1 variants that are enriched in different cohorts of neurodegenerative diseases like Parkinson's [7], [8]. As with other potential risk genes, the impact of UQCRC1 variants remains somewhat controversial and may be specific to certain populations and genetic backgrounds [9], [10]. Nonetheless, there are a growing number of reasonable mechanistic connections between UQCRC1 and biochemical pathways related to both Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease [11].

Addressing mitochondrial dysfunction in the context of both pediatric and adult neurodegenerative diseases is considered an important and emerging therapeutic avenue. One of the most direct ways to restore mitochondrial function is to transplant mitochondria from healthy cells into damaged in a process often referred to as mitochondrial transplantation [12]. While questions remain regarding the mechanisms that govern the uptake and trafficking of transplanted mitochondria, compelling evidence in animal models indicate that substantial functional correction of primary mitochondrial defects and neurodegeneration can be achieved [13].

Here we report an affected male with progressive muscle weakness and pain harboring a paternally inherited missense UQCRC1 VUS (c.302C > T; p.Thr101Ile). Functional studies on cells from both the proband and his father, including protein biochemistry and functional respirometry, provide supporting evidence for the pathogenicity of this variant independent of Complex III destabilization. We further show that transplantation of isolated mitochondria into cells from the proband can partially restore mitochondrial energetics. The potential and limitations of this treatment modality are discussed.

Methods

Genetic testing and patient consent

Whole exome sequencing was performed using genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood from the proband (designated P1) and his father (designated P2). The Agilent SureSelectXT Clinical Research Exome kit v2 was used to target known disease-associated exonic regions of the genome (coding sequences and splice junctions of known protein-coding genes associated with disease, as well as an exomic backbone) using genomic DNA from the submitted sample(s). The targeted regions were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 System with 150 bp paired-end reads. Using Illumina DRAGEN Bio-IT Platform software, the DNA sequence was aligned and compared to the human genome build 19 (hg19/NCBI build 37). The average depth of coverage across all targeted regions was calculated. The Emedgene™ software was used to filter and analyze sequence variants identified in the patient. The identified UQCRC1 sequence variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Targeted Sanger sequencing was performed on parental samples and indicated the UQCRC1 variant was paternal in origin. This study conformed to the tenets of the declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for research studies was obtained from the patient and his father using a Comprehensive Consent Research form approved by Self Regional (IRB number: Pro000085001). We sincerely thank both for their participation in these studies.

Variant and structural analysis

The RCSB Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) was used to view the 3.40 Å Cryo-EM structure of human UQCRC1, available as subunit 1 of the human respiratory complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex, pdb_00005xte) [14].

AlphaMissense was used to demonstrate the predicted pathogenicity of each amino acid substitution at position 101 in the Uqcrc1 protein [15].

Cell culture and mitochondrial isolation/transplantation

Fibroblast cultures were obtained from skin biopsies on the proband (P1) and his father (P2) using established procedures [16]. Lymphoblast cell lines were generated from blood draws on both subjects using EBV-transformation as previously described [17]. Age-matched control cells for both the proband and father (referred to as C1, C2, respectively) were obtained from healthy subjects. The fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM (ThermoFisher; #10–017-CV) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (VWR; #97068–085) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (ThermoFisher; #30–002-CI) in a 5% CO2 humidified tissue culture incubator. Lymphoblasts were cultured in RPMI 1640, 15%FBS, 1% l-glutamine, 1% antibiotic/antimycotic, 1% sodium pyruvate.

HEK293 cells stably expressing turboGFP fused to Cytochrome c (Innoprot; #P30801) were maintained in high glucose DMEM (D6429 Sigma-Aldrich) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. For mitochondria isolation, HEK293 cells were grown in T150 flasks. Trypsin was used to detach the monolayer, and the cell pellet obtained after centrifugation at 300 xg for 5 min at room temperature was resuspended in 10 ml of ice-cold sterile mitochondria isolation buffer (240 mM sucrose, 3 mM MgCl2, 2 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM K2HPO4, 1 mM potassium EGTA, 20 mM taurine, 0.1% human serum albumin, pH 7.3) and transferred to a Petri dish on ice. The suspension was loaded in a 12 ml syringe and passed 20 times through an 18G needle, followed by 20–25 times through a 23G needle. The homogenate was loaded into five 2 ml Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 700 xg for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants of this centrifugation were transferred to fresh Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 9000 xg for 10 min at 4 °C, and the corresponding pellets were combined in a minimal volume of mitochondria isolation buffer for mitochondrial transfer (see Seahorse analyses below).

Cells were transplanted with the isolated mitochondria via passive incubation which is effective for the uptake of exogenous mitochondria via macropinocytosis in most cell types.

Western blot analysis

Following protein quantification, whole cell lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE (12% gel) and western blot analysis performed using a rabbit polyclonal antibody to UQCRC1 (Proteintech; cat# 21705-1AP) or a polyclonal antibody to UQCRC2 (Proteintech #14742–1-AP). Blots were visualized using Clarity Max Western ECL. Protein from the Seahorse plates used for the mitochondrial stress test after mitochondrial transfer to lymphoblasts (equivalent to 8000 cells) was resuspended in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 2 mM diethylenetetraminepentaacetic acid (DTPA), and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.4, total protein was not quantified as the collected cells also contained Cell Tak protein, preventing accurate quantification of lymphoblast protein. The protein was resolved by SDS/PAGE, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes as previously described [18]. Primary antibodies were used for the detection of the following proteins: turboGFP (Origene #TA150041, 1:8000), and succinate dehydrogenase subunit a (SDHa, Cell Signaling Technology #11998, 1:5000). Membranes were imaged by chemiluminescence on an iBright CL1500 system (Thermo) in the Instrumentation Research Facility at the University of South Carolina (RRID:SCR_024955). Membranes were stained with Amido black to detect total protein load.

Metabolic measurements with the seahorse extracellular flux analyzer

Lymphoblasts

Twenty-four well (XF24) Seahorse cell culture microplates were coated for 20 min with 50 μl Cell-Tak (Corning, 55.7 μg/ml, prepared according to the manufacturer instructions), rinsed twice with sterile water, dried and stored at 4 °C for up to a week. After medium exchange with Seahorse XF Base Medium or Seahorse DMEM (see details below), lymphoblasts were seeded on the plates at a density of 300,000 per well in 100 μl and centrifuged at 300 xg for 5 min at 20 °C without brake. Plates were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator and before adding 400 μl of the Seahorse medium prior to the metabolic assays described below.

Fibroblasts

The cells were plated at a density of 12,000 cells per well in XF24 Seahorse microplates (coated overnight with 0.2% gelatin). The plates were cultured as described in Cell Culture for 3 days prior to the metabolic assays described below.

Several metabolic analyses were performed with the cells, as described below.

Mitochondrial Stress Test: to examine mitochondrial oxidative capacity. Lymphoblasts or fibroblasts were switched to XF Base Medium supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in basal conditions (no compounds injected), and after injections of oligomycin (1 μM), carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 0.1 μM for lymphoblasts or 1 μM for fibroblasts), and antimycin A (3 μM for lymphoblasts), or rotenone/antimycin A (1.5 μM each drug for fibroblasts) to determine ATP production, proton leak, maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity, as previously described [19].

For mitochondrial transfer and subsequent respirometry, C1 and P1 lymphoblasts were incubated for 16 h in a 24-well plate in 500 μl medium (300,000 cells/well), 50% of wells received mitochondria isolation buffer (C1 Veh and P1 Veh) and the remaining 50% received mitochondria isolated from Cytochrome c - turboGFP HEK293 cells (C1 + Mito and P1 + Mito). Transfer of cell-free mitochondria occurred over a 16 h period. Lymphoblasts were washed twice with Seahorse medium to remove non-transferred mitochondria and a mitochondrial stress test was performed as described above.

Glycolysis Stress Test: to measure glycolytic activity in lymphoblasts ± a challenge with 100 ng/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 18 h. Lymphoblasts were switched to XF Base Medium supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured in basal conditions (no compounds injected), and after injections of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 μM), and 2-deoxyglucose (2DG, 50 mM), to determine the medium acidification associated with glycolysis, the maximal acidification and the glycolytic reserve.

ATP Rate Assay: to measure the contributions of glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism to the total ATP production. Lymphoblasts or fibroblasts were switched to Seahorse DMEM supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. The ECAR and the OCR were then measured with a Seahorse XFe24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer in basal conditions and after sequential injections of oligomycin (1.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM each drug) to determine the relative contribution of glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism to ATP synthesis, and the total amount of ATP produced, as previously reported [19].

After completion of the assays, the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342, and the nuclear staining was quantified in an Agilent Biotek Cytation 5 Imaging Reader for normalization of the results to the total cell count.

Determination of citrate synthase activity

Citrate synthase activity was measured according to Leek et al. [20] with minor modifications. Briefly, patient and control lymphoblasts were collected in triplicate following trypsin detachment and centrifuged at 300 xg for 5 min at 20 °C. Cellular pellets were resuspended in 350 μl of homogenization buffer (100 mM monobasic/dibasic potassium phosphate pH 7.4, containing 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA and a protease inhibitor cocktail), followed by sonication in ice 4 times × 10 s to disrupt the mitochondrial membrane and centrifugation at 2000 xg for 4 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were then passed to fresh tubes and subjected to a freezing-thaw cycle (2 min each). The sonication process and freeze-thaw cycle were repeated, and 20 μl of the resulting samples were loaded in a 96-well plate. The measurement was performed by adding 180 μl reaction mix (100 mM Tris pH 8.1, containing 0.1 mM 5.5′-dithio-bis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), 0.2 mM acetyl-coenzyme A and 0.5 mM oxaloacetic acid) to the samples; blank wells did not contain oxaloacetic acid. The side reaction between coenzyme A and DTNB, with formation of a mercaptide ion, was followed at 30 °C in a plate reader at 412 nm at 20 s intervals for 20 cycles. The readings in the absence of oxaloacetic acid were subtracted from those in the presence of the substrate, and these corrected values were plotted as a function of time to calculate the Vmax from the slopes. Each sample was run in triplicate, and the averaged results were converted to mU/mg of protein considering Emercaptide ion(412) = 13.6 cm−1 mM−1 and a path length of 0.4 cm. Protein concentration was determined by the Lowry method [21].

Statistics

The number of technical replicates performed for control and patient cells is noted in the legend of each experiment, ranging from 3 to 10 replicates per experiment. All analyses on the technical replicates were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Results were expressed as the average ± SEM, and analyzed by either an unpaired t-test, a one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc comparisons with the Tukey's test (comparing all controls and patients) or a two-way ANOVA to determine an effect of mitochondrial transplantation on C1 and P1, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc testing to assess the effect of treatment.

Results

Clinical description and diagnostic summary

This male patient (designated as P1) presented to genetics at 32 years of age. He reported symptom onset in childhood with recurrent illnesses, fatigue, and weakness. He has experienced progressive muscle weakness, muscle pain, and nerve hypersensitivity. The patient also suffers from depression, anxiety, insomnia, sleep apnea, low testosterone, and detrusor sphincter dyssynergia. He has no cognitive impairment and his early developmental milestones were met on time, but he has issues with memory and executive functioning skills. On physical exam he was noted to have hyperextensible elbows, left single palmar crease, asymmetric pectus excavatum, and brisk reflexes. Electromyography with nerve conduction studies have been normal. X-rays of the spine, abdomen, sacrum and coccyx were unrevealing. This proband's father (designated as P2) was reported to have low testosterone and muscle weakness as well, but not to the extent noted in his son. He reported lifting weights but not being able to gain muscle mass.

Prior to this evaluation, an NGS panel identified a maternally inherited CACNA1S variant c. c.1945 A > C. Pathogenic variants in this gene are associated with autosomal dominant malignant hyperthermia, autosomal dominant susceptibility to thyrotoxic periodic paralysis, autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive congenital myopathy 19 due to dihydropyridine receptor defect, and autosomal dominant hypokalemic periodic paralysis. This variant is observed in 174 heterozygotes in gnomAD v4.1.0 and is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). The functional relevance of this variant was not explored in the present work.

Additional laboratory testing was normal including plasma total and free carnitine, plasma acylcarnitine profile, alpha-galactosidase enzyme analysis, plasma amino acids, urine organic acids. Karyotype analysis was also normal (46,XY). Whole exome sequencing identified a VUS in UQCRC1 c.302C > T (p.Thr101Ile) which is paternally inherited, and represented a possible explanation for his phenotypes.

UQCRC1 protein structure and AlphaMissense prediction of pathogenicity for p.Thr101Ile variant

The Protein Data Bank (PDB) structure of human UQCRC1 (Uniprot P31930) was used to interrogate the three-dimensional (3D) arrangement of Thr101 and its interactions with adjacent amino acids. The Cryo-EM structure of human respiratory complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex) includes subunit 1, UQCRC1 (pdb_00005xte) is available as a component of the respiratory megacomplex I2III2IV2 at 3.40 Å [14]. Thr101 is located on a surface loop of UQCRC1 that connects two α-helical regions. The Thr101 hydroxyl group is capable of H-bonding to several adjacent amino acids, which may include Asp 149, Asn 153, Cys 154 or Lys 102 (Fig. 1A). Substitution of this polar threonine with the larger uncharged isoleucine (Thr101Ile) would be predicted to disrupt this hydrogen bonding and distort the local protein structure. The AlphaMissense pathogenicity score [15] for human UQCRC1 T101I was 0.9652, indicating likely pathogenic. Interestingly, substitution with T101S, another polar amino acid with the -OH side chain, was considered ambiguous. Oxidative respiration is impaired in patient-derived cells harboring the UQCRC1 variant

To further explore the functional impact of the variant, we performed a mitochondrial stress test using the Seahorse XFe24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. This analysis included two sex- and age-matched controls (C1 and C2), and the proband (P1) and father's (P2) cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, the traces for lymphoblasts from P1 and P2 showed a marked decrease in the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) compared to the respective controls. More specifically, P1 and P2 lymphoblasts showed a 41.1% and 32.5% basal OCR decrease compared to their respective controls (p < 0.001, Fig. 3B). Reductions were also observed for the OCR associated with ATP synthesis (39.7% for P1 vs. C1 and 24.6% for P2 vs. C2, p < 0.0001 and p < 0.01, respectively, Fig. 3C) and the patient cells also showed decreased proton leak (Fig. 3D). The maximal respiration rate was significantly reduced in response to uncoupling (65.6% for P1 vs. C1 and 52.0% for P2 vs. C2, p < 0.0001, Fig. 3E), however, the P2 lymphoblasts achieved 59.1% higher maximal respiration than P1 lymphoblasts (p < 0.05, Fig. 3E). The decrease in maximal respiration yielded a marked deficit in spare respiratory capacity in the P1 and P2 cells (Fig. 3F). There was a trend for decreased non-mitochondrial respiration between P1 and the age-matched control C1, with a significant decrease measured in P2 vs. C2 (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3G). The activity of citrate synthase, an enzyme used to estimate mitochondrial content, did not differ in the lymphoblasts of the patients vs. controls (Fig. 3H), confirming the deficit primarily impacted the electron transport chain function. We also measured total ATP synthesis and the contribution of mitochondria and glycolysis to ATP production. In agreement with other studies [22], we found that the lymphoblasts primarily rely on ATP production by glycolysis (glycolytic ATP production rate ∼ 10 pmol/min/1000 cells vs. mitochondrial ATP production rate ∼ 3.5 pmols/min/1000 cells in healthy controls C1 and C2). In lymphoblasts from the patients, the ATP production rate derived from both glycolysis (Fig. 4A) and mitochondrial metabolism (Fig. 4B) was markedly reduced compared to their respective controls. More specifically, P1 and P2 lymphoblasts showed a 62.5% and 36.3% reduction in glycolytic ATP (p < 0.0001 vs. C1 and p < 0.0001 vs. C2, respectively, Fig. 4A), and an 82.6% and 37.1% reduction in mitochondrial ATP (p < 0.0001 vs. C1 and p < 0.05 vs. C2, respectively, Fig. 4B), Overall, these contributed to a significant decrease in the total ATP synthesis rate, with a larger deficit shown by the proband (P1) than his father (P2) (68.4% for P1 vs. C1, p < 0.0001, and 37.1% for P2 vs. C2, p < 0.001, Fig. 4C).Fig. 3Mitochondrial respiration is impaired in lymphoblasts harboring a UQCRC1 missense variant. Mitochondrial respiration is reduced in skin-derived patient fibroblasts

We also investigated mitochondrial metabolism parameters in skin-derived fibroblasts from P1 and C1. A mitochondrial stress test performed with these cells showed an overall l decrease in the oxygen consumption rate in P1 fibroblasts when compared to C1 fibroblasts (Fig. S1A). As shown in Fig. S1B, the OCR was significantly decreased for several respiratory parameters including basal OCR (16.4%), ATP-associated OCR (16.1%), maximal respiratory capacity (18.3%), and spare respiratory capacity (20.2%) in P1 vs. C1 fibroblasts (p < 0.05 for all comparisons), mirroring the respiratory changes measured in the lymphoblasts. Further examination of the ATP production rate showed similar levels of glycolytic ATP synthesis in P1 vs. C1, but mitochondrial and total ATP synthesis were reduced in P1 vs. C1 (16.5 and 14.4%, respectively, p < 0.05 for both comparisons, Fig. S1C). For the fibroblasts, the primary source of ATP production was the mitochondria, indicating more oxidative metabolism than the lymphoblasts (Fig. S1C versus Fig. 4C).

Metabolic flexibility in response to an immune stressor is impaired in patient lymphoblasts

In immune cell populations, the activation of toll-like receptors signals a switch in energy production pathways with the remodeling of mitochondrial metabolism and a greater demand on glycolysis [23], [24], [25], [26]. Given that the lymphoblasts were metabolically poised to prefer glycolysis (Fig. 4A), we reasoned that the flexibility of this metabolic response could be affected in the patients. Lymphoblasts from the patients and their respective controls were stimulated with LPS (18 h, 100 ng/ml) prior to a glycolysis stress test. As shown in Fig. S2A (for P1 vs. C1) and Fig. S2B (for P2 vs. C2), the control cells treated with LPS showed an expected increase in the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), whereas for the patient cells did not remodel their metabolism to increase glycolysis. More specifically, the injection of glucose (G) increased the ECAR in LPS treated C1 cells (13.0%, p < 0.01, Fig. S2C), with a trend to increase in C2 cells (Fig. S2F). After the injection of oligomycin (O) to block the mitochondrial ATP synthase, thereby increasing the reliance on glycolysis, LPS increased the glycolytic capacity in C1 cells (13.5%, p < 0.01), and in C2 cells (18.7%, p < 0.05), but not in P1 and P2 cells (Figs. S2D, S2G). In addition, the glycolytic reserve showed a trend to increase by LPS in C1 cells, and a significant increase in C2 cells (58.3%, p < 0.05), but no changes were observed in P1 and P2 cells (Figs. S2E, S2H). These results indicate a reduced metabolic flexibility in the patient lymphoblasts after an immunologically relevant challenge.

Mitochondrial transplantation partially restores deficient mitochondrial respiration in P1 lymphoblasts

Heterologous healthy mitochondria have been shown to provide therapeutic benefit during mitochondrial bioenergetic deficits [27]. Mitochondria were isolated from HEK293 cells expressing a cytochrome c-turbo(t)GFP protein and transplanted by passive incubation overnight to the proband P1 lymphoblasts and to the age-matched control, C1. Mitochondrial function was assessed using a mitochondrial stress test (Fig. 5A). Notably, the transplanted mitochondria boosted the basal respiration (Fig. 5B), with a 70.2% increase in ATP synthesis in the proband (P1) and a 40.1% increase in control (C1) lymphoblasts over their respective non-transplanted controls (Fig. 5C). Although basal and ATP-linked respiration improved, proton leak, maximal respiration and the spare respiratory capacity were not significantly changed (Fig. 5D, E, F). Proton leak showed a small increase, and this may relate to the increased uptake of mitochondria in the patient cells. The total protein was recovered from the Seahorse plate and assessed to determine the success of mitochondrial uptake following detection of the tGFP signal. Fig. 5H indicates a robust increase in the tGFP signal in the C1 and P1 lymphoblasts that received the exogenous mitochondria (C1 + Mito, P1 + Mito) versus the controls (C1 Cont, P1 Cont). Amido black staining of the membrane indicated less protein overall in the P1 lanes, however, the P1 cells appeared to take up a similar amount of mitochondria to controls based on the intensity of the tGFP signal (Fig. 5H). Discussion

Here we report the clinical summary of an affected male and his father who bear the same missense alteration (p.Thr101Ile) in UQCRC1. The biochemical and respiratory evidence provided support the pathogenicity of this variant and strongly indicate that the variant effect may also underlie their somewhat variable clinical presentation. The position of the Thr101Ile on an outer loop of UQCRC1 does not interact directly with UQCRC2 and may not destabilize Complex III. The affected region appears to project towards the mitochondrial matrix. However, the loss of the Thr101 hydroxyl group upon substitution with Ile likely disrupts hydrogen bonds that stabilize adjacent helices in the secondary structure. Interestingly, the cryo-EM resolution of the human respiratory chain megacomplex-I2III2IV2 (MCI2III2IV2) revealed that a separate surface loop of UQCRC1 is inserted into the groove formed by the supernumerary subunits NDUFB9 and NDUFAB1 of Complex I [14], and this was also documented for the interaction of porcine UQCRC1 with Complex I NDUFB9 [28]. Therefore, disruption of surface loops of UQCRC1 may disrupt proximal secondary structure and the assembly of Complex III into respiratory-competent supercomplexes.

Despite bearing the same variant in UQCRC1, the proband and his father exhibited differences in disease severity, with the latter not subject to the same pain and sensitivity as his son. We cannot rule out a possible additive contribution of his maternally inherited CACNA1S variant, although the allele frequency of this variant is suggestive of a benign effect. Fig. 3 demonstrated that while oxidative respiration was impaired in both patients compared to their respective controls, the decline was more exaggerated in the proband. In particular, the father had a significantly improved response to mild uncoupling, indicating that the maximal response to a mitochondrial stressor was better tolerated. This was also evident in the glycolytic reserve in response to an immunological stressor, the father's lymphoblasts demonstrated improved metabolic flexibility. The father's employment encompasses substantial physical activity, which may have resulted in higher overall mitochondrial output and function over time. Many reports underscore the benefits of exercise for mitochondrial disease [29], [30]. Other factors such as genetic modifiers and/or environmental exposures can also contribute to the variable clinical severity.

Our mitochondrial transplantation experiments demonstrated that transfer of healthy human mitochondria into the lymphoblasts of the proband resulted in improved respiration, restoring basal respiration and ATP synthesis to the levels measured in the wild-type control cells (Fig. 5B,C). Interestingly, we did not see an increase in the maximal respiration or spare capacity upon mild uncoupling (Fig. 5E,F). The heterologous mitochondria were derived from a GFP-labeled cytochrome c HEK293, so it is possible that the fluorescent tag impacts the rate of electron loading or release, or transfer of mobile cytochrome c between CIII and CIV during a metabolic challenge. However, under basal conditions, the mitochondria added compensated for the respiratory deficit in the proband's mitochondria. These in vitro experiments allow confirmation that transplanted mitochondria do confer a bioenergetic benefit to the acceptor cells, which is important to move the field of mitochondrial transplantation forward [13].

As with many primary mitochondrial defects, the exact mechanisms whereby loss-of-function variants in ETC complex subunits like UQCRC1 and UQCRC2 cause neurological disease are still not fully appreciated. In many disorders, including adult neurodegenerative diseases, the lack of sufficient energy production in neurons is thought to trigger cell death and eventual loss of neural cells. UQCRC1 has reported functions in apoptosis, and is associated with cytochrome c, whose release into the cytosol is a well-known apoptotic trigger. UQCRC1 deficiency was shown to cause enhanced cytosolic release of cytochrome c, leading to increased caspase activation [31]. Based on this mechanism of action, it is possible that compounds that block cytochrome c release into the cytosol, such as melatonin [32], [33], may represent a therapeutic strategy for patients with UQCRC1 deficiency. Separately, the use of phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5) inhibitors has been self-reported to improve mixed mitochondrial disease symptoms in several patients. PDE5 inhibitors improve respiratory parameters in patient fibroblasts that exhibited a ‘hypermetabolic’ phenotype [34]. A hypermetabolic phenotype exhibits elevated respiratory parameters consuming excess energy relative to control cells, which is believed to be the result of maladaptive stress responses [35]. This differs from the hypometabolic phenotype that we describe in the patient cells here, however, PDE5 inhibitors also showed benefit in survival in the NDUFS4 knockout mouse model of Leigh Syndrome that shows a respiratory oxidative deficit similar to the proband [36]. Therefore, given the established safety profile of PDE5 inhibitors, these may be considered.

As with variants found in genes involved in lysosomal function, there is growing evidence that similar variants within mitochondrial genes can contribute to the onset and progression of neurodegenerative disorders. Both UQCRC1 and UQCRC2 protein-truncating variants have been proposed to be enriched in patients with neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, presumably acting to compromise mitochondrial function and working in concert with other genetic and/or environmental factors to trigger neuronal dysfunction or death [37]. This possibility raises the challenge of following and counseling those that harbor these variants regarding their risk for adult-onset neurodegeneration in addition to their primary symptoms.
