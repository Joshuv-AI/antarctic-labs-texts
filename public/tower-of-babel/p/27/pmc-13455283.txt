Benchmarking reveals the superiority of nucleic acid foundation models in predicting lncRNA coding potential
Authors: Yu Yang, Liping Ren, Juan Feng, Yang Zhang, Tianyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA subset of long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) contains short open reading frames and can encode functional micropeptides. However, identifying these coding lncRNAs (codlncRNAs) remains challenging due to weak coding signals, short peptide products, and heterogeneous evidence across databases. Existing computational tools lack unified benchmarks, and the utility of nucleic acid foundation models for this task remains unclear.ResultsWe construct the first multi-species, evidence-stratified benchmark for codlncRNA prediction and systematically characterized codlncRNAs across molecular dimensions. CodlncRNAs consistently exhibited transitional features between mRNAs and untranslated lncRNAs in sequence, structural, and physicochemical properties. Using this benchmark, we evaluate 12 classical tools and 4 foundation models. Classical methods show limited zero-shot performance, whereas RNA-FM, RiNALMo, and DNABERT-2 achieve substantial gains after fine-tuning. Notably, DNABERT-2, trained solely on DNA, performs competitively or even superior to RNA-specific models. An ensemble framework integrating foundation and classical models further improves robustness and has been deployed as an accessible web server.ConclusionsOur study establishes the first benchmark for codlncRNA prediction, delineates their distinctive transitional molecular profile, and supports the utility of nucleic acid foundation models for codlncRNA prediction within the current benchmark scope. Moreover, the proposed framework provides a practical, scalable computational foundation for micropeptide discovery and RNA functional characterization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04134-7.

Background

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) were traditionally defined as noncoding regulatory transcripts that participate in chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and RNA–protein complex formation, thereby influencing gene expression homeostasis and cell-fate determination [1]. One of the earliest discoveries was the 2002 discovery of the plant lncRNA ENOD40, which translates biologically active peptides involved in sucrose metabolism [2]. Subsequently, advancements in ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq) and mass spectrometry (MS) uncovered such translation in transcriptome-wide detection [3–5]. This technological leap catalyzed a series of pioneering pre-2020 discoveries demonstrating that lncRNA-encoded micropeptides act as vital functional entities in diverse physiological contexts. Seminal discoveries include Toddler in embryonic signaling [6], MLN [7] and DWORF [8] in muscle physiology, Mtln in mitochondrial regulation [9], and Aw112010 in mucosal immunity [10]. These foundational studies suggest that some lncRNAs contain short open reading frames (sORFs) that can translate into micropeptides or microproteins.

Micropeptides translated from lncRNAs exhibit features that parallel those of emerging protein-coding genes, including characteristic sequence signatures and early-stage evolutionary conservation patterns [11, 12]. Beyond their evolutionary relevance, accumulating evidence shows that these micropeptides participate in a wide range of biological processes, including muscle development, immune regulation, metabolic homeostasis, tumor progression, and cellular reprogramming, demonstrating that they are not merely translational by-products but functional molecular entities [13–15]. These findings collectively point to a vast and largely uncharted “micropeptidome” hidden within noncoding regions of the transcriptome [11, 16]. Consequently, systematically identifying and characterizing lncRNAs with translational potential (hereafter referred to as codlncRNAs) and their encoded products is essential not only for expanding the conceptual boundaries of RNA biology but also for enabling the discovery of new biomarkers, immunogenic neoantigens, and therapeutic targets [14, 17, 18].

Recent advances in Ribo-seq, MS, and integrative proteogenomics have greatly facilitated the detection of lncRNA translation events [19–21]. High-throughput CRISPR/Cas9 and CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screens have further uncovered roles of lncRNA-derived micropeptides in cell proliferation, signaling, and stress responses [22, 23]. Moreover, single-cell perturbation technologies such as Perturb-seq are pushing micropeptide research toward greater resolution and quantitative depth [23]. Despite these advances, existing experimental approaches each have inherent limitations: Ribo-seq can be confounded by ribosome-occupancy artifacts, MS has limited sensitivity for detecting very short peptides, and CRISPR screens may suffer from off-target effects and cell-type specificity. As a result, identifying high-confidence codlncRNAs at scale and across biological contexts remains a major unresolved challenge.

In parallel, computational approaches have become a key driver of codlncRNA research. Early methods such as PhyloCSF [24] and sORF Finder [25] rely on comparative genomics and conserved sequence features, whereas machine-learning tools such as CPC [26] and CPAT [27] incorporate sequence statistics to improve coding-potential prediction. Deep learning models have further enhanced the ability to learn complex sequence patterns and long-range dependencies, as exemplified by the transformer-based LncReader model we previously developed [28]. Meanwhile, the continued expansion of codlncRNA resources, from cncRNAdb [29] to SPENCER [30], TransLnc [31], LncPepAtlas [32], and the high-confidence codLncDB [33], has enriched the evidence base for codlncRNA discovery. Notably, RNA foundation models have rapidly emerged and achieved state-of-the-art performance (SOTA) across diverse RNA-related tasks, including secondary-structure prediction and RNA–protein interaction modeling [34–37]. Motivated by these gaps, we systematically integrated and standardized multiple resources to construct a codlncRNA benchmark dataset stratified into 4 confidence levels. Using this dataset, we conducted the first hierarchical, cross-species evaluation of 12 published models and 4 nucleic acid foundation models (3 RNA models and 1 DNA model). By comparing zero-shot performance and task-specific fine-tuning, we demonstrate the advantages of foundation models in identifying lncRNAs with subtle coding potential and exhibit favorable transferable potential. Furthermore, leveraging ensemble learning, we integrated classical models with foundation models to build a highly accurate codlncRNA prediction framework, which we deployed as a publicly accessible web server to support the broader codlncRNA research community. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive performance landscape of existing codlncRNA prediction tools, highlights the promise of nucleic acid foundation models for codlncRNA inference, and establishes a scalable computational paradigm for future micropeptide discovery and RNA characterization.

Results

Overview of the benchmarking framework

To enable a systematic and reproducible assessment of codlncRNA prediction, we developed a multi-stage analytical framework comprising 3 major components (Fig. 1). First, we constructed a comprehensive, multi-species codlncRNA resource stratified into 4 confidence levels, providing a unified benchmark resource for downstream model evaluation (Fig. 1A, Additional file 1: Fig. S1). CodlncRNAs were collected from 5 public resources across 6 model organisms (human, mouse, rat, Arabidopsis, fruit fly and zebrafish). Based on evidence strength, we defined four confidence levels: Dataset 1 contains high-confidence sequences supported by low-throughput experimental validation; Dataset 2 represents medium-confidence entries with diverse but heterogeneous evidence types; Dataset 3 includes sequences jointly supported by Ribo-seq and MS, representing medium–low confidence; Dataset 4 provides the broadest coverage but relies solely on large-scale MS reanalysis, thus representing the lowest-confidence evidence. Together, these datasets form a structured evaluation hierarchy that enables rigorous testing of model robustness and transferability across evidence strengths. In parallel, we performed a systematic characterization of codlncRNAs across 3 biological dimensions relevant to translational potential: (i) RNA element features, including ORF length, ORF coverage, and internal ribosome entry site (IRES) probability; (ii) RNA structure and modification features, including GC content, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) probability, and minimum free energy (MFE); and (iii) physicochemical properties, including signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), Signal Q2, and isoelectric point (pI). Next, we performed a systematic evaluation of 12 classical methods and 4 nucleic acid foundation models (Fig. 1B). To establish a unified, cross-model comparison, we assessed each model from 3 complementary perspectives: (i) zero-shot inference, reflecting intrinsic representation quality without task-specific training; (ii) task-specific fine-tuning, quantifying adaptation to codlncRNA prediction; and (iii) cross-species transferability, evaluating performance across species using heterogeneous training–testing combinations. All comparisons used statistical tests to ensure reproducibility and significance.

Finally, to overcome the limitations of individual models in robustness, we developed CodLncEnPred, an ensemble framework integrating 10 complementary base models covering both traditional methods and nucleic acid foundation models (Fig. 1C). The ensemble strategy could integrate their distinct sequence-, structure-, and representation-level strengths. To interpret the contribution of each component, we applied SHAP analysis and stepwise ablation to quantify the base model’s importance. We further implemented CodLncEnPred as an online, user-accessible web server, providing a high-accuracy and scalable codlncRNA prediction tool for diverse research scenarios.

Cross-dimensional feature landscape of codlncRNAs

To systematically compare mRNAs, codlncRNAs, and untranslated lncRNAs, we evaluated 9 features across 3 biological dimensions. These dimensions were chosen to capture complementary aspects of translational potential, including ORF-related sequence organization [38] and initiation-related elements [39], RNA structural [40, 41] and modification states [42] associated with stability and translation, and physicochemical properties linked to coding-associated characteristics [43, 44]. The overall landscape reveals that codlncRNAs consistently exhibit intermediate distributions across all features, falling between mRNAs and untranslated lncRNAs (Fig. 2, Additional file 1: Fig. S2). This pattern is highly stable across functional, structural, and physicochemical dimensions. For RNA element features, codlncRNAs displayed ORF length and ORF coverage values that were significantly smaller than those of mRNAs but markedly higher than those of untranslated lncRNAs (Fig. 2A, B). This indicates that codlncRNAs retain partial coding-like structural organization despite not being canonical protein-coding genes. A similar trend was observed for IRES, with codlncRNAs again occupying an intermediate position, suggesting modest cap-independent translation initiation potential (Fig. 2C). For RNA structure and modification features, codlncRNAs also exhibited a clear gradient pattern (Fig. 2D-F). m6A probability was highest in mRNAs, lowest in untranslated lncRNAs, and intermediate in codlncRNAs, consistent with the known roles of m6A in translation initiation and RNA stability. GC content and MFE followed the same trend: mRNAs showed the highest GC content and most stable secondary structures, untranslated lncRNAs the lowest, and codlncRNAs consistently in between. For physicochemical properties, both SNR and Signal Q2, which are indicators of canonical coding-related periodicity signals, showed intermediate values in codlncRNAs, supporting the existence of residual coding signatures (Fig. 2G-H). Interestingly, pI exhibited an inverse pattern, with untranslated lncRNAs showing the highest values, mRNAs the lowest, and codlncRNAs again between the two groups (Fig. 2I). This likely reflects differences in the physicochemical characteristics of putative peptides associated with each RNA class. In addition, we performed a targeted case-study analysis using 10 experimentally validated codlncRNAs with reported translational signal. In these cases, model-derived importance signals from several models, particularly DNABERT-2 and RiNALMo, showed positive correspondence with annotated ORF regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).Fig. 2Comparative profiling of nine features across three RNA categories. Taken together, codlncRNAs exhibit a highly consistent, cross-dimensional transitional molecular profile, sharing partial sequence, structural, and physicochemical features with mRNAs while remaining distinct from untranslated lncRNAs. This intermediate pattern aligns with their known weak coding potential and supports the notion that codlncRNAs represent a distinct and functionally meaningful class positioned between coding and noncoding transcripts [11, 45]. However, these observations are primarily correlative and insufficient to establish a causal relationship with translational activity, since it could be influenced by current annotation and discovery biases, including ORF-centered identification pipelines.

Zero-shot performance of classical models

We first evaluated the zero-shot performance of 12 classical methods across 4 codlncRNA datasets with decreasing evidence confidence. For each dataset, codlncRNAs were treated as positive samples, and an equal number of negative samples were randomly selected from untranslated lncRNAs in LNCipedia to ensure balanced test sets. Without any retraining or parameter adjustment, we applied each tool in its official implementation and computed 5 metrics: Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), accuracy, F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), to obtain a comprehensive view of its zero-shot capabilities.

Across these datasets, the 12 methods displayed pronounced heterogeneity (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). As summarized in the heatmap (Fig. 3A), ABLNCPP achieved the highest Accuracy and F1-score, indicating strong threshold-based discrimination, whereas CPAT obtained the best AUROC and AUPRC, reflecting more reliable probability ranking across decision thresholds. In contrast, CNCI consistently ranked among the lowest across metrics, highlighting weak generalizability when applied directly to codlncRNAs without retraining. To quantify ranking differences across metrics, we performed a Friedman rank test (Fig. 3B-F). Four metrics, F1-score (p = 4.36 × 10⁻4), AUROC (p = 1.15 × 10⁻3), AUPRC (p = 4.24 × 10⁻3), and Accuracy (p = 1.73 × 10⁻2), showed statistically significant differences among models, whereas MCC did not (p = 0.137). These results were consistent with the observed performance landscape: tools such as ABLNCPP, LncReader, and mRNN, which ranked highly in F1-score, were more effective at balancing precision and recall at fixed thresholds; tools like CPAT and CPPred, despite weaker threshold-dependent metrics, yielded higher AUROC and AUPRC values, indicating strong probability ordering that default cutoffs fail to capture. Although the overall ranking significance of MCC was limited, the absolute performance differences between some pairs of algorithms remained substantial (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Overall, these results demonstrate that classical methods exhibit limited and highly heterogeneous zero-shot generalizability across codlncRNA datasets with varying evidence levels. Fine-tuning performance of nucleic acid foundation models

Given the substantial differences in evidence sources and sample sizes across the 4 codlncRNA datasets, directly training models on individual-level datasets would lead to underfitting and instability. We merged evidence tiers during training mainly for practical reasons, as the highest-confidence tier alone was too small for stable model training. All models were evaluated using fivefold cross-validation to ensure reliable and comparable performance estimates. The result showed that models trained on the merged dataset generally showed more favorable overall performance (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). For 4 nucleic acid foundation models, we applied a two-stage progressive fine-tuning strategy. In Stage I (frozen encoder), all encoder layers were kept fixed while a fully connected classification head was trained. In Stage II (layer-wise unfreezing), we initialized from the best Stage I checkpoint and progressively unfroze encoder layers from top to bottom. Model performance was continuously monitored using validation AUROC to determine the optimal unfreezing depth automatically. Across models, RNA-FM, DNABERT-2, and RiNALMo achieved strong baseline performance even in the fully frozen state and exhibited further AUROC improvements after unfreezing several top layers, reaching model-specific performance peaks. Beyond these optimal depths, additional unfreezing produced marginal gains or slight declines, most notably in RNA-FM, DNABERT-2, and RiNALMo, indicating that excessive unfreezing may introduce task-specific noise and weaken pretrained representations. RNABERT showed the lowest overall performance and minimal improvement during fine-tuning, likely reflecting its substantially smaller pretraining corpus, parameter count, and embedding dimensionality. We next compared Stage I and Stage II performance with the retrained versions of six classical tools that support supervised training (Fig. 4E-I). All foundation models benefited from Stage II fine-tuning, with RiNALMo, DNABERT-2, and RNA-FM consistently ranking highest across AUROC, AUPR, F1-score, and MCC. This indicates that their pretrained representations are highly transferable and can be effectively refined through moderate fine-tuning. Although classical methods also improved markedly relative to their zero-shot performance, they remained inferior to the top foundation models. Among them, RNAsamba and ABLNCPP showed the most substantial gains, surpassing LncReader after retraining and indicating that deep-learning architectures can better leverage the expanded training set to capture weak coding signals. In contrast, CPAT, IRSOM, and mRNN showed reduced AUROC after retraining, with mRNN exhibiting the most pronounced degradation, suggesting sensitivity to distributional shifts and limited robustness to cross-domain adaptation.

Cross-species evaluation of models

To assess the transferable potential of different methods across species, we evaluated their performance on codlncRNA datasets derived from 5 non-human. For each species, codlncRNAs were used as positive samples, whereas an equal number of untranslated lncRNAs were randomly sampled from LNCipedia as negatives, yielding 5 balanced cross-species test sets. Importantly, all models were evaluated in their “warm-start” state, using the final weights obtained after Stage II fine-tuning (foundation models) or retraining (classical methods) on human data. This design ensures that performance differences directly reflect the intrinsic cross-species transferability of each model. Model performance was quantified using five metrics (AUROC, AUPR, accuracy, F1-score, MCC), and a min–max normalized composite score was computed to summarize cross-metric behavior for each species (Additional file 1: Fig. S8).

Overall, nucleic acid foundation models showed relatively favorable performance across species, though their advantages were not universal across all organisms (Fig. 5). DNABERT-2 achieved the highest or near-highest composite score across all species. This is consistent with its pretraining on large-scale genomic sequences, which may enable it to capture conserved regulatory and compositional patterns shared across distant taxa. RiNALMo and RNA-FM also performed strongly in the mammalian datasets (mouse and zebrafish), indicating that large-scale RNA-based pretraining is particularly effective when the target species is evolutionarily closer to the pretraining domain. Classical methods showed more heterogeneous behavior but remained competitive. For example, RNAsamba achieved the highest composite score on the rat dataset, suggesting that engineered features combined with neural architectures can still be advantageous under specific cross-species sequence distributions. Ensemble learning performance and model analysis

To integrate complementary information from heterogeneous methods, we constructed a multi-model ensemble framework using a random forest as the meta-learner (Fig. 6A). 6 retrainable classical methods (CPAT, IRSOM, mRNN, RNAsamba, LncReader, ABLNCPP) and 4 fine-tuned nucleic acid foundation models (RNABERT, RNA-FM, DNABERT-2, RiNALMo) were included as base models. For each transcript, the predicted probabilities from all base models were aggregated into a multi-model probability matrix, which was then used as input to the random forest for final classification. Web server construction and implementation

To facilitate broad and convenient access to our ensemble prediction framework, we deployed all components as an online web service, called CodLncEnPred. The platform follows a decoupled front-end/back-end architecture: the user interface is implemented with HTML/CSS/JavaScript, while the back end is powered by PHP and hosted on a laboratory-managed Linux server. To ensure full consistency with the benchmarked offline results, all 10 base models (6 retrained classical methods and 4 fine-tuned foundation models) were retrained on the complete training dataset and deployed alongside the final random forest meta-learner. Consequently, predictions generated through the web interface are identical to those reported in this study. Specifically, users can submit sequences in two formats: (1) pasting RNA sequences in FASTA format (Fig. 7A); (2) uploading the FASTA file (Fig. 7B). Upon submission, the server sequentially queries all base models, returns their predicted probabilities, and also provides the prediction probabilities generated by the meta-learner (Fig. 7C). Users can optionally specify custom probability thresholds for their outputs. Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized the molecular features of codlncRNAs and benchmarked a broad spectrum of computational models, establishing the first multi-level framework for codlncRNA prediction. Our comparative analyses revealed that codlncRNAs consistently exhibit a transitional molecular profile across key dimensions, including RNA elements, RNA structure, RNA modification, and physicochemical properties. For nearly all features examined, codlncRNAs occupy an intermediate distribution between mRNAs and untranslated lncRNAs. This cross-dimensional intermediate state indicates that codlncRNAs retain some features like mature mRNAs, yet lack the full coding architecture typical of canonical protein-coding genes. Such a stable transitional signature supports the view that codlncRNAs reside between coding and noncoding categories, and may represent a subset of transcripts that have not fully diverged along the RNA evolutionary continuum or that preserve vestigial translational capacity [46, 47].

By constructing a benchmark spanning multiple confidence levels and species, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of 12 published prediction tools and 4 nucleic acid foundation models. Under zero-shot conditions, classical machine-learning and earlier deep-learning methods showed limited performance, suggesting that these models are primarily tuned to detect strong, canonical coding signals characteristic of mRNAs and therefore struggle with short ORFs, weak coding signatures, or the higher structural noise inherent to lncRNAs. Although some deep-learning methods, such as RNAsamba and ABLNCPP, achieved modest improvements on specific metrics, their overall performance remained strongly dependent on the retraining dataset. In contrast, nucleic acid foundation models demonstrated clear advantages when paired with either a zero-shot or fine-tuning strategy. RNA-FM, RiNALMo, and DNABERT-2 showed relatively favorable performance across several metrics and species within the current benchmark, suggesting that the ability of large-scale self-supervised pretraining may help capture transferable features. RNABERT performed comparatively poorly, likely reflecting its smaller pretraining corpus, reduced model capacity, and more limited sequence diversity, highlighting the importance of data scale and model size in the development of nucleic acid foundation models [48]. Notably, DNABERT-2, despite being trained exclusively on DNA sequences, performed competitively or even surpassed RNA-specific models in several settings, including cross-species prediction. This observation suggests that translational potential might be influenced not only by RNA-intrinsic features but also by upstream DNA elements, conserved regulatory sequences, or broader genomic context [49]. Although we cannot directly validate such mechanisms here, these findings indicate that future large models may benefit from integrating multi-layer regulatory information, moving beyond RNA-only representations toward more holistic DNA-RNA modeling. Our ensemble analyses further demonstrate that no single model fully captures the diverse feature space of codlncRNAs. Foundation models contribute high-dimensional abstract representations, whereas classical tools provide explicit, biologically informed features that remain informative in certain difficult-to-classify cases. When both are integrated, these complementary strengths led to substantial and more stable performance improvements. SHAP explainability analyses corroborated this complementarity and revealed model-specific contributions across samples. In addition, although the negative set in this study was sourced from the high-confidence subset of LNCipedia, negative-set purity remains an inherent challenge in codlncRNA prediction. Because some transcripts classified as untranslated lncRNAs may in fact encode peptides but have not yet been experimentally validated. however, our robustness analysis suggests that the main benchmarking conclusions are not highly sensitive to a modest degree of such uncertainty (Additional file 1: Fig. S9).

Despite establishing the most comprehensive codlncRNA benchmark to date, several limitations remain. First, experimentally validated high-confidence codlncRNAs are still scarce compared with the abundance of annotated mRNAs, which may restrict models’ ability to learn rare patterns fully. Second, both Ribo-seq and MS data contain experimental noise, and short peptides are inherently challenging to detect, potentially introducing labeling errors. Third, although our cross-dimensional feature analyses revealed a transitional molecular profile of codlncRNAs and the added case-study interpretability analyses, these observations remain primarily correlative and do not establish a direct causal relationship between the identified features and translational activity. Fourth, the performance of nucleic acid foundation models remains constrained by pretraining corpus size, tokenization design, and architectural choices, reflecting the field’s early stage of development. Finally, our study focuses on translational potential rather than translation efficiency, condition-specific translation, RNA structural dynamics, or RNA–protein interactions, all of which represent important future directions.

Looking forward, we anticipate that next-generation multimodal foundation models integrating DNA, RNA, Ribo-seq, and MS signals will enable more mechanistic and biologically grounded prediction. Coupling such models with wet experimental platforms will facilitate a shift from statistical classification toward mechanistic interpretation of codlncRNA function. More systematic interpretability analysis, together with independent translational evidence and experimental validation, will also be important for determining whether model-derived signals truly reflect biologically functional coding regions. Overall, this study provides the first systematic benchmark for codlncRNA prediction, demonstrates the potential of nucleic acid foundation models for RNA translational inference, and offers a scalable computational framework to support micropeptide discovery and RNA functional characterization.

Conclusions

In this study, we present the first systematic and multi-level benchmark for predicting lncRNAs with translational potential, addressing a major gap in the evaluation of codlncRNA prediction methods. By integrating multi-species data across four evidence levels, we show that codlncRNAs exhibit a consistent transitional molecular state between mRNAs and untranslated lncRNAs, reflecting residual coding capacity and a unique position along the coding-noncoding system. Our benchmarking demonstrates that nucleic acid foundation models, particularly RNA-FM, RiNALMo, and DNABERT-2, substantially outperform classical machine-learning and deep-learning tools in zero-shot prediction and supervised fine-tuning. Finally, we introduce CodLncEnPred, an ensemble method and public web server that integrates foundation-model representations with classical features, providing a robust and accessible resource for the community. Together, these findings highlight the power of nucleic acid foundation models for decoding translation signals in the noncoding transcriptome and provide a scalable computational framework to accelerate micropeptide discovery, functional annotation, and future applications of large language models in genome analysis.

Progressive fine-tuning strategy for foundation models

All foundation model pretrained weights used in this study were obtained from the official Hugging Face repositories. The three RNA foundation models, RiNALMo, RNA-FM, and RNABERT, rely on the Multi-molecule library in their official implementations to perform unified tokenization and embedding construction for nucleic acid sequences. As a general-purpose encoding framework for nucleic acid and protein macromolecules, Multi-molecule provides a stable and consistent backbone for training heterogeneous foundation models within a unified workflow. To maximize the transferability of pretrained knowledge while avoiding catastrophic forgetting during task adaptation, we designed a two-stage progressive freeze–unfreeze fine-tuning strategy.

Stage 1: fully frozen adaptation

In the first stage, the transformer encoder of each foundation model was completely frozen, and a randomly initialized fully connected classification head was added on top. The classifier was trained using cross-entropy loss with a relatively high learning rate to rapidly adapt to the task-specific distribution. Model checkpoints were monitored using AUROC on the validation set, and the best-performing checkpoint was selected via early stopping. The purpose of this stage was to allow the classification head to learn task boundaries within a fixed pretrained representation space, thereby establishing a stable baseline before deeper structural fine-tuning.

Stage 2: layer-wise unfreezing with progressive search

In the second stage, we initialized training from the optimal checkpoint obtained in Stage 1 and performed a systematic layer-wise unfreezing search in a top-down manner. Specifically, transformer layers were incrementally unfrozen from shallow to deeper layers. For each unfreezing depth, the classification head was re-initialized and trained independently. A strict performance criterion was applied to determine the optimal unfreezing range: the search was terminated if (i) AUROC improvements across successive layers failed to exceed a predefined minimum improvement threshold, or (ii) performance degradation indicated that the unfreezing depth had surpassed the beneficial inflection point. This progressive strategy avoids the instability and overfitting risks commonly associated with full-model fine-tuning, while enabling the model to gradually incorporate task-specific sequence features without compromising its pretrained representational knowledge.

Ensemble learning framework and ablation strategy

The ensemble framework employs a random forest classifier as the meta-learner, integrating six retrainable classical models and with four foundation models. This design aims to maximize representational diversity and information gain across heterogeneous model types. For classical tools, our main inclusion criterion was whether the tool provides an accessible retraining interface and supports training on custom datasets. For the foundation models, we retained all four models, even RNABERT had weaker performance. As a result, the ensemble model incorporated ten base predictors, including six classical tools (CPAT, IRSOM, mRNN, RNAsamba, LncReader, and ABLNCPP) and four foundation models (RNABERT, RNA-FM, DNABERT-2, and RiNALMo).

For each base model, the predicted probability for each sample was treated as an independent feature, forming a model-level prediction matrix. This formulation enables the meta-learner to capture the decision preferences and discriminative patterns of individual models, thereby exploiting complementarities in how different algorithms process various lncRNA sequences. To systematically construct and tune the ensemble, we employed a randomized search strategy to optimize the hyperparameter space (spanning parameters such as n_estimators from 100 to 600, max_depth from 6 to 16, and min_samples_leaf from 1 to 5). The final optimized random forest meta-learner consisted of 600 decision trees (n_estimators = 600) with a maximum depth of 12 (max_depth = 12) and employed the square-root feature subsampling rule (max_features = “sqrt”) to enhance generalization and reduce overfitting. Class imbalance was addressed by utilizing balanced class weights (class_weight = “balanced”).To rigorously evaluate the ensemble without risking data leakage, model training and validation were performed using a five-fold stratified cross-validation strategy combined with Out-Of-Fold (OOF) probability generation. In this setup, the meta-learner was iteratively trained on four folds and generated independent probability scores for the unseen fifth fold. These concatenated OOF predictions were subsequently used to compute comprehensive evaluation metrics, including Accuracy, F1-score, AUROC, AUPRC, and MCC. This rigorous evaluation mechanism ensures unbiased, consistent performance estimation across all data splits.

Progressive ablation analysis

To quantify the marginal contribution of each base model to the ensemble, we implemented a progressive ablation strategy. We first constructed a baseline ensemble containing only the six classical models. Subsequently, the four foundation models were added one by one in ascending order of their single-model AUROC performance—RNABERT, RNA-FM, DNABERT-2, and RiNALMo. This allowed us to systematically examine the performance gain associated with each foundation model and identify the optimal combination strategy.

Model interpretability

To further elucidate the roles played by different base models within the ensemble, we applied SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [66] to the final meta-model. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each prediction feature (i.e., the probability output of each base model) to the ensemble’s final decision, enabling us to uncover cooperative relationships among models and interpret their sample-specific importance patterns.

Statistical test

To objectively and comprehensively assess model performance in codlncRNA classification, we established a multi-dimensional and hierarchical statistical evaluation framework. Five complementary performance metrics were used: AUC, AUPR, MCC, accuracy, and F1 score. These metrics collectively capture different aspects of predictive behavior, including global discriminative ability (AUC), robustness under positive-class sparsity (AUPR), performance under class imbalance (MCC), overall correctness (accuracy), and the precision–recall trade-off (F1). For pairwise comparisons of feature distributions, Welch’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U test was employed to assess differences in group means.

Case-study interpretability analysis of codlncRNAs

To assess whether the computational models captured biologically meaningful coding regions, we performed a targeted interpretability analysis on 10 high-confidence, experimentally validated codlncRNAs. For each transcript, the full-length RNA sequence and its experimentally verified ORF were retrieved to serve as the ground truth. For the fine-tuned transformer-based foundation models (e.g., DNABERT-2, RiNALMo, RNA-FM, and RNABERT), we utilized the self-attention mechanism to quantify sequence importance at a nucleotide-level resolution. For the baseline Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, nucleotide-level attribution scores were analogously derived using the Integrated Gradients (IG) algorithm and subjected to the same statistical evaluation pipeline. We then computed the point-biserial correlation coefficient and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between the normalized attention scores and the ground-truth.
