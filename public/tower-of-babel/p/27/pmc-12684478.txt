Comparative Mortality in Heart Failure on Guideline‐Directed Medical Therapy Versus Malignant Cancer: A Report From a Global Federated Research Network
Authors: Luca Monzo, Guillaume Baudry, Kevin Duarte, Gema Hernandez, Olivier Denquin, Gianluigi Savarese, Nicolas Girerd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT), including angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors, has significantly improved outcomes in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). We examined whether modern GDMT alters the historically observed parity in mortality between HF and cancer.MethodsFrom the TriNetX Global Collaborative Research Network (2015–2024), we identified 32 125 patients with HFrEF on GDMT (angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor or angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, beta blockers, and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors), 403 389 not on full GDMT (not receiving all 4 drugs concurrently), and 2 481 106 with malignant cancers. All‐cause mortality was compared between HFrEF and cancer overall and across the most common cancers in men (prostate, colorectal, lung) and women (breast, lung, colorectal) using propensity score matching.ResultsPatients with HFrEF on GDMT with angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors had significantly better survival than patients with cancer (hazard ratio [HR], 0.44 [95% CI, 0.41–0.47]; P<0.001). A survival benefit, though less pronounced, was also seen for GDMT with angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors instead of angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor (HR, 0.62 [95% CI, 0.57–0.69]; P<0.001). Patients not on GDMT had higher mortality than patients with cancer (HR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.13–1.17]; P<0.001). Among the most common sex‐specific cancers, only patients with prostate and breast cancer had better survival than those with HFrEF on GDMT, whereas patients not on GDMT showed worse outcomes across most cancers, except lung cancer.ConclusionsIn this large real‐world analysis, patients with HFrEF on GDMT showed better survival than those with cancer overall and across most sex‐specific cancers. Patients not on GDMT experienced worse outcomes, emphasizing the need to optimize treatment in HFrEF.Keywords: cancer, guideline‐directed medical therapy, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, mortalitySubject Categories: Heart Failure, Cardio-Oncology

Clinical Perspectives

What Is New?

Patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction treated with all 4 pillars of contemporary guideline‐directed medical therapy (angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitor or angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, beta blockers, and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors) have significantly better overall survival than individuals with malignancies, both in general and across most frequent sex‐specific cancers. In contrast, those not receiving comprehensive GDMT had higher mortality than patients with malignant tumors.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Underscoring the transformative impact of comprehensive guideline‐directed medical therapy in improving survival for patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction serves as a compelling call to action to optimize the use of modern therapies in routine clinical practice.

Cardiovascular diseases and cancer are the leading contributors to morbidity and mortality worldwide, 1 with heart failure (HF) standing out as a major global health challenge within the spectrum of cardiovascular conditions. 2 Although the incidence of HF has stabilized or declined in high‐income countries over the past decade, its prevalence continues to rise due to population aging, increasing cardiovascular risk factors, and improved survival. 3 , 4

Comparative analyses of mortality between HF and cancer have historically aimed to highlight HF as a life‐threatening condition. A foundational study from the 1990s comparing patients with first hospitalization for HF to those with common sex‐specific cancers found that HF had similar or even worse 5‐year survival rate than most malignancies. 5 Similar findings were reported in a Swedish analysis of >1.1 million hospital admissions between 1998 and 2004. 6 A more recent Scottish study (2000–2011) involving 56 658 individuals showed that despite improvements in HF management, the condition remained as “malignant” as several of the most common cancers. 7 Nonetheless, HF is often perceived as less lethal than cancer, 8 a misconception that may hinder the implementation of life‐saving therapies.

Notably, over the past decade, the introduction of angiotensin‐receptor neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), alongside mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) and beta blockers, has substantially reduced mortality in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). 9 , 10 Treatment with these 4 drug classes, as part of modern guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT), 11 has the potential to shift the paradigm, challenging the previously observed similarity in mortality between HF and cancers.

This study leveraged real‐world data from a large global federated electronic health records (EHR) database to assess whether the previously observed equivalence in mortality between patients with HF and cancer still holds in the era of contemporary GDMT for HFrEF.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data used in this study are available on the TriNetX Research Network Database (trinetx.com; TriNetX LLC, Cambridge, MA). Access to TriNetX data requires submitting requests to TriNetX and a data‐sharing agreement.

Study Design and Data Derivation

We conducted a retrospective analysis using the TriNetX Global Collaborative Research Network Database (TriNetX LLC, Cambridge, MA), a global platform providing access to real‐world data derived from federated EHR systems and medical claims of nearly 150 million patients across >130 health care organizations worldwide.

This extensive federated database includes data from both academic and community‐based institutions, spanning inpatient and outpatient settings. Participating health care organizations contribute deidentified, aggregated data sets mapped to a master clinical terminology aligned with standardized coding ontologies, such as the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Current Procedural Terminology, and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes. 12 Rigorous quality controls, such as validation against clinical standards and expert review, ensure data reliability. 13 Through the TriNetX platform, researchers have real‐time access to longitudinal clinical data, covering demographics, diagnoses, procedures, laboratory results, and medication prescriptions sourced from electronic prescribing systems or pharmacy claims within the EHRs. TriNetX performs rigorous data preprocessing to minimize missing values, mapping data to a consistent clinical model that supports uniform querying across diverse sources. Covariates are standardized as binary, categorical (expanded into binary columns), or continuous. Age is always available, and missing values for sex, race, and ethnicity are represented as “unknown.” Given the use of only aggregate deidentified data, this study was exempted from institutional review board approval.

Study Population

During the identification period (October 31, 2015–October 31, 2024), a span deliberately restricted to nearly a decade to ensure alignment with contemporary medical treatment practices (ie, Food and Drug Administration approval of sacubitril‐valsartan in July 2015), we identified 926 322 patients with a first diagnosis of HFrEF and 2 694 668 patients with a first diagnosis of malignant cancer (data accessed on December 17, 2024). Patients with any prior or concurrent diagnosis codes for HF or malignant cancer were excluded. In the cohort with HF, the population was further refined to include only those actively receiving GDMT, defined as the concurrent use of an ARNI, MRA, beta blocker, and SGLT2i (overlapping prescriptions in EHR), within 6 months from the HF diagnosis (GDMT‐ARNI group). A sensitivity analysis was performed using angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) instead of ARNI, as this drug class is also recommended by guidelines in class IA (GDMT‐ACEi group). Patients not on GDMT were defined as those not concurrently treated with ARNI or ACEis, MRAs, beta blockers, and SGLT2is. Patients with severe chronic kidney disease (CKD; glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min per 1.73 m2) or on dialysis were excluded from the analysis. This restriction was applied because such patients are often undertreated with GDMT, 14 leading to underrepresentation in the group with HF. In contrast, these patients may be more prevalent in the cohort with cancer, potentially leading to a substantial imbalance in renal function and consequently in baseline mortality risk between groups, which would be difficult to mitigate even with propensity score matching (PSM).

Patients were further stratified by sex to enable outcome comparison in sex‐specific cancers. Specifically, the 3 most prevalent cancers worldwide in 2022 (according to the latest data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization) were selected for men (prostate, colorectal, and lung) and women (breast, lung, and colorectal). 15 , 16 The codes used to select the populations are listed in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted on the TriNetX online research platform, which enables patient‐level analyses while reporting only population‐level data through its built‐in analytical functions (ie, incidence, prevalence, outcomes, survival analysis, PSM).

Data are presented as mean±SD for continuous variables, and proportion (%) for categorical variables. PSM was performed on the TriNetX platform with 1:1 matching using logistic regression for groups with HFrEF versus malignant cancer, employing nearest‐neighbor matching with a tolerance of 0.01 and PSM caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs. Matching variables were selected based on their potential impact on all‐cause mortality and included age, sex, race (White, African American, Asian, Other), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, Not Hispanic or Latino), body mass index, hemoglobin levels, and relevant health conditions identified from EHRs, such as hypertensive disease, ischemic heart disease, CKD, previous ischemic stroke, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation or flutter (Table S2). 2 , 17 , 18 , 19 The strictly standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to quantify the differences between the 2 groups, both before and after statistical weighting.

The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality over a 5‐year follow‐up period, starting from HFrEF or malignant cancer diagnosis. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were calculated to compare the time‐to‐event outcome. Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests were used to illustrate survival analysis. Patients were censored from the study after their last record available in TriNetX if it happened during the observation time‐window. Statistical significance was set at a 2‐sided alpha <0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 32 125 patients with HFrEF on GDMT (22 091 in the GDMT‐ARNI cohort, and 10 034 in the GDMT‐ACEi cohort) and 2 481 106 patients with malignant cancers met the selection criteria and were included in the study. Among them, sex data were available for 21 279 individuals (15 161 men and 6118 women) in the GDMT‐ARNI cohort, 9665 individuals (6983 men and 2682 women) in the GDMT‐ACEi cohort, 420 402 individuals (261 848 men and 158 554 women) in the non‐GDMT cohort, and 2 405 562 individuals (1 124 921 men and 1 280 641 women) in the cohort with cancer (Figure S1).

Before PSM, patients with HFrEF in the GDMT‐ARNI cohort were younger, predominantly men, with a high prevalence of African American individuals and with more comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, CKD, atrial fibrillation) compared with those in the group with malignant cancer group. Following PSM, each group consisted of 20 986 patients, with all variables showing good balance (all SMDs ≤0.1) (Table 1). Similarly, within sex‐specific categories (comparing the 3 most incident cancers with HFrEF in men and women), PSM ensured that all demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics were comparable between the groups (Tables S3 and S4).

After PSM the group of patients with HFrEF receiving GDMT with ACEi inhibitors instead of ARNI (n=9977), as well as those not on GDMT (n=287 024), achieved good variables balancing with the subgroup with malignant cancer (Tables S5 and S6). Notably, in the non‐GDMT group, 58% of patients were on renin‐angiotensin system inhibitors (ACEis: 27%; angiotensin receptor blockers: 24.9%; ARNI: 6%), 14% on MRAs, 72% on beta blockers, and 5% on SGLT2is, with a mean of 1.5 drugs per patient.

Clinical Outcomes

After a median follow‐up of 14.8 months (interquartile range, 0.8–28.7), patients with HFrEF treated with GDMT‐ARNI exhibited a >50% reduction in the risk of all‐cause mortality compared with patients with any form of malignant cancer (HR, 0.44 [95% CI, 0.41–0.47]; P<0.001; 5‐year survival: HF 88% versus cancer 79%) (Figure 1A). In contrast, patients with HFrEF not receiving simultaneous treatment with all 4 pillars of GDMT demonstrated a significantly higher risk of all‐cause mortality compared with those with malignant cancer (HR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.13–1.17]; P<0.001; 5‐year survival: HF 71% versus cancer 76%) at a median follow‐up of 15.0 (interquartile range, 0–33) months (Figure 1B). Patients with HFrEF on GDMT with ACEis instead of ARNI also exhibited a reduced risk of all‐cause mortality compared with patients with malignant cancer, although less pronounced (HR, 0.62 [95% CI, 0.57–0.69]; P<0.001; 5‐year survival: HF 85% versus cancer 79%) (Figure S2). A graded, stepwise decline in survival benefit was observed as the number of GDMT drugs decreased (with the HR for mortality rising from 0.53 in patients receiving all 4 drugs simultaneously (ARNI or ACEi/MRA/beta blocker/SGLT2i) to 1.55 in those receiving none), compared with cancer (Figure S3).

Clinical Outcomes in Sex‐Specific Cancers

After PSM, male patients with HFrEF receiving GDMT‐ARNI had higher mortality compared with those with prostate cancer (n=10 194; HR, 2.13 [95% CI, 1.86–2.45]; P<0.001) but lower than patients with colorectal cancer (n=6705; HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.73–0.98]; P=0.029) or lung cancer (n=7213; HR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.23–0.30]; P<0.001). Among women, patients with HFrEF in the GDMT‐ARNI group showed higher mortality compared with those with breast cancer (n=5438; HR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.16–1.74]; P<0.001), whereas patients with colorectal cancer (n=3860; HR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.48–0.73]; P<0.001) or lung cancer (n=4428; HR, 0.28 [95% CI, 0.24–0.34]; P<0.001) fared worse (Figure 2).

Patients with HFrEF on GDMT‐ACEi showed a higher risk of all‐cause mortality at 5 years compared with patients with breast cancer in women (n=2572; HR, 2.18 [95% CI, 1.69–2.80]; P<0.001) and prostate cancer in men (n=5563; HR, 2.55 [95% CI, 2.16–3.00]; P<0.001), whereas they had a similar mortality compared with those with colorectal cancer (men, n=3984; HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 0.88–1.21], P=0.727; women, n=2024; HR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.70–1.12], P=0.292) and a better survival than lung cancer (men, n=4301; HR, 0.36 [95% CI, 0.32–0.42], P<0.001; women, n=2224; HR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.36–0.56], P<0.001), irrespective of sex. Conversely, patients with HFrEF not receiving GDMT consistently demonstrated a higher 5‐year mortality risk compared with those with prostate (n=82 656; HR, 3.96 [95% CI, 3.83–4.11]; P<0.001), breast (n=63 701; HR, 3.55 [95% CI, 3.41–3.70]; P<0.001), or colorectal cancer (men, n=33 005; HR, 1.76 [95% CI, 1.68–1.84], P<0.001; women, n=30 039; HR, 1.69 [95% CI, 1.60–1.77], P<0.001) but not compared wtih patients with lung cancer (men, n=31 748; HR, 0.64 [95% CI, 0.62–0.67], P<0.001; women, n=32 934; HR, 0.78 [95% CI, 0.75–0.81], P<0.001), although a trend toward a higher mortality risk was noted compared with patients on GDMT‐ACEis (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

In this large real‐world EHR‐based cohort analysis, we demonstrated that patients with HFrEF treated with all 4 pillars of contemporary GDMT (ARNI or ACEis, MRAs, beta blockers, and SGLT2is) had better overall survival than those with malignant cancers. Conversely, patients who did not receive comprehensive GDMT (ie, all 4 GDMT drugs concurrently) experienced higher overall mortality than patients with malignancies. Among the most common sex‐specific cancers, only prostate cancer in men and breast cancer in women showed better survival than patients with HFrEF on GDMT, whereas those not on GDMT had consistently higher mortality than patients with sex‐specific malignancies, except lung cancer. Notably, patients with HFrEF on GDMT‐ACEi showed intermediate survival, with lower rates than prostate and breast cancer, outcomes comparable to colorectal cancer, and better survival than lung cancer. However, although the overall survival gradient across treatment groups was consistent, the overlap and width of the CIs between GDMT‐ARNI and GDMT‐ACEi suggest some uncertainty in the precision of the effect estimates and preclude definite conclusions about the magnitude of the incremental benefit of 1 regimen over the other when compared with cancer survival.

Previous studies across various settings have shown that HF is associated with similar or worse survival than cancer. 5 , 6 , 7 However, these analyses had significant limitations. Trials conducted in acute settings often lacked adjustment for comorbidities, which are commonly higher in HF than in cancer and independently associated with increased mortality. Additionally, the exclusive focus on acutely hospitalized patients likely biased survival comparisons against HF. 5 , 6 A large primary care study in Scotland (2000–2011) also reported worse HF mortality compared with prostate and bladder cancers in men and breast cancer in women. 7 However, this study included mixed HF phenotypes with different prognosis, 20 low use of HF treatments (MRAs 7.2%; beta blockers 61.2%; ACEis 35.3%), and no patients on modern GDMT drugs (ARNI and SGLT2is), likely resulting in an outdated and inequitable comparison. Moreover, the analysis was limited to the 4 most common forms of sex‐specific cancer, precluding a comprehensive comparison of mortality between HF and all malignant cancers.

Although HFrEF is undoubtedly a highly lethal condition, 20 albeit with substantial differences in clinical phenotypes, 21 advancements in medical therapy have significantly improved outcomes. Foundational treatments such as ACEis, beta blockers, and MRAs 22 , 23 have been recently complemented by the introduction of ARNI and SGLT2is 9 , 10 , 24 as part of the modern GDMT. 11 , 25 A recent network meta‐analysis of 75 randomized controlled trials involving 95 444 participants showed that the combination of ARNI, beta‐blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2is provides the most substantial aggregate benefit, reducing all‐cause mortality by 61% compared with placebo, and potentially extending life expectancy by up to 5 years for a 70‐year‐old patient. 26 Similarly, a study conducted in the United States estimated that quadruple therapy could lower the mortality risk by ≈70%. 27 These findings are consistent with our results, which demonstrated a survival advantage in patients with HFrEF receiving GDMT‐ARNI compared with those not on GDMT. Notably, treatment patterns in our non‐GDMT cohort aligns with current real‐world data, with a mean of 1.5 drugs per patient. This is comparable to the CHAMP‐HF (Change the Management of Patients With Heart Failure) registry, which reported a mean of 1.7 drugs per patients among outpatients with chronic HFrEF 14 and exceeds the 1.2 drugs per patients observed in a Scottish primary care cohort comparing mortality between patients with cancer and HF. 7

Our study demonstrated that patients with HFrEF receiving comprehensive GDMT (with either ARNI or ACEis) experienced significantly better survival than those with malignant cancers, representing a notable improvement over previous observations. 5 , 6 , 7 , 28 In contrast, the absence of adequate GDMT was associated with higher mortality in HF than in cancer, underscoring the critical role of optimized medical therapy in improving outcomes. These findings are particularly striking given the substantial progress achieved in oncology over recent decades. Advances in cancer treatment, screening programs, staging, and surgical procedures have contributed to marked reductions in mortality across many malignancies, 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 drawing parallels to the improving outcomes in HF. 3 Indeed, the 5‐year survival rate for all cancers has risen from 49% in the 1970s to 68% between 2012 and 2018. 32 Among the most common malignancies, only prostate cancer and breast cancer exhibit better survival rates compared with HFrEF in patients receiving GDMT. Prostate cancer is often slow‐growing and nonaggressive, particularly in older individuals, with an overall 5‐year survival rate exceeding 90%, 32 partly driven by earlier detection through widespread prostate specific antigen screening. 33 Similar heightened emphasis on screening, along with advancements in treatment, could be also responsible for the modern improvements in breast cancer outcomes, with mortality decreasing by 44% over the past 30 years and a 5‐year survival rate of approximately 90% (2012–2018). 34 However, oncology treatments continue to advance rapidly (more than 120 cancer drugs approved by the Food and Drug Administration in the past 2 decades), 35 and novel therapeutic modalities, including small molecule targeted agents, antibody drug conjugates, cell based therapies, and gene therapy, hold the potential to further improve outcomes by enabling more personalized and precise tumor targeting, 36 potentially shifting the balance in future outcome comparisons.

Our findings hold important clinical relevance as they underscore the transformative impact of GDMT in improving outcomes for HFrEF. From a clinical standpoint, these results are particularly significant as they go beyond a mere comparison of 2 highly prevalent conditions and instead emphasize the urgent need to optimize treatment strategies for patients with HFrEF. However, the persistent gap between guideline recommendations and real‐world practice underscores the critical role of therapeutic inertia, which continues to hinder the widespread implementation of life‐saving treatments. Data from the CHAMP‐HF registry revealed that only 1% of patients with HFrEF received target doses of a renin‐angiotensin system inhibitor, beta blocker, and MRA, 14 whereas the more recent EVOLUTION HF study (Utilization of Dapagliflozin and Other Guideline Directed Medical Therapies in Heart Failure Patients: A Multinational Observational Study Based on Secondary Data) showed limited uptake of GDMT (particularly for SGLT2is and ARNI) and high discontinuation rates at 1 year (23% for dapagliflozin, 26% for ARNI, 33%–38% for renin‐angiotensin system inhibitors, 25% for beta blockers, and 42% for MRAs). 37 Although multiple factors may contribute to the underprescription or lack of uptitration of GDMT (whether patient related or health care related), 38 clinical inertia remains the primary barrier to treatment intensification. 39 Addressing this barrier is essential to fully realize the benefits of GDMT and improve outcomes for patients with HFrEF, reinforcing the growing recognition of this challenge in cardiovascular disease management. 38 , 40

LIMITATIONS

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. As a retrospective observational study, no causal inferences can be drawn. Additionally, patients in the TriNetX database (https://trinetx.com/) represent those who had medical encounters with health care systems contributing to the TriNetX platform. Although this platform includes >150 million patients worldwide, it does not necessarily represent the entire global population. All analyses were conducted using the built‐in TriNetX platform, which applies a standardized logistic regression model for propensity score estimation and 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching with a fixed caliper. As such, more flexible modeling strategies (ie, splines or interaction terms) could not be applied to refine the PSM. Likewise, additional sensitivity analyses (ie, varying caliper widths, inverse probability of treatment weighting) were not feasible due to the technical constraints of the platform. Although these limitations precluded certain robustness checks, our matched cohorts demonstrated excellent covariate balance, suggesting adequate adjustment for the available covariates. The study is subject to limitations inherent to observational studies and those based on patient EHRs, including overdiagnosis, misdiagnosis, underdiagnosis, unmeasured or uncontrolled confounders, self‐selection, and reverse causality. Moreover, as an EHR‐based data set, the population primarily consists of patients from inpatient, emergency, and day hospital settings. We were unable to directly assess the severity of HF and cancer, or their associated comorbidities (including how they were managed). This lack of granularity may have introduced residual confounding, as both HF severity and cancer stage are key prognostic factors that could influence mortality independently of treatment exposure. EHR data provided limited information on medical treatments, making it challenging to confirm whether the GDMT drug administration schedules and dosages were appropriately prescribed for all patients in the study. This limitation also hindered the evaluation of GDMT dose optimization. Nevertheless, as a real‐world platform capturing patient data, the EHR likely reflects the routine practices of physicians, suggesting that adherence to clinical guidelines and product labeling recommendations is generally high. Due to the limitations of federated EHR data, adherence beyond prescription, such as dispensing, refill rates, or patient‐level compliance, could not be assessed. Finally, the overall 5‐year survival rates for cancer observed in our study were higher than those reported in published statistics (76%–79% in our cohort versus 69% in data from the 2012–2018 period). 16 , 32 Potential explanations for this discrepancy include the exclusion of high‐risk categories (eg, severe CKD or dialysis), the use of innovative therapies, and an increased detection of early‐stage cancers driven by the adoption of screening measures. Rather than weakening our findings, this difference reinforces them by highlighting an even greater relative benefit for patients with HF on GDMT compared with more lethal forms of cancer. Similarly, 5‐year survival rates for patients with HF were also slightly higher in our cohort (71%–88% versus 60%–65% in historical data). 41 , 42 This difference is likely attributable to the exclusion of high‐risk patients in our study (ie, those with severe CKD or on dialysis), the inclusion of patients with a prior HF diagnosis, and the absence of modern therapies (ie, ARNI, SGLT2is) and comprehensive GDMT in earlier published cohorts.

CONCLUSIONS

In this report from a global federated research data set, patients with HFrEF treated with guideline‐recommended quadruple therapy (ARNI or ACEis, MRAs, beta blockers, and SGLT2is) showed better overall survival than those with cancer, overall and across most sex‐specific malignancies. Conversely, patients not receiving comprehensive GDMT experienced higher mortality than individuals with malignant tumors. These findings challenge the long‐standing paradigm equating HF and cancer mortality, while also highlighting the survival benefits of modern GDMT and serving as a call to action for treatment optimization in HFrEF.
