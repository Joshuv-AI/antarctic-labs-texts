Operando Micro- and Nano-Computed Tomography Reveals Silicon–Electrolyte Interface Dynamics and Anisotropic Contact Loss in All-Solid-State Batteries
Authors: Mao Matsumoto, Yuya Sakka, Chengchao Zhong, Keiji Shimoda, Ken-ichi Okazaki, Hisao Yamashige, Takashi Ozeki, Toshiaki Matsui, Akihisa Takeuchi, Masayuki Uesugi, Kentaro Uesugi, Yuki Orikasa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
All-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) with silicon anodes offer high energy density and mitigate issues such as continuous solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation in lithium-ion batteries with liquid electrolytes. However, the evolution of the mechanical contact interface between silicon (Si) and the rigid solid electrolyte during cycling remains poorly understood. This study utilized operando synchrotron X-ray micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and nano-computed tomography (nano-CT) to achieve high-resolution, 3D visualization of the silicon–electrolyte interface during lithiation and delithiation. Micro-CT revealed that silicon particles retain partial contact with the solid electrolyte as they delithiate and shrink to form shell voids, preserving ionic conduction pathways. High-resolution nano-CT further revealed a thin, previously undetectable solid electrolyte layer that adheres to the surfaces of the silicon particles and helps maintain these contact points. Additionally, interfacial delamination of the silicon was found to be highly anisotropic, initiating from sides that were laterally unconstrained due to uneven mechanical pressure and reaction inhomogeneity. Meanwhile, the vertically compressed interface remained largely intact. These findings elucidate the morphological evolution of the Si/electrolyte interface in ASSBs and demonstrate that continuous ion transport can be partially maintained despite significant volume changes.

Introduction

All-solid-state rechargeable batteries (ASSBs) with silicon (Si) anodes offer a promising route to achieve the high energy densities required for next-generation batteries. Si, an abundant material with a high theoretical capacity (4200 mAh g–1), that could far exceed the performance of conventional graphite-based lithium-ion batteries. − However, Si anodes suffer from large volume changes (up to ∼410% expansion) during lithiation/delithiation, which cause intense mechanical stress, leading to particle cracking/pulverization and loss of electrical contact within the electrode. − In traditional liquid-electrolyte lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), repeated exposure of fresh Si surfaces causes continuous solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI) growth, rapidly degrading performance. − In contrast, ASSBs can address some of these problems. The rigid, inorganic solid electrolyte does not flow and coat newly formed silicon surfaces. Therefore, continuous SEI reformation is prevented, even if the silicon cracks. − Furthermore, the solid electrolyte constrains the volume expansion of Si, mitigating cracking, and improving the capacity retention. − These characteristics make ASSBs attractive for harnessing Si’s high capacity; nonetheless, several critical challenges remain unresolved.

A key question is the chemo-mechanical behavior at the Si/solid–electrolyte interface during battery operation. While ASSBs could avoid the continuous SEI growth, the electrochemical performance still depends on maintaining good physical contact between Si particles and the solid electrolyte. − If the contact is lost, ionic transport is impeded. In fact, mechanical contact at the Si–electrolyte interface is essential for charge/discharge to occur, since lithium ions must be continuously conducted across the interface. However, the evolution of the Si–electrolyte contact under repeated expansion/contraction is not fully understood. The interface within electrode structure exhibits significant heterogeneity owing to variations in the material distribution, particle size, and pressure, thereby rendering it challenging to capture these complex spatial interactions. Directly observing these three-dimensional (3D) interface dynamics is experimentally challenging but crucial for understanding failure mechanisms in Si-anode ASSBs.

To elucidate the impact of Si’s expansion/contraction on the solid interface, high-resolution 3D observation techniques are indispensable. The inherent heterogeneity arising from variations in material distribution, particle size, and pressure, makes quantitative characterization of interfacial behavior particularly challenging. Synchrotron-based X-ray computed tomography (CT) allows the high-resolution, real-time imaging of internal structures, enabling operando measurements of ASSBs to be performed during charge/discharge cycling. ,− Recently, X-ray CT has been employed to monitor changes at the Si particle/solid electrolyte contact interface during charge/discharge reactions. , For example, a Si|Li10GeP12S­(LGPS)|LiNi1/3Co1/3Mn1/3O2(NCM) cell was analyzed by operando X-ray CT to observe the formation of shell-like voids on the Si particle surfaces, which was attributed to separation at the solid–electrolyte interface during discharge (delithiation). In a Si|LPSC|Li cell, where the composite electrode is intentionally free of solid electrolyte, X-ray CT analysis has identified three distinct crack formation mechanisms: the propagation of vertical cracks perpendicular to the electrode thickness direction, interfacial delamination occurring during delithiation, and interfacial delamination recurring during relithiation. These behaviors are detrimental to the battery performance since it hinders the ionic conduction pathway. However, previous studies have been limited by a spatial resolution of submicrometer per voxel, which has prevented visualization of the anisotropic contact interface changes and evolving ionic conduction paths critical to understanding the interfacial chemo-mechanical behavior. Thus, a more advanced 3D imaging technique with nanoscale resolution and enhanced contrast is needed to fully capture the interfacial phenomena in Si–anode ASSBs.

X-ray nano-computed tomography (nano-CT) with phase contrast addresses this need by pushing tomography into the submicron regime. By employing advanced focusing X-ray optics using Fresnel zone-plate lenses, nano-CT can achieve voxel sizes on the order of tens of nanometers, enabling an order-of-magnitude improvement over conventional micro-CT. , The term nano-CT originates from the nanometer-scale voxel size, although the field of view is shown at the micrometer scale. Moreover, using phase-contrast imaging to detect subtle X-ray phase shifts instead of relying solely on X-ray absorption greatly enhances the ability to discern interfaces and differences in light-element materials. , This approach dramatically improves contrast for light or compositionally similar phases; for instance, full-field nano-CT with Zernike phase contrast has been shown to distinguish lithium peroxide deposits from a carbon matrix in Li–O2 battery cathodes at ∼50 nm resolution. These developments highlight the immense potential of nano-CT to reveal 3D morphological and interfacial phenomena in batteries that were previously inaccessible due to resolution or contrast limitations.

In this work, we report an operando nano-CT study of a silicon-anode ASSB using phase-contrast imaging to achieve high resolution and insight into the electrode’s 3D morphology. Specifically, we investigate a composite Si anode with a sulfide solid electrolyte (Li6PS5Cl, LPSC) during charging and discharging. By utilizing a focused synchrotron X-ray beam with Fresnel zone plate optics, we attain a voxel size of ∼100 nm along with enhanced phase contrast. This advanced nano-CT approach allows us to directly observe the evolution of the Si particle/solid–electrolyte contact interface, with a level of detail not previously possible. We are able to visualize the initiation and growth of interfacial gaps, the morphology of Si deformation, and the connectivity of ion-conduction pathways through the composite electrode as lithiation progresses. The combination of high spatial resolution and phase contrast sensitivity reveals subtle, anisotropic changes at the Si–LPSC interfaces such as nonuniform gap formation around different facets of Si particles, and how these changes impact the percolation of voids and solid electrolyte around the active material. The insights obtained in this study including the identification of nanoscale interfacial separation phenomena and their effect on ionic transport deepen our understanding of the chemo-mechanical interplay in Si-based ASSBs and provide guidance for the design of more robust high-capacity composite electrodes.

Results and Discussion

Although microsilicon anodes exhibit significant degradation in LIBs, , they have demonstrated relatively good cycling performances in ASSBs, with the exception of an initial irreversible capacity. ,, To investigate this behavior, the Si|LPSC|NCM all-solid-state cell based on Si particles with a diameter of ∼10 μm was cycled at 0.07C, and its galvanostatic charge/discharge profile (corresponding to Si lithiation/delithiation) is shown in Figure . The open-circuit voltage was ∼0 V, consistent with the initial potentials of the Si anode and NCM cathode active materials. An initial charge capacity of 2085 mAh gSi –1 (based on the Si anode) was obtained, corresponding to Si lithiation to generate Li2.1Si. Additionally, a charge of ∼50 mAh gNCM –1 (based on the NCM cathode) was observed at ≤3.5 V during the initial charge, which was attributed to decomposition of the LPSC solid electrolyte at the electrochemically unstable Si anode. , In the initial discharge, a capacity of 1257 mAh gSi –1 was obtained, resulting in a charge/discharge efficiency of 60.3%. This low efficiency has been reported due to the oxidative decomposition of the sulfide electrolyte on the cathode side. However, the efficiency improved to 94.8% in the second cycle. Interestingly, no significant further decrease in capacity was observed up to the fifth cycle.

To overview the mechanical behavior between Si particle and solid electrolyte, we first provide the results from operando micro-CT of the entire cell with submicrometer resolution during the charge/discharge process. This technique can track the expansion/contraction of the Si particles, along with the contact between Si and the solid electrolyte interface, and the formation of cracks in the solid electrolyte during charge/discharge. In the X-ray transmission image of the cell shown in Figure , the Si electrode layer, solid electrolyte layer, and NCM cathode layer can be clearly observed (from top to bottom) with thicknesses of 0.25, 0.45, and 0.49 mm, respectively. Figure illustrates the morphological changes around a Si particle within the Si anode layer, along with the charge/discharge profiles recorded over two cycles. Figure a–e show sliced X-ray CT images of the same Si particle at different stages, namely the pristine particle, after the initial charge, after the initial discharge, after the second charge, and after the second discharge. The segmented images are presented in Figure f–j. Figure h presents the corresponding charge/discharge profile, with black circles indicating the X-ray CT measurement points. In Figure , the central white region corresponds to the Si particle (high absorption coefficient), the surrounding gray area represents the solid electrolyte, and the black areas indicate voids. During charging, lithiation causes expansion of the Si particles, whereas delithiation during discharging leads to volume shrinkage. After the initial lithiation (Figure b), the Si density decreases, resulting in a gray appearance. Additionally, cracks are formed in the solid electrolyte around the Si particles, as indicated by arrows. These cracks are likely caused by the stress exerted on the surrounding solid electrolyte owing to the volume expansion of Si. During the initial delithiation process (Figure c), cracks within the Si particles are observed, along with shell voids at the Si particle/solid electrolyte interface. These voids result from the separation of Si particles from the solid electrolyte owing to the volume shrinkage of Si, consistent with previous operando X-ray CT studies performed using Si|LGPS|NCM cells. The shell voids originated from plastic deformation of the LPSC solid electrolyte, which is caused by the Si volume expansion imposing a stress that exceeds the yield point of the LPSC. Following relief of this stress during the volume shrinkage of Si, the plastically deformed LPSC does not return to its pristine state, thereby preventing it from accommodating further morphological changes at the interface. Conversely, the solid electrolyte contact interface cracks that formed during lithiation becomes smaller during delithiation because Si volume shrinkage removes the stress on the solid electrolyte. This suggests that if the stress applied to the LPSC does not exceed its yield point, such as in areas away from the Si particles, the LPSC remains elastic and can undergo crack repair. After the second lithiation cycle (Figure d), the shell voids and cracks in the Si particles disappear due to Si volume expansion. However, following the second delithiation cycle (Figure e), cracks and shell voids reappear in the same areas, owing to shrinkage of the Si volume. Notably, the Si and the solid electrolyte microstructures remain reversible after the first delithiation. To validate these observations across the entire Si electrode, CT images were recorded for different slices (Figures S1–S3), confirming the general behavior observed within the Si electrode. For some Si particles, cracks are observed in the particles and in the electrolyte surrounding the particles. In contrast, the formation of shell voids caused by separation of the Si particles from the solid electrolyte interface during delithiation occurs in the majority of Si particles. Therefore, shell-void formation is identified as the dominant factor responsible for large polarization after discharge.

Figure i shows the calculated three-dimensional volume changes of the Si particles along with the corresponding changes in the contact area fraction between the Si particles and the solid electrolyte over two cycles. The volume of the Si particle is estimated at ∼7.5 × 104 voxels in the pristine state, 1.3 × 105 voxels after the initial lithiation, and 9.0 × 104 voxels after the initial delithiation, representing an increase of ∼1.5 × 104 voxels. These results indicate that the alloyed lithium within the Si particles is not fully dealloyed during delithiation. Comparison of the results between the pristine and first delithiation samples reveals a reduction in the Si particle/solid electrolyte contact area fraction by ∼60%. Such separation during delithiation contributes to an irreversible capacity loss by limiting the lithium-ion reaction pathway. This suggests that shell-void formation is a significant issue in the initial irreversibility, even when LPSC solid electrolytes are used. The loss of the contact area fraction was more pronounced in the first cycle, suggesting that shell void formation played a role in defining the irreversible capacity during the first cycle.

To examine the relationship between morphology at the Si particle/solid electrolyte contact and interfacial resistance, we performed electrochemical impedance spectroscopy at the various states of charge: pristine, after the first lithiation, after the first delithiation. Nyquist plots are shown in Figure S4. The low frequency semicircle can be attributed to the charge-transfer at the electrode–electrolyte interface, whereas the high frequency semicircle corresponds to the interfacial/contact contribution. The high-frequency resistance decreases after the lithiation and increases again after the delithiation. This change is consistent with the volume expansion of Si improving the real contact fraction during lithiation, while shrinkage during delithiation creates shell voids that degrade contact.

Subsequently, the shell voids shown in Figure were analyzed in greater detail, wherein enlarged images of the Si particle/solid electrolyte interface after the initial delithiation are presented in Figure b,c, wherein the yellow lines indicate the contact interface. As shown, some contacts are maintained, appearing as bridges in areas where the Si particles are otherwise thought to be completely separated from the solid electrolyte interface. Although the shrunken Si particles partially separate from the contact interface, complete blocking of ion conduction is avoided due to incomplete separation, thereby preserving the lithium-ion conduction pathway. Figure d,e compare the remaining contact parts after the first and second delithiation cycles, respectively. Notably, certain contact regions circled in Figure d,e are retained across both delithiation cycles. This consistency implies that the Si particle volume change exhibits anisotropic behavior, wherein some regions tend to detach from the solid electrolyte interface, whereas others maintain sustained contact. Additionally, Si particle detachment and subsequent recovery of the contact appear to be reversible, potentially leading to a good cycle performance after the second cycle.

Since the resolution of micro-CT is insufficient to thoroughly investigate the above phenomena, the relationship between the contact interface and the charge/discharge characteristics remains inadequately explored. To gain a more detailed understanding of the separation behavior of Si particles from the solid electrolyte interface during delithiation, phase-contrast nano-CT was employed to achieve a higher resolution. Changes in the Si particles were observed every 2 h throughout the delithiation process. An overview of the cell used for these measurements is shown in Figure . The cell structure consists of a 0.14 mm-thick Si electrode layer, a 0.18 mm-thick solid electrolyte layer, and a 0.25 mm-thick NCM electrode layer. To enhance the visibility of the contact interface phenomenon, the electrode and electrolyte layers were thinned and optimized to increase the charge/discharge capacity. Figure shows the operando nano-CT results along with the corresponding charge/discharge profiles. CT images of the same Si particles were extracted from the X-ray CT data for various slices, and the CT images of the Si electrode layer are shown in Figures S5–S7. Phase-contrast nano-CT provides a superior differentiation, revealing two factors that lead to a good cycling performance after the initial cycle, namely anisotropy in the separation of Si particles and the maintenance of some contact areas.

During the first lithiation (Figure c), a contrast difference is observed within the Si particles, indicating the linear propagation of lithium alloying into the Si particles, a phenomenon not detectable by micro-CT. This reveals anisotropy in the lithiation process, highlighting the preferential insertion of lithium along the ⟨110⟩ direction, consistent with previous studies. Furthermore, the Si particles at the solid electrolyte contact interface appear darker, suggesting that lithiation is initiated at this position. As shown in Figure d, a small amount of unreacted Si crystallites remains inside the particle even after lithiation. Since the observation was made in an expanded state of Si, the formation of microcracks that electrochemically isolate specific crystallites is unlikely to be the primary cause. Therefore, we attribute intraparticle reaction heterogeneity mainly to the anisotropy of surface reaction kinetics and lithium diffusivity in crystalline silicon. Crystallites that expose slow-reacting/diffusing facets undergo delayed lithiation compared to their surrounding crystallites. During delithiation, the gradual separation of Si particles from the solid electrolyte is successfully observed, beginning on the horizontal side of the Si particles (Figure i,s), followed by the solid electrolyte layer side (Figure m,t), and finally, the current collector side (Figure o,v). This sequential separation is likely due to the lower pressure exerted on the left and right sides compared to the top and bottom. Additionally, a greater volume shrinkage occurs at the bottom side, where the delithiation reaction progresses closer to the solid electrolyte layer. Consequently, the separation of Si particles from the solid electrolyte interface is nonuniform and influenced by both the pressure and reaction direction; this leads to a high cyclability of Si in ASSBs. Examination of the overall image recorded for the XZ plane after delithiation (Figure S6g) shows that the Si particles on the solid electrolyte side, where the reaction proceeded relatively well, are also separated in the vertical direction, but contact is maintained in the vertical direction for the Si particles on the current collector side. This anisotropy of Si-particle separation is a phenomenon that partially maintains contact between Si and the electrolyte, resulting in a good cycling performance.

The circled areas shown in Figure x, which presents an enlarged view of the Si particle/solid electrolyte contact interface, illustrate that as the separation progresses at the contact interface, the solid electrolyte remains adhered to the surfaces of the Si particles. This suggests that the remaining contact between the Si particles and the solid electrolyte is maintained not through deformation of the Si particles, but by the solid electrolyte adhering to the Si surface and to the solid electrolyte itself. To validate this, the elemental distribution of the Si particle/solid electrolyte contact interface after the initial charge/discharge cycle was examined by focused ion beam–scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) (Figure S8). Notably, elements that originate exclusively from the sulfide solid electrolyte, such as S and P, were detected on the surfaces of the Si particles after delithiation. Because these elements are not components of Si itself, their detection directly evidence that fragments of the solid electrolyte remain adhered to the Si particle surfaces. Furthermore, S and P were also detected in the regions of contact between the Si particles and the solid electrolyte, indicating that the adhered electrolyte on the Si surface provides connection points with the surrounding bulk electrolyte. These results demonstrate that in ASSBs, even though Si particles undergo separation from the solid electrolyte during delithiation, the residual electrolyte on the Si surfaces helps to maintain partial interfacial contact and thus preserve ion-conduction pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, the operando X-ray tomography study revealed that the formation of shell-like interfacial voids around silicon particles during the initial delithiation process results in irreversible capacity loss during the first cycle. We found that a thin layer of solid electrolyte remains adhered to the surfaces of the silicon particles. Despite apparent separation, this layer maintains partial contact with the surrounding electrolyte via a partial bridging structure. Furthermore, delamination of silicon from the solid electrolyte is highly anisotropic. Separation begins at the sides of the particle that are less constrained laterally where external pressure is lowest and reaction-induced shrinkage is greatest, and progresses toward the top and bottom. Contact along the vertical compression axis remains largely intact. This partial retention of contact provides continuous ionic pathways during cycling. Though interfacial void formation is generally considered undesirable, our results suggest that silicon can effectively function in ASSBs, even with limited ionic contact.

Experimental Section

Electrochemical Measuring Cells Assembly

The cathode composite materials were prepared by mixing LiNi1/3Co1/3Mn1/3O2 (NCM) coated with LiNbO3 as the active cathode material, Li6PS5Cl (LPSC) (∼325 mesh, Ampcera Incorporated) as the solid electrolyte, and acetylene black (AB) (Denka Black, Denka Company Limited) as a conductive additive at a weight ratio of 1:1:0.1. The mixing was performed using a mortar in a glovebox under an argon atmosphere.

Similarly, the anode composite materials were prepared by mixing crystalline silicon powder (average particle size 18.4 μm, Japan Natural Energy & Resources Company Limited) as the active anode material, LPSC as the solid electrolyte, and AB as a conductive additive at a weight ratio of 1:4.4:0.6. For the micro-X-ray computed tomography (CT) measurements, a weight ratio of 1:2.78:0.42 was used.

A cylindrical pellet cell (10 mm in diameter) was used to evaluate the cycling performance. The cell was assembled in a glovebox under an argon atmosphere by sequentially stacking 60 mg of the cathode composite, 45 mg of the solid electrolyte, and 20 mg of the anode composite, followed by pressing at 313 MPa. Charge–discharge measurements were performed under a constant stack pressure of 65 MPa.

Operando X-ray CT Cells Assembly

For the operando X-ray CT measurements, we used a cell with pressure monitoring capability, as previously described. Pellets of cathode composite (60 mg), solid electrolyte (45 mg), and anode composite (20 mg) were each prepared under a pressure of 313 MPa using a 10 mm-diameter molding die. For the micro-CT measurements, we used 80 mg of cathode composite, 90 mg of solid electrolyte, and 30 mg of anode composite. Each pellet was punched to a diameter of 0.9 mm using a hole punch and stacked inside a 1 mm diameter acrylic cylinder. In this setup, a load cell was placed beneath the cell to monitor the applied pressure, while a screw-driven rod from the top was used to apply the stack pressure. Charge–discharge measurements and X-ray CT measurements were performed under a constant stack pressure of 65 MPa. The thicknesses of the cathode, solid electrolyte, and anode layers were approximately 0.14 mm, 0.18 mm, and 0.25 mm, respectively. For the micro-CT measurements, the thicknesses were approximately 0.25 mm, 0.45 mm, and 0.49 mm, respectively.

Electrochemical Measurements

The cycling performance of the cylindrical pellet cell (10 mm in diameter) was evaluated by galvanostatic charge–discharge measurements using an electrochemical measurement system (HJ1001SD8E, Meiden Hokuto Corporation). The cycle test was performed at room temperature with a cutoff voltage range of 1.5 to 4.5 V and a current density of 0.71 mA cm–2 (0.07C rate). Charge–discharge measurements of the cell for operando X-ray CT measurements were performed using an electrochemical measurement system (SP-150, Bio-Logic Science Instruments) under room temperature at 0.07C rate.

X-ray Computed Tomography

X-ray CT measurements were conducted at beamline BL20XU of the SPring-8 synchrotron radiation facility (Japan). Undulator radiation was monochromatized to 20 keV using a liquid-nitrogen-cooled Si(111) double-crystal monochromator. A visible-light-conversion-type X-ray detector, consisting of a CMOS camera (C13440-20CU, Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.) with a pixel size of approximately 0.5 μm and a field of view (FOV) of 1.0 × 1.0 mm, was used. Nano-CT scans were performed under a phase-contrast imaging system with a pixel size of about 85 nm and an FOV of 60 × 60 μm. An apodization Fresnel zone plate was used as the objective lens. For each X-ray CT measurement, 1800 projections were acquired over a 180° angular range with an exposure time of 0.05 s per frame (125 s for nano-CT measurements). The recorded data were reconstructed into cross-sectional images representing the X-ray absorption coefficient distribution by the convolution back-projection method. Segmentation and analysis of the three-dimensional data were performed using Dragonfly 2024.1 software (Dragonfly 3D World, Comet Technologies).

Focused Ion Beam Scanning Electron Microscopy

For the focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) measurements, one cycle of constant-current charge–discharge was performed at room temperature with a cutoff voltage range of 1.5 to 4.5 V and a current density of 0.71 mA cm–2 (0.07C rate) on cells fabricated under the same conditions as those used for the cycle test. After the measurements, the cells were disassembled, and the silicon anode layer was removed. The measurement was carried out using a dual-beam FIB–SEM (Nvision 40, Carl Zeiss-SIINT) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, Oxford).
