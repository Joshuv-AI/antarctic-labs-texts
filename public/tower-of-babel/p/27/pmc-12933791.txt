Bringing AI to the OR: integrating a machine learning predictive model in the EHR – a pilot on user-friendliness
Authors: Sara Ben Hmido, Ewout Ingwersen, Houssam Abder Rahim, Martin van Maanen, Sander Groot, Matthijs Schakel, Annika Rausch, Geert Kazemier, Freek Daams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEvaluate the technical integration and usability of an intraoperative predictive machine learning model for colorectal anastomotic leakage within the Epic electronic health records (EHRs) at a single academic centre, with outputs blinded.MethodsThe system used 28 data elements from patient records, intraoperative monitoring equipment and structured operating room (OR) observations. Data were collected every 15 min and processed in the cloud with encrypted, pseudonymised transfer. Usability was assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and additional questions addressing access, clarity, responsiveness, workflow impact, safety and training needs. Convenience sampling was used, with all available OR staff involved in eligible procedures invited to participate.Results15 procedures (9 October 2024 to 6 March 2025) were included. Nine unique users responded (≈75% of 12 exposed; four surgeons, five OR assistants). The interface was accessed in all cases, and predictions were generated each time from all three sources. Mean SUS was 79.2 (SD 10.4; 95% CI 71.2 to 87.2). Diagnostic items favoured access speed and clarity; prediction responsiveness scored lower.DiscussionThe system could be implemented and operated reliably within a single centre and EHR environment and was perceived as easy to use, despite the small sample size. However, findings may not generalise to other hospitals or EHRs without adaptation and further multi-site evaluation.ConclusionThe system functioned reliably and was positively received, supporting readiness for activation in real-time clinical use and prospective evaluation. Future deployment should incorporate regulatory planning and a quality-management framework to monitor performance, safety and changes in model behaviour over time.

Introduction

Machine learning (ML) holds significant potential in improving medical decision-making by offering data-driven support in complex clinical scenarios. An emerging application of ML is the prediction of postoperative complications, including anastomotic leakage following colorectal surgery.1 2 Colorectal anastomotic leakage (CAL) is such a complication with a multifactorial aetiology that limits the effectiveness of traditional prediction methods based solely on clinical expertise or nomograms.3 4

To address this challenge, an intraoperative predictive machine learning (IPML) model was developed to estimate the risk of CAL.5 The model integrates both static and dynamic intraoperative data, such as tissue perfusion. Its clinical value depends not only on predictive accuracy, but also on effective implementation within existing surgical workflows.

In this context, the model is intended for use during the surgical time-out—a routine moment in colorectal cancer surgery when the team confirms the operative plan, including key decisions such as stoma creation or anastomosis. Integrating risk predictions at this point should support decision-making without disrupting workflow. Successful implementation depends on intuitive design, seamless integration, alignment with clinical routines and acceptance by operating room (OR) staff.

Accordingly, the aim of this study is to evaluate the integration of the IPML model into colorectal surgery, with attention to its technical usability and the user experience within the clinical environment. As the model output is blinded, the study does not assess its impact on clinical decision-making, but instead focuses on practical aspects such as interface intuitiveness, efficiency and compatibility with surgical workflows that are critical for successful implementation.

Methods

Technical integration

The ML-based predictive model for CAL was integrated into the Epic electronic health record (EHR) system at Amsterdam UMC, an academic hospital.6 This integration was achieved through Epic’s cloud-based infrastructure, Nebula, and the Reporting Workbench, enabling real-time intraoperative data collection and supporting a user interface (UI) within the OR. It is embedded within Chronicles, Epic’s operational database, which enables us to extract real-time data directly from the electronic patient record, rather than relying on periodic database loads that are often not real time. The overall system architecture, including the flow of data from patient records, OR equipment and manual observations to the cloud-based model and output interface, is illustrated in figure 1.

Integration of a predictive machine learning model into the Epic ecosystem. Input data from patient records, intraoperative equipment and surgeon observations are processed through Epic’s Reporting Workbench and sent in anonymised form to the Nebula cloud platform, where the model generates a risk prediction. The output is returned on-premise to be presented in the OpTime monitor. However, for this study, the model results were not shown to the surgical team. ML, machine learning; OR, operating room.

The implementation incorporates three main data sources: preoperative patient information available in the EHR (such as demographics, diagnosis and tumour characteristics), intraoperative physiological parameters collected from surgical equipment (including blood pressure, temperature and perfusion-related variables) and structured observations recorded by the surgical team during the procedure. These observations are entered using a SmartForm within Epic’s surgical module, by the circulating OR assistant.

All data are compiled through a Reporting Workbench report, which is executed every 15 min via Epic’s badge-based scheduling system. The aggregated data are then securely transmitted to Nebula, Epic’s Microsoft Azure-based cloud environment where the predictive model is deployed. All transmissions are encrypted and pseudonymised to safeguard patient privacy.

The model generates preliminary risk predictions throughout the procedure as data become available. A complete risk estimate is produced only once all 28 required variables have been collected. This information is presented through Epic’s UI used by the OR assistant, which displays the risk score, contributing variables and data completeness in a dedicated visualisation. During the study period, the system operated in shadow mode, meaning predictions were not visible to the surgical team and did not influence intraoperative decision-making.

Usability evaluation

This study was designed as a pilot implementation study to evaluate the technical usability of the system in a clinical setting. It used a single-centre implementation design with descriptive mixed-methods and is reported in line with Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies.7 The usability of the system was assessed using a convenience sample of surgical team members at Amsterdam UMC. Participants included colorectal surgeons, assisting surgeons and circulatory OR assistants who were involved in the use of the model during the colorectal procedures.

A single-centre design was chosen to allow controlled evaluation of technical integration, usability and workflow compatibility within a defined EHR and surgical environment before broader multicentre deployment.

Eligible cases were defined by a set of inclusion and exclusion criteria applied to the patients undergoing surgery. Inclusion criteria were: (1) patients undergoing colorectal surgery involving the construction of an anastomosis, (2) patients aged 18 years or older and (3) the ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) missing more than 25% of the required variables for the predictive model, (2) significant comorbidities likely to influence surgical outcomes (defined as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status classification 3 or higher) and (3) non-elective surgical procedures.

To facilitate system access and encourage correct usage, instructional materials were developed and made available prior to the evaluation. These materials included step-by-step guidance on how to locate the system within Epic, complete the SmartForm and navigate the UI. An example of the provided material is provided in the online supplemental appendix.

Usability was assessed using two complementary approaches. First, participants completed the System Usability Scale (SUS) after their first use of the system. The SUS is a standardised questionnaire developed to measure perceived usability across a variety of systems.8 It consists of 10 items rated on a five-point Likert scale, alternating between positively and negatively worded statements. Responses are converted to a single score ranging from 0 to 100 using a standardised scoring method and summarised with means and 95% CIs; diagnostic items were summarised as means. Subgroup summaries by role were descriptive only.

A process evaluation was pre-specified to examine mechanisms by which the implementation strategy was expected to operate: (1) system accessibility within workflow (access frequency and timing), (2) clarity and completeness of data entry and (3) prediction responsiveness (latency from data refresh to output). Mechanisms were operationalised via six diagnostic Likert-scale items (access speed, clarity of required information, perceived workflow burden, patient-safety impact, training sufficiency, output timeliness) and technical logs (interface access timestamps, data-completeness fields, refresh intervals), mapped a priori to the three mechanisms.

In addition to the SUS, participants were also prompted with a set of more specific diagnostic questions tailored to the context of system use. These items addressed aspects such as system responsiveness, clarity of data entry requirements, perceived impact on task performance, patient safety and adequacy of training. This allowed for a more detailed understanding of the user experience and the identification of potential areas for improvement beyond the general SUS score.

The questionnaire was administered digitally via the Castor electronic data capture system and was not anonymous.9 Second, technical logs were used to track system interaction. These logs captured usage patterns such as access rates, completeness of input data and timing within the surgical workflow. Together, these methods offered both subjective and objective insights into the usability and integration of the system.

Due to the exploratory and implementation-focused nature of the study, no formal sample size or power calculation was undertaken. Convenience sampling was used to recruit all available and exposed colorectal surgeons, assisting surgeons and circulating OR staff during the study period, with participation dependent on clinical availability and exposure to the system during eligible procedures.

Results

A total of 15 colorectal surgical procedures were conducted during the evaluation period, which ran from 9 October 2024 to 6 March 2025, during which the predictive model system was active in shadow mode. Across these procedures, nine unique users completed the SUS questionnaire.

Technical integration

System interaction logs confirmed that the model interface was accessed in all 15 procedures (100%). The technical integration of the predictive model into the Epic EHR system was successful. In each case, the model received input data from all three intended sources: patient records, intraoperative monitoring equipment and manually entered surgical observations. Based on these inputs, the system was able to generate a risk prediction during each procedure.

Importantly, the system operated in shadow mode throughout the evaluation period, which means that the model’s output was not visible to the surgical team at any point. This ensured that clinical decision-making was not influenced by the predictions during the study phase.

Response rate

Respondents included four surgeons and five circulating OR assistants. An estimated total of 12 eligible staff members were exposed to the system across the included procedures, resulting in a response rate of approximately 75% (9 of 12). Notably, one surgeon and two OR assistants who were eligible did not complete the questionnaire.

Usability evaluation

The overall mean SUS score was 79.2 (SD=10.4, 95% CI (71.2 to 87.2)). Role-specific scores showed variation: surgeons reported the highest mean SUS score of 82.5 (SD=10.0, 95% CI (74.8 to 90.2)), followed by circulating OR assistants at 76.5 (SD=11.0, 95% CI (68.1 to 84.9)). The highest average item scores were recorded for Item 3 (‘I thought the system was easy to use’) at 4.4, Item 9 (‘I felt very confident using the system’) at 4.2 and Item 7 (‘I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly’) at 3.9. Discussion

This pilot implementation study evaluated the usability and technical integration of an IPML-based predictive model for CAL. The system was deployed in shadow mode and tested across 15 colorectal surgical procedures without influencing intraoperative decision-making. Findings indicate that the fundamental integration of the system into the EHR environment was successful and that surgical staff reported generally positive perceptions of its usability.

The overall SUS score was 79.2 (SD=10.4, 95% CI (71.2 to 87.2)), exceeding the commonly cited threshold of 68. The point estimate corresponds to a large difference from this benchmark (Hedges’ g≈0.96), although the CI includes values below the ‘A/excellent’ benchmark of 80.8. Taken together, these results suggest promising usability in the intraoperative setting rather than demonstrating it conclusively, particularly given the small sample (n=9). Implementation occurred within the Epic framework; therefore, generalisability to other EHRs is uncertain without adaptation.

Role-specific findings are exploratory and descriptive due to small subgroup sizes. Surgeons reported a higher mean SUS (82.5, SD=10.0, 95% CI (74.8 to 90.2)) than circulating OR assistants (76.5, SD=11.0, 95% CI (68.1 to 84.9)), but these differences should not be interpreted inferentially.

The diagnostic results suggest that users found the system both accessible and easy to use. High mean scores for system access and clarity of required input indicate that the interface design and data entry process aligned well with user expectations. Meanwhile, low scores on items related to workflow disruption, patient safety risks and the need for additional training suggest that the system did not interfere with existing clinical tasks or introduce significant usability concerns. Taken together, these findings support the feasibility of integrating the system into routine surgical workflows with minimal burden on end users and without compromising safety or efficiency.

While the sample size was limited, the participation rate (75%) and consistency of system access confirmed through interaction logs in all 15 cases indicated that integration was reliable without operational issues. Importantly, the model successfully received and processed data from all three input sources (patient records, intraoperative equipment and manual observations) and returned a prediction in every case. However, predictions remained hidden from the clinical team, as the system functioned in shadow mode throughout the study.

The study has important limitations. The use of convenience sampling at a single tertiary referral centre may have introduced selection bias, as participation depended on staff availability and exposure during eligible procedures rather than systematic recruitment. Consequently, respondents may not fully represent the broader population of end users, and perceived usability may be overestimated. In addition, the single-centre setting constrains generalisability, as workflows, staffing models and technical infrastructure may vary across institutions and EHR environments.

Within these constraints, the findings demonstrate that the system can be integrated and operated reliably in a real-world surgical setting and is perceived as usable by exposed end users. These results therefore support technical feasibility and local usability, while underscoring the need for further evaluation under conditions of active clinical use.

Subsequent work should focus on enabling real-time prediction delivery and reassessing the system’s role in intraoperative decision-making and its impact on downstream clinical outcomes. As system functionality transitions from observational to interventional use, additional considerations related to regulatory classification and governance will become increasingly relevant.

In particular, deployment of a live predictive system could bring the application within the scope of the European Union Medical Device Regulation and, given its ML-based functionality, the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act.10 11

In this context, the system should be further developed within a broader quality-management framework that supports ongoing performance monitoring, safety oversight and management of model drift, with clearly defined human oversight mechanisms. These should include explicit roles and responsibilities for clinical users, support for informed interpretation of model outputs and safeguards to ensure that final clinical decisions remain under human control, thereby mitigating automation bias and the risk of undue reliance on algorithmic recommendations in high-stakes intraoperative settings.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrates that the intraoperative ML-based system for predicting CAL can be technically integrated into a live surgical setting and is well-received by clinical users. Usability scores exceeded established benchmarks, and diagnostic feedback indicated minimal disruption to workflow, with no safety concerns reported. The system functioned reliably in shadow mode, processing real-time data across all cases without technical issues. These results support the system’s readiness for real-time activation and further clinical evaluation. As the model transitions towards predictive use, future development must account for regulatory requirements associated with medical device classification and include a robust quality-management framework to monitor performance, safety and potential model drift over time.
