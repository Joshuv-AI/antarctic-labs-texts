Ultrasound–assisted maillard conjugation of gliadin with dextran and fucoidan to enhance hydrophilicity for hydrogel applications☆
Authors: Hyun Ju Lee, Jong Hyeon Han, Su Min Park, Ji Hwan Ryoo, Sung Gu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Gliadin, the alcohol-soluble fraction of wheat gluten, accounts for 40–50% of wheat endosperm protein [1] and exhibits high hydrophobicity and poor aqueous solubility because of its high glutamine (38–40%) and proline (14–20%) contents and lack of charged residues [2]. These characteristics facilitate the formation of spherical aggregates through hydrogen bonding and disulfide cross-linking and thus provide essential elasticity to gluten networks. However, the high hydrophobicity of gliadin limits its hydration and dispersion in aqueous food systems and thus restricts its application under processing conditions requiring sufficient protein hydration for matrix development, with examples including dough-based systems, aqueous dispersions, and hydrogel formulations [3], [4]. Although previous studies have utilized this insolubility to prepare nanoparticles via antisolvent precipitation, persistent self-aggregation limits the stability of such nanodispersions in complex food matrices [5]. Therefore, precise structural modifications enhancing gliadin hydrophilicity are required for broader industrial applications.

The inherent rigidity and limited hydration of gliadin can be addressed through ultrasound-induced structural unfolding due to the intense shear forces, localized high temperature, and high pressure generated by acoustic cavitation [6], which can disrupt noncovalent interactions and increase the conformational flexibility of protein molecules. Appropriate ultrasonication was shown to modify the conformations of wheat gliadin and green wheat gliadin, reduce particle size, increase the content of free sulfhydryl groups and surface hydrophobicity, and improve solubility and related functional properties [7]. Similar ultrasound-induced changes in secondary structure and surface charge have been observed for other globular proteins [[8], [9]]. Thus, ultrasound pretreatment may be a useful structural activation strategy for improving gliadin reactivity.

Although physical unfolding can increase molecular accessibility, the realization of stable compatibility with aqueous media requires more persistent hydrophilization. The Maillard reaction, a nonenzymatic glycation reaction between the primary amine groups of proteins and carbonyl groups of reducing sugars, provides an effective strategy for this purpose [10]. The covalent attachment of hydrophilic carbohydrates to exposed amino groups can modulate surface hydrophobicity and improve long-term dispersion stability [11]. Compared with monosaccharides, polysaccharides possess higher molecular weights and stronger hydration capacities, thereby exerting more pronounced effects on the technofunctional properties of protein conjugates [12]. The Maillard glycation of gliadin with glucose and variable-molecular-weight starch hydrolysates was found to improve its solubility, emulsifying properties, and structural stability [13].

Although previous studies have reported ultrasound-induced functional improvements in gliadin and its glycation-based modification, the use of ultrasound-induced reactive-site exposure to facilitate subsequent glycation with polysaccharides is insufficiently understood. In particular, no relationships have been established among ultrasound-induced structural activation, charge-dependent polysaccharide conjugation, and the performance of modified gliadin in aqueous hydrogel matrices. Herein, we prepared amphiphilic gliadin–polysaccharide conjugates by ultrasound pretreatment followed by Maillard conjugation and characterized their structural evolution and performance as structural modulators in curdlan-based hydrogels, linking molecular transformation with macroscopic gel properties to provide a framework for improving the functional utility of gliadin in aqueous food architectures.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wheat gluten (protein content: 70–80%) was obtained from Bob's Red Mill Natural Foods (Milwaukie, USA). Dextran (Mw = 35–45 kDa) was purchased from Daejung (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Fucoidan (Mw = 10.63 kDa, purity = 85%, fucose content = 25.8%, sulfate group content = 25.1%) was purchased from Shandong Jiejing Group Co., Ltd. (Cat. No. 85, Shandong, China). o-Phthalaldehyde (OPA) and sodium tetraborate were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). β-Mercaptoethanol and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) were purchased from VWR Amresco (Randor, PA, USA). The bicinchoninic acid assay kit and bromophenol blue were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Gliadin extraction

Gliadin was extracted from wheat gluten powder using a slight modification of a previously reported method [14]. Briefly, wheat gluten (560 g) was dispersed in 70 vol% aqueous ethanol at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v), and the dispersion was stirred using a propeller stirrer at 450 rpm and 20–25 °C for 3 h and then was centrifuged at 8,000 g for 20 min. The supernatant was collected, stored at 4 °C overnight, and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Ethanol was removed from the resulting supernatant using rotary evaporation at 45 °C. The concentrated extract was frozen at −20 °C for 5 h, the aqueous layer was removed, and the residue was washed three times with distilled water and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain purified gliadin (extraction yield ≈ 11%, crude protein content = 85.31%).

Conjugation of gliadin with polysaccharides (glycation)

Optimization of glycation conditions

To determine the optimal conditions for the Maillard reaction, we examined the effects of ultrasound intensity (200, 400, and 600 W), protein-to-polysaccharide mass ratio (1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4), heating temperature (70, 80, and 90 °C), and reaction time (2, 4, and 6 h). During the evaluation of each variable, the other parameters were maintained at their intermediate values (ultrasound intensity = 400 W, mixing ratio = 1:2, temperature = 80 °C, and reaction time = 4h). For each condition, grafting degree, protein solubility, absorbance at 294 nm (A294, a proxy for the concentration of Maillard reaction intermediates), and absorbance at 420 nm (A420, a proxy for the concentration of advanced Maillard reaction products) were measured.

High grafting degree, A294, and protein solubility were considered favorable and assigned higher scores. A420, representing the accumulation of potentially harmful advanced glycation end-products that may impair nutritional quality [15], was scored inversely because lower browning intensity was considered preferable. The standardized scores were visualized as radar charts to compare the overall performance of each treatment, with the enclosed areas indicating favorable conditions for conjugate formation.

Positive indicators were normalized using Eq. (1), and A420 was normalized using Eq. (2). All normalized indicators were assigned equal weights and used to construct radar charts. Normalized score was used as a practical screening index to select processing conditions for subsequent experiments rather than as a formal statistical optimization model.(1)Ni=Xi-Xmin/Xmax-Xmin(2)Ni=Xmax-Xi/Xmax-Xmin

Here, Ni is the normalized score, Xi is the value measured for each sample, Xmin is the minimum value within the corresponding screening set, and Xmax is the maximum value within the corresponding screening set.

The optimal conditions were established as ultrasound pretreatment at 400 W for 10 min, 1:2 mixing ratio, and heating at 90 °C for 4 h.

Ultrasound pretreatment

A 4% (w/v) gliadin solution was prepared by dispersing gliadin in deionized water and adjusting the pH to 11 using 1 N NaOH or 1 N HCl. The pH adjustment was performed to improve the dispersibility of gliadin (which is poorly soluble at near-neutral pH) and facilitate the subsequent Maillard reaction between its amino groups and polysaccharide carbonyl groups [16]. The solution was magnetically stirred overnight at XXX °C to ensure complete hydration. Ultrasound treatment (Q700 Sonicator® instrument equipped with a 13 mm–diameter probe operating at a frequency of 20 kHz, Qsonica, USA) was performed in pulsed mode (5 s on/1 s off) for 10 min under constant stirring. The sample volume did not exceed the maximum recommended probe capacity (250 mL). During sonication, the solution temperature was maintained below 30 °C using an ice–water bath to prevent thermal denaturation.

Maillard glycation

Dextran or fucoidan was dissolved in deionized water to a concentration of 8% (w/v) upon magnetic stirring, and pH was adjusted to 11 using 1 N NaOH or 1 N HCl. Based on previous studies, a gliadin-to-polysaccharide mass ratio of 1:2 was selected as optimal for glycation [17]. After heating, the reaction mixture was immediately cooled in an ice bath for 5 min and freeze-dried for subsequent analysis.

Six sample groups—native gliadin (NG), ultrasound-treated gliadin (UG), gliadin–dextran conjugate without ultrasound pretreatment (G–D), gliadin–fucoidan conjugate without ultrasound pretreatment (G–F), gliadin–dextran conjugate with ultrasound pretreatment (UG–D), and gliadin–fucoidan conjugate with ultrasound pretreatment (UG–F)— were established to evaluate the individual and combined effects of ultrasound pretreatment and conjugation with different polysaccharides on the structural and functional properties of gliadin.

Characterization of glycated gliadin complexes

Grafting degree and browning intensity

Grafting degree and browning intensity were determined as described previously [18]. The OPA assay was used to estimate the reduction in the content of accessible free amino groups after Maillard treatment as an apparent glycation indicator. The OPA reagent was prepared by dissolving OPA (80 mg) in methanol (2 mL) and adding 20 wt% SDS solution (5 mL), β-mercaptoethanol (200 μL), and 0.1 M sodium tetraborate buffer (50 mL, pH 9.7). The volume was brought to 100 mL with deionized water, and the solution was thoroughly mixed. For the assay, freshly prepared OPA reagent (4 mL) was mixed with the sample solution (200 μL, 10 mg/mL). After incubation at 35 °C for 2 min, absorbance at 340 nm was measured using an ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA). NG was used as the control, and distilled water was used as the blank. Each sample was measured three times. Grafting degree was calculated as(3)Graftingdegree%={(A0-Ai)/A0}×100where A0 is sample absorbance before the reaction (native gliadin), and Ai is sample absorbance after the reaction.

Browning intensity was evaluated to determine glycation intensity. The absorbance of a 5 mg/mL sample was measured at 294 and 420 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

FT-IR transmittance spectra (FT/IR–4700, JASCO, Tokyo, Japan) were acquired by averaging 32 scans performed within 500–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 [19].

For secondary-structure analysis, the band at 1600–1700 cm−1 was deconvoluted using the PeakFit 4.12 software (SeaSolve Software Inc., Framingham, USA). The relative contents of different secondary structures were quantified using the Gaussian area under the curve of the corresponding peaks [18]: 1615–1640 and 1690–1700 cm−1 for β-sheets, 1650–1665 cm−1 for α-helices, 1640–1650 cm−1 for random coils, and 1665–1690 cm−1 for β-turns. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy was used to evaluate the conformational changes in protein structure using a slight modification of a previously reported protocol [20]. Samples were dissolved in deionized water to a final concentration of 2 mg/mL, and fluorescence spectra were recorded (SpectraMax Gemini EM spectrofluorometer, Molecular Devices, USA) using an excitation wavelength of 283 nm and emission range of 300–500 nm. Measurements were conducted at a high scan rate with excitation and emission slit widths set to 2 nm. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Protein solubility

Protein solubility was measured using a slight modification of a previously reported method [21]. Sample dispersions (5 mg/mL in deionized water) were thoroughly mixed and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min. The protein concentrations of the supernatants were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Protein solubility was calculated as the content of soluble protein in the supernatant relative to the protein content of the dispersion before centrifugation. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity (H0) was determined using 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (ANS) as a fluorescent probe [22]. Samples were diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.5). A fresh 8 mM ANS solution in PBS was prepared in an amber volumetric flask. Gliadin solutions were serially diluted to concentrations of 0.625–10 mg/mL. Subsequently, freshly prepared ANS solution (20 μL) was added to each diluted sample (4 mL), and the mixture was vortexed. After 10 min of incubation, fluorescence intensity (FI) was measured (SpectraMax Gemini EM spectrofluorometer, Molecular Devices, USA) using an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and emission range of 400–650 nm. The FI of the blank was subtracted from that of the sample, and the corrected FI was plotted against protein concentration. H0 was determined as the slope of the corresponding linear fit (R2 > 0.99). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI)

Particle size and PDI were determined using a Litesizer™ 500 particle analyzer (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) based on a slight modification of a previously reported method [23]. To minimize multiple scattering, the dispersions were diluted to 0.25% (w/v) with distilled water prior to measurement. All measurements were conducted in triplicate at 25 °C.

Emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI)

EAI and ESI were determined using a slight modification of a previously reported method [22]. Emulsions were prepared using corn oil and an aqueous protein dispersion (20 mg/mL) at a water-to-oil ratio of 4:1 (v/v). The mixture was homogenized for 1 min at 12,000 rpm using a high-speed homogenizer (IKA-T18, IKA Staufen, Germany). After homogenization, an emulsion aliquot (10 μL) was diluted with 0.1% (w/v) SDS solution (990 μL). A 200 μL aliquot of the diluted emulsion was transferred into a 96-well microplate, and absorbance at 500 nm (A500) was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA). Absorbance was recorded immediately after dilution (A0) and after 10 min of standing at 20–25 °C (A10). All measurements were performed in triplicate. EAI and ESI were calculated as(4)EAIm2/g=[(2×2.303×A500×D)/{C×(1-ϕ)}]×104(5)ESI(%)=(A10/A0)×100

where D = 250 is the dilution factor, C (g/mL) is the protein concentration before emulsification, and ϕ = 0.2 is the volume fraction of oil in the emulsion.

Preparation of curdlan-based hydrogels

Hydrogels were prepared using a slight modification of a previously reported method [24]. Preliminary experiments were performed to determine appropriate curdlan and gliadin concentrations. Hydrogels prepared with 2% curdlan and 2% gliadin exhibited unstable physical properties, including hardness, water-holding capacity (WHC), and porosity. Based on these findings, hydrogels were subsequently prepared using curdlan and gliadin conjugate concentrations of 2% each to assess the stabilizing effects of these conjugates and potential applicability in aqueous systems.

Each curdlan-conjugated and freeze-dried gliadin sample was dispersed in distilled water at a concentration of 2% (w/v). The mixtures were magnetically stirred at 25 °C overnight to ensure complete hydration, and pH was then adjusted to 9.0 using 0.1 N NaOH. The pH-adjusted samples were gelled by heating in a dry oven at 90 °C for 30 min, immediately cooled to 4 °C, and stored for at least 12 h prior to further analyses. For these analyses, a portion of the hydrogel was freeze-dried and sealed for storage.

Seven hydrogel sample groups were established: H–CUR (2% curdlan control), H–NG (2% curdlan and 2% NG), H–UG (2% curdlan and 2% UG), H–G–D (2% curdlan and 2% G–D), H–G–F (2% curdlan and 2% G–F), H–UG–D (2% curdlan and UG–D), and H–UG–F (2% curdlan and UG–F).

Hydrogel characterization

WHC

WHC was determined using a slight modification of a previously reported method [24]. Briefly, each hydrogel sample (W0 = 1.0 g) was accurately weighed in a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully discarded, and the remaining gel was weighed (W1). All measurements were performed in triplicate. WHC (%) was calculated as(6)WHC%=(W1/W0)×100

Hardness

Hydrogel hardness was determined using a texture analyzer (TA–XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Surrey, UK) [25]. Samples with a height of 2.0 cm and diameter of 2.2 cm were penetrated with a 40 mm cylindrical probe (P/40). Measurements were performed in triplicate at 20–25 °C, a compression strain of 70%, pretest speed of 2.0 mm/s, test speed of 1.0 mm/s, posttest speed of 4.0 mm/s, maximum load of 2 kg, head speed of 2.0 mm/s, and trigger force of 5 g.

Porosity

Hydrogel porosity was determined using the solvent replacement method [26]. Freeze-dried hydrogel samples were weighed (m1), immersed in absolute ethanol, gently agitated at room temperature until complete saturation, removed, dried with absolute ethanol, and weighed (m2). All measurements were performed in triplicate. Porosity was calculated as(7)Porosity%={(m2-m1)/(ρ×V)}×100

where ρ is the density of ethanol (0.789 g/cm3), and V (cm3) is the sample volume.

Microstructure

The microstructures of freeze-dried hydrogels were observed using focused-ion-beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM; FEI Nova Nano SEM200, FEI Company, Oregon, USA). The samples were affixed to a conductive adhesive tape on a stainless-steel stage and coated with platinum using ion sputter coating (E-1031, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) for 110 s under vacuum. FIB-SEM observations were performed at an accelerating voltage of 10.0 kV, and images were captured at a magnification of × 80.

Rheological properties

Rheological measurements were carried out using a rheometer (MCR 92, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) equipped with a 25 mm–diameter parallel plate [27]. Each hydrogel sample was carefully loaded on the lower plate, and the gap between the plates was maintained at 1 mm. All measurement conditions were maintained constant throughout the frequency, strain, and temperature sweep tests. The linear viscoelastic region (LVR) was determined by conducting strain sweep tests using a strain range of 0.01–10% and frequency of 1 Hz. Frequency sweep tests were then performed at an angular frequency of 0.1–100 rad/s, temperature of 25 °C, and strain of 0.5% (within the LVR). Subsequently, strain sweep tests were conducted at a frequency of 1 Hz and strain amplitude of 0.01–100%. Temperature sweep tests were performed at a frequency of 1 Hz and strain of 0.5%. The temperature was increased from 25 to 90 °C at 5 °C/min to evaluate thermal stability. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded to describe the dynamic viscoelastic behavior of the hydrogels during heating. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Thermal properties

The thermal properties of the hydrogels were analyzed as described elsewhere [24]. For thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), freeze-dried samples (∼10 mg) were placed in a ceramic crucible and heated from 25 to 500 °C at 10 °C/min in a flow of nitrogen (20 mL/min) using a TGA 550 instrument (TA Instruments, New Castle, USA).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was performed for freeze-dried samples (∼5 mg) using a DSC 25 instrument (TA Instruments, New Castle, USA) under the conditions used for TGA measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS PASW Statistics software (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were performed using three independently prepared replicates. Each replicate was prepared in a separate tube under the same processing conditions and used as an independent experimental unit. Measurements were conducted in triplicate when applicable, and the mean value of each independently prepared sample was used for statistical analysis. Data were presented as means ± standard deviations. One-way analysis of variance followed by Duncan’s multiple range post hoc test was performed to identify significant differences among groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Graphical analysis was performed using the OriginPro 2025 software (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Selection of glycation conditions

Dextran and fucoidan were selected for conjugation with gliadin to investigate the influence of polysaccharide charge. The former was used as a neutral polysaccharide capable of improving protein–water interactions without markedly changing the net charge, whereas the latter was selected as a negatively charged sulfated polysaccharide capable of providing additional electrostatic interactions in protein–polysaccharide systems [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. The overall response was highest at an ultrasound power of 400 W and reaction temperature of 90 °C, whereas prolonged heating for 6 h weakened the response. These changes may be associated with excessive protein unfolding or aggregation under severe ultrasound and heating conditions, which can reduce the accessibility of reactive amino groups for glycation [28]. As screening was performed using a one-factor-at-a-time approach, interactions among processing variables were not evaluated. Based on the normalized responses and processing feasibility, a power of 400 W, temperature of 90 °C, and heating duration of 4 h were concluded to be optimal for gliadin–polysaccharide conjugation.

Characterization of glycated gliadin complexes

Grafting degree and browning intensity

Grafting degree was used as an indicator of the reduction in the content of accessible free amino groups rather than as a direct measure of covalent conjugation [18]. In the OPA assay, the decreased availability of free amino groups can result not only from Maillard glycation but also from noncovalent protein–polysaccharide association, alkali-induced structural rearrangement, and heat-induced aggregation [32]. Therefore, the obtained values (Fig. 2A) were interpreted as apparent glycation degrees for Maillard-treated gliadin–polysaccharide complexes. G–F and UG–F exhibited significantly higher grafting degrees than G–D and UG–D, respectively (p < 0.05; Fig. 2A), possibly because of the ability of the anionic sulfate groups of fucoidan to promote close contact with the positively charged regions of gliadin through electrostatic association [33]. Ultrasound pretreatment slightly increased the grafting degree of G–D and G–F, probably because cavitation induced the partial unfolding of gliadin and increased the accessibility of its reactive amino groups [6]. The Maillard reaction involves an initial intermediate formation stage and a final melanoidin generation stage. The reaction pathway is influenced by the structural characteristics of protein–polysaccharide complexes and processing conditions. Fig. 2B and C show the intermediate (A294) and final (A420) products of the Maillard reaction, respectively. No samples exhibited significant differences in intermediate content (p > 0.05). However, polysaccharide type influenced final-product contents. A420 levels decreased for G–D and UG–D (p < 0.05). According to a previous study, the cavitation-induced rearrangement or decomposition of Maillard reaction intermediates suppresses polymerization and melanoidin formation, resulting in low A420 [34]. In contrast, UG–F exhibited a significantly higher A420 than G–F (p < 0.05). Fucoidan, an anionic sulfated polysaccharide, may enhance the Maillard reaction by promoting electrostatic interactions with gliadin and thereby increasing reactant proximity and reducing the number of carbonyl groups. Accordingly, the higher A420 of UG–F suggests that ultrasound pretreatment facilitated the Maillard reaction, probably by inducing charge polarization or generating free radicals to strengthen fucoidan–gliadin interactions.

Ultrasound treatment affected the physical and chemical environment of the Maillard reaction, including radical generation, molecular mobility, and exposure to reactive residues, with the reaction pathway depending on the molecular structure, charge distribution, and hydrophilicity of protein and polysaccharide components. The contrasting browning intensities observed for UG–D and UG–F suggest that ultrasound pretreatment may differentially influence specific stages of the Maillard reaction.

Structural characterization by FT-IR spectroscopy

FT-IR spectroscopic analysis demonstrated that ultrasound pretreatment induced a structural rearrangement of gliadin, facilitating interactions with polysaccharides and promoting glycation (Fig. 3A). Distinct changes were observed in the –OH stretching region. The peak of NG appeared at 3272 cm−1, whereas that of UG was red-shifted to 3296 cm−1. This shift reflects ultrasound-induced cavitation and vibrational effects, which partially unfolded gliadin and weakened pre-existing intramolecular hydrogen bonds [8]. Such structure loosening increased the accessibility of free amino groups and thereby facilitated Maillard-related reactions and polysaccharide association, as supported by grafting degree and browning intensity data (Fig. 2). Compared with NG and UG, G–D, UG–D, G–F, and UG–F displayed broader bands in the 3200–3600 cm−1 region, which indicates that new intermolecular hydrogen bonds were formed upon polysaccharide binding [[13], [35]]. The most pronounced broadening was observed for UG–F and attributed to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic association between the anionic sulfate groups of fucoidan and positively charged regions of gliadin.Fig. 3Structural characterization of gliadin and its conjugates. The decreased intensity of amide I and II bands was closely related to secondary-structure rearrangement, as confirmed by the corresponding analysis (Fig. 3B). Random coil content decreased significantly for G–D (25% to 20%) and UG–D (26% to 16%), while slight decreases were observed for G–F (25% to 24%) and UG–F (26% to 24%), which indicates that increased noncovalent interactions reduced protein chain flexibility and promoted a more compact conformation [20]. Compared with UG, UG–D and UG–F displayed concurrent increases in α-helix and β-turn contents, which suggests that glycation, particularly following ultrasound pretreatment, promoted ordered structural organization that was probably associated with enhanced hydrogen bonding [36]. β-Sheet content remained at ∼38% across all treated samples, which suggests that this structural element serves as a stable backbone core of gliadin and is largely resistant to glycation-induced reorganization.

Spectral bands associated with polysaccharides and Maillard-related products were also examined as indirect indicators of complex formation. The band at 1014 cm−1 observed for treated samples was assigned to the stretching of C–O–C units in glycosidic structures derived from polysaccharide chains and Maillard-related products, and its higher intensity for UG–D and UG–F suggests a greater contribution of polysaccharide-related structures and stronger protein–polysaccharide association after Maillard treatment [37]. Additionally, bands corresponding to Maillard reaction intermediates, including Amadori compounds and pyrazine-related structures, were observed in the 700–950 and 1150–1154 cm−1 regions, respectively, and were more pronounced for UG–F and UG–D, which supports the occurrence of Maillard-related transformations in these samples [34]. However, secondary-structure shifts, glycosidic stretching, and Maillard intermediate bands are insufficient to independently establish the chemical nature of the interaction in question, as similar changes may accompany noncovalent complex formation, pH-induced structural rearrangement, or heat-induced aggregation. Therefore, the above findings should be interpreted in conjunction with those of complementary analyses to distinguish covalent glycation from noncovalent association.

In summary, ultrasound treatment promoted gliadin–polysaccharide binding by inducing partial gliadin dissociation and exposing amino groups. The structural rearrangement depended on polysaccharide type. G–D and UG–D exhibited an ordered structure with decreased random coil and increased α-helix contents, whereas UG-F showed the most pronounced increase in the extents of hydrogen bonding and Maillard intermediate formation, which was ascribed to the high binding affinity and strong structural influence of fucoidan.

Intrinsic fluorescence analysis

Intrinsic fluorescence analysis has been used to evaluate tertiary-structure changes in proteins based on the fluorescence characteristics of aromatic amino acids, including Trp, Tyr, and Phe [20]. NG and UG maintained their FI, whereas glycated samples displayed reduced FI owing to gliadin–polysaccharide interactions (Fig. 3C). Among the glycated samples, ultrasound pretreatment had no significant effect. This result was attributed to a structural rearrangement that shielded the fluorescent residues of gliadin from the polysaccharide surroundings and thereby suppressed fluorescence emission [37]. The most substantial decrease was observed for G–F and UG–F, as the anionic functional groups of fucoidan interacted more strongly with gliadin, enhancing structural constraints and quenching effects. These results are consistent with the higher grafting degree observed for G–F and UG–F (Fig. 2A). Overall, gliadin glycation induced structural modifications with fluorescence quenching, whereas ultrasound pretreatment had no significant effect.

Solubility

Table 1 lists the solubilities of glycated gliadin complexes. As gliadin is a prolamin-type protein that dissolves only in 70% ethanol or basic environments, NG showed inherently limited aqueous solubility. UG exhibited a significantly higher solubility (0.89 mg/mL) than NG (p < 0.05), which indicates that ultrasound treatment induced structural modifications enhancing the accessibility of gliadin to the aqueous phase. However, all glycated samples featured a significantly higher solubility than NG and UG (p < 0.05). The effect of ultrasound pretreatment on conjugate solubility depended on polysaccharide type. UG–D (1.67 mg/mL) showed a solubility comparable with that of G–D (1.64 mg/mL; p > 0.05). Conversely, UG–F (1.45 mg/mL) exhibited a significantly higher solubility than G–F (1.37 mg/mL; p < 0.05), which indicated that ultrasound pretreatment slightly improved solubility, particularly for fucoidan conjugates. Solubility increased upon glycation, probably because of the incorporation of hydrophilic polysaccharide chains. G–D and UG–D showed the highest solubility (p < 0.05), which was attributed to the neutral nature and high water solubility of dextran. As a nonionic polysaccharide, dextran is less likely to induce the electrostatic complex formation commonly observed in protein mixtures with anionic polysaccharides, thereby reducing protein aggregation and improving dispersibility [38]. H0

H0 is a critical indicator of the structural unfolding or aggregation state of a protein [39]. The ultrasound pretreatment of NG significantly increased H0 (p < 0.05; Table 1). This phenomenon was attributed to the physical effects of cavitation, which induces protein unfolding and structural rearrangement, exposing the internal hydrophobic amino acid residues to aqueous environments [6]. By contrast, compared with NG and UG, all glycated conjugates exhibited significantly reduced H0 (p < 0.05). This reduction was attributed to two primary mechanisms. First, thermal aggregation during glycation leads to hydrophobic group burial within the protein core. Second, the steric hindrance provided by the polysaccharide chains causes the formation of a hydrophilic coating on the protein surface, thereby limiting the accessibility of the ANS probe to hydrophobic sites [40].

Since this study was conducted under aqueous conditions, the shielding effect of polysaccharides was interpreted to be more dominant than thermal aggregation. In particular, G–F and UG–F exhibited the most pronounced decreases in H0. This finding was ascribed to the anionic sulfate groups of fucoidan facilitating electrostatic interactions and thereby severely limiting ANS accessibility [41]. In addition, the lower H0 of UG–F compared with that of G–F suggests that ultrasound pretreatment expanded the gliadin structure. This structural expansion promoted polysaccharide grafting, thereby inducing a robust hydrophilic coating effect associated with a high grafting degree (Fig. 2A). The enhanced hydrophilic shielding is consistent with the increased solubilities of G–D, UG–D, G–F, and UG-F compared with those of NG and UG (Table 1), probably owing to improved surface hydration facilitating dispersion in aqueous media. Overall, these data demonstrate that ultrasound pretreatment and glycation reduced the H0 of gliadin complexes by enhancing polysaccharide-driven hydrophilic shielding and limiting hydrophobic-site exposure.

Particle size and PDI

Emulsifying properties are intrinsically related to particle dimensions [42]. Ultrasound treatment decreased the mean hydrodynamic diameter of gliadin from 2501.04 nm for G to 651.58 nm for UG, although this difference was not statistically significant (Table 1). This decrease may be associated with the partial disintegration of gliadin aggregates and disruption of interchain interactions caused by cavitation and shear forces during ultrasound treatment [43]. Consequently, the PDI of UG was lower than that of NG, indicating a more uniform dispersion of smaller particles within the solution. UG–D exhibited a higher particle size than UG, which may be associated with dextran chain incorporation and the resulting increase in hydrodynamic diameter. Despite the size increase, UG–D maintained a relatively uniform particle size distribution, which suggests that dextran grafting induced controlled particle growth without causing severe aggregation. UG–F formed macrocomplexes with particle sizes more than 10 times those of other samples (p < 0.05) and exhibited a markedly increased PDI, which indicated pronounced polydispersity and heterogeneous aggregation behavior [44]. This behavior was ascribed to strong electrostatic association between the anionic fucoidan and cationic regions of gliadin, which could have promoted multivalent bridging and aggregate growth to afford loosely connected macroaggregates [[45], [46]].

In summary, ultrasound treatment effectively reduced gliadin particle size and improved dispersion uniformity. Dextran grafting caused moderate particle enlargement while maintaining a relatively narrow size distribution. In contrast, fucoidan grafting afforded large macrocomplexes with a markedly elevated PDI, which indicates heterogeneous aggregation probably driven by strong polysaccharide-mediated association and bridging interactions.

EAI and ESI

The effects of ultrasound pretreatment and polysaccharide conjugation on the emulsifying properties of gliadin are presented in Table 1. The EAI represents the total interfacial area created by protein adsorption at the oil–water interface, whereas the ESI indicates the capacity of the emulsion to maintain its stability over time. Compared with NG, UG exhibited a significantly higher ESI (p < 0.05), which was attributed to protein structure unfolding that enhanced interfacial adsorption and droplet formation efficiency [7]. This enhancement was driven by ultrasound-induced hydrophobic residue exposure, as reflected by the increased H0, which provided a stronger driving force for interfacial gliadin adsorption and thereby improved emulsifying stability [7]. In addition, this improvement can be interpreted in conjunction with the results of particle size and PDI analysis, as ultrasound treatment substantially reduced particle size and improved dispersion uniformity. Compared with NG, G–D showed no significant change in EAI (p > 0.05) and a significantly higher ESI (p < 0.05), while UG–D exhibited a slight yet significant increase in the EAI (p < 0.05) and higher ESI (p < 0.05). Overall, dextran grafting improved the EAI and ESI of gliadin, probably because the increased hydrophilicity resulting from the reduced H0 slightly enhanced interfacial stability. Since dextran grafting increased particle size compared with that of UG, we concluded that glycation may improve emulsion stability by enhancing hydration and steric contributions.

Compared with NG, G–F exhibited a lower EAI and higher ESI. By contrast, compared with UG, UG–F showed a lower EAI and ESI (p < 0.05). Overall, fucoidan grafting increased ESI without ultrasound pretreatment, whereas applying fucoidan grafting to UG with a reduced particle size decreased the EAI and ESI and thereby reduced the overall emulsifying performance. This behavior was ascribed to the excess hydrophilicity imparted by the sulfate groups of fucoidan, which significantly reduced the interfacial mobility of the conjugates [47]. Generally, H0 is positively correlated with emulsifying capacity [42], a trend also observed in this study. These findings are consistent with those of particle size and PDI analyses: fucoidan conjugates formed large and highly polydisperse macrocomplexes, which could hinder efficient interfacial coverage and compromise emulsifying performance [43]. Overall, ultrasound pretreatment enhanced emulsifying performance, primarily by reducing particle size and increasing the hydrophobic driving force, whereas the influence of glycation depended on polysaccharide type and resulting dispersion state of gliadin.

Hardness

The hardness of hydrogels is intrinsically related to their structural rigidity and WHC. Herein, hardness and WHC exhibited consistent trends (Table 2). H–CUR showed the lowest hardness (p < 0.05), which was attributed to the insufficient formation of a three-dimensional network and the resulting lack of structural stability [26]. Although H–NG and H–UG exhibited slightly higher hardness than H–CUR, no significant differences were observed between these samples (p > 0.05), i.e., the reinforcing effect of gliadin was limited. These results correlated with those of WHC and structural stability analyses (Table 2, Fig. S1), suggesting that the hydrophobic nature of gliadin hindered the formation of a robust network. Glycated samples (H–G–D, H–UG–D, H–G–F, and H–UG–F) showed a significant increase in hardness (p < 0.05), which was ascribed to their increased hydrophilicity and hydrogen-bonding capacity. These complexes established a denser structure within the curdlan network. All glycated hydrogels (H–G–D, H–UG–D, H–G–F, and H–UG–F) exhibited significantly higher hardness than H–CUR, H–NG, and H–UG (p < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed among the glycated hydrogels (p > 0.05). This indicates that glycation-driven polysaccharide conjugation, rather than ultrasound pretreatment, primarily contributed to gel network reinforcement and the resulting increase in hardness. Protein–polysaccharide interactions can strengthen gel networks by promoting structural rearrangement toward a more compact configuration [52]. Overall, glycated gliadin conjugates improved hydrogel hardness by reinforcing the curdlan-based network.

Porosity

Hydrogel porosity is a key indicator of internal-network uniformity and density and is typically negatively correlated with WHC and hardness. Table 2 lists hydrogel porosities. H–CUR exhibited the highest porosity (66.20%; p < 0.05), which was attributed to the insufficiency of 2% curdlan for forming a dense three-dimensional network and the resulting generation of large ice crystals during freezing. The sublimation of these crystals generated a macroporous structure with high porosity (∼80%), which limited stable network formation [26]. H–NG exhibited a slightly lower porosity (60.79%) than H–CUR, which suggests that gliadin acted as a filler within the gel network, partially occupying space and inhibiting pore formation. However, owing to its inherently low hydrophilicity, gliadin could not induce strong interactions with the curdlan matrix to fully densify the network [4]. H–UG (68.35%) exhibited a slightly higher porosity than H–NG (60.79%), which was explained by the physical interference with the initial hydrogen bonding between curdlan chains caused by ultrasound-treated gliadin particles. Consequently, a looser structural arrangement was formed, as reflected by the increased porosity. In contrast, samples containing glycated complexes showed a significant reduction in porosity (48.20–51.30%; p < 0.05). Similar to the increased WHC and hardness, the gliadin–polysaccharide complexes established a compact and continuous cross-linked network within the curdlan matrix, increasing the number of interchain connection points and hydrogen-bonding sites, enhancing network density, restricting ice crystal growth during freezing, and thus causing the formation of smaller pores [53].

Microstructure

The microstructure of freeze-dried hydrogels was examined using FIB-SEM (Fig. 4). The identified morphological features were generally consistent with the results of porosity analysis (Table 2), with high porosity corresponding to large and heterogeneous pores and reduced porosity to a relatively fine and compact network structure. For H–CUR (Fig. 4A), large irregular pores were observed, along with an incomplete cylindrical structure, which indicates a weak structural framework resulting from low network density [54]. H–NG and H–UG (Fig. 4B–C) exhibited a slight reduction in pore size, and visual observations supported a filling effect, with gliadin particles partially occupying the interstitial spaces within the network. Compared with H–CUR, which exhibited a macroporous structure, H–G–D and H–UG–D (Fig. 4D–E) exhibited denser surface morphologies with smaller pores. This difference suggests that the gliadin–dextran conjugates helped fill the voids between curdlan chains and increase the cross-linking density. H–G–F and H–UG–F (Fig. 4F–G) exhibited a denser network morphology than H–G–D and H–UG–D and featured pores virtually indiscernible at the employed magnification. This morphology was ascribed to the negative charge and branched structure of fucoidan, which could have promoted multivalent interactions and physical entanglement with curdlan to afford a highly compact three-dimensional structure. Therefore, combined with the increased hardness and decreased porosity data, the above results suggest that the conjugates assisted in establishing a more cohesive structural framework, although the employed magnification limits the direct quantification of micropore uniformity.

Although H–G–F and H–UG–F also exhibited high viscoelasticity, they showed lower G′ values than H–G–D and H–UG–D, probably because of large aggregate formation. Thermal properties

Thermal decomposition behavior was evaluated using TGA and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) (Fig. 6A). All samples exhibited a two-step mass loss pattern featuring an initial moisture loss followed by polymeric backbone decomposition. Below 150 °C, all hydrogels showed mass losses of 10–12% attributed to the volatilization of free and weakly bound water. The sharp mass loss at 270–330 °C was ascribed to the thermal degradation of the β-1,3-glucan framework of curdlan and protein chains of gliadin [58]. H–CUR showed the most rapid decomposition at ∼300 °C (88.26% to 39.90%) and exhibited the lowest residue content at 500 °C. This reduction indicated the limited high-temperature durability of the curdlan network in the absence of additional reinforcing components. The higher residue content of H–NG at 300–500 °C was probably due not to improved network stability but rather to greater carbonaceous char formation from gliadin during pyrolysis. In addition, protein-derived carbon and nonvolatile inorganic residues could have remained as a solid layer even at 500 °C, contributing to the elevated residual mass [24]. The final residue content at 500 °C followed the order H–NG (25%) > H–G–F (20–21%) > H–G–D (17–18%) > H–CUR (15%). The low residue content of H–CUR was attributed to the rapid volatilization of the polysaccharide-based matrix at elevated temperatures, whereas the protein-containing samples produced larger amounts of carbonaceous residue during pyrolysis. The thermal transition behavior of hydrogels was further investigated by DSC at 25–400 °C (Fig. 6B). All samples exhibited a distinct endothermic peak between 80 and 100 °C (∼90 °C for H–CUR, 90–95 °C for H–NG, 95 °C for H–G–D, and 80–110 °C for H–G–F). The lower Tp of H–NG compared with that of H–CUR suggests that protein particle incorporation partially disrupted curdlan network continuity and thereby promoted an earlier degradation onset, in line with TGA results (Fig. 6A). By contrast, H–G–D and H–G–F exhibited a clear increase in Tp, which indicates that glycation enhanced molecular cohesion through additional hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions and thus delayed thermal decomposition. Moreover, the appearance of split- and shoulder-type exothermic peaks implies a two-step degradation process, reflecting the sequential decomposition of distinct structural domains within the composite network. Collectively, these results indicate that glycation promoted the formation of a dense and reorganized hydrogel structure, thereby enhancing thermal resistance [[35], [62]].

In summary, H–NG exhibited earlier degradation and higher char residue content owing to the partial weakening of network continuity and formation of protein-derived pyrolysis products. However, H–G–D and H–G–F showed delayed degradation and stepwise decomposition behavior because of strengthened noncovalent interactions and partial crosslinking. These findings demonstrate that G–D and G–F enhanced the thermal stability and structural integrity of curdlan hydrogels.

Conclusion

Ultrasound pretreatment combined with heat-induced glycation effectively improved the hydrophilicity and functional performance of gliadin in aqueous systems. This treatment partially unfolded gliadin molecules, thereby exposing reactive amino groups and facilitating subsequent glycation with polysaccharides. Dextran– and fucoidan–gliadin conjugates exhibited significantly enhanced solubility and reduced H0 compared with unfunctionalized gliadin. When incorporated into curdlan-based hydrogels, these conjugates notably improved hardness, WHC, and viscoelastic behavior. These enhancements were associated with a homogeneous and compact microstructure. Glycation enhanced the thermal stability of the hydrogel network. Thus, through ultrasound pretreatment and glycation, gliadin was effectively converted into a hydrophilic structurally flexible protein suitable for aqueous food matrices. This approach provides a practical strategy for broadening the scope of gliadin applications in food systems and other biopolymer-based matrices.
