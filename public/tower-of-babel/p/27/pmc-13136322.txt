Development and characterization of an aloe vera-based bio-emulsifier for sustainable bio-emulsion liquid membrane systems for wastewater treatment
Authors: Manisha Wakle, Snigdha Khuntia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emulsion instability poses a challenge in the development of emulsion liquid membrane (ELM) systems, as coalescence and phase separation hinder their effectiveness in wastewater treatment. This study presents a bio-emulsion liquid membrane (Bio-ELM) system that uses Aloe vera leaf (AVL) extract as a natural bio-emulsifier. AVL extract was characterized using HPLC and GC–MS, confirming the presence of amphiphilic bioactive compounds that reduce interfacial tension and form uniform nanoscale droplets. The critical micelle concentration (CMC) of the bio-emulsifier was 2% v/v, which corresponded to a surface tension of 18 mN/m. Bio-ELM exhibited droplet sizes of 50–200 nm, shear-thinning behavior verified by rheological analysis, and FTIR evidence indicating bio-emulsifier incorporation into the membrane phase. Optimization studies identified the optimal conditions as a bio-emulsifier concentration of 2.5% v/v, stirring speed of 800 rpm, internal phase concentration of 0.25 M, pH 8, and membrane-to-internal phase ratio of 1:1.5. Under these conditions, Bio-ELM achieved a 98% extraction efficiency for methylene blue dye from a synthetic aqueous solution. Dimensionless numbers (Capillary, Bond, and Marangoni numbers) provide insights into the balance of viscous, interfacial, and elastic forces governing emulsion stability. Stability assessments showed that the Bio-ELM maintained structural integrity and performance for up to 15 days. This study demonstrates the potential of an Aloe vera-derived bio-emulsifier to address emulsion instability, offering a sustainable alternative for environmental remediation using Bio-ELM technology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41280-1.

Introduction

Emulsion liquid membrane (ELM) technology has attracted significant attention owing to its efficiency, selectivity, and potential for separation and purification. ELM is an advanced separation technique based on a three-phase system consisting of an external feed phase, membrane phase, and internal phase. In this configuration, the membrane phase encapsulates the internal phase to form water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion droplets, which are subsequently dispersed in an external aqueous phase1. Target solutes diffuse from the external phase through the membrane phase and are trapped in the internal phase, driven by the concentration gradients. This simultaneous extraction and stripping mechanism offers high mass transfer efficiency, low solvent consumption, and strong selectivity, making ELM particularly well-suited for wastewater treatment applications2. However, the practical implementation of ELM is strongly limited by emulsion instability, including coalescence, swelling, and membrane rupture, highlighting the need for improved stabilization strategies. Recently, there has been increasing interest in substituting synthetic surfactants with natural, biodegradable alternatives, driven by environmental concerns, toxicity issues, and high costs associated with synthetic emulsifiers3. These natural emulsifiers stabilize the ELM, thereby enhancing the extraction efficiency in wastewater treatment. The natural-based material employed in the ELM is referred to as Bio-ELM (Bio-emulsion liquid membrane)3. Bio-ELM is a cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and operationally simple method characterized by high emulsion stabilization and extraction efficiency4,5. Various green diluents, including vegetable, edible, and essential oils, have been used in the membrane phase of ELMs. Bio-emulsifiers, such as naturally extracted plant-based compounds, can effectively stabilize emulsions, and when combined with green diluents, they form an optimal pairing that enhances emulsion stability3.

Aloe vera, belonging to the Liliaceae family, is a medicinal plant widely used in the cosmetics, food, and pharmaceutical industries6. It is cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions and has gained prominence as a promising candidate owing to its availability, low cost, and ease of harvest, rendering it an attractive source of bio-based emulsifying agents. The constituents of Aloe vera leaves include glycosides, proteins, chromones, amino acids, carbohydrates, minerals, lipids, vitamins, sugars, and other phytochemicals such as alkaloids, phenols, saponins, and lignin7,8. Saponin-based and phenolic compounds exhibit emulsifying, antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties, contributing to the plant’s surface activity and stabilizing characteristics9. These natural components can reduce surface tension and enhance oil–water interface stability, positioning Aloe vera as a potential bioemulsifier for sustainable emulsion systems. Utilizing Aloe vera as a bio-emulsifier in ELM systems, in combination with a synthetic surfactant, enables it to serve as a co-emulsifier rather than a complete replacement, while its interfacial activity and antioxidant properties enhance emulsion stability, improve extraction efficiency, and contribute to the overall sustainability of the ELM process10–12. Given its low cost and ease of processing, Aloe vera presents an economical solution for large-scale ELM operations, particularly for water treatment and pollutant removal13.

Based on the literature on Aloe vera leaves (AVL), Oliveira et al. reported the use of AVL as a natural coagulant in water treatment, demonstrating its eco-friendly potential as an alternative to conventional chemical coagulants. The polysaccharide-rich gel extracted from AVL facilitated turbidity removal down to 5 NTU, meeting drinking water standards, while being biodegradable and producing no toxic residues14. These findings emphasize the versatility of AVL in wastewater purification applications, suggesting that its functional components may extend beyond conventional coagulation to advanced separation techniques, including ELM processes. In parallel, green diluents such as rice bran oil, palm oil, waste cooking oil, and paraffin oil have been employed in ELM synthesis15,16. The recent literature on the use of plant-based bioemulsifiers for stabilizing ELM synthesis is limited. Several studies have examined the replacement of petrochemical-based diluents with environmentally sustainable alternatives such as vegetable oils. Recently, Sharmmah and Manuel reported the synthesis of a green ELM utilizing cellulose as the emulsifier and a green diluent for the extraction of methylene blue dye from wastewater17. Tmakova et al. investigated the surface-active properties and antioxidant activity of plant-derived surfactants as substitutes for synthetic ones18. Firdous et al. extracted AVL and conducted qualitative phytochemical analyses to confirm the presence of various compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, carbohydrates, proteins, saponins, phenols, terpenoids, and phytosterols19. These studies suggest that green diluents can effectively replace petroleum-based solvents without significantly compromising the extraction efficiency. However, the stability of ELM is not solely determined by the choice of diluent; destabilization mechanisms such as membrane coalescence, internal phase leakage, and Ostwald ripening can still occur, ultimately compromising the emulsion’s structural integrity and reducing its operational performance20. Surfactants are essential for mitigating the destabilization phenomena in ELM. Although synthetic surfactants, such as Span 80 and Tween 80, are widely employed because of their effective emulsifying performance and partial biodegradability, incorporating plant-derived bio-emulsifiers as co-emulsifiers offers a sustainable strategy to reduce dependence on synthetic surfactants while enhancing emulsion stability. Plant-derived bio-emulsifiers, obtained from renewable sources, provide amphiphilic structures capable of reducing interfacial tension and improving membrane integrity, while offering biodegradability and non-toxicity. Only a few studies have explored the integration of green diluents with plant-derived bioemulsifiers in ELM systems, highlighting a significant research gap.

To the best of our knowledge, no systematic studies have reported the direct use of Aloe vera leaf extract as a bioemulsifier in the ELM system. The present work introduces a Bio-ELM formulation combining soybean oil as a green diluent, Span 80 as a primary surfactant, and AVL-derived bio-emulsifier as a co-emulsifier to enhance stability while reducing reliance on synthetic components. In addition to demonstrating high extraction efficiency, methylene blue (MB) was selected as a model pollutant because of its extensive application in the textile, paper, and pharmaceutical industries and its frequent occurrence in industrial effluents. MB is a chemically stable cationic dye that remains highly detectable even at low concentrations and is resistant to conventional biological degradation. This study provides a comprehensive physicochemical characterization, rheological analysis, microscopic stability evaluation, and dimensionless number (Ca, Bo, and Ma–1) modelling to elucidate the stability mechanisms of Bio-ELM. This integrated experimental-theoretical framework, together with the utilization of plate-derived bio-emulsifiers, represents a novel and sustainable approach for advancing ELM-based wastewater treatment.

Experimental section

Materials

The chemicals and materials used in this study were aloe vera leaves (harvested from a personal garden, species barbadensis miller), methanol (Merck Life Science Pvt. Ltd.), acetonitrile (Merck Life Science Pvt. Ltd.), sorbitan monooleate (Span 80, purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. Ltd.), soybean oil (doTERRA India Pvt. Ltd.), isopropyl alcohol (Merck India), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) used as an internal phase (IP) or stripping agent, and methylene blue dye (Loba Chemie, Mumbai, India). For pH adjustment, NaOH and H2SO4 (Merck, India) were used. The aloe vera used in this study is commonly found in Gujarat, India, and is also used commercially for personal care and pharmaceutical products.

Methods

Extraction process of bio-emulsifier from AVL

Mature AVL was used to extract the bio-emulsifier. To maintain cleanliness and avoid contamination, gloves were worn while handling and cutting leaves. The entire leaves, including both the gel and rind, were processed without any separation. The whole leaf material was blended for 5 s, and a 70% methanol solution was added at a ratio of 1:2 (sample: solvent) and mixed at 1500 rpm for 60 min. After solvent treatment, the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 30 min to separate the supernatant from the solid residue. The collected supernatant, as shown in Fig. 1, was concentrated by evaporating excess methanol using a rotary evaporator and stored in sealed bottles at 4–5 °C for further use.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the process pathway for the extraction of AVL bio-emulsifier from aloe vera leaves.

Schematic diagram of the process pathway for the extraction of AVL bio-emulsifier from aloe vera leaves.

Synthesis process of bio-ELM

An AVL-based bio-emulsifier was used for the Bio-ELM synthesis. The membrane phase (MP) was formulated by combining a bio-emulsifier (2%v/v), Span 80 (2%v/v), and soybean oil (96% v/v). This mixture was homogenized at 1500 rpm for 60 min to ensure uniform dispersion and to form a stable MP. An internal phase (IP), comprising 0.25 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH), was added dropwise to the MP while maintaining the stirring speed at 800 rpm for 5 min. The MP: IP ratio was maintained at 1:1. This procedure facilitated IP encapsulation within the membrane phase, leading to Bio-ELM synthesis, as shown in Fig. 2. The white milky texture indicates that the Bio-ELM was successfully synthesized.Fig. 2Schematic representation of the synthesis of Bio-ELM using AVL-based bio-emulsifier and soybean oil as a diluent.

Schematic representation of the synthesis of Bio-ELM using AVL-based bio-emulsifier and soybean oil as a diluent.

Characterization of bio-emulsifier and Bio-ELM

High-performance liquid chromatography analysis

HPLC-grade solvents were procured from Merck Life Science Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India, and ultrapure water was obtained from a Milli-Q water-purification system (Millipore, USA). Chromatographic analysis of the AVL bio-emulsifier extract was performed using a Shimadzu LC-2030 HPLC system equipped with a reverse-phase C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of water (65%) and acetonitrile (35%) and was delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min under isocratic conditions. The column temperature was maintained at 20 °C, and the injection volume was 10 μL for all the samples. Detection was carried out using a dual-wavelength UV detector set at 235 and 254 nm, based on the reported absorption maxima of Aloe vera phytoconstituents. Previous studies have shown that Aloin A and B (anthraquinone glycosides) exhibit strong absorbance near 235 nm, whereas chromones, phenolic acids, and aloe-emodin derivatives respond more intensely at 254 nm. Therefore, both wavelengths were employed to ensure the comprehensive detection of the bioactive components present in the AVL extract.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis

GC–MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 5977C single quadrupole GC/MSD system equipped with a (5% phenyl)-methylpolysiloxane capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm film thickness) using helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow of 1.0 mL/min. Samples (1 µL) were injected in the splitless mode at an inlet temperature of 250 °C. The oven temperature was programmed to 110 °C (2 min), ramped at 10 °C min⁻1 to 200 °C, then at 5 °C min⁻1 to 280 °C, and held for 9 min, followed by post-run cooling to the initial temperature. The transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole temperatures were maintained at 280 °C, 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. Mass spectra were acquired in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV with a scan range of m/z 35–650, scan rate of 10 scans/s, and a solvent delay of 1.0–1.5 min. The detector voltage was set by auto-tuning/auto-gain before the batch run. Data were acquired in full scan mode for qualitative profiling, and selected ion monitoring (SIM) with dwell times of 20–50 ms per ion was applied when required to ensure at least 10–12 data points across chromatographic peaks. Compound identification was performed using the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) mass spectral library by matching the experimental spectra with the reference spectra.

CMC determination by surface tension method

To determine the critical micelle concentration (CMC), the concentration of the extracted bio-emulsifier from AVL was varied in the range of 0.5–5% v/v, and the measurements were performed in triplicate using the Wilhelmy plate (Model K20, KRÜSS GmbH, Germany) method. The surface tension was monitored for 2 h while the plate was immersed in the respective solutions. This duration was considered sufficient to evaluate the effect of the bio-emulsifier concentration at room temperature. The underlying principle of this method is that the surface tension of aqueous solutions containing surface-active agents decreases progressively with the addition of the bio-emulsifier until the CMC is reached, beyond which it remains nearly constant.

Zeta potential measurement

The zeta potential of the bio-emulsifier was determined using a Zeta-sizer Nano system (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK) equipped with electrophoretic light scattering (ELS). Measurements were performed at 0 min (Run 1), 30 min (Run 2), and 60 min (Run 3) after bioemulsifier extraction to evaluate time-dependent aggregation and stability. The samples were diluted 1:50 in deionized water to avoid multiple scattering prior to measurement. Approximately 1 mL of the diluted sample was loaded into a disposable folded capillary cell (DTS1070).

Dynamic light scattering analysis

The droplet size distribution of the bio-emulsifier and Bio-ELM samples was determined using a Zetasizer Nano system (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Measurements were performed at 0 min (Run 1), 30 min (Run 2), and 60 min (Run 3) after extraction/emulsification to evaluate time-dependent aggregation and stability. Each sample was diluted (1:50) with deionized water to avoid multiple scattering, and 1 mL of the diluted sample was transferred to a DLS cuvette. The instrument parameters were adjusted using Malvern software to obtain hydrodynamic diameter data in the form of intensity-weighted size distributions, along with the polydispersity index (PDI).

Rheological analysis of bio-emulsifier and bio-ELM

The rheological properties of the bio-emulsifier and Bio-ELM samples were investigated using a rotational rheometer (Modular Compact Rheometer, MCR 302e, Anton Paar) equipped with RheoCompass® software. A parallel-plate geometry (diameter of 25 mm) was used to measure the rheological properties. During the test, the shear rate (s⁻1) was varied from 0.1 to 100 s⁻1 over a total duration of 180 s, and the corresponding shear stress was continuously recorded. A steady shear flow test was conducted over a temperature range of 20–40 °C to examine the temperature-dependent behavior of the bio-emulsifier and Bio-ELM. Before each measurement, the sample was meticulously positioned on the lower plate, and the gap between the plates was adjusted to ensure optimal contact with the sample surface, thereby minimizing the air entrapment.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

FTIR (Bruker ALPHA II FTIR spectrometer) analysis was performed using an ECO-ATR (Attenuated Total Reflectance) module (diamond crystal). The ATR crystal surface was cleaned with isopropanol and dried with a lint-free tissue before measurements. A background spectrum was recorded before the sample analysis to account for atmospheric water vapor. For analysis, 1 µL of each sample was deposited onto the diamond ATR crystal using a micropipette. No pressure clamp was required because the liquid sample formed a uniform contact with the crystal surface. Spectral data were collected in 4000–500 cm⁻1 at a resolution of 4 cm ⁻1, averaging 32 scans per sample to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

Microscopy analysis of bio-ELM

The synthesized Bio-ELM derived from AVL was analyzed using an optical microscope (Carson Microscope) with an adjustable magnification in the range of 50 × to 250x. For microscopic observation, a small aliquot (~ 10 µL) of the Bio-ELM was placed at the center of a clean glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and examined under a microscope. The average emulsion droplet size was determined using ImageJ (open-source software). Furthermore, the overall size distribution of the emulsion globules was assessed to evaluate the stability of Bio-ELM.

Extraction of methylene blue dye

The synthesized Bio-ELM was used to extract methylene blue (MB) dye. In the extraction process, an initial MB dye concentration of 20 mg/L was used, with a Bio-ELM-to-feed phase ratio of 1:5. The extraction was carried out at a stirring speed of 800 rpm for 25 min. The residual concentration of MB dye after extraction was determined using a UV–visible spectrophotometer at 663 nm wavelength. Results and discussions

Characterization of bio-emulsifiers and Bio-ELM

High-performance liquid chromatography analysis

The AVL bio-emulsifier extracted from Aloe vera was analyzed using HPLC at detection wavelengths of 235 and 254 nm, and the resulting chromatograms are presented in Fig. S1.a) for 235 nm and Fig. S2.b) for 254 nm, respectively. At 235 nm, a major peak was observed at a retention time (RT) of 2.183 min, along with several minor peaks in the range of 2.5–4.0 min (Fig. S2.a). Based on the literature, Aloe vera contains anthraquinones, chromones, phenolic acids, and flavonoids as its characteristic phytochemicals19,21. The early eluting major peak (RT 2.183 min) corresponds to Aloin A (barbaloin) and Aloin B (iso-barbaloin), the principal anthraquinone glycosides of Aloe vera22. Similar findings were reported by Brown et al.23, who developed an HPLC method for the detection and quantification of Aloin A and B in Aloe vera raw materials. The smaller peaks appearing between 2.8 and 4.0 min may correspond to phenolic acids (e.g., p-coumaric, ferulic acids) and chromone derivatives (such as aloesin or aloeresin A)24. The presence of these phytochemicals indicates that the extraction process efficiently isolated the bioactive constituents of the AVL bio-emulsifier. When the sample was analyzed at 254 nm, a distinctly different chromatographic pattern was observed (Fig. S2.b). A major peak appeared at RT 7.642 min, accompanied by several minor peaks between 1.5 and 8.35 min. The dominant peak at 7.642 min may be attributed to chromone and anthraquinone derivatives, such as aloe-emodin, aloesin, or aloeresin A, which are known to exhibit strong UV absorbance near 254 nm25. The minor peaks eluting earlier in the chromatogram likely corresponded to lower-molecular-weight phenolic acids and flavonoids. The detection of these compounds at 254 nm highlights the enhanced sensitivity of this wavelength toward aromatic and conjugated systems, thereby complementing the information obtained from the 235 nm scan.

The structural features of these compounds suggest a combination of hydrophilic and hydrophobic functionalities, consistent with the observed emulsifying behavior of AVL in the Bio-ELM system. Terpenoid-like alcohols such as 3-trans-(1,1-dimethylethyl)-4-trans-methoxycyclohexanol provide a hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance and can adsorb at the oil–water interface, stabilizing emulsion droplets. Fatty acid methyl esters, such as methyl 9,11-octadecadiynoate, contribute hydrophobic tails that enhance steric stabilization in the oil phase. Hydrophilic compounds, particularly sugar-like polyols and polysaccharide fragments such as 4-benzyloxy-6-hydroxymethyl-tetrahydropyran-2,3,5-triol and 3-(benzyloxymethyl)hex-5-ene-1,2-diol, form hydrated layers around emulsion droplets, reducing coalescence and providing steric and electrostatic repulsion. Aromatic alcohols and esterified phenolic derivatives (1-(4-ethoxyphenyl)propan-1-ol, benzoic acid 4-ethoxyethyl ester) exhibit amphiphilic properties that enhance interfacial rigidity and contribute to overall emulsion stability28,29. Overall, the GC–MS profile indicated that the bioactive constituents of AVL possess a natural amphiphilic character, with synergistic contributions from terpenoid, fatty acid, and polysaccharide derivatives.

CMC analysis by surface tension measurement

The CMC represents the specific concentration at which surfactant molecules self-assemble into micelles. Figure 4 illustrates the variation in surface tension with respect to the concentration of the extracted bio-emulsifiers in the range of 0.5–5%v/v. The schematic (Fig. 4) also illustrates micelle formation in the aqueous phase, highlighting the orientation of the hydrophilic heads toward the water and the hydrophobic tails within the micellar core. At concentrations below the CMC, the bio-emulsifier molecules, containing both hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups, remain dispersed within the aqueous phase30. Their hydrophilic groups predominantly interact with water molecules, whereas the hydrophobic tails remain oriented away from the aqueous environment. As the concentration increases and approaches the CMC, aggregation occurs, leading to micelle formation, which significantly reduces the surface tension31,32. The CMC value for the bio-emulsifier was observed at 2% v/v, with a surface tension of 18 mN/m. This slight reduction in surface tension indicates that the bio-emulsifier exhibits stronger surface activity. Beyond the CMC, no further significant decrease in surface tension was observed, indicating saturation of the interface and stabilization of micelle formation33. This plateauing effect confirms that additional surfactant molecules preferentially form micelles in the bulk solution rather than further contributing to surface tension reduction. The relationship between CMC values and surface tension highlights the efficiency of bio-emulsifiers in micelle formation, which is critical for their application in emulsion stabilization. These results demonstrate that the extracted bio-emulsifiers exhibit effective surface activity and can reduce surface tension to as low as 18.5 mN/m at a CMC concentration of 2 v/v%.Fig. 4Critical micelle concentration of extracted bio-emulsifier, by Wilhelmy plate method of surface tension measurement, Concentration (0.5–5 v/v%) (5% ± error).

Zeta potential measurement

The zeta potential was measured at pH 6, which corresponds to the native pH of the AVL bio-emulsifier extract and represents near-neutral conditions relevant to wastewater applications, while avoiding possible structural degradation of bioactive components at extreme pH values. The distribution curves obtained from three independent runs (Run 1, Run 2, and Run 3) exhibited consistent peak positions, indicating the good reproducibility and reliability of the measurements. The average zeta potential of the system was − 35 mV, as shown in Fig. 5. A zeta potential value more negative than − 30 mV is widely accepted as an indicator of sufficient electrostatic repulsion among dispersed droplets or particles, thereby reducing the probability of aggregation and enhancing the long-term stability of the colloidal system. Similarly, Hou et al. found that Tremella fuciformis polysaccharide showed the highest zeta potential values, correlated with superior stability through increased electrostatic repulsion34. The negative surface charge of the bio-emulsifier plays a crucial role in stabilizing emulsion droplets. When two droplets approach each other, the overlap of their electrical double layers generates electrostatic repulsion, which counteracts the natural tendency of the droplets to coalesce35. This repulsive interaction prevents close contact, thereby reducing phase separation and enhancing emulsion stability. The combination of electrostatic stabilization from the negative charge and steric hindrance from interfacial adsorption collectively enhanced the robustness of the emulsion. Thus, the measured -35 mV zeta potential demonstrates that the bio-emulsifier is highly effective in imparting both surface charge and stability to the droplets, ensuring good colloidal stability of the emulsion under the conditions studied.Fig. 5Zeta potential analysis measurement (mV) for bio-emulsifier based on AVL (5% ± error).

Dynamic light scattering measurement

The particle size distribution of the bio-emulsifier was analyzed using DLS, and the results are presented in Fig. 6a. The intensity (%) of scattered light versus bio-emulsifier size (nm) showed unimodal distributions for all runs, indicating homogeneous particle populations without secondary peaks. The bio-emulsifier particles were predominantly observed in the 800–1400 nm range, with a maximum intensity between 1000 and 1200 nm. Run 1 exhibited the highest peak intensity (~ 80% at 1100–1200 nm), followed by Run 2 (~ 60% at ~ 1200 nm) and Run 3 (~ 50% at ~ 1100 nm), suggesting slight time-dependent aggregation or rearrangement of bio-emulsifier assemblies in aqueous medium. Nevertheless, the overall reproducibility across the runs demonstrated a consistent size distribution, confirming the stability of the extracted bio-emulsifier. The micron-scale size observed reflects aggregated biopolymeric structures prior to emulsification, which subsequently dissociate during bio-ELM formation. The particle size distribution of the synthesized Bio-ELM is illustrated in Fig. 6b. The size distribution curves from the three runs demonstrated consistent nanoscale droplet formation, with emulsion droplets primarily between 50 and 200 nm (0.05–0.2 µm). All runs exhibit unimodal distributions with distinct peaks: Run 1 (25% at ~ 110 nm), Run 2 (35% at ~ 120 nm), and Run 3 (30% at ~ 100 nm). Beyond 200 nm, the intensity decreased sharply, indicating the minimal presence of larger droplets. The broader tail up to ~ 1000 nm observed in Run 3 was attributed to minor homogenization variability. The substantial reduction in size from micron-scale bio-emulsifier aggregates to nanoscale Bio-ELM droplets confirms the effective adsorption of AVL components at the oil–water interface under shear, resulting in stabilized fine droplets and reduced coalescence. Rheological analysis of bio-emulsifier and bio-ELM

The rheological properties of the bio-emulsifier and Bio-ELM were studied using rheological analysis. The variation in viscosity with temperature for the AVL bio-emulsifier and the prepared emulsion is shown in Fig. 7a. The viscosity of the AVL bio-emulsifier remained nearly constant (5–7 m.Pa.s) across the studied temperature range (20–40 °C), indicating that temperature variations did not significantly affect its bulk flow behavior. In contrast, the viscosity of the emulsion decreased sharply with increasing temperature. At lower temperatures (20–25 °C), the emulsion viscosity was relatively high (50 m.Pa.s) but decreased sharply to 10 m.Pa.s as the temperature approached 30 °C, followed by a gradual reduction and stabilization between 35 and 40 °C. This reduction is attributed to the temperature-induced disruption of intermolecular interactions in the oil phase and weakened interfacial film rigidity. An increase in temperature enhances molecular motion, reduces intermolecular interactions, and consequently lowers the viscosity36,37. The comparative analysis showed that the bio-emulsifier stabilized the emulsion system, but above 30 °C, it affected the viscosity and structure of the emulsion. This was confirmed by microscopic analysis. The micrographs clearly reveal that at 20–30 °C, the emulsion consisted of fine, uniformly dispersed droplets, leading to a higher interfacial area and stronger droplet–droplet interactions, which contributed to a higher viscosity. However, at elevated temperatures (35–40 °C), significant droplet growth and coalescence were observed. Both systems exhibited shear-thinning behavior, wherein the viscosity decreased with increasing shear rate. Calculation of the dimensionless numbers

The Capillary number (Ca), bond number (Bo), and inverse Marangoni number (Ma–1) were calculated using Eqs. (2–4). The viscosity of the continuous phase (μ = 7.17 × 10⁻2 Pa.s) and shear rate (γ = 100 s⁻1) were obtained from rheological measurements under the optimized conditions. The equilibrium interfacial tension (σ_eq = 1.55 × 10⁻2 N.m⁻1) and interfacial tension gradient (Δσ = 1.69 × 10⁻2 N.m⁻1) were determined from surface tension experiments using the optimized bio-emulsifier concentration. The droplet radius (a) was obtained from optical microscopy image analysis and varied with the storage time. The density difference between the membrane and aqueous phases was experimentally measured as Δρ = 120 kg.m⁻3. Gravitational acceleration was considered a constant (g = 9.18 m.s⁻2). These constant values were used for all calculations, whereas the droplet radius was treated as a time-dependent variable governing changes in Ca, Bo, and Ma–1. The detailed numerical values are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3.

The variations in the dimensionless numbers, capillary number (Ca), bond number (Bo), and inverse Marangoni number (Ma–1) as a function of storage time are presented in Fig. 10 (Tables S1–S3 show the details of the calculated parameters for Ca, Bo, and Ma–1). The experimental variation showed good agreement with the theoretical model, confirming that the dimensionless analysis effectively described Bio-ELM stability. The results indicated increasing Ca and Ma–1 with time, while Bo remained low throughout the 30-day period. Initially (Day 1), the Ca and Ma–1 values were near zero, indicating that interfacial tension forces dominated over viscous and Marangoni effects, resulting in uniform, well-dispersed droplets. As the storage time increased, Ca and Ma–1 rose to approximately 0.27 and 0.25 by Day 30, respectively, indicating increasing viscous and Marangoni stresses. The increase in Ma–1 reflects enhanced interfacial tension gradients from surfactant redistribution, corresponding to intensified Marangoni convection, which suppresses droplet coalescence. The Bond number remained constant (0.01–0.02), suggesting a negligible gravitational influence compared to the interfacial tension forces, which is typical of emulsions with small droplets. Microscopic analysis supported these findings, with micrographs showing a gradual increase in droplet size over time. Initially, the droplets were uniform and well dispersed, maintaining homogeneity until Day 10. By Days 15–20, larger droplets emerged with minor phase separation, progressing to pronounced coalescence by Day 30. This destabilization can be attributed to coalescence, Ostwald ripening, and weakening of the interfacial film. The AVL-derived bio-emulsifier initially provided strong stabilization; however, prolonged storage led to structural rearrangements and decreased film strength. The combined analysis confirmed that Bio-ELM stability is governed by viscous and Marangoni forces, with interfacial tension remaining dominant throughout storage.

Parameter optimizations of bio-ELM

A parameter optimization study was conducted by considering five critical factors identified from the literature and previous experimental experience. These include the concentration of the bio-emulsifier, internal phase concentration, membrane-to-internal phase ratio, stirring speed, and pH. The influence of these parameters on the extraction efficiency and droplet size was evaluated to determine the optimal operating conditions for the Bio-ELM system.

Effect of bio-emulsifier concentration

To investigate the effect of AVL bio-emulsifier concentration, experiments were performed by varying the concentration from 1 to 3.5% v/v while keeping stirring speed (600 rpm), internal phase concentration (0.1 M NaOH), pH (7), and MP:IP ratio (1:1) constant. The concentration of bio-emulsifiers is critical for stabilizing emulsion droplets and enhancing extraction efficiency. As shown in Table S4, increasing the bio-emulsifier concentration from 1 to 2.5%v/v resulted in a sharp reduction in emulsion droplet size from 12.5 to 4.8 µm, indicating the enhanced surface coverage and interfacial film strength47. Beyond 2.5%v/v, the droplet size slightly increased, possibly due to excess emulsifier molecules causing interfacial crowding and reduced packing efficiency. Correspondingly, the extraction efficiency increased from 72 to 96% up to 2.5%v/v concentration (the color reduction of MB dye is shown in Fig.S6), after which it declined marginally. This suggests that an optimal emulsifier concentration (2.5%v/v) exists, beyond which emulsion destabilization and coalescence occur. Similar trends have been reported in earlier studies where excess surfactant resulted in micelle formation rather than droplet stabilization2. Therefore, a 2.5% v/v AVL bio-emulsifier concentration was found to be adequate and was used for further experiments.

Effect of stirring speed

After optimizing the AVL bio-emulsifier concentration to 2.5% v/v, the effect of stirring speed was evaluated by varying the speed from 200 to 1200 rpm while maintaining the internal phase concentration (0.1 M NaOH), pH (7), and MP: IP ratio (1:1) constant. The stirring speed significantly influenced the droplet breakup and stability. As illustrated in Table S4, the droplet size decreased with increasing stirring speed from 200 to 800 rpm, with a minimum of 3.2 µm at 800 rpm. This can be attributed to the higher shear forces, which promote droplet breakup and uniform dispersion of the membrane phase. However, at speeds beyond 800 rpm, the droplet size increased slightly, likely because of turbulence-induced coalescence. Al-Khateeb et al. who reported that increasing agitation enhances extraction efficiency due to improved droplet breakup and interfacial area, up to an optimum speed. Beyond this point, further increases in stirring resulted in emulsion instability and membrane breakage, leading to a decline in extraction performance48. Excessive stirring destabilizes the emulsion by causing membrane breakage and rupture of the liquid membrane. Therefore, moderate and controlled agitation is crucial for maintaining emulsion stability during extraction. An optimum stirring speed of 800 rpm was found to be the most suitable for forming stable emulsions, providing an effective balance between droplet dispersion and membrane integrity.

Effect of internal phase concentration

The AVL bio-emulsifier concentration was optimized at 2.5% v/v, and the stirring speed was 800 rpm. The internal phase (NaOH) concentration was varied from 0.05 to 0.3 M, while keeping the pH (7) and MP:IP ratio (1:1) constant. The IP concentration governs the driving force of solute extraction. As shown in Table S4, increasing the NaOH concentration from 0.05 M to 0.25 M improved the extraction efficiency from 75.12 to 97.63%. The higher availability of reactive sites in the internal phase enhanced the solute transport across the membrane. However, the droplet size also increased slightly with concentration, from 5 to 6.1 µm at 0.05 and 0.25 M, respectively, to achieve a more uniform droplet distribution and increase stability at 0.25 M. Beyond that, the emulsion droplet size increased and reduced the extraction efficiency. This can be explained by osmotic swelling caused by higher solute concentration differences across the membrane, which increases internal phase volume and leads to droplet expansion49. A similar finding was reported by Raval et. al is that an increase in IP concentration reduces extraction efficiency due to osmotic swelling and emulsion breakage2. Despite the swelling, the system remained sufficiently stable up to 0.25 M. Therefore, an IP concentration of 0.25 M provided the best balance between stability and efficiency.

Effect of pH

After optimizing the bio-emulsifier concentration (2.5% v/v), stirring speed (800 rpm), and internal phase concentration (0.25 M), the effect of pH was investigated by varying the pH from 2 to 12 while maintaining the MP:IP ratio at 1:1. The pH strongly affects the stability and extraction behavior of the bio-ELM system. As shown in Table S4, the emulsion droplet size decreased steadily from 4.5 µm at pH 2 to 2.9 µm at pH 8, indicating improved electrostatic stabilization of the droplets under neutral to slightly basic conditions. At higher pH values (> 10), the droplet size increased again, likely due to the weakening of the interfacial films at extreme alkalinity, leading to coalescence. The extraction efficiency followed a parabolic trend, increasing from 70.89% under acidic conditions to 97.91% at pH 8, before declining to 88.36% at pH 12. Similar pH-dependent behavior has been reported in earlier ELM studies, where extraction efficiency was maximized under neutral to mildly alkaline conditions, while acidic and highly alkaline environments led to increased droplet size and emulsion destabilization due to the weakening of the interfacial film50. This highlights that neutral to mildly alkaline conditions provide the best interfacial stability and mass transfer, whereas extreme pH conditions may compromise the structural integrity of the bio-emulsifier. A pH of 8 was optimal and selected for further experiments.

Effect of the membrane to internal phase ratio

Under the optimized conditions of AVL bio-emulsifier concentration (2.5% v/v), stirring speed (800 rpm), internal phase concentration (0.25 M), and pH (8), the MP: IP ratio was varied from 1:0.5 to 1:3. The MP-to-IP ratio determines the thickness of the liquid membrane surrounding the IP droplets, which directly influences emulsion stability and solute transport. As shown in Table S4, at low ratios (1:0.5 to 1:1.5), the emulsion droplet size decreased from 5.3 to 3.6 µm, suggesting that an adequate amount of MP ensured better encapsulation and stability of the droplets. The smallest droplet size was observed at a 1:1.5 ratio, indicating optimum encapsulation capacity. The extraction efficiency followed a similar trend, increasing from 82% at 1:0.5 to a maximum of 97.98% at 1:1.5. Beyond 1:1.5, the droplet size increased gradually (4.9 µm at 1:3), while the extraction efficiency declined to 85%. Similar behavior has been reported in earlier ELM studies, where increasing the internal phase volume at constant membrane phase initially enhanced extraction due to greater stripping capacity; however, further increase led to incomplete encapsulation, emulsion swelling, and membrane leakage, resulting in reduced stability and extraction efficiency51,52. These findings indicate that maintaining an optimum balance between the MP and IP volumes is critical for maximizing the extraction performance. Therefore, the 1:1.5 ratio was identified as the most suitable condition, providing stable droplets and the highest extraction efficiency.

In contrast, the present study introduces a novel system that combines soybean oil as a renewable diluent, Span 80 as a surfactant, and an Aloe vera-derived bio-emulsifier as a natural stabilizing agent (bio-emulsifier). Even the preliminary emulsion formulation screening confirmed that the AVL bio-emulsifier + Span 80 + soybean oil provided superior long-term stability compared to the AVL bio-emulsifier alone and the synthetic formulation; detailed results are provided in Fig. S7 and Table S5. Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed and characterized a novel Bio-ELM system using AVL extract as a natural bio-emulsifier. The Aloe vera-derived bio-emulsifier exhibited strong surface-active properties, with a CMC of 2% v/v and the ability to reduce surface tension to 18 mN/m. Bio-ELM exhibited favorable colloidal stability, with droplet sizes of 50-200 nm and a zeta potential of -35 mV. Rheological analysis confirmed the shear-thinning behavior of both the bio-emulsifier and Bio-ELM. FTIR spectroscopy verified the successful incorporation of the bio-emulsifier into the membrane phase. Optimized conditions (2.5% v/v bio-emulsifier, 800 rpm stirring, 0.25 M internal phase, pH 8, and 1:1.5 membrane: internal phase ratio) achieved approximately 98% extraction efficiency for MB dye removal. The Bio-ELM maintained its structural integrity for up to 15 d, after which noticeable changes in droplet size were observed. Microscopic analysis and dimensionless number evaluation provided valuable insights into the long-term stability mechanisms of Bio-ELM. This study demonstrates the potential of Aloe vera-derived bio-emulsifiers for developing sustainable Bio-ELM systems for environmental remediation. This approach provides a cost-effective and eco-friendly alternative for wastewater treatment. Future research should focus on scaling up, evaluating performance with diverse pollutants, and improving long-term stability. Overall, this study advances green separation technologies and highlights the viability of plant-based emulsifiers for industrial use.
