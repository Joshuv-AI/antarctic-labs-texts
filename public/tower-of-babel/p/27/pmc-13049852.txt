Exploring the median effective dose of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in patients with heart valve disease: a prospective, single-arm, single-centre study
Authors: Qi Mao, Beiwei Liang, Yizhi Lu, Danfeng Luo, Fang He, Lifang Zhou, Guofeng Liu, Yanhua Chen, Yubo Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo determine the median effective dose of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in patients undergoing cardiac valve replacement and to evaluate its safety.MethodsTwenty-seven patients with heart valve disease (HVD), aged 18–64 years with a BMI of 15–28 kg/m² and classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II or III, were enrolled. The effective dose of ciprofol was determined using Dixon’s up-and-down method, starting from an initial dose of 0.30 mg/kg with an increment of 0.02 mg/kg. A probit model was employed to calculate the ED50 and ED95. Hemodynamic parameters, including heart rate (HR), mean arterial pressure (MAP), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and the bispectral index (BIS), were recorded at baseline and every minute for 5 min following drug administration. The incidence of adverse events, such as hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, and injection pain, was also documented.ResultsThe Median Effective Dose (ED) 50 (with 95% confidence intervals, CIs) for ciprofol-induced loss of consciousness were 0.307 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.272–0.358 mg/kg) and the ED95 was 0.366 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.334–0.938 mg/kg), reflecting inherent limitations of the up-and-down method when estimating upper quantiles. Hypotension occurred in 15% of patients, and respiratory depression was observed in 26%. No patients experienced injection pain, hypoxemia, or clinically significant bradycardia.ConclusionFor anesthesia induction in patients with HVD, the ED50 and ED95 of a single intravenous dose of ciprofol were 0.307 mg/kg and 0.366 mg/kg, respectively. Ciprofol was associated with a low incidence of anesthesia-related adverse events, suggesting it is a suitable agent for anesthesia induction in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.Trial registrationhttp://www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2300071427, 15/5/2023).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03705-2.Keywords: Ciprofol, Heart valve disease, Effective dose, Dixon’s up-and-down method, Anesthesia induction

Introduction

Heart valve disease (HVD) is a prevalent cardiac condition in adults, for which valve replacement or valvuloplasty serves as the primary surgical intervention. Patients with HVD often present with poor cardiac functional reserve and a high risk of hemodynamic instability, including hypotension and arrhythmias [1, 2]. The induction of anesthesia represents a period of particularly high risk [3]. Consequently, the selection of anesthetic agents for patients with HVD requires meticulous consideration by anesthesiologists, especially during the induction phase.

To maintain hemodynamic stability, anesthetic agents with minimal circulatory depressant effects are preferred for patients with HVD. Propofol has been a standard anesthetic for cardiac surgery [4]. Despite its potent sedative properties, propofol has several drawbacks, including a narrow therapeutic window, injection site pain, hypotension, and respiratory depression [5]. These can be disadvantageous during anesthesia induction in cardiac patients.

Ciprofol is a novel intravenous general anesthetic that exhibits a stronger affinity for γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors than propofol, attributable to the introduction of a cyclopropyl group and the formation of a chiral structure [6]. It is characterized by a wide therapeutic window, rapid onset, rapid elimination, hemodynamic stability, and a low incidence of adverse reactions [7–9]. These properties may render it particularly suitable for outpatient procedures [10]. To date, clinical studies on ciprofol have primarily focused on its use in painless gastroenteroscopy and gynecological surgery.

Evidence suggests that ciprofol is safe and effective for patients undergoing cardiac surgery [4]. However, the recommended dose of ciprofol for the induction of anesthesia in cardiac surgery has not been established. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the ED50 and ED95 of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in patients undergoing cardiac valve surgery, evaluate its safety profile, and provide a reference for its rational clinical application.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This single-center, prospective study was conducted between May and July 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Medical Ethics Committee (2023-S087-01) and registered at http://www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2300071427). All participants provided written informed consent. Eligible patients were adults (age ≥ 18 years) scheduled for elective heart valve replacement surgery under general anesthesia, with a BMI of 15–28 kg/m², an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of II or III, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class II or III. The exclusion criteria were as follows: known allergy to the study drug or its components, history of mental illness, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, heart failure within one month prior to surgery, history of drug or alcohol abuse, and anticipated difficult airway. The withdrawal criteria included: failure to achieve complete anesthesia induction with ciprofol alone, occurrence of significant adverse events rendering continuation in the trial unsuitable, and voluntary withdrawal for any reason.

Study protocol

Informed by prior studies [11], the research strategy was established. All patients received standardized anesthetic management without premedication. Upon arrival in the operating room, routine monitoring was established using a Mindray monitor, including electrocardiogram (ECG), pulse oximetry (SpO₂), respiratory rate and bispectral index (BIS). Furthermore, a hose was placed inside the patient’s nostril to monitor end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) during spontaneous breathing. Data were recorded at 1-second intervals and stored in the Anesthesia Information Management System for subsequent analysis. Peripheral intravenous access was secured, and invasive arterial pressure (IAP) was monitored via radial artery catheterization under local anesthesia. Prior to induction, patients were pre-oxygenated with 100% oxygen via a facemask for 3 min at a flow rate of 5–8 L/min.

The modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) scale was used to assess the depth of sedation [12]. Based on previous literature and preliminary experiments, the initial dose of ciprofol was set at 0.30 mg/kg, with an increment of 0.02 mg/kg [13]. The dose for each subsequent participant was determined by the response of the previous participant using Dixon’s up-and-down sequential method. A positive response (successful induction) was defined as a loss of consciousness (LOC) with MOAA/S ≤ 1 and BIS < 60 within 1 min (± 10 s) of ciprofol administration. If LOC was not achieved, a supplemental dose equivalent to 20% of the initial dose was administered. If MOAA/S remained > 1 or BIS was ≥ 60 after the supplemental dose, induction was considered a failure (negative response), and rescue anesthesia with propofol or midazolam was provided. A positive response led to a 0.02 mg/kg dose decrease for the next patient, while a negative response led to a 0.02 mg/kg increase. The study was terminated after seven crossover points between positive and negative responses. Drug administration and patient assessment were performed by two independent anesthesiologists to maintain blinding. Following the determination of the total ciprofol dose for each participant, the drug was administered intravenously at a constant rate via an infusion pump over 3 min.

During induction, atropine (0.3–0.5 mg) was administered intravenously for bradycardia (heart rate < 50 bpm). Hypotension, defined as a > 20% decrease from baseline Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) or an absolute value < 65 mmHg, was treated with intravenous norepinephrine (4–8 µg), ephedrine (1.5–3 mg), or phenylephrine (50–100 µg). Assisted ventilation via facemask was administered to prevent hypoxemia in the event of respiratory depression. Respiratory depression was defined as the occurrence of any of the following criteria, warranting immediate intervention: respiratory rate < 8 breaths per minute; the presence of a typical “shark fin” pattern on capnography (characterized by a prolonged and sloping expiratory upstroke, loss of the alveolar plateau, and an asymmetric triangular waveform), irrespective of respiratory rate; or a sustained rise in EtCO₂ exceeding 55 mmHg.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the ED50 and ED95 of ciprofol for successful anesthesia induction in patients with HVD, presented with 95% CIs. Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic parameters (HR, MAP, SpO₂, BIS) recorded at the following time points: baseline (T0), and 1 (T1), 2 (T2), 3 (T3), 4 (T4), and 5 (T5) minutes after induction. Demographic data, use of vasoactive drugs, total ciprofol dose, and the incidence of adverse events (hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, injection pain) were also recorded.

Sample size

Dixon’s up-and-down method requires a minimum of six pairs of negative-positive responses to reliably estimate the ED50 [14]. This study was terminated after seven crossover points, including 27 patients, which is considered adequate for this design.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean (standard deviation, SD), median (interquartile range, IQR), or number (percentage), as appropriate. Hemodynamic data are expressed as mean ± SD. A repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze data across different time points. When sphericity was not assumed, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Post hoc tests were Bonferroni-corrected. The ED50, ED95, and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using probit analysis. The up-and-down sequence and dose-response fitting curves were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Dose–response to ciprofol for anesthesia induction

Among the 27 patients, 13 had positive responses, and 14 had negative responses. The sequence of responses from the up-and-down experiment is illustrated in Figure 2. Probit analysis determined that the ED50 of ciprofol for anesthesia induction was 0.307 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.272–0.358 mg/kg), and the ED95 was 0.366 mg/kg (95% CI: 0.334–0.938 mg/kg). The wide confidence interval (0.334–0.938 mg/kg) reflects the inherent limitations of the Dixon up-and-down method when estimating high quantiles such as ED95, as noted in previous pharmacokinetic studies. The incidence of adverse reactions is summarized in Table 1. Hypotension occurred in four patients (15%) and was rapidly corrected with vasopressors. Transient respiratory depression was observed in seven patients (26%) within 5 minutes of induction, which was managed effectively with assisted ventilation; consequently, SpO₂ remained near 100% throughout the observation period. No other anesthesia-related adverse events, such as bradycardia, injection pain, muscle stiffness, or allergic reactions, were observed.

Discussion

Patients undergoing valve replacement surgery often have reduced cardiac output and functional reserve [15]. Maintaining hemodynamic stability during anesthesia induction is therefore critically important in this population [16, 17]. Ciprofol is a newly developed intravenous anesthetic reported to offer hemodynamic stability and a favorable safety profile [8]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effective dose and safety of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in cardiac surgery.

Characterizing the dose-response relationship is fundamental for any anesthetic agent [18]. Dixon’s up-and-down design is a classical and efficient method for estimating the median effective dose (ED50). This method typically requires a smaller sample size (a minimum of six pairs of negative-positive responses is recommended) than traditional designs to yield reliable estimates of the ED50 [14]. Our study was terminated after seven crossover points. The ED50 is a commonly used indicator of a drug’s potency and often serves as the initial dose in clinical trials. In contrast, the ED95 is extrapolated from the dose-response curve and relies on limited direct data in the extreme effect region [19]. Consequently, the 95% CI for the ED50 is typically narrower, allowing for more reliable comparisons of drug potency. Therefore, the ED50 may be a more clinically relevant metric for pharmacodynamic evaluation than the ED95 [20]. In our study, the 95% CI for the ED50 of ciprofol (0.272–0.358 mg/kg) was indeed narrower than that for the ED95 (0.334–0.938 mg/kg).

Few studies have investigated ciprofol dosing for cardiac anesthesia. A published case report described the successful induction for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) using ciprofol [4]. The dosage employed was 0.4 mg/kg in a patient with a BMI of 27.8, whereas our cohort had a lower mean BMI (20.11 ± 0.56 kg/m²). Another study suggested that 0.2–0.3 mg/kg ciprofol provides more stable blood pressure than etomidate [21]. A clinical trial on ciprofol pharmacokinetics proposed 0.3 mg/kg as a suitable induction dose for elderly patients [13]. Our results indicate an ED50 of approximately 0.3 mg/kg, lower than the doses in studies of healthier populations [22–25]. This suggests dose requirements depend on patient health status. Patients with valvular heart disease may exhibit greater sensitivity due to compromised cardiac function.

Hypotension is a common and potentially dangerous complication during anesthesia induction [26]. Our 15% incidence of hypotension was higher than in similar ciprofol studies [10]. This is likely due to our exclusive enrollment of ASA III patients and a stricter definition (a > 20% vs. commonly used 30% decrease from baseline). Comparisons with studies involving healthier patients (ASA I-II) further highlight how patient factors and outcome definitions can influence side effect profiles [27]. Interestingly, another one study with relatively healthy patients reported a higher hypotension incidence (38.7%) than ours [27], potentially due to their higher induction dose. Our protocol of administering ciprofol as a slow bolus over 3 min may have contributed to greater hemodynamic stability. The observed increase in heart rate following ciprofol administration, consistent with another phase III trial [28], may be a reflex response to decreased blood pressure. This suggests that ciprofol, like other anesthetics, may cause dose-dependent hemodynamic effects, warranting further investigation. Yu et al. [29]found that both ciprofol and propofol caused some degree of adverse events such as hypotension during anesthesia induction in cardiac surgery, but no significant differences in hemodynamics or oxygenation index were observed between them. The safety of ciprofol for anesthesia in cardiac surgery has been preliminarily confirmed by current published evidence. Despite the low incidence of adverse events, the observed decreases in MAP and increases in HR underscore the need for vigilant hemodynamic monitoring.

The incidence of respiratory depression in our study (26%) was lower than that reported by Yuan et al. [11]. This difference likely reflects their focus on more susceptible elderly patients. Definitions of respiratory depression vary across studies, with many using apnea or hypoxemia as criteria [10, 30]. For instance, one study defined respiratory depression as SpO₂ <95% and found no events with ciprofol doses ≤ 0.3 mg/kg and a 12.9% incidence at 0.4 mg/kg [31]. A phase III trial using apnea or hypoxemia (pulse oximetry below 90% lasting more than 30 s) criteria concluded that, ciprofol did not cause significant respiratory depression [32]. Conversely, other studies suggest ciprofol has milder cardiorespiratory effects than propofol during endoscopy [10, 33]. The onset of respiratory depression precedes desaturation. The inherent delay in pulse oximetry alarms makes it a good indicator but a poor predictor of respiratory depression [34]. Given the cardiac patients’ vulnerability and potential SpO₂ monitoring lag, we defined respiratory depression by reduced rate or tidal volume and intervened early. This approach prevented any hypoxemic events.

None of the 27 patients experienced significant injection pain, aligning with findings from a ciprofol phase III trial [9]. This benefit is attributed to ciprofol’s higher hydrophobicity and lower free plasma concentration compared to propofol [35]. Reduced injection pain is particularly advantageous for cardiac patients, as it minimizes emotional stress and associated hemodynamic fluctuations.

This study is the first to define the effective dose and confirm the safety of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in heart valve surgery patients. Firstly, we established an evidence-based ciprofol induction framework for HVD patients. The recommended initial dose of ~0.30 mg/kg aligns with the calculated ED50 (0.307 mg/kg). The ED95 of 0.366 mg/kg provides a valuable upper reference for cases requiring deeper sedation. Secondly, ciprofol demonstrated a favorable hemodynamic profile, with manageable hypotension and no bradycardia. It may therefore be suitable for cardiac patients requiring hemodynamic stability. This characteristic may potentially reduce the frequency and dosage of vasopressor interventions during induction compared to propofol. Furthermore, the complete absence of injection pain enhances patient comfort and avoids the sympathetic activation associated with propofol injection, which is another significant clinical advantage. Based on these results, we propose that ciprofol is a viable and potentially preferable alternative for anesthesia induction in patients undergoing cardiac valve surgery. Compared to commonly used propofol induction doses (typically 1.5–2.5 mg/kg in cardiac patients), the ED50 and ED95 values of ciprofol observed in this study suggest a potency ratio that may guide clinical titration, particularly in patients with valvular heart disease who are more susceptible to hemodynamic instability. Future large-scale, randomized controlled trials directly comparing ciprofol and propofol are warranted both to confirm these benefits and to define its role in cardiac anesthesia.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as a single-arm dose-finding study, it did not investigate the potential influences of age or cardiac function classification on the effective dose of ciprofol through a stratified randomized design. Second, the sample size of this study is small. While it was adequate for the modified Dixon’s up-and-down method, it was inadequate for precise estimation of higher effective doses like the ED95, as evidenced by the wide confidence interval. Larger studies (n > 100), potentially employing alternative designs such as the k-in-a-row up-and-down design or biased-coin design, are needed for accurate ED95 determination [19]. Third, this study evaluated ciprofol as a sole induction agent; the appropriate dose and hemodynamic effects when co-administered with opioids or other adjuncts remain to be investigated. Fourth, a direct comparative analysis with propofol was not performed, which has important clinical significance. Actually, this is our next research direction.

Conclusion

In summary, the ED50 and ED95 of a single intravenous dose of ciprofol for anesthesia induction in patients with heart valve disease were 0.307 mg/kg and 0.366 mg/kg, respectively. Ciprofol was associated with a low incidence of anesthesia-related adverse events, supporting its potential as a suitable anesthetic for induction in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.
