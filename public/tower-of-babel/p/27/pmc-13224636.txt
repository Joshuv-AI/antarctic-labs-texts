Re-administration of thrombolytics in massive pulmonary embolism with initial therapy failure: a case report
Authors: Seyed amir Hoseini, Maede Aghahoseini, Mohammad javad Aminizadeh, Fatemeh Sadat Hosseini Khajouei, Negar Azami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMassive pulmonary embolism (PE) is a life-threatening emergency with high mortality. Systemic thrombolysis is the standard reperfusion therapy for patients with hemodynamic instability. However, when initial thrombolysis fails, therapeutic options are limited and evidence supporting subsequent interventions is scarce.Case presentationWe describe a 45-year-old man with hyperlipidemia and depression who presented with dyspnea, hypoxemia, and cardiogenic shock. Echocardiography revealed right ventricular dilatation and dysfunction, consistent with massive PE. Initial therapy with full-dose alteplase (100 mg) and anticoagulation achieved only transient hemodynamic stabilization. The patient remained severely hypoxemic with persistent right ventricle (RV) dysfunction. As catheter-directed therapy and surgical embolectomy were unavailable, repeat systemic thrombolysis was pursued. A second full dose of alteplase (100 mg) was administered on hospital day four. Within 24 h, oxygen saturation improved from 65% to 92% on room air, and echocardiography confirmed substantial recovery of RV size and function. No major bleeding or other complications occurred during hospitalization.ConclusionCurrent guidelines do not recommend repeat thrombolysis for massive PE unresponsive to initial fibrinolysis, and available evidence is confined to case reports. This case demonstrates that, in carefully selected patients without contraindications, repeat systemic thrombolysis may represent a feasible and potentially lifesaving rescue option, particularly in resource-limited settings where other reperfusion strategies are not available. Further studies are needed to define safety, patient selection, and long-term outcomes.Keywords: Fibrinolytic therapy, Intravenous thrombolytic therapy, Massive pulmonary embolism, Re-administration, Venous thromboembolism (VTE)

Background

Acute venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and acute pulmonary embolism VTE is the leading cause of lost disability-adjusted life years and the third leading cause of cardiovascular death in the world [1]. pulmonary embolism (PE) occurs when emboli originating from venous thrombi migrate through the bloodstream and obstruct the pulmonary arteries [2]. The most clinical presentation may include classic symptoms such as dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain; however, in some cases, the manifestation is atypical, ranging from gradually progressive dyspnea over days to weeks to syncope with minimal respiratory signs. Consequently, a high index of clinical suspicion is essential in patients presenting with potential cardiopulmonary symptoms, as delayed or missed diagnosis of PE can result in severe consequences [3].

Massive PE that increased short-term mortality and adverse prognosisis defined by sustained hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg for ≥ 15 min or requiring inotropic support), pulselessness or persistent severe bradycardia (heart rate < 40 bpm with signs or symptoms of shock) [4]. Over time, more accurate diagnostic tools and risk stratification methods have been developed. Concurrently, a broader array of treatment modalities has emerged, especially for massive PE [5]. Anticoagulation remains the cornerstone of treatment in all PE patients [6]; however, thrombolytic therapy is typically indicated for those with massive or high-risk PE. Evidence suggests that thrombolytics may facilitate faster clot resolution compared to heparin and may reduce PE-related mortality [7]. Nonetheless, in cases where patients fail to show a clinical response to initial thrombolytic therapy, available treatment options become limited and are often guided by clinical judgment. Data on the efficacy and safety of repeat thrombolysis in such scenarios are limited. Here, we present a rare case of massive pulmonary embolism unresponsive to initial thrombolysis, in which significant clinical improvement was achieved following a second dose of thrombolytic therapy.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old man with a medical history of hyperlipidemia and depression presented to the emergency department with altered mental status, dyspnea, nausea, and left lower extremity pain and edema. His symptoms had begun three days prior to admission. He developed sudden-onset, progressive cyanosis the day before presentation. The patient also reported non-radiating chest pain. He had a 20-year history of cigarette smoking (equivalent to 3 pack-years) and was receiving methadone therapy. His current medications included atorvastatin (20 mg), tiotropium, salbutamol, olanzapine (5 mg), sodium valproate (500 mg), gabapentin (300 mg), and bupropion. Family history was notable for pulmonary embolism in his mother, which had resulted in her death.

On physical examination, vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 70/palpable mmHg, heart rate 131 bpm, respiratory rate 26 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 65% on room air, improving to 84% with a reservoir bag mask. Cardiac auscultation revealed no significant abnormalities. Pulmonary examination demonstrated diminished breath sounds bilaterally at the lung bases, along with expiratory wheezes. Abdominal examination was unremarkable.

Based on the patient’s clinical presentation, a Geneva score of 12, was calculated. In addition, the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) score was retrospectively calculated as 195 (Class V), indicating a very high-risk pulmonary embolism and supporting the decision for aggressive reperfusion therapy. Emergent bedside transthoracic echocardiography revealed right ventricular dilatation and dysfunction, along with the presence of McConnell’s sign, suggesting cardiogenic shock secondary to pulmonary embolism. Given the absence of absolute contraindications to fibrinolytic therapy, the patient received full-dose alteplase (100 mg over two hours), followed by continuous anticoagulation with intravenous heparin. Three hours after thrombolytic therapy, the patient’s hemodynamic status improved. Blood pressure stabilized at 100/60 mmHg, heart rate decreased to 95 bpm, and oxygen saturation improved to 95% with a reservoir bag oxygen mask.

Anticoagulation therapy was continued accordingly; However, despite initial hemodynamic improvement, the patient continued to experience severe oxygen desaturation, with oxygen saturation dropping to 65% on room air. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography showed minimal improvement in right ventricular size and function. As catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) and surgical embolectomy were not available at our center, and transferring the patient to a specialized facility was not possible. Prior to the administration of the second thrombolytic dose, the heparin infusion was discontinued, and coagulation parameters were reassessed. Four hours later, the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) decreased to 39 s (below 50 s). Therefore, a repeat dose of alteplase (100 mg over two hours) was administered on the fourth day of hospitalization. Anticoagulation with heparin infusion was continued and transitioned to a Non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant (NOAC) 24 h after the second thrombolytic dose. The patient remained hemodynamically and clinically stable for three days and was subsequently discharged home in good clinical condition.

Discussion

Massive PE is a life-threatening cardiovascular event with high initial mortality, frequently requiring emergent reperfusion treatment. While systemic thrombolysis has been the most established reperfusion approach for hemodynamically unstable PE [8, 9], Current guidelines focus primarily on first-line thrombolysis but offer limited recommendations for cases of therapeutic failure, creating a gap that challenges evidence-based decision-making in such scenarios.

For such cases, CDT or surgical embolectomy is generally favored. These interventions; however, require expertise and institutional resources not available in most centers worldwide [10, 11]. Thus, a therapeutic gray area exists, where repeat systemic thrombolysis is a consideration although excluded from the main guideline algorithms. The following case demonstrates this challenge: a patient with a massive pulmonary embolism showed partial hemodynamic improvement but ongoing hypoxemia with echocardiographic right ventricular strain after infusion of full-dose alteplase. In the absence of access to catheter-directed thrombolysis or surgery and in the absence of bleeding complications, we elected to administer a second dose of alteplase, which resulted in marked clinical and echocardiographic improvement.

Although the available data are somewhat limited, several case reports and small studies have suggested that repeated thrombolysis may offer therapeutic benefits in certain circumstances where other modes of therapy are unavailable. While the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2019 guidelines do not actively recommend repeat thrombolysis, they also acknowledge that there is a lack of high-quality data in this area [8]. This speaks to growing discrepancy between real-world clinical need and algorithmic frameworks presently available. Of interest, Oliveira-Mendes et al. (2025) described rescue thrombolysis following failure of initial CDT in an intermediate-high-risk PE patient, in which low-dose alteplase was used in consideration of hemorrhagic risk and lack of other options [12]. Despite a different PE risk classification, their clinical reasoning parallels our decision-making in a resource-constrained setting.

Likewise, Bigdelu et al. illustrated clinical restoration following repetitive thrombolysis in a high-risk PE patient with low bleeding risk [13].

Chicangana et al. illustrated good outcomes even in the setting of prolonged Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), in which thrombolysis was initially deemed relatively contraindicated [14]. These cases illustrate the potential of individualized, context-sensitive therapeutic strategies in circumstances where rigid adherence to existing protocols may not be feasible.

The risk of rebleeding after repeat thrombolysis is a legitimate concern, particularly in those with underlying risk factors [15]; however, case-based experience such as that of Poor. Armeen et al. demonstrates that, with careful patient selection and monitoring, this approach can be both feasible and safe [16].

Multidisciplinary consultation through pulmonary embolism response teams (PERTs) has been shown to enhance decision making and outcomes in complex PE cases [17]. Although not available in our institution, this model underscores the importance of formal team-based evaluation, especially when diverging from established guidelines. This case identifies the absence of clear and specific guidelines on the management of failed thrombolysis for massive pulmonary embolism, especially in situations where further interventional techniques are not feasible. Despite the ongoing accumulation of case-series and empirical evidence [12, 13, 18, 19], the lack of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) is a significant gap in existing literature [20]. This absence generates uncertainty in clinical practice and identifies areas for future research studies and consensus statements. Pending such evidence, clinicians will need to rely on personalized risk-benefit analysis and interdisciplinary opinion in determining repeat thrombolysis.

Conclusion

Our experience supports the viability of repeat systemic thrombolysis as a rescue treatment in highly selected massive PE patients, particularly when conventional treatment is unavailable and the bleeding risk is low. Enhanced mechanistic understanding of thrombolysis failure, coupled with evolving clinical data, requires a re-examination of current guidelines and the development of risk-stratified, patient-specific treatment paradigms.
