Investigating the Gut–Brain Axis in Adolescents With Mood Disorders and Functional Gastrointestinal Symptoms
Authors: Chan-Kyu Jeong, Dae-Jin Kim, Sung-Hoon Yoon, Su-Woo Lee, Dong-Ho Lee, Su-In Jung, Sang-Yeol Lee, Chan-Mo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective This study aimed to estimate the proportion meeting Rome III criteria for functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) and examine associations with suicide ideation (Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation, BSS), depression (Center for Epidemiological Studies–Depression Scale for Children, CES-DC), anxiety (Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders, SCARED), and perceived stress (Perceived Stress Scale, PSS) in child and adolescent psychiatric outpatients, including subgroup analyses by irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), functional constipation (FC), and subtype complexity (single vs. multiple). Methods We recruited 137 outpatients aged 12–18 years (2021–2023). FGIDs were classified with the Questionnaire on Pediatric Gastrointestinal Symptoms–Rome III (QPGS–Rome III). BSS, CES-DC, SCARED, and PSS assessed psychosocial burden. Group differences used standard comparisons (two-sided p<0.05). Results Overall, 69.3% met Rome III criteria for ≥1 FGID subtype. Compared with the non-FGID group, the FGID group had higher scores on BSS (p=0.048), CES-DC (p=0.035), SCARED (p<0.001), and PSS (p=0.003). In three-group comparisons (non/single/multiple), CES-DC, SCARED, and PSS differed significantly, with single and multiple FGID groups exceeding non-FGID. In subgroup analyses, IBS-related groups showed higher anxiety and stress than non-FGID, whereas the single FC subtype did not differ significantly. Conclusion Among child and adolescent psychiatric outpatients, the proportion meeting the Rome III criteria for FGIDs was high and was significantly associated with psychological burden. These findings support integrating gastrointestinal symptom screening with mental health assessments at intake, with risk-stratified management for IBS-related presentations.Keywords: Adolescent, Functional gastrointestinal disorders, Irritable bowel syndrome, Anxiety, Depression, Suicidal ideation.

INTRODUCTION

Adolescent mood disorders increase in incidence after puberty and are associated with wide-ranging impairments in academic performance, interpersonal functioning, and longterm socioeconomic outcomes [1,2]. Prognosis is poorer when recurrent somatic symptoms, including pain, are present, with a significantly higher risk of transition to severe mental disorders in adulthood [3]. Among these, functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) interact with emotional, cognitive, and behavioral factors in the context of gut–brain dysregulation; these factors have been repeatedly reported to be associated with greater symptom burden and functional impairment [4,5].

FGIDs are defined by recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms in the absence of structural disease and are currently conceptualized within the spectrum of disorders of gut–brain interaction [4,5]. Pathophysiologically, bidirectional disturbances of the microbiota–gut–brain axis (MGBA) are considered central6: dysregulation of the stress-related hypothalamic-pituitaryadrenal (HPA) axis and autonomic control, together with alterations in gut microbiota, mediate visceral hypersensitivity and central sensitization, ultimately modifying pain perception and emotional regulation [6,7]. Accordingly, FGIDs are understood as functional imbalances of brain–gut circuitry rather than localized gastrointestinal disorders [4,7].

In children and adolescents, the epidemiologic burden of FGIDs is substantial. A meta-analysis of school- and community-based samples estimated a global pooled prevalence of 23%, with functional constipation (FC) at 12%, functional dyspepsia (FD) at 5%, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) at 3% [8]. FGIDs are also reported more frequently when mood symptoms are present; in a cross-sectional clinical sample from behavioral health and primary care settings, the prevalence of pain-predominant FGIDs was 51.5% in the mood/anxietypositive group versus 8.8% in the negative controls [9]. Similar findings have been reported in clinically diagnosed mood-disorder cohorts: in a tertiary-care adolescent sample (n=162), the frequency of IBS was 14.81%, and co-occurrence increased with higher depression severity [10].

Collectively, FGIDs are common at the community level [8] and are observed more frequently in groups with mood symptoms or diagnosed mood disorders [9,10]. However, there remains a lack of studies that directly examine these associations in child and adolescent psychiatric outpatients. We therefore sought to characterize FGID-related symptom patterns in adolescents with mood disorders presenting to a psychiatry clinic. In addition, we analyzed the associations between FGID co-occurrence and the severity of key psychological symptoms—suicidal ideation, depression, anxiety, and perceived stress.

METHOD

Participants

This study included 137 children and adolescents aged 12– 18 years who were diagnosed with a mood disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition. Participants were recruited from the Department of Psychiatry at Wonkwang University Hospital, and data were collected between March 2021 and September 2023. Questionnaires were administered in an outpatient setting under the guidance of a researcher (psychiatrist or clinical psychologist). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wonkwang University Hospital (approval no.: WKUH2020-07-026), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

Measures

Functional gastrointestinal symptoms

Functional gastrointestinal symptoms were classified using the validated Korean version of the Questionnaire on Pediatric Gastrointestinal Symptoms–Rome III (QPGS–Rome III) [11]. The QPGS–Rome III is a self-report instrument that assesses the frequency, severity, and duration of symptoms on a 5-point scale, with a standard recall period of the past 2 months [12]. This instrument has shown clinical utility in Korean children and adolescents, supporting its linguistic and cultural validity [11]. At the time the study was planned, a Korean translation and validation of the pediatric Rome IV questionnaire (R4PDQ, the latest Rome criteria for disorders of gut–brain interaction [4]) were not available in the published literature; therefore, to ensure measurement validity and to facilitate comparisons with prior studies, we used the QPGS–Rome III. Based on the Rome III criteria, the instrument identifies 10 subtypes: FD, IBS, functional abdominal pain (FAP), functional abdominal pain syndrome (FAPS), abdominal migraine (AM), FC, nonretentive fecal incontinence (NFI), aerophagia (A), cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS), and adolescent rumination syndrome (ARS) [12,13]. Linguistic, content, and construct validity and test– retest reliability have been demonstrated in Korean and international samples, supporting its use for research and epidemiological purposes [11,13,14].

Participants were classified as meeting the Rome III criteria for each subtype based on the QPGS–Rome III algorithm. This classification is a symptom-based definition for research purposes based on the questionnaire; no additional gastroenterological examinations, such as endoscopy, were included in the study protocol. The Rome pediatric guidelines endorse a symptom-based approach in the absence of alarm symptoms during an appropriate clinical evaluation, stating that assessment is possible without further testing [5].

In this study, participants were categorized based on whether they met the QPGS–Rome III criteria as follows: the “non-FGID group” included those who did not meet the criteria for any subtype, while the “FGID group” included those who met the criteria for at least one subtype. The FGID group was further divided into the “single FGID group” (meeting criteria for exactly one subtype) and the “multiple FGID group” (meeting criteria for two or more subtypes). For subtype-specific subgroup analyses focusing on IBS and FC, the following definitions were used: “FGID with IBS/FC group” (presence of the respective subtype, either alone or with other subtypes), “FGID without IBS/FC group” (complete absence of the respective subtype), “single FGID (only IBS/FC) group” (presence of only the respective subtype), and “single FGID (non-IBS/FC) group” (presence of a single subtype other than the respective one).

Suicidal ideation

Suicidal ideation was measured using the 21-item self-report Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSS), developed by Beck and Steer [15]. Each item is rated on a scale from 0 to 2, with some initial items serving a screening function. The scale covers core domains of suicidal ideation, including the desire for suicide, the specificity and intensity of plans, and behavioral preparations. Higher scores indicate greater severity of suicidal ideation. The reliability and validity of the Korean version of the BSS have been established for use in clinical research with children and adolescents [15,16].

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 20-item Center for Epidemiological Studies–Depression Scale for Children (CES-DC). This self-report scale evaluates depressive emotions over the past week. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“a lot”), with a total score range of 0–60. Higher scores reflect greater depressive severity. The developers have suggested a total score of ≥15 as a risk signal for screening purposes. This study’s choice and interpretation of the tool were informed by Korean research reporting the reliability and validity of a shortened 11-item version (CESDC-11) [17,18].

Anxiety

Anxiety was measured with the 41-item child self-report version of the Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED). The scale is composed of five subfactors: panic/somatic symptoms, generalized anxiety, separation anxiety, social anxiety, and school avoidance. Each item is rated from 0 (“not true or hardly ever true”) to 2 (“very true or often true”). A higher total score indicates a greater overall level of anxiety, and cutoff scores such as ≥25 have been proposed for screening [19].

Stress

Stress was evaluated using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). The PSS is a 10-item self-report scale that assesses the level of perceived stress during the past month. Items are rated on a scale from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“very often”). The total score ranges from 0 to 40, with positively worded items being reversescored. The scale is considered to have good reliability, with internal consistency coefficients of ≥0.70 reported across diverse populations and cultures [20,21].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean±standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To compare the prevalence of FGID by gender and age group, the chi-square test was used, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Group comparisons of psychological scale scores were performed using the independent t-test. For comparisons of means among three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance was conducted, and post-hoc analyses were performed using the Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp.). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics and prevalence of FGID criteria fulfillment

Among the 137 study participants, 40 (29.2%) were male and 97 (70.8%) were female. The sample was composed of 43 (31.4%) individuals aged 12–14 years and 94 (68.6%) individuals aged 15–18 years; the mean age of the patients was 15.5 years.

Based on the QPGS–Rome III assessment, 95 (69.3%) of 137 participants met the Rome III criteria for one or more FGID subtypes. The prevalence of criteria fulfillment by gender was 57.5% for males and 74.2% for females; this difference was not statistically significant (OR 0.47; 95% CI, 0.22–1.02; p=0.084). By age group, the prevalence was 62.8% for those aged 12–14 and 72.3% for those aged 15–18, a difference that was also not statistically significant (OR 0.65; 95% CI, 0.30– 1.39; p=0.355) (Table 1).

Distribution of FGID subtypes

Among the FGID subtypes, IBS was the most common, identified in 44 (46.3%) participants, followed by FC (n=35, 36.8%), A (n=19, 20.0%), AM (n=15, 15.8%), FD (n=14, 14.7%), FAP (n=12, 12.6%), FAPS (n=6, 6.3%), and ARS (n=1, 1.1%). No participants met the criteria for NFI or CVS. The most common subtype among males was FC (n=12), whereas for females it was IBS (n=38). IBS was the most frequent subtype in both the 12–14 and 15–18 age groups, although FC was also highly prevalent in the 15–18 age group (n=26). Of the participants who met the criteria for an FGID, 56 were classified into the “single FGID group” and 39 into the “multiple FGID group” (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the prevalence of FGID criteria fulfillment and its association with various psychosocial indicators in a sample of child and adolescent patients at a psychiatric clinic in Korea. Unlike most previous studies on FGIDs, which have predominantly focused on general adult populations or community-based pediatric samples, this study of-fers a distinct approach by targeting a clinical population of children and adolescents undergoing psychiatric diagnosis and treatment.

Of the 137 participants, 95 (69.3%) met the Rome III criteria for at least one FGID subtype, with no statistically significant differences observed based on gender or age. This proportion is higher than that typically reported in school- and community-based samples [8], suggesting that FGID-related symptoms are common among children and adolescents presenting to psychiatric services. The most frequently comorbid subtypes were IBS (n=44, 46.3%) and FC (n=35, 36.8%). In comparison, a prospective Norwegian study by Helgeland et al. [22] evaluating children aged 4–15 with abdominal pain reported that 124 (87%) of 142 participants met the Rome III criteria for at least one FGID. In their study, the most common diagnosis was IBS (43%), and diagnostic overlap was frequent, with IBS often co-occurring with A (n=16) and AM (n=14). Similarly, Baber et al. [23], in a study comparing Rome II and Rome III classifications in 368 children and adolescents with chronic abdominal pain, reported that 86.6% were classified with a pain-predominant FGID under Rome III, with IBS being the most common (45.1%). Their findings also showed an increase in AM (23.1%) and FAP (11.4%) compared to Rome II, with FD at approximately 15%. The IBS proportion in our study (46.3%) was similar to that reported by Helgeland (43%) and Baber (45.1%), but the prevalence of AM (15.8%) was lower than in those studies (around 23%). This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in the study populations. Whereas the studies by Helgeland and Baber focused on pediatric gastroenterology samples centered on abdominal pain, our study targeted psychiatric outpatients, a population with a high prevalence of comorbid anxiety and depression. Consequently, the proportion of pain-centered subtypes may have been relatively lower. Other factors, such as methods for handling diagnostic overlap, gender and age distribution, and cultural or healthcare-seeking behaviors, may also have influenced the results. Therefore, there is a need for multicenter, psychiatry-based research in Korea to systematically verify subtype distribution and psychosocial comorbidity patterns.

Regarding the association with psychological indicators, the “FGID group” demonstrated significantly higher scores on all measures—suicidal ideation, depression, anxiety, and stress—compared to the “non-FGID group.” In the threegroup comparison (non/single/multiple), scores for depression (CES-DC), anxiety (SCARED), and stress (PSS) showed statistically significant differences, with post-hoc analyses indicating that the “single FGID” and “multiple FGID” groups had higher scores than the “non-FGID group.” However, no significant difference was found between the “single FGID” and “multiple FGID” groups, making it difficult to conclude that an increasing number of subtypes exacerbates psychological symptoms. Nevertheless, the general trend of mean scores (non<single<multiple) suggests a potential increase in psychological burden with a greater number of FGID subtypes.

In our subgroup analysis, groups with IBS (the “FGID with IBS group” and the “single FGID [only IBS] group”) exhibited higher anxiety (SCARED) and stress (PSS) scores than the “non-FGID group.” However, the difference between the “FGID with IBS” and “FGID without IBS” groups was not significant, precluding a definitive conclusion that the presence of IBS independently intensifies psychological distress. Nonetheless, the similar trend observed in the “single FGID (only IBS) group” suggests a close link between IBS and anxiety/stress in some patients. In contrast, the “FGID with FC group” and the “single FGID (only FC) group” showed no significant differences from the “non-FGID group” on any scale, whereas the “FGID without FC” and “single FGID (non-FC)” groups had significantly higher anxiety and stress scores. This suggests that pain-predominant FGIDs (e.g., IBS, FAP, AM) may be more strongly associated with psychological burden, while isolated constipation (FC only) has a relatively weaker psychosocial connection. This pattern aligns with previous evidence of frequent anxiety and depression comorbidity in pediatric functional abdominal pain disorders/IBS [4,5] and with reports of a higher prevalence of pain-predominant FGIDs in patient groups with anxiety and depression [9]. It is also consistent with explanations involving dysregulation of the HPA axis and autonomic nervous system via the MGBA, as well as alterations in immune-inflammatory pathways, neurotransmitter systems (serotonin, GABA), and neurotrophic factors (brain‑derived neurotrophic factor) that may mediate this association [6,7]. Clinically, a stepwise approach may be advisable: prioritizing screening for anxiety and stress and considering psychological interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) for patients with IBS [24,25], while adding psychological evaluation as needed for patients with FC. Imbalances in the MGBA, coupled with HPA axis and autonomic nervous system dysregulation, altered gut microbiota composition, and immune-inflammatory activation, can trigger and sustain visceral hypersensitivity (amplification of ascending sensory input) and central sensi-tization (weakening of descending pain inhibition), resulting in altered pain perception, emotional regulation, and stress reactivity [6,7]. The consistently elevated SCARED and PSS scores in IBS-related subgroups in our study, together with the trend toward higher scores on some indicators in the multiple FGID group, can be interpreted as clinical manifestations consistent with this gut–brain interaction model [6,7].

The clinical implications of this study can be summarized as follows. First, given the high rate of comorbid FGID in child and adolescent psychiatric patients and the associated burden of anxiety, depression, and stress, it may be prudent to consider a protocol that integrates gastrointestinal symptom screening with psychological assessment at the initial evaluation stage, using a pediatric Rome-based questionnaire (e.g., QPGS–Rome III at present) and reviewing alarm features to determine pediatric GI referral, as resources and clinical judgment allow. Second, as elevated anxiety and stress were repeatedly observed in the “FGID with IBS” and “single FGID (only IBS)” groups, an integrated gut–brain axis management approach that includes the early, evidence based application of psychological interventions, such as CBT, together with stress/sleep regulation and bowel management when indicated, may be beneficial for appropriate candidates [24,25]. Third, considering the close relationship between FGID and psychiatric symptoms, a multidisciplinary evaluation and intervention involving mental health, gastroenterology, and psychology professionals may be warranted, with measurement based follow up of GI symptoms and mental health indices (e.g., perceived stress) to guide stepped care and safety planning.

The strengths of this study include estimating the proportion meeting the Rome III FGID criteria based on the QPGS– Rome III in a Korean child and adolescent psychiatric outpatient population and its simultaneous analysis of subtypes (especially IBS and FC), complexity (single/multiple), and psychosocial scales (BSS, CES-DC, SCARED, and PSS). This helps to fill a gap in the existing evidence, which has been skewed toward adult and internal medicine patient samples, and offers high clinical utility by specifically demonstrating the psychological burden associated with FGID subtypes in a real-world mental health setting.

This study has several limitations. First, we classified FGIDs using the pediatric Rome III instrument (QPGS–Rome III); definitional differences relative to pediatric Rome IV may shift prevalence and subtype distributions and limit direct comparability with Rome IV-based studies. Second, FGIDs were defined solely by questionnaire without systematic gastroenterological work-ups; therefore, the findings reflect a questionnaire-based case definition. Third, we did not assess adverse childhood experiences/trauma—important risk and confounding factors for both FGIDs and mood disorders [26]—and we did not fully adjust for key covariates (e.g., psychotropic medications, socioeconomic variables); therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, the crosssectional, single-center design and limited sample reduce causal inference, statistical power, and generalizability.

Conclusion

In a Korean child and adolescent psychiatric outpatient sample, a substantial proportion met the Rome III criteria for FGIDs and exhibited greater psychological burden. These findings support incorporating gastrointestinal symptom screening into routine psychiatric assessments and applying individualized, risk-stratified management based on subtype.
