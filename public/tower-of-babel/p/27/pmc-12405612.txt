Chromosomal-level genome assembly of Trichogramma japonicum (Hymenoptera: Trichogrammatidae)
Authors: Zhuo Jiang, Xiaoyan Zhu, Xiao Wang, Qi Chen, Wentao Zhang, Zhaoreng Li, Xilin Jiao, Zelong Yang, Wenmei Du, Junjie Zhang, Bingzhong Ren, Ying Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrichogramma japonicum is one of the successfully industrialized Trichogramma species in China and holds significant application value for controlling the rice pests Chilo suppressalis. However, progress in exploring its gene functions to enhance parasitic efficiency has been hindered by the lack of a known genomic landscape. In this study, we assembled a chromosome-level genome of T. japonicum by combining HiFi sequencing and Hi-C technology. The assembled genome is 216.96 Mb, with a scaffold N50 of 40.53 Mb, and 98.09% of contigs were anchored to five chromosomes. The final genome assembly showed a high level of completeness with a 97.44% score according to BUSCO analysis results. Genome annotation revealed that repetitive sequences constitute 26.45% of the assembly and identified 16,894 protein-coding genes. This high-quality genome assembly of T. japonicum will serve as a genomic resource for comparative genomic analysis of Trichogramma species and provide a molecular foundation and framework for understanding the interaction mechanisms between host and parasitoids.Subject terms: Zoology, Genome

Background & Summary

Trichogramma spp., minute egg parasitoid wasps, represent the most commonly used groups of natural enemies for controlling lepidopteran pests, such as Asian corn borer (Ostrinia furnacalis, Lepidoptera: Crambidae), rice stem borer (Chilo suppressalis, Lepidoptera: Crambidae) and soybean pod borer (Maruca vitrata, Lepidoptera: Pyralidae)1,2. Parasitism preferences among different Trichogramma species vary significantly, influenced by host size, age, and species identity3–5. Notably, T. japonicum, predominantly inhabiting rice ecosystems, has shown remarkable efficacy in controlling C. suppressalis in China6,7, making notable contributions to reducing reliance on chemical pesticides and promoting environmental protection.

Recent advances in genome sequencing technology have enabled the elucidation of the biological characteristics and parasitic mechanisms in several parasitoid wasps8. Since the first three Nasonia species’ genomes initiated a new era of genomic studies of parasitoid wasps9, an increasing number of parasitoid wasps have been whole-genome sequenced, due to their unique haplodiploid mating system. In recent years, genomes of several Trichogramma species have been successfully assembled10–14. However, substantial genetic divergence exists even among closely related parasitoid species15–17. This variation underlies their distinct biological characteristics and compromises the applicability of published Trichogramma genomes for extrapolating species-specific features to T. japonicum. The whole genome sequence of the T. japonicum is not only critical to fully exploit the various biological process in T. japonicum, but also enriches the genetic resources of Trichogramma species to perform the comparative genomic analysis.

In this study, we assembled a high-quality chromosome-level genome of T. japonicum by integrating Illumina short reads, PacBio HiFi reads, and Hi-C data. The best assembly yielded a genome of 216.96 Mb with the scaffold/contig N50 lengths of 40.53 Mb that anchored to five chromosomes. The genome contained 57.72 Mb repeat sequences, accounting for 26.45% of all data. In total, 16,894 protein-coding genes following a rigorous ab initio-, homology-, and evidence-based annotation, of which 94.51% were annotated. Our research establishes a valuable dataset for delving deeper exploration in T. japonicum.

Methods

Sample collection and preparation

T. japonicum were collected from the rice fields at Jilin Agricultural University in Changchun, China (125.40°E, 43.88°N) and were confirmed through morphological examination of male genital structures using stereomicroscopy and molecular confirmation through rDNA-ITS2 sequence alignment. T. japonicum was reared on the eggs of Corcyra cephalonica (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) under laboratory conditions at 25 ± 1 °C, 75 ± 5% relative humidity (RH) with 16:8 h (L:D) photoperiod. This isogenic line was maintained in isolation for over three years through strict single-line propagation to prevent genetic exchange with other populations.

PacBio, Illumina and Hi-C data generation

Due to the phenomenon of arrhenotoky in T. japonicum, we selected haploid male adults as sequencing samples to ensure the acquisition of genomic data with minimal interference from heterozygosity, thereby achieving higher continuity and accuracy. To address the challenges posed by T. japonicum’s diminutive body size and meet the substantial DNA quantity requirements for sequencing, genomic DNA was extracted from a pooled sample of > 10,000 haploid male adults (Fig. 1a, left) using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen) to ensure high-quality isolation. The genome of T. japonicum was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 and PacBio Sequel II System platforms. In accordance with the recommended protocol, large insert (20 Kb) SMRTbell libraries were prepared and subjected to sequencing on the PacBio Sequel II system, generating 10.60 Gb (51.65 × coverage) of high-quality long reads. Genomic DNA extracted from haploid male adults was employed for constructing Illumina sequencing libraries. For the genomic survey, paired-end libraries with an average insert size of 350 bp were fabricated and sequenced with 150 bp paired-end reads on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform, resulting in 15.65 Gb (76.26× coverage) of short reads. A Hi-C library was generated from cross-linked genomic DNA of haploid male adults and sequenced with 150 bp paired-end reads on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform, resulting in 25.13 Gb (122.45× coverage) of short reads (Table 1).

Genome survey and assembly

Illumina short-read data underwent quality processing through fastp v0.23.4 with parameter settings (-q 10 -u 50 -y -g -Y 10 -e 20 -l 100 -b 150 -B 150). These processed reads were subsequently employed for genome size estimation via 17-mer frequency analysis. A 17-mer frequency distribution was generated using Jellyfish v2.1.4 (-h 10000000000), followed by genomic feature calculation with Genomescope v2.0 (-k 17 -p 2 -m 100000). This analytical pipeline predicted a haploid genome size of 205.15 Mb for T. japonicum with 0.29% heterozygosity, which showed a relatively high genetic homogeneity (Fig. 1b).

High-fidelity PacBio HiFi circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads were employed for genome assembly using Hifiasm v0.19.0 with default parameters. High-fidelity reads underwent three consecutive cycles of base-level error correction and overlap recalibration to optimize sequence fidelity. Subsequent haplotype-aware phased assembly resolved allelic redundancies through Hifiasm’s integrated graph-based algorithms, systematically eliminating haploid duplications through phased string graph decomposition. The initial draft genome consisted of 51 contigs with a total size of 218.32 Mb and a contig N50 of 22.93 Mb (Table 2).

Hi-C scaffolding was implemented through a dual-phase approach combining error correction and chromatin interaction-guided assembly. Initially, contigs from the draft genome were fragmented into 50 Kb segments for Hi-C-based error detection. Candidate misassembly regions were identified through iterative Hi-C reassembly, with low-coverage positions (<10×) in non-restorable sequences flagged as error sites for subsequent correction. Corrected contigs were then subjected to chromatin interaction analysis using paired-end reads from Hi-C libraries processed through BWA v0.7.17 (default parameters). Invalid interactions (self-ligation/non-ligation pairs) were filtered prior to scaffolding. The LACHESIS v2.0.1 pipeline was deployed with optimized parameters (CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 376, CLUSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 2, ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_TRUNK = 15, ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_SHREDS = 15) to perform hierarchical clustering. The final chromosomal-level assembly was achieved through manual curation of interaction heatmaps, anchoring 216.96 Mb (99.4% of total genome) (Table 2) across five chromosomes (Fig. 1c, Fig. 2), ranging from 33.41 Mb to 58.10 Mb (Table 3). The assembled genome size is comparable to T. dendrolimi but larger than T. chilonis (Table 4). The decontaminated contig-level assembly was assessed using BUSCO v5.2.2 with insecta_odb10. The BUSCO assessment of the genome assembly completeness is demonstrated to be 97.44%, which is similar to T. chilonis but higher than T. dendrolimi (Table 4).

Repeat and gene annotation

Repeat annotation integrated de novo predictions from RepeatModeler2 v2.0.1 (utilizing RECON v1.0.8 and RepeatScout v1.0.6) and LTR-specific detection via LTR_retriever v2.9.0 (incorporating LTRharvest v1.5.10 and LTR_FINDER v1.07 outputs). These predictions were merged with Dfam v3.5 and RepBase databases, then dereplicated to construct a species-specific library. Final TE annotation was performed genome-wide using RepeatMasker v4.1.2 with the custom repeat database, leading to the discovery of 397,058 repeat sequences covering 57.72 Mp, representing 26.45% of the genome. The repetitive elements based on proportion included LTR retrotransposon (6.22%), long-interspersed elements (LINEs, 3.38%), short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs, 0.06%), DNA transposons (10.45%), Microsatellite (1–9 bp units) (1.95%), Minisatellite (10–99 bp units) (0.90%), and Satellite (> = 100 bp units) (3.49%) (Table 5, Fig. 2).

Protein-coding genes in the T. japonicum genome were identified through an integrated evidence-based approach combining homology prediction, ab initio algorithms, and transcriptomic evidence. Protein sequences from five reference hymenopteran species (Nasonia vitripennis, Apis mellifera, Copidosoma floridanum, Pteromalus puparum, and Trichogramma pretiosum) were aligned against the genome using TBLASTN v2.8.1 (E-value < 1e-5). Reciprocal alignments were refined through Genewise v2.4.1 to generate evolutionarily conserved gene models. Concurrently, ab initio prediction was performed on the repeat-masked genome using AUGUSTUS v3.3.2 parameterized with T. japonicum-optimized training sets (1,000 high-confidence genes) and SNAP v2006-07-28 employing lineage-specific hidden Markov models. Transcriptomic evidence was generated through alignment of the RNA-seq reads to the assembly via TopHat2 v2.1.1 for exon boundary identification, complemented by de novo transcript assembly using Trinity v2.8.4; both Cufflinks v2.2.1 and Trinity assemblies were integrated into a unified transcriptome database through PASA v2.2.0. These heterogeneous evidence streams were consolidated into a consensus gene set by EvidenceModeler v1.1.1 (Fig. 3), followed by comprehensive functional annotation through BLASTP v2.16.0 homology searches (E-value < 1e-5) against Swiss-Prot, NCBI-NR, and KEGG databases coupled with InterPro domain profiling, culminating in reconciled gene descriptions synthesized from multi-source annotation data (Table 6).

Data Records

PacBio, Illumina and Hi-C sequencing data have been deposited to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive with accession numbers SRR3384022818, SRR3377968919, and SRR3377952620, respectively. The assembled genome can be found on NCBI’s GenBank through accession number JBPBLQ01000000021. The genome annotations were openly available from figshare22.

Technical Validation

The accuracy and completeness of the genome assembly of T. japonicum were evaluated using multiple analyses. First, the Hi-C heatmap demonstrated consistent results across all chromosomes, confirming the correct contig ordering and orientation in the assembly (Fig. 1c). Second, clean short and long reads were re-mapped to the assembled genes using BWA v0.7.17 and Minimap2 v2.24, yielding mapping rates of 99.56% and 99.99%, respectively. Assembly completeness was rigorously evaluated through BUSCO v5.2.2 benchmarking against the insecta_odb10 database, revealing a 97.44% completeness metric for the T. japonicum chromosomal assembly (Table 4). Detailed ortholog analysis identified 93.93% single-copy, 3.51% duplicated, 0.51% fragmented, and 2.05% missing BUSCOs. Generally, these findings suggest the high quality of the T. japonicum assembly.
