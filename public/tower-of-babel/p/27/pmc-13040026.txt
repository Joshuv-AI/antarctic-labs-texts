Clarifying the origin of pseudocapacitance from a perspective of RuO2
Authors: Wenxin Yan, Taowen Dong, Wei Zhang, Weitao Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudocapacitance is dominantly considered to store charges through a unique faradic reaction (contributing to high energy density), while exhibiting electrochemical characteristics of supercapacitors (enabling high power density and excellent stability). It is still not fully understood why and how these two fundamentally distinct electrochemical processes, i.e., battery-like and capacitor-like, can be integrated into a single electrode. Conway et al. emphasized their faradic origin, whereas Costentin et al. emphasized their capacitive origin. In this Perspective, we pave a new paradigm for understanding their origin by correlating their physical structure with their electrochemical response. Specifically, we systematically review the charge storage process in hydrous RuO2, a benchmark material for pseudocapacitance, with particular emphasis on its microstructure, the role of water molecules in proton transport, and its metallic nature, which enables unique redox reactions. Finally, we propose a conjunctive mechanism, revealing that the pseudocapacitive response likely originates from the gain and loss of electrons, without semiconductor bandgap limitations, and from ion interfacial transfer and bulk diffusion, free of kinetic limitations.Subject terms: Batteries, Supercapacitors

Introduction

Batteries and supercapacitors are the most widely studied electrochemical energy storage devices, which are a trade-off between energy density and power density1. Surpassing fast-charging batteries for higher power density and hybrid supercapacitors for optimized energy density, pseudocapacitive electrodes can simultaneously achieve high energy and power densities, and excellent cyclic stability, particularly with a single electrode2. On the one hand, such comprehensive electrochemical performance offers a way to design the desired device for modern society in the era of post-Li-ion; on the other hand, these electrochemical behaviors challenge the theory of electrochemistry, which holds that redox peaks appear when a redox reaction occurs according to the Nernst equation. Therefore, it has become a prominent area of research for elucidating the charge storage mechanism of pseudocapacitance, sparking a long-term debate within the community.

Debates on the pseudocapacitance

The concept of pseudocapacitance can be traced back to the pseudo-capacity, which, in a literal similarity, was proposed by Grahame to describe the extra capacity generated by faradaic processes on the electrode surface, distinct from the capacity of the traditional electric double layer (EDL)3. Conway et al. further linked this pseudo-capacity with the fractional coverage of an electrode surface through the Langmuir and Frumkin isotherms, and finally proposed the concept of pseudocapacitance. They indicate that the response current, including the peak current, is proportional to the scan rate (i = n2F24RTνAΓ, where i is the response current, n is the number of electrons transferred, F is Faraday constant, R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, ν is the scan rate, A is the electrode surface area, and Γ is the amounts of matter adsorbed), which is similar to the relation in the electric double layer capacitance (i = νCd, and Cd is capacitance). They also emphasized the special relation between the charge obtained and the potential change (ΔQ/ΔV = finite number), while for a typical battery, ΔQ/ΔV = ∞. Furthermore, they originally elucidated the pseudocapacitances as underpotential deposition (UPD), redox pseudocapacitance, and intercalation pseudocapacitance for the processes with fractional coverage, redox, and intercalation, respectively4. One should note that the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curve of the pseudocapacitance proposed by Conway includes prominent redox peaks, distinct from the rectangular-like CV curves of the carbonaceous electrodes. Their understanding also starts the confusion about pseudocapacitance. Therein, Costentin et al. have long held that pseudocapacitance is equivalent to true electric double-layer capacitance5, and Conway’s claim is indeed true for a battery. For the phosphate cobalt oxide electrode, it was demonstrated that the transition from insulator to conductor with increasing anodic potential provides sufficient electrons to form an electric double layer at the interface6. Costentin et al. view the uniqueness of pseudocapacitance as lying in the non-carbonaceous electrode; however, they exhibit rectangular CV curves from carbonaceous electrodes. In other words, the transition metal oxide can also store substantial energy in an electric double layer, in agreement with recent literature by Xiao and Maier7. In our opinion, Conway’s point is that pseudocapacitance is a particular case in batteries, where the reaction occurs without electrochemical and concentration polarization, as well as over an expanded potential window. For clarification, this does not imply it is not subject to the constraints of electrochemical thermodynamics and kinetics. As shown in Fig. 1a, the case exhibits rationality: if considered in a differential potential, it will also display a rectangular-like CV curve. However, as clarified by capacitance and differential capacitance, the pseudocapacitance claimed by Conway indicated a variable capacitance at different potentials, whereas typical carbonaceous electrodes exhibit a constant capacitance. In addition, in the case of electrochemical or concentration polarization, as shown in Fig. 1b, the rectangular-like CV curve no longer exists, and it is unreasonable and unnecessary to discuss the so-called pseudocapacitive response. Costentin’s point emphasizes that pseudocapacitance is purely an electric double-layer (EDL) effect, while it occurs with non-carbonaceous electrodes. They indicated that the CV of pseudocapacitance should be rectangular and believe that CV curves with peaks must be indicative of battery behaviors. However, this is also incomplete, as pure EDL capacitance exhibits maximum and minimum values, along with the evolution of the EDL structure under different potentials. For example, at zero charge potential, the mercury electrode shows a minimum capacitance8. At this level, the origin of the rectangular CV curves of the carbonaceous electrode remains elusive. In addition, a shift in the chemical valence of cobalt was observed in the phosphate cobalt oxide they studied6 through the collaborative analysis of cyclic voltammetry and X-ray absorption spectroscopy9. The operando scanning transmission X-ray microscopy (STXM) Co LIII-edge XAS spectra also observed this phenomenon10. However, from a thermodynamic perspective, the CV curve of a redox reaction must have a pair of peaks; therefore, the origin of pseudocapacitance should be further re-understood. Recently, Augustyn’s group indicated that pseudocapacitance lies in a continuum between double-layer capacitance and Faradaic intercalation, particularly under confinement conditions11. Our group proposed a mechanism, named conversion pseudocapacitance, which describes the conversion reaction between two phases with structural similarity and is achieved through a diffusionless phase transition12. Merited by the critical role of electrochemical interfaces13, our group also discovered that the composition of EDL can modulate the electrochemical energy storage mechanism of an electrode14. These results imply that the focus should be on how the physical structure, including the electrode and corresponding electrode-electrolyte interface, and its evolution during charging, determines the pseudocapacitive response.

The existence of pseudocapacitance at the surface or in the bulk is another ambiguous point. Conway’s insight dominates the community, suggesting that pseudocapacitance is a surface or subsurface reaction. On the one hand, if we define this surface as the atomic-level interface between the electrode material and the electrolyte, i.e., including the inner and outer surfaces15, it is reasonable. On the other hand, if we define it as the only outer surface, then it is unreasonable. Studies on typical pseudocapacitive materials, such as hydrous RuO2 and δ-MnO2, show that ions, including protons and alkaline cations, respectively, are inserted into their bulk16,17. In this regard, we have even proposed bulk pseudocapacitance as an ultimate target for enhancing capacitance in industrial applications18.

Additionally, the power-law exponents of 1 and 0.5 are typically used to distinguish between capacitance and batteries, respectively. In fact, this exponent of 1 indicated only redox reactions in accordance with the Langmuir model, while 0.5 indicated diffusion-controlled kinetics from the Randles-Sevcik equation. In other words, those exponents only imply the mass-transport feature, not all aspects of electrochemical reactions. In accordance with Costentin et al., they pointed out the challenges in using ʋ/ʋ1/2 diagnosis method19.

In short, Conway and Costentin try to elucidate pseudocapacitance in unique batteries and in EDLs, respectively. As we discussed above, their theories should be further updated. Obviously, pseudocapacitance is a peculiar electrochemical phenomenon that occurs under specific conditions and requires further clarification.

Pseudocapacitance in hydrous RuO2

In 1971, Trasatti and Buzzanca’s pioneering discovery of the RuO2 electrode in H2SO4, i.e., the beginning of pseudocapacitance, exhibited electrochemical behavior with a rectangular and mirror-image CV curve, similar to that of EDLC20. They described that RuO2 prepared by thermochemical decomposition is a new interesting electrode material, a transition metal oxide, but behaves as an electric condenser. Their discussion is highly in agreement with what Costentin et al. proposed. Furthermore, along with the discovery of this unusual electrode, they also highlight the significance of metallic conduction, non-stoichiometric, and hydrous conditions to its electrochemical response. Subsequently, Conway et al. systematically investigated the electrochemical conversion process of Ru metal into RuO2. They attempted to identify the specific chemical reaction equation for RuO2 in an acid solution and demonstrated that electrochemically formed RuO2 is hydrated, thereby facilitating proton transport. As we mentioned above, Conway et al. classified the pseudocapacitance in RuO2 as redox pseudocapacitance based on their theory of fractional Ru atom involvement or fractional proton coverage on the surface in the redox reaction. They attributed the rectangular CV curves to several overlapping stages of the redox reaction4. In 1995, Zheng et al. reported amorphous hydrous RuO2 prepared by a sol-gel method, which exhibited a capacitance of approximately 720 F g−1. They attributed this unprecedented performance to the presence of water molecules, which enable the proton transport into the bulk, achieving not only surface/subsurface but also bulk reaction16. Their seminal work has led the studies on the pseudocapacitive origin of the RuO2 electrode, focusing on the function of water molecules in modulating proton transport. The hydrogen-bond network is a key factor in modulating ion transport and charge transfer at the solid-electrolyte interface; however, it should be noted that not all hydrous transition metal oxides exhibit pseudocapacitive behavior, such as Ni(OH)221. In α-Ni(OH)2 in KOH, it includes water within the electrode, but shows typical battery behavior, due not only to its semiconductor properties but also to the interaction between the electrode and OH-, although the atomic-level mechanisms are still lacking.

In addition, the number of electrons transferred in the hydrous RuO2 electrode storing protons remains debatable. For example, Conway proposed a successive redox stage involving RuⅡ to RuⅥ. Scherson et al. concluded the redox of RuⅢ and RuⅣ by in situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS)22. Moreover, density-functional theory (DFT) calculations indicated that only 0.3 electrons are acquired by the Ru atom due to the metallic nature of RuO223. Another controversy is the contribution percentage of EDL capacitance to the total capacitance. It is stated that the EDL contributes approximately 10%, while Faradic reactions account for the remaining pseudocapacitance24. Sugimoto et al. conducted a collaborative analysis of the correlation between potential windows and capacitance. Thus, they concluded that the EDL capacitance accounts for ~40% and the Faradaic component accounts for 60%25.

The above consensus and inconformity prompt further understanding of the electrochemical charge-storing process of RuO2 in H2SO4, particularly by focusing on its microstructure, proton transport, and electron conduction.

Structure and its evolution under different water contents

On the foregoing, it is necessary to re-understand the pseudocapacitive origin of RuO2 from a physical/material science perspective, that is, to correlate its pseudocapacitive response with its features in atomic structure, proton transport, electron conduction, and proton/electron storage. Compared to its crystalline counterpart, determining the atomic structure of amorphous hydrous RuO2 is challenging, particularly its structural evolution with varying water content. The dominant model proposed by Dmowski et al. is based on the atomic pair density function (PDF) analysis of RuO2·xH2O26. That is, rutile-type RuO2 nanocrystals are dispersed in the confined water (Fig. 2). They also contributed to the nanocrystal, enabling electron conduction and water, facilitating proton transport to achieve electron-proton “double insertion”. On this basis, they further elucidated the optimal specific capacitance achieved at a water content of 0.5, which mediates electron conduction and proton transport. Yoshida et al.27 further deepened this model by using small-angle X-ray scattering; they indicated that the nanocrystals could aggregate during heating, thereby reducing the accessible surface area and leading to different capacitance. This electron-proton “double insertion” primarily focuses on the kinetic transport of electrons and ions; the interaction between them is neglected. Additionally, based on electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis, Sugimoto et al. proposed a fractal tree-root model, indicating that the nanocrystal, which comprises mesopores and micropores, modulates proton transport and thereby influences the capacitance. For the atomic-level structure of RuO2 nanocrystal, McKeown et al. demonstrated that RuO2·0.29H2O has similar RuO6 octahedrons to those of its crystal counterpart, while the three-dimensional network of octahedral RuO6 octahedrons cannot be paralleled to that of crystal RuO2. Furthermore, RuO2·2.32H2O is composed of chains of disordered RuO6 octahedrons, but no longer exists in a three-dimensional ordered structure28. They also caught a new emergence peak of 2.2 Å in the spectrum of Fourier transform-extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) of hydrous RuO2, which was assigned to the Ru-O-H bonding. Twenty years later, Parker et al. verified this deduction through the neutron scattering technique. They demonstrated that the surface of RuO₂·xH₂O is mainly composed of Ru-OH, and a small content of water is linked via hydrogen bonds. They also showed that the hydroxyl groups can be stable up to 200 °C, i.e., in the water content range of 0.2–2, and that in the range of 0.5–0.7, the surface holds fully hydroxylated29.

Hydrogen-bond network and corresponding proton transport

Electron-proton “double insertion” into hydrous RuO2, which essentially describes the pseudocapacitive origin of hydrous RuO2: the electron and proton can simultaneously arrive at the interface, leading to linear increases or decreases in potential and charge during charge and discharge, respectively. For a charged-ion insertion process, typically, it includes three main steps: diffusion in solution, transfer at the electrode-electrolyte interface, and diffusion within the electrode. In comparison, the presence of water molecules and protons makes proton transport distinct from other processes, such as Li+ intercalation into graphite, which is a typical battery behavior.

As shown in Fig. 3a, charged-ion transfer at the electrode-electrolyte interface: a solvated ion must be desolvated to a naked ion, which requires an activation energy (Einterface, Ei). This still does not take into account the activation energy of Li+ ion transport within the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) in a Li+-graphite system. In addition, an activation energy is also required for the diffusion of Li+ ions within the graphite layers (Ediffusion, Ed). In contrast, in the H+ (H2SO4-water solution)-hydrous RuO2 system, water molecules within the RuO2 readily interact with solution water, enabling proton transfer at the interface with a negligible activation energy. Proton transport in water is considered via both structural and vehicle mechanisms30; the former is more widely known as the Grotthuss mechanism (Fig. 3b). Although the origin of the Grotthuss mechanism is still not fully understood, it enables protons to be transported with negligible activation energy31. Thus, in the case of the H+ (H2SO4-water solution)-hydrous RuO2 system, a proton, or hydronium, can arrive at the (inner and outer) interface without requiring activation energy.

As the temperature increases, not only does the dimension of nanocrystal aggregates change, determining the nanocrystal surface exposed to the electrolyte, but also the structure of the hydrogen-bond network is reconstructed. The confined short hydrogen-bond (SHB) network was recently shown to exhibit competitive proton conductivity32. This indicates that heating at different temperatures also affects proton transport, although this has not been experimentally demonstrated.

It is still not clear whether a proton is inserted into the lattice of the nanocrystal of RuO2. McKeown et al. reported that, despite the different local structures of RuO2·0.29H2O and RuO2·2.32H2O, their specific capacitances are essentially equivalent. This result implies that the nanocrystal’s structure does not influence its proton storage capacity. In other words, the proton may not be inserted into the nanocrystal but confined to the interface, and the reason remains under further investigation.

Metallic nature and the corresponding behavior of electron gain and loss

How the electron obtains and loses within the electrode is another factor that determines the electrochemical behavior, whether battery or pseudocapacitance. As shown in Fig. 4, for a semiconductor electrode, in the oxidation process, the Fermi level of the electrode should be lower than the highest valence band of the electrode, or the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) for more specifically, i.e., electrons from the valence band move to the external circuit; in the reduction process, the Fermi level of the electrode should be higher than the conduction band of the electrode, or the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), i.e., electrons from external circuit move to conduction band. The movement of the Fermi level in such a case implies the different potentials for the oxidation and reduction reactions. In contrast, for a metal or metallic electrode, which has a high density of states at the Fermi level, the gain and loss of electrons are not limited by the above-mentioned HOMO-LUMO system.

a Energy band structure of a metal or metallic electrode, which has empty orbits for electron storing as an electron condenser, b Energy band structure of a semiconductor electrode, where electrons are obtained and lost during redox reactions.

In addition, for a metallic electrode, the electrons are more delocalized, and the activation energy of electron movement is lower than that in a semiconductor. As revealed by DFT calculations, RuO2 exhibits a metallic nature due to π overlapping of Ru 4d orbitals between neighbouring RuO6 octahedra23. This means that storage and release of electrons in RuO2 are not dependent on a single fixed potential, thus they work as an electron container. Intriguingly, in accordance with our understanding, Fontaine’s group recently reported that pseudocapacitive materials, including RuO₂ and MnO₂, possess a high number of redox centers (n) ranging from 20 to 60 per nm³ and a total electron count (Z) between 0 and 120, implying a high degree of electron delocalization. This feature of electron configuration leads to a particular electron hopping (Khopping, n ≫ 1) and forms a continuous potential distribution, explaining its “electron container” trait of non-fixed potential-dependent electron gain and loss33.

The universality of the conjunction mechanism

The analysis above clearly shows that the pseudocapacitive response in hydrous RuO2 is driven by its unique electronic conduction, proton transport, and interactions between the electrode and protons, although the latter is not yet fully described. This understanding is rooted in the discipline’s philosophy of materials science and engineering, which holds that structure determines properties. In other words, pseudocapacitance is a high-level Faradic process: it involves both electronic conduction in a metallic way and ionic charge transport, with negligible kinetic barriers, and a unique interaction between the electrode and ionic charges, all of which are indispensable. Thus, we named our understanding as conjunction mechanism. For the conjunctive mechanism, this concept is used to emphasize the synergistic contributions of electronic conduction, mass transport, and interactions between the electrode and protons to the pseudocapacitive response. Specifically, metallic electronic conduction eliminates bandgap limitations, while proton transport through the Grotthuss mechanism occurs with a low energy barrier. Meanwhile, electrode-proton interactions are mediated by interfacial water molecules. This mechanism ensures that the pseudocapacitive response does not exhibit any obvious peaks in cyclic voltammetry. The absence of any kinetic barrier is the primary cause of this phenomenon.

To further verify the universality of our understanding, the typical pseudocapacitive electrodes (δ-MnO₂, MXene Ti3C2Tx, etc.) are also examined. Augustyn group’s insightful work on δ-MnO₂ reveals that the presence of interlayer structure water mediates the interaction between K+ and MnO6 slabs17. Furthermore, their DFT calculations indicated that δ-MnO₂ is metallic across different K+ and H2O contents. MXene Ti3C2Tx34 is an ideal electrode to strengthen our understanding; the carbide core enables rapid electron gain and loss, while the presence of water within the interlayer corride not only facilitates ionic charge transport but also mediates the interaction between ionic charges and the Ti3C2Tx host. Another example is c-V2O3/r-VO2−x35. Via the corundum-to-rutile phase transition, Lang et al. constructed a VO2−x phase for kinetically favorable Na+ (de)intercalation and a c-V2O3/r-VO2−x phase for electronic conductivity by the metallic V–V chains.

Characterization techniques

To clarify the pseudocapacitive mechanism we proposed, specifically the hydrogen-bond network and electron delocalization, as illustrated in Fig. 5, the cutting-edge in situ spectroscopic characterization techniques and theoretical calculations should be adopted. Raman spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)36 can be used to analyze the O-H vibration, distinguishing the forms of water. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations can be performed to calculate hydrogen bond binding energies, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can be conducted to investigate the hydrogen bond network37. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) can be used to determine the chemical valence state and coordination environment of transition metals38 such as Ru and Mn. These results can also be used to identify the extent of electron delocalization. Meanwhile, DFT calculations are also effective tools for investigating the electrode’s electronic structure. An in situ conductive atomic force microscope (C-AFM) can be used to simultaneously observe the surface conductivity of materials (such as the resistance characteristics of CuOₓ islands), and the local friction force, related to the interfacial molecular configuration, in an in situ condition39.

Perspectives

As recently discussed40, the electrochemical energy storage process, whether in batteries or capacitors, is an electron-ion coupled kinetic process. For hydrous RuO2 in H2SO4, proton transport is not impeded by energy barriers at the solid-liquid interface or within the electrode due to the presence of water molecules. Meanwhile, electron gain and loss are not confined to a fixed potential in the electrode due to its metallic nature. Based on these analyses, we propose a conjunction mechanism that breaks with the theory of Conway and Costentin. This mechanism stems from the correlation between their physical structures and electrochemical behaviors, in line with the philosophy that structure determines properties. The physical structure includes the electrode’s crystalline and electronic structures, as well as the electrode-electrolyte configuration. Specifically, in pseudocapacitive materials, charge is stored via the formation of an electric double layer, with electronic and ionic charges simultaneously accumulating at the electrode-electrolyte interface. However, unlike traditional EDL, electron gain and loss occur faradically, without potential limitation, due to their metallic nature. Ionic charges reach the outer or inner interface15 with negligible energy barriers, facilitated by the hydrogen-bond network and the special proton transport mechanism.

Outlook

To refine our understanding, in the future, factors such as the hydrogen-bond network and layered or tunnel dimensions, which modulate the transport of ionic charges at the interface or in the bulk of the electrode, should be further elaborated. The non-water-molecule-solvated proton should be fabricated to examine how the energy barrier to charged-ion transfer at the interface influences the pseudocapacitive response. How ionic charge transport on the time-domain scale influences the pseudocapacitive response. In addition to the metallic nature of the electrode we have already emphasized, factors that essentially determine electron gain and loss in Coulombic or Faradic reactions should be further investigated. The interaction between the electrode and ionic charges should also be emphasized, particularly how it influences the electrode’s stability and electron gain and loss. Cutting-edge characterization techniques and theoretical calculations should be used to investigate the issues above. For hydrous RuO2, specifically, the evolution of the hydrogen-bond network during heating, specifically how structured water and free water within the electrode affect proton transport and the pseudocapacitive response. Although the delocalized electron is found to be prone to the electrochemical reaction, the descriptor of electron delocalization should be found to distinguish the degree of coupling of the electron and charged ions and the influence on the pseudocapacitive response.
