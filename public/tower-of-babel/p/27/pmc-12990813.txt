Dynapenic Abdominal Obesity and the Risk of Stroke in Postmenopausal Women
Authors: Mingying Cao, Yan Zhang, Zhuhua Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is highly prevalent among middle-aged and older adults, posing a significant global health burden. Dynapenic abdominal obesity (DAO), a clinical phenotype integrating dynapenia and abdominal obesity, has been linked to various chronic diseases. However, its association with incident stroke in postmenopausal women remains unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the prospective association between DAO and new-onset stroke in postmenopausal women.MethodsWe included 2,654 participants from five waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Dynapenia was defined as handgrip strength < 18 kg, and abdominal obesity as waist circumference ≥ 85 cm. Participants were categorized into four groups: non-dynapenic/non-abdominally obese (ND/NAO), abdominally obese only (ND/AO), dynapenic only (D/NAO), and DAO (D/AO). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the association between DAO and incident stroke.ResultsDuring a median 9-year follow-up, 309 incident stroke cases were documented. The incidence of stroke was highest in the D/AO group compared to other groups. After adjustment for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and cardiometabolic risk factors,the D/AO group showed a significantly higher risk of stroke compared to the ND/NAO group (HR: 2.341, 95% CI: 1.511–3.628).ConclusionThe coexistence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity is independently associated with a markedly elevated risk of incident stroke among postmenopausal women. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying biological mechanisms in more detail and on designing and evaluating interventions specifically aimed at mitigating the stroke risk associated with this critical phenotype.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, posing a substantial burden on global public health systems and socioeconomic resources.1,2 The epidemiological profile of stroke exhibits a distinct gender disparity, with postmenopausal women facing an accelerated risk.3,4 This critical period of heightened vulnerability underscores the insufficiency of traditional cardiovascular risk factors alone in explaining the excess risk, thereby directing scientific inquiry towards the unique pathophysiological shifts occurring during the menopausal transition.

Traditionally, sarcopenia has been characterized primarily by a decline in muscle mass.5 However, accumulating evidence indicates that muscle function, specifically strength and power, collectively termed dynapenia, may serve as a more sensitive and earlier predictor of functional decline, disability, and mortality than muscle mass alone.6,7 Abdominal obesity, defined by excessive accumulation of visceral adipose tissue, is a well-established contributor to systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and the metabolic syndrome.8,9 The coexistence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity delineates a distinct clinical phenotype, termed Dynapenic Abdominal Obesity (DAO).10 This phenotype embodies a “double burden,” in which the metabolic dysregulation of obesity amplifies the functional impairments and metabolic inefficiency associated with reduced muscle strength, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle that predisposes individuals to a broad spectrum of health disorders.11,12 The biological plausibility linking DAO to stroke risk may involve several interconnected pathophysiological pathways. Visceral adiposity is a key driver of chronic low-grade inflammation, characterized by increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which promote insulin resistance (IR), oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction.13–16 These processes contribute to vascular injury and play central roles in the development of atherosclerosis and cerebrovascular disease. Concurrently, dynapenia reflects impaired muscle quality and reduced metabolic reserve, which may further exacerbate IR and dysglycemia.17,18 Given the endocrine function of skeletal muscle, diminished muscle strength may also be associated with reduced secretion of vasoprotective and anti-inflammatory myokines.19,20 The coexistence of abdominal obesity and dynapenia may therefore create a metabolically adverse milieu that accelerates endothelial injury, atherosclerotic progression, and thrombogenic processes, thereby increasing susceptibility to stroke.21,22

The associations of DAO with frailty progression, chronic kidney disease, and all-cause mortality have been increasingly recognized.10,23–25 Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that DAO is associated with an increased risk of heart disease,26,27 a condition that shares multiple risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms with stroke. However, its potential association with stroke remains largely unexplored, particularly among postmenopausal women. The heightened vulnerability to stroke observed in postmenopausal women may be partially attributable to sex-specific biological mechanisms accompanying the menopausal transition. The alterations in body composition following menopause are dual-faceted. Postmenopausal women undergo a marked shift in body composition due to declining estrogen levels, characterized by increased visceral fat accumulation and accelerated loss of skeletal muscle mass.28–30 These alterations may synergistically exacerbate cardiometabolic dysregulation and cerebrovascular risk, rendering postmenopausal women particularly susceptible to stroke. To date, no large-scale prospective study has specifically examined the association between DAO and incident stroke in postmenopausal women. Therefore, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we aimed to investigate the prospective association between DAO and incident stroke in postmenopausal women. By integrating simple assessments of muscle strength and central adiposity, we aim to provide novel evidence supporting DAO as a clinically relevant and readily identifiable risk phenotype for stroke prevention in this high-risk population.

Methods

Study Population

Data for this cohort study were derived from five waves of the CHARLS conducted between 2011 and 2020. CHARLS targets Chinese residents aged 45 years and older, with the primary objective of investigating population aging and monitoring longitudinal changes in health status among middle-aged and older adults. The present analysis used de-identified, publicly available CHARLS data, which had received prior ethical approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent. Following review by the Ethics Committee of the Tianjin Union Medical Center, this study was determined to meet the criteria outlined in the Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (effective February 18, 2023), Article 32, items (1) and (2). Detailed information about the CHARLS study is available at https://charls.pku.edu.cn/index.htm. Participants were categorized as postmenopausal if they answered “yes” to the direct question: “Have you started menopause?” Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach. A total of 6,091 postmenopausal women were included at baseline. After excluding individuals with incomplete or missing data, the final analytical cohort comprised 2,654 participants (Figure 1). Assessment of Dynapenia, Abdominal Obesity, and Dynapenic Abdominal Obesity

Handgrip strength (HGS) was assessed by trained personnel using a calibrated, standardized dynamometer (Yuejian WL-1000, China).31 Dynapenia was defined as HGS < 18 kg in women, in accordance with the 2019 consensus of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS).32 Abdominal obesity was assessed using waist circumference (WC), measured by trained personnel according to standardized protocols at the level of the navel over light clothing.24 Abdominal obesity was defined according to the 2024 Chinese Medical Association Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Obesity, with sex-specific thresholds of ≥ 85 cm in women.33 Participants were classified into four mutually exclusive groups based on the presence or absence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity: (1) no dynapenia and no abdominal obesity (ND/NAO), (2) abdominal obesity alone (ND/AO), (3) dynapenia alone (D/NAO), and (4) concurrent dynapenia and abdominal obesity (D/AO).

Definition of Stroke

Consistent with previous studies,34,35 stroke diagnosis was determined based on doctor-confirmed diagnosis via self-reported medical histories recorded during survey interviews. The onset time was defined as the midpoint between the follow-up wave in which the diagnosis was reported and the participant’s most recent preceding follow-up. The median duration of follow-up was 9 years.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on existing literature36–39 and clinical judgment. Figure S1 illustrates the hypothesized causal framework of the study. Sociodemographic factors (age, residence, education and marital status) and lifestyle behaviors (smoking and alcohol use) were considered potential confounders because they may influence both body composition and stroke risk. Body mass index (BMI) was included to account for overall adiposity independent of central fat distribution. Cardiometabolic factors, including hypertension, diabetes, glycohemoglobin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and C-reactive protein (CRP), were included to examine whether the association between DAO and stroke was independent of established vascular risk pathways. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages and evaluated using the chi-square test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the association between dynapenic abdominal obesity and incident endpoint event, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was unadjusted for covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age, residence, educational attainment, and marital status. Model 3 additionally adjusted for alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, LDL, CRP, glycohemoglobin, hypertension and diabetes. Because the aforementioned cardiometabolic factors may lie on the causal pathway linking DAO to stroke, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding these potential mediators to estimate the total effect of DAO. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations were detected. Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed to depict the survival probabilities for endpoint event across the four study groups. Subgroup analyses were conducted, stratified by age, residence, and hypertension. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this large, prospective cohort study of postmenopausal women, we identified that the co-occurrence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity, a condition termed DAO, was robustly and independently associated with a significantly elevated risk of incident stroke. Individuals with the DAO phenotype faced more than a twofold increased risk of stroke compared to those with neither condition, even after comprehensive adjustment for a wide spectrum of potential confounders, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle habits, and cardiometabolic risk factors. Notably, the risk associated with DAO appeared to be synergistic, as it exceeded the risk observed in groups with either abdominal obesity or dynapenia alone.

The associations between DAO and frailty progression, chronic kidney disease, and all-cause mortality are increasingly acknowledged.10,23–25 For instance, in a 7-year longitudinal study involving 11,980 middle-aged and older adults, the DAO group (HR: 1.84, 95% CI: 1.45–2.33) had significantly higher all-cause mortality risk compared to the reference group. In a prospective analysis utilizing data from 5932 participants, DAO was associated with a significantly increased risk of CKD (OR:1.57, 95% CI: 1.03–2.35). Results from our study indicated that DAO was robustly and independently associated with a significantly elevated risk of incident stroke. The pathophysiological mechanisms underpinning the potent association between DAO and stroke are likely multifactorial and interrelated. First, the DAO phenotype encapsulates a state of profound metabolic dysregulation. Abdominal obesity, particularly excess visceral adiposity, is a well-established source of chronic, low-grade inflammation,13,14 secreting pro-inflammatory adipokines including TNF-α and IL-6,40,41 and contributing to endothelial dysfunction and IR,15,16 all of which play central roles in the initiation and progression of cerebrovascular disease. Dynapenia, on the other hand, diminishes the body’s largest glucose-disposing organ and a crucial regulator of systemic metabolism,17,18 thereby further exacerbates this adverse metabolic milieu. The loss of muscle mass and function reduces insulin-mediated glucose uptake, promotes systemic IR and dysglycemia, and is independently linked to higher levels of inflammatory markers.19,20 The coexistence of these conditions likely creates a vicious cycle: systemic inflammation from visceral fat can accelerate muscle protein breakdown, while dynapenia-induced physical inactivity further promotes visceral fat accumulation and worsens metabolic health. The concurrence of dynapenic abdominal obesity may thus exert synergistic detrimental effects on vascular health, promoting atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombogenic processes, and thereby substantially increasing susceptibility to stroke.21,22,42

It needs to be emphasized that our findings must be interpreted within the unique endocrine context of postmenopause. The decline in estrogen levels during this period directly facilitates the phenotypic of DAO by promoting central fat redistribution and accelerating the loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength.28,43,44 Estrogen deprivation not only exacerbates the individual components of DAO but may also amplify their combined deleterious effects on vascular health.45 Therefore, DAO can be viewed as a tangible clinical manifestation of the adverse body composition changes that characterize the menopausal transition, making it a highly relevant risk marker for this specific population. Our study moves beyond the limitations of BMI by demonstrating that the quality of body composition, specifically the dysfunctional combination of excess visceral fat and inadequate muscle strength, is a more critical determinant of stroke risk than overall adiposity in postmenopausal women.

The clinical and public health implications of our findings are substantial. They advocate for a shift in the risk assessment and primary prevention of stroke among postmenopausal women. Current clinical practice often relies on BMI, which fails to distinguish between fat and muscle mass and may misclassify individuals with normal weight but high body fat and low muscle strength as low risk.46,47 Our results suggest that the simple, low-cost, and non-invasive assessment of both waist circumference and handgrip strength could serve as a practical screening strategy and should be integrated into routine health screenings for middle-aged and older women. The identification of the DAO phenotype would allow for the early stratification of women at exceptionally high risk for stroke, enabling targeted and intensive lifestyle interventions. These interventions should be dual-pronged, combining resistance training to combat dynapenia and improve muscle quality with caloric restriction and aerobic exercise to reduce visceral adiposity, thereby offering a feasible lifestyle-based approach for stroke prevention in postmenopausal women. Such a tailored approach holds greater promise for breaking the detrimental cycle of DAO than generic weight-loss advice. Although WC and HGS are simple and low-cost assessments, their routine implementation may encounter practical challenges in resource-limited settings, particularly with respect to equipment availability and personnel training. Therefore, a stepwise or targeted screening approach may represent a pragmatic initial strategy in these settings.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, ascertainment of stroke was based on patient self-report, which may be prone to recall bias and misclassification. Nevertheless, the validity of self-reported stroke events has been previously confirmed, providing reasonable assurance of reliability.48,49 Second, the absence of stroke subtype classification in the CHARLS questionnaire may obscure differences in underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and risk factors between ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes, highlighting the need for subtype-specific analyses in future research. Third, although adjustments were made for a broad spectrum of potential confounders, residual confounding such as physical activity intensity, dietary intake and heterogeneity in cumulative hormonal exposure cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, given that this study was conducted predominantly in a Chinese population, variations in ethnicity, lifestyle patterns, body composition profiles, and diagnostic cut-offs may limit the generalizability of these findings to other populations. Accordingly, validation in diverse ethnic and cultural contexts is warranted. Finally, Given that participants with dynapenia comprised a relatively small proportion of the cohort, the statistical power for these comparisons may have been limited. Future studies including larger samples are warranted to confirm these associations.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that DAO is independently associated with a markedly elevated risk of incident stroke among postmenopausal women. These findings support the clinical relevance of DAO as an important and potentially modifiable risk phenotype, beyond traditional measures of adiposity. Given the observational design of this study, the findings support an association rather than a causal relationship. Nevertheless, the incorporation of simple assessments such as WC and HGS into routine clinical practice may facilitate early identification of postmenopausal women at heightened stroke risk. Integrated strategies combining resistance training to enhance muscle strength with interventions targeting abdominal adiposity may constitute a promising approach to reducing stroke risk in this high-risk population. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to evaluate the efficacy of targeted preventive interventions.
