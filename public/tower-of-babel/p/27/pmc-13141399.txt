From missed visits to missed tests: determinants of antenatal care non-attendance and mother-to-child HIV transmission risk among pregnant women in Tanzania: a population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Fabiola Vincent Moshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal care is essential for preventing mother-to-child HIV transmission and improving maternal and child health in Tanzania. However, many pregnant women do not attend ANC, leading to missed HIV testing and increased transmission risk. This study examined the association between ANC non-attendance and missed HIV testing and identified factors associated with ANC non-attendance among pregnant women in Tanzania.MethodThe study used data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey, analyzing a weighted sample of 5,431 women. Descriptive analysis estimated the proportion of women who never attended ANC, while logistic regression was used to examine the association between ANC non-attendance and missed HIV testing and to identify predictors of ANC non-attendance.ResultsIn Tanzania, 609(10.4%, at 95% CI = 10.0%-11.6%) of pregnant women never attended ANC. Not attending ANC was significantly associated with never tested for HIV, (aOR = 4.752 at 95% CI = 3.446–6.553, p < 0.001), age category of a woman [20-24years (aOR = 1.943at 95% CI = 1.261–2.993, p = 0.003), 25-29years (aOR = 1.938, at 95% CI = 1.206–3.114, p = 0.006), 30–34 (aOR = 2.661,at 95% CI = 1.600-4.427, p < 0.001), 35–39 (aOR = 2.518, at 95% CI = 1.453–4.366, p = 0.001), 40–44 (aOR = 2.184, at 95% CI = 1.172–4.072, p = 0.014) and 45–49 (aOR = 2.897, at 95% CI = 1.268–6.616, p = 0.012)]; Zone of residence [Northern zone (aOR = 1.465, at 95% CI = 1.053–2.037, p = 0.023), Central zone (aOR = 0.181, at 95% CI = 0.107–0.306 p < 0.001), Southern zone (aOR = 0.055, at 95% CI = 0.013–0.232 p < 0.001), Lake zone (aOR = 0.409, at 95% CI = 0.303–0.553 p < 0.001), Eastern zone (aOR = 0.522, at 95% CI = 0.345–0.791, p = 0.002) and Zanzibar zone (aOR = 0.1 at 95% CI = 0.028–0.358, p < 0.001)]; respondent working status, not working (aOR = 2.522, at 95% CI = 2.069–3.074 p < 0.001), and never used anything to prevent or delay pregnancy (aOR = 2.373, at 95% CI = 1.908–2.952, p < 0.001).ConclusionIn Tanzania, a notable proportion of pregnant women did not attend antenatal care, which significantly increased the likelihood of never being tested for HIV. Non-attendance was associated with maternal age, zone of residence, employment status, and prior contraceptive use. Targeted interventions addressing older women, unemployed women, and those with no history of contraception use, particularly in high-risk zones, are crucial. Enhancing community outreach and ensuring equitable access to ANC services could significantly reduce missed visits, promote early HIV testing, and improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.Keywords: Antenatal Care, Non-Attendance, Human Immunodeficiency Virus, Pregnant Women, Tanzania

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a significant global public health challenge despite considerable advances in prevention, testing, and treatment strategies. By the end of 2024, an estimated 40.8 million people were living with HIV worldwide, with nearly two-thirds (65%) residing in the WHO African Region [1]. At the national level, Tanzania continues to bear a substantial HIV burden. The estimated HIV prevalence among adults aged 15–49 years is approximately 4.4%, corresponding to about 1,548,000 adults living with HIV [2]. Notably, the epidemic disproportionately affects women, with a prevalence of 5.6% compared with 3.0% among men [2]. In the same year, approximately 630,000 deaths were attributed to HIV-related causes globally, underscoring the persistent and substantial impact of the epidemic on public health [1].

In 2024, over 1.3 million people were newly infected with HIV, with women and girls accounting for 44% of these cases [3]. Despite global efforts to control the spread of HIV, progress remains significantly off-track. Each year, around 1.3 million women and girls living with HIV become pregnant [4]. Without intervention, the risk of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding can range from 15% to 45% [4]. This underscores the urgent need for immediate linkage to lifelong treatment and care once HIV is diagnosed, ensuring viral suppression and partner services are consistently offered.

Efforts to eliminate MTCT are reinforced by global commitments, emphasizing the integration of prevention interventions into maternal, newborn, child, and adolescent health services, alongside strengthening health systems. Additionally, promoting access to sexual and reproductive health services, including the prevention of unintended pregnancies and the screening and treatment of sexually transmitted infections among women and girls living with HIV, is actively encouraged.

In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), which accounts for two-thirds of new HIV infections globally, routine HIV testing of pregnant women during ANC is nearly universal [5], with treatment initiation for those who test positive being a standard practice. Couples are typically screened for HIV during the first ANC visit, and for those identified as HIV-positive, timely initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) is implemented to prevent vertical transmission from mother to child. This early intervention is crucial in reducing the risk of HIV transmission during pregnancy, labor, delivery, and breastfeeding.

The adoption of the 2013 World Health Organization’s Option B+ guidelines for HIV treatment during pregnancy has been instrumental in accelerating progress toward universal HIV treatment for pregnant and postpartum women across SSA, including Tanzania [6]. This approach integrates a comprehensive package of care into ANC visits, ensuring immediate and lifelong ART for all HIV-positive pregnant women. The implementation of Option B + has significantly strengthened efforts to prevent mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) while improving maternal and child health outcomes in the region [7].

The successful implementation of the Option B+ plan is closely tied to access to ANC services. However, like many other low-income countries, Tanzania faces significant challenges in achieving universal ANC coverage [8]. Despite adopting the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation for eight ANC visits, the proportion of women in Tanzania who complete the recommended visits remains critically low [9]. Moreover, there are stark disparities in access to ANC services, with rural areas and marginalized populations being disproportionately affected [10]. This gap in care undermines efforts to ensure comprehensive HIV treatment during pregnancy and access to PMCTC services.

Previous studies across SSA have highlighted stark regional disparities in adequate ANC utilization, with the Eastern region exhibiting the lowest prevalence at just 53.4%, while the Southern region leads with 78.9% [11]. Several critical predictors of inadequate ANC visits have been consistently identified, including young maternal age [12], rural residence [10–12], lower educational attainment [11–14], belonging to the lowest household wealth category [11–14], multiparity [12], limited media access [11–15], insufficient awareness of danger signs, appropriate timing, and the recommended number of antenatal visits [15], negative attitudes toward ANC services [15], low male partner involvement [16], and a lack of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making [11].

While the majority of previous studies have focused on the magnitude and determinants of inadequate ANC utilization, there is a significant lack of data on the prevalence and predictors of non-attendance in low-income countries like Tanzania, particularly in relation to the association between never attending ANC and never being tested for HIV. This study aimed to address that critical gap.

Method

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design, utilizing secondary data from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey and Malaria Indicator Survey (2022 TDH-MIS). The 2022 TDHS-MIS is a nationally representative survey that collected comprehensive information on various health-related indicators, including maternal and child health, reproductive health, and fertility.

Study population

The analysis for this paper focused on women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who provided responses regarding the number of antenatal care visits they attended during their most recent pregnancy.

Sample size

The 2022 TDHS-MIS utilized a stratified two-stage sample design to provide estimates for the entire country, including urban and rural areas in mainland Tanzania and Zanzibar. In the first stage, sampling points (clusters) were selected from enumeration areas delineated in the 2012 Tanzania Population and Housing Census (2012 PHC), with selection probability proportional to the size of each sampling stratum. A total of 629 clusters were chosen. In the second stage, 26 households were systematically selected from each cluster, resulting in a total of 16,312 households. All women aged 15–49 who were either usual residents or visitors in the household on the night before the survey interview were included in the 2022 TDHS-MIS and were eligible to be interviewed. Out of the 16,312 selected households, 15,907 were occupied. Of these, 15,705 households were successfully interviewed, yielding a response rate of 99%. Within the interviewed households, 15,699 women aged 15–49 were identified as eligible for individual interviews. Interviews were completed with 15,254 women, resulting in a response rate of 97%.

Data extraction

The Individual Recode (TZIR82SV.ZIP) file was utilized to extract eligible participants. After excluding 9,800 women who were not pregnant in the five years prior to the survey and 23 who were unsure of the number of ANC visits they had made, the final sample consisted of 5,431 women.

Study variables

The outcome variable was ANC attendance and was coded as 1 for women who never attended and 0 for those who attended at least once. Independent variables included age categories, highest education level, place of residence, zone of residence, wealth index, number of children ever born, internet exposure, use of any pregnancy control methods, and current employment status. Additionally, HIV testing status was included to assess the risk of mother-to-child HIV transmission among women who never attended ANC in Tanzania.

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. The analysis accounted for the complex sampling design of the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) by applying the sampling weight variable v005 to obtain nationally representative estimates. Following DHS guidelines, the weight variable was normalized by dividing v005 by 1,000,000 before use in the analysis. Weighted analyses were conducted using the Complex Samples module in SPSS, which incorporates sampling weights during estimation. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics. Both bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to assess the predictors of ANC non-attendance and the association between ANC non-attendance and HIV testing status.

Potential confounders identified from prior literature and bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to control for their effects. Effect modification was assessed by introducing interaction terms between selected variables. The goodness of fit for the multivariable model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test with SPSS version 25. The results indicated a good fit for the data: 𝜒2 (8) = 11.251, p = 0.188. The final model correctly classified 94.6% of the cases. Statistical significance was set at p-values < 0.05.

Discussion

This study assessed the association between antenatal care (ANC) non-attendance and missed HIV testing and identified factors associated with ANC non-attendance among pregnant women in Tanzania. The findings indicate that a notable proportion of pregnant women do not attend ANC at all, and a large share of women who had never been tested for HIV were among those who did not attend ANC even once. A significant association was observed between ANC non-attendance and never having been tested for HIV, highlighting the critical role of ANC as an entry point for HIV screening during pregnancy. Factors associated with ANC non-attendance included increasing maternal age, unemployment, lack of family planning use, and geographic residence.

The WHO recommends that women with uncomplicated pregnancies attend at least eight ANC visits [17]. However, only 3.1% of women in Tanzania achieve this target, placing the country among those with the lowest proportions of women completing the recommended number of visits in sub-Saharan Africa [18]. Although 65.2% of pregnant women attend at least four ANC visits, this level of attendance does not necessarily translate into adequate care. Previous studies report that only 39.3% [19] to 41% [20] of women receive the full package of essential ANC services, including blood pressure measurement, urine and blood tests, tetanus vaccination, and counseling on pregnancy danger signs. These findings highlight an important gap between ANC attendance and the quality or completeness of services provided. Nevertheless, even a single ANC visit remains critical because it provides an opportunity for HIV testing and other essential maternal health interventions [21].

The observed ANC non-attendance rate of 10.4% represents an important public health concern, as women who miss all ANC visits also miss critical opportunities for HIV testing and prevention of mother-to-child transmission. Comparatively, a lower non-attendance rate of 2.7% has been reported in Ethiopia [22], while a much higher rate of 30.7% was documented in Afghanistan [23]. Differences across countries may reflect variations in health systems, accessibility of maternal health services, and study populations. These comparisons highlight the importance of context-specific strategies to improve ANC utilization.

The study further found that most women who had never been tested for HIV were those who did not attend ANC during their most recent pregnancy, emphasizing the central role of ANC services in ensuring HIV screening during pregnancy. However, a notable proportion of women who attended several ANC visits still reported never having been tested for HIV, suggesting missed opportunities within the health system. This concern is particularly important given that HIV prevalence among pregnant women in Tanzania exceeds the national average [24].

From a policy perspective, the findings highlight several priority groups that require targeted attention. Older women were more likely to miss ANC visits, possibly due to perceptions that previous pregnancy experience reduces the need for formal care [25]. However, this perception can result in missed opportunities for essential interventions, including HIV testing and monitoring for pregnancy complications.

The study also identified regional disparities in ANC non-attendance. Women in the Northern zone had higher odds of non-attendance compared with those in the Western zone, while women in the Central, Southern, Lake, Eastern, and Zanzibar zones showed lower odds. These regional differences suggest that targeted interventions and resource allocation are needed in areas with higher levels of non-attendance to improve equitable access to maternal health services. Similar geographic disparities have been reported in other settings, including South Sudan [26].

Socioeconomic factors also played a significant role. Women who were not employed were nearly three times more likely to miss ANC visits. This may reflect lower access to health information or financial barriers that limit healthcare utilization. Previous research indicates that women who are aware of obstetric danger signs are more likely to attend ANC services [26] highlighting the importance of strengthening community-based health education.

In addition, women who had never used family planning were twice as likely to miss ANC during pregnancy. A similar study conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa reported comparable findings [27]. This finding suggests that gaps in reproductive health knowledge and service utilization extend beyond pregnancy care alone [28]. Integrating family planning education with maternal health services may therefore improve both contraceptive use and ANC attendance.

The cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between ANC non-attendance and HIV testing status. However, multivariable regression analysis with adjustment for potential confounders was conducted to reduce bias and strengthen the observed associations. The use of “never tested for HIV” as the main explanatory variable may have underestimated the proportion of women who missed HIV testing, specifically during their most recent pregnancy. Nonetheless, the findings provide important insights into the link between ANC utilization and HIV testing coverage.

Conclusion

This study highlights important gaps in ANC utilization and HIV testing among pregnant women in Tanzania. Women who did not attend ANC were significantly more likely to have never been tested for HIV, highlighting the critical role of ANC as the primary platform for HIV screening during pregnancy. Factors such as increasing maternal age, unemployment, lack of family planning use, and regional disparities were associated with higher odds of ANC non-attendance. These findings indicate the need for targeted policy interventions that promote early and consistent ANC attendance, strengthen the integration of HIV testing within maternal health services, and address socioeconomic and geographic barriers to care. Improving women’s access to reproductive health information and services, particularly in high-risk groups and underserved regions, will be essential for enhancing maternal health outcomes and advancing efforts to prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV in Tanzania.
