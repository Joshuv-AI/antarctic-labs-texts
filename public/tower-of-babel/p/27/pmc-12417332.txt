Stem Cell‐Based Cancer Therapies: A Commentary on Therapeutic Promise, Health Impacts and Translational Barriers
Authors: Zar Soe Thin, Nancy Choon‐Si Ng, Long Chiau Ming, Rebecca Shin‐Yee Wong, Bey Hing Goh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStem cell‐based cancer therapies offer novel mechanisms for tumour targeting, immune modulation and tissue repair, with applications from haematopoietic to induced pluripotent stem cells. While showing strong therapeutic promise, their clinical translation is hindered by ethical, regulatory and economic barriers. Coordinated global action is essential to unlock their full potential in oncology.Keywords: cancer immunotherapy, clinical translation, regenerative medicine, stem cell therapy, translational oncology

Cancer is one of the most relevant global public‐health issues; in 2022, over 20 million new cases and almost 10 million deaths due to cancer were registered [1]. Despite the recent advances in conventional treatment modalities, such as radiation, chemotherapy, immunotherapy and surgery, a large number of patients still face relapse, resistance and high levels of treatment‐related toxicity [2]. This fact has led to the development of interest in stem cell (SC)‐based therapies that provide a paradigmatic shift to precision medicine through tissue repair, immune modulation and targeted drug delivery.

Over the last 10 years, the field of haematopoietic, mesenchymal, neural and induced pluripotent stem cells has evolved into laboratory research to clinical trials. Due to the peculiarities of their biological characteristics, SCs may serve as a therapeutic agent, drug carrier and part of a customised immunotherapy. However, cancer therapies involving SCs are still mainly at the research level due to ambiguous regulation systems, ethical dilemmas, poor infrastructure and financial limitations.

The current commentary provides a brief and up‐to‐date analysis of stem cell‐based cancer treatments, especially focusing on the rapid development of the corresponding interventions between 2020 and 2024. This article does not provide an exhaustive review of the literature, in contrast to traditional reviews. Rather, it provides a critical lens through which to view current progress, concentrating on three main themes: (1) the novel mechanisms and therapeutic promise of SC‐based interventions in oncology; (2) the multifaceted health impacts on patients, providers and healthcare systems and (3) the ongoing translational barriers—economic, regulatory and ethical—that need to be addressed in order to facilitate wider clinical adoption.

Therapeutic Promise and Mechanisms of Action

SC‐based therapies use various mechanisms including homing to tumour sites, immune regulation and paracrine signalling. Haematologic malignancies are clinically approved to use haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and they provide graft‐versus‐leukaemia effects when used in allogenic contexts [3, 4]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) with their tumour‐homing and immunomodulatory characteristics have been identified as potential local delivery vehicles of chemotherapy, cytokines and suicide genes [5].

Recent studies have promoted the application of genetically modified MSCs and neural stem cells (NSCs) in delivering therapeutic payloads across physiological barriers, including the blood–brain barrier in the treatment of glioblastoma [5, 6]. In addition, the possibility of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) has provided new opportunities in personalised therapy because they can be used as sources of cancer vaccines and immune cell reprogramming [7].

Emerging delivery technologies such as SC‐derived exosomes and nanoparticle‐armed SCs have further expanded the therapeutic toolkit, enabling precise cargo release at tumour sites while minimising off‐target effects [8, 9].

On Patients

Stem cell therapies have demonstrated improved survival rates in high‐risk cancer patients. Tandem HSC transplants have yielded superior event‐free survival in paediatric neuroblastoma. MSC coadministration in transplantation settings has shown efficacy in reducing graft‐versus‐host disease [10]. Moreover, SC‐derived exosomes and engineered MSCs are under investigation for delivering therapeutic mRNA and cytokines to solid tumours [26].

While the use of iPSC‐based vaccines remains experimental, they show promise in activating broad immune responses against tumour‐associated antigens, potentially reducing relapse risks [11].

On Healthcare Providers

SC‐based therapies are transforming oncologic practice, requiring a multidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, oncologists, cell biologists and clinical pharmacists. Institutions must comply with stringent standards, such as FACT‐JACIE, and adapt to novel procedures, such as SC expansion, cryopreservation and cell banking [12].

The emergence of unproven SC clinics offering ‘miracle cures’ has further complicated professional responsibilities. Clinicians must be prepared to counsel patients navigating unregulated markets, highlighting the need for continuing education and ethics literacy [16, 17].

On Healthcare Systems

SC‐based therapies require a lot of infrastructure, such as Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)‐compliant laboratories, cold‐chain management and multidisciplinary staff. Such requirements are especially straining to low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where such transplants can still be unaffordable [20, 23].

The hospitals often collaborate with the manufacturing and storage companies. Even though such partnerships may facilitate logistical issues, they tend to create economic inequalities and hinder fair access. The lack of standardised reimbursement policies further complicates integration into national health systems [15].

Translational Barriers

Ethical Challenges

Embryonic SCs (ESCs) remain ethically contentious due to the destruction of embryos. While iPSCs address this issue, concerns about tumorigenicity, long‐term effects and unintended differentiation persist [21, 22]. Ethical issues also arise in paediatric transplants, where decisions involve legal considerations and consent complexity [27].

The rise in SC medical tourism—where patients seek unproven therapies abroad—raises concerns about exploitation, especially among terminal patients [18]. Clinics often omit informed consent or misrepresent therapeutic readiness, exacerbating patient vulnerability [19].

Regulatory Inconsistencies

Over 3000 SC clinical trials are registered globally, but only a small fraction have achieved full regulatory approval. Countries vary in defining ‘minimal manipulation’, enabling some clinics to circumvent oversight by falsely classifying interventions as compliant [24].

While Japan has embraced a rapid approval model to boost innovation, critics argue that such leniency risks patient safety. Conversely, FDA guidelines updated in 2020 strive to balance innovation with safety by clearly delineating product classifications [22, 25]. An international harmonisation of regulatory standards is urgently needed.

Economic Constraints

The development of SC therapies requires approximately a decade and investments exceeding $1 billion. These costs deter investment in LMICs, where transplant availability is limited or non‐existent [28]. Even in high‐income settings, reimbursement remains a hurdle due to insufficient long‐term outcome data and the absence of cost‐effectiveness studies [27].

The creation of the DATRI Action Campaign, a community‐based stem cell donor registry in India, is a good example that shows how community‐based organisational frameworks can be used to enable better access to stem cell transplantation in low‐resource environments [23].

Future Directions

The realisation of the therapeutic potential of stem cell‐based cancer interventions requires that future efforts be informed by consistent, internationally coordinated plans that can address current translational challenges. The first of these considerations is harmonisation of regulatory regimes across jurisdictions. Despite having created fast‐track approval schemes to regenerative therapies, Japan still has inconsistent terminologies and market authorisation thresholds, which undermine equal access and patient safety [22]. An internationally agreed system, with governance by organisations like the World Health Organization and the International Society for Stem Cell Research, would establish uniform standards of classification, quality control and regulatory processes of stem cell products, and thus facilitate more predictable and safe clinical use.

The development of workforce is also an essential endeavour alongside the investment in cellular therapy. The industry is undergoing an exponential development, creating an increased demand of clinicians, laboratory specialists, regulatory professionals and bioethicists who understand cellular therapies. There is an urgent need to close this skills gap. Specific training programmes that will be able to respond to the increasing complexity of stem cell research and clinical application are necessary [13, 14]. Without such efforts, the growth of safe and effective stem cell treatment will remain crippled by a lack of personnel and capacity constraints.

The involvement of the population is one of the key factors that will define the direction of SC‐based oncology. Evidence‐based and clear discussion of the potential and the shortcomings of stem cell therapies is necessary to overcome misinformation, limit the appeal of unproven treatments and prompt patients to make informed choices. As a result, clinicians, researchers and regulators ought to work together to create and spread ethics literacy, thus providing patients with a precise understanding of the possible advantages, risks and eligibility requirements.

Fair access is one of the key requirements of the further development of healthcare initiatives. There is thus a need to break down not only costs‐related barriers but also structural differences in infrastructure and in clinical trial representation. In that regard, international partnerships should clearly incorporate low‐ and middle‐income nations into collaborative funding, decentralised trial structures and capacity‐building alliances. The Indian programme DATRI is an example of the possibility of grassroots innovations that can increase donor registries and close access gaps [23].

The ethical supervision should be constantly evolving to the development of scientific knowledge. The informed consent procedures, the transparent publication of clinical‐trial findings and the thorough regulation of the commercialisation of stem cells are essential to maintaining the confidence of the general population and protecting the freedom of patients. When ethical, regulatory and social concerns are instilled in all stages of stem cell therapy development, the field can move towards an inclusive, effective and accountable model of innovation in cancer care.
