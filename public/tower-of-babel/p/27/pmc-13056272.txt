The Computational Cation Electrode: A Case Study on CO2RR
Authors: Emmanouil Pervolarakis, Amanda Schramm Petersen, Alexander Bagger, Jan Rossmeisl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electrochemical reduction of CO2 is a promising way to store renewable energy in chemical bonds and convert CO2 to value‐added products. In this reaction, the role of electrolyte cations has attracted a lot of attention in the past decade. Nevertheless, computational studies still lack a standardized approach for incorporating cation effects. Here, we introduce a computational cation electrode framework that provides a consistent reference state of metal cations in density functional theory calculations. We first outline the limitations of current reference schemes and then propose a new approach that combines the cation reduction potential with an intermediate bulk state that better mimics the cation in the interface. Using this computational cation electrode framework, we evaluate how the choice of reference influences adsorption energetics across metals and discuss strategies for selecting appropriate bulk states for the computational cation electrode. This work establishes a unified protocol for modeling cation effects in the reduction of CO2 and related electrochemical reactions.

Introduction

With the advent of renewable electricity, the electrochemical reduction of CO2 (CO2RR) offers a promising route to store renewable electricity in chemical bonds while closing the carbon cycle. By converting CO2 into value‐added products, CO2RR can reduce reliance on fossil‐derived feedstocks and help balance the energy supply.

Despite decades of study, the reaction remains challenging due to its complex network of pathways and products, which sensitively depend on catalyst material and reaction environment [1, 2, 3, 4]. In addition to adsorption thermodynamics, a variety of electrolyte effects influence activity and selectivity. These include the carbonate equilibrium [5, 6], which is pH dependent [7], the identity and concentration of cations and anions [8, 9, 10], the nature of the proton source [11], and even the CO2 pressure [12, 13].

Among these, the role of alkali metal (AM) cations has received particular attention. Murata and Hori first reported in 1991 that cation identity affects CO2RR selectivity on Cu [9], but this finding has mostly come into the spotlight in the past decade [8, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Recent studies demonstrated that the presence of the cation is essential for CO2RR on Au and Ag [19] and that their identity strongly impacts selectivity [20]. Several mechanisms have been proposed for this effect, ranging from pH buffering [8] and electrode double layer modification [21] to the direct stabilization of reaction intermediates [19, 22]. Recent evidence suggests that the latter plays a dominant role: cations locally stabilize *CO2‐like intermediates by downshifting their lowest unoccupied molecular orbital, enabling stronger interaction with the catalyst's d‐band [22, 23]. In weakly interacting metals such as Ag, Au, and Cu, this cation‐induced stabilization is critical for CO2 activation, whereas in transition metals such as Fe, Co, or Pd, intrinsic overlap with the d‐band reduced the dependence of cations [23]. Similar cation effects are also being investigated across other electrochemical reactions as well [24, 25, 26, 27]. The significance of the cations in these reactions motivated Koper to develop a Marcus‐type theory for cation‐coupled electron transfer (CCET) that introduces a term for the electrostatic interaction between the reactant and the cation that lowers the barrier for electron transfer [28].

Many of these studies combine experiment and theory. Recently, Shin et al. [22] performed CO2RR experiments in a variety of different pHs, both alkaline and acidic. In Figure 1, their polarization curves are plotted using a cation‐corrected electrode (CCE), which was defined as: VCCE=VSHE−0.059 log[K+]along with the conventional reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) and standard hydrogen electrode (SHE). This representation revealed a Nernstian shift with cation concentration in alkaline conditions, suggesting that the rate‐limiting step may involve a cation‐coupled electron transfer rather than a proton–electron transfer. In contrast, in acidic conditions, the effect of cation concentration appeared less pronounced, which could be attributed to the limited access of the alkali cations to the interface.

In the work of Shin et al. they report datasets up to pH 15 based on the KOH concentration but their pH values will likely be lower in alkaline and higher in acidic conditions due to the carbonate equilibrium.

To explore this behavior further, we examined additional experimental works reporting pH‐dependent CO2RR activity on polycrystalline Ag. In particular, Deng [29] measured CO2RR from near neutral to acidic pH values. By extracting data from both studies and plotting them using the CCE methodology introduced by Shin et al., we obtained a combined perspective in Figure 1. Interestingly, the data from the two sources largely overlap at similar pH values, despite differences in experimental conditions.

These observations imply that the role of the alkali cations in CO2RR may differ between acidic and alkaline conditions. Notably, in the acidic range, the experiments only included a limited range of cation concentrations (0.1 and 0.07 M from Shin et al. and 0.1 and 0.3 M from Deng et al.), whereas in the alkaline conditions, Shin et al. explored concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 10 M K+. These findings highlight the need for further investigations to elucidate how the cation concentrations modulate CO2RR activity in acidic conditions.

While such experimental studies have motivated the introduction of a CCE scale, the situation is less clear on the computational side. Despite the widespread recognition of cation effects in CO2RR, there is still no standardized way to incorporate them in density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Most studies have either (i) treated cations implicitly by including them directly at the surface [22, 30, 31], or (ii) adopted a simple bulk bcc metal as the reference state [17, 19, 32, 33]. Using the former approach is definitely convenient but in using that workaround, one cannot know whether the alkali atoms actually accumulate at the interface or whether they will be there at all. The latter, using the bcc metal as the reference, provides only a partial solution, as it compares an ionic species at the interface to a metallic reference. A few alternative approaches exist, such as the oxide reference [24] or the gas‐phase ion reference corrected by the experimental solvation energies [34], but these too suffer from limitations. Thus, while individual studies have included cation effects in different ways, a comprehensive and systematic framework—analogous to the computational hydrogen electrode [35] (CHE)—is still missing.

In this work, we present a computational cation electrode framework that extends the principle of the CHE to AM cations. Such an computational cation electrode was first introduced by Resasco et al. [17], but instead of relying solely on the metallic bcc phase, the computational cation electrode we propose, employs schemes that preserve the ionic character of the alkali cation by combining ionic‐bonded references (MCl, M2O, M2O2, and MOH) with the standard reduction potentials to define a consistent cation reference state. Using this framework, we evaluate the adsorption energetics for relevant intermediates (K, COOK, COOH, and COOH‐K) on four metals (Au, Ag, Cu, and Pd) and compare the resulting surface Pourbaix diagrams to provide a demonstration of the framework. Finally, we propose guidelines for selecting the appropriate reference scheme.

Computational Methods

The atomic structures in this work were modelled with the atomic simulation environment [36] and consisted of (3×3×5) unit cells of the fcc (111) facet with the two bottom layers fixed. All DFT calculations were performed with the grid‐based projector‐augmented wave (GPAW) [37] package, using the projector‐augmented wave method, the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (RPBE) [38] exchange and correlation functional and the wavefunctions were expanded in plane waves. All atomic structures were relaxed until the max force in any of the atoms in the structure was below 0.05 eV/Å.

For the adsorption energy calculations, the k‐space sampling was 3×3×1 and the plane wave cutoff energy was set to 400 eV. For the gas phase references, the CO2, Cl2, H2, and H2O molecules were used.

For the bulk structures of M‐bcc, MCl, M2O, M2O2, and MOH (M: Li, Na, K), the structures were relaxed with a plane wave cutoff of 800 eV. It should be noted that especially the oxides, peroxides and hydroxides required the increased cutoff to relax properly. For the bulk energies that were required in the reference scheme that will be introduced in the Results and Discussion section the relaxed geometry of the structure with the 800 eV plane wave cutoff was used but a static calculation was performed with 400 eV for the bulk energies to be comparable to the surface‐adsorbate systems.

For the energies used in Figure 6, all the bulk structures and gas phase molecules were calculated using 800 eV plane wave cutoff. For the same figure, zero‐point energy (ZPE) corrections were applied for Cl2(g), H2(g), and H2O(g) that were found from DFT calculations. The ZPEs for the bulk structures were considered to be 0.

Bader Charge analysis was performed with the algorithm from the Henkelman Group [40].

Results and Discussion

The Computational Cation Electrode

Fundamentally, a true physical observable should be independent of the choice of reference. In practice, however, absolute adsorption energies—and therefore the predicted stability of competing surface species—can shift substantially depending on the reference. Selecting the appropriate reference is thus essential for obtaining not only internally consistent but also quantitatively meaningful absolute energetics.

In electrochemical modeling, a central challenge is how to reference ionic species such as K+ or H+, which cannot directly be simulated in periodic DFT calculations. A widely adopted strategy is the CHE, which provides a consistent potential‐dependent reference for protons and electrons. Following that framework, as mentioned in the Introduction, Resasco et al. proposed in 2017 a cation reference based on the bcc bulk phase of the AM [17]. While this scheme is simple and intuitive, it has a fundamental limitation: surface cations or outer Helmholtz plane ions are compared to a metallic bulk state, even though the relevant species are ionic. The latter limitation stems from the incomplete cancellation of errors in DFT between species with dissimilar bonding nature [41, 42]. To address this limitation, we propose a modification to the framework of Resasco et al. where the alkali atom is taken from bulk ionic compounds—oxides, peroxide, and chlorides—instead of the metallic bcc phase. These ionic references better resemble the electronic environment of alkali cations in the electrochemical interface and reduce the systematic DFT errors.

Derivation of the CCE

We follow the methodology of Resasco et al. [17] inspired by the computational hydrogen electrode of Nørskov et al. [35].

For the cation‐electron reference, we can start by using the AM phase due to the well‐established experimental standard reduction potential of the AMs. If we take K as an example

(1) K(s) → K+(aq), U°=−2.93 V versus SHE

(2) μK(s)=μK++μe−, at U°=−2.93 V versus SHE

In line with the CHE, where the chemical potential of a proton‐coupled electron transfer can be related to the one of gaseous hydrogen through Equation (3), we derive a similar relation for a cation‐coupled electron transfer

(3) 12μH2=μH++μe−+|e−|(U−U°) , with U°=0 V versus SHE

(4) μK(s)=μK++μe− +|e−|(U−U°) , with U°=−2.93 V versus SHE where U° is the standard reduction potential and μK(s) can be calculated with DFT from the bulk bcc crystal of the alkali. Thus far the reference scheme is the same as the one presented by Resasco.

Following the computational cation electrode, we avoid using the bulk bcc crystal of the alkali, as we argued earlier in this section, and opt to use an intermediate bulk state that more closely resembles the cation in the interface instead. If we select, for example, KCl as the bulk reference

(5) μK(s)+12μCl2(g)→μKCl(s), ΔHform,KCl=−4.523 eV at 0K

So, it follows that

(6) μK(s)=μKCl(s)−12μCl2(g)− ΔHform,KCl

Therefore, when a cation‐coupled electron transfer takes place, its chemical potential can be quantified as

(7) μK++ μe−=(μKCl(s)−12μCl2− ΔHform,KCl)−|e‐|(U−U°) with U°=−2.93 V versus SHE

In Equation (7), the blue chemical potential can be calculated from DFT, while ΔHform,KCl and U° are taken from experimental datasets.

A key advantage of this framework is that it requires only minor computation: beyond standard surface and adsorbate calculations, one needs the DFT energies of a few bulk ionic compounds and gas‐phase molecules.

For the rest of the manuscript we will focus on the first step of CO2RR, i.e. the activation of CO2, which most researchers consider as the rate‐determining step of CO2RR and for which the cations play a crucial role. Besides CO2RR, the computational cation electrode could be used in any other reaction in which the cations are included in the simulation and are not treated as part of the surface. Our model can also work in conjunction with the CHE or newer models based on it like the Computational Multi‐Ion Reference Electrode [45].

Effect of Reference Choice on Adsorption Energetics

With the new computational cation electrode framework established, we next examine how the choice of reference influences the adsorption energetics of key CO2RR intermediates. As summarized in Figure 3, we computed adsorption energies of four CO2RR relevant species: a specifically adsorbed AM atom (*K), an adsorbed CO2 molecule coordinated with an AM atom (*COOK), an adsorbed *COOH and an adsorbed *COOH coordinated with an AM atom (*COOH‐K). Adsorption of bare CO2 was tested, but found to be unstable on all surfaces considered: it could only be stabilized in the presence of a coordinated cation. Additional geometries were tested for *COOK where the AM atom is coadsorbed in the surface next to CO2 that yielded lower adsorption energies by about 0.5 eV, which is not surprising due to both CO2 and K interacting with the surface, but these geometries were deemed unrealistic due to presence of water molecules in the real systems. A specifically adsorbed AM atom might also be a rare occasion [34] but should still serve as an interesting use case for the proposed framework.

Adsorption energetics of COOH, K, COOK, and COOH−K on 4 different fcc(111) surfaces calculated within the computational cation electrode using 5 different reference schemes for the alkali atom energy. On Au and Ag, *COOH preferred an on‐top adsorption site while in Cu and Pd it preferred a bidentate configuration. Some of the geometries can be seen in the images on the right. The non‐K containing species (*COOH) are not affected by the choice of the K‐reference.

The adsorption energies of these four species were evaluated on the fcc(111) facets of: Au, Ag, Cu, and Pd using five different reference states for the alkali cation: the conventional metallic bcc, oxide (M2O), peroxide (M2O2), chloride (MCl), and hydroxide (MOH). A notable feature of the proposed framework is that it isolates the effect of the reference on the cationic contribution to the energetics. Species that do not contain the alkali cation, such as *COOH, remain unaffected by the choice of reference, while those that involve K+ (*K, *COOK, and *COOH‐K) can shift significantly as observed in Figure 3.

The results in Figure 3 reveal a markedly more favorable adsorption of the ‐K‐containing species when going from the conventional metallic bcc reference to any of the ionic‐bonded alkali compound references. The trend in adsorption favorability follows

bcc < oxide < hydroxide≈peroxide ≈chloride

Switching from bcc to the chloride reference can change the adsorption energy by up to 0.75 eV, a substantial offset for surface thermodynamics.

On Au, Ag, and Cu, the conversion from activated *CO2 (stabilized by K) to *COOH‐K incurs a small energy penalty, whereas on Pd the same transition is slightly exergonic. Figure 3 shows the adsorption energies at 0 V versus SHE, where *COOH is the most stable intermediate on all four surfaces. However, the stability ordering is sensitive to both pH and applied potential.

The adsorption of *COOH (*COOH‐K) can be treated as a proton–electron transfer reaction starting from a CO2 (or K‐coordinated CO2) molecule. Consequently, its adsorption energy depends on both the applied potential and pH, that change the chemical potential of the electrons and protons, respectively.

These results demonstrate that beyond simply changing the absolute adsorption energies of K‐containing species, the choice of reference can exert an even stronger influence by altering their energy relative to noncationic intermediates such as *COOH. This selective influence moves the stability crossover point, while if all adsorbates were equally influenced by the reference choice, the surface Pourbaix diagrams would remain qualitatively similar across all references.

The pronounced shifts in adsorption energetics and pH‐dependent stability (Figures 3 and 4) highlight that the reference choice does more than simply offset energies—it fundamentally alters the relative stability of cation‐stabilized intermediates. To understand the origin of these differences, it is necessary to examine how well each reference state represents the actual electronic environment of the cation at the interface. In particular, the metallic bcc reference is chemically quite unlike the partially charged cationic species bound at the electrode surface. We therefore turn to a more detailed analysis of the DFT description of metallic versus ionic bonding. This allows us to rationalize why ionic‐bonded bulk compounds provide a more consistent reference for modeling interfacial cations.

Choosing a Reference

So far, we have shown that the choice of reference state within the computational cation electrode framework strongly influences the calculated adsorption energetics and even alters qualitative trends in the surface Pourbaix diagrams. A natural question is why certain reference states—such as the oxides, peroxides, or chlorides—predict more favorable adsorption for the relevant species compared to the conventional bcc reference.

Our initial concern was that the metallic bcc phase is an unrealistic representation of the cationic species present at the electrochemical interface: in the bcc bulk, the alkali atom forms metallic bonds and is essentially uncharged, while in the interface, the alkali species behave as partially charged cations. To test whether more ionic references better represent the interfacial charge state, we performed Bader charge analyses for both bulk reference phases and the adsorbed species.

The results for potassium are shown in Figure 5a. The Bader analyses reveal that the potassium atom adsorbed on the surface carries a partial charge that is much closer to that of K in bulk KCl than in metallic K. In other words, ionic‐bonded reference states better reflect the actual interfacial cation.

(a) Bader charges of *K, *COOK, *COOH on Au111 (top row) and of bulk K(bcc), K2O, K2O2, KCl, and KOH (bottom row). The numbers next to the atoms represent the calculated charge. The K, Au, O, C, H, and Cl atoms are represented by purple, gold, red, grey, white, and green spheres, respectively. (b) Effect of the absolute Bader charge difference (|qAMref−qAMinterface|) between the alkali in the bulk reference and the interface on the adsorption energy when compared to the conventional bcc reference. The energy differences are the same between all studied adsorbed species and surfaces.

Interestingly, as demonstrated in Figure 5b, the difference in Bader charge between the reference and the interfacial species correlates with the difference in adsorption energies computed using the reference, i.e., the larger the mismatch in Bader charge, the less favorable the calculated adsorption energy. This suggests that the reference error has a clear physical origin.

Beyond the charge representation, we also examined whether the chosen exchange‐correlation (xc) functional and DFT setup itself introduces a systematic bias between metallic and ionic bonding. To investigate this concern, we considered six reactions involving three AM (Li, Na, K) chosen to probe either (i) transitions from metallic bonding to ionic bonding or (ii) reactions involving only ionic compounds and compared them to experimental values [39]. To test our hypothesis, we introduce an error term, εion, that will be added for each AM in the reaction, to all reactions that involve a metallic to ionic transition. The six reactions are

(r1) 2M(s)+ O2(g)→M2O2(s)+2εion

(r2) 2M(s)+12O2(g)→M2O(s)+2εion

(r3) 2M(s)+Cl2(g)→2MCl(s) +2εion

(r4) M2O(s)+ Cl2(g)→2MCl(s)+12O2(g)

(r5) 2MCl(s)+ O2(g)→M2O2(s)+Cl2(g)

(r6) M2O(s)+12O2(g)→M2O2(s)

In Figure 6, we plot the results for the different reactions and the different AMs. Again, to get the O2(g)DFT energy, the ORR was used, ΔEO2=2ΔEH2O−2ΔEH2+4.92eV. As shown in Figure 6a, where the εionterm was set to 0—i.e., no correction applied—reactions that convert metallic to alkali atoms into ionic compounds (Equations (r1)–(r3)) exhibit the largest deviation from experiment, whereas reactions involving only ionic compounds (Equations (r4)–(r6)) show smaller discrepancies. This observation can be attributed to a more complete cancellation of errors of DFT due to the similarity between the ionic compounds.

To capture this systematic offset, we can optimize the εionin Equations (r1)–(r3) in a way that it minimizes the deviation from experiments. As shown in Figure 6b, this correction largely removes the systematic shift and appears to be similar across the different alkali atoms. We additionally evaluated the statistical uncertainty using the BEE [46] for these DFT calculations to check whether there is a change in the calculated error for the different reactions. While the error bars show some variation between the reaction types, the origin of this variation is not entirely attributable to the difference in ionic versus metallic bonding.

In the analysis above we have assumed the same ionic character for M2O(s), M2O2(s), and MCl(s) due to their significant deviation from M(s). The correction could also be added to reactions Equations (r4)–(r6) as well if multiplied by an ionicity degree since it has been shown to affect the thermodynamics of such compounds [47] but the correction itself is not important for the computational cation electrode and is mainly used to reject the alkali‐metal as a viable reference for our scheme.

For completeness, the same analysis was attempted for Cs, but the lack of reliable experimental references—particularly for Cs2O2—prevented a robust comparison. Interestingly, repeating the analysis with other GGA functionals shows that the systematic error in reactions Equations (r1)–(r3) persists for PBE but is mitigated by using BEEF‐vdW (Figure S1). The latter is a GGA functional that has been constructed in such a way so that it minimizes the error from experiments for many compounds, so it is not that surprising that it predicts formation energies better.

Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced the computational cation electrode, a reference framework that enables a consistent treatment of AM cations in atomistic simulations of electrochemical reactions. By providing a reference state that reflects the cationic nature of AM species at the electrochemical interface, the corrected computational electrode allows for more realistic and physically meaningful adsorption energetics.

We demonstrated the impact of reference choice on adsorption energies of CO2RR intermediates on Ag, Au, Cu, and Pd surfaces. Even though the calculations were performed without implicit or explicit solvation, the reference framework itself can seamlessly be applied to both continuum‐solvation and explicit solvent atomistic calculations.

Our analysis of alternative reference states—based on metallic (bcc) versus ionic‐bonded bulk phases (oxide, peroxides, and chlorides)—revealed two main insights. First, the energetic trends correlate with the similarity of the Bader charge of the reference phase to that of the cation in the interface: the more similar the charge, the more favorable the calculated adsorption energies. Second, within the tested DFT‐RPBE framework, there is evidence of a systematic error when transitioning between metallic and ionic bonding, which can be mitigated by using ionic reference phases. These findings highlight that the choice of reference is not merely a computational detail but can qualitatively alter predicted adsorbate stabilities and Pourbaix diagrams and should therefore be a conscious choice based on the nature of the adsorbate species.

An important next step would be to benchmark the computational cation electrode or equivalent computational electrode setups against experimentally well‐characterized systems, like Cu(111) surfaces, which have been widely used for CO2RR. This could be achieved by comparing the computed theoretical overpotentials to experimental values but such a comparison would first require the refining of the other aspects of the computational cation electrode beside the cation reference, like the treatment of solvation and local electric field effects. Such an extensive study was beyond the scope of the current work, though we expect that it can guide future investigations.

The computational cation electrode complements the CHE by providing a standardized and transferable protocol for treating alkali cations, which play a key role in CO2RR and potentially other electrochemical reactions. We anticipate that this approach will not only improve the accuracy and reproducibility of atomistic studies of cation‐dependent electrochemical processes but also facilitate direct comparisons across theory and experiment.
