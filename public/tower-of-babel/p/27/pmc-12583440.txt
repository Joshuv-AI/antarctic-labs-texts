Kinematic modeling of musculoskeletal systems considering muscle inertia for monoarticular muscles
Authors: A. Parsian, M. H. Korayem, F. Allahverdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding human musculoskeletal dynamics is the key to further advancement in both biomedical engineering and humanoid robotics. Numerical models today have certain setbacks that hinder the accuracy of analyses, especially in orthopedic surgery and robotic design. A kinematic behavior-based analytical model of musculoskeletal systems is presented that explores muscle inertia and center of mass (COM) variations across six configurations and modes. This model incorporates long and asymmetric tendons, along with a dynamically reducing joint radius relative to the joint angle. A novel 2D wrapping radius method extends the radius by up to 50% compared to the Garner and Pandy method for monoarticular muscles. Additionally, a new analytical method for calculating muscle contraction speed, the velocity of the COM of muscle-tendon systems, and joint positions is proposed. An algorithm for calculating system velocity and acceleration is introduced based on this method. MATLAB simulations compared the model’s accuracy against OpenSim results, showing consistency at shorter angles and demonstrating that the algorithm remains reliable at wider angles, unlike the Garner and Pandy algorithm. Results highlight that COM and dynamic tendon length variations can be incorporated into the suggested algorithm, which is essential for biomechanical analysis and the development of therapeutic and robotic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22338-y.

Introduction

With advancements in biomedical engineering and humanoid robots, the imperative to enhance the understanding and accuracy of the musculoskeletal systems is constantly evolving. Additionally, each human has unique characteristics, and every injury presents new complexities within the human body1. The error of orthopedic surgeries can affect the future health of both patients2,3 and surgeons4. The necessity for improved modeling and the utilization of more comprehensive, analytical, and transparent models for experimentation and research, while remaining cost-effective and prioritizing human safety, has never been more critical. Given that muscles account for 40% of the human body mass5, the development of more precise models related to human anatomy, including the effects of muscle inertia and the positions of each limb’s COM, facilitates a deeper understanding of human movement, balance, and musculoskeletal behavior6–8. The expansion of humanoid robots9–11 and soft actuators12–14 underscores the increasing utilization of these actuators in robotics. Given that muscles (and actuators) contribute significantly to the musculoskeletal system mass, their length and position changes can substantially impact stability. Due to the muscle’s viscoelastic characteristics15, one crucial factor influencing overall muscle forces is the variation in muscle length16,17 and its contraction speed18,19. Numerical models, such as those implemented in OpenSim, provide estimations of these changes20. In the realm of orthopedic biomechanics, minor surgical errors can have profound effects during the post-surgery period and potentially lead to future injuries and complications resulting from alterations in human gait due to new muscle activation21.

Kinematic modeling is essential for calculating the dynamics of the musculoskeletal systems20 and existing stability models are often oversimplified and fail to reflect reality accurately. Limbs are frequently treated as rigid links with unchanging centers of mass, disregarding the dynamic changes that occur with muscle movements7,22,23, or the muscle-tendon (MT) systems only move in an over-constrained path24–26. As far as we know the effect of muscles movements and COM has not been included as it could be. Muscle paths can vary with joint angle changes, and The MT system may either maintain contact with the joint surface, forming a curved path, or avoid wrapping around the joint, taking on a straight-line trajectory. Furthermore, numerical programs are limited in their ability to modify the length of MT (details like asymmetrical tendon length for the origin and end insertions of a certain muscle), leading to inaccuracies in determining the final positions of muscles, limbs, and the overall center of the mass. These inaccuracies result in significantly less accurate determinations and simulations8, and this places imperative calls for exact measurements and simulations in the stability analysis. Limitation should also be welcome for numerical tools and software’s, but software-based solutions have inherent limitations. For instance, some of them like OpenSim, do not take into consideration the inertia of muscles. Although software like AnyBody provides a highly customizable framework with an advanced object-wrapping method27, it remains primarily a numerical tool. While effective, this does not undermine the value of analytical models. Analytical models allow a lot more freedom and ease to correct with the aim of arriving at the higher accuracy. Analytical models remain indispensable for generating high-fidelity synthetic data to train numerical and data-driven frameworks. However, virtually most existing musculoskeletal models treat musculotendon units as massless and assign each limb segment a fixed center of mass, neglecting how muscle shape and mass distribution shift with joint angle. This simplification leads to errors in predicted joint torques, balance, and gait. By explicitly modeling individual muscle mass and allowing the composite center of mass to vary with movement, our approach yields more accurate dynamic load and stability estimates, reducing simulation-to-reality failures and improving overall cost-effectiveness.

This study aims to propose an adaptable analytical approach that addresses these gaps, offering a robust framework for both biomechanical analysis and robotic applications. While AI can come to be of aid in the identification of various properties which will be discussed, there are several limitations and problems with it8,28. As mentioned before, every individual’s physical characteristic like bone lengths, tendon lengths, muscle cross sections and the body parts weights is unique, making it challenging to develop general algorithms that are globally accurate and effective for all humans. Also, by employing more accurate model simulations, it becomes possible to discern which errors are less detrimental, thereby enhancing surgical outcomes.

The study will principally focus on the analysis of the kinematics of the human body, specifically examining changes in the length of the muscle-tendon system that influence body movement. In this regard, it considers asymmetric and long tendons, looking at different wrapping modes of the MT systems in the event of soft tissue involvement. It does also address the reduction of the radius of joints at wider angles or joints. For the exact calculation of the length and speed of muscle contractions and the COM of the MT systems, it is taken into consideration whether the muscle interacts with the joint or not, whether the muscle wraps around the joint reel or not, and to what extent it covers the joint reel. Also taken into account is the rate of contraction and expansion of the muscles in various positions and motions. A way of combining all these different conditions into one equation is suggested. Described is the process of obtaining analytically the derivatives which will be required from this equation for use in the dynamics of the system. Section 2 provides the model description in detail by stating those possibilities which occur by changing the joint angles. It has listed assumptions taken in modeling for tendons, radius of joints, and systematization done for numbering the MT systems in order to make the equations general. It then introduces the mathematical conditions of each muscle configuration along with the presentation of an analytical modeling using switch functions in order to decide on the activation of equations based on specific events. The paper describes a means of calculating MT system lengths and velocities, including a new methodology for differentiating models through the use of switch functions. In Sect. 3, forward kinematics analyzes the local COM of MT systems relative to the joints, followed by the kinematics of joints and bones, combining these elements to address global COM kinematics. Finally, in Sect. 4, simulation results are compared with OpenSim, evaluating the new method for differentiating MT system lengths against traditional numerical techniques. The speed of contraction is derived from length figures, and the COM results are validated through both OpenSim and analytical methods.

Model description

In this paper, various geometric models representing the potential shapes and configurations of muscles in relation to their corresponding joints is explored. Switch functions to activate specific equations under certain conditions, ensuring each model accurately reflects muscle behavior are introduced. It is viewed as six distinct conditions of the muscle shape, each corresponding to different geometric states muscles can assume during movement. These switch functions are fundamental since after lengthening, muscles can change in shape and switch the conditions and the equations. Since the geometry of a muscle is determined by the joint angle it acts on, switch functions also depend both on joint angles and time. After defining the geometric models, the forward kinematics for each muscle shape with respect to the joint angle is derived. To have these equations active only when the respective conditions were satisfied, the kinematic equations were multiplied by their switch functions. This way, the forward kinematics for each muscle and the bones it acts on can be accurately modeled by this methodology. This approach would be more interactive in understanding the complex interplay between geometry, joint angles, and time to give more reasonable predictions of musculoskeletal behavior.

Configuration 1: If the end tendon lengthens beyond the joint curve, the entire muscle part of the system positions adjacent to the origin bone (Fig. 2-b).

Configuration 2: If the end tendon is short enough, the muscle may cover a section of the joint curve. This can also occur if the joint angle widens, causing the muscle to lengthen sufficiently to slide over the joint curve. In this scenario, the MT system is divided into a straight part that does not slide on the joint and a curved part that does (Fig. 2-c).

Configuration 3: When the origin and end tendons are short enough, the muscle slides on the joint curve and extends into the end part of the system. This configuration results in the MT system being split into three parts: the initial straight part, the curved part on the joint, and the final straight part (Fig. 2-d).

Configuration 4: Similar to the second configuration, but with a longer origin tendon. Here, the muscle covers a section of the joint curve, with the main mass of the system split into a straight section and a curved section on the joint (Fig. 2-e).

Configuration 5: Opposite to the first configuration, where the end tendon is very short, causing the muscle to remain predominantly in the origin region (Fig. 2-f).

Each of the configurations and shapes can occur in the human body and some shapes can transform into each other because of joint movement and the increase of joint angle. The shape of the muscle is thus dependent on the corresponding angle of the joint it acts upon, as well as the lengths of the tendons.

System assumptions

Given the complexities inherent in human anatomy and the objective of developing generalized equations for the model, several assumptions have been made. These assumptions aim to simplify the system without compromising the critical aspects that significantly impact human kinesiology. By focusing on the most influential factors, it is ensured that the model remains manageable and relevant to the study’s goals. Muscles are assumed to be prismatic, uniform cables with weight. The midpoint of the muscle is considered to be at half of its total length. The MT system is assumed to either move freely or have contact with the joint radius at the corresponding angle without friction. For tendons and joint radius, more complex assumptions are made. It is assumed that the lengths of the origin insertion tendon (the tendon that attaches the muscle to its origin) and the end insertion tendon (the tendon that connects the other head of the muscle to the opposing bone) are constant; however, their lengths can vary independently. This reflects the anatomical reality observed in the human body, where tendons often have differing lengths, such as in the calf muscles where the origin and insertion tendon lengths are not the same. Tendons may also slide over the joint surface, such as in the quadriceps case, or the muscle may entirely cover the joint, as in the case of the gluteal muscles. These variations lead to multiple possible configurations of the MT system, each influencing the system’s behavior differently. This model explores and accommodates these different configurations to capture the diverse range of MT interactions.

Joint radius

Each joint in the MT system is modeled as a circular reel with a radius that varies for each specific one. The radius is measured from the center of the joint to the center of the muscle cross-section. Given that the cross-section changes with muscle movement, two assumptions are made:

A- Constant radius: In this scenario, the joint radius is considered constant. This constant radius, denoted as R0, is calculated using two MRI images of the individual taken at different angle positions. The first image is captured at the angle where the MT system starts the wrapping mode and the second at the maximum angle the joint can achieve the wrapping mode. By measuring the joint radius from both ends and averaging them, R0is estimated the constant radius.

B- Variable radius: Here, the joint radius is treated as a variable. After calculating R0 a ∆R is introduced as a function of the angle to account for changes in soft tissue, including the muscle cross-section. ∆R represents the range of changes. Each configuration is created when its corresponding mathematical equation is satisfied, allowing creation of functions that switch between configurations according to needs. Forward kinematics

To calculate the forward kinematics of the system, the local forward kinematics of the muscles should first be determined in relation to the corresponding angles. Then the forward kinematics of bones should be calculated and using the vectors of both bones and local vectors of muscles, the final forward kinematics of the system is calculated.

Forward kinematics of muscles

In this section, the forward kinematics for each muscle shape using the geometric configurations and switch functions discussed previously is derived. The forward kinematics process involves determining the position of the COM of the MT system based on the joint angles and muscle lengths for each shape. The reference point for the coordinates is the joint on which the muscle acts and the general coordinates are the angles.

Discussion and conclusion

This study presents an analytical framework for musculoskeletal kinematics that addresses several shortcomings of existing numerical tools. Advantages of this approach include allowing the joint wrap radius to vary dynamically with angle—better reflecting in vivo soft-tissue deformation—whereas traditional pulley models assume a fixed radius. As demonstrated in a 10% radius-reduction test (Fig. 9), this refinement yields a shorter muscle–tendon (MT) path at peak extension compared to constant-radius models, correcting a systematic overestimation that can propagate into errors in simulated muscle speed and force. Equally, the origin and insertion tendon lengths decoupled, accommodating anatomical realities of asymmetrical heads, rather than forcing equal lengths as in most prior studies. Together, these geometric enhancements produce MT lengths and contraction velocities that remain valid even at extreme joint sweeps (–π to π), a range over which some existing implementations “unwrap” or fail (Fig. 7).

Another key advantage is the treatment of the MT center of mass (COM) as a moving quantity rather than as a static point affixed to bone. By deriving closed-form expressions for each configuration—free, partially wrapped, or fully wrapped—our switch-function approach generates continuous, millimeter-level–accurate COM trajectories (Fig. 10). OpenSim and similar platforms rely on fixed markers or simplified estimators, which cannot capture the subtle mass redistributions that occur as a muscle wrap over or detaches from a joint. Since muscle inertia contributes to joint moments and whole-body stability, the variable COM calculations lay critical groundwork for any future force-based or stability analyses.

This framework also brings the transparency and differentiability of analytical models to a field dominated by black-box numerical solvers. Each muscle configuration is represented by its own algebraic formula, and switch functions seamlessly activate or deactivate these formulas based on tendon lengths and joint angles. However, because each new configuration requires its own derived expression, the total number of formulas -and the effort to implement and maintain them- grows rapidly in more complex models, which may constrain scalability. This clarity allows the direct derivation of velocities and accelerations—without resorting to numerical differencing or ad hoc case handling—and it exposes parameter sensitivities (for example, how a slight tendon-length adjustment affects MT path) that standard software conceals. In doing so, this model can serve as a benchmark for tuning or validating more complex simulators (e.g., OpenSim, AnyBody, OpenKnee) and can integrate experiment-guided parameter estimation schemes.

Several limitations remain, pointing to clear opportunities for future enhancement. First, the current formulation is confined to planar, monoarticular muscles; extension to three-dimensional geometries and inclusion of biarticular configurations would capture a broader spectrum of human movement but would also require derivation and management of a vastly greater number of algebraic configurations. In practice, each additional degree of freedom or biarticular pathway introduces new switch functions and closed-form expressions, increasing implementation complexity and potentially compromising computational efficiency and maintainability in full-body simulations. Although this added complexity could be exploited by learning-based methods to generate richer training data, it still imposes a substantial computational overhead, reducing overall simulation speed and scalability. Second, the simplifying assumptions of rigid, constant-length tendons and uniform muscle mass distribution could be relaxed by introducing tendon elasticity and heterogeneous mass profiles to better reflect physiological behavior. Finally, although closed-form solutions for forward kinematics, musculotendon length derivatives, and dynamic COM trajectories have been provided, active muscle force generation and neural activation dynamics are not yet incorporated; integrating these elements will be essential for faithfully modeling coordinated, three-dimensional, multi-joint activities.

Nevertheless, the derivation of these analytic inputs establishes a robust foundation for any subsequent dynamic or clinical application. For example, tendon-transfer surgical planning could be informed by predicted post-operative MT lengths and inertia shifts, thereby optimizing attachment sites to preserve natural gait patterns. In robotic and exoskeleton contexts, real-time adjustment of actuator mass and inertia based on joint-angle changes can be implemented to enhance control precision and energy efficiency. In this way, the proposed framework delivers a transparent, extensible basis for advancing both biomechanical research and biorobotic simulation.
