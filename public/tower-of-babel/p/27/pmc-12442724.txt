Engineering β-sheet morphologies via metal cross-linking and side chain modifications
Authors: Eisuke Tsunekawa, Takahiro Nakama, Makoto Fujita, Tomohisa Sawada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Natural and artificial β-sheet assemblies often form helical structures due to the inherent twisting of peptide strands, typically observable by AFM and TEM but rarely analysed with single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCD) due to structural disorder. This study overcame such challenges by periodically cross-linking peptide strands in β-sheet tapes via metal coordination, creating perfectly ordered helical structures suitable for SCD analysis. Side chain interactions were found to drive helical twisting, and adjusting side chain substituents enabled tuning of the helical morphology, including the formation of double helices. These findings open new possibilities in β-sheet assembly design, advancing peptide engineering.

Introduction

Both natural and artificial β-sheet assemblies often exhibit various helical morphologies on the submicron scale due to the inherent twisting of peptide strands1 or the flexibility of β-sheet tapes.2 These helical structures can typically be observed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM),3 but precise molecular-level structural analysis4 by single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCD) has rarely been applied.5 This is because β-sheets, composed of peptide strands, frequently develop types of disorder6—such as mixed parallel/antiparallel arrangements and/or shearing (i.e., residue slips along the N- to C-terminal backbone direction between adjacent β-strands)1b—that prevent obtaining suitable diffraction data for SCD analysis. In this study, we successfully constructed helical β-sheet tapes completely free of such heterogeneity by cross-linking i and i + 2 peptide strands7 through metal coordination (Fig. 1a). Thanks to this non-disordered structure, we were able to observe infinite twisted β-sheet tapes via SCD analysis. Careful examination of these helical structures revealed that side chain interactions within or between peptide strands induce the helical twist. By modifying side chain substituents, we were able to tune the helical morphology and even generate a double helical β-sheet composed of two β-sheet tapes. While recent advancements in protein de novo design have enabled accurate prediction of α-helix structures,8 the design and structural prediction of β-sheet assemblies remain largely unexplored. This study is thus anticipated to open new avenues in peptide engineering based on β-sheet assemblies.

We recently reported that the coordination of pentapeptide 1a with Ag+ ions leads to the self-assembly of four-stranded β-sheet [Ag2(1a)2]2 (3) with a [2]catenane topology consisting of two Ag2(1a)2 cyclic frameworks (Fig. 2a).9 In the sequence of 1a, the core sequence, –D3pa-Gly-3pa– (where 3pa/D3pa stand for β-(3-pyridyl)-l/d-alanine), contains two coordinating side chains (3-pyridylmethyl groups) oriented in opposite directions, enabling metal coordination to cross-link the i and i + 2 strands. In the crystalline state, the outer two strands (the first and fourth strands) are found to further form amide hydrogen bonds with adjacent four-stranded β-sheets, resulting in planar β-sheet tape structure (3)n. Metal cross-linking from both sides of the β-sheet face, enabled by the incorporation of the d-amino acid residue, isolated β-sheet tapes from lamination, which exhibits potential for efficient helical induction. Here, we aim to investigate the relationship between the structure of the side chains on 1a and the morphology of the resulting β-sheet tapes. Peptides 1b–1f and 2a–2c were synthesised where the side chain structure of the N- and/or C-terminal residues of 1a were replaced with different residues (Fig. 1b, see also Experimental section in the SI and Fig. S1–S18). In contrast to many established pH-responsive β-sheet peptides governed by the interactions of charged side chains,10 we chose non-charged side chains so as not to interfere the metal-crosslinking effect. The morphology of β-sheet tapes generated through Ag+ coordination was studied by SCD analysis (Tables S1–S3 and Fig. S19–S37).

Results and discussion

Peptide 1b with 2-aminobutyric acid (Abu) residues introduced at the N-terminal first residue (R = Et) and AgNTf2 (0.25 μmol each) were mixed in CHCl3/MeOH (1 : 1 (v/v), 50 μL), and slow vapor diffusion of ethyl acetate into this solution yielded colourless block crystals within three weeks (Fig. S19a). SCD analysis revealed that peptide 1b self-assembled into in-register11 antiparallel four-stranded β-sheet [Ag2(1b)2]2 (4) with a catenated topology, similar to 3, in which the i and i + 2 strands are cross-linked by Ag+ coordination (Fig. 2g).

In the crystal structure, four-stranded β-sheet 4 is further assembled into β-sheet tape (4)n through hydrogen bonding of the outer strands, similar to (3)n (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, (4)n displayed a right-handed helical morphology with a helical pitch of 10.4 nm and a diameter of 2.2 nm. This long helical pitch consists of 24 β-strands, with half of them crystallographically independent (Fig. S20). The β-sheet tape was based on an in-register antiparallel β-sheet configuration at the hydrogen bonding sites (Fig. 3a). A top-down view (Fig. S37a) reveals the position of each residue within (4)n in the helical tape, showing that all D3pa side chains are positioned on the outer side of the helix, while all 3pa side chains are oriented inward. The alkyl side chain of the Abu and valine (Val) residues projects outward, contributing to the packing between helices. The C-terminal methyl ester also positions outward.

A superimposed view of each of the four-stranded β-sheet units, comprising flat (3)n and twisted (4)n structures, demonstrated that the twist originates from steric repulsion between the side chains (Fig. 3b). In the case of (4)n, steric repulsion between the side chains of the Abu and D3pa residues causes the peptide structure around the N-terminus to distort more than in (3)n (Fig. 3b, left). This distortion leads to slight structural changes in the metal cross-linking, which propagated to form the helical morphology along the β-sheet axis.

Since it was confirmed that steric repulsion from the side chains of the Abu and D3pa residues induces strand twisting and the emergence of a helical morphology, we attempted complexation with Ag+ using peptide ligands 1c–1e, in which the Abu residue was replaced with bulkier Val, isoleucine (Ile), and tert-leucine (Tle) residues, respectively, to observe changes in the helical morphology. SCD analysis of the crystal formed through complexation of Ag+ ions with ligand 1c revealed the formation of a helical β-sheet tape structure, (Ag·1c)n ((5)n), similar to (4)n (Fig. S21). Unexpectedly, it was found that this helical tape (5)n forms a double helix, [(5)n]2, by intertwining of two tapes (Fig. 2c). Although the helical structure of (5)n resembled that of (4)n, the metal–peptide binding pattern differed. The cross-linking between the i and i + 2 strands did not give a discrete [2]catenane framework; instead, i + 2 linked with i + 4 through another 3-pyridylmethyl group within the strand, forming a coordination polymer (Fig. 2h). This change to more uniform cross-linking mode enabled packing into the duplex where interactions between two tapes consisted primarily of packing of D3pa residues and counter anions. In addition, the Boc group in (5)n is oriented deeper towards the interior of the double helix than that in (4)n (Fig. S37b). The helix of (5)n twists in a right-handed direction, with a pitch and diameter of 10.2 nm and 2.6 nm, respectively, and is slightly more tightly wound compared to (4)n. Similar double helical topology, [(Ag·1d)n]2 ([(6)n]2, Fig. S38a and b), was observed in the Ag+ complex of 1d.

Ligand 1c′, with a dodecyl group at the C-terminus of 1c, formed a single helical tape (5′)n similar to (4)n (Fig. 3c and S39). This structure is suggestive. First, (5′)n is composed of a catenane unit, supporting the fact that the structures of the single and double helical tape(s) are generated from catenaned and polymeric modes, respectively (Fig. S40). Furthermore, Ag3(1c′)4 unit, which lacked one Ag+ ion, co-existed in the structure of (5′)n (Fig. S39c and d). Thus, as a possible mechanism for the β-sheet tape formation, three β-strands first assemble at the nucleation step, and when the fourth β-strand join them, either pathway of the catenated or polymeric mode is selected for propagation (Fig. S41).

For ligand 1c, we obtained a single crystal without metal complexation. In this case, the observed structure was a flat β-sheet with mixed orientation (Fig. S29e and f). This fact suggests that the steric repulsion of the side chains leads to the helix induction when the metal cross-linking suppresses strands' shearing and aligns the strands' orientations.

To investigate the effect of the fifth residue and to achieve better crystal quality for the following investigation, the C-terminal Val in 1c was replaced with Tle (2a). Complexation with AgNTf2 also showed double helical structure [(7)n]2 (Fig. 2d), with the same manner of the β-sheet pattern and metal–peptide linkages as [(5)n]2 (Fig. S29c and S38c). The crystal structure of [(7)n]2 was a relatively squashed duplex (helical pitch: 9.0 nm, diameter: 3.2 nm) with larger inner cavity (Fig. S37d). This result indicates that the introduction of Tle at the fifth residue does not significantly affect the metal–peptide linkage or the duplex formation. Overlaying each strand in double helices [(5)n]2 and [(7)n]2 showed only slight differences in twist (Fig. S42), and such slight differences were found to alter the helical parameters of pitch and width of β-sheet tapes. Inside [(7)n]2, even less inter-helical contacts existed those in [(5)n]2 (Fig. S43). This indicates the double helix formation was prompted by packing effects rather than attractive interactions as in DNA.

Regarding these insights, peptide 2b was used instead of 1e, since the diffraction intensity of the obtained crystal from 1e was insufficient. In this case, the steric hindrance between residues became too large, making it difficult to adopt a β-sheet tape aggregation. Instead, complex helical structure (Ag·2b)n ((8)n) containing bulged12 dimer unit (2b)2, is generated (Fig. 4), in which Ag+ ions are helically surrounded by the β-bulged dimer units (Fig. 2e), forming a one-dimensional chain, (Ag+)n (further structural details are described in the SI and Fig. S44).

Close up views of amide hydrogen-bonding pattern (yellow dashed lines) in (8)n.

Finally, we show the results of complex formation with Ag+ for ligands 1f and 2c, in which a threonine (Thr) residue was introduced at the N-terminal first residue. Such a hydrophilic residue was chosen for wider scope of peptides without precluding the metal cross-linking. Unfortunately, no crystal suitable for SCD analysis was obtained from 1f, but from 2c, a left-handed single helical structure, (Ag·2c)n ((9)n), opposite to (4)n–(7)n, was observed (Fig. 2f). The helical pitch measures 5.2 nm, which is half the pitch length of (4)n or (5)n, and it is composed of 16 peptide strands. Crystallographically, only two independent peptide strands exist, making it a more uniform helical structure (Fig. S25). The cross-linking mode of the metal–peptide complex differs significantly from that of helical β-sheet tapes (4)n–(7)n, with distinct binding modes observed on the inner and outer sides of the helix (Fig. 2i). On the outer side, adjacent i and i + 1 strands are cross-linked, while on the inner side, i and i + 3 strands are cross-linked, forming a β-sheet helical tape. Conclusions

In summary, through the folding and assembly of pentapeptides with metal ions,13 we successfully constructed helical β-sheet tapes with precise single-crystal structures. The β-sheet tape cross-linked with Ag+ ions at the core sequence –D3pa-Gly-3pa–adopted four types of helical assemblies depending on the bulkiness and interactions of the terminal side chains. Generally, even for synthetic helical structures constructed with reversible bonds, obtaining structures with atomic precision remains difficult.5,14 Therefore, β-sheet tapes, with their diverse structural modes and intense aggregation characteristics, are extremely challenging targets for precise construction to a defined helical structure at the molecular level. Decades of studies in β-sheets from the viewpoint of molecular structures also report various factors contributing to the twisting of β-sheets,3 making it difficult to design from scratch. However, by introducing metal–peptide cross-linking within a simple system using short β-sheet-forming peptides, we were able to reduce the complexity to a simple factor, steric effects of aliphatic side chains. While many β-sheet-based helical structures exhibit pronounced polymorphism in fibrillisation,15 the process of folding and assembly based on reversible metal coordination bonding and the flexibility of short peptides produced a thermodynamically stable structure, enabling single-crystal formation. Crystallisation of short β-sheet-forming peptides often results in planar β-sheets due to the lamination of β-sheet layers.16 However, such lamination was prevented for metal cross-linked β-sheets and facilitated helical induction in high efficiency. In addition, incorporation of a d-residue may have another effect to induce a bent conformation, as seen in β-sheet-forming l/d-alternating cyclic peptides.17 Careful examination of the d-residue at different positions is needed in the future. We believe that such atomic-level structure analysis contributes to the rational understanding and design of β-sheet helical morphologies.
