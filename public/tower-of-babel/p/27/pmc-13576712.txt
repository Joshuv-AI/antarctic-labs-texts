Low-Dose Radiation Therapy (LDRT) for Osteoarthritis: A Retrospective Study of 94 Patients and 136 Joints Treated
Authors: Nayan Shah, Rahul Bhandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis retrospective study aims to examine the efficacy of low-dose radiation therapy (LDRT) delivered via a linear accelerator in patients with osteoarthritis, measuring pain relief across various joints.Methods and MaterialsA total of 94 patients and a total of 136 affected joints were treated with LDRT between January 2023 and May 2025 at a radiation therapy institute. Each patient received 6 treatments of 0.5 Gy every other day, totaling 3 Gy over the course of 2 weeks. Pain was documented using the numeric rating scale (NRS) before treatment (preradiation therapy) and at follow-up visits approximately 12 weeks posttreatment, with the von Pannewitz score also assessed at follow-up.ResultsThe mean age of patients was 74.5 years, with 38% male and 62% female patients. The treated joints included 61 hands (45%), 46 knees (34%), 15 shoulders (11%), 8 hips (6%), and 6 feet (4%). The mean pain score before treatment was 7.30 ± 1.90, while after treatment it dropped to 2.58 ± 3.09 on the NRS scale (P < .0001). Using a minimal clinically important difference threshold of a ≥2-point reduction in NRS, 79% of patients achieved clinically meaningful pain improvement following LDRT. Age (P = .23), sex (P = .20), and joint size (P = .35) were not found to significantly affect treatment outcomes. Most patients received a single course of LDRT treatment, with only 3.7% of patients receiving a second treatment course.ConclusionsThe study indicates that LDRT may be a safe, noninvasive option for treating osteoarthritis-related pain and improving joint function. Well-powered randomized controlled trials, especially with an American cohort, are required in the future to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis and a major cause of disability worldwide.1 The most common joints affected by OA are the knees, hips, hands, spine, ankle, feet, and shoulders.2 Globally, OA affected 595 million people in 2020, representing 7.6% of the world's population.1 In the United States alone, OA affects 32.5 million people, with an annual economic burden of $136 billion in health care costs.3 Because OA is a degenerative joint disease, its prevalence and associated morbidity and mortality are likely to grow as the life expectancy and other key risk factors such as obesity and physical inactivity continue to rise worldwide.4 Additionally, declining estrogen level, an important risk factor for OA, is associated with increased joint degradation and a higher incidence of OA in women as they age.5

Traditional management of OA in the United States has progressed from noninvasive conservative therapies to invasive treatment. Although noninvasive treatment options for OA, such as analgesics and physical therapy, are routinely used, they have minimal efficacy.6 On the other hand, more invasive treatments like corticosteroid injections and orthopedic surgery have been associated with significant morbidity and costs.7 Additionally, recent studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that corticosteroid injections provide only limited and short-term symptomatic benefit, with little evidence of long-term efficacy in OA management.8,9

Low-dose radiation therapy (LDRT) has been investigated as a low-cost, noninvasive treatment for musculoskeletal disorders, including OA, due to its anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects.10 The therapeutic effect of LDRT in OA appears to derive from modulation of inflammation rather than direct cytotoxicity. Experimental data suggest that doses <1.0 Gy per fraction alter immune activity and vascular responses in ways that collectively promote resolution of periarticular and synovial inflammation.11, 12, 13 Macrophages represent a central target. Low doses reduce inducible nitric oxide synthase activity, lowering nitric oxide production, and can shift polarization away from a proinflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory, tissue-repair M2 state.14, 15, 16

Calabrese and Kozumbo17 found novel mechanistic insights linking a single LDRT treatment (0.5-1.0 Gy) to the activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related transcription factor (Nrf2), which drives immunological cells to the M2 state.17 Under basal conditions, Nrf2 levels are modulated by Kelch-like erythroid cell-derived homology-associated protein1 (Keap1) that binds Nrf2 and targets it for proteasomal degradation. Low-dose ionizing radiation oxidizes Keap1, allowing the accumulation and translocation of Nrf2 to the nucleus, where it binds with the antioxidant response element. This interaction enables Nrf2 to regulate inflammation through 2 complementary mechanisms: a redox-dependent pathway that promotes macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, and a redox-independent pathway that directly suppresses proinflammatory cytokine expression.18

Endothelial cells are also affected by LDRT, with adhesion molecule expression being depressed and leucocyte trafficking reduced at doses between 0.5 and 1.0 Gy.19 This may inhibit immune cell infiltration into periarticular tissues and break the cycle of chronic synovitis.20 Additional effects include reduced secretion of tumor necrosis factor alpha, Interleukin-6, and similar cytokines from irradiated leukocytes, and selective apoptosis of activated immune subsets and synovial fibroblasts.21,22

Thus, LDRT can affect several pathways involved in the local inflammatory process, modulating the joint microenvironment through convergent effects on macrophages, endothelium, cytokine networks, and redox signaling pathways. By affecting the periarticular and synovial milieu, LDRT can achieve clinically relevant improvements in pain and function.23,24

Although LDRT was more common in the United States for treating benign conditions in the mid-1900s, its use declined due to several reasons, including cultural and scientific reluctance to use radiation for nonmalignant conditions, as well as the rise of effective pharmaceuticals.25 In contrast, LDRT is widely practiced in Europe, especially in Germany, where over one-third of all radiation therapy (RT) treatments are for benign diseases, including over 15,000 patients with OA.10,26 Numerous European retrospective studies have found significant evidence in favor of the efficacy of LDRT.10

However, caution is warranted when extrapolating European LDRT results to the American population. The American patient population differs significantly from that of Europe in terms of diet, obesity rates, physical activity levels, and overall metabolic health.27

Such differences underscore the need to conduct LDRT research with American patient cohorts.

To our knowledge, this study is the second modern American retrospective study, as well as the largest (by patient cohort) American study conducted to evaluate the efficacy of LDRT on patients with OA. Thus, this study aims to assess a wide range of patient responses to LDRT across various joints (hands, knees, shoulders, feet, and hips) in a cohort of American patients with OA. As a secondary aim, this study investigates whether the effectiveness of LDRT varies among patients based on age, sex, or joint size.

Methods and Materials

Ethics

In accordance with institutional policy for retrospective chart reviews, only existing clinical records were evaluated, without any direct patient contact or intervention. The requirement for informed consent was waived based on the minimal-risk nature of the study, impracticability of obtaining consent for retrospective data, and the determination that the research posed no adverse effect on the rights or welfare of patients. All data were deidentified before analysis and handled in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act with institutional standards to ensure patient privacy, data protection, and confidentiality.

Patient inclusion criteria

A chart review of patients with OA treated at a single RT institute between January 2023 and May 2025 was conducted. Patients were considered eligible for inclusion if they had a clinical diagnosis of OA that was confirmed by imaging, using either plain radiography or magnetic resonance imaging; imaging confirmation was required for study inclusion. All included patients had OA in peripheral, weight-bearing, or functional extremity joints (hips, knees, shoulders, feet, and hands). Patients with spinal OA were excluded due to an absence of robust evidence supporting both the safety and efficacy of LDRT in the spinal region.

All patients were symptomatic with persistent pain despite a minimum of 3 months of prior conventional treatments such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, analgesics, physical therapy, intra-articular injections, or lifestyle adjustments. For patients with prior intra-articular corticosteroid injections, a minimum 6-week washout period was required before initiation of LDRT. Patients were not required to discontinue concomitant treatments, including pain medications or physical therapy, during LDRT.

Specific data on patient treatments other than LDRT (both prior and concomitant treatments) were not systematically collected and analyzed as part of this study. OA severity was not characterized using established grading systems such as the Kellgren–Lawrence scale. Patients were excluded from this study if they (1) were younger than 50 years, (2) were pregnant, (3) had prior joint replacement at the intended site, (4) had active malignancy within the prospective treatment field, or (5) had any other contraindication to RT.

Treatment protocol

All patients were treated with LDRT using a Varian VitalBeam linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems). The standard treatment comprised 3.0 Gy in 6 fractions (0.5 Gy per fraction) administered every other day over 2 to 3 weeks. The dosage regimen was selected based on German Society for Radiation Oncology recommendations for fractionation schemes at 0.5 Gy or 1.0 Gy for benign musculoskeletal conditions. Comparative clinical evidence from European retrospective studies suggests similar pain response rates observed between lower-dose (3.0 Gy) and higher-dose (6.0 Gy) regimens. In a large randomized prospective trial, Ott et al28 found no significant effect in long-term pain response between the 0.5 Gy and 1.0 Gy arms. Thus, patient dosage was limited to 3.0 Gy (0.5 Gy) for radiation protection reasons.

A 3-dimensional conformal technique was employed, most commonly using parallel-opposed anterior-posterior/posterior-anterior or lateral field arrangements. Photon energies of 6 to 10 MV were selected according to patient body habitus and target depth. For superficial or small-joint disease, a 0.5 to 1 cm bolus was applied to optimize dose deposition within periarticular tissues. Protection of adjacent radiosensitive organs was achieved with tailored shielding and individualized treatment planning. All plans were generated with the intent to minimize hotspots using standard dosimetry practices; when feasible, field-in-field techniques were used to improve dose uniformity within the constraints of parallel-opposed 3-dimensional conformal arrangements. Planning goals were consistently achieved with no protocol deviations made for any case within the study.

Patient setup was adapted to the anatomic site under treatment. Lower extremity joints were typically positioned supine, with custom-molded cushions or vacuum devices to stabilize the hip, knee, or foot. For the upper extremities, arm supports and immobilization devices were used, and custom equipment was fabricated as needed to ensure reproducibility. Comfort, reproducibility, and minimization of setup error were prioritized in every case. kV imaging was used to verify patient positioning. Image guided RT was used for all cases. Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy or Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy were not considered for any joints, as 2-dimensional plans were sufficient to meet planning goals.

Treatment planning was based on CT simulation. Target definition followed consensus recommendations from the German Society for Radiation Oncology. For large joints (eg, hip, knee, shoulder), the clinical target volume encompassed the joint space along with adjacent osseous structures, periarticular soft tissue, capsular regions, and any areas of effusion or inflammation. In contrast, small-joint OA (eg, thumb base) was targeted regionally, while diffuse involvement of a hand or foot prompted coverage of the entire anatomic region. The proportion of cases treated using each target volume approach was not systematically recorded and therefore could not be quantified retrospectively. Dose was prescribed to the treatment volume with an objective of achieving planning target volume D95% ≥100% of the prescribed dose when feasible; formal dose homogeneity criteria were not applied beyond standard institutional dosimetry practices. Quality assurance (QA) included daily machine performance verification using Varian Machine Performance Check per the vendor test schedule and an independent Daily QA device, with additional imaging and output verification performed monthly in accordance with standard machine QA protocols.

Pain assessment

Pain and associated treatment response were evaluated using 2 measures: the numeric rating scale (NRS) and the von Pannewitz score (VPS).

Numeric rating scale

The NRS measures pain on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 represents no pain and 10 represents the worst possible pain.

Changes in NRS scores were interpreted according to the minimal clinically important difference. A reduction of ≥2 points on the NRS has been validated as clinically meaningful and was used as the threshold for clinical significance in this study.29

von Pannewitz score

The VPS is a subjective patient-reported measure of overall improvement in OA pain and symptoms. The VPS has been widely used in European studies to evaluate LDRT outcomes for benign conditions. The VPS measures changes in pain using the following categories: 0 (complete improvement), 1 (marked improvement), 2 (slight improvement), and 3 (no improvement).

FU procedures

FU assessments were conducted between 10 and 14 weeks posttreatment with a mean FU time of 12.3 weeks posttreatment. Data collection primarily occurred through in-person visits, with telephone interviews only used if necessary. All patients and treated joints included in this study completed an FU visit, resulting in complete pain scores with no missing values. FU duration did not vary significantly based on age, sex, joint type, or treatment response.

Reirradiation protocol

To determine if reirradiation was necessary, clinical reassessment was performed again at 12 weeks post-LDRT. Patients with persistent symptoms—including ongoing pain, reduced mobility, and continued reliance on analgesics—were considered for an additional treatment series. Reirradiation decisions were individualized based on patient preference, relative pain value, functional status, and clinical trajectory.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 94 patients met our inclusion criteria, with 136 joints treated with LDRT for OA. The mean age of patients was 74.5 years, with 38% male and 62% female patients. The distribution of treated joints included 61 hands (45%), 46 knees (34%), 15 shoulders (11%), 8 hips (6%), and 6 feet (4%). Most patients completed a single treatment course, with only 3.7% of joints (5/136) receiving a second treatment course. Forty-nine percent of affected joints treated were small joints (hands and feet), and 51% were large joints (shoulders, knees, and hips).

NRS results

The mean pre-RT NRS of all 136 joints decreased from 7.30 to 2.58 posttreatment (Fig. 1). Comparable decreases in NRS were observed across the following subgroups: males (6.98-2.46), females (7.50-2.66), ≤70 years (7.16-3.41), >70 years (7.35-2.28), small joints (7.24-2.77), and large joints (7.36-2.41). Safety and toxicity

LDRT was well tolerated with no grade ≥2 acute toxicities, treatment-related serious adverse events, or unexpected observations. These findings are consistent with prior European studies reporting minimal toxicity with LDRT. Given the low total dose administered and the advanced age of the study population, the theoretical risk of radiation-induced secondary malignancy is considered extremely low and unlikely to be clinically meaningful within the patients’ expected lifetime.

Statistical analysis

In this study, several patients received treatments at more than 1 joint site, leading to individuals having multiple pain measurements for different joints. As a result, joint-level observations were not independent but clustered at the patient level. To appropriately account for patient clustering while preserving joint-level data, pain outcomes were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with a patient-level random intercept. Timepoint (initial vs final) was included as a fixed effect. The mixed-effects model demonstrated a significant reduction in pain following LDRT (F [1, 94.35] = 192.6, P < .001). Estimated marginal means showed mean NRS scores decreased from 7.29 pre-RT (95% CI, 6.91-7.66) to 2.90 at FU (95% CI, 2.29-3.51), corresponding to an average reduction in NRS by 4.39 points. Degrees of freedom for fixed effects were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation to reduce small-sample bias. The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.73, indicating that approximately 73% of the total variability in pain scores occurred at the patient level rather than at the joint level. This high degree of within-patient correlation confirms substantial clustering of joint-level observations and supports the use of mixed-effects modeling to obtain valid inference.

Subgroup analysis

Multivariable linear mixed-effects modeling was used to evaluate pain outcomes while accounting for within-patient clustering of multiple treated joints by including a patient-level random intercept. Timepoint (initial vs final), age (continuous), sex, and joint size (small vs large) were included as fixed effects (Table 1). Neither age (F [1, 91.72] = 1.45, P = .23) nor sex (F [1, 89.65] = 1.66, P = .20) nor joint size (F [1, 92.11] = 0.88, P = .35) was significantly associated with pain outcomes, indicating that treatment response was consistent across the following subgroups. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and may have been underpowered to detect modest effect modification. However, CIs for subgroup effects were narrow and centered near zero. The estimated effect of age on pain outcomes was −0.03 points per year (95% CI, −0.07 to 0.02), the estimated difference between males and females was −0.24 points (95% CI, −0.61 to 0.13), and the estimated difference between small and large joints was 0.21 points (95% CI, −0.18 to 0.60). Discussion

Interest in LDRT for OA has recently been revived in the United States, primarily due to a critical review by Dove et al,30 which emphasized the growing need for effective noninvasive treatment options for OA due to rising OA prevalence and associated health care costs in the United States.30 Following this review, several RT institutes have begun treating patients with LDRT, with some collecting data on patient outcomes. In April 2025, Koneru et al27 published the first modern American case series of 69 patients with OA and 168 joints treated with LDRT (total 3 Gy, 0.5 Gy per fraction), with 72% of the treated joints showing significant and sustained pain relief with LDRT at 10 weeks of FU based on VPS assessment. The radiation dose and pain assessment protocol used in the current study are consistent with Koneru et al.27

The proportions of patients who responded favorably to LDRT in the current study (79%) are slightly higher than those reported by most studies, which had response rates of 29% to 75% (Table 2). The only exception was the study by Alvarez et al,31 which reported pain relief in 94% of patients with OA, including 41% who had complete relief. Incidentally, this study also had the highest reradiation rate (63%).

In addition, the current study had an extremely low reirradiation rate (3.7%) compared with retrospective and prospective studies, which had reirradiation rates ranging from 7.3% to 63% (Table 2). The low reirradiation rate in this cohort reflects institutional patterns during early implementation of LDRT in the United States to establish a higher threshold and more stringent justifications for reirradiation. In addition, many patients in the cohort who met criteria for reirradiation declined further treatment. Additionally, our shorter FU duration may have contributed to a low reirradiation rate, as patients may not have yet experienced reoccurrence of symptoms warranting reirradiation.

The effectiveness of LDRT varies based on study design. Level III evidence from the retrospective studies shows strong support for the effectiveness of LDRT. In the 5 retrospective studies in Table 2,32, 33, 34, 35,40 the response rate, or the rate of significant improvement in pain levels, ranges from 62.4% to 70%. Similar response rates were also observed in the American cohort reported by Koneru et al27 (72%) and the current study (79%). Some level II evidence for the efficacy of LDRT can be found in the 2 prospective studies in Table 2.31,37 As previously discussed, Alvarez et al31 reported an extremely high response rate and also had the highest rate of reirradiation. However, this study only included patients treated for hand OA. The other prospective study by Micke et al37 had a much larger sample size, with 703 joints treated with LDRT across the body, but observed a response rate at only 37.6%.

In contrast, there is little level I evidence in support of LDRT from the 3 European RCTs.36,38,39 In the studies by Mahler et al36 and Minten et al,38 patients were either treated with a standard dose 6 Gy (1 Gy) or given sham radiation. In both RCTs, patients receiving sham radiation experienced similar outcomes than the standard dose (43% vs 44% in Mahler et al36 and 36% vs 29% in Minten et al38). After publication of these 2 RCTs, the German Cooperative Group on Radiotherapy for Benign Diseases commented that the RCTs were well designed but underpowered (n = 55 in Mahler et al36 and n = 56 Minten et al38). Furthermore, the German Cooperative Group on Radiotherapy for Benign Diseases argued that the 2 RCTs included patients with advanced OA, characterized by severe anatomic damage in the bony joints and periarticular soft tissue, who may be less responsive to LDRT.24

In the 2022 ArthroRad trial, Niewald et al39 used a larger cohort of patients with OA (n = 244) who were either treated with a standard dose 3 Gy (0.5 Gy) or an experimental dose 0.3 Gy (0.05 Gy). Similarly, response rates between both groups were nearly identical: 61% in the treatment group and 65% in the experimental group.

More recently, several RCTs have shown some positive effects of LDRT. In a single-blind, sham-controlled RCT, Fazilat-Panah et al41 reported significant improvements in pain and functionality as measured by visual analog scale and Lysholm scores. However, this trial had several limitations including a small sample size (n = 60), an elderly cohort (mean age = 76.77 years), a short FU duration (6 months), and restriction to knee OA.

In a recent South Korean double-blind, sham-controlled RCT presented at American Society for Radiation Oncology, 114 patients with knee OA received either a treatment dosage of 3 Gy (0.5 Gy) or a sham dosage of 0.3 Gy (0.05 Gy). The treatment group had a response rate of 70.3% compared to the 41.7% response rate in the sham group.42

Overall, there still lacks conclusive level I evidence in support of LDRT. Hammadeh et al43 performed a recent meta-analysis assessing both the efficacy and safety of LDRT in OA of 6 RCTs. The authors found no significant advantage of LDRT over sham treatment for pain reduction (standardized mean difference [SMD]: − 0.92; P = .13) or functional improvement (SMD: 0.22; P = .22). However, LDRT was associated with a significantly higher incidence of adverse events.

The discordance between substantial positive level III evidence in support of LDRT and limited level I evidence in favor of LDRT needs to be addressed. Possible explanations for the lack of level I evidence include underpowered RCTs with small sample sizes, heterogeneity in cohort populations, and variability in measures of treatment outcomes. Thus, well-powered RCTs are necessary in the future to offer more evidence on the efficacy of LDRT for pain relief in patients with OA.

Limitations

While this study contributes to level III evidence on the efficacy of LDRT for pain relief in patients with OA, several limitations must be noted. First, given the retrospective nature of our study and the absence of a control group, our results cannot establish causality and may be susceptible to bias, as improvements in pain may be due to placebo effects or regression to the mean. Second, the current study used patient-reported pain scores through the NRS and VPS, rather than measures of functional responses, such as the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) or the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology−Osteoarthritis Research Society International. Third, our short FU duration with a mean FU of 12.3 weeks reflects early treatment response only and does not allow us to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of LDRT. Additionally, we did not have any intermediate pain assessments between baseline (pre-RT) and FU visits, limiting evaluation of response timing.

Recall bias represents another limitation that may have influenced patient recollection and subsequent pain scores both pre-RT and at FU. Additionally, selection bias is prevalent as patients in this study presented to a single-center RT institute and were deemed appropriate for LDRT, which limits generalizability to the larger OA population. Heterogeneity in OA severity was not documented, limiting the ability to discuss whether treatment response varies based on OA phenotype. Concomitant treatments, including analgesics and physical therapy may have had confounding effects on treatment outcome. Finally, publication bias may misrepresent LDRT effectiveness as positive retrospective studies are more likely to be reported than negative or neutral findings.

As institutional experience with LDRT for OA continues to evolve, there is an opportunity to pursue more systematic long-term outcome assessment. Future efforts at our institution will measure baseline OA severity, previous individual patient treatments, and improvements in joint mobility and aim to improve the relevance and generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

This single-institution retrospective study of an American cohort of patients with OA indicated that LDRT may be an effective and safe noninvasive modality for treating OA. Additionally, patients in our cohort experienced significant positive effects of LDRT, regardless of their age, sex, or type of joint treated. These results highlight LDRT as a viable emerging treatment option for OA and underscore the need for further research, especially well-designed RCTs with larger cohorts.
