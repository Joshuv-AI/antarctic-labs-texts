Plant-derived insoluble protein aggregates: Formation mechanisms, influencing factors, solubilization, functional properties, and applications☆
Authors: Yanli Ji, Jie Xue, Jiayang Wu, Chenxi Fan, Endian Guo, Hui Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Plant protein is a high-quality protein resource and has been widely used in various fields of the food industry. However, during food processing, especially thermal processing, enzymatic hydrolysis and enzymatic cross-linking, insoluble protein aggregates are inevitably formed. Hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces are the main forces that maintain the structural stability of insoluble protein aggregates, and the structure of insoluble protein aggregates could be changed by physical, chemical and enzymatic hydrolysis treatments to improve their solubility or reduce the degree of formation. At present, due to the insufficient utilization of insoluble protein aggregates, they are often discarded or used as raw material for animal feed, resulting in a large waste of plant protein resources. This paper summarizes the formation mechanism, influencing factors, functional properties and applications of insoluble protein aggregates, which provides a theoretical basis for further research and expansion of insoluble protein aggregates in the food industry.

Introduction

Plant protein is an important and sustainable protein resource with a low price and rich nutritional value. Nowadays, it has been widely used in various fields of the food industry, serving as food ingredients, supplements, emulsifiers, functional hydrolysates, edible coating ingredients, and carriers of various functional compounds (Wang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2022). For example, nut protein-based beverages (such as almond-emulsified products) are used as alternative to milk due to the high levels of phosphorus, potassium, and calcium in these products (Qamar et al., 2020). At appropriate ratios, the extrudates of soybean protein isolate and surimi showed increased hardness, chewiness, gel strength and fiber (Zhang, Feng, et al., 2022). Soybean protein products are also used as functional additives or as good nutritional fillers, that is, to replace meat ingredients in meals (Jovanovic et al., 2019). However, during food processing, especially thermal processing and enzymatic hydrolysis, insoluble protein aggregates are inevitably formed, which significantly reduces the availability of protein.

Insoluble protein aggregates are mainly divided into two types. The first type is derived from the inevitable exposure of hydrophobic peptides and/or clusters during the hydrolysis of proteins by proteases, leading to the formation of undesirable insoluble peptide aggregates. For instance, insoluble rice peptide aggregates with a weight of approximately 30 % of total rice protein can be obtained by hydrolysis with alkaline protease (Zhang, Yuan, et al., 2021). Enzymatic cross-linking also plays a pivotal role in the formation of insoluble protein aggregates. In a system containing cross-linking enzymes, such as transglutaminase and peroxidase, these enzymes catalyze the generation of covalent bonds between protein molecules, thus connecting multiple protein molecules together to form a more stable protein network or aggregate (Zou et al., 2022). The second type involves the generation of insoluble proteins from plant proteins under physical or chemical treatment. For example, alkaline treatment renders more than 90 % of green, oolong and black tea proteins insoluble in water (Ren, Chen, Zhang, Lin, & Li, 2019). While microwave heating treatment has been shown to produce larger quinoa protein aggregates (Huang et al., 2022). Additionally, spray drying disrupts protein structure and exposes hydrophobic residues on heavy chain CDR-1, leading to structurally damaged proteins forming insoluble aggregates through hydrophobic interactions and water reconstruction (Tao et al., 2023). Oxidation is another factor that can induce the production of insoluble protein aggregates. Excessive oxidation treatment promotes malondialdehyde-induced protein aggregation and cross-linking through non-disulfide covalent bonds while decreasing solubility in rice bran protein (Li et al., 2021). A similar phenomenon has been observed for hazelnut protein as well (Hu et al., 2023).

At present, due to the insufficient utilization of insoluble protein aggregates, they are often discarded or used as raw material for animal feed, leading to a significant waste of plant protein resources. Therefore, in-depth study of insoluble protein aggregates has great prospects. This paper mainly reviews the formation mechanism, influencing factors, functional properties and applications of insoluble protein aggregates.

Factors influencing the formation of insoluble protein aggregates

Factors such as temperature, pH, and the addition of other substances can have an effect on the formation of insoluble aggregates by influencing enzyme activity or peptide structure (Fig. 1). In general, the higher the reaction temperature, the faster the rate of generation of insoluble aggregates (Zhao & Li, 2009). In addition, the pH at which the aggregates reach their maximum yield varies (3.0–11.0). The formation of insoluble aggregates is also related to factors such as the hydrophobicity of their own surface and the addition of other substances (e.g., peptides, polysaccharides, salts, lipids, etc.) (Hagan & Villota, 1987). In addition, proteases containing hydrophobic groups are more likely to induce aggregate formation. For example, Alcalase alkaline protease promotes the formation of insoluble aggregates more readily than papain, whereas trypsin does not produce aggregates from soybean peptides (Liu et al., 2014; Otte et al., 2000). In the process of protein hydrolysis, different types and concentrations of substrates can lead to differences in the yield of aggregates. Sukan and Andrews (1982) used pepsin to hydrolyze fungal proteins and casein proteins, in which casein hydrolysis yielded 14 % more insoluble aggregates than fungal proteins. Tsai et al. (1972) found that as the concentration of the substrate increased, the insoluble aggregates yield increased exponentially.Fig. 1Factors influencing the formation of insoluble protein aggregates.Fig. 1

Research progress on the formation of insoluble protein aggregates

Insoluble protein aggregates are mainly divided into two types: one that is produced during the hydrolysis of protein and other is produced during heating treatment. Many studies on the aggregation behavior of soybean protein have been reported. For example, in the process of alkaline protease and protamex hydrolysis, soybean protein formed peptide aggregates with a molecular weight between 10 and 18 kDa through hydrophobic interactions between hydrophobic amino acids and sulfur-containing peptides. Furthermore, the insoluble peptide aggregates could be well digested by pancreatin and exhibiting better antioxidant activity (Zhang et al., 2017). Fischer et al. (2002) found that the interaction between peptide aggregates and other components formed during hydrolysis reduced the extractability of protein. The experiment of Kuipers and Gruppen (2008) proved that soybean globulin hydrolyzed by trypsin had a strong tendency to aggregate, and that aggregation occurs mainly in the vicinity of the disulfide bond between basic and acidic peptides. During heating treatment, β-conglycinin, glycinin and their separated subunits/polypeptides in soybean protein exhibited different heat-induced aggregation behaviors. In the aqueous dispersion system of soybean protein, αα’ subunit and acidic polypeptide demonstrated high solubility and zeta-potential value, which inhibited thermal aggregation. Meanwhile, β subunit and basic polypeptide formed large-sized insoluble thermal aggregates through hydrophobic interaction (Ju et al., 2023). Li, Ye, et al. (2023) analyzed the thermal aggregation behavior of soybean milk protein, and found that when the protein concentration was less than 40 mg/g, soybean milk protein was denatured, dissociated and aggregated to form rod-like aggregates. With an increase in protein concentration, both the content and size of aggregates increased as well as the surface hydrophobic groups, sulfhydryl groups (-SH) and the electrostatic charge of exposed protein aggregates also increased. When the protein concentration exceeded 40 mg/g, the protein subunits formed aggregates and bind to each other along the axis through the hydrophobic groups on the surface and -SH, thereby forming linear or even micro-network aggregates. Ren et al. (2009) investigated the aggregation patterns of polypeptides in soybean milk protein granules formed by heating, and found that these protein aggregates were mainly composed of alkaline and acidic polypeptides in glycinin linked by disulfide bonds and a small amount of β-conglycinin. Compared with β subunit, the degree of aggregation for various components is as follows: denatured basic polypeptides > native glycinin > denatured β subunit > native β-conglycinin > denatured acidic polypeptides > denatured αα’ subunit (He et al., 2015). Further research showed the soluble aggregates formed by β-conglycinin had a limited size and less compact conformation, whereas the insoluble aggregates formed by glycinin had a denser cores and a less dense shells (Guo et al., 2012). It is worth noting that the differences in aggregation behaviors between plant and animal proteins warrant further investigation.

Formation mechanism of insoluble protein aggregates

It has been demonstrated that over-processing of protein and exposure of too many hydrophobic amino acid sites can activate hydrophobic interactions between the exposed sites, leading to aggregation of protein molecules. Additionally, enzymatic hydrolysis can promote protein cleavage, resulting in the release of different peptide residues. The hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds between these residues further promote peptide-peptide/protein interactions and induce the formation of insoluble aggregates.

Both covalent and non-covalent interactions play a role in the formation of insoluble protein aggregates (Zhang et al., 2017). Non-covalent forces involved in maintaining the structural stability of insoluble protein mainly include hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces (Fig. 2). Among these, hydrophobic interactions are the main driving forces for protein folding. Hydrolysis can increase the effective hydrophobicity of certain globular proteins by exposing buried non-polar residues, leading to aggregate formation (Creusot & Gruppen, 2007). Through the observation of atomic force microscopy and the comparison of protein secondary structure, it was found that rice protein hydrolysed aggregates were formed by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Li et al., 2019). Other studies have also shown that the disruption of hydrophobic interactions between protein molecules and acceleration of protein molecule motion led to the gradual aggregation of quinoa proteins (Cao et al., 2021). Similarly, hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds partially stabilize insoluble pea protein aggregates (Moll et al., 2021). Zhang et al. (2023) demonstrated that heat treatment resulted in the formation of insoluble aggregates in dry-heated soybeans at 160 °C through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Covalent forces involved in maintaining the structural stability of insoluble proteins are mainly disulfide bonds. When dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane was added to the insoluble pea protein dispersion, the average particle size of the insoluble pea protein dispersion decreased, and the turbidity increased significantly, indicating that insoluble pea protein aggregates were interconnected by disulfide bonds (Moll et al., 2021). Thermal inactivation further accelerates intermolecular peptide-peptide/protein interactions via disulfide bonds and induces larger aggregates between the generated peptides (Zhang et al., 2017). The increase of β-sheets and the decrease in surface hydrophobicity suggested that the hydrophobic aggregates of irradiated rice protein were formed by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Li et al., 2019). Additionally, it was found that the insoluble aggregates in 200 °C dry-heated soybeans were mainly formed by oxidation-induced covalent bonds (Zhang et al., 2023).Fig. 2The formation and utilization of insoluble protein aggregates.Fig. 2

Physical modification

Sonication

Ultrasonic modification refers to the use of high-intensity ultrasound to produce high temperature, strong shear force and high pressure in solution, promote the interaction between protein molecules and the mutual conversion between sulfhydryl and disulfide bonds, increasing the hydrophobicity of the protein surface and affecting its advanced structure and solubility. The effect of ultrasonic treatment on the oxidized soybean protein aggregates was different, among which, the medium-power ultrasonic treatment could depolymerize the oxidized aggregates and increase the solubility of insoluble soybean protein (Wang et al., 2022). Similarly, Zhang et al. (2021a) conducted ultrasonic treatment on insoluble rice peptide aggregates produced during rice proteolysis. It has been found that the mechanical action of ultrasound could destroy non-covalent interactions, break large aggregates into smaller aggregates, produce soluble dispersions, and increase their solubility. In addition, with increased ultrasonic time, the shear force generated by cavitation could directly act on the oxidized protein to decompress the aggregates and improve the functional properties of insoluble quinoa protein (Cao et al., 2021). Ultrasonic cavitation effect could depolymerize larger walnut protein aggregates into smaller particles, which enhances the interaction between protein and water (Zhu et al., 2018). Further studies confirmed that ultrasonic treatment promoted the degradation of soybean peptide aggregates by destroying non-covalent interactions, leading to a significant increase in protein solubility (Tian et al., 2020).

High-pressure homogenization

High-pressure homogenization has been shown to improve the solubility and stability of insoluble protein aggregates under high pressure and shear force. Moll et al. (2022) used a microfluidic device to conduct high-pressure homogenization on insoluble pea protein dispersions, demonstrating that larger protein aggregates were broken down into a larger number of smaller aggregates. Similarly, the micro-fluidization of insoluble pea protein and quinoa protein aggregates suspensions significantly reduced the particle size and improved protein solubility under increased homogeneous pressure (Ong et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023). Further investigation revealed that the combined effects of cavitation, high shear and turbulence led to the unfolding of insoluble pea protein aggregates and production of soluble proteins (Luo et al., 2022). Other studies have indicated that high-pressure homogenization can expose the hydrophobic points of large insoluble kidney bean protein aggregates, increase their surface hydrophobicity and solubility, and even break them down into smaller aggregates (Guo et al., 2021).

Electron beam irradiation treatment

Electron beam irradiation affects chemical bonds and molecular bonds in the protein structure due to high energy, resulting in protein unfolding and denaturation, which improves its solubility. Li et al. (2019) observed that the aggregation of peptides led to an increase in the solubility of rice proteins through non-covalent interaction between exposed hydrophobic groups and sulfhydryl groups. However, high irradiation doses promoted the formation of higher-molecular-weight peptides through nonspecific interactions. Similar results were obtained by Zhang, Zhang, et al. (2022), who found that with the increase of irradiation dose, the solubility of okara protein increased significantly, reaching the maximum at 6 kGy. These findings were attributed to the fact that electron beam irradiation at an appropriate dose destroys the ordered structure of the protein, causes more polar groups to face water, thereby increasing the interaction between protein and water. However, excessive irradiation dose can lead to cross-linking between protein molecules, forming insoluble aggregates and protein with higher molecular weights.

Chemical modification

Acid and alkali treatment

When soybean peptide aggregates were subjected to extreme pH values (10.0, 11.0, and 12.0), it was found that the spatial structure of insoluble macromolecular aggregates was disrupted by dissociation into polypeptides, and the hidden hydrophilic amino acid residues were exposed, which improved their solubility (Zhang, Zuo, et al., 2021). Other studies have demonstrated that peanut protein isolates treated at pH 2 or pH 12 lost its gelling ability due to the formation of large protein aggregates (Li et al., 2020).

Glycosylation

Glycosylation modification is a widely used chemical method in the food industry. Studies have shown that glycosylation could increase the electrostatic repulsion between particles, enhance the flexibility of protein, effectively hindering protein-protein aggregation (Kim et al., 2024). Glycosylation also results in better structural stability of soybean globulin in the secondary and tertiary structures, leading to better solubility of modified emulsions (Hao et al., 2020). In addition, the covalent coupling of protein and polysaccharide through Maillard reaction can improve the solubility of plant protein. For example, the hydroxyl group on gum Arabic has strong hydrogen bonding ability, which improve the affinity between protein and water, and significantly increasing the solubility of pea protein isolate (Zha et al., 2019). Similar results have been found for rice protein and rapeseed protein as well (Cheng et al., 2022; Qu et al., 2018).

Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is the esterification of the reactive groups of protein side chains by the action of phosphate. It has been shown that the introduction of a large amount of negatively charged PO43− during phosphorylation increases the electrostatic repulsion of protein, making it easier to disperse protein in a solution system and thus increasing its solubility. Lu et al. (2023) modified soybean protein isolate with sodium trimetaphosphate, and the solubility of protein was 1.3–2.4 times higher than that of untreated samples. Similar results were reported in studies on wheat protein and rice protein (Hu et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2022). Yan and Zhou (2021) also demonstrated that phosphorylation of walnut protein isolate led to an increase in solubility due to the introduction of negative charge, which enhanced electrostatic repulsion and dispersion of the protein in solution. Further investigation revealed that the polar phosphate groups improved the hydration of the protein and increased the thickness of the hydration layer, making interactions between proteins more difficult and ultimately improving solubility (Liu et al., 2020).

Acylation

Acylation modification is the process of introducing new functional groups into protein molecules through reactions between the nucleophilic groups in the protein molecules and the carbonyl groups in the acylation reagents. It was found that acetylation increased the rate of protein degradation, reduced protein-protein interactions, and consequently improved pumpkin protein solubility (Miedzianka et al., 2021). Similarly, succinylation modification induced greater electrostatic repulsion within proteins, leading to conformational changes that enhanced interaction with water and subsequently increased solubility (El-Adawy, 2000). After succinylation modification, mung bean protein aggregates decreased in size while water solubility increased (Charoensuk et al., 2018). Shah and Singhal (2019) found that succinylation significantly altered the secondary structure of pea protein polymer and improved its solubility as well.

Biological modification

Enzyme modification

Enzyme modification improves the solubility of insoluble protein aggregates by partial hydrolysis peptide or amide bonds using proteases. Common proteases can be divided into animal proteases, plant proteases and microbial proteases. Trypsin-mediated moderate hydrolysis of pea protein resulted in the breaking of disulfide bonds and degradation into small molecular peptides, transforming insoluble protein into soluble components (García Arteaga et al., 2022; Shuai et al., 2022). Further investigation revealed that proteolysis exposed the hydrophilic region on the surface of small molecule polypeptides, thereby enhancing the interaction between protein and water and improving protein solubility (Shahbal et al., 2023). García et al. (2020) also obeserved that papain and bromelain significantly enhanced the solubility of pea protein isolates at pH 4.5, especially after 120 min.

Arginine modification

Arginine is widely employed as an additive to modulate protein behavior. Its guanidine group inhibits protein aggregation and enhances solubility by binding to aromatic residues, proving particularly effective in protein renaturation, dissolution, and purification (Tsumoto et al., 2004). Li, Zhang, et al. (2023) reported significantly improved solubility and emulsifying properties in soy protein isolate. Zhu et al. (2024) documented a concentration-dependent increase in soy protein hydrolysate solubility from 69.39 % to 82.72 % as arginine concentration rose from 0 % to 1.0 %. Similar enhancements were observed in arginine-modified pea protein isolate (Cao et al., 2022). Arginine regulates the system's pH to increase electrostatic charge and hydration on the protein surface. Concurrently, it interacts with amino acid side chains to form partially unfolded intermediates and utilizes its ionizable groups (α-COOH, α-NH2, and guanidine) to bind to proteins. This elevates the aggregation energy barrier, synergistically suppressing protein aggregation while significantly enhancing solubility.

Combined processing

Physical modification methods are often cost-effective with less nutrient loss, but low modification efficiency. Chemical modification can yield remarkable effects using a variety of reagents but may lead to side effects or produce difficult-to-control by-products. Enzymatic modification offers high efficiency and specificity but comes with expensive enzyme costs and strict reaction conditions. Combining two or more modification methods can leverage the advantages of multiple approaches or compensate for individual method shortcomings to maximize protein properties at minimal cost (Fig. 3). Studies have demonstrated that pH transfer and ultrasonic treatment effectively disrupt existing large agglomerates in broad bean protein isolates, forming smaller submicron agglomerates that significantly enhance solubility (Alavi et al., 2021). High-pressure homogenization and alkali treatment reduce interactions between hydrophobic groups on insoluble soybean peptide aggregates, increase hydrophobic amino acid content, and improve solubility as well as emulsification properties (Du et al., 2020). Similarly, alkali treatment and high-pressure homogenization have been shown to reduce the size and transparency of quinoa protein aggregates, leading to a significant increase in solubility from 7.85 % to 78.97 % (Yildiz & Yıldız, 2023). In summary, the combination of alkali treatment with ultrasonic treatment and high-pressure homogenization can effectively enhance the solubility of insoluble proteins. Mechanism of improving the solubility of insoluble protein aggregates.

Although the above methods can effectively improve the solubility of plant insoluble proteins, the stability of proteins after depolymerization is also crucial. Wang et al. (2025) found that the soybean protein isolate aggregates exhibited a trend of depolymerization followed by reaggregation with the increase of pulsed electric fields (PEF) intensity. At 10 and 15 kV/cm, the polarization effect of PEF induced the unfolding of the tertiary structure of soybean protein isolate, leading to the enhancement of the electronegativity of the side chains, and the electrostatic repulsive force between the molecules promoted the depolymerization of soybean protein isolate aggregates. However, with the withdrawal of PEF, the structure of the soybean protein isolate was partially reversible, resulting in a limited depolymerization effect. When the PEF intensity reached 20 kV/cm and above, soybean protein isolate underwentstructure unfolding, subunit dissociation, continuous exposure of hydrophobic groups and sulfhydryl groups, and ultimately reaggregation mediated by hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonding, resulting in the formation of high molecular weight soluble and insoluble protein aggregates. Similarly, High-pressure homogenization (HPH) at low pressure (30, 60 MPa) could induce the formation of protein soluble aggregates by promoting free SH-disulfide bond (SH/SS) exchange, increased ζ-potential and decreased surface hydrophobicity. HPH at high pressure (90, 120 MPa) could cause disulphide bonds of the formed aggregates to be broken and disrupt the noncovalent interactions in the protein aggregates, such as hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, resulting in collisions between protein aggregates and a reduction in large protein aggregates (Guo et al., 2021). In addition, studies on the oxidative aggregates of quinoa protein have shown that ultrasound treatment can improve the stability of depolymerized proteins in the early stages, such as reducing particle size and turbidity, increasing solubility, surface hydrophobicity, and zeta potential, and promoting the decomposition of large molecular aggregates. However, prolonged ultrasound time (40 min) can lead to protein re aggregation and decreased stability of depolymerized proteins (Cao et al., 2021). Variations in the degrees of protein aggregation can result in different physicochemical and functional properties of the final product.

Functional properties and application of insoluble protein aggregates

Studies have shown that the insoluble protein aggregates have a higher content of hydrophobic amino acids and exhibit better anti-gastric digestive properties and good antioxidant activity (Udenigwe et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). In addition, the insoluble aggregates also have good salt ion stability (Zhang, Zuo, et al., 2021). Currently, various studies have been conducted on the processing and utilization of insoluble protein aggregates, particularly in relation to nanoparticles, food-grade emulsifiers, and protein nutritional supplements.

Preparation of protein nanoparticles

Protein nanoparticles are defined as ultrafine particles that fall within the transition region between atomic clusters and microscale objects. Their applications in the food industry primarily involve encapsulating and protecting nutrients such as vitamins, providing active packaging for food products, and enhancing stability in pickering emulsions used in food production processes. Insoluble peptide aggregates can also be utilized for nanoparticle preparation. Research has demonstrated that novel soybean peptide nanoparticles with excellent storage stability and antioxidant activity can be successfully derived from insoluble aggregates by controlling pH using bioactive substances (Zhang et al., 2021b). Furthermore, a new type of soybean peptide-based nanoparticle can be formed through self-assembly of large peptide aggregates generated during proteolysis alongside ultrasound-induced thermal inactivation. These resulting nanoparticles have been proven to possess bifunctional properties as effective interfacial stabilizers; they exhibit excellent lipid oxidation stability when used within food emulsion systems (Zhang, Zhao, et al., 2018). Zhang, Zhou, et al. (2022) also prepared spherical soybean peptide-based nanoparticles through the pH-driven assembly of large insoluble peptide aggregates, forming functional particle emulsifiers to create stable oil-in-water Pickering emulsions. These emulsions can be utilized for the continuous delivery of lipophilic nutrients, offering a promising approach for the high-value application of peptide aggregates in food via functional emulsions. Additionally, hydrolyzed soybean peptide aggregates were assembled into soybean peptide nanoparticles through ultrasonic treatment, effectively adsorbing onto the corn oil water interface to form a stable viscoelastic interface layer (Zhang et al., 2020).

Food grade emulsifiers and delivery system

Recent research has shown that insoluble peptide aggregates can serve as raw materials for producing food-grade emulsifiers. Burgos-Díaz et al. (2019) discovered that lupin protein aggregates with high surface charge and partial wettability effectively stabilized O/W emulsions, which remained highly stable in milk fat even after storage for more than 14 days. Zhang et al. (2021a) developed a novel food-grade Pickering stabilizer with physical and oxidative stability using insoluble rice peptide aggregates formed during ultrasonic hydrolysis of rice protein. This not only proposed an effective method for utilizing insoluble aggregates from protein hydrolysis but also provided a new bifunctional Pickering stabilizer with intrinsic antioxidant properties. Jing et al. (2022) confirmed that insoluble soybean peptide aggregates, after homogenization and pH change treatments, exhibited higher surface hydrophobicity and interfacial adsorption. The Pickering emulsion gel prepared using these insoluble soybean peptide aggregates had smaller droplet size and higher gel strength, remaining stable even after 60 days of storage (Jing et al., 2023). Interestingly, although soy milk visually resembles cow's milk, the fundamental mechanisms governing protein suspension stability and insoluble aggregate formation differ significantly. Consequently, ongoing research focuses on modifying soy proteins to function as emulsifying alternatives to casein (Du et al., 2020). Similarly, excellent rheological properties, storage stability and gel-like behavior in Pickering emulsions can be achieved by employing insoluble protein nanoparticles in tea residue (Ren, Chen, Zhang, Zhao, et al., 2019; Ren et al., 2020). Tea water-insoluble protein / κ-carrageenan mixtures can also be applied to prepare stable Pickering emulsions and fish oil gels (Ren et al., 2021). These findings will help explore new edible materials, construct novel colloid structures of underused edible materials and their eventual use in real food manufacture.

Furthermore, protein-based Pickering emulsions can be used to create colloidal delivery systems for application in the food industries. Yang et al. (2023) demonstrated that rice peptide aggregate–chitosan complexes emulsions had better storage stability, lower free fatty acid release (79.8 % and 76.3 %, respectively), and higher curcumin bioaccessibility (65.2 % and 68.2 %, respectively) than rice peptide aggregate emulsions. This study showed that a low-value insoluble rice peptide precipitate could be used as a valuable emulsifier in foods, which may increase the economics and sustainability of the food supply.

Protein nutritional supplement

Studies have shown that insoluble protein aggregates is also a nutritious protein resource that can be used as a nutritional supplement. The large insoluble aggregates formed during protein hydrolysis contain a high proportion of essential amino acids. After in vitro digestion, it was found that insoluble peptide aggregates are digested more efficiently in the intestinal digestive tract, releasing low molecular weight peptides with better antioxidant activity (Zhang et al., 2017). Additionally, the content of essential amino acids in insoluble soybean peptide aggregate meets the adult recommended standard of FAO/WHO (1991), indicating its high nutritional value (Du et al., 2020). In summary, insoluble peptide aggregates could be used as a protein supplement or potentially as an active ingredient for human consumption.

Conclusions and outlook

Insoluble protein aggregates are inevitably formed during food processing, especially thermal processing and enzymatic hydrolysis. Both covalent and non-covalent interactions contribute to the formation of these aggregates. They can be categorized into those produced during proteolysis and those produced during physical or chemical treatment based on their production pathways. The solubility of insoluble protein aggregates can be improved or their production reduced through physical, chemical, and biological methods. Currently, research on the application of plant insoluble protein aggregates mainly focuses on the preparation of nanoparticles, food-grade emulsifiers, and protein nutritional supplements. However, there are still challenges such as low protein utilization and insufficient processing utilization. Although effective processing methods can enhance the solubility of insoluble peptide aggregates, the stability of depolymerized proteins has not been thoroughly evaluated and analyzed. Therefore, it is imperative to further investigate methods for enhancing the utilization and stability of insoluble peptide aggregates. Meanwhile, more research is needed to construct insoluble protein aggregates and protein related multi-component composite systems, explore their synergistic effects with different substances, and further optimize the composite conditions to enhance performance. Additionally, a systematic evaluation system needs to be established to study the stability of depolymerized proteins under different environmental conditions (such as temperature, pH value, ionic strength, storage time, etc.), including its physical stability (such as aggregation and precipitation tendency), chemical stability (such as oxidation and hydrolysis sensitivity), and biological stability (such as resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis). By optimizing the processing technology and adding appropriate stabilizers, the stability of depolymerized proteins can be improved to ensure its quality and function during food processing and storage.
