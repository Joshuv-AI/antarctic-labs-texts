Identification of gene modules associated with B cell activation and tissue remodeling in primary Sjögren’s syndrome
Authors: Jun Huang, Chen Zhang, Tingting Liu, Zhiyong Deng, Lei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePrimary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a systemic autoimmune exocrinopathy affecting salivary and lacrimal glands. This study presents an exploratory single-cell transcriptomic analysis of labial salivary glands to generate hypotheses about B-cell–associated gene modules in pSS, and to uncover novel therapeutic targets for B cell modulation in pSS.MethodsThe high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) was performed on gene expression data obtained from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of 32,337 cells from labial glands of three pSS patients and three healthy controls. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted to investigate the functional roles of the identified gene modules. Additionally, the scRank method was applied to evaluate the responsiveness of key B cell-related targets across different cell types, providing new insights into the role of B cells in the pathogenesis of pSS.ResultsThrough hdWGCNA analysis, we resolved seven co-expression modules in pSS. Module 5, restricted to plasma cells, contains POU2AF1, SLAMF7, SPCS2, CD79A and PDIA6 and is highly enriched for COPI/II-mediated vesicle trafficking and B-cell-receptor signaling, thereby driving autoantibody production and chronic inflammation. Modules 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7 align with extracellular-matrix remodeling, epithelial stress and metabolic reprogramming, underscoring the disease’s multifactorial pathobiology. scRank ranked Module 5 as the most drug-responsive cluster and highlighted POU2AF1, SLAMF7 and CD79A as tractable B-cell targets for restoring immune homeostasis in pSS.ConclusionsOur study identified distinct gene modules associated with pSS, with a particular emphasis on B cells, unveiling novel potential therapeutic targets. The activation of B cells, coupled with immune dysregulation and epithelial dysfunction, appears to play a critical role in pSS pathogenesis, offering valuable insights for developing targeted therapeutic strategies that address both immune activation and tissue repair.These findings nominate B-cell–associated modules—including a plasma cell–enriched module featuring POU2AF1, SLAMF7, and CD79A—as hypotheses for future functional validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-025-00765-w.

Introduction

Primary Sjögren’s Syndrome (pSS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that primarily affects the exocrine glands, leading to dryness of the mouth and eyes [1]. The pathogenesis of pSS is complex and multifactorial, with B cells playing a pivotal role in driving autoimmunity [2]. These cells contribute to disease progression by producing aberrant autoantibodies, including anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, which are characteristic biomarkers of pSS [3]. The excessive production of these autoantibodies forms immune complexes that deposit in glandular tissues, triggering complement activation and causing inflammation and tissue damage [4]. This autoimmune cascade is further amplified as activated B cells also act as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), presenting self-antigens via HLA class II molecules to T cells, thereby perpetuating the inflammatory cycle [5]. Thus, B cells not only contribute to the formation of autoantibodies but also play a central role in shaping the immune microenvironment and maintaining chronic inflammation in pSS.

Beyond their involvement in antibody production, B cells secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as BAFF (B-cell activating factor) and IL-6, which further promote their own survival and activation [6, 7]. These cytokines also recruit other immune cells to the affected glands, exacerbating the inflammatory response and sustaining the autoimmune environment. In pSS, B cells interact with T cells to form ectopic lymphoid structures within the exocrine glands [8]. These structures are sites of continuous autoantibody production and B cell activation, further contributing to glandular damage. Importantly, regulatory B cells (Bregs), which typically suppress immune overactivation by producing anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, are reduced in number or functionally impaired in pSS [9]. The loss of Breg function leads to a breakdown in immune tolerance, allowing for the persistence of autoreactive immune responses and contributing to the chronicity of the disease.

Recent advances in single-cell technologies have provided a deeper understanding of the heterogeneity of B cells in Sjögren’s Syndrome, offering insights into their role in both the immune response and tissue damage [10–12]. By analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing data from pSS patients and healthy controls, researchers have been able to identify specific gene expression patterns and molecular signatures that distinguish diseased tissue from healthy tissue. This analysis is crucial for uncovering the underlying mechanisms that drive B cell dysregulation in pSS and identifying potential therapeutic targets. In particular, high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) [13] allows for the identification of gene modules that are associated with different biological processes, such as immune activation, inflammation, and tissue repair, which are all central to the pathogenesis of primary Sjögren’s Syndrome.

In this study, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from the labial gland tissues of pSS patients and healthy controls to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying B cell dysfunction in pSS. By applying hdWGCNA, we identified several gene modules associated with key biological processes involved in the pathogenesis of pSS. The insights gained from this analysis provide valuable information on gene expression patterns and their functional roles in various cell types, particularly B cells, within the salivary glands.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue specimens

Labial gland tissues were obtained from three pSS patients and three healthy donors, as described in the previous study [14]. All participants, recruited from Kunshan First People’s Hospital in 2022, met the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for pSS [15]. Written informed consent was obtained, and the Ethics Committee of Kunshan First People’s Hospital approved the use of tissue samples for research (2024-03-014-K01).

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data analysis

The scRNA-seq data were processed using the same protocols as described in the previous study [14]. Briefly, cell capture and cDNA synthesis were performed using the BD Rhapsody™ Cartridge Reagent Kit and BD Rhapsody™ Cartridge Kit, with 300–600 viable cells per microliter loaded onto the cartridge. After cell capture, RNA was barcoded during reverse transcription, followed by cDNA amplification and quality control, as previously outlined. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted using the BD Rhapsody™ WTA Amplification Kit and Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, respectively.

For data analysis, expression matrices were generated using the BD Rhapsody™ sequencing pipelines, converted into Seurat objects, and analyzed following the methods described in [14]. Key steps, including data normalization, identification of highly variable genes, PCA, and clustering analysis, were performed using the Seurat package, with dimensionality reduction via UMAP.

Data preparation for HdWGCNA analysis

To optimize the dataset for Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), a gene selection procedure was applied, retaining only genes expressed in at least 5% of the cells. This was achieved using the SetupForWGCNA function from the hdWGCNA package [13], with the “fraction” method applied for gene selection and a threshold of 0.05, which simply retains genes expressed in at least a chosen fraction of cells to reduce sparsity and stabilize co-expression estimation.

Metacell construction

To enhance the resolution and stability of the co-expression network, we aggregated individual cells into “metacells” based on shared attributes. Metacell construction was performed using the MetacellsByGroups function, which grouped cells based on metadata variables, including “Cluster” and “Group”. The Harmony algorithm [16] was employed for batch effect correction through dimensionality reduction. K-nearest neighbor (KNN) clustering was applied to group cells, with a predefined parameter of 25 nearest neighbors (k = 25) and a maximum of 10 shared cells per metacell (max_shared = 10 limits how often a single cell can contribute to multiple metacells to avoid over-smoothing; this is a technical stability parameter without direct biological interpretation). The resulting metacells were subsequently normalized using the NormalizeMetacells function.

Gene expression matrix and network construction

After metacell aggregation, the gene expression matrix was constructed using the SetDatExpr function, with the “Cluster” metadata used to define the group of interest for network construction. The normalized RNA data layer was selected for network analysis. To identify the optimal soft-thresholding power for constructing the co-expression network, we utilized the TestSoftPowers function, selecting a “signed” network type. The optimal soft-thresholding power was determined based on the correlation of the adjacency matrix with the topology overlap matrix. The power value of 9 was selected for network construction, and the co-expression network was subsequently constructed using the ConstructNetwork function. A hierarchical dendrogram, illustrating the clustering of genes into modules, was generated using the PlotDendrogram function.

Module identification and eigengene computation

Gene modules, defined as groups of highly co-expressed genes, were identified using the ModuleEigengenes function. The module eigengenes (MEs), representing the first principal component of each gene module, were computed, taking into account the “Batch” metadata. To evaluate module connectivity, we used the ModuleConnectivity function, followed by the ResetModuleNames function to assign new labels to the modules, which were designated as “B cells-M”. Module expression was evaluated by calculating both harmonized and non-harmonized eigengenes (hMEs and MEs) using the GetMEs function. Gene-module association scores (kME), reflecting the strength of the correlation between individual genes and their respective modules, were computed. The top 25 hub genes exhibiting the highest kME values were identified within each module, and their expression profiles were evaluated using the UCell method, implemented through the ModuleExprScore function.

Module visualization and feature plots

Module eigengene expression (hMEs) was visualized using the ModuleFeaturePlot function, which generates feature plots ordered by the highest eigengene values for each module. These plots were then combined into a single composite figure using the wrap_plots function from the patchwork package, providing a comprehensive visualization of module expression across the dataset.

Module enrichment analysis

To functionally annotate the identified gene modules, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the RunEnrichr function. Enrichment tests were conducted using three GO categories: Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function. The results were visualized as dot plots using the EnrichrDotPlot function, which displayed the top 5 enriched GO terms for each module. These visualizations highlighted the biological pathways and functions most significantly associated with the gene modules.

Hub gene network and module networks

To investigate the relationships between hub genes and other genes within the co-expression network, we generated hub gene network plots using the HubGeneNetworkPlot function. These plots focused on the top 10 hub genes within each module and their interconnections with other genes. Additionally, module network plots were generated using the ModuleNetworkPlot function, which depicted the interactions among genes within each module. The plots displayed the top 20 genes in the inner ring and the top 30 genes in the outer ring, thereby illustrating the structural organization of each gene module.

ScRank analysis of Drug-Responsive cell types

To identify drug-responsive cell populations in scRNA-seq data, we applied the scRank package to analysis [17]. The target genes were selected for drug response analysis, and a scRank object was created using the CreateScRank function, integrating the gene expression data with cell type information. The gene regulatory networks (GRNs) were constructed using the Constr_net function, and cell types were ranked based on their drug response using the rank_celltype function. The ranked cell types were then visualized in reduced-dimensional space with the plot_dim function.

Results

Identification of gene modules associated with biological processes in primary Sjögren’s syndrome

In this study, we applied hdWGCNA to identify gene modules linked to key biological processes in pSS. Seven distinct gene modules were identified, each associated with different molecular pathways central to the disease (Supplemental Fig. 1 A and 1B). Module 1 included genes such as GSN, CLU, and SMAD1, which are involved in cytoskeletal organization and extracellular matrix functions, suggesting a role in maintaining tissue integrity (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1A). Module 2, enriched in immune-related genes like SLPI and PIGR, was associated with immune defense processes in glandular tissues (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1B). Module 3, containing ribosomal proteins such as RPS27A and RPL21, supported protein synthesis and cellular metabolism (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1C). Module 4, enriched in genes like VWA1 and THBS1, was linked to extracellular matrix remodeling and cell adhesion, which are crucial for tissue repair and cellular interactions (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1D).

Module 5 stood out due to its strong association with immune activation, particularly within plasma cells. It contained genes such as CD79A and SLAMF7, which are essential for B cell activation (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1E). Modules 6 and 7 revealed the importance of epithelial dysfunction and cellular stress in pSS. Module 6, including genes like KRT19 and HSPG2, was linked to epithelial differentiation and stress responses (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1F), while Module 7, enriched in genes such as ITGB1 and GAPDH, was involved in cellular metabolism and cytoskeletal alterations (Supplemental Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1G). Together, these findings demonstrated that primary Sjögren’s Syndrome involved more than just immune dysregulation; disruptions in epithelial function and cellular homeostasis contributed significantly to the disease, underscoring the need for therapies that target both immune activation and tissue repair.

Gene Co-expression network among different modules

The network analysis provided a comprehensive view of gene interactions within and between the identified modules, shedding light on how these genes collaborate to regulate key biological processes in pSS (Fig. 2). Each node in the network represented a gene, with edges indicating co-expression relationships, highlighting the functional interdependence of genes within and across modules. For example, Module 5, enriched in immune-related genes such as CD79A and SLAMF7, demonstrated strong co-expression, indicating their joint role in immune regulation, particularly within plasma cells. This close interaction underscored their critical involvement in immune activation and inflammation, hallmark features of pSS.

Beyond Module 5, the network revealed cross-module interactions. Module 1, enriched with genes like TM4SF1 and PLAMD, showed co-expression with Module 2 genes such as SLPI and LTF, which are involved in immune defense. This may reflect that these genes collaborate to regulate immune responses and maintain immune homeostasis in glandular tissues, while also contributing to immune dysregulation in pSS. Additionally, Module 3, which includes ribosomal proteins like RPL27A and RPL21, was closely linked to Module 7, containing genes such as PKM and ITGB1, reflecting the integration of protein synthesis and metabolic regulation with immune activation.

The interactions between these modules highlighted the complexity of Sjögren’s Syndrome, where immune dysregulation, epithelial stress, and metabolic disturbances are tightly interconnected. Genes from Module 6, involved in epithelial stress responses, and Module 4, associated with tissue repair, interacted with immune-related modules like Module 5, suggesting that immune dysfunction and tissue damage are intricately linked in the disease’s progression. These interactions provided a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving Sjögren’s Syndrome and suggested that targeting multiple interconnected genes might offer a more effective therapeutic approach, addressing both immune activation and tissue damage.

GO enrichment analysis of genes from different modules

GO enrichment analysis of the identified gene modules revealed the functional roles of these genes across three categories—Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function (Fig. 3A to C). In the Biological Process category, Module 5 was enriched in processes related to vesicle transport and membrane targeting, particularly COPI coating of Golgi vesicles, cotranslational and post-translational protein targeting to the membrane, and vesicle budding. These processes are critical to the cellular mechanisms involved in vesicular trafficking and protein transport (Fig. 3A).

In the Cellular Component category, Module 5 was prominently enriched in genes related to COPI-coated Vesicle and COPII Vesicle Coat, highlighting its central role in vesicular trafficking and protein transport. Modules 3 and 7 showed enrichment in ribosomal components, emphasizing their involvement in protein synthesis, while Module 4 was enriched in genes associated with the Endoplasmic Reticulum Membrane, supporting its role in protein processing (Fig. 3B).

In the Molecular Function category, genes in Module 5 were enriched for terms such as Immunoglobulin Receptor Binding and Ubiquitin-Specific Protease Activity, emphasizing their roles in regulating cellular interactions, immune signaling, and stress responses (Fig. 3C). Module 6 was significantly enriched in genes associated with RNA Binding and Ubiquitin–Protein Transferase Inhibitor Activity, highlighting its critical role in RNA processing and regulation of protein ubiquitination. Additionally, genes in Module 4 showed enrichment in functions related to mRNA 5’-UTR Binding and Alpha-Actinin Binding, which are involved in mRNA regulation and cytoskeletal organization. Modules 3 and 7 were enriched for functions such as Ubiquitin Ligase Inhibitor Activity and Protein Binding Involved in Heterotypic Cell-Cell Adhesion, further emphasizing their roles in cellular signaling and adhesion processes. These molecular functions underscore the importance of post-translational modifications, RNA regulation, and cell-cell interactions in pSS pathophysiology, suggesting potential therapeutic targets in these processes.

Cell-Type specific expression of gene modules in Sjögren’s syndrome

Gene expression analysis across various cell types revealed distinct patterns of gene module enrichment, reflecting their roles in pSS. Each module exhibited preferential expression in specific cell types, contributing to the disease’s pathogenesis (Fig. 4A to C). For instance, Module 1, enriched with genes such as GSN and CLU, is linked to cellular structure and extracellular matrix functions. It showed strong expression in epithelial and stromal cells, suggesting its role in maintaining tissue integrity. Module 2, containing immune-related genes like SLPI and PIGR, was predominantly expressed in epithelial cells, highlighting its involvement in immune defense and tissue interactions. Module 3, composed of ribosomal proteins such as RPS27A and RPL21, was expressed in immune cells, indicating its role in protein synthesis and cell metabolism. Module 6, which includes genes like KRT19 and HSPG2, was primarily expressed in epithelial cells, linking it to stress responses and tissue repair. Module 7, associated with cellular metabolism, showed significant expression in plasma cells and myeloid cells, suggesting its role in regulating immune functions and sustaining inflammation.

Discussion

Targeted B cell therapies have emerged as a promising approach for treating Sjögren’s Syndrome (SS), an autoimmune disease characterized by excessive B cell activation, autoantibody production, and chronic inflammation [18]. The central role of the BAFF signaling pathway in B cell survival and activation has made it a key therapeutic target. Elevated levels of BAFF in SS promote the production of autoantibodies, such as anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, which are hallmarks of the disease and contribute to its progression. Belimumab, a BAFF inhibitor, has shown efficacy in reducing autoantibody levels and systemic inflammation, although its benefits as monotherapy are limited. This limitation has led to growing interest in combination therapies. Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, depletes both circulating and glandular B cells, thereby alleviating systemic symptoms and inflammation through mechanisms such as B cell apoptosis and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) [19]. Although effective, its use also underscores the complexity of treating SS, as targeting B cells alone may not sufficiently address the full range of disease mechanisms.

In addition to targeting CD20, other therapeutic strategies focus on interfering with B cell receptor (BCR) signaling. Ibrutinib, a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor, blocks downstream BCR signaling, effectively suppressing B cell activation and autoantibody production [20]. Targeting the CXCL13-CXCR5 axis, which regulates B cell migration and ectopic lymphoid structure formation, represents another promising strategy to mitigate local inflammation by preventing B cell accumulation in glandular tissues [21]. Similarly, Epratuzumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD22, aims to restore inhibitory regulation of B cells, preventing their hyperactivation [22]. These advances in B cell-targeted therapies highlight the potential to modulate the immune system more precisely and effectively in SS.

Through hdWGCNA, we identified several key genes, including POU2AF1, SLAMF7, SPCS2, PDIA6, and CD79A, that play critical roles in B cell dysfunction in SS. POU2AF1, a central regulator of immunoglobulin gene transcription [23], is implicated in the excessive production of autoantibodies, which are a hallmark of pSS. CD79A, a component of the BCR signaling complex, is crucial for sustaining aberrant BCR signaling, which drives chronic inflammation and autoreactive responses. SLAMF7, an immune receptor highly expressed in plasma cells [24], contributes to the inflammatory microenvironment in SS, promoting plasma cell expansion, hyperactive antibody secretion, and glandular damage. Furthermore, SPCS2 [25] and PDIA6 [26], which regulate protein folding and processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), are linked to the elevated secretory activity of plasma cells in pSS. Dysregulation of these genes exacerbates ER stress, enabling sustained antibody production while amplifying tissue damage. These findings underscore the complex interplay between B cell hyperactivation, autoantibody production, inflammation, and ER stress in pSS pathogenesis.

The scRank analysis further refined our understanding of these genes by evaluating their sensitivity across different cell types. Targeting POU2AF1, SLAMF7, and CD79A showed the highest sensitivity in plasma cells, which are critical effectors in autoimmune responses. Given that plasma cells are responsible for secreting autoantibodies, targeting these genes with specific inhibitors could effectively modulate immune activation and alleviate disease progression in pSS. However, the analysis revealed a noteworthy distinction: while SPCS2 and PDIA6 were highly expressed in plasma cells, epithelial cells exhibited the greatest sensitivity to these genes. This finding suggests that SPCS2 and PDIA6 might be more therapeutically relevant in the context of epithelial dysfunction and tissue repair, particularly in the salivary glands, where the pathology of pSS is most prominent. The differential sensitivity across cell types highlights the complex nature of pSS, where immune dysregulation and tissue damage coexist, and suggests that therapies targeting SPCS2 and PDIA6 may be more effective in addressing tissue inflammation and glandular dysfunction.

While targeting SPCS2 and PDIA6 in plasma cells may not be as effective, their therapeutic potential in epithelial cells offers an exciting opportunity to tackle the tissue damage aspect of pSS. The development of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) could offer a solution to this challenge [27]. By designing antibodies that target CD79A and CD79B, which are highly expressed on B cells and plasma cells, and coupling them with inhibitors targeting SPCS2 and PDIA6, it may be possible to selectively deliver these inhibitors to plasma cells, where they can mitigate ER stress and promote tissue repair. This combined approach would allow for precise modulation of both immune activation and plasma cells dysfunction, two major contributors to the pathology of pSS.

In addition to the potential of ADCs, the insights from this study underscore the importance of personalized therapeutic strategies in Sjögren’s Syndrome [28]. The differential sensitivity of B cells and epithelial cells to key genes in Module 5 suggests that therapeutic interventions must be tailored to target specific cell types and disease mechanisms. For instance, plasma cell-targeted therapies such as Rituximab or Ibrutinib may be highly effective for reducing autoimmune responses, while therapies aimed at SPCS2 and PDIA6 could focus on tissue repair and glandular dysfunction. Such tailored treatments would allow for a more comprehensive approach to managing SS, addressing both the immune and tissue aspects of the disease simultaneously.

This study is exploratory and descriptive. The small cohort size and the absence of external replication or protein-level assays limit generalizability and causal interpretation. We therefore frame all associations as hypothesis-generating and anticipate orthogonal validation in future work and larger cohorts.

Ultimately, the combination of targeted immune therapies and tissue-specific interventions represents a promising direction for SS treatment. By combining the power of B cell-targeted therapies with strategies that promote epithelial cell repair, we may be able to reduce the burden of Sjögren’s Syndrome, enhance patient outcomes, and pave the way for more effective, personalized treatments. The development of ADCs and other novel therapeutic strategies, informed by advances in gene expression analysis and targeted drug delivery, could represent a transformative step forward in the treatment of this complex autoimmune disease.
