Sex-specific associations of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations with incident sarcopenia in older adults: A 2-year follow-up study
Authors: Sihan Song, Joong-Yeon Lim, Miji Kim, Chang Won Won, Hyun-Young Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the association of circulating vitamin D levels with incidence of sarcopenia and its diagnostic components in older men and women.DesignTwo-year longitudinal study.SettingNationwide Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study.ParticipantsA total of 1,292 adults (53% women) aged 70–84 years without sarcopenia at baseline and who completed the 2-year follow-up sarcopenia assessment were included.MeasurementsSerum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay and categorized into sex- and blood collection period-specific quartiles. The appendicular skeletal muscle mass was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Muscle strength was evaluated using handgrip strength, and physical performance was assessed using the five-times-sit-to-stand test, usual gait speed, and Short Physical Performance Battery. Sarcopenia was defined in accordance with the 2019 consensus of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia. Logistic regression and generalized linear models were used to assess the association of serum 25(OH)D concentrations with sarcopenia and its components.ResultsOver the 2-year follow-up, incident sarcopenia occurred in 15.7% of men and 12.1% of women. Serum 25(OH)D concentrations were inversely associated with incident sarcopenia in men (odds ratio [OR] for the highest vs. lowest quartile, 0.47; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.23–0.94; P for trend = 0.03), whereas no significant association was observed in women. When examining the association between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and components of sarcopenia, the third quartile was associated with lower odds of low muscle mass in men (OR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.20–0.77) and low physical performance in women (OR, 0.32; 95% CI, 0.15–0.68), compared to the first quartile. Additionally, higher serum 25(OH)D concentrations were associated with more favorable annual changes in usual gait speed in men but not in women.ConclusionLow serum 25(OH)D concentrations are associated with a higher likelihood of incident sarcopenia and its components, particularly among men. Adequate 25(OH)D concentrations may help slow age-related decline in muscle mass and function, with potential sex differences.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized skeletal muscle disorder characterized by accelerated loss of muscle mass and function [1]. The pooled prevalence of sarcopenia in generally healthy older adults ranges from 11% to 35% in men and 2% to 27% in women, depending on the diagnostic criteria and cutoff points used [2]. Sarcopenia is associated with an increased risk of falls and fractures [3], limitations in activities of daily living [4], and mortality [5]. Moreover, this condition is more prevalent in individuals with diseases such as cancer, kidney dysfunction, and liver disease and is associated with poorer survival, more severe complications, and prolonged hospitalizations [6]. Chronic diseases, iatrogenic conditions, poor nutrition, and low physical activity have been suggested as causes of sarcopenia [1]. However, bidirectional relationships exist between sarcopenia and chronic diseases, and evidence of modifiable risk factors remains limited, warranting further investigation [6].

Given the essential role of vitamin D in musculoskeletal health [7], low vitamin D levels have been suggested as a modifiable risk factor for sarcopenia [1,6]. Vitamin D is primarily obtained through cutaneous synthesis upon exposure to ultraviolet B radiation from sunlight and, to a lesser extent, from dietary sources and supplements [8]. Aging reduces the efficiency of vitamin D synthesis in the skin and alters its metabolism, thereby increasing the requirement for vitamin D [9]. Emerging evidence on the role of vitamin D in extraskeletal diseases, such as diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease [10], further supports the need to investigate its potential involvement in sarcopenia. Lower concentrations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the primary circulating form of vitamin D [8], have been observed in individuals with sarcopenia than in those without [[11], [12], [13]]. However, the evidence from prospective studies is limited and inconsistent. In older Australian men, serum concentrations of 25(OH)D and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)₂D], the active form with a short half-life [8], were inversely associated with incident sarcopenia [14], whereas no such association was found in middle-aged or older European men [15]. Among older German adults, low 25(OH)D concentrations were linked to unfavorable changes in muscle mass and physical performance, but not with incident sarcopenia [16]. Additionally, despite several cross-sectional studies having reported sex differences in the association between 25(OH)D concentrations and sarcopenia or its components [[17], [18], [19]], sex-specific evidence from longitudinal studies is limited.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the associations between baseline serum 25(OH)D concentrations and the incidence of sarcopenia over a 2-year follow-up period among community-dwelling older adults, with analyses conducted separately for men and women.

Material and methods

Study population

The Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study (KFACS), a prospective, population-based cohort study of older adults in the Republic of Korea, was designed to identify frailty-related risk factors and develop predictive and prognostic indicators for effective prevention and management [20]. A total of 3,011 older adults (women, 53%) aged 70–84 years were enrolled from 10 centers nationwide between May 2016 and November 2017. The study recruited relatively healthy individuals who were able to visit the study centers, communicate effectively, had not been diagnosed with dementia, and did not have plans to move away within the next 2 years. Baseline data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Follow-up surveys were conducted biannually through center visits, home visits, or telephone interviews. When participants were unable to respond, information was obtained through proxy interviews.

At baseline, 2,400 of the 3,011 participants underwent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Among them, 397 participants (16.5%) did not have DXA data at the follow-up survey (2018–2019), either because they did not participate in the survey (n = 150) or did not undergo DXA at the follow-up (n = 247). Participants without DXA data at the 2-year follow-up were slightly older than those with DXA data (baseline mean age 76.8 vs. 75.8 years) but did not differ by sex and in serum 25(OH)D concentrations, muscle mass index, or the number of comorbidities. Of the 2,003 participants with DXA data at both time points, 216 with knee arthroplasty, limb prostheses, or internal fixation devices (e.g., pins) in the appendicular regions were excluded. In addition, 27 participants with incomplete data on muscle strength or physical function tests required for sarcopenia classification according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 consensus [21] were excluded. Of the 1,760 remaining participants, 109 were excluded due to liver disease, renal disease, moderate to severe kidney dysfunction (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <30 mL/min/1.73 m2); hypercalcemia (albumin-adjusted calcium >10.5 mg/dL); hemiplegia or bilateral paralysis; malnutrition (Short-Form Mini Nutritional Assessment [SF-MNA] score ≤7) or cancer. Finally, 207 men (25.5%) and 152 women (18.1%) with sarcopenia at baseline were excluded from the analysis. This resulted in a final sample of 1,292 participants (53% women) for the analysis (Supplementary Figure S1).

The KFACS protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kyung Hee University Medical Center (No. 2015-12-103). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (No. 2024-02-12).

Measurement of serum 25(OH)D concentrations

Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast between spring and autumn during the baseline survey and transferred to the central clinical laboratory (Seegene Medical Foundation, Seoul, Korea) within 24 h. All blood samples were stored at −80 °C prior to analysis. Baseline serum 25(OH)D concentrations were measured using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (UniCel DxI; Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). The inter-assay coefficients of variation were <10%. The laboratory participated in the College of American Pathologists proficiency testing program.

Assessment of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was defined according to the AWGS 2019 consensus as an age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass accompanied by low muscle strength and/or low physical performance [21]. Detailed methods for assessing sarcopenia in KFACS participants have been described previously [22]. Body composition was measured using DXA devices (Lunar, GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA; or Hologic, Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA, USA). Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was calculated as the sum of the lean mass in the arms and legs (kg), while muscle mass index was calculated as ASM (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Low muscle mass for diagnosing sarcopenia was defined as ASM/height squared <7.0 kg/m2 for men and <5.4 kg/m2 for women. Handgrip strength was assessed using a digital dynamometer (T.K.K.5401; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The highest of two measurements for each hand was defined as the maximum handgrip strength, expressed in kilograms. Low muscle strength for diagnosing sarcopenia was defined as handgrip strength <28 kg for men and <18 kg for women. Physical performance was evaluated using the usual gait speed, five-times sit-to-stand test, and Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). The usual gait speed over a 4-m distance was measured using an automatic gait speed meter (DynamicPhysiology, Daejeon, Korea), with acceleration and deceleration phases of 1.5 m each. Gait speed (m/s) was calculated as the average of two trials. The five-times sit-to-stand test measured the time taken for participants to stand up and sit down five times from a straight-backed chair without using their arms. The SPPB comprises three components: standing balance, gait speed, and repeated chair stands, each scored from 0 to 4, with a total score of 0 to 12 [23]. Low physical performance for diagnosing sarcopenia was defined as usual gait speed <1.0 m/s, five-times sit-to-stand test ≥12 s, or SPPB score ≤9.

Assessment of covariates

Data on demographic and lifestyle factors and medical conditions were obtained using interviewer-administered questionnaires [20]. Participants were recruited from 10 centers nationwide, and residential areas were classified either as metropolitan or non-metropolitan. Education level was categorized into middle school or below and high school or above. Smoking status was classified as never or ever smoked. Participants who had consumed alcohol in the past year were classified as current drinkers. Nutritional status was assessed using the SF-MNA and classified as normal (scores, 12–14) or at risk of malnutrition (scores, 8–11) [24]. Physical activity was assessed using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which includes questions on the frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate, and walking activities during the past 7 days [25,26]. Total physical activity was calculated as metabolic equivalent minutes per week, and participants were classified into three groups (low, moderate, or high) according to the IPAQ scoring protocol [27]. Anthropometric measurements were collected during the physical examination. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight (kg) by the square of the height (m2). The serum concentrations of creatinine, albumin, calcium, and triglycerides were measured, and the creatinine-based eGFR [28] and albumin-adjusted calcium [29] were calculated. Participants self-reported comorbidities that were diagnosed by physicians and were currently present. The number of comorbidities was calculated based on the presence of the following conditions: hypertension, myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis, asthma, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Participants also self-reported the number of prescription medications and non-prescription products (including over-the-counter medications, herbal remedies, and dietary supplements) that they had been using regularly for ≥3 months.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted separately for men and women. To account for seasonal variations in serum 25(OH)D concentrations, quartiles were defined by sex and month of blood collection (April to June, July to September, October to November). Baseline characteristics of men and women are presented according to quartiles of serum 25(OH)Dconcentrations, as means ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the incidence of sarcopenia over 2 years according to serum 25(OH)D quartiles at baseline, using the lowest quartile as the reference. Model 1 was adjusted for age (years). Model 2 was adjusted for age, residential area (metropolitan or non-metropolitan areas), education level (middle school or below, high school or above), current alcohol drinking (no, yes), nutritional status (normal or at risk of malnutrition), physical activity (low, moderate, high), BMI (kg/m2), triglyceride concentration (mg/dL), eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2), albumin-adjusted calcium concentration (mg/dL), number of comorbidities, and number of non-prescription products. Among men, smoking status (never or ever smoked) was also included in the fully adjusted model. The number of prescription drugs was not included in the final model because of its strong correlation with the number of comorbidities. The P for trend was calculated by assigning the median value of each quartile and modeling it as a continuous variable. Associations between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and diagnostic parameters of sarcopenia, including low muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical function, were examined among at-risk participants. Effect modification by sex was assessed in all participants by comparing models with and without the interaction term using the likelihood ratio test. Additionally, the annual percentage change was calculated for ASM/height2 (kg/m2), grip strength (kg), usual gait speed (m/s), and the five-time sit-to-stand test (s). To improve the normality of the annual changes in sarcopenia-related parameters, outliers exceeding three times the interquartile range (IQR) were excluded. Multivariable-adjusted least-squares means (LSmeans) and 95% CIs were estimated across serum 25(OH)D quartiles using generalized linear models. Pairwise differences were assessed using the Bonferroni post hoc test. The main analyses were repeated using two sets of guideline-recommended clinical cut-offs for serum 25(OH)D (<30, 30–<50, and ≥50 nmol/L; <50, 50–<75, and ≥75 nmol/L) [30], to account for sex differences in serum 25(OH)D concentrations, with additional adjustment for the month of blood draw.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value of <0.05. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The median (IQR) serum 25(OH)D concentrations were 60.1 (46.9–74.7) nmol/L among men and 49.3 (36.6–67.2) nmol/L among women. Additionally, 3.6% of men and 12.9% of women had 25(OH)D concentrations <30 nmol/L, indicating vitamin D deficiency. The proportion of participants enrolled between April and June was lower in men (11.4%) than in women (23.8%). In both sexes, participants with higher serum 25(OH)D concentrations were more likely to use non-prescription products. Men with higher 25(OH)D concentrations were more likely to live in non-metropolitan areas. Discussion

In this 2-year prospective study of older Korean adults, we observed sex-specific associations between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and sarcopenia-related outcomes. In men, higher serum 25(OH)D concentrations were associated with a lower incidence of sarcopenia and with a slower decline in gait speed over time. Moderate concentrations of serum 25(OH)D were also associated with lower odds of low muscle mass in men and with lower odds of low physical performance in women.

Vitamin D plays a central role in musculoskeletal health by regulating calcium and phosphate homeostasis [7]. Through interaction with vitamin D receptors in the intestinal mucosa, active vitamin D enhances calcium absorption, which is essential for bone mineralization and neuromuscular function [7,31]. Inadequate vitamin D levels in older populations have been linked to secondary hyperparathyroidism, increased bone turnover, and subsequent bone loss [32]. In our study, significant inverse associations were observed between serum vitamin D concentrations and incident sarcopenia over a 2-year follow-up in men. Similarly, a study of Australian men aged ≥70 years reported significantly higher odds of incident sarcopenia among those in the lowest quartiles of 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)₂D compared to those in the highest quartiles, over 2- and 5-year follow-up periods [14]. Sarcopenia in that study was defined using appendicular lean mass divided by BMI, based on cut-points proposed by the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health Sarcopenia Project [33], and low muscle strength assessed by handgrip strength [34]. In contrast, a study on European men aged 40–79 years found no significant association between these vitamin D markers (per SD) and incident sarcopenia or annual changes in sarcopenia parameters, including appendicular lean mass, gait speed, and grip strength, possibly because of the lower incidence of sarcopenia in this younger cohort [15]. In that study, the incidence of sarcopenia was 3.0% by the International Working Group on Sarcopenia definition [35], and 1.6% by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2010 criteria [36], with a mean follow-up of 4.3 years.

In our study, serum 25(OH)D concentrations were associated with sarcopenia components overall in men, whereas in women, the associations were mainly observed with physical performance. Similar sex-specific findings have been reported in a few prospective studies. In the Rancho Bernardo Study (mean age: 75 years), women in the lowest 25(OH)D quartile had greater declines in the Timed Up and Go test and timed chair stand over 2.5 years compared with those in the highest quartile, but no significant associations were observed among men [37]. In the Newcastle 85+ Study, women in both the lowest and highest season-specific quartiles of 25(OH)D showed slower baseline performance on the Timed Up and Go test but no significant changes over 5 years [38]. In that study, men in the lowest quartile of serum 25(OH)D experienced a greater annual decline in handgrip strength over time than those in the middle quartiles. Additionally, the Tasmanian Older Adult Cohort Study (mean age: 63 years), which collected repeated measurements of serum 25(OH)D and lower limb muscle parameters over 10 years, found that higher average 25(OH)D concentrations were associated with greater muscle mass and strength, and that within-person increases in 25(OH)D concentrations were linked to greater muscle strength [39]. The study also reported that the association between 25(OH)D and muscle mass was stronger in men than in women, which is consistent with our findings.

Ageing disrupts skeletal muscle homeostasis through complex and not yet fully elucidated mechanisms, leading to progressive muscle loss [1]. Sexual dimorphism in skeletal muscle is well established, with men having greater muscle mass and larger individual fibers than women, largely due to endocrine influences [[40], [41], [42]]. In addition, it has been suggested that higher body fat percentages in women compared with men may contribute to greater storage of vitamin D in the adipose tissue [43,44]. Beyond these biological factors, behavioral characteristics affecting vitamin D status and muscle health often differ by sex. In our study, women reported lower levels of physical activity and greater use of non-prescription products, and they had lower serum 25(OH)D concentrations than men. These differences may have contributed to the sex-specific associations observed. Although our observational study should be interpreted with caution, given the relatively short follow-up period and the lower incidence of sarcopenia in women compared with men, the findings support the importance of examining associations between 25(OH)D and sarcopenia in a sex-specific manner.

A recent meta-analysis of population-based studies reported a higher global prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (<30 nmol/L) among women (17.8%; 95% CI, 13.9–21.9) than among men (13.6%; 95% CI, 10.6–16.9) [45]. In our study, the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency, which was also higher in women than in men, was lower than the global summary estimates in both sexes. Prospective studies of older adults have shown that deficient 25(OH)D concentrations (<25 or <30 nmol/L) in older adults are associated with declines in muscle mass [16], muscle strength [46], and physical performance [16,47,48], compared with sufficient concentrations (≥50 or ≥75 nmol/L). The significant associations observed in the middle category for low muscle mass in men and for low physical performance in women may be partly attributable to the relatively higher 25(OH)D concentrations in our study population. These findings may also indicate the presence of an optimal range of 25(OH)D concentrations for muscle health, warranting further investigation.

The strengths of this study include the inclusion of a nationwide sample of community-dwelling older men and women; comprehensive assessment of multiple indicators of sarcopenia, enabling its definition based on the criteria proposed by the AWGS 2019; and inclusion of several lifestyle and health-related variables relevant to sarcopenia. However, this study has some limitations. First, the exclusion of participants without DXA data at the 2-year follow-up may have introduced selection bias, although their baseline characteristics were comparable to those included. Second, body composition was not measured using the same type of DXA device across study centers. Nevertheless, more than 90% of participants were assessed using the same device at both time points, and the device use distribution did not differ across serum 25(OH)D quartiles. Third, the 2-year follow-up period may have been insufficient to capture long-term outcomes. Fourth, due to the observational nature of the study, causal inference is limited, and residual or unmeasured confounding factors may have influenced the observed associations. Although we excluded participants with baseline sarcopenia and major diseases related to vitamin D status, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be ruled out. Finally, because relatively healthy older adults were included, the findings may not be generalizable to a broader older population.

Conclusions

In this 2-year follow-up study of community-dwelling older Korean adults, serum 25(OH)D concentrations were inversely associated with incident sarcopenia and declines in muscle function among men. Moderate concentrations of serum 25(OH)D were also associated with lower odds of low muscle mass in men and with lower odds of low physical performance in women. These findings support the potential sex-specific role of vitamin D in preserving muscle health and underscore the importance of maintaining adequate vitamin D status in older populations. Further longitudinal studies with repeated vitamin D measurements in diverse populations are warranted to clarify the causal relationships and identify the optimal circulating levels for mitigating age-related declines in muscle mass and function in both men and women.
