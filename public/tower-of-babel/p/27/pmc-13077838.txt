The impact of folic acid supplementation in early to mid-pregnancy on the risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy: the Japan environment and children’s study
Authors: Akira Oku, Yoshihiro Saito, Hiroyoshi Iwata, Sachiko Itoh, Ami Hosokawa, Takeshi Yamaguchi, Mariko Itoh, Keitaro Makino, Maki Tojo, Hidemichi Watari, Yoshiya Ito, Yasuaki Saijo, Reiko Kishi, Michihiro Kamijima, Michihiro Kamijima, Shin Yamazaki, Maki Fukami, Reiko Kishi, Chiharu Ota, Koichi Hashimoto, Kenichi Sakurai, Shuichi Ito, Ryoji Shinohara, Hidekuni Inadera, Takeo Nakayama, Ryo Kawasaki, Yasuhiro Takeshima, Hideki Nagashima, Narufumi Suganuma, Mayumi Tsuji, Kimitoshi Nakamura

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are potentially life-threatening conditions for both the pregnant woman and fetus with long-term effects on maternal health, and while folic acid supplementation has been suggested to reduce the risk of HDP, current epidemiological evidence remains inconclusive. This study evaluated the impact of the frequency of folic acid supplementation on the risk of HDP and postpartum hypertension in a large-scale prospective cohort study within the Japanese population.MethodsData were obtained from the national birth cohort of the Japan Environment and Children's Study (JECS), collected between 2011 and 2014. Maternal folic acid supplementation during pregnancy was categorized into three groups (none, low frequency, and high frequency) based on self-reported intake frequency. Crude and adjusted binomial logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between maternal folic acid supplementation during pregnancy and HDP as well as postpartum hypertension. HDP was further classified according to onset timing as early-onset (Eo) or late-onset (Lo), and multinomial logistic regression was applied to examine these subtypes.ResultsA total of 91,069 pregnant women were included in the analysis. High frequency folic acid supplementation was associated with a lower risk of HDP compared with no supplementation (adjusted OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78–0.94), whereas low frequency supplementation showed no significant association. For postpartum hypertension, both low and high frequency folic acid supplementation were associated with reduced risks (adjusted OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79–0.97 and 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.92, respectively). In additional analyses stratified by HDP onset timing, high frequency folic acid supplementation was associated with a lower risk of Lo HDP (adjusted OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78–0.96), but not Eo HDP.ConclusionsFolic acid supplementation during pregnancy was inversely associated with the incidence of HDP, particularly Lo HDP. Furthermore, it may contribute to the prevention of postpartum hypertension. In addition to its established role in preventing neural tube defects, continuous folic acid supplementation before conception may be beneficial for maintaining maternal health throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08898-5.

Background

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), affecting approximately 5–10% of all pregnancies [1], can result in potentially life-threatening complications for both pregnant woman and fetus [2], emphasizing the critical need to identify and understand associated risk factors to improve maternal and perinatal health. Furthermore, women with a history of HDP have an increased risk of developing chronic hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular diseases later in life [3–5]. Persistent hypertension at six weeks postpartum has been linked to elevated blood pressure a decade later [6], highlighting the importance of timely normalization of blood pressure during the postpartum period.

HDP represents a heterogeneous group of conditions, including gestational hypertension (GH) and preeclampsia (PE), which differ in their clinical presentation, severity, and underlying pathophysiology. Risk factors for HDP include maternal factors such as advanced age [7], obesity [8], chronic hypertension [9], renal disease [7, 10], antiphospholipid syndrome [7], diabetes mellitus [11], primiparity [7], long interpregnancy interval [12], and smoking status [13]; pregnancy-related factors such as multiple pregnancy [14] and assisted reproductive technologies [15]; genetic/environmental factors such as family history of HDP [16], hypertension [17], and diabetes mellitus [17]; and nutritional factors such as vitamin D [18] or calcium [19] deficiency and excessive salt intake [20]. As delivery remains the only definitive treatment, prevention through the modification of risk factors is a critical priority.

Folic acid supplementation has gained increasing attention in the field of obstetrics because of its potential protective roles in both fetal and maternal health. Folic acid, a member of the B-vitamin complex, is essential for cell proliferation through its role in DNA synthesis [21] and is involved in homocysteine metabolism [22]. Its metabolic activation facilitates the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine, thereby reducing plasma homocysteine levels. Hyperhomocysteinemia is a well-established risk factors for vascular diseases, including endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and occlusive vascular disorders [23]. During pregnancy, homocysteine-induced endothelial dysfunction in the placenta contributes to the development of HDP [24–26]. Folic acid exerts protective effects against vascular endothelial dysfunction [27], and in vivo studies have demonstrated its role during placental development [28, 29].

Investigation of the association between folic acid and HDP began in 2002, when Hernández-Díaz et al. reported, based on a retrospective cohort study of women, that folic acid supplementation might reduce the risk of GH [30]. A recent meta-analysis [31] published in 2021 assessed the relationship between folic acid supplementation and HDP. Although no significant association was observed between folic acid intake and GH, a modest risk reducing effect was identified for PE. However, the included studies had small sample sizes, wide confidence intervals, and comprised retrospective designs, contributing to considerable heterogeneity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

This study aimed to investigate the association between the frequency of folic acid supplementation and HDP using a large-scale birth cohort in Japan to provide robust and reliable evidence. In addition, the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS) enabled assessment of the association between folic acid supplementation and postpartum hypertension by leveraging its birth cohort design to capture maternal outcomes after delivery.

Methods

Study design

The present study was based on data obtained from the JECS, an ongoing nationwide prospective birth cohort study. Participants were recruited between 2011 and 2014 from 15 Regional Centres across the country, comprising over 100,000 pregnancies. The detailed methodology of JECS has been described elsewhere [32, 33]. During the recruitment period, the JECS covered approximately 45% of all births in the Study Areas. The eligibility criteria for participation were as follows: (1) residence within one of the Study Areas at the time of recruitment and registration at a Co-operating Health Care Providers; (2) an expected delivery date on or after August 1, 2011; and (3) the ability to comprehend Japanese and complete self-administered questionnaires.

Data collection

This study utilized the JECS dataset released in January 2025. The data used in the analysis consisted of the following four categories of information: (1) self-administered questionnaires completed during early pregnancy, which included basic maternal information and a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ); (2) self-administered questionnaires completed during mid-to-late pregnancy, which collected socioeconomic data such as maternal educational background and household income; (3) obstetric outcomes and maternal medical background data transcribed from medical records at the Co-operating Health Care Providers where each participant received care; and (4) maternal blood test results collected during mid-to-late pregnancy.

Pregnancy-related information, including parity, use of fertility treatments, history of HDP, diabetes mellitus, antiphospholipid syndrome, and smoking status was obtained from self-administered questionnaires completed at the time of JECS enrollment. Data on folic acid intake, including the frequency and timing of folic acid supplement use, as well as dietary folic acid intake, were also derived from the same questionnaire. Dietary folic acid intake was assessed using the FFQ included in the self-administered survey [34]. The average gestational age at the time of questionnaire completion was 16.3 (SD 7.5) weeks. Maternal age, height, pre-pregnancy weight, history of recurrent pregnancy loss (including low-dose aspirin use), maternal comorbidities (e.g., hypertension and kidney disease), and blood pressure data from the postpartum check-up were transcribed from medical records. Socioeconomic status variables, such as household income and educational attainment, were derived from self-administered questionnaires completed during mid-to-late pregnancy. In addition, data on maternal serum folate concentrations (ng/mL) measured during mid-to-late pregnancy (mean gestational age: 27.2 [SD 3.2] weeks) were collected. Serum folate levels were measured using an Access Folate Assay Kit (FOLW; SRL Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) [35].

Participants

The study population comprised mothers of children who were registered in the fixed dataset at the age of six years of age. Participants were included if they met this criterion and were excluded if they had multiple pregnancies, or missing data related to these conditions. Additionally, mothers were excluded if data for any variables required for model construction were missing.

Definitions of HDP and postpartum hypertension

Information on HDP was transcribed from medical records. The dataset indicated whether participants had been diagnosed with HDP but did not specify the subtypes (i.e., GH, PE, superimposed preeclampsia, or eclampsia). In this study, HDP was defined as the onset of hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) occurring after 20 weeks of gestation and resolving by 12 weeks postpartum, with or without proteinuria (≥ 300 mg/day), or as the presence of eclampsia [36].

Based on the timing of onset, cases diagnosed before 34 gestational weeks were classified as early-onset (Eo) HDP, and those diagnosed at or after 34 gestational weeks were classified as late-onset (Lo) HDP.

Postpartum hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg at the 1-month postpartum check-up.

Assessment of folic acid supplement intake

Information on folic acid intake was collected via a self-administered questionnaire. Detailed information regarding the exact dose and formulation of folic acid supplements (folic acid alone versus multivitamin preparations) was not available in the JECS questionnaire.

Participants were asked, “Are you currently taking folic acid as a supplement?” with the following response options: (1) not taking it, (2) once a month, (3) two to three times a month, (4) one to three times a week, (5) four to six times a week, (6) once daily, and (7) more than once daily. Based on the reported frequency of intake, participants were categorized into three groups: none (1), low frequency (2–4), and high frequency (5–7). The cutoff for high frequency supplementation was selected with reference to previous studies in which adherence of approximately 80% or greater was used to define regular supplement use [37].

The pathophysiology of HDP may be partly determined during the early to mid-pregnancy placentation period [38]. Therefore, the primary exposure was defined as the frequency of folic acid supplement intake based on the questionnaire at enrollment. Maternal serum folate concentrations measured in mid-to-late pregnancy were used to assess folate status and validate self-reported intake but were not included in the analytical model, as they may fall outside the critical etiological window.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed to evaluate the basic characteristics of the mothers. Subsequently, crude and adjusted multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted, with the development of HDP and postpartum hypertension as outcome variables. For additional analyses, HDP onset timing was categorized as Eo or Lo, and adjusted multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed. The primary independent variables included maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), presence of comorbid internal medical conditions (diabetes mellitus, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, chronic hypertension, and kidney disease), aspirin use during pregnancy, history of HDP, primiparity, assisted reproductive technology (ART) conception, educational background, smoking status, dietary folic acid intake (assessed by FFQ), timing of initiation of folic acid supplementation (≥ 1 year before conception, < 12 gestational weeks, ≥ 12 gestational weeks), and frequency of folic acid supplement use. Covariates for logistic regression analyses were selected based on a directed acyclic graph (DAG) constructed using the R package “Dagitty” [39] (Additional File 1) for minimal adjustment. Maternal age, ART conception, BMI, education, chronic hypertension, kidney disease, smoking status and primiparity were identified as confounders between folic acid supplementation and HDP. Household income was excluded due to correlation with education. Past HDP history and low-dose aspirin use were excluded as risk indicators or causal pathway variables. Maternal age was categorized into two groups using 35 years as the cutoff for advanced maternal age. BMI was classified into three categories: < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, and ≥ 25 kg/m2. Educational background was divided into two categories based on years of schooling: ≤ 12 years and > 12 years. Smoking status was categorized into two groups: those who continued smoking during pregnancy, and those who had smoked previously or quit after becoming aware of their pregnancy. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

In logistic regression analysis, the number of events in the smaller outcome group should be at least ten times the number of variables included in the model [40]. In this study, eight main and covariate variables were planned for inclusion in the logistic regression model, indicating that a minimum of 80 participants was required. Even in the smallest group (low frequency), there were 366 and 451 cases in the HDP and postpartum hypertension groups, respectively, which was considered sufficient for the planned analyses.

As a sensitivity analysis, we evaluated the associations between folic acid supplementation frequency and HDP and postpartum hypertension using multiple imputation, including participants with missing covariates that were adjusted for in the model.

Results

This study included 91,069 pregnant women from the JECS. A total of 104,043 fetal records were available in the JECS between 2011 and 2014. Of these, 1,994 women with multiple pregnancies were excluded. In addition, 10,980 women with missing data on confounding factors were excluded from the analysis. Discussion

This study represents the first large-scale investigation in Japan to examine the association between folic acid supplementation and HDP. Folic acid supplementation during early to mid-pregnancy was associated with a reduced risk of HDP. Specifically, a significant reduction was observed in the risk of Lo HDP, whereas no statistically significant reduction was observed for Eo HDP. Additionally, folic acid supplementation was associated with a lower risk of postpartum hypertension. The findings of sensitivity analyses suggested that this association was generally robust. Moreover, similar results were obtained when the main analysis was repeated using a binary exposure definition, comparing women with high frequency folic acid supplementation to those with low frequency or no supplementation (Additional file 3).

While crude HDP incidence was similar across folic acid supplementation categories, women with higher supplementation had more HDP risk factors like advanced maternal age and ART conception. After adjusting for these confounders, folic acid supplementation was associated with lower HDP risk, supporting its protective role during pregnancy.

The observed associations were statistically significant, but of modest magnitude of the effect, and the absolute incidence of HDP was low in this population. Therefore, the clinical impact of folic acid supplementation at the individual level may be limited. However, given the high prevalence of folic acid supplementation during pregnancy and the large number of pregnancies at the population level, even a modest relative risk reduction could translate into a meaningful reduction in the overall burden of HDP. Moreover, folic acid supplementation is inexpensive and already recommended for other maternal and fetal health benefits, which may enhance its potential public health relevance despite the modest effect size.

In the present study, folic acid supplementation was associated with a reduced risk of Lo HDP, which corresponds to GH and is believed to share a similar pathophysiological mechanism [41–43]. This finding aligns with that of Hernández-Díaz et al., who observed that folic acid supplementation was associated with a reduced risk of GH [30], supporting its potential protective benefit. However, that study was limited by its retrospective design and reliance on self-reported GH diagnoses. In contrast, Li et al. found no preventive effect of folic acid on GH [44]; however, their retrospective design and limited control for confounders suggest that differences in study design may partly explain the discrepancy. Similarly, Oken et al. reported no association between folic acid intake (from diet and supplements) and either GH or PE [45]; however, their study may have been underpowered because of its smaller sample size, differing from the conditions of the present analysis. Li et al. reported an increased risk of GH with folic acid intake [46]; however, the use of differing diagnostic criteria for GH (130/80 mmHg) and a high disease incidence in that population limit direct comparison with the current findings. Timmermans et al. found no clear association between folic acid supplementation and HDP [47], although low event numbers and multiple adjustment factors may have influenced their analysis. Taken together, these inconsistencies across previous reports and the present study suggest that variations in study design and evaluation criteria contribute to the inconsistent findings in this field.

Regarding Eo HDP, this study did not demonstrate a clear preventive effect of folic acid supplementation. This finding contrasts with some reports [48, 49] indicating that high-dose folic acid (4–5 mg) may be effective in preventing severe cases. The present results warrant further investigation, particularly considering that in this cohort, only approximately 10% of women initiated folic acid supplementation before pregnancy, generally at relatively low doses. Such limited intake may have resulted in insufficient elevation of maternal plasma folate levels during the critical period of placental formation. The biological significance of folate during placentation has been supported by in vivo studies, suggesting that the timing and dosage of supplementation could critically determine its efficacy. In our study, compared with preconception supplement intake, post-conception supplementation showed no significant association with HDP (adjusted OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.84–1.18).

Further studies are needed to elucidate the potential protective effects of folic acid against Eo HDP.

Folic acid supplementation during pregnancy has been associated with reduced risk of HDP, particularly Lo HDP. This association may reflect differences in HDP subtypes' pathophysiology. Eo HDP and PE are primarily driven by abnormal placentation [50, 51]. However, our classification could not distinguish GH from PE, suggesting Lo HDP encompasses heterogeneous disorders. Lo HDP is characterized by maternal cardiovascular and endothelial dysfunction, often without placental insufficiency [41–43]. Folic acid may enhance maternal vascular endothelial function through homocysteine-lowering pathways, making it more relevant to Lo HDP than early placental development. Further studies with placental and vascular biomarkers are needed to elucidate these mechanisms.

At the one-month postpartum checkup, postpartum hypertension was lower in the folic acid supplement group. No large-scale studies have examined the association between postpartum hypertension and folic acid supplementation. While Olapeju et al. reported low postpartum folate levels in women with preeclampsia [52], blood pressure outcomes were not evaluated. Women who develop HDP have higher risk of postpartum hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellites, and cardiovascular diseases [3–5], being three to six times more likely to develop chronic hypertension [53]. Elevated blood pressure at six weeks postpartum has been linked to long-term blood pressure increases [6]. The lower postpartum hypertension in our folic acid supplement group suggests potential benefits for women's long-term cardiovascular health. In Japan, folic acid supplementation is recommended before and during early pregnancy, yet only about one-third of women in our cohort reported taking supplements. Since Japan lacks mandatory food fortification, intake depends on individual behavior. Our findings highlight the need to actively promote folic acid supplementation among women planning pregnancy.

The strengths of this study include its large cohort size (104,043 records) and prospective study design. The consistency between complete case and multiple imputation analyses, with minimal change in eligible participants relative to the JECS population, supports our analytic approach. Despite these strengths, residual selection bias from missing data remains possible, potentially affecting result generalizability. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the HDP classification did not distinguish between GH and PE because the data analyzed in this study were collected between 2011 and 2014, whereas the subclassification of HDP in Japan was revised in 2018. Second, it remains unclear whether the reported folic acid supplementation consisted of folic acid alone or as part of a multivitamin preparation, and the other nutrients were not included in our regression analyses. In addition, the exact dose and form of folic acid supplementation were unavailable; however, given the recommended dose in Japan (0.4 mg/day), intake likely reflected a low-dose regimen, with any misclassification expected to be non-differential. Third, although folic acid is thought to exert protective effects on vascular endothelial cells by lowering homocysteine levels [54], homocysteine concentrations were not measured in this study, and thus this mechanistic inference remains indirect. Furthermore, genetic variations affecting folic acid metabolism were not considered in this cohort. Fourth, postpartum hypertension was assessed at 1-month checkups. Cases up to 6 weeks postpartum may have been missed. Blood pressure measurements varied across sites, and missing antihypertensive treatment data may have affected interpretation. Future large-scale prospective studies incorporating information on ethnicity, timing and dosage of folic acid supplementation, dietary patterns, and objective biomarkers including serum folate and homocysteine levels are warranted to more directly investigate these underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

Folic acid supplementation during early to mid-pregnancy was associated with a reduced risk of HDP. In particular, supplementation was linked to a decreased risk of Lo HDP and postpartum hypertension, whereas no significant effect was observed for Eo HDP. These beneficial effects may reflect the protective role of folic acid supplementation against maternal vascular endothelial dysfunction.

In light of the observed reduction in postpartum hypertension, folic acid supplementation during pregnancy might be associated with maternal cardiovascular health later in life; however, this association requires further investigation.

Along with providing accurate information on preconception folic acid supplementation, these findings suggest that maintaining folic acid intake during pregnancy may help reduce the incidence of HDP and postpartum hypertension, potentially through protection of the maternal vascular endothelium.
