BMA-YOLO: an object detection model for microscopic images of mouse embryos
Authors: Yingsai Zhao, Weijun Zeng, Yuan Wang, Jiawei Wu, Weiguo Liang, Linyu Xu, Haixuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In vitro embryo culture is a pivotal technology in life sciences and medical research. However, automated monitoring remains challenging due to factors such as bubble interference and the frequent omission of small or peripheral embryos. To overcome these limitations, this study introduces BMA-YOLO, an enhanced model built upon the YOLOv8 architecture. By integrating BiFormer and Multi-Scale Attention Aggregation (MSAA) mechanisms, the model strengthens its capacity for feature extraction and multi-level feature fusion. Furthermore, a detection head specifically designed to capture small objects is incorporated to improve sensitivity toward diminutive embryos. Experimental results on a private embryo dataset demonstrate that BMA-YOLO achieves an mAP50 of 96.94%, an mAP50-95 of 69.26%, and a recall rate of 94.20%. Compared with the baseline model, these results correspond to improvements of 3.57%, 3.30%, and 6.69%, respectively. BMA-YOLO effectively mitigates bubble interference and significantly enhances the detection performance for small and peripheral embryos, providing robust algorithmic support for subsequent automated monitoring systems.

Introduction

In vitro embryo culture1 is a fundamental technique in life science and biomedical research, with broad applications in assisted reproduction2, embryonic development studies3, and toxicological evaluation4. Its success depends on maintaining an optimal growth environment5 and enabling researchers to monitor developmental stages in real-time to facilitate timely strategic adjustments6. Therefore, accurate and efficient localization of embryos in complex culture environments is essential for downstream automated observation and analysis.

During post-implantation development, embryos undergo significant volumetric changes7. Taking the mouse embryo as a model, the blastocyst diameter at embryonic day 4.5 is only approximately 0.1 mm, whereas by day 12.5, the size increases to several millimeters. Consequently, single-scale imaging modalities can no longer meet observation requirements. As embryo volume expands, determining the spatial coordinates of embryos across multiple scales becomes a prerequisite for experimental monitoring. This study presents a multi-scale imaging and monitoring technique designed to resolve the challenges of 3D localization within the culture micro-space. This technique employs a low-magnification, wide-field objective to perform bottom-up, layer-by-layer scanning of the culture area to acquire tomographic images. Then the images are analyzed using an object detection model to estimate the planar coordinates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(x,y)\:$$\end{document}of each embryo. These estimates also provide a necessary foundation for future efforts to recover the vertical coordinate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(z\right)$$\end{document}, for example through image clarity evaluation.

Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown strong performance in object detection tasks for natural scenes8–10. However, their direct application to embryo monitoring remains challenging due to several key factors. In practical culture environments, perfusion is commonly used to better approximate physiological conditions, as it facilitates mass transfer and introduces fluid shear stress. This process inevitably produces bubbles of varying sizes, which can be mistakenly identified as embryos and thus lead to false positives. In addition, early-stage embryos are small and tend to adhere to the culture dish walls, leading to a high rate of missed detections.

In object detection, the YOLO (You Only Look Once) series11–13 has been widely used as a representative family of one-stage detectors, making it particularly appealing for automated monitoring tasks. However, its application to embryo monitoring remains limited. Among existing variants, YOLOv814 is a commonly adopted as a baseline in contemporary computer vision tasks, owing to its relatively simple architecture, strong performance, and flexible modular design. Using a well-established framework like YOLOv8 improves reproducibility and provides a solid basis for evaluating the proposed BMA-YOLO model. To address false positives and missed detections in embryo identification, we develop BMA-YOLO as an enhanced variant of YOLOv8. It incorporates the BiFormer attention mechanism15 and Multi-Scale Attention Aggregation (MSAA)16, and introduces an additional detection head for small objects. Related work

Early cell detection methods largely relied on morphological analysis. For instance, H.T. Madhloom et al.17 exploited the distinct chromatic signatures of lymphoblasts within the HSV color space to distinguish them from blood smear backgrounds. By coupling HSV transformation with contour detection, they enabled localized cellular extraction. But these methods are intuitive, their reliance on handcrafted features limits their robustness under complex imaging conditions.

Recent advancements in object detection have been largely catalyzed by Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)18. Current architectures typically diverge into two paradigms: two-stage frameworks, represented by R-CNN19 and its successor, Faster R-CNN20, this methodology initially generates a series of sparse, high-potential region proposals via a Region Proposal Network (RPN), followed by feature pooling, category prediction, and bounding box regression on these localized areas; and one-stage algorithms, such as YOLO11 and SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector)21. Their core characteristic lies in the omission of the region proposal step, enabling simultaneous object classification and localization through a single forward pass. More recent work has explored Transformer22-based designs. For example, DETR23 removes the need for anchor boxes and non-maximum suppression, and thereby simplifies the overall pipeline. Object detection has demonstrated immense potential across diverse scientific and engineering disciplines; advanced detection algorithms and attention mechanisms have been successfully deployed in high-resolution remote sensing24,25, aerial vehicle tracking26,27, and driver state monitoring system28.

Within cell image analysis, deep convolutional neural networks have been applied in a variety of ways. Rahat I. S. et al.29 compared several deep learning frameworks for automated blood smear analysis, showing their effectiveness in handling complex cell morphologies. Focusing on blood cell detection, Chen X. et al.30 improved YOLOv8 by introducing a Space-to-Depth Convolution (SPD-Conv) module to better preserve low-level details. They also incorporated a BiFPN to strengthen feature fusion, leading to improved detection and classification performance. In a different line of work, M. Liang et al.31 combined traditional image processing with deep learning. By integrating the Shearlet Transform with a Multiscale Feature Fusion CNN (MFF-CNN), their method achieved strong performance in classifying colon pathological images.

Advanced feature integration strategies have increasingly been leveraged to address instance-level complexities in embryonic imaging. To mitigate the errors arising from cell overlap, Liu et al.32 introduced a Sparse Cascade segmentation network, which utilizes a Multi-Stage Cascaded Fusion (MSCF) architecture and a Shape Dictionary Guidance (SCG) strategy to refine boundary delineation. For YOLOv8-based embryo detection, Talha et al.33 developed the YOLOv8-C2fCA model. By embedding a Coordinate Attention (CA) mechanism into the standard C2f module, they construct a more spatially-aware feature representation. The model improves detection accuracy on both private and public embryo datasets while remaining lightweight. Although these methods have achieved promising results, they do not explicitly address the challenges posed by bubble formation or the missed detection of embryos located near the dish boundaries during the culture process.

Methodology

The framework of the BMA-YOLO algorithm proposed in this study is as shown in Fig. 1. The model follows a modular design and consists of three main components: the Backbone, the Neck, and the Head.

The Backbone network processes the input images to generate feature maps with five distinct scales, denoted as P1 through P5, as shown in Fig. 2. In the original YOLOv8, C2f modules based on standard convolutions are effective for capturing local patterns. As the network deepens, their ability to model global context remains limited despite the increase in receptive field. To address this issue, we replace the C2f modules with BiFormer blocks. With its Bi-Level Routing Attention (BRA), BiFormer applies a content-adaptive sparse attention mechanism to allocate attention more effectively. And an MSAA module is inserted after each Concat operation to further refine the fused features. It enhances responses around key boundaries, leading to more stable predictions and fewer false positives. The fused and recalibrated features are passed to the detection heads. We add an extra head for small objects, allowing the network to better use high-resolution features and capture fine details. The multi-scale heads then produce predictions including bounding box coordinates, confidence scores, and class probabilities.

BiFormer attention mechanism

In in vitro culture environments, embryo imaging is often affected by bubbles of different sizes and condensation on the dish walls, posing challenges for accurate detection and localization. The C2f module in YOLOv8 is effective at capturing local features but has limited ability to model long-range dependencies. Transformer-based models use self-attention to capture global context and can address this limitation. However, full self-attention is computationally expensive and memory-intensive, especially for high-resolution images. To balance accuracy and efficiency, we introduce the BiFormer attention mechanism. BiFormer is a vision Transformer framework based on BRA, as shown in Fig. 3. MSAA attention mechanism

In Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs), high-level features capture rich semantic information but often lack precise spatial details, while shallow features preserve positional accuracy but carry limited semantic context. During fusion, fine details of small objects or boundaries can be lost. To address this, we introduce the MSAA module, which uses an attention mechanism to recalibrate fused features, directing the model to focus on channels and spatial locations important for detection.

The architecture of the MSAA module is as shown in Fig. 4. In the spatial refinement pathway, 1 × 1 convolutions reduce channel dimensions to lower computational cost. Multiple \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\times\:3$$\end{document} convolutions capture features at different scales and combine them. Pooling and convolution are applied to further strengthen target features, then a Sigmoid function is employed to generate spatial attention weights. In the parallel channel aggregation pathway, spatial information is compressed across channels, followed by a non-linear transformation using 1 × 1 convolutions to produce channel attention weights. These weights are fused with the spatially refined features, achieving joint optimization across both spatial and channel dimensions.

Due to the strong feature aggregation of the MSAA module, it is inserted after the Concat operations in the neck to enhance the representation of multi-scale fused features. This design improves detection performance for targets at multiple scales, especially small objects and fine boundaries.

Small objects detection head

Analysis of the embryo dataset shows that early-stage embryos occupy very few pixels in the images. The baseline YOLOv8 model struggles to recognize such small targets, resulting in frequent missed detections. To accommodate these scale variations, we extend the original detection architecture with an additional head dedicated to small objects, forming a four-head detection framework. This design leverages higher-resolution feature maps, which preserve richer spatial details and contour information, enhancing the network’s sensitivity to small embryos.

In practice, The module applies convolution to the Backbone’s highest-resolution P2 feature layer to extract fine-grained features for small targets. These features are fused with upsampled deep-level features to perform cross-scale integration. The fused features are then processed by a CSP block for deeper feature aggregation, enhancing representation. Based on these high-resolution, multi-scale features, the additional small-target detection head generates the final predictions. As shown in Fig. 5, the architecture bifurcates to balance deep feature extraction via a Bottleneck series against a spatial-preserving skip connection. By employing 3 × 3 transition convolutions and Concat operations, we not only maintain a robust gradient flow but also mitigate the computational overhead inherently associated with processing high-resolution inputs.

Experimental results

Dataset

The embryo image dataset used in this study is a private dataset created to monitor embryonic development and capture dynamic processes. The dataset comprises a total of 594 images, each with a resolution of 5120 × 5120. The images were acquired between June 14, 2025, and August 30, 2025, covering three independent culture batches. The duration of embryo culture varied across batches. Embryo images were acquired once daily, with nine images captured at different focal planes for each grid location during every acquisition, aiming to capture diverse samples under various culture conditions, The image acquisition details are summarized in Table 1, and the collected images are shown in Fig. 6. To ensure reliable evaluation and generalizability, the dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test subsets with a ratio of 7:2:1. Since each nine-image group forms a cohesive set, the actual distribution was 423, 117, and 54 images, respectively. All images were manually annotated with LabelMe for the object detection task. All animal procedures are approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Suzhou Institute of Biomedical Engineering and Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Experimental environment and evaluation metrics

Experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU using Python 3.8, PyTorch 1.2.0, and CUDA 11.8. All models were trained for 200 epochs with input images resized to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:640\times\:640$$\end{document}. SGD was used as the optimizer (learning rate 0.01, momentum 0.937, weight decay 0.0005), and no pre-trained weights were used to ensure fair comparison.

The primary objective of this study is to improve the detection precision of the network model. Therefore, the following evaluation metrics were selected for this experiment: Precision (P), Recall (R), and mean Average Precision (mAP), where mAP includes mAP50 and mAP50-95.

Precision (P) is the ratio of correctly predicted positive samples to all samples predicted as positive. Its calculation method is shown in Formula (6):

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$$\end{document}

Recall (R) is the proportion of true positive samples correctly identified relative to the total number of actual positives. Its calculation method is shown in Formula (7):

7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$\end{document}

Where TP and TN represent the counts of correctly predicted positive and negative samples, respectively. FP counts negative samples incorrectly predicted as positive, and FN counts positive samples misclassified as negative.

Average Precision (AP) is the area under the Precision-Recall (P-R) curve. Its calculation method is shown in Formula (8):

8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:AP=\underset{0}{\overset{1}{\int\:}}Precision\left(Recall\right)d\left(Recall\right)$$\end{document}

Mean Average Precision (mAP) is the arithmetic mean of (AP) across all object categories. Its calculation method is shown in Formula (9):

9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:mAP=\frac{1}{N}{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{N}A{P}_{i}$$\end{document}

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document} denotes the AP for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document}-th category, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{N}$$\end{document} is the total number of categories. Specifically, mAP50 measures precision at a fixed IoU threshold of 0.5, while mAP50-95 averages it over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 (step 0.05).

In practical deployment, the resource overhead of the model must be taken into consideration. To evaluate the practical application potential of our model, this paper introduces FPS, Parameters, and GFLOPs as metrics for assessing computational efficiency. FPS reflects inference speed, higher is better. Parameters represent the model’s size; a lower parameter count signifies reduced memory occupancy, facilitating easier deployment on embedded devices or mobile terminals. GFLOPs measure computational complexity, where smaller values imply more efficient execution and reduced hardware demand.

Ablation test

To quantify the contribution of each core component in BMA-YOLO, ablation studies were conducted under controlled settings. The experimental results are presented in Table 2.

BiFormer module. The integration of the BiFormer module catalyzed a substantial gain in mAP50, reaching 95.71%, a 2.34% improvement relative to the baseline. Such elevation underscores the efficacy of the BRA mechanism in augmenting feature extraction. While Recall advanced to 89.86%, a marginal contraction in Precision (88.43%) was observed. This decline might be attributed to the stronger feature representation power leading to more predicted boxes, which are simultaneously accompanied by a higher rate of false positives.

MSAA Module. The inclusion of the MSAA module resulted in an mAP50 of 94.62% (an increase of 1.25%). The most significant contribution of this module was its Recall of 90.58%, which was the highest Recall achieved among all single-module improvements. This validates that MSAA’s optimized spatial and channel fusion of multi-scale features within the Neck network helps the model locate more objects, especially those with subtle features or small objects.

Small Object Detection Head. The addition of the small object detection head alone resulted in a modest change in mAP50 (93.38%). However, its Precision reached the highest value of 95.03%, while Recall slightly decreased (86.96%). We acknowledge that the original intent of incorporating the small object detection head was to enhance the capture of tiny targets, which theoretically should significantly improve Recall. The observed slight decrease in Recall, therefore, appears counter-intuitive. Upon re-evaluating the experimental process, we attribute this phenomenon to the fact that the small object detection head operates on high-resolution feature maps characterized by shallow semantic information. While these maps offer rich spatial details, they also carry substantial background noise. Without the guidance of deep semantic context or an attention mechanism, the model struggles to differentiate between authentic small targets and noise. This leads to an influx of low-quality candidate boxes, which may inadvertently suppress correct detections during the NMS stage, thereby reducing Recall. When combined with the BiFormer and MSAA modules, the Recall improved significantly. This further validates the synergistic necessity of integrating high-resolution spatial details with deep semantic guidance for effective detection in complex backgrounds.

To explore the synergistic effect among modules, we introduced the BiFormer and MSAA modules, resulting in a substantial performance leap. The mAP50 further improved to 96.70%, and the mAP50-95 reached 69.79%, representing a significant improvement compared to the baseline model’s 65.96%. When BiFormer, MSAA, and the small-object detection head are jointly applied, the model attains its best performance, reaching an mAP50 of 96.94% and a Recall of 94.20%.

In summary, the ablation study results demonstrate that the proposed BiFormer, MSAA, and small object detection head modules all contribute positively to enhancing the performance of YOLOv8 on the embryo dataset. The final complete model, integrating all proposed improvements, proves its superiority and effectiveness with an optimal mAP50 of 96.94% and a Recall of 94.20%.

Comparison test

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed model, we conducted comparative experiments against current mainstream object detection models. The comparison included several representative models, such as RT-DETR34, various versions of the YOLO series8,35–38 and YOLOv8-C2fCA33. All models used the same private embryo dataset for training and testing. The experimental results are presented in Table 3.

Overall, the model proposed in this paper exhibits superior performance across multiple key metrics. BMA-YOLO secured the top rank in both mAP50 (96.94%) and mAP50-95 (69.26%). Most notably, our model’s Recall reached 94.20%, substantially outpacing other frameworks and ensuring an exceptionally low false-negative rate in embryo localization. While the Precision (92.62%) slightly trails YOLOv10 and RT-DETR, this reflects a deliberate trade-off to prioritize target capture over strict filtering. Ultimately, our model achieved the best score of 69.26% on the more comprehensive metric, mAP50-95, proving its stable and outstanding detection performance.

Regarding the three metrics for measuring computational efficiency, BMA-YOLO achieves a performance of 625 FPS, 2.7 M parameters, and 7.1 GFLOPs. Although the FPS is slightly lower than the 666.7 FPS of the fastest model, YOLOv11, and the parameters and GFLOPs are higher than the 1.7 M of YOLOv9 and 5.8 of YOLOv5 respectively, the model’s computational efficiency fully satisfies the real-time requirements for embryo development monitoring. This paper contends that the moderate increase in computational load is a necessary investment for integrating the BiFormer module and the small-target detection head, allowing the model to strike a balance between efficiency and detection accuracy.

Conclusion

To mitigate the pervasive challenges of bubble interference, small early-stage embryos, and large scale variations in in vitro culture, this study introduced BMA-YOLO as a high-performance evolution of the YOLOv8 architecture. By integrating BiFormer, MSAA module and a specialized small-object detection head, the framework achieves a synergistically enhanced capacity for global dependency modeling and fine-grained feature recalibration. Rigorous ablation and comparative analyses validate the efficacy of these structural innovations. Specifically, the BRA mechanism within BiFormer extends the network’s spatial reach, while the MSAA module and the auxiliary detection head jointly fortify the model against the loss of diminutive morphological details. These optimizations culminated in BMA-YOLO securing the top rank across three pivotal benchmarks, including mAP50 (96.94%), mAP50-95 (69.26%), and recall (94.20%). These results demonstrate that the model provides more accurate and comprehensive localization of embryos in complex images, effectively addressing missed and false detections.

Although this study achieved promising results, future research could explore several directions. First, the model’s computational efficiency could be improved to enable deployment on resource-limited platforms, such as embedded devices. Second, the dataset used in this study contains only 594 images, and the relatively small scale of the dataset presents a certain limitation. Future work could utilize larger-scale and multi-source embryo datasets for more extensive validation to ensure the model’s generalizability across diverse imaging conditions and laboratory settings. Third, the detection framework could be combined with IQA techniques to more accurately localize embryos in three-dimensional space.
