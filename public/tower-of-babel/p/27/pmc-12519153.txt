Host dietary niche and site location on the river continuum shape trematode (Renifer aniarum) infection patterns in sympatric watersnakes (Nerodia spp.)
Authors: MJ Janecka, DR Clark, O Duthoy, CD Criscione
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough parasite probability of infection and intensity are central to understanding parasite distributions and their ecological and evolutionary impacts, the drivers of these parameters remain poorly understood. At a local scale, we examined how host dietary niche partitioning and river landscape processes influence infections of the trematode Renifer aniarum in a community of watersnakes (Nerodia spp.). Host-specific dietary preferences and body size (SVL) were associated with infection probability among sympatric congeners. However, contrary to predictions of the stream drift hypothesis, infection probability increased with distance upstream. Infection intensity, in contrast, was unrelated to stream position and less predictable across host species. Thus, infection probability and intensity were heterogeneous among host species and across the river system. These results highlight that, even at local scales, multiple factors can distinctly shape the infection dynamics of a generalist parasite across closely related, co-occurring hosts.Keywords: Infection parameters, Dietary niche, River continuum, Stream drift, Renifer aniarum, Nerodia spp, Watersnake

Introduction

Data on factors associated with the distribution of a parasite species across hosts, i.e., prevalence (percentage of hosts infected; Bush et al., 1997) and intensity (number of parasites in a host; Bush et al., 1997), are essential. These distributional parameters underlie key ecological and evolutionary interactions between hosts and parasites, influencing the fitness costs of infection for hosts and shaping parasite competition, transmission, reproductive success, and genetic diversity (Poulin, 2007; Mideo, 2009; Hulke and Criscione, 2024; Detwiler et al., 2017; Martins and Poulin, 2024). Moreover, data on fine-scale factors related to infection patterns are essential for informing possible applications such as epidemiological/epizoological control, host or parasite conservation, and model development of host-parasite adaptive evolution in complex landscapes (Carlson et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2020; Walsman et al., 2022; Lion and Gandon, 2016; Fernandes et al., 2019). Nevertheless, as exemplified by a question posed by Martins and Poulin (2024) in their recent meta-analysis - “Why are different populations of the same parasite species achieving different prevalence or infection intensity in different host populations?” - gaps remain in our understanding of what shapes variation in parasite prevalence and intensity. We return to the findings of Martins and Poulin (2024) in the discussion but note here that across six family-level taxa of amphibian helminths, there were no universal factors explaining the variation in prevalence or intensity among populations. They concluded that several site factors (climate variables and host community richness) and host factors (terrestrial vs aquatic, body size, and litter size) of parasite infection levels were “highly idiosyncratic and taxon-specific”. As such, their findings underscored the need to assess the factors leading to variation in prevalence and intensity for individual parasite species, especially on more local scales of analysis.

In this study, we revisit the topic discussed by Martins and Poulin (2024), but at the finer scale of infection probability and intensity. Ultimately, the factors influencing the probability of a host being infected and individual infection intensities determine population-level infection parameters (i.e. prevalence). Therefore, we examined how individual infection probabilities and intensities of a trophically-transmitted, generalist parasite vary among sympatric host species across a short stretch at the confluence of two rivers. Our goal was to explore how the hypothesized variables of host dietary niche and unidirectional stream drift (part of the River Continuum Concept discussed below; Vannote et al., 1980) interact to shape variation in infection patterns.

In relation to the encounter/compatibility paradigm (Holmes, 1987; Combes, 2001) where parasite infection is a function of both 1) spatial and temporal overlap with a potential host and 2) the ability of the parasite to evade host defense and draw upon the host as a resource, both host diet and stream drift fall under the encounter filter. Host diet is often found associated with the community composition of trophically-transmitted helminths in a host population (Leung and Koprivnikar, 2019; Paterson et al., 2019; Poulin and Leung, 2011). Indeed, multiple studies have shown differences in helminth community structure of sympatric host species that occupy different dietary niches (Bertrand et al., 2008; Knudsen et al., 2003; Lagrue et al., 2011). Dietary shifts across life stages (juvenile to adult) could also alter infection patterns over the life span of an individual host and thus, lead to different infection patterns across age cohorts of hosts (Stephenson et al., 2015; Bellay et al., 2020; Zelmer and Arai, 1998).

River systems may induce variation in infection parameters across the river landscape via several non-mutually exclusive factors impacting the encounter filter. Such factors may include habitat suitability for intermediate hosts (e.g., nutrient composition), surrounding land use (e.g., anthropogenic impacts such as runoff), and unidirectional stream flow (Blanar et al., 2016; Zemmer et al., 2020; Falke and Preston, 2021). Of primary interest in our study is the impact of stream drift, which is a component of the River Continuum Concept, a framework in which biotic changes instream may occur longitudinally in relation to hydrologic and geomorphic properties (Vannote et al., 1980). Because unidirectional stream flow can facilitate downstream-biased dispersal of free-living fluvial organisms (Blondel et al., 2019; Crispo et al., 2006; Petranka et al., 1987; Poff et al., 1997), Blasco-Costa et al. (2013) proposed that gradients in helminth infections may form, with infections levels decreasing as the distance upstream increases.

Our study system for testing the role of host dietary niche and unidirectional stream drift in shaping parasite infections at a fine spatial scale was the trematode Renifer aniarum among its three sympatric, congeneric watersnake hosts (Nerodia erythrogaster, N. rhombifer, and N. harteri paucimaculata) along ∼175 km stretch (in total) of the Colorado-Concho River confluence in Texas, USA. Renifer aniarum (Family Plagiorchidea, subfamily Reniferinae; Byrd, 1935, Tkach, 2008) has a three-host life cycle with pulmonate snails in the genus Physa as first hosts, tadpoles of various anuran genera as second intermediate hosts, and snakes as final hosts (Byrd, 1935; Byrd and Denton, 1938; Santoro et al., 2011; Sears et al., 2012; Talbot, 1934; Walker, 1939). The presence of fully formed, gravid individuals in 23 snake species spanning seven genera and two families, including Colubridae and Viperidae (McAllister and Bursey, 2008), suggests that phylogenetic relatedness (at least among the reported snake species) does not significantly influence the ability of R. aniarum to infect and develop to maturity in its hosts. Snakes become infected only by ingesting tadpoles infected with metacercariae (McAllister et al., 2008; Santoro et al., 2011).

The watersnake-R. aniarum system is ideal to test two aspects of host dietary niche. First, watersnake community coexistence is facilitated by interspecific differences in dietary niche where each species has a distinct diet but with some overlap and dietary differences among water snakes have been well documented (Camp et al., 2020; Gibbons and Dorcas, 2004; Plummer and Goy, 1984; Greene et al., 1994; Mushinsky et al., 1982; Mushinsky and Hebrard, 1977; Perkins et al., 2020). Specifically, yellow-bellied/plain-bellied watersnakes, Nerodia erythrogaster, consume significantly more anurans than diamond-backed watersnakes, N. rhombifer (Mushinsky et al., 1982; Mushinsky and Hebrard, 1977; Perkins et al., 2020). In contrast, N. rhombifer feeds primarily on fish at higher trophic levels (Plummer and Goy, 1984; Perkins et al., 2020) and the Concho watersnake, N. h. paucimaculata, forages mainly on small fish in riffle habitats (Greene et al., 1994). Based on a synthesis of dietary data compiled from multiple sources, Camp et al. (2020) found that anurans constitute 33 % of the diet of Nerodia erythrogaster, whereas they make up only 0.14 % and 0.33 % of the diets of N. rhombifer and N. h. paucimaculata, respectively.

Due to some dietary overlap, we expected R. aniarum would infect all three watersnake species. However, we predicted that the primarily anurophagous N. erythrogaster would exhibit the highest probability of infection and infection intensities of R. aniarum relative to the primarily piscivorous N. rhombifer and N. h. paucimaculata. This prediction is grounded in extensive literature on snake feeding ecology and the transmission of R. aniarum, and is consistent with the expectation articulated by Fontenot and Font (1996), who suggested that snake species consuming a high proportion of tadpoles would be more likely to harbor a rich trematode fauna, given the role of tadpoles as second intermediate hosts. Additionally, because larger hosts generally ingest more prey and are thus exposed to more trophically-transmitted parasites (Poulin and Morand, 2004), our second prediction was that the probability of infection and infection intensities would increase with snout-vent length (SVL) across all three host species, reflecting ontogenetic shifts in diet.

Based on unidirectional stream drift alone, one would predict the probability of infection and infection intensities would decrease with distance upstream. While Blasco-Costa et al. (2013) found this pattern among autogenic trematodes (those with life cycles completely within the aquatic system; Esch et al., 1988), several allogenic trematodes (those that cycle in the aquatic system but have terrestrial final hosts; Esch et al., 1988) have shown the reverse pattern or no association (Blasco-Costa et al., 2013; Blanar et al., 2016; Zemmer et al., 2020; Falke and Preston, 2021). We note, however, studies on allogenic trematodes have focused on prevalence or intensity in intermediate hosts and not a final host. Here, we test the stream drift hypothesis in an allogenic parasite from its snake definitive host.

Materials and methods

Study area

Collections were carried out in 2013–2015 at eleven locations along the Colorado and Concho Rivers, one site on Lipan Creek (a tributary to the Concho River) and one site on Elm Creek (a tributary to the Colorado River), for a total of 13 collection locations distributed in Coke, Runnels, Concho, Coleman, Brown, and Tom Green counties Texas, USA (∼175 river km; Fig. 1 and Table S2). The Concho and Colorado Rivers form a confluence within O.H. Ivie Reservoir, separating the river system into three qualitative reaches: the Upper Colorado River (UCR) above O.H. Ivie Reservoir, the Lower Colorado River (LCR) downstream of O.H. Ivie Reservoir, and the Concho River. Located in semi-arid west-central Texas, this system is characterized by a relatively low annual baseflow index, with similarly low baseflow in both the UCR and Concho River, and comparatively higher baseflow in the LCR (Fig. S1). The elevation gradient in this drainage ranges from 1000 m upstream to 400 m (Fig. S2). We note that collection sites 7–9 on the Colorado and 11–13 on the Concho are upstream of available gauges and so are not accurately represented in the available data. Habitat descriptions of this river system are given in the supplemental files of Janecka et al. (2021).

Collections

Watersnakes were captured by hand or with partially submerged minnow traps with little stinker catfish bait, which attracts fish into the traps. Snakes are then attracted to the fish. After capture, snake species, total length, and snout-vent length (SVL) were recorded, and sex was determined by probing. As this research was carried out concurrently with the post-delisting monitoring of the Concho watersnake (Janecka et al., 2021), complete dissections were not performed to keep all hosts alive for release and recapture. Rather, because R. aniarum infection occurs in the mouth and upper esophagus of the definitive snake hosts, adult parasites could easily be removed from each infected snake's oral cavity and uppermost esophagus prior to release (Fig. 1). Snakes, which were held for no longer than 24 h, were taken to a nearby facility for parasite removal and subsequently, marked with a subcutaneous pit tag prior to release at their capture site. We did not include recaptured individuals in this analysis. Flukes were heat-killed under a cover slip, identified under compound scope, and preserved in 70 % EtOH. A subset of individuals was also stained and mounted to confirm identification; R. aniarum is easily distinguishable from its congeners by the location of the genital pore, which is even with the oral sucker, and the pre-and post-acetabular clusters of vitellaria follicles (Dyer and Ballard, 1991). While other species of the genus Renifer have been reported from Nerodia spp. (Fontenot and Font, 1996; Detterline et al., 1984), R. aniarum was the only Renifer species found in the Colorado-Concho River system. Moreover, we have genotype data confirming that the collected specimens were a single parasite species (unpublished data, M.J.J.).

Statistical analyses

All statistics were conducted in R (code is provided in Supplemental file 1 (R Core Team, 2021). River distances were measured between collection sites in ArcGIS 10.5.1 (ESRI Inc.). The distance was measured from the most downstream location of one site to the most upstream point of the next site (Paz-Vinas et al., 2015). Network position was measured as the distance from the most downstream site on the LCR (Fig. 1, Site 1). Generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) were employed to assess if various factors (details below) were related to R. aniarum probability of infection (the probability of a host being infected given an independent variable), abundance (the number of individuals in a host regardless of if the host is infected or not), and intensity of infection (number of individuals in an infected host) (Bush et al., 1997) using the glmmTMB package (Brooks et al., 2007). The drop1 function in the stats package with a likelihood ratio test (LRT) was used to test for the significance of fixed effects (R Core Team, 2021). Model fits were validated by simulating and plotting the quantile residuals with the Dharma package (Hartig, 2021), and all model validation plots are provided in the R markdown (Supplemental file 1).

Analysis of probability of infection

To determine the factors that were important for a host's probability of infection, individual infection status was tested as the response variable with host sex, host SVL, host species, year, and distance upstream (network position) from the most downstream collection locations (Site 1 in Fig. 1) as explanatory variables in a GLMM with a binomial family distribution. Watersnake body mass and SVL were significantly correlated (not shown). SVL was included in the analysis because SVL is also correlated with gape size (Perkins and Eason, 2019). The collection site was included as a random effect to control for the non-independence of hosts from the same site. We tested sex and SVL by host species interaction terms, but model selection confirmed the model fit was better without these interaction terms and none significantly explained the probability of infection (not shown). Post-hoc analyses were conducted to assess pairwise differences in the probability of infection among the three watersnake species.

Analyses of abundance and intensity of infection

Results for abundance and intensity were qualitatively similar (Supplemental file 1); therefore, only intensity is discussed. To examine which factors were associated with parasite intensity of infection, the log-transformed parasite intensity was used as the response variable with host sex, SVL, host species, distance upstream from the most downstream sampling location (network position), year, species by sex interaction, and species by size interaction as explanatory variables in a GLMM with a gamma family distribution with a log link function. Because of significant interactions (see Results below), post-hoc analyses were used to split the models for each host species. However, too few infected hosts precluded an analysis for N. h. paucimaculata.

Probability of infection

In the overall model, host species, SVL, and distance upstream from the most downstream collection location were all significant factors predicting the infection probability of hosts (Table S1). The probability of infection increased with increasing SVL (Likelihood test ratio; LRT; Λ = 47.18, p < 0.001; Table S1; Fig. 2a) and increased with distance upstream (LRT; Λ = 4.92, p = 0.03; Table S1; Fig. 2b). Host species was a significant predictor of the host's probability of infection (LRT; Λ = 19.03, p < 0.001; Table S1; Fig. 3). Sub-setting out one species at a time to conduct post hoc pairwise tests showed N. erythrogaster had a higher probability of being infected compared to either N. h. paucimaculata (LRT; Λ = 11.47, p = 0.001) or N. rhombifer (LRT; Λ = 10.97, p = 0.001; Fig. 3). There was no significant difference between N. rhombifer and N. h. paucimaculata (LRT; Λ = 0.26, p = 0.60).

Intensity of infection

Year sampled, the interaction between SVL and host species, and the interaction between host sex and species were significant predictors of log-transformed parasite intensity, but distance upstream from the most downstream collection location was not significant (Table S1; Supplemental file 1). Post-hoc analyses for N. erythrogaster and N. rhombifer showed that log-transformed parasite intensity increased with increasing N. erythrogaster size (LRT; Λ = 63.07, p < 0.001), but there was no relationship for N. rhombifer (LRT; Λ = 0.19, p = 0.65) (Table S1; Fig. 4). Year was a significant predictor of infection intensity for N. rhombifer (LRT; Λ = 10.46, p = 0.01) but not for N. erythrogaster (LRT; Λ = 3.90, p = 0.14). There was no significant sex effect for either N. erythrogaster (LRT; Λ = 3.36, p = 0.14) or N. rhombifer (LRT; Λ = 0.92, p = 0.33) (Table S1).

Discussion

We examined R. aniarum infection among a community of three sympatric watersnake species across a sample of 267 hosts on the Colorado and Concho Rivers. All three species were infected, indicating that frogs and/or tadpoles comprised at least a portion of the diet of each of the snake species. Nerodia erythrogaster had a significantly higher probability of infection than N. rhombifer or N. h. paucimaculata. These results support our prediction as these patterns are consistent with studies showing N. erythrogaster consuming more anurans than N. rhombifer, and N. h. paucimaculata specializing on small fish prey (Camp et al., 2020; Plummer and Goy, 1984; Greene et al., 1994; Durso et al., 2013). Our results are also consistent with a survey of watersnake endohelminths in Alabama by Detterline et al. (1984) who found 90 % of 10 N. erythrogaster infected with R. aniarum and 21 % of 19 N. rhombifer infected. Our study provides a first report of R. aniarum infection in the Concho watersnake.

We recognize we cannot rule out the compatibility filter (i.e., differences in host immune defense) for the differences in the probability of infection. However, we favor host dietary differences as the main driver for the differences in host species for the following two reasons. First, adult trematodes recovered in our study were gravid (MJJ personal observation), suggesting R. aniarum can develop normally in all the host species. Second, the three definitive hosts in this study were closely related congeners which present similar living conditions for parasites and use similar immune defenses against them (Poulin, 2010). Thus, differences in immunity are less likely to generate differences in prevalence and intensity of infection compared to phylogenetically distant hosts (Alarcos and Timi, 2012; da Silva et al., 2022; Poulin, 2010; Rodriguez et al., 2012; Valtonen et al., 2001).

We also found a significant effect of SVL in explaining the probability of infection (Falke and Preston, 2021; Graham, 2003; Mahony et al., 2022; Poulin, 2013). There is a recurrent and consistent link between host body size and parasite infection in previous work. For example, Poulin and Leung (2011) found that host body size was a stronger predictor of helminth infection than trophic level in fish. Arneberg (2002) found a similar effect of body size, specifically mass, in mammalian hosts infected by nematodes. At the community level, Bell and Burt (1991) found that fish host body size and diet had the strongest influence on parasite community diversity. Consistent patterns linked to host body size across parasites with diverse life history strategies may result from larger hosts having higher encounter rates, greater infection exposure with age, or dietary changes related to growth and development. The results from our study are consistent with dietary shifts in the number or size of prey items as snakes age. Host size and age are often correlated in fish, and older hosts have had more time to accumulate parasite infections (Poulin, 2013). Similarly, watersnakes are gape-limited predators exhibiting ontogenetic shifts in diet (Mushinsky et al., 1982; Plummer and Goy, 1984). For example, Mushinsky et al. (1982) reported that the diet of N. erythrogaster in Louisiana shifts from primarily fish to primarily adult frogs as the snakes increase in SVL. Thus, our results are consistent with the idea that larger snakes are likely consuming more infected prey items.

In contrast to the probability of infection, the results for intensity of infection did not uniformly support our diet-based predictions. Significant interactions between host species and sex, as well as host species and SVL complicates interpretation of body size and host species as independent predictors. Post hoc analyses revealed that N. erythrogaster exhibited a positive relationship between infection intensity and SVL, whereas no such relationship was found for N. rhombifer. Instead, only sampling year was a significant predictor for N. rhombifer. These findings suggest that factors influencing intensity of infection are host-species specific. One possible explanation is that infection intensity in the final host may be shaped by variation in infection intensity within the intermediate host, thereby altering the infective dose of metacercaria that a snake is exposed to, regardless of its size or overall dietary tendencies.

Contrary to the predicted effect of stream drift, R. aniarum's probability of infection increased with distance upstream (Blasco-Costa et al., 2013; Resetarits and Byers, 2023; Zemmer et al., 2020). To date, only a few papers have examined the importance of stream drift in determining the spatial distribution of parasite infection in river systems (Blasco-Costa et al., 2013; Falke and Preston, 2021; Hasegawa and Koizumi, 2021). Blasco-Costa et al. (2013) found that trematode abundance decreased with distance upstream in three autogenic trematodes (life cycle completed in all aquatic hosts) infecting fish in a river system. Blasco-Costa et al. (2013) suggested that a decrease in parasite infection with increasing distance upstream might be a characteristic common among trematodes within rivers. However, they noted that host traits and habitat may override this pattern. An exception to the general trend of increased parasite infection downstream was an allogenic parasite, which infects birds as the definitive host. Its high dispersal ability likely helps the parasite spread throughout the system (Blasco-Costa et al., 2013). We found neither uniform levels of infection throughout the river gradient (as might have been predicted from final snake host dispersal ability alone) nor a single-site anomaly that was responsible for our results (Blasco-Costa et al., 2013; Louhi et al., 2010). Rather, our results were similar to those found by Falke and Preston (2021), who reported an increase in prevalence with increasing distance upstream for the trematode (Nanophyetus salminicola) infecting the first intermediate snail hosts. They attributed this pattern to spatial variation in host density (direct effects) and the deposition of parasite eggs in upstream habitats by final hosts in the life cycle (Falke and Preston, 2021). Hasegawa and Koizumi (2021) found a positive association with the effects of elevation, rather than the distance upstream, on infection in Samincola spp., an ectoparasitic copepod infecting white-spotted char (Salvelinus leucomaenis). However, the importance of the elevation gradient was no longer significant when included in a principal coordinates analysis with other variables. Rather, copepod infection intensity and prevalence were instead associated with char density, stream flow rate, and habitat fragmentation in the form of dams located within the system (Hasegawa and Koizumi, 2021).

At the community level, Zemmer et al. (2020) also found the highest total prevalence of trematode infection in snails at headwater sites, but when broken down by species, two allogenic parasites retained this pattern, while the other species did not. The increased total prevalence with increasing distance upstream was associated with snail density, which co-varied negatively with stream width, depth, and discharge (Zemmer et al., 2020). Blanar et al. (2016) did not find an association between distance upstream and the parasite community structure in trout-perch (Percopsis omiscomaycus); instead, community structure was determined by site-level effects from local land use.

According to the river continuum concept, upstream-downstream gradients in abiotic factors shape species distributions, with distance upstream serving as a surrogate for the actual abiotic parameters affecting parasite infection intensity or probability. Linear measures like elevation, distance, and distance upstream are proxies for the abiotic gradients influencing parasite abundance. For instance, elevation reflects temperature, humidity, and seasonality, which may drive parasite abundance, rather than elevation itself. In our system, the increase in the probability of infection with distance upstream is likely to be a function of two non-mutually exclusive processes: 1) Going upstream along the Colorado and Concho Rivers, river beds become increasingly more shallow, have lower stream flows and are intermittently dry (Janecka et al., 2021). 2) We did not quantify potential intermediate hosts, but the shift in habitat along the upstream-downstream gradient is likely facilitating a greater abundance of snail and anuran intermediate hosts. Upstream infection probability in this system may be driven by habitat features that support intermediate hosts. Because upstream reaches of the river are shallow and intermittently dry, there is a lack of permanent fish predators that prey on snails, anurans, or the eggs of these intermediate hosts (Fig. S2; MJJ personal observation). Intermediate host density can be an essential determinant of infection in the definitive host (de Montaudouin and Lanceleur, 2011; Hechinger and Lafferty, 2005; Stutz et al., 2014; Sures and Streit, 2001). Thus, local habitat characteristics along the river continuum that support intermediate hosts appear to be a plausible factor driving the increase in upstream infection probability in this system.

Martins and Poulin's (2024) meta-analysis of amphibian helminths, conducted at a broad scale, revealed complex and taxon-dependent patterns of parasite prevalence and intensity. They identified four non-mutually exclusive factors that can influence variation among populations: (1) parasite species-specific traits affecting transmission efficiency, (2) host traits influencing susceptibility (which encompass both encounter and compatibility filters), (3) local biotic conditions such as the presence of intermediate hosts or other suitable hosts at specific parasite life stages, and (4) abiotic factors that affect either the host or parasite. While our study focused on a single parasite species and cannot address evolutionary differences in parasite traits (factor 1), it does provide fine-scale insight into host traits (factor 2), specifically dietary patterns, as a driver of species-specific differences in infection probability. By using a generalist parasite (R. aniarum) and comparing snake species within the same genus (Nerodia), we also reduced the potential influence of the compatibility filter, as these hosts are likely to share similar physiological and immunological traits. In contrast, predictors of infection intensity were less consistent across host species. Additionally, our assessment of stream drift, a component of the River Continuum Concept, allowed us to collectively address factors 3 and 4. In this system, infection probability increased with upstream distance, likely reflecting changes in habitat structure and hydrology. For example, intermittent upstream habitats may support more suitable intermediate hosts due to reduced fish predation, a hypothesis that warrants future investigation. Overall, for the generalist parasite R. aniarum in the Colorado River system, the probability of infection appears to be shaped by host diet and local river dynamics, while infection intensity is less predictable. These findings suggest that predicting infection patterns of R. aniarum across other snake species, especially those that are terrestrial or not closely related, is unlikely to be straightforward. Given the interplay of host and parasite life history traits within variable environmental contexts, universal rules governing parasite infection patterns may not exist. Instead, our results support Martins and Poulin's (2024) broader conclusions, but at a much finer spatial scale. Because the probability of infection and intensity underpin key ecological and evolutionary host–parasite interactions, continued research at fine scales will be essential for understanding the factors that shape parasite distributions.
