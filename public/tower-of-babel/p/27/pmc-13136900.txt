A kinetic approach for mapping seed regions of CRISPR ribonucleoprotein and improving specificity
Authors: Wei Feng, Jianyu Hu, Hongquan Zhang, X Chris Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe binding of an activator (target nucleic acid) to a crRNA-Cas ribonucleoprotein (RNP) in CRISPR systems is critical to the activation, kinetics, and specificity of the CRISPR technology. Key to this activation process is the interaction between the protospacer region of the activator and the spacer region of the crRNA in the RNP complex. However, how the nucleotides in the spacer region of the crRNA contribute to the kinetics of RNP binding is not well characterized. We report here profiling of the kinetically critical regions in the process of RNP binding to activators (RNA targets). We introduced the concept and strategy of kinetic manipulators, which enabled mapping of the seed regions (6–9 nucleotides within the spacer that is sensitive to mismatches) of the CRISPR-Cas13a system, including the LbuCas13a and LwaCas13a homologs. The characterization of the binding kinetics and the introduction of kinetic manipulators provided the foundation for a new kinetic approach to improve the specificity of CRISPR techniques without sacrificing the activity. Profiling the kinetically critical regions in the CRISPR system and designing corresponding manipulators maximized the kinetic differences, between the on-target and off-target, and increased discrimination of single-nucleotide mismatches.

Introduction

The binding of a target (activator) to the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) in CRISPR-Cas systems is critical to the kinetics, sensitivity, and specificity of the CRISPR technology [1–3]. In the process of activator binding to the RNP, the protospacer region of the activator binds to the spacer region of CRISPR RNA (crRNA) within the crRNA-Cas RNP complex (Supplementary Fig. S1) [4]. Although the process of target binding by RNP varies for different classes and types of CRISPR-Cas systems, the hybridization of the protospacer to the seed region of the spacer initiates the base-pairing interactions between protospacer and spacer [1, 5, 6]. Structural studies have revealed that nucleotides in the seed region of the crRNA-Cas RNP are exposed and readily available for target binding [1, 5, 6]. Other nucleotides in the spacer region of crRNA, but not in the seed region, are less accessible to hybridize with the activator. This activator-RNP interaction is very different from the complementary hybridization between two free strands of nucleic acid sequences and is more complex to predict, because it involves multiple reactions among the activator, crRNA (the spacer region), and the Cas protein. However, how the nucleotides in the spacer region of the crRNA contribute to the kinetics of RNP binding to activators remains unknown.

Because the seed region governs the initiation of binding that nucleates formation of the crRNA–activator duplex and strongly influences the binding kinetics and specificity of the RNP–activator interaction, the seed region plays a critical role in the binding kinetics between the RNP and the activator [2, 6–9]. Studies have shown that mismatches in this region caused the largest reductions in binding kinetics and overall RNP activity, whereas mismatches outside the seed region generally had smaller effects [2, 7, 9]. Therefore, understanding the relative contributions of different regions in the spacer region of crRNA to the binding kinetics is critical for several reasons. First, this knowledge can be leveraged to identify the seed regions of CRISPR-Cas systems. Identifying the seed regions in each RNP provides essential insights into the sequence elements that govern the specificity and activity of Cas proteins. However, seed regions for many Cas proteins remain undefined due to technical challenges. Structure studies and mismatch screening methods have been primarily used for identifying the seed regions [6]; however, these approaches are often expensive and technically demanding or lack sensitivity and precision. Consequently, there is an increasing need for simpler and more reliable methods to identify seed regions. Second, this understanding helps elucidate interactions between Cas proteins and other regions of the crRNA in addition to the seed regions. Such insights are important for explaining why RNPs containing different crRNA spacer sequences exhibit varying levels of nuclease activity, as previously observed [10, 11], although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Third, these insights can be used to develop new strategies to enhance the specificity of CRISPR systems. Understanding how different spacer regions influence binding kinetics can be used to predict the specificity of an RNP for mismatches at various sites of the spacer. Manipulating the binding kinetics, e.g. selectively reducing the kinetics of RNP binding to a mismatch activator without affecting target binding, could improve the specificity of CRISPR-Cas systems for the target activator.

We report here a simple and precise technique to study the contributions of specific spacer regions to the binding kinetics. We introduce the strategy of kinetic manipulation, which is facilitated by short single-stranded DNA sequences (e.g. 11-nucleotide manipulators) that hybridize to different regions of the protospacer of CRISPR-Cas13a systems (Supplementary Fig. S2). The sequences of the 11-nt manipulators are complementary to an 11-nt region of the protospacer in the activator (target). Thus, a series of manipulators hybridize to different regions of the protospacer. When these activator-manipulator hybrids interact with the RNP, the spacer region in the crRNA competes with the manipulator for binding to the protospacer, resulting in a decrease in binding kinetics. The extent of this kinetic change reflects the contribution of the specific spacer region. A series of manipulators, staggered by a single nucleotide, facilitate interactions with key regions of the protospacer with single-nucleotide resolution. The technique enables kinetic profiling of the critical binding regions in the RNA-targeting RNPs formed with two Cas13 homologs, LbuCas13a and LwaCas13a.

Our technique offers two key advantages over the commonly used biochemical approach for identifying seed regions: it is more sensitive and more precise. Mismatch screening typically involves the introduction of one or more mismatches between the crRNA spacer and protospacer, followed by measuring the effects of mismatches at different locations. However, because seed regions often span >5 nucleotides, a single mismatch may not induce a significant change in binding kinetics. While introducing multiple mismatches can lead to larger changes in binding kinetics, these modifications can also alter the structure of the final ternary complex, the binding thermodynamics, and the activity of the CRISPR-Cas system, thereby introducing confounding effects [6]. Binding affinity studies of the RNP to short segments (e.g. 8 nt) of the protospacer is another biochemical approach for identifying the seed region. The protospacer segment complementary to the seed region exhibits higher binding affinity to RNP than those complementary to other spacer regions [12]. However, this approach lacks sensitivity. In contrast, our technique examines the impact of kinetic manipulators comprising 11 nucleotides, which induce substantial and measurable changes in binding kinetics. Importantly, this approach does not disrupt the structure of the final ternary complex, thereby avoiding the structural and functional ambiguities associated with mismatch screening. Our technique allows sensitive and simple binding kinetics measurement without confounding from thermodynamics. As a result, our method delivers more reliable and accurate identification of kinetically critical regions.

Our method differs from a recent study that used RNA or DNA occluders binding to RNA activators to investigate how RNA secondary structure affects Cas13 activity [13]. The authors used occluders (21–28 nt) to create RNA structural occlusion and examined the effects of occluders on Cas13 activity through a strand-displacement mechanism. While the occluder approach provided insights into how RNA structure modulated Cas13 activity, it did not provide kinetic information on RNP–activator binding.

Understanding the kinetic contributions of spacer regions to the activator binding established the basis for manipulating the binding kinetics and enhancing the specificity of RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas systems. We applied this knowledge to create a new strategy for improving the discrimination of single-nucleotide mismatches. Short kinetic manipulators (e.g. 9 nt) bind stably to the mismatch RNA but not to the perfect match (PM) target RNA activator. This design significantly slows the kinetics of the crRNA–Cas13a RNP binding to the mismatch RNA, while leaving binding kinetics to the PM target unaffected. This unique feature provides two key advantages: (a) the target binding kinetics remain fast and unaffected, and (b) the free energy change of target binding is preserved, ensuring that the sensitivity of target detection is not compromised. Other discrimination techniques using occluders or hairpins to block the spacer region of crRNA in the Cas13a [13, 14]. These strategies slow down the RNP binding to both the mismatch and the PM target, and therefore, reduce the detection sensitivity. In addition, hybridization of DNA or RNA to the spacer region of crRNA could cause undesired activation of Cas13a even when there is no target [13]. The kinetic manipulation techniques are designed to overcome these issues.

Materials and methods

In vitro transcription of RNA

DNA sequences were ordered from IDT and Sangon (Shanghai). crRNAs and activators (RNA targets) were produced through in vitro T7 transcription using protocols modified from previous reports [3, 15]. The DNA templates for T7 transcription were single-stranded DNA complementary to each RNA. A segment containing the T7 promoter complementary sequence was added to the 3′ end (TA TAG TGA GTC GTA TTA atttc). DNA templates were annealed with a T7 promoter oligo (gaaatTAATACGACTCACTATAGG) at a final concentration of 10 µM in Nuclease-Free Duplex Buffer (IDT). The annealing was done in a thermocycler, and the temperature was programmed to decrease from 80°C to 30°C at a rate of decreasing 0.1°C every 0.05 s. Five microliters of the duplex was used as the template for 20 µl T7 transcription using HiScribe™ T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, #E2040S). RNA was treated with DNase I (NEB, #M0303S), purified with TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15596026) or Monarch Spin RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, #T2050S), and quantified using a NanoVue Plus Spectrophotometer (GE Healthcare) or Infinite M200 Pro with nanoplate (TECAN). RNA was subsequently aliquoted and stored at −80°C. The sequences of all crRNA and RNA activators are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Design of activators, crRNA, and kinetic manipulators for kinetics profiling

Two Cas13 homologs, LbuCas13a and LwaCas13a, were used in this study (GeneScript, #Z03742-100, #Z03486-100). The sequences of three activators (targets) and their corresponding crRNA are shown in Supplementary Table S1. The RNA folding energy (∆G) at 25°C for activators 1, 2, and 3 are −1.57, −1.25, and −3.26 kcal/mol, respectively, predicted using NUPACK with RNA model RNA95, at the default salt concentration of 1 M Na+. Three staggered crRNA sequences, with protospacer location shift by 4 nucleotides each, were designed for activator 2. In the system of LbuCas13a, crRNA, and activators 1 and 2, the lengths of the protospacer and spacer are 20 nt. For both LbuCas13a and LwaCas13a homologs, along with activator 3, the protospacer and spacer are 23 nt long. In the system of LwaCas13a, crRNA, and activators 1 and 2, the protospacer and spacer are 28 nt in length.

Kinetic manipulators are single-stranded oligonucleotide sequences (DNA blockers) that can hybridize with part of the protospacer region. For each activator, a set of manipulators was designed to hybridize with the activator at various locations staggered by 1 nucleotide. Each kinetic manipulator hybridizes to 9–12 nt of the activator. The sequences and relative positions of kinetic manipulators for profiling are listed in Supplementary Tables S2–S4. The length of the manipulators was designed based on the length of typical seed regions, free energies of nucleic acid hybridization (∆Ghybrid), and the melting temperature (Tm) (Supplementary Table S5). (i) The length of the seed region is around 6 nt [7, 8]; therefore, complete blocking of the seed region with manipulators longer than 6 nt will provide a significant kinetics decrease. (ii) Within each set of manipulators for one activator, the length was slightly adjusted to make the free energy of activator-manipulator hybridization (∆Ghybrid) similar (Supplementary Table S6). (iii) Positions of the outmost manipulators were decided based on the melting temperature (Tm): if end manipulators bind to a nt at the end of the protospacer and to b nt beyond the protospacer, the Tm of entire activator-manipulator hybrids (a + b nt) should be ~5°C above the reaction temperature for stable blocking. The Tm of the b-nt hybrid should be at least 5°C below the reaction temperature to ensure complete manipulator dissociation from the activator after the displacement of a nt of the manipulator from the protospacer by the crRNA (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S7). The estimation of activator-manipulator hybridization free energies and Tm was done using the thermodynamic parameters of dinucleotide base pairs and the nearest-neighbor model reported by Sugimoto et al. [16], under a salt concentration of 1 M Na+. With these design criteria, we minimized the difference in the free energy change of RNP binding to an activator in the presence of various manipulators. As a consequence, the binding rate between RNP and activator is primarily affected by binding kinetics, not thermodynamics.

Determination of the kinetics of RNP binding to an activator in the presence of various manipulators

The concentrations of reactants were chosen to meet the theoretical conditions. A reporter concentration [S] of 50 nM, an RNP concentration of 1 nM [1 nM crRNA and 2 nM Cas13a (GeneScript, #Z03742-100, #Z03486-100)], and an activator concentration of up to 0.1 nM were used. Overall, a relatively low concentration of RNP and activator was used to ensure that most of the binding reactions with different manipulators did not reach equilibrium during the time period in which the data were used to calculate initial velocity. The concentration of manipulators was 100 nM. Each manipulator was annealed with the activator in Nuclease-Free Duplex Buffer containing 0.005% Tween-20. The annealing concentration was 10 times the concentration in the profiling assay. The RNP was pre-formed for 45 min to 1 h before other reagents were added at room temperature (25°C). The buffer solution contained 40 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 60 mM NaCl, 6 mM MgCl2, and 0.005% Tween-20. To make sure each set of reactions with the same RNP and target but different manipulators starts at the same time, 10 µl of the target with or without the manipulator was first loaded onto a 96-well plate (Costar, #3915). The fluorescence reporter (RNaseAlert, IDT) was mixed with the RNP in the reaction buffer, and 90 µl of this master mix was added to each well using a multichannel pipette. Immediately after the master mix (containing the reporter and RNP) was added to the well plate (containing the target with or without a manipulator), fluorescence was measured every minute at 25°C using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, FilterMax F5, λex = 485 nm, λem = 535 nm).

Design of the kinetic manipulator to increase the contribution of single-nucleotide mutation on RNP-target binding kinetics and to improve the mismatch discrimination

Design considerations for detecting single-nucleotide mutations: (i) design a crRNA that perfectly matches the mutant sequence; (ii) design a kinetic manipulator (∼9 nt) that perfectly matches the wild-type (WT) sequence, covering the entire seed-complementary region with the mutation site located near the center; and (iii) slightly adjust the manipulator sequence and concentration based on Tm estimation. The Tm of the hybridization between the manipulator and the WT sequence should be ~5°C higher than the reaction temperature to ensure effective blocking of the WT sequence. The Tm of the hybridization between the manipulator and the mutant sequence should be ~5°C lower than the reaction temperature so that the manipulator does not block the mutant sequence.

In the experiment involving mismatches at different positions, kinetic manipulators for discriminating PM from mismatch were designed to bind stably to the mismatch target but not to the PM target at 25°C. These relatively short (e.g. 9 nt) manipulators formed a stable hybrid (Tm > 30°C at the manipulator concentration of 500 nM) with the mismatch target, but not with the PM target (Tm < 20°C). Melting temperatures were estimated using the TM MISMATCH function in IDT OligoAnalyzer. Input parameters were 500 nM manipulators, 60 mM Na+, and 6 mM Mg2+. The sequences and relative positions of kinetic manipulators for mismatch discrimination are listed in Supplementary Table S8.

Discrimination of Escherichia coli carrying wild-type or mutant gyrA genes in river water

River water samples were collected from the Yuhangtang River (Hangzhou, China). Aliquots of an untreated river water sample were spiked with two types of Escherichia coli bacteria: one carrying the S83L mutation in the gyrA gene and the other WT. The proportion of the E. coli carrying the mutant gene relative to the WT in a series of spiked water samples was 0%, 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 50%, and 100%, respectively. Results

Principle of profiling the kinetics of ribonucleoprotein binding to activator

The profiling technique for understanding the kinetic contributions of different spacer regions is built on the basis of the structural information of crRNA–Cas13a RNPs and the kinetics of nucleic acid interactions. The binding between the RNP and the activator (target) involves hybridization between the spacer and the protospacer (Interaction 1, Supplementary Fig. S5). However, this binding is not solely determined by these two RNA sequences, because the spacer within the RNP also interacts with the Cas13a protein (Interaction 2, Supplementary Fig. S5). In the process of RNP binding to the activator, hybridization between the spacer and protospacer requires altering the interaction between the spacer and Cas13a in the RNP complex. Structural studies of RNP complexes formed between crRNA and the Lsh [17], Lbu [8], Rc [18], and LbaCas13a [19] homologs have revealed that the phosphate-sugar backbone of the spacer interacts with Cas13a proteins through hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions. Some nucleotide bases of the spacer also interact with amino acids of the Cas13a protein through stacking interactions. These interactions make many nucleotides in the spacer inaccessible or poorly accessible. On the other hand, the exposed segment of the spacer, commonly referred to as the seed region, does not directly interact with the Cas13a protein and is critical to the initial binding to the activator (Fig. 1A).

Principle of profiling the kinetics of binding between crRNA–Cas13a RNP and its activator (target). (A) Kinetic manipulators and their effect on the binding kinetics of RNP to activator. The seed region of crRNA (highlighted in red) in the RNP is exposed and responsible for the initial binding to the activator. Kinetic manipulators block parts of the protospacer region of the activator. When a manipulator sequence (denoted in red color) hybridizes to the region of the activator that is complementary to the seed region of the crRNA in RNP, the kinetics of RNP binding to the activator slows down. Manipulators (denoted in green color) hybridizing to other regions of the activator do not affect the binding kinetics. (B) Locating the seed region based on the kinetics profiles. The overlap region of all manipulators that result in the slowest binding kinetics (highlighted in red box) is identical to the seed region.

The seed region initiates and facilitates the binding of the RNP to the activator, which shares some similarities with the process of a toehold-mediated DNA strand displacement (TMSD) reaction. The toehold region is critical to the kinetics of TMSD, which is evident from a 106-fold decrease in the strand-displacement rate constant with the shortening of the toehold region from 6 to 0 nt [20]. Like the toehold region, the seed region of the crRNA in the RNP is not blocked by the Cas13 protein and is available to hybridize with the activator. Therefore, the seed region plays a crucial role in the kinetics of RNP binding to the activator. Understanding the binding kinetics will enable determination of the seed region. However, unlike a TMSD reaction, the kinetic contribution of the seed region is difficult to study because the interaction between the spacer and Cas13a is more complex than DNA hybridization alone, and Cas13a also interacts with the hybrid formed between the spacer and activator. Any direct alterations to the seed region and other parts of the spacer cannot be used to accurately determine the contributions of seed and other spacer regions to the activator binding kinetics because they would also affect the thermodynamics of the RNP formation and its subsequent activation [6].

To profile the kinetics contribution of spacer regions without altering the thermodynamics of the binding process, we kept the seed region, crRNA, Cas13a protein, and the activator all unchanged (Supplementary Table S1). Instead, we introduced short DNA manipulator sequences that hybridize to the protospacer region of the activator (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Tables S2–S4). Each manipulator with a sequence identical to one spacer region (e.g. 11 nt) pre-blocks the corresponding region of the protospacer in the activator. As a result of introducing the manipulator, the binding of the RNP to the activator now involves three interactions (Supplementary Fig. S5): Interaction 1, the hybridization between the spacer of the crRNA and the protospacer of the activator; Interaction 2, the pre-existing interaction between the spacer and Cas13a, leaving only the seed region exposed; and Interaction 3, the intervening interaction between the manipulator and the activator. For the activator to bind with the RNP, the spacer of crRNA must displace the manipulator that has pre-blocked the protospacer region of the activator (Fig. 1A). If the manipulator does not block the seed-complementary region, the kinetics of RNP binding to the activator remains similar to reactions without manipulators. However, blocking the seed-complementary region within the protospacer by the manipulator disrupts the binding of the activator to the seed region of crRNA in the RNP and slows the binding kinetics (Fig. 1B). When the entire seed-complementary region is blocked, the binding kinetics decreases to a minimum level. Therefore, when different protospacer regions are hybridized with a series of manipulator sequences, changes in the kinetics provide insights for profiling the kinetics of RNP binding to the activator (Fig. 1B). The overlapping region of the manipulators, which reduces binding kinetics to the minimum, hybridizes to the kinetically critical region in the activator and is identical to the seed region in the crRNA-Cas13a RNP (Fig. 1B).

We rationally designed manipulators based on the simulation of free-energy (∆G) and melting temperature (Tm) of nucleic acid interactions. Kinetic manipulators of the same length but hybridizing to different locations of the activator have comparable binding energies, with ∆G of −10 to −16 kcal/mol (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S6). The similar ∆G values indicate that the location of the manipulator hybridization has little effect on the thermodynamics of the binding between the crRNA-Cas13a RNP and the activator. This ∆G range is relatively small compared to the ∆G of −37 kcal/mol for the manipulator hybridization to the spacer region of crRNA (Supplementary Table S6). On the basis of Tm estimation, manipulators equal to or longer than 10 nt are expected to form stable hybridization with the activator at a reaction temperature of 25°C (Supplementary Table S5). Binding of the RNP to the activator results in the displacement of the manipulator, which is supported by the estimated Tm (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S7) and experimental results (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Profiling the location-specific effect on the binding kinetics of LbuCas13a RNP to RNA

Having established the basis for profiling and manipulating the binding kinetics between crRNA–Cas13a RNP and its activator, we studied how the nucleotides in the spacer region contribute to binding kinetics of the crRNA–LbuCas13a RNP to its activator. We first chose two sets of linear activators and crRNA spacers to avoid any confounding effects of RNA secondary structures on the folding energy and binding kinetics. The typical length of the crRNA spacer for LbuCas13a is 20 nt. We designed 22 manipulators (Fig. 2A, sequences shown in Supplementary Table S2). Importantly, all manipulators were designed to have comparable hybridization energies (∆Ghybrid) between the activator and the manipulator (Fig. 2C). Tm measurements confirmed that the manipulators exhibited similar hybridization stability (Supplementary Table S11). Therefore, the binding reaction free energy change is similar among reactions involving different manipulators. These designed manipulators are 11 or 12 nt in length, and they are staggered by 1 nt. Such a length enables complete coverage of the seed region, as supported by studies on the seed regions. These manipulators also bind stably to the activator, ensuring effective blocking (Supplementary Table S5). These 22 manipulators are complementary to 22 overlapping regions of Activator 1 (Fig. 2A). Together, they cover the entire protospacer sequence of the activator.

We monitored the concentrations of the cleaved reporter every minute for 60 min (Supplementary Fig. S7), from which we obtained the real-time reporter cleavage rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $v),$\end{document} the real-time concentration of the remaining uncleaved reporter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $[ S ]$\end{document}), and the binding rate constant (kobs) (Fig. 2) using our previously reported method with minor modifications [3]. When manipulators were hybridized to the activator at locations 8th–18th, 9th–19th, and 10th–20th nt, the binding rate constant kobs was the lowest. Conversely, manipulators hybridized to the activator away from these locations had little or no effect compared to the absence of any manipulator. Therefore, the overlapping spacer region targeted by these three manipulators, spanning 10th–18th nt, is the most kinetically critical for RNP binding to the activator. We designate this region as the kinetically critical region, which likely corresponds to the location of the seed region. When this kinetically critical region is partially blocked by the manipulator, the kobs was also decreased compared to the absence of any manipulator. The extent of the decrease correlates with the number of nucleotides blocked within the critical region. Although all manipulators have comparable hybridization energies to the activator (∆G shown in Fig. 2C), they result in varying degrees of reduction in the kinetics of RNP binding to the activator. These experiments demonstrate that without directly changing the crRNA or RNP, the use of a set of kinetic manipulators provides insights into the kinetically critical regions in the crRNA-Cas13a RNP.

We confirmed that the kinetics of RNP-activator binding is the primary reason for the effect of different manipulators by conducting two sets of experiments: adding everything at once or pre-incubating RNP with the target (with and without manipulator) before adding the reporter. In the case of 3 h pre-incubation of RNP and activator-manipulator hybrids, the initial cleavage rates are similar among the different manipulators used(Supplementary Fig. S8). These results support our interpretation that the manipulators act upstream on activation formation.

Using the profiling technique, we studied whether the kinetically critical region in the crRNA–Cas13a RNP is affected by the sequence of the spacer. To control for other effects, we used three protospacer regions of the same activator (Activator 2) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S3). These protospacer regions, and the corresponding spacer regions, were shifted by 4 nt (Fig. 3A). The kinetics profiles (Fig. 3B–D) consistently showed that the kinetically critical region is within the 8th–16th nt (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S9), the 9th–16th nt (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S10), and the 7th–14th nt (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. S11).

To further test the profiling capability, we used a third set of crRNA–Cas13a RNP and an activator that contains a slight secondary structure (Activator 3, Supplementary Table S4). The manipulator blocking the 8th–18th nt and 9th–19th nt of the activator leads to the slowest RNP binding to the activator, suggesting that the 9th–18th nt is the kinetically critical region (Supplementary Figs S12 and S13).

Taken together, using three different activators corresponding to five protospacers, we consistently identified kinetically critical regions for crRNA–LbuCas13a RNP binding to activators at nucleotides 10th–18th, 8th–16th, 9th–16th, 7th–14th, and 9th–18th nt of the spacer, with an overlapping region spanning 10th–14th nt. These results are in good agreement with previous structural studies that identified the seed region at ~9th–15th nt of the spacer [7, 8]. These consistent results demonstrate that our strategy using short kinetic manipulators can effectively identify kinetically critical regions that correspond to the seed region of Cas13a. The successful profiling of the binding between different RNPs and activators demonstrates the validity of our technique. Our simple biochemical approach, on the basis of kinetic profiling, complements those of structural techniques, such as crystallography and cryo-EM, in studies of CRISPR systems [6, 8]. Furthermore, the kinetic profiles may suggest subtle differences in Cas13a binding fingerprints for different spacer sequences.

Analysis of the seed region in the crRNA–LwaCas13a RNP

Having successfully profiled the seed region of the crRNA–LbuCas13a RNP, we applied the kinetic profiling technique to determine the seed region of the LwaCas13a homolog. The typical length of crRNA spacer for the LbuCas13a homolog is 20 nt, whereas the crRNA spacer for the LwaCas13a homolog is 28 nt. The seed region for the crRNA–LwaCas13a RNP has not been identified. We applied our profiling technique to map the seed region of the LwaCas13a system (Fig. 4). The profiling pattern for LwaCas13a is different from LbuCas13a. A wider range of manipulators caused a significant decrease in the rate of LwaCas13a RNP-activator binding compared to LbuCas13a RNP-activator binding. Overall, the results from testing three sets of activators and spacers (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs S14–S16) indicate that the seed region is between the 10th nt and the 18th nt from the 5′ end of the spacer. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the kinetically sensitive region (or seed region) of the crRNA–LwaCas13a system.

Manipulating the RNP binding kinetics to increase the specificity of mismatch detection without affecting sensitivity

The binding kinetics profiling and the identification of the seed region provided information and a foundation for increasing specificity of the CRISPR system, with an example of improving the discrimination of single-nucleotide mismatches of interest. The results of the binding kinetics profiling reveal that manipulators can slow down the initial velocity of RNP binding to the activator by over 60-fold (Supplementary Fig. S17), through blocking the kinetically critical region. This finding inspired us to develop a new strategy to improve the discrimination of a single-nucleotide mismatch (Fig. 5). To differentiate a PM target from a mismatch (MM) target, we designed a crRNA to position the mismatch within the kinetically critical region. We then introduced a short kinetic manipulator that is perfectly complementary to the kinetically critical region of the mismatch target. Consequently, the kinetic manipulator binds stably to the mismatch target but not to the PM target (Fig. 5B), which results in a significant decrease in the kinetics of the crRNA–Cas13a RNP binding to the mismatch target, but no kinetic effect on the PM target (Fig. 5B and D). Maximizing the binding kinetics difference enabled discrimination of the mismatch because the fluorescence generation in the Cas13a fluorescence assay is delayed due to the slow binding of RNP to the mismatched activator and the slow activation (Fig. 5F). If no kinetic manipulator is used, a mismatch between spacer and protospacer leads to a minor change in binding kinetics and energy (Fig. 5A and C), which is insufficient to differentiate the mismatch and PM RNA targets (Supplementary Fig. S18 and Fig. 5E) [21].

To examine the efficiency of our strategy, we designed 20 mismatch targets, each containing a mismatch at a different location within the spacer, and 20 corresponding kinetic manipulators for binding to these mismatch targets (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Table S8). These kinetic manipulators were designed to have a melting temperature Tm above 30°C for binding to the mismatch target and a Tm below 20°C for binding to the PM target (Supplementary Fig. S19). Therefore, each manipulator can stably bind to the mismatch target but not to the PM target under the reaction temperature (25°C). We also experimentally verified the selective binding (Supplementary Fig. S20).

Analysis of river water containing E. coli that carry gyrA S83L mutation

To demonstrate a practical application of the improved specificity, we applied the kinetic manipulation technique to the discrimination of a single-nucleotide mutation in the gyrA gene of DNA gyrase. The single-nucleotide mutation at codon 83 in the gyrA gene results in the substitution of serine with leucine (S83L). The S83L mutation confers resistance of E. coli to fluoroquinolone antibiotics.

Having demonstrated that the greatest improvement in the discrimination factor when the single-nucleotide mutation is located at the center of the kinetically critical region (the 14th nt of the spacer), we designed an LbuCas13a crRNA targeting S83L mutant such that the mutation site is positioned at the 14th nt of the spacer (Supplementary Fig. S23). The crRNA spacer is perfectly complementary to the mutant sequence but has a single mismatch with the WT sequence. Accordingly, we used a manipulator that is complementary to 9 nucleotides of the WT sequence but has a mismatch with the mutant sequence at the central nucleotide of the manipulator. The manipulator binds stably to the WT gyrA sequence (Supplementary Fig. S23) but not to the mutant sequence. The use of the kinetic manipulator consistently improved the discrimination between the mutant and the WT at a range of RNA concentrations (varied from 10 to 250 pM) (Fig. 7A and B; Supplementary Fig. S23). The use of a scrambled DNA sequence of the same length, instead of the manipulator sequence, did not improve the discrimination (Supplementary Fig. S24), confirming the specific effect of the manipulator.

We then analyzed river water samples that were spiked with E. coli carrying WT gyrA and E. coli carrying the gyrA S83L mutation (Fig. 7C). Our method was able to clearly detect E. coli carrying the gyrA S83L mutation at levels as low as 1% relative to the WT (Fig. 7C). The rate of the reporter cleavage in the first 5 min of fluorescence measurements is proportional to the percentage of E. coli carrying the gyrA S83L mutation (Fig. 7D). The ability to differentiate 1% gyrA S83L single-nucleotide mutation in the presence of 99% WT indicates a discrimination factor on the order of 100. The relatively low R2 value of 0.980 is reasonable within experimental variability, given that the assay included a PCR amplification step and that the semi-quantitative amounts of PCR products contributed to the variability. Similar deviations from linearity have been observed by others in detection of mutations [22–24]. Zhang et al. [22] reported that their “fitted curves exhibited a strong linear correlation with R² ranging from 0.896 to 0.999.”

Discussion

The binding of RNP to the target nucleic acid is an essential step in the RNA-guided regulation of gene expression and immune response [1, 25], and is critical to genomic and transcriptomic techniques [26]. Understanding the RNP-target binding kinetics can help unravel the mechanisms of biological interactions and guide the design of CRISPR systems for the best technical performance [1, 2, 27–29]. We developed a kinetic profiling technique to study the contributions of different segments of the spacer region in crRNA to the kinetics of crRNA–Cas13a RNP binding to RNA activator (target). The kinetic manipulation, at the single-nucleotide resolution, enabled mapping of the seed region in the crRNA–Cas13a RNPs. Because the seed region within the RNP is critical for initiating the contact and binding with the RNA activator (target) [1, 7, 8, 30], determination of the seed region is important for fundamental understanding and practical applications of CRISPR systems. Our kinetic profiling technique correctly determined the seed region in the crRNA–LbuCas13a RNP, consistent with results from previous structural studies [8]. Our kinetic profiling studies also identified the seed region in the crRNA–LwaCas13a RNP, which has not been reported previously. In comparison with structural studies using crystallography and cryo-EM, our kinetic profiling technique is simpler and provides additional binding kinetic details that indicate the contributions of the seed region and the different nucleotide segments in crRNA. Given that the seed-binding mechanism applies to many different RNPs [1], such as microRNA–Argonaute RNP, small RNA–Hfq RNP, and various crRNA–Cas RNPs, our kinetic profiling technique can also be used to study the interactions between different RNPs and their targets. The kinetic manipulation technique is not limited to the RNP that has trans-cleavage activity, although trans-cleavage activity has the benefit of enhancing sensitivity for monitoring target binding. Our technique is applicable to RNPs that lack trans-cleavage activity, with the option to label the activator for the real-time measurement of bound or unbound activator to obtain the binding rate constant.

The kinetic profiling technique using manipulators to interact with activators overcomes limitations of other techniques that directly modify crRNA or alter the activator sequences. For example, introducing mismatches to kinetically critical regions could affect both the binding kinetics and the binding equilibrium. The activity of RNPs could also be affected because of changes to the protein–RNA interactions. Therefore, with all effects intertwined, these other techniques could not clearly determine the location of the seed region [31]. With the use of kinetic manipulators, our kinetic profiling technique mapped the seed regions and revealed subtle differences in the location contribution profiles among the different crRNAs, activators, and Cas13a homologs. Small differences in the kinetic profiles may suggest different accessibility of the spacer nucleotides in the RNP complex. This additional kinetics information has not been obtained from structural studies.

The kinetics information generated from the profiling directed the development of a new kinetic approach for improving the specificity of CRISPR techniques, with an example of increasing discrimination between the PM target and mismatch targets containing a single-nucleotide mutation. Importantly, the kinetic manipulation method maintains the sensitivity while improving the specificity. The primary reason for the high discrimination ability is the combined effect of the CRISPR recognition and the manipulation of the kinetics of RNP binding to the activator (target molecule). The rationally designed kinetic manipulators hybridize stably to the seed-complementary region of the mismatch target and significantly slow down RNP binding to the mismatch target. The same manipulators do not form a stable hybrid with the PM target and do not change the kinetics of RNP binding to the PM target. The significant difference in the kinetics, between RNP binding to the PM target and RNP binding to the mismatch target, improves the discrimination that has not been achieved by the CRISPR recognition mechanism alone.

Our kinetic manipulation strategy offers two additional advantages: (a) the kinetics of RNP binding to the PM target remains fast and unaffected by the manipulator, and (b) the free energy change for the binding to the PM target is not affected, ensuring that the sensitivity is not compromised. Manipulating the RNP-target binding kinetics and thermodynamics is a strategy to improve the single-nucleotide specificity, but previous work often sacrificed sensitivity. Our kinetic manipulation technique overcame this limitation. Previous approaches, such as directly blocking the spacer region of crRNA using hairpins [14, 32] or occluders [13], could reduce the free energy of RNP formation and slow down the RNP binding to the mismatch target. However, the RNP binding to the PM target also slowed down, resulting in decreases in sensitivity and slow signal generation. Alternatively, adding more mismatches [15, 33] into the crRNA design or shortening the length of the spacer region in the crRNA [34, 35] changed the free energy of the binding product and the thermodynamics for both mismatch and PM targets. In contrast, our kinetic manipulation strategy does not require direct sequence alteration to the crRNA or RNP. Instead, the kinetic manipulators hybridize to the seed-complementary region of the mismatch target, reducing the kinetics of its binding to the RNP. The same kinetic manipulators do not form stable hybrids with the seed-complementary region of the PM target, and thus do not affect the kinetics of RNP binding to the PM target. The rationally designed kinetic manipulators maximized the kinetic differences between the PM and mismatch, in their binding to the RNP, resulting in substantial improvement in the discrimination of single-nucleotide mismatch.
