Interaction between physical frailty and sleep quality on risk of migraine in later life: a cohort study of over 350,000 participants
Authors: Wei Hu, Tao Zhang, Cunxian Jia, Fei Xue, Baopeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty was associated with neurological disorders, but has not been extrapolated to migraine. It’s unclear whether maintaining healthy sleep mitigates this association. We examined the independent, joint, and interactive relationships between frailty status and sleep quality and migraine risk.Methods356,326 participants from the UK Biobank without migraine at baseline were included. The five-component Fried phenotype was used to assess frailty status and categorized participants as non-frail, pre-frail, and frail. A five-factor sleep questionnaire was employed to quantify sleep quality and categorize individuals into poor, intermediate, and healthy sleep. Cox proportional hazard models were applied to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Their interactions on the multiplicative and additive scales were also assessed.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13.6 years, 4,641 (1.3%) participants experienced migraine. Compared with non-frail individuals, the HRs (95% CIs) for migraine were 1.149 (1.079–1.222) and 1.413 (1.225–1.630) for those with pre-frailty and frailty. Individuals with intermediate (HR = 1.170; 95% CI: 1.101–1.243) and poor (HR = 1.353; 95% CI: 1.146–1.598) sleep showed an increased risk of migraine compared to those with healthy sleep. The joint test suggested that frail individuals with poor sleep faced the highest migraine risk (HR = 1.897; 95% CI: 1.339–2.687), with evidence of an additive interaction. Also, healthy sleep weakened the frailty-migraine relationship, with evidence of a multiplicative interaction (P for interaction = 0.014).ConclusionAlthough both frailty and poor sleep quality are associated with a higher risk of migraine, achieving healthy sleep might mitigate the adverse impact of frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25061-5.

Introduction

Migraine is a neurovascular disorder with symptoms including nausea, vomiting, and photophobia [1]. As the second leading cause of disability, over 1 billion individuals worldwide are afflicted by migraine [2, 3]. In light of the low treatment response rate and reversible course [4], the identification of modifiable risk factors for migraine is of paramount importance.

Frailty is marked by reduced multisystem functioning and increased vulnerability to stressors [5], leading to a higher risk of adverse outcomes such as disability, hospitalization, and death [6]. Recently, frailty has garnered significant attention in the neurological disorders [7, 8], but has not yet been extrapolated to migraine. Frailty and migraine may be interconnected due to shared risk factors such as low socioeconomic status (SES), dysfunction, inflammation, and comorbidities [4, 6]. Previous studies have identified components of frailty associated with migraine, such as fatigue [9], reduced grip strength [10], slow walking speed [11], and low physical activity (PA) [12]. Nevertheless, no studies have explored the association between frailty and the risk of migraine.

Accumulating evidence suggested that sleep behaviors, like insomnia [13], short sleep duration [14], snoring [15], daytime sleepiness [16], and late chronotype [17], are associated with migraine. However, most studies have typically focused on the relationship between individual sleep factors and migraine, ignoring the nature of these sleep factors being closely related. Although several existing studies have explored the relationship between sleep quality and migraine by considering multiple sleep factors together, these studies have suffered from small sample sizes, cross-sectional designs, and limited consideration of sleep factors [14, 17–20]. Few studies have considered the five sleep factors collectively to investigate the relationship. Importantly, research has indicated that maintaining healthy sleep can reduce the risk of worsening frailty status [21]. However, whether maintaining healthy sleep quality mitigates the adverse impact of frailty on migraine remains unknown.

Based on a prospective cohort, we investigated the independent, joint, and interactive associations between frailty status, sleep quality, and the risk of migraine.

Methods

Study participants

Data from the UK Biobank, a prospective cohort enrolling over 500,000 adults in England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. Health-related data of the participants were accessed by completing touchscreen questionnaires and undergoing physical and biological examinations [22]. All participants gave informed consent before being recruited. The Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (21/NW/0157) provided ethical approval for the UK Biobank project. In the study, participants who were withdrawn or lost to follow-up (n = 1,297) and those diagnosed with migraine (n = 21,151) at baseline were excluded. Second, we excluded participants with missing data on frailty (n = 36,207) or sleep components (n = 72,930). Third, participants with incomplete data on covariates were excluded (n = 14,390). A total of 356,326 participants were incorporated into the final analysis (Figure S1).

Assessment of frailty status

The frailty phenotype (FPP) developed by Fried et al. and revised by Hanlon et al. to accommodate UK Biobank data was utilized to assess the frailty status of participants in this study [23, 24]. The five-component FPP (weight loss, exhaustion, low PA, slow gait speed, and low grip strength) is the most commonly employed assessment of frailty in clinical and epidemiological studies [25]. Each component was assigned a score of 1 if it met the specified criteria and 0 otherwise, resulting in a continuous frailty score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. Detailed information for each component is provided in Table S1. Participants were categorized as non-frail (0 points), pre-frail (1–2 points), or frail (≥ 3 points) [23–25].

Assessment of sleep quality

Five sleep factors (chronotype, duration, insomnia, snoring, and sleepiness) were included to evaluate participants’ sleep quality [26, 27]. Each factor was categorized as either healthy or unhealthy, whereas healthy sleep consisted of early chronotype, sleep 7–8 h/day, no frequent insomnia, no snoring, and no frequent sleepiness. Each factor was encoded as 1 if it followed the healthy criteria and 0 otherwise. Aggregating the five factors will produce a sleep score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating healthier sleep. Detailed information for sleep behaviors is displayed in Table S2. Participants were classified as poor (0–1 points), intermediate (2–3 points), and healthy (≥ 4 points) sleep [26, 27].

Ascertainment of migraine

Consistent with prior research [28–30], migraine occurring during the follow-up were identified through International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code G43 in the UK Biobank [Data-Field 131052-Date G43 first reported (migraine)], which were sourced from self-reported medical conditions, primary care, and hospital admissions. The follow-up time was calculated as the time interval from the baseline recruitment to the date of migraine diagnosis, death, or the censoring date (31 October 2022 for England, 31 August 2022 for Scotland, and 31 May 2022 for Wales), whichever occurred first.

Assessment of covariates

The study considered multiple covariates, including sociodemographic characteristics [age, sex, ethnicity, educational level, employment status, and Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI)], lifestyle factors [smoking status, alcohol consumption status, sedentary time, diet, and body mass index (BMI)], mental health issues, and medical histories (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer). The TDI encompassed information on social class, employment, and housing, indicating area-level deprivation, where higher scores corresponded to increased deprivation [8]. Sedentary time was calculated by summing the total time spent watching television, using computers outside of work hours, and driving [7]. A healthy diet was defined as fulfilling the ideal intake of at least 5 dietary components [31] (Table S3). Mental health issues were evaluated by asking, “Have you ever seen a psychiatrist for nerves, anxiety, tension, or depression?” [32]. Detailed measurements and missing percentages for the covariates can be found in Tables S4 and S5.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics by migraine status were performed for continuous variables [expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR)] and categorical variables [presented as n (%)]. Cox proportional hazard models were employed to calculate the hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the associations of frailty status and sleep quality with migraine risk. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residual method, and no violations were found.

Multiple analytical strategies were applied. First, we processed the continuous frailty score to determine the HR for each 1-point increase. Second, we analyzed frailty status as a tri-categorical variable, using the non-frail group as the reference. By treating the tri-categorical variable as a continuous variable and using the median values of each category as predictors, we tested the P values for linear trends. Moreover, we assessed the mutually adjusted associations between the five frailty indicators and migraine risk. The cumulative hazard of migraine by frailty status was plotted using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method with Log-rank test. The same analytic approach was used for sleep quality. We conducted three Cox models with stepwise covariate adjustments: Model 1 adjusted for sociodemographic factors and either sleep quality or frailty status; Model 2 added lifestyle factors to Model 1; Model 3 incorporated mental health issues and medical histories into Model 2. After making covariate adjustments in Model 3, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was applied to assess the dose-response associations of frailty and sleep scores with migraine.

Using participants categorized as non-frail and healthy sleep quality as the referent, the joint associations of frailty status and sleep quality categories with migraine were examined by creating nine distinct combinations. The frailty-migraine association was also estimated across sleep quality categories. To investigate whether healthy sleep could mitigate the frailty-migraine association, we fitted a Cox model to test for the multiplicative interaction by introducing an interaction term between frailty status and sleep categories. The likelihood test examined the significance of the multiplicative interaction. We developed an additive interaction model, assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) [33]. Specifically, an additive interaction was confirmed when the 95% CI for the RERI and AP excluded zero [33].

Several secondary analyses evaluated the robustness of the findings. To minimize the impact of potential residual confounders, we adjusted for the following factors at baseline in Model 3: (1) inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD, including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease) and mental disorders (including anxiety, depression, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorder) [4, 34]; (2) environmental risk factors (particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm and noise pollution) [30]; and (3) coffee and tea consumption [35]. To mitigate reverse causation bias, we eliminated participants with other headache syndromes and/or other neurological disorders (PD and dementia) at baseline, as well as migraine cases that occurred within the first two years of follow-up. To address the potential bias of missing data, multiple imputation methods were implemented. To address the potential bias of censoring data, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants with a follow-up time of 15 years or longer, where a jump in the survival curves was observed. Considering that both frailty and sleep quality are closely associated with seasonality, we further adjusted for season at the time of participant recruitment. Subgroup analyses examined the potential modification role of sociodemographic factors, lifestyles, and mental health.

All analyses were conducted with SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, version 9.4) and R Statistical Software (version 4.4.2). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 4,641 (1.3%) migraine cases (9.9 cases/10,000 person-years) were documented during a median follow­up of 13.6 years (IQR: 12.9–14.2). Of the included 356,326 participants [mean age (SD), 56.5 (8.1) years; 53.8% female], 11,443 (3.2%) and 8,340 (2.3%) individuals were categorized as frail and poor sleep. Compared to participants who did not experience migraine, those who did were younger, predominantly female, more likely to be unemployed, living in more deprived areas, and exhibited lower alcohol consumption frequency, unhealthier diets, worse mental health, and pre-existing cardiovascular diseases (Table 1).

Independent association of frailty status with the risk of migraine

KM curves indicated that the cumulative hazard of migraine was highest in frail participants (Log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 1A). A positive linear association between the frailty scores and migraine was observed (nonlinear P = 0.871, Figure S2A). Frail individuals had a 41.3% increased risk of migraine (HR = 1.413; 95% CI: 1.225–1.630) compared to non-frail individuals (P for trend < 0.001, Table 2). The HR (95% CI) for migraine was 1.112 (1.073–1.152) for each 1-point increment in frailty scores (Table 2). The association of individual components of frailty with migraine risk are demonstrated in Table S6. When adjusting for covariates in Model 3, the HRs (95% CI) were 1.066 (0.987–1.152) for weight loss; 1.457 (1.348–1.575) for exhaustion; 1.133 (1.033–1.243) for low PA; 1.086 (0.973–1.213) for slow gait speed; and 0.987 (0.905–1.077) for low grip strength. After mutually adjusting for the five frailty indicators, we observed that exhaustion continued to be the strongest predictor (HR = 1.450; 95% CI: 1.341–1.568) of migraine.

Independent association of and sleep quality with the risk of migraine

The highest cumulative hazard of migraine was observed in participants with poor sleep (Fig. 1B). A linear association of sleep score with migraine was observed (nonlinear P = 0.949, Figure S2B). Compared to healthy sleepers, individuals with intermediate and poor sleep were associated with 17.0% (10.1%−24.3%) and 35.3% (14.6%−59.8%) elevated risk of migraine (P for trend < 0.001, Table 2). The HR (95% CI) was 0.900 (0.875–0.926) for each 1-point increase in the sleep scores (Table 2). Healthy sleep duration (HR = 0.885; 95% CI: 0.831–0.943) and no frequent insomnia (HR = 0.769; 95% CI: 0.721–0.819) were associated with migraine (Table S7).

Joint and interaction analyses of frailty status and sleep with migraine

Compared to the referent, frail individuals with poor sleep faced the highest risk of migraine (HR = 1.897; 95% CI: 1.339–2.687) (Fig. 2). Among frail individuals with unhealthy sleep quality, the RERI and AP were 0.575 (95% CI: 0.150–0.999) and 0.310 (95% CI: 0.107–0.512), respectively, suggesting a 0.575 relative excess risk due to the additive interaction, contributing to 31.0% of the risk of migraine in these individuals (Table S8). Additionally, in analyses stratified by sleep quality, healthy sleep mitigated the increased risk of migraine due to being frail (P for multiplicative interaction = 0.014) (Table 3). In the healthy, intermediate, and poor sleep stratification, the HRs (95% CI) for frail individuals was 1.135 (0.858–1.502), 1.602 (1.332–1.927), and 1.504 (0.886–2.556), respectively (Table 3).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The associations of frailty and sleep with migraine were consistent with the primary analysis in the sensitive analyses: (1) adjusting for mental disorders, IBD, environmental risk factors, and coffee and tea consumption (Tables S9-11); (2) eliminating individuals with other headache syndromes, PD, and dementia at baseline (Table S12); (3) eliminating individuals with migraine within the first two years of follow-up (Table S13); (4) using imputed covariates (Table S14); (5) excluding participants with a follow-up time of 15 years or longer (Table S15); and (6) adjusting for season at the time of participant recruitment (Table S16). The association between frailty and migraine risk was similar across subgroups except for gender and age subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Table S17). Specifically, the detrimental impact of frailty on migraine was more pronounced in younger participants (P for interaction < 0.001) and females (P for interaction = 0.042) compared to older adults and males.

Discussion

The study observed that frailty and poor sleep were associated with the increased risk of migraine. Frail individuals with poor sleep were associated with the highest relative risk of migraine. Additionally, the study provided quantitative evidence of the additive and multiplicative interactions of frailty and poor sleep on migraine risk. Healthy sleep could diminish the harmful association between frailty and migraine risk.

For the first time, the current study explored the association between frailty and the risk of migraine. Our findings complemented and extended previous evidence that frailty with associated neurological disorders. A prospective cohort study revealed that frailty was associated with an 87% increased risk of PD [8]. Another cohort study involving over 140,000 participants revealed that frail individuals have a 1.98 times higher risk of dementia compared to non-frail individuals [7]. Similar to these findings, we observed that frailty with associated with the risk of migraine. The precise mechanisms underlying the association are yet unknown, but common risk factors like fatigue, low PA, slow gait, low grip strength, low SES, comorbidities, and similar pathologic mechanisms like multisystem dysfunction and inflammatory disorders might explain the relationship [4, 6, 9–12]. Among the five components of frailty, fatigue was most strongly associated with migraine risk. Supporting our findings, a symptom-based study suggested that anxiety-related fatigue was associated with an increased risk of migraine [9]. The fatigue-migraine link could be attributed to underlying neurobiological pathways such as dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine [36]. Moreover, the frailty-migraine association was more pronounced among females and younger individuals. One possible explanation is that the prevalence of migraine is higher among females and young adults [37]. Given the reversibility of frailty and the relatively low cost of measurement [5], early screening to identify those in the pre-frailty stage may have far-reaching impact on migraine prevention.

Sleep problems were prevalent in migraine patients, yet most of studies on the relationship between sleep problems and migraine have tested one sleep factor at a time [13, 16, 17, 29, 38]. Prior studies demonstrated that insomnia increased the risk of migraine [13, 38]. Based on UK Biobank data, Lei et al. observed that either short or long sleep duration was associated with an increased risk of migraine [29]. A cross-sectional survey of 21,177 individuals revealed that migraineurs were more likely to report excessive daytime sleepiness [16]. Nevertheless, sleep factors are generally interrelated, meaning that changes in one sleep factor tend to trigger modifications in other factors. Hence, it is essential to examine the relationship between overall sleep and migraine. A meta-analysis of 32 cross-sectional studies revealed that migraineurs exhibited poorer sleep quality compared to healthy individuals [20]. Still, there is a paucity of studies investigating the association between composite sleep and the risk of migraine. The study validated and extended previous work by combining five sleep factors to examine the longitudinal association. Our findings not only reinforced the relationships between individual sleep-related factors (e.g., sleep duration and insomnia) and migraine [13, 29, 38] but also provided first-hand evidence that healthy sleep is beneficial in reducing the risk of migraine.

This study revealed an interaction between frailty and poor sleep in increasing migraine risk. The increased risk of migraine associated with frailty was mitigated by healthy sleep. Supporting our findings, a prospective study among Chinese adults concluded that maintaining healthy sleep could reduce the risk of transitioning from non-frailty to frailty [21]. Huang et al., utilizing the same cohort data as our study, discovered that healthy sleep reduced the heightened risk of mortality associated with low PA (a component of frailty) [32]. Furthermore, we found that individuals with both poor sleep and frailty faced the highest risk of migraine, emphasizing the necessity of simultaneously considering both in migraine. Significantly, the additive interaction identified in our study hinted that unhealthy sleep and frailty synergistically augmented the risk of migraine. Approximately 57.5% of migraine risk could be attributed to this interaction effect, meaning that the joint effect of simultaneous exposure to unhealthy sleep and frailty was much stronger than the sum of the simple addition of their individual effects. The underlying mechanism of this interaction remains unclear. Poor sleep and frailty have both been associated with decreased growth hormones (e.g., insulin-like growth factor-1) and sex hormones (e.g., testosterone), increased oxidative stress, elevated inflammatory factors, and neurological dysregulation (e.g., serotonin and dopamine) [39]. The complex interplay between these biological factors might thereby exacerbate the increased risk of migraine [4].

The study was the first to examine the associations of frailty and healthy sleep with the risk of migraine. The prospective design with a large sample size, extended follow-up, sufficient adjustment for confounders, and multiple robust sensitivity analyses reinforced the reliability of our findings. Another significant innovation of the study was to explore the joint and interactive associations of frailty and poor sleep with migraine risk. The observed interaction facilitated the precision prevention targeted at-risk populations. The study has several limitations. First, while studies have indicated replicable risk factor associations in the UK Biobank [40], the low response rate (5.5%) in the cohort might introduce selection bias, potentially impacting the representativeness of the findings. Second, the impact of residual confounders is unavoidable in observational studies, despite adjusting for a range of confounders. Third, this study is its design as a prospective cohort study, which, while providing valuable insights into associations over time, does not allow for definitive causal inferences. Future research, including randomized controlled trials, would be valuable in confirming the causal nature of these associations. Fourth, although previous high-quality studies based on the UK Biobank cohort have validated the predictive validity of the same measures of sleep and physical frailty as in the present study for a wide range of disease outcomes [24, 26, 27], the reliance on these self-reported measures introduces the potential for measurement bias due to their subjective nature. Future research should consider using more objective and clinically validated assessment methods to improve accuracy. Fifth, a potential limitation of this study is ascertainment bias, as the mean age of participants (56.5 years) is above the typical peak incidence of migraines, potentially capturing individuals with previously unreported migraines. Despite baseline exclusion of prevalent migraine cases, the overlap with other conditions may still influence diagnosis. Finally, the study’s participants were predominantly White, highlighting the necessity of replicating the findings across diverse ethnic groups.

Conclusion

The large cohort study found that frailty and poor sleep were associated with higher migraine risk. The stratification and multiplicative interaction analyses observed that achieving healthy sleep might mitigate the adverse impact of physical frailty. The joint and additive interaction analyses observed that frailty and poor sleep may synergistically increase the risk of migraine, with the highest risk in frail individuals exhibiting poor sleep. Our findings provide preliminary clues that targeting both frailty and sleep interventions may reduce the burden of migraine.
