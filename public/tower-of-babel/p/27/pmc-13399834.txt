Arthroscopic Anterior Talofibular Ligament Repair Without Anchors for Chronic Lateral Ankle Instability
Authors: Semih Yaş, Orkun Büyüksayın, Muhammet Özsoy, Mustafa Can Erdem, Mehmet Ali Tokgöz, Ulunay Kanatlı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, arthroscopic anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) repair techniques have been increasingly used in the treatment of chronic ankle instability. Arthroscopic approaches not only allow for the management of concomitant intra‐articular pathologies but also help minimize wound‐related complications associated with open surgery and improve the assessment of anatomical structures. Ankle sprains are common and tend to recur, often leading to pain and chronic lateral ankle instability. The lateral ankle ligament complex consists of the ATFL, the posterior talofibular ligament, and the calcaneofibular ligament. The primary function of the ATFL is to provide stability by preventing anterior translation and limiting internal rotation of the talus. In this report, we describe our surgical technique for arthroscopic ATFL repair without the use of anchors in patients with chronic ankle instability. By performing this technique without any implant, we aim to reduce costs and eliminate implant‐related complications.

Ankle sprains are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries, accounting for up to 20% of all sports‐related traumas. 1 Although most patients recover uneventfully with conservative treatment, up to 20% to 40% may develop chronic lateral chronic ankle instability (CAI), resulting in recurrent sprains, pain, and functional limitations. 2 The lateral ligament complex of the ankle consists of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), and posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL), with the ATFL being the structure most frequently injured during inversion sprains. 3

Surgical management is indicated for symptomatic CAI when conservative measures fail. Open anatomic repair techniques such as the Broström or Broström‐Gould procedures have long been considered the gold standard; however, wound complications, postoperative stiffness, and limited visualization of intra‐articular pathology remain concerns. 4 With the evolution of arthroscopic techniques, arthroscopic ATFL repair has gained popularity as it allows for minimally invasive access, better visualization, and simultaneous management of associated intra‐articular lesions. 5

The purpose of this report is to describe an arthroscopic technique for ATFL repair without the use of anchors, in which the elongated ATFL remnant is sutured to the Bassett's ligament to restore lateral ankle stability.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications and Contraindication

This technique is indicated for symptomatic chronic lateral ankle instability. The contraindication for this procedure is insufficient quality of the ATFL for repair.

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine, and a pneumatic tourniquet is applied to the operative limb. The feet are allowed to hang freely beyond the distal edge of the operating table. After appropriate skin preparation and draping, the standard ankle arthroscopy portals—anteromedial (AM), anterolateral (AL), and accessory anterolateral (AAL)—are marked.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

For diagnostic arthroscopy, the ankle joint is first distended with 20 cc of saline through the AM portal. A skin incision is then made for the AM portal using a No. 15 scalpel blade. The capsule is penetrated with the help of a clamp, and visualization is initiated through the AM portal using a 30° arthroscope. The location for the AL portal is determined with a needle, which is visualized intra‐articularly, and the AL portal is created using an outside‐in technique. The AL portal serves as the working portal. Soft tissue debridement is performed through the AL portal using a shaver. With the arthroscope in the AM portal, intraoperative inversion and anterior drawer tests are performed to assess ankle instability. After confirming the diagnosis of ATFL rupture, the elongated ATFL on the lateral side of the ankle is evaluated for tissue quality. If the tissue is deemed suitable for plication, ATFL repair is performed.

Arthroscopic ATFL Repair Without Using Anchors

With the ankle held in dorsiflexion, the soft tissues on the lateral side are excised using a shaver to expose the elongated ATFL and the Bassett's ligament. To assist in suture management during ATFL repair, an AAL portal is created using an outside‐in technique with a needle. Through the AAL portal, a suture lasso (Doratek Suture Shuttle 90°, Ankara, Türkiye) is used to pass a shuttle suture through the Bassett's ligament. Using this shuttle suture, a reinforced suture (Doratek Strong Braided UHMWPE Suture without needle, Ankara, Türkiye) is then passed through the Bassett's ligament. Next, another shuttle suture is passed through the elongated ATFL using the suture lasso. The reinforced suture from the Bassett's ligament and the shuttle suture from the ATFL are retrieved through the AL portal. Subsequently, both sutures are passed through the AAL portal so that the reinforced suture also passes through the ATFL. In this way, a single free suture passes through both the Bassett's ligament and the ATFL. The limb of the suture emerging from the Bassett's ligament is then used as a post, and a sliding locking knot (Samsung Medical Center technique) is tied. Rehabilitation

In the postoperative period, a short leg posterior splint is applied with the ankle in a neutral position. The patient is kept nonweightbearing with the splint for 2 weeks. Between the 2nd and 6th weeks, the splint is replaced with a walking boot, and the patient is allowed to bear weight on the operated side as tolerated while wearing the boot. After the 6th week, the walking boot is removed, and the patient is followed up without any movement restrictions.

DISCUSSION

This article describes a surgical technique for restoring ankle stability in patients with CAI due to ATFL rupture. The technique involves augmenting the ATFL remnant to the Bassett's ligament without the use of any implants. By avoiding the use of implants, this method aims to eliminate implant‐related complications. 6 , 7 , 8

The Bassett's ligament, a thickened accessory ligament located beneath the anterior‐inferior tibiofibular ligament, was first described in the literature in a cadaveric series of 7 specimens. 9 In a study conducted by Akseki et al., it was reported that this ligament was present in 83% of cadaveric ankles. 10 Subsequently, in a study by Nikolopoulos et al., it was reported that this ligament was present in 92% of cadaveric ankles. 11 In another study, Dalmau et al. showed connections between the ATFL and the PTFL, between the ATFL and the CFL, and between the CFL and PTFL in all examined ankles. They noted that these connections could have important implications for arthroscopic lateral ligament repair and reconstructive procedures. 12 Cordier et al. reported that the connecting fibers between the inferior fibers of the ATFL and the CFL are strong enough to transmit tissue tension from the ATFL to the CFL. They also suggested that in proximal lesions involving the inferior fibers of the ATFL and the CFL, the ligaments can be repaired with a single‐suture technique. 13 Using this technique, we aimed to eliminate instability in patients with lateral CAI by suturing the elongated ATFL to the Bassett's ligament, thereby increasing the tension of the lateral ankle ligament complex.

The described technique also has some disadvantages. The first is that it does not constitute an anatomic repair of the ATFL. Additionally, augmenting the ATFL to the Bassett's ligament, which has been associated with anterior ankle impingement, may lead to impingement syndromes. 14 The learning curve for this surgical procedure is long, and it should be performed by a surgeon experienced in ankle arthroscopy. Furthermore, this repair technique can only be applied in patients with a repairable ATFL remnant and a present Bassett's ligament.
