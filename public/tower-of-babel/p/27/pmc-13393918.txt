Heat shock protein 60-targeted peptide-conjugated platinum nanozyme: redox regulation by multi-enzyme activities induces mitochondrial reprogramming and acute leukemia cells fate switch
Authors: Jing Yao, Yanyan Li, Yiling Meng, Tao Wang, Jie Meng, Jian Liu, Tao Wen, Haiyan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is characterized by differentiation arrest and apoptosis resistance, frequently with mitochondrial dysfunction and aberrant heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) overexpression as key drivers of malignant progression. Platinum-based nanozymes exhibit multi-enzymatic activity but lack organelle specificity, limiting their therapy efficacy. Based on that there are abundant HSP60 in mitochondrial, we constructed a peptide-conjugated nanozyme PtNPs-P17 by conjugation platinum nanoparticle (PtNPs) and HSP60-targeting peptide P17. It was shown that PtNPs-P17 retained intrinsic oxidase (OXD), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT)-like activities while acquiring the targeting ability. In the myeloperoxidase (MPO)-positive AML cell line MOLM-13, PtNPs-P17 specifically localized to mitochondria in cells, significantly increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), oxygen and especially singlet oxygen due to its multi-enzymatic redox regulatory function, which disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential and triggered mitophagy evidenced by the elevated LC3BII/actin ratio and LC3B-mitochondria co-localization. Moreover, PtNPs-P17 induced dual inhibition of mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis, leading to total NAD levels reduced. These metabolic perturbations together resulted in the differentiation of the AML cells, with up-regulated levels of CD235a, CD11b, and CD41a, and enhanced apoptosis. In a refractory AML mouse model, PtNPs-P17 significantly down-regulated the HSP60 expression while reduced spleen GFP+ leukemia cells, suggesting significantly enhanced anti-leukemia effects compared to PtNPs. In conclusion, it was demonstrated that PtNPs-P17 targeted mitochondrial HSP60 to induce mitophagy by orchestrating multi-enzymatic redox regulation and mitochondrial reprogramming, thereby overcoming AML differentiation block and apoptosis resistance. This study provides a novel AML therapy option via precise mitochondrial targeting and metabolic intervention, highlighting the translational potential of HSP60-targeted multi-enzyme nanozymes.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04596-8.

Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant proliferative disorder originating from hematopoietic stem cells, characterized by the accumulation of immature myeloid cells in the bone marrow due to a differentiation block [1, 2]. This pathological accumulation disrupts normal hematopoiesis, leading to bone marrow failure [3]. For decades, chemotherapy is recognized as the standard intensive chemotherapy for AML, but prolonged administration leads to significant adverse effects and the emergence of resistance [4]. While the advent of targeted therapies has led to substantial progress in treatment over recent years [5], achieving consistently satisfactory therapeutic results remains an unresolved issue.

Accumulating evidence indicates that reactive oxygen species (ROS) plays a dual regulatory role in the occurrence and development of AML [6]. ROS at normal physiological level participates in normal physiological processes as a signal molecule, such as cell proliferation and differentiation. But for AML, the cellular ROS is usually at abnormal high levels to induce genomic instability, activate cancerous signaling pathways and escape immune surveillance, which provide proliferation and survival advantages for leukemia cells. At the same time, AML cells maintain ROS by remodeling the antioxidant system, forming a unique redox adaptive phenotype, which is also an important reason for its chemotherapy resistance [7]. Based on this, to developing approaches that can break the redox balance of AML cells to induce effective apoptosis represents one of promising therapeutic strategies [8]. Pioneering studies have been performed using anti-oxidant small molecules such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and glutathione to regulate the level of ROS to intervene the progression of leukemia [9]. Nevertheless, fast metabolism rate and lack of cell target are challenging to achieve accurate and long-term regulation of intracellular ROS [10].

Nanozymes provides new powerful tools to break through the above bottleneck, because of the properties of easy modification and enzyme-like activity for longer time than small molecules [11]. As a typical nanozyme, platinum nanoparticles (PtNPs) has been proved to have multi-enzyme activities including oxidase (OXD), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) [12, 13], which exhibited therapeutic potential in solid tumor [14]. The application potentials of Pt-based nanozyme in leukemia has been explored in recent years [15]. We previously reported that a core-shell Au@Pt nanozyme promotes chronic myeloid leukemia K562 cells to differentiate to mature-like megakaryocytes by inducing spatio-temporal regulation of ROS fluctuations [16]. A Janus structure formed by Fe3O4 nanoparticles and PtNPs was linked with CXCR4 antagonistic peptide E5 (Fe3O4@Pt@E5) induce a large amount of ROS with cascade catalytic reaction and effectively kill AML cells and significantly prolong the survival time of mice [17]. These results suggest Pt-based nanozyme may play crucial roles in blood cancer treatments through regulating cellular ROS.

ROS is produced mainly in mitochondria, while AML cells are highly dependent on the coordinated metabolic mode of mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis to maintain energy supply [8]. Destroy of mitochondrial function can break the metabolic adaptation advantage of leukemia cells by increasing ROS level, activating mitochondrial apoptosis pathway and inducing mitochondrial autophagy [18]. Heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) is over expressed by AML cells, in particular, largely locating in mitochondria as well as distributing on the cell membrane [19]. It is well known that HSP60 plays key roles in mitochondrial homeostasis maintenance and oxidative stress response [20] to mediate chemotherapy resistance and poor prognosis in AML patients [21], therefore suggesting an attractive target for therapy and detection. We previously developed a chemically synthesized peptide targeting HSP60, named as P17 [22]. Moreover, when P17 was bound to gold nanorods, the resulting conjugate, AuNRs-P17 increases the cellular uptake and can co-locate with the mitochondria of murine breast cancer tumor cells due to its specific binding to HSP60, facilitating photothermal disruption of mitochondria and leading to the effective photothermal immunotherapy against the triple-negative breast cancer [23]. Therefore, HSP60 is a dual-site target in cell membrane and mitochondrial for AML cell targeting and ROS regulation.

In this study, the HSP60-targeting peptide P17 was conjugated to PtNPs (PtNPs-P17). The conjugate was applied to the AML cell line MOLM-13, a well-characterized human AML cell line of FAB M5a subtype and is myeloperoxidase (MPO)-positive [24, 25], as well as to a refractory AML mouse model. We investigated whether PtNPs-P17 was able to locate in mitochondria and regulate cellular ROS in the cells and explored the therapeutic potential of PtNPs-P17 against AML. It was shown that PtNPs-P17 was internalized by the cells and localized to mitochondria inducing oxidative stress and exerting dual inhibition on both oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis pathways by its multi-enzymatic nanozyme activity, and finally leading to multi-directional differentiations and enhanced apoptosis of AML cells.

Synthesis and characterizations of PtNPs and PtNPs-P17

K2PtCl4 (1 mL, 16 mM) was mixed with sodium citrate (1 mL, 40 mM) in 38 mL deionized water, followed by stirring at 400 rpm and 30 °C for 30 min. Upon the dropwise addition of the ice-cold NaBH4 solution, the reaction mixture changed color from colorless to light brown, indicating the formation of PtNPs. After reacting for an additional hour, the solution was purified by ultrafiltration (4400 rpm, 10 min) to obtain the final PtNPs. The retentate was collected, resuspended in deionized water, and stored at 4 °C. PtNPs-P17 was prepared by co-incubating the peptide P17 solution with PtNPs. Specifically, an aqueous mixture containing 0.8 µM P17 and 20 nM PtNPs was incubated at 30 °C for 40 min. Following centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 5 min), the supernatant was discarded to remove unbound P17. The pellet was then collected, resuspended in deionized water, and stored at 4 °C as the PtNPs-P17 for subsequent use.

The morphology of the nanoparticles (NPs) was examined via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, TEM-1400 plus, JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) on a copper net. The absorption spectrum of PtNPs-P17 was measured by a UV-Vis-NIR spectrometer (UH5300, Hitachi High-Technologies, Japan). The hydrodynamic diameter was determined by a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (NanoBrook Omni, Brookhaven Instruments Corporation, USA). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha X-ray photoelectron spectrometer.

Determination of NPs concentration

Firstly, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800, USA) was employed to determine the total concentration of Pt atoms in a given nanoparticle suspension. Based on the molar volume of platinum (9.1 cm3/mol), the total volume (V) of all NPs in the suspension can be derived. From the TEM images, the diameter of the NPs was measured. Assuming spherical morphology, the average volume of a single NP (V₀) can be calculated. Consequently, the molar concentration of NPs in the suspension can be estimated as V/(AV₀), where A is the Avogadro constant. During the synthesis, the absorption spectrum of NPs at an identical particle concentration was obtained. Therefore, according to the Lambert-Beer law, which states that absorbance is proportional to concentration, the particle concentrations of NPs can be estimated.

Measurement of enzyme-like activities of NPs

OXD-like activity:

In this study, OPD was employed as the chromogenic substrate to assess the OXD-like activity of NPs under different pH conditions: acetate buffer (pH 3.9) and phosphate buffers (pH 7.4 and 9.4). A reaction mixture was prepared in each microplate well by sequentially adding 20 µL of NPs (5 nM), 10 µL of OPD (10 g/L), and 170 µL of buffer at the indicated pH (3.9, 7.4, or 9.4). After mixing, the absorbance at 431 nm was monitored every 10 s using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

POD-like activity:

Using an OPD-based colorimetric assay in the presence of H2O2, we investigated the POD-like activity of NPs under different pH conditions: acetate buffer (pH 3.9) and phosphate buffers (pH 7.4 and 9.4). First, a mixture was prepared with 20 µL NPs (3 nM), 10 µL OPD (10 g/L), and 150 µL buffer at the specified pH. Then, 20 µL of 3% H2O2 was added to start the reaction. The reaction mixture was quickly transferred to a microplate reader, and absorbance at 431 nm was taken and recorded every 15 s.

SOD-like activity:

Electron spin resonance (ESR) was used to assess the SOD-like activity of NPs under ambient conditions. In phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), superoxide anion radicals (•O2⁻) were generated by reacting hypoxanthine (1 mM) with xanthine oxidase (0.005 U/mL) in the presence of DTPA (0.05 mM), and subsequently trapped by BMPO (2.5 mM) to form the stable BMPO/•OOH spin adduct for ESR detection. NPs were then introduced into the reaction system at a final concentration of 2 nM. The sample solution (50 µL) was loaded into a 1 mm inner diameter glass capillary tube and the spectrum was recorded by Magnettech ESR spectrometer (MS-5000X, Germany).

CAT-like activity:

Observation of the oxygen bubbles for CAT-like activity of NPs was performed firstly. In a quartz cuvette, 635 µL buffer (at specified pH), 15 µL NPs (5 nM), and 850 µL 3% H2O2 were mixed, and the resulting bubble formation after 5 min was showed. The CAT-like activity of NPs was also assessed using ESR spectroscopy with 15N-PDT as the spin probe. A mixture was prepared with 15N-PDT (0.2 mM), NPs (0.1 nM) and buffer at the target pH. The reaction was then started by the addition of H2O2. After mixing, the sample was transferred to a glass capillary and both the ends were sealed. The ESR spectrum was recorded after reaction for 10 min.

Cell culture

AML cell line MOLM-13, OCI-AML3 and U937 were obtained from the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital (Tianjin, China) and cultured in modified RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 U/ ml) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Mononuclear cells preparation

For human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) preparation, approximately 12 mL of whole blood of a healthy volunteer was transferred from the collection tube to a 50 mL centrifuge tube and diluted with an equal volume (12 mL) of sterile PBS. Following dilution, the blood was gently overlaid onto 4 mL of human lymphocyte separation medium (equilibrated to room temperature) in three separate centrifuge tubes, ensuring no mixing between the two phases. The tubes were centrifuged at 800 × g for 20 min at room temperature, with low acceleration and slow braking settings. Subsequent to centrifugation, three distinct layers were observed: plasma (upper), Ficoll solution (middle), and erythrocytes (lower). PBMCs were collected from the white, cloudy interface layer situated between the plasma and Ficoll layers.

To prepare bone marrow mononuclear cells of mice, femurs and tibiae were aseptically dissected from healthy mice bilaterally. Attached muscle tissues were carefully removed while preserving the integrity of the periosteum. Using a syringe, the diluent from the mouse lymphocyte separation kit was injected into the bone marrow cavity through the end of the bone to repeatedly flush out the bone marrow, and the resulting cell suspension was collected. This suspension was gently layered onto Ficoll separation solution and centrifuged at 800 × g for 20 min at room temperature with low acceleration and slow braking. After centrifugation, three distinct layers were formed from top to bottom: the diluent layer, the Ficoll separation solution layer, and the erythrocyte layer. The white, cloudy interface layer between the diluent and Ficoll layers represented the mouse bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMNCs).

Cellular uptake assay

MOLM-13 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well and exposed to 4 nM NPs for 4 h. For the HSP60 blockade group, MOLM-13 cells were first incubated with an anti-HSP60 antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After centrifugation to remove unbound antibodies, the cells were then treated with 4 nM PtNPs-P17. Then, the cells were washed by PBS buffer for 3 times to wash off the loosely bound NPs. The extinction spectra of 200 µL of cells suspension (1 × 105 cells in PBS) were measured by a microplate reader. Correspondingly, 200 µL of PBS was set as absorbance background. The extinction spectra of the cells in each group were measured to assess the extent of NPs uptake by subtracting the extinction spectra of cells without any treatment.

TEM assay

TEM was employed to investigate the subcellular localization of NPs and its effects on organelle morphology after 3 h of co-incubation with cells. MOLM-13 cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes at 1 × 107 cells per sample and co-incubated with 4 nM NPs for 3 h. Following incubation, the cells were collected and washed once with PBS. Each pellet was resuspended in 700 µL PBS, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was carefully aspirated. Subsequently, the cell pellets were fixed by slowly adding 1 mL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde to avoid disturbance. After removal of the fixative, samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 h. Following standard dehydration and embedding, ultrathin Sects. (70–90 nm) were cut and doubly stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate (each for 5–10 min) prior to TEM observation.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential

The effect of NPs on mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using JC-1 assay. The fluorescence shift was analyzed both quantitatively via flow cytometry and qualitatively via fluorescence microscopy. After seeding at a density of 2 × 105 cells per sample in a 24-well plate, MOLM-13 cells were co-incubated with 4 nM NPs for 3 h. The positive control was established by treating the cells with 0.5 mM carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) for 30 min. For JC-1 staining, cells were washed with PBS and subsequently incubated with a 10 µg/mL JC-1 working solution at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. And then, cells were subjected to flow cytometric analysis (Accuri™ C6, BD Biosciences, USA), where the change in the red-to-green fluorescence intensity ratio was calculated. For microscopic observation, cells were stained with JC-1 as described, then seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated confocal dishes. Prior to observation, the cells were allowed to settle naturally in the dark for 20 min. Subsequently, the distribution and intensity of red and green fluorescence were immediately recorded under a fluorescence microscope (EVOS M7000, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Confocal microscopy assay

For the detection of HSP60 distribution in MOLM-13 cells:

MOLM-13 cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were first stained with 200 nM MitoTracker Red at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min to label mitochondria. After staining, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at room temperature for 1 h to reduce non-specific binding. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-HSP60 primary antibody for 2 h, followed by incubation with FITC-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After thorough washing with PBS, the cells were resuspended in 200 µL of PBS, and cytospin preparations were prepared using a cytocentrifuge. The slides were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium to stain cell nuclei, and images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

For the detection of LC3B localization in MOLM-13 cells:

MOLM-13 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at 2 × 105 cells/well and co-incubated with 4 nM NPs for 3 h. Following treatment, cells were collected, washed once with PBS, and stained with 200 nM MitoTracker Red FM at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% FBS. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-LC3B primary antibody, followed by a FITC-conjugated secondary antibody. After PBS washes, cells were prepared by cytospin and mounted for observation using a confocal microscope.

Detect the content of 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG)

MOLM-13 cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well and treated with 32 nM PtNPs for 72 h. The culture supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 5 min to remove cellular debris, and the level of 8-OHdG in the supernatant was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of total ROS concentration

The total intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH-DA. Briefly, MOLM-13 cells were seeded in a 48-well or 24-well plate at 2 × 105 cells per well and treated with various concentrations of NPs or P17 for 24 h. Then, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by adding PBS, and intracellular fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry.

Oxygen detection

MOLM-13 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 48-well cell culture plates. Subsequently, Ru(dpp)3Cl2 with a final concentration of 6.7 mg/L was added, and the cells were incubated for 16 h. After washing the cells once with PBS, 4 nM NPs was added to incubate with cells for 24 h. Then, the fluorescence intensity of Ru(dpp)3Cl2 was measured using flow cytometry.

Singlet oxygen detection

MOLM-13, OCI-AML3 or U937 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and treated with 4 nM NPs for 72 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with HBSS buffer, and incubated with 100 nM Si-DMA working solution at 37 °C for 45 min. After washing with HBSS, the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the fluorescence intensity for each group.

HClO detection

The intracellular level of hypochlorous acid (HClO) in MOLM-13 cells was measured using the specific fluorescent probe HKOCl-4. MOLM-13 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and co-incubated with 4 nM NPs for 48 h. After incubation, all cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 200 µL of 5 µM HKOCl-4 probe at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Then, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in 200 µL PBS. Finally, the solutions were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Evaluation of oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis functions

Detection of cell oxygen consumption rates (OCR):

The XF culture plate was coated with poly-L-lysine, followed by rinsing with sterile PBS. Prior to cell seeding, MOLM-13 cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well and cultured until adhesion was complete, after which the culture medium was removed. Treatment groups were incubated with fresh medium containing 4 nM NPs, while the control group received medium without NPs. After an additional 24-hour incubation, the cellular OCR of each group was measured using a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) with the Seahorse XF mitochondrial stress test kit.

Detection of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR):

MOLM-13 cells were seeded at 8 × 104 cells per well on prepared XF plates and allowed to adhere. For treatments, the medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing 4 nM NPs. For controls, it was replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium only. The cultures were then maintained for 24 h. Glycolytic capacity was evaluated by measuring the ECAR using a Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit and a Seahorse XFe24 analyzer (Agilent Technologies).

Detection on the activity of phosphofructokinase (PFK)

MOLM-13 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well, and then treated with 4 nM PtNPs or PtNPs-P17 for 24 h. After the treatment, the cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed. The PFK activity was detected using a commercial PFK activity assay kit following the manufacturer’s instructions.

NAD+/NADH assay

MOLM-13 cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in a 12-well plate and treated with 32 nM NPs for 24 h. Cells were collected and washed three times in ice-cold PBS. The cellular content of NADtotal and NADH were determined using a NAD+/NADH assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed to investigate the expression of the autophagy-related protein LC3B. Cells were plated in a 6-well plate at 1 × 106 cells per well and treated with 4 nM NPs for 24 h. Collected cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated sequentially with the anti-LC3B primary antibody and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Finally, immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence.

Wright-Giemsa staining

MOLM-13 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate at 2 × 105 cells per well and treated with 32 nM NPs for 24 h. The cells were then washed once with PBS and resuspended. Cell slides were prepared via cytospin centrifugation. Next, the cell smears were then outlined with a hydrophobic barrier pen, fixed with 70% ethanol, and air-dried. Subsequently, they were stained with Wright-Giemsa solution, rinsed gently with deionized water, and air-dried again. Finally, the slides were mounted with nail polish and observed under a microscope.

Detection of cell differentiation markers

MOLM-13 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and treated with 32 nM NPs. In the NAC treatment experiment, MOLM-13 cells were first pretreated with 10 mM NAC at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, culture medium containing NPs was added, and the concentration of NAC was adjusted to 5 mM. The cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. To evaluate the effect of singlet oxygen, cells were pretreated with 2 mM L-histidine (1 h, 37 °C) prior to the addition of NPs. During the subsequent 24 h incubation with NPs, the L-histidine concentration was maintained at 1 mM. After the treatment, cells were collected, washed once with PBS, and stained by incubation with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD235a, CD41a, CD11b, and Ter119 for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Unbound antibodies were removed by washing, and cells were resuspended in 200 µL PBS for flow cytometric analysis.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (q-RT PCR)

The RNA was extracted by Trizol reagent (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and reverse transcribed to cDNA. q-RT PCR was performed using SYBR probe (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) and fluorescence was detected with a CFX96 Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA). Apoptosis assay

At a seeding density of 2 × 105 cells/well in a 48-well plate, MOLM-13 cells were treated with NPs at 32 nM or 16 nM for 72 h. With adding autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, MOLM-13 cells were pretreated with 2 mM 3-MA for 1 h, followed by replacement with fresh medium containing 32 nM PtNPs-P17 and 1 mM 3-MA for 72 h. For BMMNCs from healthy mice, the cells were treated with NPs at 16 nM for 24 h. Then, the cells were washed once with PBS. Cells were then subjected to Annexin V-APC and PI double-staining following the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentages of early and late apoptotic cells were determined by flow cytometric analysis.

In vivo therapeutic effect of NPs

Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (body weight 17–19 g) were firstly subjected to X-ray irradiation (dose: 450 cGy, dose rate: 200 cGy/min). Approximately 4 h post-irradiation, a refractory AE & CKITD816V mouse model was established by intravenous injection via the tail vein with 200 µL of a splenic single-cell suspension (containing approximately 1 × 106 cells) derived from donor mice. On day 7 post-transplantation, the percentage of GFP⁺ cells in peripheral blood was determined. Based on this, mice were randomly allocated into three groups: Control, PtNPs, and PtNPs-P17. Both PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 were dissolved in 5% glucose solution. Starting on day 8, treatments were administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 8 mg/kg. The control group received an equal volume of 5% glucose solution. Injections were performed every other day for a total of two doses. On day 12 post-modeling, mice were euthanized, and spleens were collected.

Ex vivo effects of PtNPs-P17 on spleen cells from AML mice

For ex vivo efficacy assessment, primary GFP+ leukemic cells were isolated from the spleens of AML mice. Briefly, mice were sacrificed, and spleens were aseptically removed, rinsed with PBS, and mechanically dissociated through a cell strainer. The resulting GFP+ cell suspension was plated at 4 × 106 cells per well in a 24-well plate and exposed to 16 nM NPs for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were collected for differentiation and apoptosis assays.

Detection of HSP60 expression levels

AML mouse spleen cells were treated with 16 nM NPs for 24 h. Following collection, cells were washed with PBS and pelleted by centrifugation. MOLM-13 cells were first pretreated with 3-MA at 2 mM for 1 h at 37 °C. Then, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 32 nM NPs and 1 mM 3-MA, and the cells were cultured for an additional 24 h. PBMCs was obtained by isolating from whole blood of healthy human volunteer. For intracellular staining of HSP60, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with ice-cold 90% methanol (10 min, on ice), and then blocked if necessary. Cells were then incubated sequentially with a rabbit anti-HSP60 primary antibody and a fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody. After each antibody incubation, cells were washed to remove excess reagent. Finally, stained cells were resuspended in PBS for flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescent staining and transferase mediated dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) assay were performed according to the manufacturer’s standard protocols (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Briefly, the spleen tissues were routinely processed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde immediately. For immunofluorescent staining, the slides of spleen were stained with GFP and other antibodies (CD41a, CD11b, Ter119 and HSP60). Treatment-induced apoptosis was specifically evaluated by TUNEL staining.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001).

Results

Synthesis and characterization of PtNPs-P17

PtNPs were synthesized via directly chemical reduction [26], and the conjugate PtNPs-P17 was fabricated through self-assembly of PtNPs with peptide P17. Both NPs exhibited spherical morphology under TEM with average diameters of 8.10 ± 2.41 nm for PtNPs and 8.47 ± 2.12 nm for PtNPs-P17 (Fig. 1A), respectively. DLS measurement revealed that the hydrodynamic diameters of PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 were 23.40 ± 1.78 nm and 54.93 ± 4.86 nm, respectively (Fig. 1B). XPS spectra of PtNPs-P17 showed a positive shift in Pt 4f and a concurrent negative shift in S 2p binding energies relative to PtNPs and free P17 (Fig. 1C), indicating the formation of Pt–S bond. The stability of PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 in ddH2O, 5% of glucose and culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (‌FBS) were monitored within one week. The results showed that the hydrodynamic diameter for both the NPs did not change over time (Fig. S1), indicating they had excellent stability in the three kinds of solutions.

Enzyme activity assays showed that both PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 possess OXD and POD activities, with significantly higher activity under acidic conditions (pH 3.9) compared to neutral (pH 7.4) and alkaline (pH 9.4) conditions (Fig. 1D, E). Under acidic conditions, the OXD activity of PtNPs was notably higher than that of PtNPs-P17, while no significant difference was observed in their POD activity. ESR experiments demonstrated that both NPs effectively scavenge superoxide anion radicals (•O₂⁻) generated by the hypoxanthine/xanthine oxidase system under neutral conditions, indicating SOD activity (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, oxygen bubble formation was observed in samples containing nanoparticles and H2O2 across different pH conditions, confirming that both possess CAT activity capable of catalyzing H2O2 decomposition into oxygen (Fig. 1G). ESR oximetry further revealed that this CAT activity was strongest under alkaline conditions, followed by neutral and then acidic conditions, with PtNPs exhibiting higher activity than PtNPs-P17 (Fig. 1H). In summary, PtNPs-P17 successfully retains the multi-enzyme activities of PtNPs while combining the P17-mediated targeted delivery with the diverse catalytic functions of the PtNPs.

Uptake of NPs and effect on mitochondrial membrane potential

Results of UV-vis-NIR extinction indicated that PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 had similar spectra (Fig. 2A). It was shown that the intensity of the spectrum for cells incubated with PtNPs-P17 for 4 h was higher than that with PtNPs (Fig. 2B), indicating more PtNPs-P17 was taken up by the cells than PtNPs [27]. When MOLM-13 cells were pre-incubation with HSP60 antibody, the cellular uptake of PtNPs-P17 was markedly reduced to a level comparable to that of unmodified PtNPs, indiucating that the enhanced internalization of PtNPs-P17 was attributable to the P17-mediated specific recognition to HSP60 highly expressed by MOLM-13 cell (Fig. S2A), the HSP60 expression in MOLM-13 was 93.3%, while it was 25.9% in PBMCs obtained from healthy human. To examine the subcellular distribution of HSP60, immunofluorescence staining was applied with a mitochondrial probe (red) and HSP60 antibody (green). Results from confocal microscopy revealed extensive yellow fluorescence in the merged images, indicating that HSP60 was mainly localized in mitochondria, meanwhile, distinct green fluorescence (pointed by arrow heads) was also observed, confirming the presence of extra-mitochondrial HSP60 (Fig. S2B). Under TEM, PtNPs-P17 can be clearly seen in cells (indicated by arrow heads), especially in mitochondria, and mitochondria damage was also observed (Fig. 2C). In addition, results from JC-1 measurement showed that the control group exhibited strong red fluorescence and negligible green fluorescence, and PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 significantly enhanced green fluorescence and weakened red fluorescence, similar to the positive control of mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibitor CCCP (Fig. 2D, E), clearly indicating PtNPs-P17 significantly reduced mitochondrial membrane potential. To further investigate whether PtNPs-P17 and PtNPs affected mitochondrial function, a typical biomarker of mitochondrial DNA oxidative damage, 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) was examined It was shown that both PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 significantly increased 8-OHdG levels in MOLM-13 cells, and the PtNPs-P17 resulted in a much more pronounced increase (Fig. 2F), indicating more severe mtDNA oxidative damage and mitochondrial impairment induced by tPtNPs-P17.

Effects of PtNPs-P17 on oxidative stress and metabolic reprogramming

The decrease of mitochondrial membrane potential is closely related to mitochondrial oxidative stress [28], which is predominantly characterized by the excessive accumulation of ROS. Therefore, we first measured intracellular ROS using the probe of DCFH-DA. Figure 3A showed that fluorescence intensity increased with concentrations of the NPs, with a greater increase for PtNPs-P17 than PtNPs at 32 nM. Noted that P17 itself showed little effect on ROS (Fig. S3), indicating that P17 does not have a significant effect on oxidative stress. The induced ROS generation can be attributable to the OXD and POD activities as well as to the SOD and CAT activities of the NPs. It was plausible to consider that the different enzyme activities played their roles coordinately in the presence of MPO in AML cells: the SOD activity converted superoxide anion radicals existing intracellularly [29] into H2O2 and O2, and CAT activity further converted H2O2 into O2, which was confirmed by significantly reduced fluorescence after 24 h NP treatment using the oxygen-sensitive probe Ru(dpp)3Cl2 [30] (Fig. 3B). With O2 as the substrate, the OXD activity of PtNPs-P17 can promote the generation of various ROS from oxygen, such as superoxide anion radicals [31], singlet oxygen [32] and hydroxyl radical [33, 34]. Notably, significantly increased singlet oxygen was detected in MOLM-13 cells treated with NPs for 72 h by probe Si-DMA [35] (Fig. 3C), which was generated from H2O2 in chloride-containing physiological environments via cellular MPO [25, 36], confirmed by detecting HClO [37], an intermediate in this pathway, using the specific probe HKOCl-4 [38] (Fig. 3D). To confirm the essential role of MPO, OCI-AML3 and U937 were employed as they are MPO negative AML cells [25]. It was shown that neither PtNPs nor PtNPs-P17 significantly induced singlet oxygen generation in these cells (Fig. S4). Noted that in the above processes, PtNPs-P17 exhibited stronger effects than PtNPs.

It is known that cells generate energy by oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis metabolism [39, 40]. Therefore, we next examined effects of the NPs on the OCR and ECAR using the Seahorse XFe24 analyzer. As shown in Fig. 3E, all tested parameters decreased significantly by PtNPs-P17 and PtNPs, the former displayed much stronger effects than the latter. In brief, the decreased basal OCR indicated that the NPs directly impaired mitochondrial basal function and reduced cellular energy production. Both ATP-linked respiration and maximal respiration were significantly reduced, demonstrating that the NPs inhibit ATP synthesis. The spare respiratory capacity did not change significantly, suggesting that MOLM-13 cells consistently operate near their maximal respiratory rate, likely due to their rapid proliferation. The overall reduction in these parameters demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect of the NPs on mitochondrial function in MOLM-13 cells. In the ECAR assay, non-glycolytic acidification, glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve were evaluated (Fig. 3F). It was shown that the NPs induced a coordinated decrease across all these parameters, indicating a broad suppression of glycolytic function. To clarify whether the inhibition of glycolysis stemmed from direct intervention in glycolytic enzymes, we examined the activity of phosphofructokinase (PFK), a key rate-limiting cytosolic enzyme in the glycolytic pathway. The result showed that both PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 significantly reduced PFK activity in MOLM-13 cells, with a more pronounced reduction observed in the PtNPs-P17 group (Fig. S5), demonstrating that the nanozyme directly targets and impairs cytosolic glycolytic enzymes. Moreover, PtNPs and PtNPs-P17 reduced NADH levels by 26.67% and 44.47%, and NAD+ levels by 6.67% and 21.31%, respectively (Fig. 3G). The slight increase of the NAD+/NADH ratio suggests that the reduction of NADH consumption or production is more significant than that of NAD+, which may be due to the inhibition of glycolysis or the compensatory efforts of cells to mobilize residual NADH to maintain mitochondrial function. Together, the above dual inhibitory functions of the NPs forced the cells to undergo metabolic reprogramming, shifting from a high- to a low-metabolic state.

PtNPs-P17 triggered mitochondrial autophagy

The disturbed energy homeostasis can activate autophagy of the mitochondria [41]. Consequently, there were more destroyed mitochondrial accompanied with autophagosome observed in the cells treated with PtNPs-P17 than that with PtNPs under TEM (Fig. 4A-C). To validate this phenomenon, expressions of autophagy related protein LC3B were examined by western blot. The results showed that PtNPs-P17 induced higher LC3B-Ⅱ than PtNPs (Fig. 4D). Consistently, the results of immunofluorescent staining of LC3B and mitochondria showed a significantly increased number of LC3B positive (green) punctate structures (Fig. 4E), and most of these structures were co-located with mitochondria (red), providing direct and visual evidence of inducing mitochondrial autophagy in MOLM-13 cells. Again, there were more green fluorescent dots detected in PtNPs-P17 group, indicating a stronger effect.

PtNPs-P17 induced differentiation and apoptosis of AML cells

The cellular ROS alternation-induced autophagy can induce the differentiation of leukemia cells [42]. Results of Wright-Giemsa staining showed that control AML cells presented a homogeneous population of undifferentiated blasts, featured by large volume, high nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio, loose chromatin and distinct nucleoli, while cells treated with PtNPs alone showed negligible morphological changes compared with the control, retaining typical immature leukemic blast characteristics. Conversely, cells treated by PtNPs-P17 exhibited obvious differentiation phenotypes, including decreased nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio, condensed chromatin and nuclear lobulation in partial cells, showing mature-like myeloid cells (Fig. 5A). Statistics of the cell size distribution supported the morphological observations (Fig. 5B), as the cells of PtNPs-P17 induced a significantly broader size distribution in the groups, implying the emergence of diverse cell subsets including differentiated cells. At the same time, the expression levels of erythroid (CD235a), granulocyte (CD11b), and megakaryocyte (CD41a) differentiation markers on the surface of MOLM-13 cells exposed to NP were all up-regulated (Fig. 5C), and the increase was suppressed by NAC or L-histidine, indicating the differentiation were originated from the cellular ROS variations while the singlet oxygen played the most important role. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that PtNPs-P17 significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of differentiation-related transcription factors CEBPA, PU.1, and GATA1 in MOLM-13 cells (Fig. S6), which demonstrated that the ROS-induced leukemic cell differentiation occurred through the activation of these key transcriptional regulators. When MOLM-13 cells were incubated with the NPs for 24 h and double-stained with differentiation markers (CD235a, CD41a or CD11b) and Annexin V, it could be seen that the PtNPs-P17 induced more double positive cells than PtNPs (Fig. 5D) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5E), this is because differentiated AML cells are likely to undergo apoptosis [43, 44]. When MOLM-13 cells were pretreated with the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, PtNPs-P17-induced apoptosis could be markedly attenuated, suppressing approximately 79% of the apoptotic effect (Fig. S7), which indicated that mitophagy served as a primary pathway mediating AML cell apoptosis triggered by PtNPs-P17.

The differentiation effect of the NPs was also examined in AML cells separated from the spleen of a refractory AML mouse [45]. Results from flow cytometry showed that the NPs increased expressions of Ter119, CD41a and CD11b on the cells (Fig. 6A), and again PtNPs-P17 presented the stronger effect than PtNPs. Along with the differentiations, the apoptosis rate was increased (Fig. 6B), and the percentage of GFP+ cells in the spleen cells decreased from 81.4% for the control to 79.7% and 67.5% for PtNPs and PtNPs-P17, respectively (Fig. 6C). In addition, PtNPs-P17 significantly decreased the expression of HSP60 (Fig. 6D) while PtNPs did not, implying that PtNPs-P17 interacted with HSP60 and might affect its activity. To understand underlying mechanisms, autophagy inhibitor 3-MA was employed in MOLM-13 cells. As shown in Fig. S8, 3-MA was able to rescue the PtNPs-P17-mediated downregulation of HSP60, whereas HSP60 expression remained unchanged in PtNPs-treated cells with or without 3-MA, suggesting that PtNPs-P17 promotes HSP60 degradation through an autophagy-dependent pathway.

In vivo therapeutic effect of PtNPs-P17 in the AML mice

The therapeutic effect of PtNPs-P17 was examined by intravenous injection of NPs in the refractory AML mouse model (Fig. 7A). With immunofluorescence staining of spleen cells from AML model mice, the effects on differentiation markers, apoptosis and HSP60 were revealed. As shown in Fig. 7B-D, PtNPs-P17 increased levels of the three differentiation markers including CD41a, CD11b, and TER119, while the control group displayed little positive signals and PtNPs induced less up-regulations of the three markers, demonstrating the ability of PtNPs-P17 to drive AML cells differentiate toward mature-like cell more effectively. For apoptosis, TUNEL staining images showed that only the PtNPs-P17 treatment group exhibited prominent red apoptotic signals, whereas the control and PtNPs groups showed almost no apoptosis (Fig. 7E), indicating that PtNPs-P17 not only induces differentiation but also selectively eliminated differentiated AML cells, consistently, the fluorescence intensity of GFP+ cells in the spleen decreased with the NPs treatment (Fig. 7F), and PtNPs-P17 group displayed the stronger effect. At the same time, flow cytometry analysis showed that PtNPs-P17 was able to decrease the proportion of GFP+ cells in bone marrow significantly compared with PtNPs (Fig. S9). Consistent with the ex vitro result, the expression of HSP60 in the spleen of PtNPs-P17 group also significantly decreased in the spleen of the AML mice model (Fig. 7G), confirmed the interaction between PtNPs-P17 and HSP60. To investigate potential adverse effects of PtNPs-P17 on the normal hematopoietic system, the apoptosis in healthy murine BMMNCs were examined using flow cytometry. It was shown that PtNPs-P17 and PtNPs did not induced significant increase in apoptosis relative to the control group (Fig. S10), implying minimal adverse effects of the NPs toward normal hematopoietic cells.

Discussion

AML cells maintain their malignant proliferation by the imbalance of redox homeostasis and metabolic reprogramming, which enable the cells to avoid apoptosis and produce chemotherapy resistance [7]. Therefore, to effectively destroy the abnormal redox state represents a promising way to develop new therapeutic approaches.

Mitochondria are one organelle that is a reservoir of H2O2 for cell metabolism [46], and interestingly, HSP60 is stored in mitochondria as well as highly expressed in the AML cells membrane [20, 21]. According to these characteristics, we constructed the novel peptide-modified PtNPs, using HSP60 targeted peptide P17 as a targeting head to enhance cellular uptake and enable the PtNPs-P17 to locate to the mitochondria. It was the localization at mitochondria that allowed PtNPs-P17 to play stronger modulatory effects on ROS levels through its intrinsic multi-enzyme activities.

It could be noticed that PtNPs-P17 hold both SOD-like and OXD-like activities that appear functionally opposing, meanwhile, PtNPs-P17 has the CAT-like activity. These activities can act in orchestration rather than in conflict ways in the mitochondrial microenvironment as follows. PtNPs-P17 scavenged superoxide anion radicals and converted them into H2O2 by playing its SOD-like activity, besides, there are also abundant of H2O2 in mitochondria. The H2O2 was transformed into O2 by PtNPs-P17 playing its CAT-like activity. The generated oxygen was utilized as a substrate of PtNPs-P17 by its OXD-like activity to produce superoxide anion radicals and other ROSs. Importantly, there is a high amount of MPO in MOLM-13 cells; the endogenous can catalyzed H2O2 to generate large amounts of singlet oxygen, which contribute largely to the ROS generation. Since all these reactions occur in the confined space of mitochondria where PtNPs-P17 were specifically enriched, it is plausible to infer that the ROS production overwhelmed the superoxide anion radicals scavenging, ultimately leading to an increase of intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 8A). The above reactions modulated by PtNPs-P17 significantly amplified the oxidative stress injury of MOLM-13 cells, ultimately leading to the occurring of cells differentiation and enhanced apoptosis.

The high energy and biosynthetic requirements of malignant proliferation force tumor cells to modify their metabolic profiles through metabolic reprogramming. One hallmark of this adaptation is the dependence on the Warburg effect [47, 48]. The alteration of metabolic patterns indicates a shift in cellular survival strategy. The outcome of such adaptive metabolic restructuring dictates cellular fate—either the preservation of homeostasis or the progression to functional decline and cell death [49–52]. Increasing studies demonstrate a strong link between the principal mediators of mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming and the pathogenesis of AML [53, 54]. In the current work, we showed that PtNPs-P17 was able to simultaneously inhibit the mitochondrial respiratory rate and glycolytic rate of AML cells, resulted from the disruption of mitochondrial structure and the collapse of respiratory chain function, which is significantly different from traditional single-targeted metabolic treatment strategy. The decrease in metabolic rate affected the NAD+ and NADH, resulting in the reprogramming of metabolic patterns. This change not only exacerbated oxidative stress but also promoted the differentiation and apoptosis of AML cells towards mature-like phenotypes by regulating downstream related signaling pathways. This approach promoted the differentiation of leukemic cells and then inducing apoptosis in differentiated cells can precisely eliminate malignant proliferating primitive cells, which provided a new strategy in addressing the therapy of AML and the challenge of drug resistance.

HSP60 is a core homeostatic protein that maintains the folding and function of mitochondrial matrix proteins, and the down-regulation of HSP60 can cause mitochondrial stress, metabolic collapse and autophagy clearance [55]. As high expressed in AML cells, HSP60 plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, regulating ROS metabolism and apoptosis. Because HSP60 mainly locates in mitochondria, the specific binding of P17 to HSP60 endowed PtNPs-P17 with location at mitochondria. The down-regulation of HSP60 further weakened the metabolic adaptation and survival ability of the cells and had a synergistic effect with the oxidative metabolic collapse directly caused by PtNPs-P17, thus effectively eliminating leukemia cells, which indicated that PtNPs-P17 has great potential in the therapy of leukemia.

Up to date, AML treatments still largely rely on intensive combination chemotherapies [56], however, most of chemotherapeutic regimes kill those unmatured disease cells as well as normal cells through their strong cytotoxicity, which brings severe systematic toxic effects. Differentiation-induction is an ideal and low toxic strategy in leukemia treatment, which means to induce the leukemia cells to differentiate towards matured phenotypes, and the matured leukemic cells are much more sensitive to the cytotoxic drugs, so that the disease cells can leave the status of uncontrolled clonal expansion and easily undergo apoptotic program in chemotherapies [57]. Unfortunately, therapeutic medicines for the differentiation induction are quite limited in clinical practices except for the all-trans retinoic acid that is only effective in acute promyelocytic leukemia [58]. In this work, PtNPs-P17 was demonstrated the therapeutic effect of inducing AML cells differentiation and enhancing apoptosis in vitro, ex vitro and in vivo, showing attractive potentials for use in the development of differentiation inductive therapies.

In conclusion, this study successfully developed a mitochondria targeted nanozyme, PtNPs-P17, which exhibited significant anti-AML activity in vitro, ex vitro and in vivo through its mitochondrial location and multi-enzyme activities and the involvement of cellular MPO in the leukemic cells. PtNPs-P17 induced mitochondrial damage and autophagy, and ultimately impaired cellular metabolic homeostasis, leading to differentiation induction and apoptosis. In a refractory AML mouse model, PtNPs-P17 not only induced multi-directional differentiation and post-differentiation apoptosis, but also effectively reduced AML cells and the level of HSP60 (Fig. 8B). The construction of the novel PtNPs-P17 platform, which combined targeting capability, multi-enzyme activity and metabolic regulation ability, provides a precise regulation strategy for redox regulation and metabolic reprogramming in mitochondria. It offers a potential candidate for the treatment of AML and expands new ideas for the application of nanozymes in hematological tumors.
