The prevalence of antiretroviral drug interactions with other drugs used in women living with HIV and its association with HIV drug change and patient compliance
Authors: Mohammadreza Heydari, Zohre Foroozanfar, Sina Bazmi, Zahra Mohammadi, Hassan Joulaei, Ghavam Ansari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug-drug interactions (DDIs) between antiretroviral therapy (ART) and commonly used co-medications in HIV patients, especially women, impact treatment efficacy and patient safety.ObjectiveThis study aimed to study the prevalence and types of drug-drug interactions (DDIs) between antiretroviral therapy drugs (ARTs) and comedications among a female population with HIV. Additionally, the study investigates the association of these DDIs with ART medication changes and treatment adherence.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 632 adult women living with HIV (WLHIV). Data was retrospectively extracted from patient files. Drug.com interaction checker website was used to assess DDIs between ART and non-ART medications. Changes to the ART regimen previously attributed to ART side effects or patient non-adherence were considered drug changes.ResultsA total of 429 WLHIV (mean age: 44.05 ± 9.50) were eligible. The prevalence of DDIs between ART and non-ART medications was 21.4%, with 4.7% minor, 18.4% moderate, and 8.9% major interactions. The highest prevalence of DDI was among cardiovascular medication users (71.7%), followed by central nervous system drugs (69.2%). Changing medications resulted in a decrease in DDIs, with significant reductions in total and minor interactions. Participants without DDIs had better adherence to ART. DDI between ART and non-ART medications was significantly associated with ART drug change, even after accounting for side effects attributed to ARTs, indicating an independent twofold association (OR = 1.99, CI 1.04–3.77). Moreover, further adjustments for HIV viral load and CD4 + cell count did not change the significance of the association (OR = 2.01, CI 1.03–3.92).ConclusionDDIs in WLHIV impact adherence to ART. Altering ART may not be directly related to ART side effects, but rather primarily due to interactions with non-ART medications. Modifying non-ART drug regimens can reduce the likelihood of DDIs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-09958-x.Keywords: Anti-retroviral agents, Drug interactions, HIV, Patient compliance, Polypharmacy, Women

Background

Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) occur when a drug is affected pharmacodynamically or pharmacologically by another drug prescribed at the same time [1, 2]. This interaction can lead to increased or decreased concentrations of the affected drug in the blood, either below the therapeutic level or to toxic levels [3]. Insufficient drug concentration can result in treatment failure, while excessive concentration can cause bothersome symptoms, non-compliance, hospitalizations, or even death [1, 2, 4, 5]. Therefore, DDIs have a substantial influence on the efficacy and safety of medications [5]. They encompass a spectrum of effects, ranging from mild and insignificant side effects to severe and hazardous consequences [6]. The impact of DDIs extends to both individuals and the community, leading to a significant proportion of hospital admissions, approximately 20%, being associated with drug-related issues including DDIs. Furthermore, research has established a correlation between DDIs and prolonged hospital stays and heightened healthcare expenses [7].

The introduction of antiretroviral therapy drugs (ARTs) has greatly improved the prognosis of people living with HIV (PLWHIV), transforming HIV from a fatal disease into a chronic condition [8, 9]. Consequently, PLWHIV can now live as long as those without HIV [10]. Accordingly, strict and lifelong adherence to ARTs is crucial for every PLWHIV for suppressing viral load and maintaining a normal life [11]. As a result, these PLWHIV live longer, develop more co-morbidities, and require multiple medications to manage underlying conditions [8]. Furthermore, PLWHIV tend to experience chronic diseases at a younger age compared to non-HIV individuals [10]. Typically, HIV treatment itself requires a combination of three drugs [11], and individuals with HIV commonly take three additional medications to manage HIV symptoms, side effects of ARTs, and mentioned co-morbidities [12]. Therefore, polypharmacy, the use of five or more drugs simultaneously, is prevalent among PLWHIV. Polypharmacy significantly increases the likelihood of DDIs, which can have detrimental effects on their health [12]. Antiretroviral therapies stand out as the drug class with the highest tendency to induce DDIs among all known drug classes [13]. ARTs can act as both victims and culprits of DDIs [14]. The recommended primary ART regimens in Iran include a combination of two nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) with a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI), or two NRTIs with an integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI). Beginning ART with different regimens necessitates a compelling justification, and in all other instances, the preferred regimen should be employed [15].

Following an HIV diagnosis, frequent follow ups lead to the identification of co-morbidities and the prescription of medications [12]. However, unawareness of potential HIV drug interactions among non-infectious disease specialists can result in inappropriate prescriptions for these co-morbidities [11]. This can lead to DDIs, symptoms, and the addition of more drugs, intensifying polypharmacy and creating a prescription cascade [2, 16]. On the other hand, they can lead to patient non-adherence to either ART or medications related to comorbidities [13], ultimately worsening the outcomes for PLWHIV [17]. Overall, concerns regarding the side effects of multiple drug use, such as DDIs, currently appear to outweigh concerns about the side effects of HIV itself.

Despite scattered studies, the significance of interactions involving HIV drugs is often overlooked, leading to common prescriptions of medications with potential DDIs [5, 17]. Moreover, studies have consistently shown a considerable prevalence in prescribing medications contraindicated with ARTs [12, 17, 18], highlighting a significant lack of attention and knowledge in this area. Furthermore, most studies on ART DDIs have been conducted in developed Western countries [19], where different rates of polypharmacy are influenced by factors such as better insurance coverage, improved treatment access, higher participation in scientific studies, and higher education [12]. Moreover, Studies have constantly shown that polypharmacy is more prevalent in women than in men [17, 19, 20]. Furthermore, women have been underrepresented in clinical trials, including those for HIV treatments [21], indicating that much of the data on ART efficacy and safety, including drug interaction profiles, are based predominantly on male populations [20]. In addition, there is limited data specifically addressing how ARTs interact with drugs commonly prescribed to women, such as those for reproductive health, mental health, and autoimmune conditions. This study focused on a female population with HIV, reporting on the prevalence and types of DDIs between ARTs and comedications, and their association with ART medication change and treatment adherence.

The study hypothesis was that physicians often prioritize HIV medications in WLHIV, neglecting the accompanying medications and their interactions with ARTs, which can impact patient compliance and lead to sequential ART changes by the misguided physician.

Methods

Study setting and participants

The present cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2021 to April 2022 at the Voluntary, Counselling, and Testing (VCT) Center, a major referral center for HIV-infected individuals in southwest Iran. A total of 632 WLHIV were registered at the VCT center. Among them, 429 WLHIV who were on ART and had active healthcare files were included in this study.

Women older than 18 years old.

Exclusion criteria

Women with incomplete medical records.

Women who were identified and confirmed as HIV-positive for less than six months, whom are excluded to avoid including those still adjusting their treatment, ensuring a focus on stable ART regimens and more accurate assessment of drug interactions and adherence.

Data collection

In this study, clinical and socio-demographic data of the subjects were collected through direct interviews and from information recorded in patients’ files at VCT center. Demographic information including job status, place of residence, spouse infected with HIV, and behavioral information (history of smoking, alcohol consumption, and drug use [22]) were questioned by trained interviewers and recorded in a researcher-made checklist.

Clinical information, encompassing the WHO clinical stage, ART treatment status, patient adherence to ART, disease and treatment duration, adverse drug reactions attributed to ART by the physician, socioeconomic status (SES), and laboratory measurements such as HIV Rapid, HIV ELISA, HIV Viral load, CD4+ Count, along with other follow-up tests conducted on patients by drawing blood samples in the same center’s laboratory, were extracted from the patients’ files at VCT center. These details were also recorded in the researcher-made checklist. We considered assets as an indicator of SES, as assets are a stable measure of Socioeconomic Status and are widely used as a proxy for SES. We used a standardized questionnaire developed by Sertipi et al. [23], including assets such as LED or LCD TV, Video, PC, Washing Machine, Dish Washing, Freezer, Vacuum, and Car and household ownership. We applied Principal Component Analysis to analyze the data obtained from this questionnaire.

During each follow-up visit, a clinical psychologist conducted a brief interview with the patient, assessed their adherence to treatment, and recorded it in a checklist (Good/Weak) in the patient’s file. Adherence to treatment in the visit where the patient’s ART medication changed, or if there was no change in medication, adherence to treatment at the last visit was determined as the basis for the adherence variable.

Treatments

The current treatment regimen and any previous regimen, if there were any changes, were extracted from patients’ files and recorded in the relevant checklist after being approved by the attending physician.

If the patient was taking medications other than ART for underlying diseases, this information was also extracted from the patient’s file and recorded in the checklist after approval from the treating physician. Other medications were medications other than ARTs such as those used for cardiovascular, neurological or psychiatric, or hormonal conditions.

Interactions

“Drug-drug interactions occur when two or more drugs – prescription and/or OTC – react with each other. Some drug interactions can make the drug you take less effective. And some combinations of drugs can be dangerous.” [24]. In this study, the interactions between ARTs and other medications were studied. To assess drug-drug interactions, we utilized the online drug interaction checker provided by Drug.com [25, 26]. All interactions were categorized as minor, intermediate, or major based on the data available on the website. The frequency and severity of interaction were recorded in a data collection sheet and then analyzed using SPSS software.

Drug change

Any change in the ART treatment regimen attributed by the physician to ART side effects or patient non-adherence was considered a drug change in this study. The involuntary change of medication due to drug shortage has not been considered as a medication change.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by Shiraz University of Medical Sciences ethical committee under Ethical code: IR.SUMS.REC.1400.379. After the approval, an informed written consent form was completed by all patients.

Statistical analyses

Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and qualitative variables were reported as numbers (percentages). T-test and chi-square test were used to assess differences in health-related variables between participants with or without drug interactions. A logistic regression model was used to determine the association between drug interactions and drug changes. Data were analyzed using Graph Pad prism version 9, and SPSS software version 20. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 429 WLHIV participated in this study. The mean age of the participants was 44.05 ± 9.50, and 213(49.7%) were married. The prevalence of smoking, alcohol use, and illicit drug use was 12.8%, 9.6%, and 6.8%, respectively. Thirteen participants (3.0%) were in stages 3 and 4 of HIV, 125 (29.1%) had a CD4+ cell count below 500, 71 (16.6%) had detectable HIV Viral load, and 196 (45.7%) had history of HIV drug complications (with hematologic complications being the most common, accounting for 16.3% of cases). Additionally, 132 (30.8%) had a history of using other drugs, with central nervous system (CNS) drugs being the most commonly used (49.2% in WLHIV who had a history of using other medications). The association between drug interactions and drug change

The frequency of drug change was 66.3% in patients with drug interaction and 49.0% in patients without drug interaction. This difference was statistically significant (Fig. 4). There was a direct and significant association between drug interaction and drug change. WLHIV who experienced drug interactions were 2.05 times more likely to change their medication compared to those without drug interactions (OR (95% CI) = 2.05 (1.26–3.32), p-value = 0.003). Also, after adjustment for the presence or absence of ART adverse drug reactions, the significance remained (OR (95% CI) = 1.99 (1.04–3.77), p-value = 0.035) (Table 4). Moreover, further adjusting the model with HIV viral load and CD4 + cell count did not change the significance of the association (OR (95% CI) = 2.01 (1.03–3.92), p-value = 0.039).

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of DDIs among WLHIV in the VCT center was reported to be 21.4%, primarily consisting of moderate interactions. The highest level of interactions was observed between ARTs and medications related to cardiovascular diseases and their risk factors, followed by drugs related to the CNS. The ART regimen that showed the highest prevalence of DDIs was ABC + 3TC + EFV, which was considerably reduced after medication change. These DDIs were associated with a significant decrease in patients’ adherence to ART. Additionally, the rate of interactions decreased by 2.5% after changes in the ART regimen. DDI between ART and non-ART medications showed a significant association with ART drug change. Furthermore, even after accounting for the presence of side effects attributed to ARTs, the significance persisted, indicating that the association is independent of ART side effects. Furthermore, after considering HIV viral load and CD4+ cell count, this association remained.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate these associations in PLWHIV in our country, since prior research on these subjects were merely epidemiological reports [27]. Previous research has shown that due to the high prevalence of polypharmacy in PLWHIV, the likelihood of DDIs and subsequent events like the prescribing cascade increases [18, 28–31]. Moreover, evidence suggests that the majority of DDIs occur between ART and non-ARTs, and only a small percentage occurs among ARTs themselves [2, 6, 28]. In developing countries, a study in China by Chen et al. [13] reported a DDI prevalence of 2.99%, with 7% being major interactions. The most common interactions between ART and other medications, similar to our study, included cardiovascular drugs. Despite many similarities in study design, the considerably higher prevalence of DDIs between our study and theirs may be due to much lower prevalence of co-medication use in their investigation. On the other hand, various studies conducted in developed countries have reported the prevalence of potential DDIs in PLWHIV as 27% [32], 40% [33], and 54% [34] in Europe, 41.2% [35] and 52% in the United States, and 63% in Canada [2], all being much higher than the present study. This could be attributed to lower patient awareness and follow-up regarding their health status, reduced patient access to healthcare facilities, and cultural and stigma issues among HIV patients, particularly among women with HIV, in our country. All of these factors contribute to less frequent patient visits to physicians, fewer diagnoses of comorbid conditions, fewer prescriptions of medications, and less recognition of potential interactions. However, the study by Evans et al. in England reported that only 36% of potential interactions were identified by physicians [32]. This difference in prevalence compared to studies in different countries may also be partly due to the strong influence of patients’ genetic factors on drug interactions [1].

In line with the findings of our study, other studies from various parts of the world have reported the highest prevalence of DDIs with cardiovascular and CNS medications [2, 29, 32, 34–37]. A recent review also demonstrated that CNS-related medications have been widely prescribed in PLWHIV, increasing the likelihood of DDIs [5]. Furthermore, across all the referenced studies, the prevalence of major drug interactions, where medications are essentially contraindicated when used together, was consistently substantial, ranging from 1 to 7%. This suggests a notable risk in the prescription of medications for people living with HIV. Notably, our study reported a slightly higher frequency of major drug-drug interactions at 8.9%, raising additional concerns about this issue within our healthcare system.

In our study, we found a significant and direct association between potential DDIs involving ART and non-ART medications, and changes in the ART regimen. The discovery made in this study implies that the association is not influenced by the side effects of ARTs, and that altering HIV medications in patients may not be directly linked to the side effects caused by the ARTs themselves, but rather primarily due to interactions between ART and non-ART medications. Physicians diagnosed the need to switch a patient’s ART to a different regimen based on clinical symptoms or abnormal laboratory findings. However, they overlooked the fact that the primary cause of the clinical complaints or laboratory abnormalities in these patients could likely be the result of DDIs between ART and non-ART medications, which are commonly used in HIV patients, including those in our center. Consequently, the physicians did not consider these accompanying medications and did not make any changes to them, attributing the symptoms and side effects solely to the ARTs. For instance, in our population, high usage of neurologic, psychiatric, and hypnotic medications, which interacted with ART medications, was observed. These interactions could significantly amplify the concentrations and effects of CNS medications in the bloodstream. However, when patients reported excessive drowsiness and fatigue, the physician attributed the side effects to EFV medication and made a change. Yet, it is more probable that these side effects were caused by the interactions between ART and CNS medications. The reduction in potential DDIs after modifying the ARTs, along with the simultaneous improvement in patient compliance with treatment in the group with higher DDIs, supports our perspective. Moreover, further adjusting the model with HIV viral load and CD4+ cell count did not change the significance of the relationship, and the direct association between DDI and ART changes remained independent of the viral load level and CD4+ count.

In contrast to our study, Chen et al. found no significant correlation between DDIs and patient adherence to ART [13]. This difference could be attributed to the low occurrence of DDIs in their study, most of which were mild, unlike ours. Moreover, Ssonko et al. did not directly investigate this relationship and found no significant association between polypharmacy and patient adherence to ART [19], which may be due to the age group studied (patients over 50 years old), who have shown higher treatment adherence to ART [19]. On the other hand, a cross-sectional study of 230 Asian PLWHIV concluded that those with ART changes due to DDIs had lower treatment adherence and QOL [38]. However, the study’s self-reported data collected within a strict time frame may introduce biases despite supporting our findings.

Complications caused by DDIs can affect the patient’s desire to continue treatment [39].The majority of DDIs are caused by induction or strong inhibition of a metabolic pathway [40]. DDIs between ARTs and other medications used by our center’s patients can occur through the following mechanisms: From a pharmacokinetic perspective, these interactions can occur in the liver, kidneys, or gastrointestinal system. In the liver, inhibition or induction of hepatic cytochrome (CYP) enzymes, hepatic transporters, or glucuronidase enzymes can cause these DDIs. The CYPP450 group of proteins, especially 3A4 isoform, are the most common and metabolize most drugs. ARTs and co-administered drugs can induce or inhibit these enzymes, thereby decreasing or increasing the bioavailability and levels of the drugs, respectively [12, 14], especially in the case of statins, which are commonly prescribed to WLHIV in our center [41]. Alternatively, ARTs can modulate the activity of transmembrane proteins involved in the transportation of many drugs [5]. Additionally, in the kidneys, inhibition of tubular transporters can lead to these DDIs. For example, another commonly used drug in our center, metformin, is actively transported into the proximal tubule of the kidney, where some ARTs inhibit its reabsorption from tubular cells, and others inhibit its excretion in the urine, both of which increase the drug’s contact time [42, 43]. Furthermore, in the gastrointestinal system, some ARTs can increase the absorption of drugs like dabigatran by inhibiting intestinal P-glycoprotein and increase the risk of bleeding [44]. Moreover, from a pharmacodynamic perspective, interactions can occur through mechanisms such as renal toxicity and prolongation of the QT interval. The association between acute and chronic renal toxicity and the use of the ART TDF, which is commonly prescribed in our center, has been established. Therefore, the concomitant use of nephrotoxic drugs with TDF can increase the chances of nephrotoxicity and its symptoms, especially if used for a long duration or if the individual already has pre-existing renal disorders [45]. Additionally, some ARTs have been associated with QTc prolongation, and the concomitant use of drugs with the potential to increase QTc, especially escitalopram, can lead to this dangerous side effect and its symptoms [4]. The use of CNS drugs, particularly the mentioned was high in our center.

With the increasing age of PLWHIV in different regions, polypharmacy and the likelihood of DDIs will also significantly increase in these patients [13, 28]; Thus, it is necessary to raise awareness about the presence of DDIs and their management among all medical professionals and even the patients themselves, and to implement policies accordingly. We recommend physicians consistently utilize software for checking drug interactions between ART medications and other drugs during HIV patient visits. Alternatively, involving clinical pharmacists to evaluate interactions before altering medications due to suspicious symptoms and side effects can be a valuable approach. This partnership has the potential to improve patient adherence to HIV medications, a critical goal in HIV therapy. Considering the significant occurrence of drug interactions, notably major interactions that could be life-threatening, among PLWHIV in our facility and likely nationwide, addressing and prioritizing this concern is imperative.

Our study had considerable strengths. The findings of our study provide a very precise insight into the less focused population of women, specifically, although it is possible that the findings may not be generalizable to other groups. Additionally, geographically speaking, our results can assist physicians and policymakers in understanding the importance of awareness regarding potential DDIs in PLWHIV. Moreover, we correlated DDIs with the degree of medication changes for the first time. However, our study also has limitations. It is a cross-sectional study, which inherently makes it difficult to establish a cause-and-effect relationship. Since our information is obtained from patients’ files, we cannot be certain if patients have consistently taken the prescribed medications. Furthermore, we did not consider the use of over-the-counter drugs, herbal medications, and supplements in our analysis, although some of them can have significant interactions with ARTs. Therefore, our results may not provide a completely accurate estimation of the overall DDI burden. Lastly, our patients did not include those with severe conditions as we are an outpatient center, although including this limited population could have impactful effects on the results, and it would be better to investigate this population separately [46].

Conclusion

Awareness and attention of physicians regarding DDIs between ART and non-ART medications are crucial, as they can lead to interactions ranging from minor to major, ultimately affecting the treatment process by influencing patients’ compliance with ART usage, or even resulting in misguided physician decisions to change ART. By altering patients’ non-ART drug regimens, the likelihood of these interactions can be reduced. Longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm casual association between DDI and drug change. Furthermore, it is recommended to conduct a similar study on a male population to observe potential variations in the outcomes.
