IGFL2 suppresses gastric cancer cell sensitivity to docetaxel by activating the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway
Authors: Wei Huang, Zhirui Tao, Xiaolin Xu, Huangming Cao, Li Cheng, Jin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDocetaxel (DTX) resistance limits therapeutic efficacy in gastric cancer (GC). Through RNA sequencing of DTX-resistant GC cells, we identified insulin-like growth factor-like family member 2 (IGFL2) as a key upregulated gene. We demonstrate that IGFL2 overexpression enhances GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while suppressing apoptosis, thereby reducing DTX sensitivity. Mechanistically, IGFL2 directly binds the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) promoter, activating the TLR4/MyD88/p65 pathway and upregulating drug efflux pumps MDR1 and BCRP. Knockdown of IGFL2 resensitizes resistant cells to DTX both in vitro and in mouse xenograft models. These findings establish IGFL2 as a regulator of DTX resistance via TLR4 signaling, suggesting its relevance as a therapeutic target for overcoming chemoresistance in GC.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical science, medical specialty, gastroenterology

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a common malignancy worldwide. According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN database, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of GC in China are 25.4% and 18.4%, ranking fifth and fourth among all cancers, respectively.1 Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, the detection rate of early GC in China remains approximately 20%, with over two-thirds of patients diagnosed at advanced stages and a 5-year survival rate below 5%.2 Thus, improving the survival and prognosis of patients with GC is urgently needed.

Despite the advent of targeted and immunotherapies, chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for advanced GC.3 It has been shown to prolong survival and improve quality of life in patients with advanced disease.4 Docetaxel (DTX), a representative taxane, exerts its anti-tumor effects by stabilizing microtubules and inhibiting cancer cell proliferation.5 Clinical studies have validated the efficacy of DTX, either as monotherapy or in combination, supporting its essential role in GC treatment.5,6,7 However, the clinical benefit of DTX-based therapy remains suboptimal: Median overall survival is extended by only 1.6 months, often remaining below 10 months. The development of primary or acquired resistance is a major cause of chemotherapy failure.8 Given the increasing incidence of DTX resistance, elucidating its mechanisms and identifying predictive biomarkers is critical to improving outcomes for patients with advanced GC.9

Insulin-like growth factor-like family member 2 (IGFL2) is a member of the human insulin-like growth factor-like (IGFL) gene family.10 The IGFL family encodes a group of systemic growth factors that serve as key regulators of fundamental cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis.11 Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that IGFL family members exert pivotal regulatory roles in the tumorigenesis and progression of multiple malignancies. Notably, IGFL1 has been reported to drive DTX chemoresistance in human prostate cancer via the repression of miR-143.12 However, the biological functions and clinical implications of IGFL2 remain largely uncharacterized to date. Pan-cancer analysis based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset revealed that IGFL2 expression is significantly upregulated across a spectrum of cancer types, including GC.13 However, whether IGFL2 contributes to DTX resistance in GC and the underlying mechanisms have not been elucidated.

In the present study, we aim to decipher the association between IGFL2 expression and DTX chemotherapeutic response in GC cells and further delineate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Our findings are expected to provide insights and promising therapeutic targets for overcoming DTX chemoresistance in the clinical management of advanced GC.

Results

IGFL2 is significantly upregulated in DTX-resistant GC cells and is clinically relevant

Following the establishment of DTX-resistant cells, mRNA from parental MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells was subjected to RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), which revealed numerous differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Figure 1A). Among these, IGFL2 was identified as a significantly upregulated gene in DTX-resistant cells (Table S2). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were primarily involved in biological processes such as extracellular structure organization and extracellular matrix organization. These genes were also associated with cellular components, including the extracellular matrix and the proteinaceous extracellular matrix, and participating in processes such as heparin binding and sulfur compound binding (Figure 1B).

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs, sorted by p value, identified 20 relevant pathways associated with DTX-resistant and drug-sensitive GC cells (Figure 1C). Analysis of the bubble plot reveals that the targets of these DEGs are mainly concentrated in Pathway in cancer and Neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions.

To assess the clinical relevance of IGFL2, we analyzed its expression using the UALCAN online database, which contains 415 gastric adenocarcinoma and 34 normal tissue samples. IGFL2 mRNA expression was markedly higher in gastric adenocarcinoma tissues than in normal tissues (Figure 1D). Survival analysis based on IGFL2 gene expression levels showed no significant correlation with patient survival rates (p = 0.88) (Figure 1E). Further analysis to determine the optimal expression threshold for patient stratification yielded a p value of 0.21, indicating the utility of IGFL2 as a standalone prognostic marker (Figure 1F). Additionally, analysis of the relationship between IGFL2 and clinicopathological parameters revealed a significant association with nodal (N) staging (Figure 1G).

Based on these findings, we further investigated whether IGFL2 could serve as a critical modulator of GC chemotherapy sensitivity. Using drug-sensitivity data from the GDSC database and the R package “pRRophetic,” we predicted each tumor sample’s chemotherapy sensitivity and explored the relationship between IGFL2 and sensitivity to common chemotherapeutic agents. The results showed a significant correlation between IGFL2 and sensitivity to DTX, paclitaxel, cisplatin, doxorubicin, and mitomycin C (Figure 1H).

IGFL2 regulates the proliferation, invasion, migration, and apoptosis of MGC-803 cells

To investigate the functional role of IGFL2 in GC cells and their DTX-resistant counterparts, MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells were transfected with shIGFL2 lentivirus to knock down IGFL2 expression. RT-qPCR results showed a significant reduction in IGFL2 mRNA levels in both MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells, confirming successful transfection (Figure 2A, p < 0.01). The effect of varying IGFL2 expression on cell viability in MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells was assessed at 0, 24, and 48 h. As illustrated in Figure 2B, IGFL2 overexpression markedly enhanced cell viability in both MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells, while IGFL2 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability in these cells.

Colony formation assays revealed that IGFL2 overexpression significantly increased the number of cell colonies (Figure 2C, p < 0.001), indicating a marked enhancement in cell proliferation. Conversely, IGFL2 knockdown notably inhibited the proliferation of MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells (p < 0.001). Transwell assays demonstrated that IGFL2 overexpression significantly enhanced cell migration (Figure 3A, p < 0.001) and invasion (Figure 3B, p < 0.001), thereby substantially improving the migratory and invasive capabilities of MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells. In contrast, IGFL2 knockdown significantly reduced these abilities (p < 0.001). Apoptosis assays showed that IGFL2 overexpression markedly inhibited apoptosis in MGC-803 cells (p < 0.05) and MGC-803R cells (p < 0.001), while IGFL2 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis in both MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells (Figure 3C, p < 0.001).

IGFL2 regulates the sensitivity of GC cells and DTX-resistant GC cells to DTX

To further investigate the role of IGFL2 in DTX resistance in GC, we examined its effect on the drug sensitivity in parental and resistant GC cells. Parental MGC-803 and resistant MGC-803R cells were treated with increasing concentrations of DTX, and cell viability was assessed after 24 h. The sensitivity of MGC-803R cells to DTX was significantly lower than that of the parental cells, with IC50 values of 1.34 nM for MGC-803 and 54.40 nM for MGC-803R (Figure 4A), confirming successful establishment of the resistant cell model. To determine whether IGFL2 could serve as a therapeutic target for resistant GC, we observed that the overexpression of IGFL2 in MGC-803 cells increased the IC50 to 3.824 nM, nearly three times that of parental cells, suggesting that IGFL2 overexpression reduces DTX sensitivity in MGC-803 cells. Conversely, IGFL2 knockdown in MGC-803R cells resulted in an IC50 of 20.33 nM, which is 0.37 times that of the parental cells, indicating that IGFL2 knockdown enhances DTX sensitivity in MGC-803R cells. Based on the IC50 results, subsequent experiments used the following DTX concentrations: MGC-803 (1.3 nM), MGC-803 + oeIGFL2 (3.8 nM), MGC-803R (50 nM), MGC-803R + shIGFL2 (20 nM).

IGFL2 knockdown significantly suppressed the viability of MGC-803R cells treated with DTX (p < 0.001), whereas IGFL2 overexpression markedly reversed the DTX-induced suppression of viability in MGC-803 cells (Figure 4B, p < 0.001). Furthermore, IGFL2 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferative capacity of MGC-803R cells under DTX treatment (p < 0.05), whereas IGFL2 overexpression notably reversed the DTX-induced inhibition of proliferation in MGC-803 cells (Figure 4C, p < 0.01).

Subsequently, we evaluated the contribution of IGFL2 downregulation to the invasive and migratory capabilities of MGC-803R cells. DTX markedly decreased the number of invasive and migratory cells in MGC-803R (p < 0.001), and co-administration with IGFL2 knockdown resulted in significantly lower invasive and migratory activity than DTX alone (Figure 5A, p < 0.01). In MGC-803 cells, DTX alone significantly reduced invasion and migration (p < 0.001), whereas IGFL2 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effects of DTX on invasion and migration (Figure 5B, p < 0.05). Apoptosis analysis demonstrated that DTX monotherapy significantly increased apoptosis in both MGC-803R (p < 0.05) and MGC-803 cells (p < 0.001), whereas IGFL2 overexpression counteracted DTX-induced apoptosis in MGC-803 cells (Figure 5C, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that IGFL2 knockdown enhances the sensitivity of MGC-803R cells to DTX.

IGFL2 directly transcriptionally regulates the expression of TLR4

To explore the potential molecular mechanisms by which IGFL2 influences tumor progression, we performed Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and found that patients with high IGFL2 expression exhibit the enrichment of signaling pathways such as NOTCH_SIGNALING, TGF_BETA_SIGNALING, and MTORC1_SIGNALING (Figure 6A). Furthermore, GSEA results indicate that IGFL2 is enriched in signaling pathways, including the p53, Toll-like receptor (TLR), and cell cycle pathways (Figure 6B), suggesting that IGFL2 may impact GC progression through these pathways.

Accumulating evidence indicates that TLR4, a key receptor in the TLR pathway, is closely associated with tumor drug resistance.14,15 Additionally, its downstream effectors, MyD88 and NF-κB p65, have been implicated in regulating resistance to DTX in various cancers,16,17 raising the possibility that IGFL2 may influence GC drug resistance through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis.

To clarify whether IGFL2 directly regulates TLR4 transcription, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays. As shown in Figure 6C, we examined the binding of the IGFL2 protein to the promoter region of the TLR4 gene in GC cell lines. The IgG isotype antibody control group showed only weak background signals, whereas the enrichment fold of IGFL2 antibodies in all experimental groups was significantly higher across all experimental groups (p < 0.001), indicating specific binding between the IGFL2 and the TLR4 promoter. In parental MGC-803 cells, endogenous IGFL2 showed basal binding to the TLR4 promoter. Overexpression of IGFL2 significantly enhanced the binding intensity. Conversely, knocking down IGFL2 expression using shRNA reduced the binding intensity (p < 0.01). These findings collectively demonstrate that IGFL2 directly binds to the TLR4 promoter region, and the binding intensity depends strictly on IGFL2 expression level.

Notably, in the drug-resistant MGC-803R cells, IGFL2 binding to the TLR4 promoter was substantially higher than in parental MGC-803 cells even under control (shNC) conditions (p < 0.001), indicating that endogenously overexpressed IGFL2 persistently occupies the TLR4 promoter in the drug-resistant state. Knocking down IGFL2 (shIGFL2) significantly attenuated this binding, further confirming the specificity of the interaction (p < 0.01).

In summary, the ChIP-qPCR data demonstrate that IGFL2 functions as a transcriptional regulator that directly binds to the TLR4 promoter. This binding is significantly enhanced in drug-resistant cells and likely represents a key upstream molecular event that sustains TLR4 signaling and ultimately mediates chemotherapy resistance.

To further confirm the direct regulatory role of IGFL2 in TLR4 transcriptional regulation, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay. As shown in Figure 6D, we verified the regulatory effect of the IGFL2 regulatory function on the TLR4 promoter in parental MGC-803 cells. Compared to the empty vector control group, overexpression of IGFL2 significantly activated the TLR4 promoter (p < 0.001), markedly enhancing its transcriptional activity. Conversely, knockdown of endogenous IGFL2 expression significantly reduced basal promoter activity (p < 0.001). This clearly demonstrates that IGFL2 is not only sufficient to activate TLR4 transcription but also required for maintaining its basal transcriptional activity. More importantly, we investigated the pathological relevance of this regulatory mechanism in DTX-resistant MGC-803R cells. In these cells, basal transcriptional activity of the TLR4 promoter was abnormally elevated, consistent with the ChIP-qPCR finding that IGFL2 binding to the TLR4 promoter is significantly enhanced in the drug-resistant setting. Furthermore, knockdown of IGFL2 effectively reversed this elevated transcriptional state (p < 0.001), leading to a significant decrease in luciferase activity. This indicates that the sustained high transcription of TLR4 in drug-resistant cells is largely driven and maintained by endogenously overexpressed IGFL2.

Together, the dual-luciferase reporter and ChIP-qPCR assay confirm that IGFL2 directly binds the TLR4 promoter and activates its transcription. In DTX-resistant cells, high IGFL2 expression leads to the abnormal upregulation of TLR4 transcriptional activity, revealing the core mechanism by which the IGFL2-TLR4 axis mediates chemotherapy resistance in GC at the transcriptional level.

IGFL2 mediates DTX sensitivity by activating the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway

To determine whether TLR4, a key downstream effector of IGFL2, mediates its role in chemotherapy resistance in GC, we used WB to assess protein expression in the TLR signaling pathway. In MGC-803 cells, DTX treatment significantly suppressed TLR4 (p < 0.001) and MyD88 expression (p < 0.001), as well as p65 phosphorylation (p < 0.05). Overexpression of IGFL2 reversed these DTX-induced reductions. In MGC-803R cells, IGFL2 knockdown further enhanced the inhibitory effect of DTX on MyD88 expression (p < 0.05) and p65 phosphorylation (p < 0.001) (Figure 7A).

TLR4 recruits MyD88, promoting NF-κB nuclear translocation and inducing the release of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α.18 Therefore, we used ELISA to observe the expression of inflammatory signaling factors. Quantitative analysis in MGC-803R cells showed that in the shNC control group, DTX exposure significantly induced a marked increase in the secretion of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and TNF-α (p < 0.001), suggesting that chemotherapeutic stress activates the expression of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and TNF-α via the TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB pathway. Notably, knockdown of the IGFL2 gene (shIGFL2+vehicle) led to the increased basal secretion of IL-2 (p < 0.001), IL-4 (p < 0.05), IL-6 (p < 0.05), IFN-γ (p < 0.05), and TNF-α (p < 0.05) compared to wild-type cells, which is consistent with the enhanced nuclear translocation of NF-κB detected by western blotting. This indicates that under steady-state conditions, IGFL2 negatively regulates the synthesis of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and TNF-α by suppressing TLR4 signaling. Under DTX stimulation, the secretion of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the shIGFL2+DTX group further increased compared to its own basal level (p < 0.001) and was significantly higher than that in the shNC+DTX group (p < 0.01). These results indicate that loss of IGFL2 mimics the activating effect of DTX on the TLR4 pathway and that IGFL2 deficiency synergistically enhances DTX-induced TLR4 pathway activation (Figure 7B).

To clarify the necessity of TLR4 in the IGFL2-mediated chemoresistance phenotype, we conducted functional rescue experiments targeting TLR4. In MGC-803R cells with IGFL2 knockdown, we treated them with the TLR4-specific agonist LPS and the inhibitor TAK-242, and observed their effects on NF-κB pathway activation, inflammatory factor secretion, and cellular drug resistance. Western blot results indicated that treatment with the TLR4 agonist LPS significantly reversed the decrease in TLR4 and p-p65 protein levels caused by IGFL2 knockdown (shIGFL2). Conversely, treatment with the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 mimicked the effects of shIGFL2, further suppressing TLR4 pathway activity. Moreover, combining TAK-242 with shIGFL2 did not produce any additional inhibitory effect (Figure 7C). These complementary sets of experimental evidence demonstrate that TLR4 is a critical downstream node through which IGFL2 regulates NF-κB pathway activation.

The ELISA results showed that LPS treatment significantly reversed the shIGFL2-mediated inhibition of inflammatory factor secretion (represented by IL-6, p < 0.001), restoring levels to those of the shNC control group. Conversely, TAK-242 effectively suppressed inflammatory factors (p < 0.001), and combined treatment with shIGFL2 and TAK-242 did not further reduce cytokine levels compared to shIGFL2 alone (Figure 7D).

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis showed that under DTX stimulation, inhibition of TLR4 or knockdown of IGFL2 significantly induced apoptosis (p < 0.001), while TLR4 activation notably protected cells from DTX-induced apoptosis and partially reversed the pro-apoptotic effect of shIGFL2 (Figure 7E). Similarly, colony formation assays indicated that activating TLR4 enhanced the clonogenic survival ability of cells under DTX pressure (p < 0.001), whereas inhibiting TLR4 significantly impaired this ability (Figure 7F, p < 0.001). Finally, CCK-8 cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that these molecular and cellular changes directly translated into altered DTX sensitivity. The IC50 increased in the LPS-treated group (p < 0.001), while TAK-242 treatment or IGFL2 knockdown significantly reduced the IC50 (p < 0.001). Activating TLR4 on the basis of IGFL2 knockdown partially reversed its sensitizing effect (p < 0.001). In contrast, simultaneous knockdown of IGFL2 and inhibition of TLR4 did not produce an additive effect (Figure 7G). These results confirm that TLR4 is a direct downstream node of IGFL2 function.

IGFL2 promotes DTX resistance by upregulating the expression and activity of drug efflux pumps

To investigate whether IGFL2 mediates drug resistance by regulating drug efflux pumps, we examined the expression and function of multidrug resistance proteins. qRT-PCR results showed that knocking down IGFL2 in MGC-803R drug-resistant cells significantly downregulated the mRNA expression levels of MDR1 and BCRP (Figures 8A and 8B, p < 0.001). Conversely, overexpression of IGFL2 in MGC-803 parental cells significantly upregulated MDR1 expression (Figures 8C and 8D, p < 0.001). More importantly, functional Rhodamine 123 (Rh123) accumulation assays revealed that knockdown of IGFL2 led to a significant increase in intracellular Rh123 accumulation in MGC-803R cells to 185.0% ± 10.0 (Figure 8E, p < 0.001), indicating a marked inhibition of P-gp efflux pump function. WB results further confirmed this finding (Figure 8F). Correspondingly, overexpression of IGFL2 in MGC-803 cells significantly reduced intracellular Rh123 accumulation (Figures 8G and 8H, p < 0.001), demonstrating enhanced P-gp function.

To further investigate whether TLR4 mediates the IGFL2-induced upregulation of MDR1, we performed rescue experiments in MGC-803R cells. As shown in Figures 8I and 8J, knockdown of IGFL2 (shIGFL2) significantly decreased MDR1 mRNA and protein expression (p < 0.001), consistent with our earlier observations. However, treatment with the TLR4-specific agonist LPS markedly reversed this downregulation (p < 0.001), restoring MDR1 expression levels in IGFL2-knockdown cells. In contrast, treatment with the TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242 did not produce an additive inhibitory effect when combined with IGFL2 knockdown, suggesting that IGFL2 and TLR4 operate within the same signaling axis to control MDR1 transcription. The specificity of these pharmacological modulators was validated by the observation that LPS alone increased, while TAK-242 alone decreased, basal MDR1 expression (Figures 8I and 8J). These data collectively demonstrate that TLR4 is a necessary downstream effector through which IGFL2 upregulates MDR1 expression, thereby contributing to the multidrug resistance phenotype in GC cells.

In summary, these results demonstrate that IGFL2 positively regulates the expression of multidrug resistance proteins and enhances the functional activity of drug efflux pumps. This may represent another key mechanism by which IGFL2 reduces intracellular chemotherapeutic drug concentration, thereby mediating DTX resistance in GC.

IGFL2 regulates DTX sensitivity through activating the TLR signaling pathway in vivo

To investigate the tumor-promoting and chemoresistance-inducing effects of IGFL2 in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model and then administered intraperitoneal injections of DMSO or DTX. Figures 9A and 9B depict the macroscopic views of the mice and tumors. IGFL2 knockdown did not significantly affect mouse body weight (Figure 9C). Tumor volume and weight measurements over 31 days showed that IGFL2 knockdown significantly inhibited tumor growth (p < 0.001). Moreover, compared to DTX treatment alone, IGFL2 knockdown increased GC sensitivity to DTX, with a noticeable decrease in tumor volume starting from day 25 (Figure 9D). IGFL2 knockout markedly reduced both tumor volume and weight (Figures 9E and 9F, p < 0.001), indicating a substantial increase in DTX sensitivity in DTX-resistant cells. Additionally, IHC analysis of tumor tissues revealed that IGFL2 knockdown reduced the expression of TLR4 and MyD88, and the phosphorylation of p65, following DTX treatment compared to the control group (Figure 9G).

Discussion

In this study, we identified IGFL2 as a highly expressed gene in DTX-resistant GC cell models. Through bioinformatics analysis, RNA-seq, and in vitro and in vivo experimental validation, we found that IGFL2 promoted GC progression and induced DTX resistance by activating the TLR signaling pathway. These results indicate that IGFL2 may serve as a potential target for reversing DTX resistance in GC.

To further delineate the molecular underpinnings of DTX resistance, we performed differential gene expression and enrichment analysis between parental GC cells and their isogenic DTX-resistant counterparts, which identified significant enrichment of IGFL2 in the TLR signaling cascade. In line with our findings, Wang et al. previously revealed via gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) that genes associated with high IGFL2 expression were markedly enriched in the TLR signaling pathway in stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD).13 The TLR signaling cascade, a core component of the innate immune system, was initially extensively characterized for its canonical functions in pathogen sensing and triggering inflammatory responses.19,20 However, a growing body of evidence over recent years has established that the TLR signaling pathway exerts multifaceted and critical roles not only in tumorigenesis and malignant progression, but also in the acquisition and maintenance of chemotherapeutic resistance.21,22,23 Mechanistically, TLR4 activation facilitates the nuclear translocation of NF-κB via the MyD88-dependent axis, triggering the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α.24 This in turn drives the activation of downstream pro-survival signaling programs, ultimately endowing tumor cells with the capacity to evade chemotherapy-induced apoptotic cell death.25 Consistent with this, prior studies have validated that TLR4-dependent signaling promotes paclitaxel resistance in advanced breast cancer and melanoma,26 while Zhang Y et al. further demonstrated that TLR4 signaling confers DTX chemoresistance in PC-3 human prostate cancer cells.15 More recent studies have further expanded this concept, demonstrating that TLR4 activation contributes to chemoresistance in thyroid cancer and that targeting the TLR4/NF-κB axis can overcome drug resistance in multiple malignancies.27 These prior works have firmly established the functional significance of the TLR signaling pathway in the setting of chemoresistance, framing it not merely as a canonical inflammation-mediating pathway, but as a central signaling hub governing pro-survival and anti-apoptotic programs. Building on this foundational evidence, we herein performed chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative real-time PCR (ChIP-qPCR) and dual-luciferase reporter assays, and provided evidence that IGFL2 acts as a transcriptional regulator to directly bind the promoter region of TLR4 and activate its transcription, thereby driving the sustained activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis at the transcriptional level. This discovery redefines the functional role of IGFL2, advancing it from a passive prognostic biomarker to an active functional mediator that directly regulates gene transcription and offers mechanistic insights into the upstream regulatory control of TLR signaling in the context of chemoresistance.

We further demonstrated that IGFL2 knockdown markedly diminished the protein expression of TLR4 and MyD88, as well as the phosphorylation level of NF-κB p65. Moreover, IGFL2 silencing significantly augmented the DTX-induced downregulation of TLR4, MyD88, and phospho-p65, collectively indicating that the TLR signaling cascade is critically involved in the IGFL2-mediated impairment of DTX chemosensitivity in resistant GC cells. This finding is in full agreement with our aforementioned characterization of TLR4 as a core pro-survival signaling hub in the acquisition and maintenance of GC chemoresistance. To definitively establish the functional requirement of TLR4 in the IGFL2-driven chemoresistance signaling network, we performed a series of mechanistic rescue experiments using the TLR4-specific agonist lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and the selective TLR4 inhibitor TAK-242.28 Our results revealed that the pharmacological activation of TLR4 by LPS markedly abrogated the chemosensitization effects triggered by IGFL2 knockdown, including enhanced apoptotic cell death, impaired clonogenic capacity, and reversal of IC50 reduction. Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of TLR4 with TAK-242 fully phenocopied the functional phenotypes of IGFL2 silencing. Of note, simultaneous targeting of IGFL2 and TLR4 failed to yield an additive effect on DTX sensitivity in GC cells. These data definitively identify TLR4 as the critical, non-redundant downstream effector that mediates the pro-chemoresistance function of IGFL2. Our findings further reinforce the central regulatory role of the TLR signaling pathway in chemoresistance, establishing that this cascade is not merely a concomitant event of the drug-resistant phenotype, but rather an indispensable signaling node required for IGFL2 to drive DTX resistance in GC.

It is worth noting that previous studies have mainly focused on the oncogenic functions of the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) IGFL2-AS1 in various cancers.11,12,13,29 In contrast, our study revealed the independent role of the IGFL2 protein itself as a direct transcriptional regulator, which highlights the non-redundant role of the IGFL2 protein in mediating chemoresistance. In addition, our study also identified that IGFL2 promotes DTX resistance by upregulating the expression and function of multidrug resistance proteins (MDR1/P-gp and BCRP). Rhodamine 123 (Rh123) accumulation assay confirmed that IGFL2 significantly enhanced the efflux pump activity of P-gp. These results indicate that on the one hand, IGFL2 promotes cell survival and inflammatory cytokine release through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. On the other hand, it reduces intracellular drug accumulation by enhancing the function of drug efflux pumps. This synergistic effect of multiple mechanisms may explain why high IGFL2 expression is closely correlated with a robust drug-resistant phenotype. Meanwhile, this also suggests that there may be crosstalk between the two regulatory mechanisms mediated by IGFL2, given that the promoters of MDR1 and BCRP contain NF-κB binding sites. IGFL2-driven NF-κB activation may directly promote the transcription of efflux pumps, thus forming an integrated drug resistance regulatory system. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that NF-κB can directly regulate MDR1 transcription in various cancer models, providing a strong rationale for this potential crosstalk.30,31 Future studies are required to further elucidate this potential crosstalk through functional assays, including ChIP-sequencing and intervention with NF-κB inhibitors, which will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the multidimensional functions of the TLR signaling pathway in the regulation of chemoresistance.

Although our study has thoroughly revealed the core role of the IGFL2-TLR4 axis in DTX resistance, there are still some limitations. First, whether IGFL2 itself is regulated by upstream signals remains unknown. Emerging evidence suggests that various transcription factors and epigenetic modifiers can regulate IGFL family member expression, and future studies should explore whether similar mechanisms govern IGFL2 expression in GC.13,32 Second, the specific mechanism by which IGFL2 regulates the expression of MDR1/BCRP needs to be further investigated. Finally, this study was mainly conducted in cell lines and mouse models and lacks validation using paired samples from patients with GC before and after chemotherapy, which cannot directly demonstrate the feasibility of IGFL2 as a clinical biomarker. Therefore, future studies should focus on verifying the correlation between IGFL2 expression and the therapeutic efficacy of DTX in retrospective cohorts, and exploring the potential of circulating IGFL2 as a dynamic biomarker for liquid biopsy.

Taken together, in this study, we identified IGFL2 as a differentially expressed gene between DTX-resistant GC cells and parental cells through sequencing analysis. We confirmed via in vitro and in vivo experiments that IGFL2 directly transcriptionally activates TLR4 expression, which in turn activates the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway and upregulates the function of drug efflux pumps, thereby synergistically promoting DTX resistance. These findings not only provide a new perspective for elucidating the complex molecular mechanism of DTX resistance in GC but also offer potential therapeutic strategies for reversing chemoresistance by targeting the IGFL2-TLR4 axis.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the upstream mechanisms driving IGFL2 overexpression in DTX-resistant GC cells remain undefined. While we establish that IGFL2 directly activates TLR4 transcription, the factors responsible for its own upregulation—such as potential transcriptional regulators or epigenetic modifications—have not been identified. Elucidating these upstream signals may provide additional therapeutic opportunities. Second, our in vivo experiments were conducted exclusively in male BALB/c nude mice. Whether sex influences the IGFL2-mediated DTX resistance phenotype remains unknown, and future studies incorporating both sexes are warranted to assess the generalizability of our findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

The human gastric cancer cell line MGC-803 (male origin) was purchased from Shanghai iCell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Cat# iCell-h141, RRID: CVCL_5334). The DTX-resistant derivative MGC-803R was established in-house by continuous exposure to escalating concentrations of docetaxel as described in the method details. Both cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. For MGC-803R cells, the culture medium was additionally supplemented with 10 nM docetaxel to maintain the resistant phenotype. Cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and mycoplasma contamination was tested monthly using a PCR-based detection kit (Beyotime). All experiments were performed using cells within 10 passages after thawing.

Animal models

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital (Approval No. 2023012) and conformed to the relevant regulatory standards and institutional guidelines. Twenty-four male BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were purchased from Shanghai SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under controlled conditions: 12-h light/dark cycle, temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and humidity of 50% ± 10%, with free access to sterile food and water. After one week of acclimatization, orthotopic gastric tumor models were established. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a small incision was made along the abdominal midline to expose the stomach. A suspension of 5 × 106 MGC-803R-shNC or MGC-803R-shIGFL2 cells (in 20 μL PBS) was injected into the stomach wall using an insulin syringe. Once tumors on the stomach wall reached approximately 100 mm3, tumor fragments were harvested and transplanted subcutaneously into recipient mice. Mice were then randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group) using a random number generator: NC (vehicle control), DTX (10 mg/kg docetaxel, intravenous injection every 2 days), shIGFL2 (MGC-803R-shIGFL2 tumor-bearing mice receiving vehicle), and shIGFL2+DTX (MGC-803R-shIGFL2 tumor-bearing mice receiving DTX). Tumor volume was measured every 2 days using digital calipers and calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. Body weight was recorded concurrently to monitor general health. At the experimental endpoint (day 31), mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose followed by cervical dislocation. Tumors were excised, weighed, photographed, and divided into two parts: one part was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analyses, and the other part was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemistry. The influence of sex on the results was not assessed because only male mice were used; this is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Method details

Establishment of DTX-resistant MGC-803R cells

MGC-803 cells were initially cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 nM docetaxel (DTX). After 48 h, the medium was replaced with drug-free medium, and cells were allowed to recover and proliferate for 2–3 weeks until normal growth resumed. The DTX concentration was then increased in a stepwise manner (10 nM → 20 nM → 50 nM → 100 nM). At each concentration, cells were exposed to the drug for 48 h, followed by a recovery period in drug-free medium. After approximately 3 months of continuous selection, a stable DTX-resistant cell line was established and designated MGC-803R. The resistant phenotype was maintained by culturing MGC-803R cells in medium containing 10 nM DTX, and the cells were passaged in drug-free medium for one week prior to all experiments to eliminate acute drug effects.

Cell transfection

Lentiviral particles for shRNA targeting IGFL2 (shIGFL2), scrambled control shRNA (shNC), IGFL2 overexpression (oeIGFL2), and empty vector control were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were transfected with lentiviral particles at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 in the presence of 5 μg/mL polybrene. After 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Stable transfectants were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 7 days. Knockdown and overexpression efficiencies were validated by RT-qPCR and western blotting before each experiment.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or homogenized tumor tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the 5×TransScript All-in-One SuperMix for qPCR kit (Transgen). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SybrGreen qPCR Master Mix (Transgen) on an ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The thermal cycling conditions were: 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2ˆ(-ΔΔCt) method. All primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA was extracted from MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells (three biological replicates per group) using TRIzol reagent. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), and all samples had an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. Briefly, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. After end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, the libraries were amplified by PCR and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing reads were filtered to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences using Trimmomatic v0.39. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR v2.7.10a. Read counts were quantified using featureCounts v2.0.1. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells were identified using DESeq2 (v1.38.0) and EdgeR (v3.40.0) with thresholds of adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log2(fold change)| > 1. The complete list of DEGs is provided in Table S2.

CCK-8 assay and IC50 determination

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Beyotime). For proliferation assays, transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL complete medium. At 0, 24, and 48 h after seeding, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37°C in the dark for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad). For IC50 determination, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with DTX at the following concentrations: 0, 0.0316, 0.1, 0.316, 1, 3.16, 10, 31.6, 100, and 316 nM. After 24 h of treatment, cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay as described above. IC50 values were calculated by fitting the dose-response curves using non-linear regression (log[inhibitor] vs. normalized response – variable slope) in GraphPad Prism 9.

Colony formation assay

Cells in logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, resuspended, and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 700 cells per well. Cells were cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) for 14 days, with the medium replaced every 3 days. At the endpoint, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. After rinsing with distilled water and air-drying, colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted under a light microscope. The entire well was imaged using a 4× objective for documentation.

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was assessed using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Pharmingen). Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After the specified treatments, cells were harvested by trypsinization without EDTA, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 μL of 1× binding buffer. Cell density was adjusted to 1 × 105 cells per sample. Cells were stained with 5 μL FITC Annexin V and 5 μL propidium iodide (PI) in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. After adding 400 μL of 1× binding buffer, samples were analyzed within 1 h using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Data were processed using FlowJo v10 software. Early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI−) and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+) were combined for statistical analysis.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (8-μm pore size, Corning). For migration assays, cells were trypsinized, washed with serum-free RPMI-1640, and resuspended in serum-free medium at a concentration of 3 × 105 cells/mL. A total of 0.3 mL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 0.6 mL of RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with 80 μL of Matrigel (Corning, Cat# 356234) diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium and allowed to solidify at 37°C for 30 min prior to cell seeding. After incubation at 37°C for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 5 min. Stained cells were counted in five randomly selected fields per insert under an optical microscope at 200× magnification.

Western blotting

Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (EDTA-free, Beyotime) for 30 min on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20) containing 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used were: anti-TLR4 (1:1000, Proteintech 19811-1-AP), anti-MyD88 (1:1000, Proteintech 23230-1-AP), anti-p65 (1:2000, Proteintech 10745-1-AP), anti-phospho-p65 (Ser536) (1:1000, Proteintech 82335-1-RR), anti-MDR1 (1:1000, Proteintech 22336-1-AP), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech 10494-1-AP). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000, Proteintech SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Beyotime) and imaged using a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH), and relative protein expression was normalized to GAPDH.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were performed using a ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells (1 × 107 cells per condition) were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by quenching with 125 mM glycine for 5 min. Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, harvested, and lysed in SDS lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Chromatin was sheared to fragments of 200–1000 bp by sonication on ice using a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode). A portion of the sheared chromatin was saved as the Input control. The remaining chromatin was divided and incubated overnight at 4°C with either 2 μg of anti-IGFL2 antibody (custom-made by GeneChem) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control, Cell Signaling Technology 2729). Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to capture the immune complexes, followed by sequential washes with Low Salt, High Salt, LiCl Immune Complex Wash Buffers, and TE Buffer. After elution, reverse cross-linking, and Proteinase K digestion, DNA was purified using spin columns. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using primers specific to the human TLR4 gene promoter region (forward: 5′-CTGCAGGTGACTTCCCTAGG-3′; reverse: 5′-GAGGCAGAGACACAGTGCTG-3′). Enrichment of IGFL2 at the TLR4 promoter was calculated as the percentage of input using the formula: % Input = 2ˆ[(Ct_input – 3.32) – Ct_ChIP] × 100%.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To assess the transcriptional regulation of TLR4 by IGFL2, MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. At 70% confluency, cells were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) with the following plasmids: 400 ng of pGL3-TLR4 promoter reporter plasmid (containing the human TLR4 promoter region [-2000 to +100 bp] cloned into pGL3-Basic vector; GeneChem), 20 ng of pRL-TK Renilla luciferase internal control plasmid (Promega), and 400 ng of effector plasmids (oeIGFL2, shIGFL2, or their respective empty vector controls). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed in 100 μL of Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega). Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax 20/20 luminometer (Promega). Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity. All transfections were performed in triplicate, and each experiment was repeated three times.

ELISA

Supernatants from treated MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells were collected and centrifuged at 1,000×g for 10 min to remove cell debris. The levels of IL-1β and TNF-α were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Beyotime, Cat# PI301 and PT518) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For multi-cytokine analysis (IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IFN-γ), a custom ProcartaPlex Multiplex Immunoassay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad), and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated using recombinant protein standards provided in the kits.

Rhodamine 123 (Rh123) accumulation assay

The functional activity of the drug efflux pump P-glycoprotein (P-gp/MDR1) was assessed using the Rhodamine 123 accumulation assay. Briefly, MGC-803 and MGC-803R cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After attachment, cells were subjected to the designated transfection or drug treatments. Cells were then incubated with 10 μM Rhodamine 123 (Beyotime) at 37°C in the dark for 60 min. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, trypsinized, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in 500 μL of ice-cold PBS. Intracellular Rh123 fluorescence intensity (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 530 nm) was immediately analyzed by flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Data are presented as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) relative to the control group.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Excised tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned into 4-μm slices using a microtome. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to distilled water, and subjected to antigen retrieval by heating in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against TLR4 (1:200, Proteintech 19811-1-AP), MyD88 (1:200, Proteintech 23230-1-AP), p65 (1:500, Proteintech 10745-1-AP), and phospho-p65 (1:200, Proteintech 82335-1-RR) overnight at 4°C. Following three washes with PBS, sections were incubated with SignalStain Boost IHC Detection Reagent (HRP, Rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology 8114) for 30 min at room temperature. Color development was performed using a DAB Substrate Kit (Beyotime), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8) at 400× magnification. Staining intensity was scored semi-quantitatively by two independent pathologists blinded to the experimental groups.

Bioinformatics analysis

Processed mRNA expression data (HTSeq-FPKM) for stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), comprising 448 samples (36 normal and 412 tumor samples). Gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, version 7.0). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was performed using the GSVA R package (v1.46.0) to score each gene set and assess potential changes in biological function across different samples. Patients were divided into high and low IGFL2 expression groups based on the median IGFL2 expression level. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.6.0) to identify differentially enriched pathways between the two groups, with an adjusted p-value threshold of <0.05. Chemotherapy sensitivity prediction was performed using the R package “pRRophetic” (v0.5) based on drug sensitivity data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). IC50 values for specific chemotherapeutic agents were estimated using ridge regression, with 10-fold cross-validation performed on the GDSC training set to assess prediction accuracy. Default parameters were used, including the “combat” method for batch-effect removal and averaging duplicate gene expression entries.

TLR4 rescue experiments

For TLR4 pathway rescue experiments, MGC-803R cells transfected with shIGFL2 or shNC were treated with either the TLR4-specific agonist lipopolysaccharides (LPS, 1 μg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) or the TLR4-specific inhibitor TAK-242 (5 μM, MedChemExpress) for 24 h. Control groups received an equivalent volume of vehicle (PBS containing 0.1% DMSO). Following treatment, cells were harvested for qRT-PCR, western blotting, apoptosis analysis, colony formation assays, and CCK-8 assays as described above. For combination experiments assessing drug efflux pump regulation, cells were subjected to the same treatment regimen prior to RNA extraction and western blotting for MDR1 detection.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends. The exact value of n, what n represents, and the definition of center and dispersion measures are provided in each figure legend. For comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons was applied. For experiments involving two independent variables (e.g., treatment and genotype), two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with specific p-value thresholds indicated in figure legends as follows: p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001.

For animal experiments, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups using a random number generator to minimize allocation bias. No formal power analysis was performed to predetermine sample size; sample sizes (n = 6 per group) were based on prior experience with similar xenograft models and standard practice in the field to ensure sufficient statistical power for detecting biologically meaningful differences. Blinding was not formally applied during data collection and analysis, with the exception of IHC scoring, which was performed by two independent pathologists blinded to the experimental groups. All data points were included in the statistical analyses; no outliers were excluded. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively, and were found to be satisfied for all parametric analyses. In cases where assumptions were not met, non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were applied as noted in the figure legends.

For RNA-seq differential expression analysis, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log2(fold change)| > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. For GSEA, normalized enrichment scores (NES) and adjusted p-values were calculated using the fgsea R package with 10,000 permutations.
