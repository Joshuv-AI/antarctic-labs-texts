Introduction

Mutations in the gene coding for the transcription factor NKX2-5 were initially linked to familial secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) and progressive atrioventricular (AV) block by Schott et. al.1 Conotruncal abnormalities, particularly nonsyndromic tetralogy of Fallot, other ventricular septal defects, and cardiomyopathies were also observed1, 2, 3 with some degree of AV conduction disease (AVCD),4 ranging from first degree AV block to complete heart block (CHB). In each of these early reports involving multiple novel NKX2-5 mutations, there were cases of sudden cardiac death (SCD) either in young or middle-aged adults well beyond the time of surgical repair, and in 1 case, with no structural heart disease.3 A recent review of the literature reported a substantial number of subjects with hypoplastic left heart syndrome, in addition to the lesions previously observed, with up to 15% of SCD in NKX2-5 mutations with familial ASD and conduction disease or arrhythmia.5 There was presumption that the SCD was due to unrecognized progression to CHB. However, recent reports have implicated ventricular arrhythmias,6,7 including one describing sudden death in 2 family members with functioning pacemakers,7 and a third member having documented ventricular fibrillation (VF). Our report confirms the potentially fatal nature of ventricular arrhythmias in NKX2-5 mutation, documenting ventricular tachycardia (VT) in 2 generations of a family that presented in childhood, 1 with a cardiac arrest.

Case report

The index patient was evaluated in the nursery because of a maternal history of aborted SCD secondary to VF at the age of 17 years, 4 years following uncomplicated surgical repair of an ASD. The cardiac arrest was attributed to long QT syndrome, and the mother underwent implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation for secondary prevention. Evaluation of the infant included an electrocardiogram (ECG), which was normal (PR 127 ms, corrected QT 415 ms). Furthermore, a long QT syndrome gene panel obtained from the mother was negative for pathologic mutations.

The infant was reevaluated at the age of 11 months for a systolic murmur; the echocardiogram demonstrated a moderately large secundum ASD and valvar pulmonary stenosis. At the age of 2 years, he underwent surgical repair of the ASD and dilation of the pulmonary valve. The preoperative ECG demonstrated first-degree AV block (PR 190 ms), with no significant change on the postoperative ECG before discharge from the hospital.

Progressive AV conduction block was observed in the child. By the age of 6 years, there was AV node Wenckebach, and the following year, a dual-chamber pacemaker was implanted for CHB (Figure 1).

Routine remote pacemaker monitoring demonstrated a 9-beat run of nonsustained monomorphic VT at the age of 10 years. The patient was started on nadolol, and the dose was titrated to 1.5 mg/kg per day. However, he continued to have nonsustained VT, including a 15-beat run (Figure 2).

On an echocardiogram, there was trivial residual pulmonary stenosis/regurgitation, no residual ASD, and structurally normal ventricles; the ejection fraction was 60%. Given the history of ASD in 2 consecutive generations and progressive AVCD in the index case, whole exome sequencing (GeneDx, 207 Perry Parkway, Gaithersburg, MD) was obtained for our index patient. A likely pathogenic mutation was identified in the NKX2-5 homeodomain (Val182Leu) and subsequently confirmed in the patient’s mother as well. The index patient and the patient’s mother are the only members of the family with a confirmed NKX2-5 mutation (Figure 3).

Regarding the mother’s arrhythmia history, her ECG demonstrated dual-chamber pacing (Figure 4). Over the years, despite treatment with a beta blocker and sotalol, she continued to have episodes of self-terminating polymorphic VT (Figure 5) and sustained episodes requiring appropriate ICD discharges (Figure 6). On multiple echocardiograms performed over the years, she was not found to have left ventricular (LV) noncompaction (LVNC) (Figure 7). Mexiletine was added 6 years ago and since then there have been no further arrhythmias. The family is currently considering the option of upgrading the child’s pacemaker to an ICD.

Discussion

Since the initial descriptions of structural heart disease with progressive AVCD, the electrophysiologic spectrum of disorders for pathogenic variations in the NKX2-5 gene has expanded substantially. The arrhythmia phenotype now includes atrial fibrillation,6, 7, 8 atrial flutter,6 and nonsustained VT.7,5 In a review of the literature, among the 208 patients and 60 different pathogenic gene mutations in NKX2-5 identified, there were 19 SCDs from 9 pedigrees.5 Although this alarming number of SCDs raised concern that, in addition to CHB, ventricular arrhythmias might play a role in some families, only recently have such arrhythmias been documented. Jhaveri et al6 reported an asymptomatic, relatively slow monomorphic VT in 2 brothers whose father had died suddenly at the age of 29 years, and in whom an LVNC was suggested on the autopsy. However, the VT rate was only 140 beats per minute; a hemodynamically stable, monomorphic VT in the presence of normal cardiac function may not be related to the cause of sudden death in the father with LVNC. Helm et al9 described a kindred that included a teenager who died during sleep and a half-sibling with monomorphic VT, rate 140 beats per minute. Again, the child who died, as well as a first cousin once removed, had LVNC syndrome, complicating the interpretation of the ventricular arrhythmia presented as secondary to LVNC or primary due to the NKX2-5 mutation. The report by Perera et al,7 showing rapid, polymorphic, nonsustained polymorphic and rapid VT on an ICD tracing, gets closer to proving causation, but this was in an asymptomatic patient; no arrhythmia tracings were available from the two families with SCD who had functioning pacemakers.

Our report attempts to provide the missing link between unstable VT and SCD due to NKX2-5 mutation. The mother of our index patient required resuscitation from an SCD event as a teenager and experienced multiple episodes subsequently of rapid polymorphic VT/VF, sometimes converting spontaneously, but often triggering an ICD discharge. This report proves that in addition to CHB, polymorphic VT/VF can cause SCD in patients with an NKX2-5 mutation. As for the mechanism of VT, the rapid, polymorphic VT initiated by a long-short coupling interval on at least 2 tracings from the mother is consistent with an unstable Purkinje fiber origin, possibly focal, based upon the similarity of the coupling intervals. Benson observed that the spectrum of structural phenotypes recognized in NKX2-5 mutations demonstrates the broad impact of the transcription factor for which it codes on cardiac morphogenesis (eg, septation, conotruncal alignment, valve formation, even myocardial integrity), as evidenced by the association with LVNC.3 Regarding the electrophysiologic manifestations, it was postulated that the transcription factor was necessary for the normal function of primitive AV nodal cells, but not for the specialized fibers of the conduction system.2 However, subsequently, mouse knock-out models demonstrated impairment of His-Purkinje fiber function,10 implying an extensive downstream effect of haplotype insufficiency in the protein produced by the NKX2-5 gene.

Stable, monomorphic VT has been previously reported by others. In our index case, this was relatively rapid (shortest cycle length 230 ms), nonsustained, and asymptomatic. Is this a harbinger of the unstable, polymorphic VT his mother exhibits, or is it a different mechanism entirely? The monomorphic VT exhibited by the child has progressively shorter cycle length, implying abnormal automaticity, but the initiating coupling interval is longer, suggesting it is not necessarily of Purkinje fiber origin.

Several of the prior reports of VT linked to NKX2-5 mutations are in families with LVNC established by autopsy (in cases of SCD) or suspected by echocardiography. Neither the child in the current report nor his mother has evidence from an echocardiogram for LVNC. The mechanism of VT may have implications for the risk of SCD and consequent indications for an ICD. The mother had a class I indication for secondary prevention, having survived a cardiac arrest due to idiopathic polymorphic VT,11 whether the origin was a primary arrhythmia or secondary to LVNC. For the child with no syncope and monomorphic VT, management is less clear. Monomorphic VT plus evidence of LVNC by echocardiography or magnetic resonance imaging would constitute a class I indication for primary prevention.12 In the absence of symptoms, nonsustained VT plus LV systolic dysfunction would be a class IIa indication. However, with no evidence of cardiomyopathy, LVNC, or otherwise, he does not fall within the guidelines for primary prevention.11 Nonetheless, given the mother’s history of a resuscitated cardiac arrest, polymorphic VT, and numerous appropriate ICD discharges, waiting for symptoms and hoping the initial symptom is not a fatal one can be unnerving to the family. Unlike disorders such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or inherited arrhythmia syndromes,13 for which there are multiple registries to provide guidance, there are insufficient data to address the risks in an orphan disease such as NKX2-5 mutations. Thus, even more so than other settings, a decision regarding ICD implantation for primary prevention demands shared decision making. Finally, although anecdotal, the apparent suppression of arrhythmias with mexiletine in both the child and mother suggests another potential intervention in this disorder, short of an ICD or as augmentation in the case of recurrent appropriate ICD discharges.

Conclusion

This report establishes life-threatening polymorphic VT/VF in a patient with a novel NKX2-5 mutation as the cause of numerous aborted SCD events. The occurrence of such an arrhythmia could explain SCD reported in patients with functioning pacemakers. Furthermore, assessing the risk of VT should be independent of the degree of AVCD, as the mother in this report exhibited a life-threatening arrhythmia in the face of relatively mild AV conduction impairment. It is an autosomal dominant disorder in which VT may be monomorphic and stable, as in the proband, or polymorphic and unstable, as in his mother. Whether the former is a harbinger of the latter remains an unanswered question that would require long-term monitoring of patients with NKX2-5 mutations. Determining whether monomorphic VT evolves into polymorphic VT/VF, triggers VT/VF, or remains hemodynamically stable would be well-suited for a multicenter study.
