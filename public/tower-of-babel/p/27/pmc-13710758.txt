Predicting response to neoadjuvant therapy using artificial intelligence on digitized histopathology slides: a systematic review
Authors: Soogyeong Shin, Koen Kwakkenbos, Denise E Hilling, Andrew P Stubbs, Jifke F Veenland, Michail Doukas, Cornelis Verhoef, Carolien H M van Deurzen, Jan H von der Thüsen, Farhan Akram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoadjuvant therapy (NAT) is an important treatment strategy in surgical oncology, but not all patients benefit equally from it. This systematic review is the first to evaluate artificial intelligence (AI) models predicting NAT response from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained biopsies slides of solid tumors. A systematic search across five databases was performed following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and study quality was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2). Out of 235 studies, 25 met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed regarding their AI methodologies, data modalities, and type of NAT. Most studies reported area under the curve (AUC) ranging from 0.70 to 0.90, and approximately 40% included external validation cohorts. In conclusion, AI models show promise in predicting NAT response from pathological slides, but future work should emphasize standardized data acquisition, patient-level validation, data transparency, and code sharing.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) is a treatment given before surgery. NAT usually includes systemic therapy such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, hormone therapy, and/or radiation therapy. The use of NAT is twofold: it can be used to assess the response to systemic therapy, and it can effectively reduce surgical difficulty and morbidity by avoiding more extensive surgical procedures. A good response to NAT may result in a pathological complete response (pCR), i.e., the absence of viable invasive tumor cells, as assessed in the surgical resection specimen1. Studies have shown that pCR is associated with improved event-free survival and recurrence-free survival2,3. Given the success of NAT in achieving pCR, NAT has increasingly been extended to earlier-stage tumors in hopes of enabling organ-preserving strategies4. However, not all patients will have a good response to NAT, potentially experiencing toxicity without any clinical benefits. In extreme cases, patients might react so severely that further treatments, especially surgery, are precluded, and the patient is rendered unfit for anesthesia5. Therefore, a growing body of literature has focused on predicting treatment response to spare non-responders from ineffective NAT. Traditionally, such predictions relied on human interpretation, yet diagnostic accuracy demonstrates substantial variability, ranging from 40% to 80% depending on reader expertise6,7. This variability highlights an existing challenge, as even experienced diagnostic experts encounter limitations stemming from the complexity of image interpretation and differences in individual training backgrounds.

To overcome this challenge, artificial intelligence (AI) has been recently introduced as a promising approach to predict NAT response8,9. AI models have shown the potential to enhance prediction accuracy and reduce inter-observer variability10,11. While radiology has been the most explored field, showing meaningful results in NAT response prediction, the performance of these models in terms of sensitivity and specificity remains limited12.

In contrast to the macro-level insights offered by radiological imaging, capturing macroscopic and functional tumor characteristics, histopathology provides detailed information on tissue and at the cellular level, including nuclear atypia, tumor architectural pattern, stromal composition, and immune infiltrates. These features are often invisible in radiological images but are closely associated with treatment response.

Recent studies have shown how AI-driven analysis of histopathology can enhance treatment decisions beyond what is achievable with radiological features alone13–15. Skrede et al.14 demonstrated that a deep learning (DL)-based biomarker using H&E slides can predict cancer-specific survival in colorectal cancer. Kather et al.13 showed that machine learning (ML) on histopathology has the potential to improve outcome prediction by extracting patterns reflective of molecular characteristics. Their findings show how histopathological analysis can capture tumor heterogeneity and enhance the predictive power of existing models.

Some studies have further integrated pathological and radiological features using multimodal AI models, reporting more stable and robust performances16–18. With the complementary nature of both modalities, multimodal approaches can capture complex tumor characteristics across multiple spatial resolutions and enhance prediction performance, such as sensitivity and specificity19. This highlights the potential of pathology-driven AI, particularly when combined with other imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Despite these developments, to our knowledge, no systematic review has yet focused on AI-based NAT response prediction using digitized pathology slides on biopsy material of solid tumors. This represents a notable gap in the literature, particularly given the expanding role of AI in clinical decision-making and the established importance of histopathological features in treatment response assessment.

Therefore, in this review, we aim to evaluate the existing literature on AI-based NAT response prediction using pathological images. An overview of the systematic review framework is provided in Fig. 1. We also compare predictive performance across AI model approaches, assess methodological rigor regarding validation practices, and reproducibility. Finally, we examine the predictive biomarkers reported from a pathological perspective and discuss the main limitations and future directions of existing studies to extract clinically meaningful insights and opportunities for future research.

Results

Study selection

The PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2) provides a graphical representation of the systematic search conducted in this study. After the literature search, a total of 246 studies were included, of which 11 duplicates were removed. The remaining 235 studies were independently assessed by two reviewers (SS and KK) based on title and abstract, and 205 studies were excluded after reaching an agreement. The full text could not be retrieved for 2 papers, leaving 30 studies for full-text analysis. Finally, 5 studies were excluded, leaving a final set of 25 studies in the final analysis.

PRISMA flow diagram.

Each branch shows the number of studies excluded and the corresponding reasons.

Study characteristics

23 out of 25 studies were retrospective, and only two studies did a prospective validation17,20. Table 1 provides a comprehensive summary of the study characteristics and methods used. Most studies (n = 15) originated from China, with the remaining studies originating from the United States, Canada, Germany, the Netherlands and Italy. The studies, published between 2021 to 2025, encompassed five cancer types: the majority focused on breast cancer (n = 15), followed by rectal cancer (n = 5), gastric cancer (n = 2), esophageal cancer (n = 2), and bladder cancer (n = 1). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was the most common treatment in studies on breast, gastric and bladder cancer. Chemoradiotherapy was used as a neoadjuvant modality in rectal cancer studies, whereas studies on esophageal cancer used either chemotherapy or chemo-immunotherapy. The total number of patients across the included studies was 11,132, of which 8031 were included in 16 studies that conducted only internal validation, while the remaining nine studies (3101 patients) conducted external validation. Eleven studies employed end-to-end DL approaches for prediction, while fourteen utilized ML approaches. Of these, eight studies combined DL for feature extraction with ML as a classifier. The most used classifiers were support vector machines (SVM) and residual networks (ResNet). Six studies integrated H&E slides with radiological imaging modalities, including CT, MRI, or ultrasound, to improve pCR prediction. Ten studies applied patient-level prediction, while another ten employed patch-level approaches. Saednia et al.21 utilized a multi-level prediction strategy, and Wu et al.22 conducted an analysis comparing patch-wise and slide-wise prediction levels. Three studies did not clearly specify the level of prediction23–25. Additional details are provided in Supplementary S3. Interpretability techniques were used in twelve studies, with gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM)26 and Shapley (SHAP) values27 being the most commonly used. Out of 25 studies, eight studies provided the developed code, and two28,29 disclosed both their code and data. Code and data availability for each study are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

While all studies predicted therapy response, not all studies used the same definition. Most studies used pCR as an outcome. In 15 out of 25 studies, this was defined as the absence of the invasive component of both the primary tumor and the lymph nodes. This definition was most prevalent in the studies concerning breast cancer. Wang et al.24 defined response in rectal cancer as a tumor regression grade (TRG) of either 0 or 1. Gao et al.20 used TRG0-2 as the response group, and Zhou et al.30 used TRG1a/b as responders, both in gastric cancer. Finally, Hörst et al.31 defined a therapy response in esophageal cancer as a decrease in maximum standard uptake value (SUV max) of at least 35% on PET/CT. To investigate the impact of this heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analysis evaluating performance across various response definitions (Supplementary S6). The results showed that the mean AUC remained relatively stable (0.839 for the ypT0-based vs. 0.814 for the TRG-based groups). However, the wide variance in results suggests that differences in response definitions complicate performance comparisons between studies. Notably, less stringent definitions showed higher AUCs (e.g., 0.723 for TRG 0 vs. 0.876 for TRG 0–2). Figure 3 shows the distribution of good responder rates in the studies included. The rate of good responders ranged from 18.7% to 67.2%, based on 24 studies; the study of Wang et al.24 was excluded due to missing data. About half of the studies reported positive responder rates between 20 and 30%, while only a few studies exceeded 60%. Good responders were generally the minority class. Only in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a pCR rate of around 50% was observed32–35.

Interpretability and identified biomarkers

A subset of studies mentioned human-interpretable biomarkers, either through explicit incorporation in the models, or via post-hoc interpretability analyses (Table 2). The most frequently identified category of biomarkers was related to the anti-tumor immune response in the tumor microenvironment. Various metrics for tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were prominent, including stromal TILs (sTILs) and tumor TILs (tTILs). Several studies evaluated the relationship between the tumor and immune cells, defining measures such as the lymphocyte-to-tumor ratio and the inflamed tumor ratio. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and TILs were also highlighted in one study as predictive biomarkers28. Cellular proliferation was another commonly identified biomarker. For instance, Ki-67 was explicitly mentioned in multiple studies. In addition to Ki-67, the mitotic rate and the expression of Phospho-Histone H3 (pH3) as a mitosis marker were identified as predictive biomarkers.

Performance results

We created a forest plot36 to graphically summarize the included studies in the descriptive comparison of reported AUC values. Among the 25 included studies, only 14 provided 95% confidence intervals (CI) for their reported AUC values. The remaining 11 studies did not report CI values and were therefore excluded from the forest plot. Figure 4 presents the variation in predictive performance across studies and provides a visual estimate of the overall trend. As sensitivity and specificity depend on the cutoff point, only AUC values were plotted. Of the 14 included studies in the forest plot, eight studies employed patient-level prediction16,17,20,32,37–40, four employed patch-level prediction31,41–43, and the remaining two did not clearly report the prediction level23,25. Most of these CIs (8 out of 14) were reported in breast cancer studies. The narrowest confidence interval observed was 0.783–0.793 in the study by Li et al., 2022-138, while another study, Li et al., 2022-241 reported the widest CI at 0.560–0.880. External validation was performed in five of the eight breast cancer studies that reported CIs, yielding an average CI range of 0.71–0.92. The average range of studies concerning rectal cancer was between 0.72 and 0.90, with all studies undergoing external validation. Although both cancer types demonstrated average AUCs over 0.8, their CIs were notably wide, especially in studies employing external validation. This suggests that the predictive models have reduced certainty when applied to new, independent datasets. Nevertheless, the study by Li et al.42 was an exception, reporting a narrow CI of 0.863–0.916 in their external validation.

Forest plot of AUC values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) grouped by cancer type.

If external validation was performed, this CI is displayed; otherwise, the internal validation CI is displayed. The marker colors represent patient-level (blue), patch-level (green), and unclear (red) studies.

The two studies on esophageal carcinoma reported similar CI ranges of 0.81–0.8925,31. However, both studies did not perform external validation, and in particular, the study by Hörst et al.31 had the most widely distributed CI range, spanning 0.61–1.0. Generalizing the findings for gastric cancer is challenging due to the inclusion of only a single study by Gao et al.20, which reported an AUC of 0.88 with a CI of 0.82–0.93. Finally, three studies25,31,40 reported the highest CIs reaching the maximum possible CI upper bound of 1.0, but this result was observed only on internal validation sets.

Additionally, we explored the reported AI model performance across three methodological approaches (DL (n = 11), ML (n = 8), and a hybrid DL + ML approach (n = 3)), and examined the impact of the number of modalities used and the type of NAT. As three studies did not report AUC, they were excluded from this descriptive analysis. Given the clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in cancer type, response definition, validation strategy, sample size, and prediction level, these comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory. Kruskal-Wallis testing revealed no significant difference between AI methods (H = 0.054, p = 0.973; Fig. 5a), with DL, ML, and hybrid models achieving median AUCs of 0.873, 0.823, and 0.872, respectively. In contrast, multimodal approaches (n = 6) tended to report higher predictive performance than single-modal ones (n = 19), demonstrating a higher median AUC of 0.883 (range: 0.872–0.927) compared to 0.81 (range: 0.680–1.0). Welch’s t-test indicated that this observed difference was statistically significant (p = 0.007) with a substantial effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.01; Fig. 5b). However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of potential study-level confounding, because AUC values were derived from heterogeneous studies and were not weighted by sample size, validation strategy or study design. Finally, subgroup analysis by NAT type was conducted comparing chemotherapy (n = 19) and chemoradiotherapy (n = 4) via the Mann–Whitney U test; chemoimmunotherapy (n = 1) and mixed-protocol study (e.g., Wu et al.22) were excluded from statistical analysis. No significant difference in reported AUCs was observed between these NAT regimens (U = 46.0, p = 0.54; Fig. 5c).

Comparison of AUC distribution across the used AI methods, data modality, and NAT types.

The horizontal black lines indicate the median, and the red dashed lines indicate the mean values. a comparisons among different AI approaches (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.973); b comparison by the used modality (Welch’s t-test, p = 0.0076); c comparison by the NAT types (Mann–Whitney U = 46.0, p = 0.54).

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment is summarized in Fig. 6. The most likely source of bias in the studies included was related to concerns about algorithm applicability. This score was influenced by the lack of external validation in most of the studies. We generally found a low risk of bias and low applicability concerns for the domains of reference standard and flow and timing, as most papers clearly describe the outcome and pathological examination. Furthermore, most studies provide a good description of the standard patient diagnosis and treatment timeline. We deemed three studies to have a high risk of bias regarding patient selection. In the study by Bhattarai et al.34, there was a lack of reproducible inclusion and exclusion criteria. In Dodington et al.35, a subset of patients was excluded based on therapy response. Finally, in Wu et al.22, a mixed treatment cohort was included. The individual risk of bias analysis per study is displayed in Supplementary Table S5. Although QUADAS-2 is an established tool for assessing diagnostic accuracy studies, it was not specifically developed for AI-based prediction models. Therefore, AI-specific methodological concerns, including data leakage, overfitting, algorithmic bias, model updating, and transparency, may not be fully captured by this assessment. The risk-of-bias results should therefore be interpreted together with AI-specific reporting considerations, such as external validation, patient-level data splitting, and transparency of code and data availability.

Discussion

The ability to accurately predict NAT response is becoming increasingly important, particularly as NAT is now being offered not only to patients with locally advanced tumors, but also to those with early-stage tumors with the aim of organ preservation. Although NAT does not improve overall survival in early-stage disease, some patients wish to avoid surgery altogether. For such patients, NAT may be considered as an alternative to resection, but only if there is a high likelihood of achieving pCR. Thus, reliable prediction tools are essential to identify candidates who are most likely to benefit and to spare non-responders the risks of ineffective treatment and toxicity without benefits. In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of AI approaches using digitized H&E-stained histopathology slides for predicting response to NAT.

Most AI models showed good discriminative power in predicting the NAT response, with AUCs typically ranging from 0.70 to 0.90. However, there was considerable variability across studies, indicating that model performance varies depending on the data environment and experimental choices. We therefore descriptively explored reported performance across several dimensions, including the used modality, AI approaches, and types of NAT to ensure a multi-dimensional assessment of the current predictive capabilities. Regarding data modality, six studies adopted a multimodal approach by integrating radiomics data (MRI, CT, or ultrasound) with histopathology. All of these studies reported improved sensitivity and AUC compared to their single-modality counterparts, and more stable performance. This may suggest that combining microscopic imaging features from pathology with macroscopic imaging features may be promising to improve predictive performance by capturing a more comprehensive tumor phenotype. However, this observation may be influenced by the substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in cancer type, response definition, validation strategy, sample size, and prediction level. These comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. In terms of AI methodology, an exploratory comparison of AUC values across modeling approaches did not reveal statistically significant differences in performance, and no single approach was found to consistently outperform others in predicting pCR. DL models were more often used in conjunction with explanatory tools such as attention heatmaps, which enable clinicians to better understand the models. To capture the added value of each approach, there is a critical need for standardized datasets and evaluation protocols to facilitate direct and fair comparisons.

To further address treatment-related heterogeneity, we additionally conducted a subgroup analysis by NAT type. No statistically significant difference was observed between the chemotherapy and chemoradiotherapy groups. However, the predominance of chemotherapy cases (n = 19) compared to chemoradiotherapy (n = 4) and the exclusion of emerging therapies such as chemoimmunotherapy, due to small sample sizes, underscores the need for further validation in more balanced, multi-modal cohorts. Future studies focusing on monotherapy should incorporate more stringent criteria, such as standardized treatment duration and dosage. Such precise stratification will be essential for reducing confounding variables and enhancing the reliability of comparative analyses.

A major challenge in model development is data composition. Most studies have used datasets where good responder rates ranged from 20 to 30%, creating a class imbalance that can bias models towards predicting the majority class. Models must therefore be trained using appropriate class weights, but also validated using balanced accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, to ensure discrimination of both classes. Furthermore, external validation should be performed on datasets from multiple centers with different responder rates, to ensure that the performance is not over-optimized for a single institution’s demographic. Among the seven studies with good responder rates over 50%, only Gao et al. included a sufficiently large dataset, comprising 2519 samples20. The remaining six studies, in contrast, included only around 90 samples each. In datasets with small sample sizes, a model will in general, be more prone to overfitting, a process in which it finetunes on the noise present in the data. In larger datasets, with more training examples, the models will be able to effectively capture the real patterns present in the data through the noise. Therefore, when evaluating data composition in the future, both the balanced class distribution and the representative sample size must be considered.

Another challenge is consistency in defining good responders. While most breast cancer studies used the standard ypT0/isN0 definition, gastrointestinal and esophageal cancer studies sometimes used broader or less consistent criteria, such as TRG0–2 or TRG1a-b. These broader definitions categorize complete and near-complete responders together, which may inflate the reported good responder rate. Such variability hinders direct comparison across studies and increases the risk of introducing systematically incorrect labels that can misguide the model during training. Consistent with these results, we observed a trend where less stringent definitions (e.g., broader TRG scores) were associated with higher reported performance. Beyond definitional inconsistency, these endpoints reflect fundamentally different biological and clinical objectives. While ypT0/isN0 and ypT0 assess residual invasive disease at the tissue level, TRG-based classification captures the degree of tumor regression across a spectrum, and imaging-derived metrics such as PET SUV reduction reflect metabolic activity rather than histological response. Consequently, a model trained to predict one endpoint cannot be assumed to generalize to another, even within the same cancer type. For instance, a model optimized for ypT0/isN0 may be informative for surgical decision-making, whereas one predicting metabolic response may be better suited for early treatment monitoring. These findings indicate that clinicians should interpret AUCs in conjunction with the underlying response definition, rather than by comparing performance metrics in isolation.

Even within the studies that define response as pCR, the exact definition has important implications for the reliability and clinical applicability of these AI models in clinical practice. When pCR is defined solely based on the status of the primary tumor, the resulting model is primarily suited for use cases focused on local tumor control, such as predicting the necessity or extent of surgical intervention. In contrast, incorporating lymph node status into the pCR definition enables a more systemic assessment of treatment response, reflecting the likelihood of complete eradication of invasive disease. Models trained using such definitions may therefore be more informative for guiding decisions regarding both surgical management and the need for additional adjuvant systemic therapy. While both definitions can be clinically meaningful, transparent reporting of the underlying ground truth is essential to ensure appropriate interpretation of model predictions and to understand the specific clinical context in which an AI model can be reliably applied.

In addition, while breast cancer has been extensively studied, relatively fewer studies have focused on other tumor types, such as colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, esophageal cancer, and bladder cancer. For example, TILs have frequently been reported as a predictive indicator for therapy response in breast cancer, but their relevance in other cancer types has yet to be comprehensively validated44. The apparent heterogeneity in predictive value for therapy response across various cancer types underscores the need for standardized validation of this biomarker across all solid tumors. Validating frequently used features such as TILs in less-studied cancers is crucial to ensure their relevance beyond breast cancer and improve clinical utility. AI and ML approaches can be used to objectively extract this biomarker from digitized pathological images. While other biomarkers, such as tumor microenvironment, Ki-67 expression, and PD-L1 expression were identified, universal biomarkers remain elusive. The lack of clear and interpretable biomarkers could hinder the integration of AI-based solutions in clinical practice.

High predictive accuracy does not necessarily translate into clinical applicability. Several factors undermine the reported performance. First, only 40% of studies used patient-wise analysis, while the remaining 60% relied on patch-, or slide-wise, or unclear training schemes that risked data leakage and produced artificially inflated metrics. This concern is reflected in the forest plot data among breast cancer studies, those reporting patch-level AUCs without external validation, such as Li et al. 2021 (AUC 0.89)42, tended to report higher values than patient-level studies with external validation, such as Aswolinskiy et al. (AUC 0.71)37 and Li et al. 2022-1 (AUC 0.78)38. However, this pattern was not consistent across cancer types, suggesting that dataset size and external validation may exert a stronger influence on reported AUC than prediction level alone. Second, only 12 out of 25 studies used interpretability methods such as Grad-Cam or SHAP values. This highlights a persistent gap between the model’s technical development and the interpretability of its features, hampering its introduction in clinical settings. Third, external validation was performed in only 10 of 25 studies. However, among the 6 studies reporting outstanding performance (AUC ≥ 0.9), only one study performed external validation. In fact, Zhou et al.30 performed external validation with only 31 samples, which is insufficient for a reliable generalization assessment. On the other hand, the study by Gao et al. used an external dataset of 1,179 patient-wise samples and clearly reported the confidence interval, thereby presenting relatively more reliable results20. The last factor to consider when assessing model performance is the reporting of CI values. While external validation is important, it alone does not guarantee that the model’s performance is reliable. Notably, only 56% of studies reported CI values for model performance. Additionally, the studies by Hörst et al. and Qi et al. reported the highest CI of 1.0 on their internal validation, but a clinically implausible AUC of 1.0 on their validation data24,31. These wide confidence intervals can be largely attributed to the small sample sizes (n = 104 and n = 80, respectively), the absence of external validation, and a pronounced class imbalance between good responders and non-responders. Such conditions increase the risk of overfitting, whereby models capture noise rather than generalizable patterns. Moreover, small and imbalanced datasets are inherently prone to high variance across validation splits, which can manifest as unstable performance estimates and wide confidence intervals. Therefore, a high AUC should be interpreted with caution and should ideally be accompanied by a narrow confidence interval and robust external validation before a model can be considered for clinical implementation. This lack of external validation was also the most prevalent source of bias in our risk of bias analysis.

Of the 25 studies analyzed, only three disclosed data for follow-up studies, indicating that continued validation and extension studies in clinical settings may be limited. The experimental code was provided in only eight studies (32%), but only two studies disclosed both code and data. However, one of these studies only provided processed data without the original images, which prevents reproduction of the crucial preprocessing steps. To enhance transparency and reproducibility, the disclosure of data and code should be actively encouraged, for example, through the adoption of the FAIR (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability) data principles45, alignment with journal publication policies, and the use of public repositories. Transparent reporting of input data characteristics is essential, as variations in the use of archived slides, staining protocols, type of scanner, sample age, and biopsy cutting techniques can influence the quality of digitized histopathology slides. In particular, tissue section thickness (TST) affects visual and computational analyses, impacting biomarker identification and prediction accuracy. Shan et al. found that thinner sections produce brighter images with lower contrast, whereas thicker sections reduce nuclear texture complexity46. None of the 25 studies reviewed explicitly reported this information. This omission undermines reproducibility, likely contributes to inconsistent model performance across different datasets.

To our knowledge, there are currently no standardized reporting guidelines specifically developed for AI-based models in computational pathology to address this gap. However, existing frameworks such as TRIPOD47, STARD-AI48, and REMARK49 may provide valuable guidance in addressing key pre-analytical variables and enhancing transparency and rigor in computational pathology research. In fact, when evaluated against the reporting standards of the TRIPOD-AI checklist, the included literature shows significant gaps in clinical readiness. Specifically, we found widespread non-compliance regarding sample size and validation, open science, and performance estimates with associated confidence intervals. These deficiencies in reporting rigor and transparency indicate that while these AI models show promise, the level of evidence is not yet sufficient for clinical implementation.

Building on the limitations identified previously, we propose key guidelines for future research (Table 3). Firstly, prospective and multi-center validation studies with standardized data acquisition (e.g., staining protocols, scanner settings, and section thickness) and clearly defined response criteria, such as uniform pCR definition, are essential. Such standardization will improve the comparability and reliability of model performance across studies. Secondly, rigorous patient-level validation and external evaluation methods are necessary to prevent data leakage and ensure model generalizability. Thirdly, we advocate for increased transparency through open access to data and code following FAIR data principles45, and adherence to regulatory frameworks such as TRIPOD47, which will improve reproducibility and facilitate clinical adoption. Lastly, we encourage future research to integrate multimodal AI approaches, such as combining histopathological imaging with radiomics features to capture tumor heterogeneity and improve prediction accuracy. We also foresee a role for further integration of radiology, histopathology, and other multi-omic modalities (such as genomics or proteomics). By giving the AI models access to more biologically detailed and precise subtyping information, predictions of therapy response can be made with more confidence. Beyond multimodal integration, future researchers may also consider incorporating pathology foundation models such as CONCH50 and UNI51, which are pretrained on large-scale pathology datasets. Given the limited labeled datasets and pronounced class imbalance observed across the included studies, such models may offer a practical means of extracting robust feature representations without relying on large annotation cohorts. In summary, addressing these morphological and practical gaps is critical for developing robust and trustworthy AI models that can be successfully integrated into the clinical workflow.

This systematic review is, to our knowledge, the first to specifically focus on predicting NAT response using AI and digital histopathology slides. The included studies demonstrate promising predictive performance, with multimodal approaches tending to report higher AUCs in exploratory comparisons. This highlights the potential of pathology-based AI in informing treatment decisions. However, the field is hindered by inconsistent validation practices, heterogeneous reporting standards, and a lack of reproducibility. Our explorative analysis revealed no significant differences in predictive performance between the ML, DL, and hybrid approaches, indicating that no single methodology currently outperforms the others. This suggests that the optimal choice may be context-dependent, highlighting the need for future research into diverse modelling strategies adapted to specific clinical settings and data types. Beyond methodological refinement, future research must prioritize clinically meaningful model development by emphasizing patient-level validation, model interpretability, and transparent sharing of data and code. Addressing these gaps will be critical for the safe and effective integration of these AI tools into routine oncology practice.

Methods

Literature review

This review was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA)52,53. Both a clinical expert (JHVDT) and a technical expert (FA) were consulted during the search and review process to ensure that the information presented in the studies was analyzed accurately and evaluated critically. The Medline, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane, and Google Scholar databases were systematically searched from inception until 27-01-2025. This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO under registration ID CRD420251075563.

Study eligibility and selection

We employed the following terms in the search: ‘artificial intelligence’, ‘neoadjuvant treatment’ and ‘hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides’ for study inclusion, along with any terms relevant or analogous to them. Detailed search queries for each library and search results are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. To include all relevant studies that predicted neoadjuvant treatment response using AI on H&E-stained slides, we applied six exclusion criteria:

After removal of duplicates, two reviewers (SS, KK) independently assessed the titles and abstracts of the studies for relevance. The full texts of potentially eligible papers were obtained and independently reviewed to determine eligibility based on predefined selection criteria. If both reviewers agreed, a paper was selected; in case of disagreement, the article was discussed with a third reviewer (FA) to decide on inclusion.

Data extraction

Data extraction from eligible studies was performed by two reviewers (SS, KK). The researchers worked independently to extract the study details. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (FA). The extracted information included the source and type of image data, cancer type, type of neoadjuvant therapy, number of patients and slides, magnification used, prediction level, and diagnostic performance metrics of the developed algorithms, such as area under the curve (AUC), F1-score, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Diagnostic performance metrics were extracted at both the per-image and per-patient levels, when available. For clinical features, information on patient and tumor characteristics such as age, sex, survival rate (if available), disease subtype, definition of a good responder, good responder rate, and explicitly identified biomarkers was extracted. To assess reproducibility, data and code availability were recorded. Finally, we extracted the used interpretability methods and whether external validation was performed. When multiple algorithms were developed with different outcomes, we collected data regarding the best-performing algorithm.

Risk of bias

We used the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) risk of bias tool to analyze the quality of the included papers. This tool consists of four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. The developed algorithm or pipeline was considered the index test, while the ground truth or clinical outcome served as the reference standard. We further assessed the clarity and consistency of inclusion and exclusion criteria and the use of external validation to evaluate the generalizability of the proposed approaches.
