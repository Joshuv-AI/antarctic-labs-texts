Septic shock with multiple organ dysfunction in a neonate following Campylobacter jejuni infection: a case report and clinical insights
Authors: Dongqin Huang, Weihong Chen, Weibin Hu, Weize Cai, Jianfa Chen, Yuchao Su, Yanling Ji, Jianhui Chen, Hongru Guo, Yueyun Lin, Jianjia Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal septic shock is a life-threatening emergency with high mortality. Campylobacter jejuni is a rare but potentially severe pathogen in neonates.Case presentationWe report a 24-day-old female neonate admitted in July 2025 with fever, diarrhea, and rapid progression to septic shock with multiple organ dysfunction. Blood culture confirmed Campylobacter Jejuni infection. The infant received comprehensive treatment including respiratory support, fluid resuscitation, vasoactive drugs, and targeted antibiotics (meropenem plus azithromycin). She fully recovered and was discharged after 24 days. Early recognition, prompt intervention, and multidisciplinary management are crucial in neonatal septic shock. Campylobacter Jejuni should be considered in septic neonates with diarrhea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06802-w.

Introduction

Neonatal septic shock is a life-threatening emergency in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). It arises from pathogen invasion that triggers a systemic inflammatory response and a dysregulated host immune reaction, culminating in microcirculatory dysfunction, cellular metabolic derangements, and multiple organ failure. Reported mortality remains high (≈ 20%–50%), posing a substantial threat to neonatal survival and long-term health [1, 2]. Compared with older children and adults, neonates have immature innate and adaptive immunity, fragile skin and mucosal barriers, and unstable intestinal microbial colonization, all of which lower resistance to infection. They are particularly vulnerable to gram-negative pathogens (e.g., Escherichia coli, Salmonella Typhi). Disease progression is often rapid, and early manifestations are subtle and nonspecific, which increases the risk of misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis and may forfeit the optimal therapeutic window [3, 4]. In fact, Campylobacter jejuni is a leading cause of bacterial gastroenteritis worldwide, yet neonatal systemic infection is exceedingly uncommon [5]. A review of the literature reveals only rare reports of neonatal Campylobacter jejuni sepsis over the past two decades, with most presenting as bacteremia without shock [6]. To our knowledge, this is one of the few documented cases of Campylobacter jejuni–associated septic shock with multiorgan dysfunction in a neonate, highlighting a critical knowledge gap and the need for heightened awareness.

Early recognition of neonatal septic shock is challenging. Due to robust compensatory mechanisms, hypotension may be absent initially; instead, nonspecific signs such as tachycardia, tachypnea, and decreased responsiveness predominate and can be mistaken for uncomplicated infection. Notably, approximately 60% of neonates with septic shock present in a progressive stage on admission, frequently with evolving multiple organ dysfunction. Heightened vigilance for early warning signs, coupled with dynamic monitoring of infection biomarkers, microcirculation, and metabolic status, is therefore critical to improving outcomes [7–9].

The present case involves a 24-day-old neonate who presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and rapidly progressed to septic shock, complicated by sepsis, toxic encephalopathy, and multiple organ dysfunction. By delineating the diagnostic reasoning, antimicrobial strategy optimization, hemodynamic management, and multi-organ support employed in this patient, this report aims to distill practical points for early identification and comprehensive management of neonatal septic shock, thereby providing a useful reference for similar clinical scenarios.

Case report

A 24-day-old female neonate, the first child born to a 29-year-old mother (gravida 1, para 1), was delivered by cesarean section at 39 + 0 weeks; birth weight 3.15 kg; Apgar scores were 10 at 1 and 5 min. She was exclusively breast-fed with normal postnatal growth and no known allergies. Two days before admission, she developed fever (peak 39.6 °C) and > 10 episodes/day of yellow, “egg-drop soup–like” diarrhea. She was treated at a local clinic with ibuprofen (5 mg/kg twice daily), Babao Jingfeng Powder (a traditional Chinese medicine containing ingredients such as Gastrodia elata, Uncaria rhynchophylla, and Scorpio, commonly used for febrile seizures in infants), live combined Bacillus subtilis and Enterococcus faecium (probiotics), and montmorillonite powder (an adsorbent for diarrhea). Fever subsided and diarrhea improved slightly. About 12 h pre-admission, decreased responsiveness, dyspnea, restlessness with groaning, and staring spells appeared, without foaming at the mouth, shrill cry, convulsions, or loss of consciousness. She presented to Anxi County Hospital and was admitted on July 19, 2025 with provisional “neonatal shock.”

Initial examination (July 19, 2025)

T 36.0 °C; Heart rate(HR) 165/min; respiratory rate(RR) 80/min; oxygen saturation(SpO2) 65% on room air; weight 3.26 kg; capillary refill 3.5 s. She was lethargic, poorly responsive, and weak-crying; skin dry and pale. The anterior fontanelle was sunken. Pupils were dilated with sluggish light reflexes. Nasal flaring with suprasternal/intercostal/subcostal retractions and circumoral cyanosis indicated respiratory distress; breath sounds were coarse with wheeze bilaterally. Cardiac rhythm was regular without murmurs. Abdomen was soft/flat; liver and spleen were palpable below the costal margins; bowel sounds 4/min. Extremities were cool with nail-bed cyanosis; limb tone increased; primitive reflexes were absent, suggesting neurologic involvement.

Family and social context

The infant lived with her parents and a pet dog in a rural area. There was no recent travel or sick contacts. The mother had no history of infection during pregnancy. The household had no known environmental risk factors.

Laboratory data

Point-of-care glucose (July 19, 2025) 6.1 mmol/L. Arterial blood gas/electrolytes (July 19; Fraction of inspired oxygen(FiO2) 0.40): pH 6.831, partial pressure of carbon dioxide(PaCO2) 13.6 mmHg, partial pressure of oxygen(PaO2) 100 mmHg, K+ 6.6 mmol/L, Na+ 126 mmol/L, Ca2+ 1.47 mmol/L, lactate 3.6 mmol/L, base excess − 30.9 mmol/L—severe metabolic acidosis with hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, and tissue hypoxia. Inflammatory markers (July 19, 2025): C-reactive protein (CRP) 269.42 mg/L, procalcitonin (PCT) > 50.00 ng/mL, interleukin-6 (IL-6) 636.3 pg/mL—consistent with severe systemic infection. Complete blood count (CBC) on July 19 showed white blood cell (WBC) 5.84 × 109/L, neutrophils 35.4%, lymphocytes 52.1%, hemoglobin 141.0 g/L, platelets 605 × 109/L. CBC (July 20): WBC fell to 1.59 × 109/L, indicating leukocyte consumption in severe sepsis. Biochemistry (July 19, 2025): aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 125 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 60 U/L, creatinine 246 µmol/L, urea 25.8 mmol/L, creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) 134.5 U/L—hepatic, renal, and myocardial dysfunction. Stool (July 20, 2025): occult blood positive; 2–7 WBCs/HPF—suggestive of intestinal mucosal injury.

Microbiology

Blood culture (reported July 22, 2025; Fig. 1A–B): after 2 days of anaerobic subculture, Gram-negative curved bacilli were identified as Campylobacter jejuni by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight(MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, confirming the pathogen. While Campylobacter Jejuni typically causes gastroenteritis in older children and adults, neonatal sepsis is rare; this identification guided targeted therapy.

Imaging and ancillary examinations

Chest radiograph (July 19, 2025): increased bilateral lung markings and post-intubation changes, no focal consolidation. Echocardiography (July 19, 2025): minimal pericardial effusion, no chamber enlargement, preserved global left-ventricular systolic function—compatible with myocardial stress (in the setting of elevated CK-MB) without overt systolic dysfunction. Lumbar puncture (July 20, 2025): clear/colorless cerebrospinal fluid (CSF); nucleated cells 1 × 108/L, glucose 6.9 mmol/L, chloride 132.0 mmol/L—no evidence of purulent meningitis. Amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG) (July 24, 2025): normal background, no electrographic seizures—no severe irreversible brain injury.

Working diagnoses and differential

Final diagnoses: neonatal septic shock; neonatal sepsis; toxic encephalopathy; neonatal diarrhea; dehydration; pneumonia; stress ulcer; multiple organ dysfunction; electrolyte disturbances. Differential diagnoses considered and excluded: neonatal purulent meningitis, airway foreign body, necrotizing enterocolitis, and intracranial hemorrhage (based on imaging, CSF, and clinical course).

Treatment and outcomes

Initial resuscitation (first 24 h)

Immediate endotracheal intubation and invasive ventilation (synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation(SIMV): FiO2 0.60, peak inspiratory pressure(PIP) 21 cmH2O, positive end-expiratory pressure(PEEP) 5 cmH2O, RR 55/min) with SpO2 target 90–95% and dynamic adjustments. Two 10 mL/kg boluses of 0.9% saline (total 32 mL) for hypovolemia. Continuous dopamine and dobutamine infusions at 5 µg/kg/min to support microcirculation and inotropy. Metabolic acidosis was treated with intravenous(IV) 5% sodium bicarbonate 13 mL + 5% glucose 13 mL, then an additional 18 mL sodium bicarbonate (pH 6.831 → 7.087); a further 5 mL 5% sodium bicarbonate in glucose increased pH to 7.348. Hyperkalemia was addressed through acidosis correction and avoidance of potassium-containing fluids (K+ 6.6 → 5.5 mmol/L). Hyponatremia was corrected with 10% NaCl 6–9 mL/day (Na+ 126 → 134 mmol/L).

Antimicrobial therapy

Empiric piperacillin–tazobactam 0.24 g q12h plus meropenem 0.13 g q12h began July 19. After Campylobacter Jejuni identification on July 22, therapy was adjusted to meropenem + azithromycin; azithromycin was discontinued July 31, and meropenem completed a 14-day course. Complication management and organ support

(i) Stress-ulcer prophylaxis/management: on July 19, dark-red gastric mucus was drained via decompression; hemocoagulase 0.5 kU for hemostasis and omeprazole 2.5 mg for acid suppression; continued decompression during fasting. After switching to extensively hydrolyzed formula on July 23, no gross bloody stools occurred. (ii) Hepatic dysfunction: hepatotoxic drugs were avoided; parenteral nutrition (pediatric amino acids and lipid emulsion) provided; AST improved 125 → 23 U/L. (iii) Renal dysfunction: fluids restricted to 60–80 mL/kg/day with urine output maintained at 1–2 mL/kg/h; creatinine improved 246 → 8 µmol/L. (iv) Anemia and nutrition: July 25 hemoglobin 76 g/L → oral iron protein succinate; July 29, 2025 hemoglobin 70 g/L → transfusion of 70 mL type-A Rh-positive leukocyte-depleted packed red blood cells(RBCs); post-transfusion hemoglobin 125 g/L. Nil per os(NPO) for the first 3 days; from July 23, 2025, extensively hydrolyzed formula started at 20 mL q3h and advanced to 60 mL q3h without emesis or distension.

Monitoring and adjustment

Daily CRP/PCT/IL-6 showed control of inflammation (CRP 269.42 mg/L on July 19, 2025 → 43.3 mg/L on July 25, 2025; PCT 0.69 ng/mL on July 28, 2025). Blood gases/electrolytes were reassessed every 4–6 h with ventilator and fluid adjustments; by July 21, 2025 pH 7.391 and lactate 1.6 mmol/L. Continuous monitoring documented HR improvement 165 → ~120/min, RR 80 → ~45/min, and stable BP ~ 95/57 mmHg.

Outcome and follow-up

Hospitalization lasted 24 days

At discharge, she was afebrile; weight 3.81 kg; feeds 75 mL q3h; alert with present primitive reflexes; no apparent sequelae. Pre-discharge labs: CRP 5.04 mg/L; hemoglobin 92 g/L; liver/renal function and electrolytes normal. Echocardiography (July 22, 2025) showed resolution of pericardial effusion; abdominal radiograph (July 21, 2025) showed improved bowel gas. Planned follow-up included CBC, hepatic/renal function, and neurodevelopmental assessment at 3 days and 1 month post-discharge; at 1 month, growth and development were normal with no abnormalities.

Discussion

Early recognition is pivotal to reducing mortality in neonatal septic shock [10], yet nonspecific prodromal symptoms frequently delay diagnosis. In this case, the infant initially manifested only fever and diarrhea and was treated as routine enteritis; shock was considered only when decreased responsiveness, dyspnea, and groaning emerged—illustrating the diagnostic blind spot at presentation. Clinically, neonatal septic shock often features a prolonged compensatory phase followed by an abrupt decompensatory phase: during compensation, blood pressure may remain normal despite tachycardia (> 160 beats/min) and tachypnea (> 60 breaths/min), so relying on hypotension alone risks missing the intervention window. Once decompensation occurs, hypotension and rapid evolution to multiple organ dysfunction increase therapeutic complexity.

Targeted bedside monitoring helps bridge this gap. In our patient, capillary refill time (CRT) 3.5 s and lactate 3.6 mmol/L were early indicators of circulatory insufficiency. Reported evidence suggests that combined abnormalities—e.g., lactate > 2 mmol/L with CRT > 3 s—provide high diagnostic sensitivity for neonatal shock, and lactate clearance is useful for gauging response to therapy [10]. Markedly abnormal inflammatory indices also carry early-warning value: CRP 269.42 mg/L and PCT > 50 ng/mL in this case supported prompt initiation of antimicrobial therapy. Attention to atypical presentations is essential: Older neonates may present with lethargy, poor feeding, or temperature instability, whereas preterm infants often exhibit hypothermia and poor perfusion. Neurologic signs here—staring and sluggish pupils—signaled central nervous system involvement. Integrating history, examination, and serial objective measures is necessary to avoid anchoring on a single symptom complex [11].

The infant received ibuprofen, Babao Jingfeng Powder, probiotics, and montmorillonite prior to admission. Ibuprofen, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, may mask fever and blunt the inflammatory response, potentially delaying recognition of sepsis [12]. Although no direct evidence links these agents to the progression to shock, their use in the context of evolving infection warrants caution. Babao Jingfeng Powder is a traditional Chinese remedy containing multiple herbal and animal-derived ingredients. While commonly used for febrile illnesses in infants, its safety and efficacy in neonatal sepsis are unstudied, and it may introduce unknown risks. This case underscores the importance of obtaining a detailed medication history and considering potential contributions from over-the-counter or traditional remedies.

Campylobacter jejuni infection manifests across a wide clinical spectrum, from self-limited gastroenteritis to severe invasive disease. In the pediatric population, extraintestinal infections are rare but increasingly recognized. For instance, Campylobacter jejuni has been reported to cause subdural hygroma infection in a 2-year-old boy following head trauma, which resolved with meropenem and surgical intervention [13]. In another report, a 5-year-old immunocompetent child presented with concurrent Campylobacter jejuni bacteremia and intussusception, highlighting the pathogen’s potential to induce both systemic and gastrointestinal complications [14]. A case series from India described 12 patients with Campylobacter jejuni bacteremia, most of whom were adults with chronic liver disease; notably, only two had diarrhea, and the in-hospital mortality was attributed to subsequent sepsis rather than the initial Campylobacter infection [15]. In neonates, however, Campylobacter jejuni sepsis remains exceptionally rare, and progression to septic shock with multiorgan dysfunction is even less common. A review of the literature reveals only rare reports of neonatal Campylobacter jejuni sepsis, with most presenting as uncomplicated bacteremia. The present case is unique in its rapid deterioration to shock with involvement of the brain, heart, liver, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract, followed by complete recovery after aggressive intensive care and targeted antimicrobial therapy. This underscores the importance of early recognition, tailored antibiotics (meropenem plus azithromycin), and multidisciplinary organ support in achieving favorable outcomes even in the most severe presentations.

Principles of antimicrobial management in neonatal septic shock prioritize early broad-spectrum coverage followed by microbiology-guided de-escalation [16]. The initial combination of piperacillin–tazobactam plus meropenem in this case was chosen to rapidly cover likely pathogens—including Gram-negative organisms, Gram-positive cocci, and anaerobes—consistent with empirical therapy for severe neonatal sepsis where drug-resistant organisms are a concern. Blood culture results remain the cornerstone for refining therapy [17]. After Campylobacter jejuni was identified, treatment was tailored to meropenem plus azithromycin, consistent with known susceptibilities of Campylobacter to carbapenems and macrolides and the avoidance of fluoroquinolones in neonates due to cartilage toxicity. Although Campylobacter Jejuni is an uncommon cause of neonatal sepsis, its management underscores the value of culture-directed precision therapy and timely de-escalation once the patient stabilizes.

From a pathobiology standpoint, Campylobacter Jejuni is a motile, spiral Gram-negative organism capable of penetrating intestinal epithelium and inducing mucosal inflammation [18]. Endotoxin-mediated signaling (e.g., via Toll-like receptors) amplifies cytokine release and can precipitate a systemic inflammatory response [19]. Clinically, neonatal infections may begin with gastrointestinal-limited symptoms (fever, diarrhea) that are difficult to distinguish from benign enteritis, while toxin-mediated systemic manifestations may lag behind local signs—explaining rapid clinical deterioration once decompensation ensues [20].

Multiple organ dysfunction is the leading cause of death in neonatal septic shock and requires coordinated, organ-protective strategies alongside hemodynamic and respiratory stabilization [21]. In this infant, brain, intestinal, hepatic, renal, and myocardial involvement necessitated integrated care. Neurologic protection centered on maintaining cerebral perfusion (volume resuscitation and vasoactive support), correcting severe metabolic acidosis (pH improved from 6.831 to 7.348), and aEEG monitoring, which remained normal and supported a favorable neurologic prognosis. Intestinal management balanced “rest and repair”: fasting and gastric decompression reduced mucosal stress; omeprazole and hemostatic therapy (hemocoagulase) addressed stress-related bleeding risk; and, once stable, early enteral nutrition was re-introduced using extensively hydrolyzed formula with gradual advancement, promoting mucosal recovery and barrier function.

Hepatic, renal, and cardiac support was individualized: hepatotoxic agents were avoided and parenteral nutrition provided, with AST decreasing from 125 to 23 U/L; judicious fluid management (60–80 mL/kg/day) preserved renal perfusion with creatinine falling from 246 to 8 µmol/L without renal replacement therapy; metabolic support for myocardial stress was provided while avoiding increased oxygen demand, with CK-MB improving from 134.5 to 35.4 U/L. These changes paralleled declining inflammatory markers and improved gas exchange, reflecting effective source control and organ support.

Campylobacter jejuni infection is associated with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations beyond gastroenteritis, including rare but severe complications. The pathogen is well-recognized as a trigger for post-infectious autoimmune phenomena, most notably Miller Fisher syndrome, a variant of Guillain-Barré syndrome, with Campylobacter jejuni being the most common causative agent identified in affected patients [22]. In the cardiovascular system, Campylobacter jejuni has been implicated in myocarditis, as illustrated by a case of a 13-year-old female who developed chest pain and elevated cardiac biomarkers within days of gastroenteritis; full recovery was achieved with supportive care and antibiotics [23]. Additionally, Campylobacter jejuni bacteremia can occur in immunocompromised hosts, such as a 7-year-old boy with nephrotic syndrome receiving steroid therapy, who presented with fever, diarrhea, and abdominal pain, and responded well to azithromycin after blood culture confirmed the pathogen [24]. These reports underscore the pathogen’s capacity to invade beyond the intestinal mucosa and trigger systemic inflammatory responses. However, in neonates, Campylobacter jejuni sepsis remains exceptionally rare, and progression to septic shock with multiorgan dysfunction is even less common. The present case is unique in its rapid deterioration to shock with involvement of the brain, heart, liver, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract in a previously healthy neonate, followed by complete recovery after aggressive intensive care and targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Conclusion

Neonatal septic shock often begins with subtle, nonspecific symptoms, so early recognition should rely on vigilant clinical assessment and dynamic markers such as capillary refill time and lactate rather than waiting for hypotension. In this case, prompt airway and circulatory stabilization, rapid correction of severe acidosis and electrolyte derangements, and early broad-spectrum antimicrobials followed by culture-guided optimization to meropenem plus azithromycin for Campylobacter jejuni controlled systemic inflammation and reversed multiorgan injury, resulting in full recovery. The course supports a protocolized approach that integrates organ-protective care and cautious reintroduction of enteral feeds with continuous reassessment and timely de-escalation once microbiology is available. Clinicians should consider Campylobacter Jejuni in septic neonates with diarrhea and pair acute management with structured post-discharge surveillance, including neurodevelopmental follow-up.
