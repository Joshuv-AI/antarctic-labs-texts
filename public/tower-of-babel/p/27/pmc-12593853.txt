Association between serum sodium trajectories and all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with ischemic stroke: a retrospective analysis of the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Junhui Zou, Chong Fan, Yipan Fan, Peng Xu, Jun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between serum sodium levels and stroke prognosis has been investigated, but the effect of serum sodium fluctuations on severe stroke patients remains uncertain. We aimed to evaluate the association between serum sodium trajectories within 72 h of admission and all-cause mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with ischemic stroke.MethodsThis retrospective study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database, including patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke from 2008 to 2022. Serum sodium trajectories within 72 h of admission were classified using a latent class trajectory model. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the relationship between trajectories and all-cause mortality. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of this association across various populations.ResultsAmong 881 enrolled patients, two distinct serum sodium trajectory groups were identified: class 1 (n = 778, 88.3%) exhibited stable sodium levels within the normal range, while class 2 (n = 103, 11.7%) showed rapid fluctuations. Kaplan–Meier analyses revealed that patients in class 2 had higher all-cause mortality at 30-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year follow-ups (all P < 0.001). Multivariate COX regression analyses consistently showed elevated mortality risk in class 2 versus class 1 across all timepoints: ICU (HR: 2.17; 95% CI 1.27–3.68; P = 0.004), in-hospital (HR: 2.51; 95% CI 1.64–3.85; P < 0.001), 30-day (HR: 2.41; 95% CI 1.67–3.49; P < 0.001), 90-day (HR: 2.17; 95% CI 1.56–3.03; P < 0.001), 180-day (HR: 2.13; 95% CI 1.55–2.92; P < 0.001) and 1 year (HR: 1.99; 95% CI 1.46–2.71; P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionsIn critically ill patients with ischemic stroke, rapidly fluctuating serum sodium levels are independently associated with increased short-term and long-term all-cause mortality. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the role of sodium dynamics in stroke outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03320-w.

Background

Globally, stroke is the second leading cause of death among non-communicable diseases and the third leading contributor to both mortality and disability [1]. In the past three decades, the global prevalence of stroke survivors, stroke-related deaths, and post-stroke disabilities has increased significantly [1, 2], with projections indicating a continued upward trend [3, 4]. Ischemic stroke (IS) constitutes approximately 85% of all stroke cases, with severe IS representing a primary contributor to global mortality, disability, and disease burden [5]. Consequently, identifying available, cost-effective, and accurate predictors of poor prognosis in severe IS is clinically imperative.

As the primary cation outside cells, sodium ions are essential for maintaining osmotic equilibrium, physiological cellular processes, extracellular fluid levels, and acid–base stability [6]. Sodium and water imbalances are common in acute stroke patients, affecting approximately 50% of cases [7]. These disturbances can cause diverse clinical manifestations and are associated with prolonged hospitalization, higher morbidity, and increased mortality [8–10]. Sodium abnormalities are also prevalent in critically ill patients [11–13], with incidence rates ranging from 6.9% to 17.7% depending on the observation period and population [14]. Current researches indicate that both hyponatremia and hypernatremia affect mortality rates in hospitalized patients [10–13, 15–17].

Since serum sodium levels may fluctuate significantly over time, monitoring their dynamic patterns may offer greater clinical value for prognostic assessment. A major retrospective analysis conducted at the Mayo Clinic revealed that unstable serum sodium trajectories posed higher mortality risks than the stable pattern during hospitalization and within 1 year [18]. Nevertheless, currently, no studies have examined the association between serum sodium trajectories and mortality in critically ill patients with IS. Utilizing the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database, we sought to examine the association between variations in serum sodium levels and mortality across different follow-up periods. The study was designed to refine electrolyte management strategies and improve clinical outcomes.

Materials

Study population

As a database of significant scale, the MIMIC database provides open access to high-quality clinical information from critically ill patients at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center spanning 14 years (2008–2022). We screened data from the latest version, MIMIC-IV v3.1, which was released in October 2024 [19]. Author Junhui Zou obtained database access (Record ID: 66,659,928) by completing the required National Institutes of Health (NIH) training and passing the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program. As all personally identifiable information had been removed, patient consent was not required.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study enrolled patients whose primary diagnosis was IS, as defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)−9/10 criteria. The study applied the following exclusion criteria to patient selection: (1) aged < 18 years (n = 0); (2) multiple ICU admissions records due to IS, only the first admission data were included (n = 243); (3) ICU length of stay of < 72 h (n = 1,397); (4) combined with end-stage renal dysfunction (n = 18), which usually accompanied by difficult-to-correct water–sodium imbalance; and (5) patients without daily serum sodium for the first three days after ICU admission (n = 211). Data collection

Data extraction was performed using PostgreSQL (version 15.12) and Navicat Premium (version 17) with Structured Query Language. The extracted variables encompassed the following contents: (1) demographics, including age, gender, and race. (2) Comorbidities, comprising sepsis, hypertension, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, hyperlipidemia, renal failure, liver disease, malignant cancer, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). (3) Laboratory parameters, encompassing white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), anion gap, serum creatinine, serum sodium, and blood glucose. (4) Vital signs, including systolic (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart and respiratory rates. (5) Disease severity scores, involving Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) score. (6) Treatments, containing intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (IV-tPA), mechanical thrombectomy, mechanical ventilation, and vasoactive drugs. (7) Length of stay (LOS) and patient outcomes. All participants had serum sodium values at least once a day for 3 days after ICU admission. For patients who measured serum sodium values multiple times daily, we included the first recorded serum sodium value in the study. For vital signs, disease severity scores, and laboratory parameters other than serum sodium values, which were measured multiple times during hospitalization, we utilized the first recorded values within the first day of ICU stay records. Variables containing > 20% missing values were removed to reduce bias, while the "MICE" package in R was applied for multiple imputation of variables with lower missing rates [20].

Clinical outcomes

This study evaluated all-cause mortality at multiple timepoints: ICU, in-hospital, 30-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year post-admission.

Serum sodium trajectories grouping

We employed latent class trajectory models (LCTM) to classify serum sodium trajectories via the “LCTMtools” package in R [21]. LCTM identifies discrete latent variables representing potential categories, grouping individuals with similar longitudinal patterns into distinct trajectory clusters. This approach captures both between-group heterogeneity and within-group random effects, providing superior trajectory analysis accuracy [22]. To determine the most suitable number of latent classes, we constructed models ranging from 1 to 7 classes. Second-order polynomial functions were employed for fitting the models [23]. The fit of LCTM was assessed using log-likelihood values, entropy, and information criteria [24]. A better-performing model was characterized by decreased Akaike information criterion (AIC) values and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values, along with larger entropy and log-likelihood values. Model selection required: (1) entropy > 0.7, (2) each class comprising > 2% of the total sample, and (3) average posterior probabilities > 70% per class [21, 23, 25]. Clinical interpretability was additionally considered during model selection.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR) according to their distributional characteristics, and categorical data were reported as frequencies. Normality of continuous measures was verified by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The t test was employed for normally distributed variables, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was chosen for non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were analyzed via the chi-square test.

The study utilized Kaplan–Meier curves to evaluate variations in all-cause mortality between serum sodium trajectory classes during follow-up periods of 30 days, 90 days, 180 days, and 1 year, with statistical significance assessed through log-rank testing. To evaluate the independent relationship of trajectory patterns with clinical outcomes, Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted. Data are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We constructed three models: model 1: unadjusted; model 2: adjusted for age, gender, and race; model 3: additionally adjusted for sepsis, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, COPD, liver disease, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, renal failure, malignant cancer, CCI, heart rate, respiratory rate, WBC, anion gap, serum creatinine, blood glucose, SOFA score, GCS score, SAPS II score, IV-tPA, mechanical thrombectomy, mechanical ventilation, and vasoactive drugs. We also explored the relationship between serum sodium levels as continuous variables at ICU admission and clinical outcomes via Cox regression models. Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using the variance inflation factor, and covariates with variance inflation factor values > 5 were eliminated from the model to prevent overfitting.

The robustness of the findings was evaluated through subgroup analyses, which also served to detect significant interaction variables. The covariates used in model 3 were also included in this part of the study. The stratified variables included age, gender, race, hypertension, renal failure, and sepsis, of which age was divided into two groups using 65 years as the cutoff. Likelihood ratio tests were used to probe for interactions. All subgroup results are presented in forest plots.

In this study, statistical analyses were carried out using R (version 4.4.2). P value < 0.05 (two-sided) was used to indicate statistical significance.

Results

This study included 881 eligible patients with a mean age of 69.8 ± 15.1 years, 53% of whom were male. The ICU, in-hospital, 30-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 1-year all-cause mortality rate was 10.1%, 16.0%, 23.5%, 30%, 33.7%, and 37.2%, respectively.

Trajectory grouping based on the LCTM model

Supplementary Table 1 displays the model fit statistics for the LCTMs. While the model with seven classes showed the lowest AIC value, the largest log-likelihood, along with 70.9% entropy, its smallest class (0.2%) failed our predefined criteria. The model with two classes demonstrated the lowest BIC while meeting all stability requirements (Tables 1, S1). The trajectories of each class also showed two sets of trends with significant time-based discrepancies, which are highly clinically explanatory (Fig. 2). Since the lowest BIC value has been widely used to screen the best trajectory model, we identified the model with two classes as optimal. Class 1 accounted for 88.3% of patients, whose serum sodium levels remained stable within the normal range, and class 2 accounted for 11.7% of patients, with initially normal serum sodium levels that rapidly increased beyond the upper limit of normal. Discussion

This retrospective cohort study identified two distinct serum sodium trajectories in critically ill IS patients using LCTM: class 1, with serum sodium levels stable within the normal range, and class 2, with initial levels within the normal range but rapidly fluctuating. Compared with stable trajectories, fluctuating patterns demonstrated significantly elevated mortality risks across all evaluated timeframes. These findings remained statistically significant after multivariate adjustment. Notably, admission sodium levels showed no significant mortality association after multivariate adjustment.

Serum sodium concentration is precisely controlled by the osmoregulatory system at 135–142 mmol/L to maintain serum osmolality within the normal range (275–295 mOsm/kg) [26, 27]. Evidence shows that levels of 138–142 mmol/L are optimal for physiological function and correlate with reduced mortality [17]. In acute care settings, hypernatremia (> 145 mmol/L) is a prevalent electrolyte abnormality, affecting 4–26% of patients [28]. Hypernatremia can induce physiological abnormalities, manifesting as lethargy, restlessness, seizures, reduced left ventricular contractility, muscle cramps, and rhabdomyolysis [29, 30]. It serves as an autonomous prognostic indicator for worse clinical outcomes among inpatients and critically ill patients [28, 31–33]. In patients with acute stroke, hypernatremia occurs less frequently than hyponatremia but remains clinically significant [7]. Pan and colleagues [34] indicated that elevated baseline serum sodium levels in IS patients predicted poorer functional performance and heightened susceptibility to early neurological worsening. Vedantam et al. [35] observed that in patients with severe traumatic brain injury, hypernatremia independently increases both incidence and mortality rates, irrespective of comorbid conditions. Similarly, Liu et al. [36] found that hypernatremia at admission increased short-term mortality in non-traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage patients. However, previous studies mainly focused on the impact of serum sodium level at a given point in time on patient prognosis, with limited research on longitudinal serum sodium trajectory prediction. To our knowledge, this is the first study exploring the impact of early serum sodium variability on the prognosis of critically ill IS patients.

In clinical practice, serum sodium measurement is a readily available and frequently monitored parameter. A retrospective cohort study demonstrated that ICU-acquired hypernatremia correlated with higher short-term mortality in critically ill cerebrovascular individuals, identifying 147 mmol/L as the mortality-associated threshold for maximum serum sodium concentration [37]. Nevertheless, this study defined hypernatremia based on ICU-acquired status without accounting for dynamic serum sodium variations. Multiple studies have demonstrated a link between variations in serum sodium levels and adverse clinical outcomes in patients in critical conditions. A multicenter retrospective study elucidated that ICU patients with normonatremia or hypernatremia at admission who experienced > 5 mmol/L serum sodium elevation within 48 h had increased mortality during hospitalization [15]. The study by Li et al. [38] demonstrated that in cases of sepsis with lactic acidosis, patients maintaining steady normal-range serum sodium trajectories had superior 30-day outcomes relative to individuals with significant sodium level fluctuations. Likewise, Huang et al. [39] reported that acute kidney injury patients with ascending or descending sodium trajectories within seven days had significantly higher mortality risks compared to those with stable levels. In addition, the investigation by Xia et al. [40] observed that heart failure patients developing either hyponatremic or hypernatremia trajectories during the initial 48-h ICU period exhibited significantly elevated 1-year mortality rates. This study showed that the rapidly fluctuating serum sodium trajectory was associated with increased mortality, aligning with prior research findings. These findings underscore the importance of continuous monitoring and maintaining water–sodium balance in critically ill patients. They may also guide the timely detection and management of serum sodium imbalances in severe IS patients, warranting further high-quality studies.

Hypernatremia in critically ill patients with IS arises from multiple mechanisms. First, neurological ICUs frequently induce therapeutic hypernatremia using osmotic diuretics or hypertonic saline to manage cerebral edema or elevated intracranial pressure [41]. Second, post-stroke hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction often disrupts sodium and water homeostasis, as seen in central diabetes insipidus, where hypernatremia may indicate disease severity [7, 37]. Third, critically ill patients with IS often experience impaired consciousness, which commonly leads to inadequate fluid and solute intake [42, 43]. Finally, the presence of comorbidities, such as diabetes, renal failure, or heart failure in ICU patients, combined with routine administration of diuretics or other medications that enhance water loss, collectively elevates the risk of hypernatremia [7]. Although no consensus guidelines exist for acute stroke-related sodium disorders, neurologists must accurately diagnose hypernatremia etiologies to guide targeted treatment and optimize outcomes.

The underlying mechanisms linking serum sodium trajectories to mortality risk remain unclear, but several plausible explanations exist. First, hypernatremia increases extracellular fluid osmolarity, causing cellular dehydration and shrinkage. This impairs organelle function (particularly in mitochondria and nuclei), leading to apoptosis activation and accelerated cell death [30]. Second, hypernatremia diminishes cerebral perfusion in the internal carotid and middle cerebral arteries while leaving the brain's oxygen metabolism unchanged, causing relative hypoxia [44]. Furthermore, hypernatremia can impair the integrity of the blood–brain barrier, leading to the accumulation of cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules, which may contribute to neuroinflammation [45]. Collectively, these effects exacerbate brain damage and contribute to poor clinical outcomes.

We also recognize some limitations of this study. First, although previous studies have demonstrated that sodium and water disturbances in acute stroke patients typically manifest as hyponatremia, our trajectory analysis did not capture this pattern. This could be attributed to the limited sample size, insufficient observation duration, or selection bias inherent in a single-center database. Larger or multi-center data sets might uncover additional clinically relevant trajectories, such as progressively declining or rising-then-falling patterns. Second, due to limitations of the MIMIC database, we could not include certain variables, such as NIHSS scores and neuroimages. These omissions may affect the model's accuracy. Third, as an observational study, it can only demonstrate statistical associations rather than causality between serum sodium levels and mortality. While this study cannot define the optimal post-stroke serum sodium trajectory for minimizing mortality, maintaining levels within the normal range appears appropriate based on current findings. More high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to investigate the impact of early post-stroke serum sodium fluctuations on mortality.

Conclusions

This study employed LCTM to categorize serum sodium trajectories in critically ill patients with IS into stable and fluctuating patterns, and investigated their associations with mortality. The analysis observed that patients with rapidly fluctuating sodium trajectories exhibited significantly increased risks of both short-term (ICU stay, in-hospital, and 30-day) and long-term (90-day, 180-day, and 1-year) mortality compared to those with stable trajectories.
