Within-summer variation in the heat-related emergency department visits among older adults in South Korea: a population-based case-crossover study
Authors: Jinah Park, Ayoung Kim, Ho Kim, Yoonhee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe hazardous impact of heat on health has been extensively reported, with many studies showing long-term adaptation to heat. However, short-term acclimatization, particularly within a season, remains unclear.MethodsUsing a two-stage time-series approach and nationwide medical claims data (2011–2019, May–September) for South Korea, we estimated the association between ambient temperature and emergency department (ED) visits as well as its within-summer variation, focusing on individuals aged ≥65 years.FindingsWithin the study period, 4,637,075 ED visits were recorded. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Short-term exposure to high temperatures increases the risk of emergency department visits and death among older adults. Although long-term decreasing trends in heat-related health impacts are well documented, within-summer changes in heat-related health risk remain largely unclear. To identify studies relevant to this subject, we searched Google Scholar and PubMed using “within-season,” “within-summer,” “temperature,” “heat,” “heatwave,” “high temperature,” “mortality,” “morbidity,” “death,” “emergency department,” and “emergency room” as key search terms, focusing on English Language publications available before February 28, 2025. We noted that studies on within-summer changes in heat-health risks, particularly those focusing on potential heterogeneities owing to sociodemographic characteristics, are relatively scarce.

Added value of this study

This study, which included all emergency department visits among people aged ≥65 years between 2011 and 2019, showed a higher heat risk for emergency department visits among individuals aged ≥75 years, females, low- or middle-income groups, and those who visited the emergency department due to renal diseases. Further, the heat-related risk of emergency department visit was higher in early summer than in late summer, and this trend was consistent across all sociodemographic groups. However, minimal differences were observed in heat risk between early and late summer for females, individuals aged ≥75 years, and those with low income status.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings underscore the need to establish flexible national-scale heat adaptation strategies that account for temporal variations in the adverse effects of high temperatures on older adults. Via adaptive interventions such as the strategic allocation of medical resources and real-time adjustments to emergency response protocols, healthcare systems can ensure sustained operational capacity and resilience throughout summer months. This approach not only facilitates more efficient distribution of critical medical resources but also minimizes the risk of heat-related emergency department visits among older adults. Furthermore, enhancing public awareness regarding short-term changes in heat risk may also support the efficient implementation and operation of responsive heat adaptation policies nationwide.

Introduction

With climate change and global warming increasingly attracting attention globally, several heat adaptation policies, including the use of early warning systems, have been established and implemented in different countries.1,2 Although numerous studies have demonstrated adaptation to heat over time, showing a long-term decreasing trend in heat-related mortality risk owing to the increased use of air conditioners, infrastructural changes in housing and health services designs, and the implementation of heat adaptation policies,3,4 within-summer changes in heat risk, i.e., variations that occur within a year, such as those that occur between early and late summer, largely remain unclear.

National heat adaptation plans or prevention interventions are typically implemented uniformly, with consistent criteria and policies maintained throughout the hot season, while placing less focus on changes that occur within a single season.5 However, changes in heat susceptibility and heat acclimatization within short timescales, such as during summer, have been reported. A few studies conducted in the US and a multicountry study involving nine countries showed greater heat impacts in early summer than in late summer, highlighting the importance of short-term changes in vulnerability.6, 7, 8 According to these previous studies, the factors that contribute to this short-term adaptation include physiological acclimatization, behavioral changes, preparedness, and an increased frequency of heat events.8, 9, 10 For example, as summer continues, individuals may use cooling equipment, stay hydrated, and adjust their activities to avoid peak heat hours. Additionally, at the societal level, early warning systems for extreme weather events are effectively in operation.

The limited availability of medical infrastructure and resources, including political support, necessitates taking into account within-summer temporal changes in the risk of heat exposure when developing heat response plans and allocating medical resources.11 The importance of establishing flexible systems to enhance resilience and avoid maladaptation has also been increasingly emphasized as climate disasters become more diverse and severe.12 Further, given that heatwaves and extreme temperatures can overload medical services,13 understanding temporal changes in heat-related health risk may facilitate the establishment of resilient health systems that are well adapted to climate change. These measures are consistent with the United Nation's Sustainable Development Goal No. 13, which mandates urgent action to combat climate change and its impacts and aims at strengthening resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards and natural disasters globally. In this context, it is beneficial to raise public awareness regarding heat-related risks via public health campaigns and heat action plans.

In this study, we aimed to estimate within-summer variations in the risk of emergency department (ED) visits owing to high temperature among individuals aged ≥65 years residing in South Korea (hereafter referred to as Korea). We also compared changes in risk based on sociodemographic characteristics and the cause of ED visits. The application of the findings of this study may facilitate the establishment of efficient adaptation strategies to heat risk and ensure more efficient distribution of critical medical resources, while minimizing the risk of heat-related ED visits.

Methods

Study population

In this study, we included individuals aged ≥65 years. The Korean population is aging rapidly and the largest increase in the proportion of older individuals is expected between 2019 and 2050.14 Notably, the proportion of individuals aged ≥65 years has increased steadily over the years, accounting for 11·0% (5,525,629·5), 15·0% (7,718,615·5), and 18·5% (9,462,269·5) of the total Korean population in 2011, 2019, and 2023, respectively.15 The residential information of individuals in the study population were identified at the district (“Si-Gun-Gu”) level. Districts are second-level local administrative units under provinces. In Korea, there are 229 districts, with the median area at 402·4 km2, approximately 1·7 times larger than the average ZIP code area in the United States (233 km2).16

Emergency department visit data

ED visit data for May–September, 2011 to 2019 were obtained from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS)–National Health Insurance Database (NHID), the unified nationwide medical claims database of Korea's universal coverage health insurance system.17 Generated using beneficiaries' medical bill expenses claimed by medical service providers, the NHID provides comprehensive data, including personal information, such as age, sex, income status based on insurance premium level, residential area, and medical resource use data, with diagnosis codes recorded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10 code). Based on this database, the primary causes of ED visits included cardiovascular diseases (ICD-10: I00–I99), respiratory diseases (J00–J99), renal diseases (N00–N39), and mental disorders or neurological diseases (F00–F99 and G00–G99) for our cause-specific analysis. All-cause ED visit records were also obtained by referencing treatment codes for emergency medical care.

Environmental data

We collected daily mean temperature (°C) data for May–September, 2011 to 2019 from the Korea Meteorological Agency, which provides nationwide real-time hourly-scale temperature data for sub-administrative areas within districts. The data were collected in real time (at minute and hourly intervals) using sensors, standardized into a uniform format, and subjected to centralized quality control processes to ensure high reliability. Subsequently, the hourly data were aggregated into district-level daily mean temperatures, defined as the average hourly temperature recorded over a 24-h period for each district. Daily relative humidity data (%) were also collected and averaged at the district level in the same process.

Daily ambient ozone (maximum 8-h average ozone concentration; ppb) and fine particulate matter (24-h average, PM2·5; μg/m3) data were collected from the air pollution prediction model developed by the AiMS-CREATE network (cross-validation R2: 0·887 for ozone and 0·977 for PM2·5) and used for sensitivity analysis. Details of the model have been published previously.18 In brief, daily ozone and PM2·5 monitoring data were collected from Air Korea, operated by the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment, and were assigned to 1-km2 grid cells based on the locations of monitoring stations from 2015 to 2019. In the prediction model, these daily averages of monitored ozone and PM2·5 concentrations were used as “true values,” and an ensemble learning approach (generalized additive model) was employed to incorporate three algorithms (random forest, extreme gradient boosting, and artificial neural network). The prediction model estimated ozone and PM2·5 concentrations at a 1 km2 spatial resolution across all districts in inland Korea, and we aggregated the grid-level ozone and PM2·5 concentrations to the district level and assigned these values to each enrollee based on their residential area.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, we adopted a conventional two-stage time-series analysis approach. In the first stage, we estimated district-specific associations between temperature and ED visits using time-series regression, assuming a quasi-Poisson distribution. To control for seasonality, we used natural cubic splines (NCS) of day of the season with four degrees of freedom (df), which were multiplied by a year indicator, day of the week as the indicator variable, and accounted for long-term trends by including the NCS of time with two df, following the previous study.6

We applied a distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) to estimate both the non-linear exposure–response and lag–response associations of temperature.6 For the exposure–response dimension of this model, a quadratic B-spline of temperature modeled with one internal knot at the 75th percentile of the temperature distribution was employed.6 The lag-response dimension was modeled using an NCS with a maximum lag of two days and one internal knot placed at day one. Further, given this study's primary focus on the immediate effects of heat on ED visits, the maximum lag was determined to be two days. Nevertheless, we conducted sensitivity analyses by extending the maximum lag period to 10 days (see Sensitivity Analysis below; Appendix p 2).

We added a linear interaction between the abovementioned temperature modeled using the DLNMs and indicators of the day of summer to estimate the association between temperature and ED visits in early and late summer. The day of summer was defined as a continuous numeric variable representing the day within the summer season (May–September), coded from 1 (May 1) to 153 (September 30). In this interaction model, the main term for temperature represents the exposure-lag-response association at the reference day of summer.6 We estimated the temperature–ED visit associations for early and late summer by centering the variable on June 1 and August 31, respectively.

In the second stage, we pooled district-specific lag cumulative temperature-ED visit associations for early and late summer separately using a multivariate meta-regression model.6 Thereafter, the results were summarized as relative risk (RR) by comparing risks at the 95th percentile of the temperature distribution (∼28·4 °C) with the risk at the 50th percentile (∼22·5 °C) as the reference. This percentile-based approach was adopted to ensure that both reference and extreme temperatures were appropriately represented across monthly temperature distributions. Temperature distributions at a national level corresponding to each percentile are provided in Appendix p 3.

Additionally, we evaluated the association between temperature and ED visit during early and late summer stratified by sociodemographic groups as follows: age (65–74 years old and ≥75 years old), sex (male and female), income level (low-, middle-, and high-income), and cause of ED visit. We classified disease groups based on the primary discharge diagnosis. The identified causes of ED visits for the stratified analysis, as described above, included cardiovascular, respiratory, and renal diseases, and mental disorders or neurological diseases.

A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results obtained. First, a different model setting was applied for DLNM parameterization. For the lag-response dimension, alternative maximum lag periods of 3, 4, and 10 days were employed, and for the exposure-response dimension, knot positions at the 65th, 70th, 80th, and 85th percentiles were employed. Next, the df for seasonality was varied from four to three and then to five, and those for long-term trends from two to one. Finally, we adjusted for daily relative humidity using the NCS with three df, for ozone or PM2·5 averaged over three consecutive days (lag 0–2). Owing to differences in data availability across periods and geographic coverage, additional ozone and PM2·5 adjustments were performed only for part of the sensitivity analysis. Specifically, the analysis period was restricted from 2011–2019 to 2015–2019, and the geographic scope was confined to inland Korea, excluding Jeju and Ulleung islands. We used SAS Enterprise version 7·1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) for data preprocessing and R (version 4·0·3) packages, “dlnm,” “tsModel,” and “mixmeta” (R Core Development Team, Vienna, Austria) for statistical analyses.

Results

Within the study period, 4,637,075 ED visits were recorded for individuals aged ≥65 years. As shown in Table 1, individuals aged 65–74 years and ≥75 years accounted for 45·4% and 54·6% of the ED visits, respectively. The highest number of ED visits was recorded in August (951,959; 20·5%), followed by September (950,854; 20·5%) (Table 1, Appendix p 4). ED visits for cardiovascular disease were identified as the primary cause of the ED visits (11·5%; Appendix p 6). The average nationwide daily mean temperature and relative humidity during the study period were 22·4 °C (standard deviation (SD): 3·9 °C) and 75·1% (SD: 12·3%), respectively, and the monthly mean temperature increased from May to August, and subsequently decreased from August to September (Appendix p 7). The average nationwide daily ozone concentration between 2015 and 2019 during the warm season (May–September) was 52·3 ppb (SD: 17·1), with the highest concentration recorded in May (Appendix p 8).

From this figure, early summer showed higher heat risk estimates than that for late summer across all temperature percentiles. Overall, the high temperature risk of ED visits for the entire study population was 1·076 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1·070–1·082), and was higher among individuals aged ≥75 years (RR: 1·080, 95% CI: 1·072–1·088) than in among those aged 65–74 years (RR: 1·068, 95% CI: 1·060–1·077). Fig. 3 shows the overall cumulative RR predicted for early and late summer for the entire research population and for subgroups stratified by sex, age, income, and cause of ED visit. The heat risk of ED visits was higher in early summer (RR: 1·124, 95% CI: 1·098–1·150) than in late summer (RR: 1·054, 95% CI: 1·043–1·066). Individuals aged ≥75 years, females, and those from low-income groups were identified as more vulnerable to heat (Fig. 2), and the differences between their early and late summer heat risk estimates were smaller compared to individuals aged 65–74 years, males, and those from middle- or high-income groups. Additionally, ED visits for renal diseases and mental or neurological disorders showed a similar trend, with a higher risk in early summer (RR for renal diseases: 1·249, 95% CI: 1·149–1·357; RR for mental or neurological disorders: 1·397, 95% CI: 1·252–1·559) than in late summer (RR for renal diseases: 1·166, 95% CI: 1·122–1·211; RR for mental or neurological disorders: 0·837, 95% CI: 0·795–0·881). Cause-specific exposure–response curves for early and late summer are presented in Appendix p 5. Conversely, ED visits for respiratory diseases showed an opposite trend, with a higher risk in late summer (RR: 1·180, 95% CI: 1·139–1·222) than in early summer (RR: 1·091, 95% CI: 1·014–1·175); however, the CIs overlapped. ED visits for cardiovascular diseases showed an insignificant association with temperature in both early and late summer.

Discussion

Within-summer changes in the association between high temperatures and ED visits were examined for all-cause and cause-specific ED visits among individuals aged ≥65 years in Korea for 2011–2019. This nationwide study provided evidence of within-summer changes in susceptibility to heat, with a greater heat risk in early summer than in late summer. This finding indicated that the heat risk associated with ED visits decreases as summer progresses. Further, relatively weak within-summer changes were observed among females, individuals aged ≥75 years, individuals with a low-income status, and those with ED visits for renal diseases, suggesting greater vulnerability to heat risk during summer months and a low capacity to adapt to expected increases in heat exposure in late summer for these groups relative to other subpopulations.

Our results are consistent with those of previous studies on the mortality risks of summer heat or heat waves and their within-summer trends. A previous multicity study involving 305 cities showed a higher mortality risk in early summer than in late summer.6 The same study found significant differences in heat-related mortality risk between early and late summer, with an RR of 1·32 for early summer and 0·97 for late summer in Korea.6 Another study involving 43 US communities showed a stronger mortality risk for the year's first heatwave than for heatwaves occurring later in the season.7 Similarly, another study involving 148 US cities showed higher heat risks in spring and early summer than in other seasons.8

However, our findings contrast with those of a previous study conducted in Seoul, Korea, using 1993–2009 data. In this previous study, in which the authors applied a piecewise linear threshold model to identify different thresholds and estimate the risks above these thresholds, stratifying the data into early summer (June 1–July 15) and late summer (July 16–August 31), a greater mortality risk was reported for late summer than for early summer.4 The authors identified a lower threshold in early summer (22·8 °C) than in late summer (27·9 °C), suggesting seasonal acclimatization. However, given that mortality risks above these thresholds largely depend on the location of the thresholds in this modeling approach, a higher mortality risk was estimated in late summer (based on a steeper slope for the temperature–mortality association) than in early summer (based on a more gradual slope). This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the health outcomes of interest, study region, and study period. Specifically, the previous study primarily focused on mortality in Seoul from 1993 to 2009, whereas our analysis used nationwide data covering 2011–2019. Air-conditioning penetration in Seoul has increased substantially, from 15% in the early 1990s to 71% by the late 2000s, according to Korea Power Exchange.4 Thus, in the 1990s, limited adaptive behaviors were likely and may explain the previously reported higher heat-related mortality risk in late summer.

These temporal changes in heat susceptibility over the study period indicated population adaptation, possibly owing to several proposed mechanisms. First, possible adaptation mechanisms include behavioral changes and preparedness.8, 9, 10 For instance, as high temperatures are consistently being reported, individuals as well as communities have taken measures, such as increasing air-conditioning usage and utilizing warning systems, to adapt to thermal stress.1,2 Individuals may also have adjusted their activity patterns to minimize exposure to extreme temperatures, and institutions may have modified infrastructure and policies accordingly.20 Second, in terms of physiological acclimatization to extreme temperatures, sweat glands have become more active and cardiac output has increased to support sweating, resulting in relatively low sodium levels in sweat as summer advances.21 Overall, these mechanisms support the idea that the increased frequency of extreme heat events in late summer would decrease the health risk of heat. A previous multicountry study supported this mechanism by showing a declining risk of mortality attributable to ambient temperature as the frequency of temperature increased.10

The third proposed mechanism is mortality displacement, which refers to the fact that heat-related health impacts predominantly occur among individuals with pre-existing health vulnerabilities.19,22 That is, heat-vulnerable populations visit EDs in early summer, leading to increased risk estimates during this period, and over time, a lower heat risk is observed in late summer because vulnerable individuals have already visited EDs; hence, morbidity displacement. Finally, it is possible that unmeasured confounders, such as air pollutants or pollen,23 also increased the heat risk in early summer. In our sensitivity analysis, adjusted for lag 0–2 ozone, the heat risk in early summer was lower than that in the primary analysis without ozone adjustment (Fig. 4, Appendix p 9). The synergistic impacts of ozone and heat have been widely reported.24,25 In Korea, ozone concentration is high between April and June (early summer). Therefore, the high heat risk observed in early summer may be partially attributable to a high ozone concentration, Asian dust, or pollen.

Our results also showed significant within-summer changes in susceptibility to heat exposure among males aged 64–74 years and middle- and high-income groups. Previous studies have identified socioeconomic factors as contributors to heterogeneity in heat exposure risk.10,26 Consistently, our findings suggested that females, individuals aged ≥75 years, and those from low-income groups exhibit decreased adaptation to temporal changes in heat exposure. Aging is a well-known risk factor for heat exposure given its association with a weakening thermal regulatory system, underlying comorbidities, medication use, and cognitive decline.27 Notably, among older adults, females aged ≥75 years showed higher vulnerability to heat, possibly owing to the longer life expectancy of females.27 Low-income individuals tend to be more vulnerable to heat because financial constraints and limited social support hinder healthy behaviors, chronic disease management, and access to resources and goods necessary for effective temperature regulation (e.g., cooling devices).26 These susceptibility factors decrease adaptability to heat exposure during the warm season among females, individuals aged ≥75 years, and those from low-income groups.

Regarding ED visit causes, an opposite risk trend was observed for ED visits due to respiratory diseases, with a higher heat risk observed in late summer, while ED visits due to cardiovascular diseases showed no within-summer heat risk changes, although the CIs largely overlapped. A previous study in Korea suggested that the extent of mortality displacement differs according to population characteristics and that temperature lag effects change over time.9 Therefore, differences in risk patterns between heat-related ED visits for respiratory and cardiovascular diseases as well as the differences in the extent of adaptation among certain groups provide additional insights and indicate the existence of other adaptation-related factors. In addition, as summer progresses and extreme heat becomes more frequent, prolonged use of air conditioners may be more common, potentially reducing indoor ventilation and drying indoor air, which could increase susceptibility to respiratory infections.28 Furthermore, the high precipitation typically observed during mid-summer in Korea may promote mold and microbial growth, with delayed effects extending into late summer.29 Together, these environmental conditions, when combined with high temperatures, may help explain the higher risk of ED visits due to respiratory diseases observed in late summer. The risk of ED visits due to mental disorders or neurological diseases in late summer remained stable or declined slightly under extreme heat. This observation may be attributed to the unique exposure–response curve obtained (Appendix p 5). Further studies are needed to identify the contributing factors and confirm the replicability of these findings.

The results showed that under the same temperature conditions, heat risk in early summer was more than two-fold that in late summer. Thus, it is important for the public to be informed that a daily mean temperature of 28·4 °C in early June can be as harmful to health as a daily mean temperature of 29·9 °C in late August. Further, considering the capacity of the healthcare system in Korea to respond to extreme heat, proactive planning to mitigate early summer risks is crucial. Seasonal forecasting may also support timely interventions by enabling advance preparedness. The harvesting effect, a possible explanation for the observed decline in heat-related risks in late summer,19,22 also underscores the importance of reducing the vulnerability of at-risk populations and establishing response systems before the onset of early summer. At the population level, early interventions may minimize the overall health burden of extreme heat, and as summer progresses, shifting adaptive and flexible interventions toward populations that may exhibit reduced heat adaptability in late summer may be of great significance. It is also essential to ensure that emergency healthcare systems remain fully functional even in early summer by establishing a robust and responsive framework in advance. A dynamic approach that adjusts both system operation and resource allocation according to seasonal timing is required to enhance the resilience of the healthcare system throughout summer. Additionally, strategically distributing medical resources, considering temporal variations into account, to optimize the effectiveness of the heat-health response is also highly recommended.

This study had some limitations. First, due to data availability, we could not consider air conditioner use, a key strategy for heat adaptation, which could hypothetically affect the accuracy of our risk estimates.3 Second, we only measured the heat risk of ED visits among older adults; thus, the within-summer heat risk estimates in this study may be different from those for the entire population. Therefore, generalizing our results to the entire population requires caution. Third, because temperature exposure was assigned using district-averaged values, our analysis may not fully capture within-district temperature heterogeneity, potentially resulting in some degree of exposure misclassification at the individual level. Finally, we assumed identical temporal changes in heat susceptibility during summer for the entire study period (2011–2019).

This study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first nationwide report on short-term changes in heat risk estimates using morbidity data in Korea. Using all-cause ED visit data for individuals aged ≥65 at the national scale, we quantified heat risks in early and late summer across subgroups based on sex, age, and income level. Further, we conducted cause-specific analysis to clarify temporal variations in heat risk, taking air pollutants (ozone), which may act as potential effect modifiers of the association between temperature and ED visits, into account. Thus, we observed short-term within-summer adaptation to heat among individuals aged ≥65 years residing in Korea. Notably, heat risks were more pronounced in individuals aged ≥75 years, females, the low-income group, and those with ED visits for renal diseases. These subgroups further showed minimal changes in temporal heat risk. Taken together, this study showed that quantifying short-term temporal variations in the health risks of heat, as reflected in morbidity data, has important implications for understanding adaptation to heat and informing healthcare facility preparedness. The findings of this study also suggest that instead of implementing uniform national heat policies, adaptation structures should be flexible and detailed, taking into account the temporal heterogeneity in heat risk.
