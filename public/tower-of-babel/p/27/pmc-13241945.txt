Patient Experiences With GLP-1 Receptor Agonists
Authors: Isabella de Vere Hunt, Mariana Ramirez-Posada, Christopher Sam Babu, Cati Brown-Johnson, Eleni Linos, Fatima Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This qualitative study involves thematic analyses of semistructured interviews on the experiences of adults currently or previously taking glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists in a variety of health contexts not limited to type 2 diabetes.

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have emerged as an important pharmacological innovation in the treatment of obesity, demonstrating efficacy in weight reduction and glycemic control through effects on metabolic pathways involved in glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity and central pathways regulating appetite and satiety.1,2 Obesity, defined in adults by a body mass index (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) of 30.0 or higher, is a complex and chronic disease due in most cases to obesogenic environments, psychosocial factors, and genetic variants, and with rates rising in almost every country worldwide.3,4 People living with obesity face multiple health challenges, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, and cancer. The clinical potential of GLP-1 RAs within and beyond the obesity treatment landscape continues to expand, with therapeutic benefits for cardiovascular disease prevention, heart failure, peripheral artery disease, obstructive sleep apnea, kidney diseases, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis, and neurodegenerative disease.5,6,7

Yet GLP-1 RA therapy presents numerous challenges, including cost and access barriers, variable individual efficacy, and a range of potential adverse effects. Cohort studies estimate that approximately 50% of people with obesity discontinue GLP-1 RAs within 12 months of initiation.8,9,10 Despite the popularity and impact of these therapies, two 2025 reviews of patients’ experiences with GLP-1 RAs found that qualitative research on the topic is scarce, with the limited existing work specific to patients prescribed GLP-1 RAs for type 2 diabetes, exploration of hypothetical patient preferences or health care professional views, or discourse analyses of social media content.11,12 This study provides a nuanced understanding of the experiences of patients taking GLP-1 RA medication in a variety of health contexts, including individuals who stopped taking GLP-1 RAs, to inform optimal management practices and policies to support long-term weight management.

Methods

For this qualitative study, we conducted semistructured video (Zoom Communications, Inc) interviews between July 22 and September 10, 2025, with adults across the US who were taking or had previously taken GLP-1 RAs. We assessed participant self-reported race and ethnicity in this study to describe the demographic characteristics of the study sample. Categories of race and ethnicity were derived from investigator-defined categories informed by US Census classifications and included African American or Black, American Indian, Asian, Hispanic or Latino, White, and more than 1 race or ethnicity. All participants gave verbal informed consent before starting the interview, which included permission to record and publish their quotes. A waiver of documentation was granted by the Stanford institutional review board, which approved this study. Interview recordings were professionally transcribed and deidentified. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline was followed for the write-up.13

Our interview guide (eAppendices 1-3 in Supplement 1) focused on the medication initiation process, medication effects on behaviors, clinician support, and weight stigma or body positivity. Participants were recruited through the national health volunteer registry ResearchMatch, with further participants identified through snowball sampling.14 ResearchMatch was created by several academic institutions and is supported by the US National Institutes of Health as part of the Clinical Translational Science Award program. ResearchMatch volunteers consented to be contacted by researchers about health studies for which they may be eligible. Participants were reimbursed with a $75 gift card for their time spent participating in an interview.

Data Analysis

Authors I.D.V.H. and M.R.P. undertook an inductive thematic analysis of the full interview transcripts, using a mind-mapping technique to co-construct themes, with data compared across transcripts to iteratively refine categories as patterns in the data were identified.15,16,17 The qualitative analysis software NVivo 14 (Lumivero) was used to manage and code the interview data, and all codes were derived directly from the data. I.D.V.H. completed data immersion by reading the full transcripts and subsequently coded the first 5 transcripts and developed a draft coding manual, which was reviewed and tested on the same 5 interviews by M.R.P. This coding manual was applied to the remaining transcripts by I.D.V.H. and M.R.P., with all interviews double-coded. These 2 researchers convened at 4 intervals throughout the coding process to review discrepancies and reach consensus on any codebook edits. New codes were created when concepts arose that were not adequately captured by the existing framework, and previously coded transcripts were revisited when needed to ensure consistency of interpretation across the dataset. All revisions to the coding framework were documented to maintain an audit trail of analytic decisions. Once coding was complete, our multidisciplinary analysis team (I.D.V.H., M.R.P., C.B.J., E.L., and F.R.), including an expert patient with lived experience of GLP-1 RA use and a participant in the set of interviews, discussed and refined the proposed themes. The meeting started with each researcher reflecting on their own personal biases and professional perspectives. The processes of reflexive participant collaboration and investigator triangulation brought multiple perspectives to expand our understanding of the data.18,19,20 Data saturation was considered throughout the iterative coding process and was judged to have been reached within the first 22 interviews, when subsequent interviews added no substantial new conceptual insights. A further 8 interviews confirmed data saturation across our varied sample.21

Results

Of 30 participants, 10 self-identified as men, 1 as nonbinary, and 19 as women. Their mean (SD) age was 54.0 (8.8) years. Participants were from 15 states in the US and consisted of 9 African American or Black (30%), 4 American Indian (13%), 3 Asian (10%), 4 Hispanic or Latino (13%), and 17 White (57%) individuals, and 5 persons with more than 1 race or ethnicity (17%). In total, 23 participants were using a GLP-1 RA at the time of the interview and 7 participants had previously used a GLP-1 RA but had stopped. Reasons for cessation included intolerable adverse effects (participants 5, 12, 15, 16, and 17), deterioration in kidney function (participant 21), and pregnancy (participant 3). We extracted 8 key themes regarding patients’ experiences of taking GLP-1 RAs, categorized into 2 overarching domains. The first domain was patient-reported benefits and trade-offs. It comprised the following 4 key themes: (1) reduction in food noise, psychological hunger, or appetite; (2) GLP-1 RAs are not a standalone weight loss solution; (3) wide spectrum of adverse effects experienced; and (4) Patients prepared to withstand substantial adverse effects and logistical challenges to achieve weight loss. The second domain was social, clinical, and structural context. It comprised the following 4 key themes: (5) perceived stigma associated with taking GLP-1 RAs, influenced by primary indication for prescription; (6) information provision and clinical support are essential but highly variable; (7) access challenges and cost can be prohibitive; and (8) value of shared experiences. Domain: Patient-Reported Benefits and Trade-Offs

Theme 1: Reduction in Food Noise, Psychological Hunger, or Appetite

The majority of participants described a substantial reduction in appetite and food noise after initiating GLP-1 RA therapy, with reports of diminished psychological hunger, fewer intrusive food-related thoughts, and a reduced sense of compulsion around eating. One participant explained that prior to treatment, “My whole world was around food.…If I’m bored, food; sad, food; upset, food,” but once the food noise was gone, she felt clear-headed and able to recognize that she did not want food in those moments (participant 2). In addition to reduced cravings, participants described new sensations of fullness, often framed positively and associated with a sense of control, such as, “It feels good to be totally in control of my eating.…The eating isn’t telling me what to do” (participant 24).

Theme 2: GLP-1 RAs Are Not a Standalone Solution to Weight Loss

Building on participants’ descriptions of reduced food noise and psychological hunger, many participants emphasized that these effects operated not in isolation but as enablers of intentional lifestyle change. GLP-1 RA therapy was therefore not experienced as a standalone or “miracle” weight loss solution, but as a tool that supported active engagement in diet and lifestyle change. One participant explained that the medication “made me want to start eating right” and hoped that lifestyle changes and medication would “all work together” (participant 15). As weight loss progressed, some participants described increased confidence and willingness to engage in physical activity, particularly in public settings. Participants also described making deliberate changes to their food environment and daily routines, for example, having to “shop differently…plan differently…and cook and eat differently” (participant 22).

Theme 3: Wide Spectrum of Adverse Effects Experienced

Participants described a wide spectrum of adverse effects, ranging from minimal or transient symptoms to severe enough to necessitate stopping treatment. Some participants described reframing certain adverse effects as informative or even beneficial, interpreting sensations such as nausea and indigestion as physiological feedback that helped them regulate their intake, for example, “I kind of welcome that feeling” (participant 29). Others felt uncertain whether symptoms could be directly attributed to the medication, especially in the context of multimorbidity, with 1 participant questioning whether symptoms reflected medication effects or “getting old” (participant 4).

Theme 4: Patients Prepared to Withstand Substantial Adverse Effects and Logistical Challenges to Achieve Weight Loss

Many participants tolerated substantial adverse effects and practical challenges to achieve weight loss. One participant described enduring gastrointestinal tract adverse effects because “the results were so immediate and helpful” (participant 10). Several participants described navigating a careful balance between tolerating adverse effects and maintaining quality of life. For example, 1 participant referred to seeking a “sweet spot” at which the medication could be continued without making them “deathly sick all day” (participant 3). Alongside physical adverse effects, some participants expressed concerns about long-term reliance on the medication and uncertainty about what might happen if treatment were stopped. One participant described assessing these concerns against previous unsuccessful weight-loss attempts, stating, “If it will help me lose weight, I’m willing to take the risk” (participant 9). Participants also described logistical challenges, including medication shortages and the effort required to obtain prescriptions, underscoring the practical burdens some patients were willing to accept to continue treatment.

Domain: Social, Clinical, and Structural Context

Theme 5: Perceived Stigma Associated With Taking GLP-1 RAs, Influenced by Primary Indication for Prescription

Several participants described experiencing or anticipating stigma associated with taking GLP-1 RAs, particularly when the medication was framed as a treatment for weight loss rather than for diabetes. Participants noted that taking GLP-1 RAs was sometimes portrayed as an “easy way out,” with 1 participant describing a perception that using medication for weight loss was a “cop-out” compared with diet and exercise (participant 1), and another participant describing weight loss achieved on treatment as feeling like “cheating” (participant 24). Others described emphasizing a diabetes indication to avoid negative judgement, feeling there is “a difference in how it’s viewed for diabetes vs for weight loss” (participant 26). Despite awareness of stigma, 1 participant explicitly rejected this narrative, saying, “I don’t really care what people think about how I lost weight. If the subject comes up, I’ll shout it from the rooftops that I found it very effective” (participant 4). Insurance coverage tied to diabetes thresholds affected several participants. One individual described being required to stop treatment when their hemoglobin A1c level improved, leading to weight regain, illustrating how diagnostic thresholds shaped both access to medication and perceptions of its legitimacy (participant 7).

Theme 6: Information Provision and Clinical Support Are Essential But Highly Variable

Participants described substantial variability in the information and clinical support that they received when initiating and using GLP-1 RAs. For 1 patient, lack of counseling about adverse effects led to delayed recognition that symptoms of vomiting and diarrhea were medication-related (participant 3). Other participants described brief, transactional clinical encounters in which prescriptions were provided with little discussion or opportunity to ask questions (participant 5). Practical challenges with medication administration were also reported, including difficulties using injection devices, as 1 participant described, “I missed the first lick. All the medicine flew out, and I couldn’t figure the little dial out” (participant 22). Participants expressed a desire for clearer, more practical counseling, contrasting exhaustive lists of rare adverse effects with a need for concrete guidance about what to expect and how to manage common symptoms (participant 22). In contrast, some participants who described clinicians taking time to explain treatment in accessible language and address questions reported greater satisfaction with their care (participant 15).

Theme 7: Access Challenges and Cost Can Be Prohibitive

Participants frequently described access to GLP-1 RAs as complex and financially burdensome, with cost and insurance coverage often presenting major challenges. Several participants emphasized that navigating access often required substantial time, health literacy, and the ability to self-advocate. One participant stated, “I do a lot of research online, and I’m able to communicate and access what can work for me. I think if someone has less of that, they can’t get what they need” (participant 10). Changes in insurance status, including retirement, also precipitated loss of coverage, and 1 participant described stopping GLP-1 RA therapy due to the competing cost of their tuition (participant 2).

Theme 8: Value of Shared Experiences

Many participants described the value of connecting with others and finding “a tribe taking the medication” (participant 21) with whom experiences and information could be shared. Yet sharing of information could be hindered by fear of judgment: 1 participant described initial reluctance to discuss medication use until they discovered a friend was also taking the medication (participant 10). Another described regularly checking in with their daughter, who was also taking a GLP-1 RA, to discuss food intake, hydration, and activity, explaining how this shared effort supported their adoption of healthy behaviors (participant 24). Other participants found their support network online; for example, 1 participant described receiving helpful information from a physician they followed on YouTube regarding dietary advice for patients taking semaglutide (participant 19).

Discussion

Our analysis elucidates how patients experience GLP-1 RA therapy, balancing benefits and trade-offs within the social, clinical, and structural realities of care. Many participants experienced changes in the psychological experience of eating—most notably through reductions in food noise—facilitating more deliberate decision-making around food. However, weight loss was not experienced as a passive or automatic outcome of medication use; it required sustained effort, active lifestyle change, and ongoing negotiation of adverse effects and practical challenges. Individuals’ experiences were highly influenced by the surrounding context, including the quality of clinical care, their social network or perceptions of external judgment, and access challenges of affordability, usually dictated by insurance coverage and health care literacy. Further, the potential or actual physiological impact of GLP-1 RA therapy was observed across themes influencing motivation, willingness to withstand adverse effects, and ultimately decision-making.

We found substantial heterogeneity in adverse effect experiences, which unsurprisingly influenced both perceptions of the medication and decisions about continuation. Notably, some participants were prepared to withstand substantial adverse effects because of the weight loss benefits, consistent with findings from a recent systematic review.11 This concept is also reflected in wider weight loss literature. For example, in a study exploring patients’ willingness to accept risks of weight loss surgery, 20% of 654 patients were willing to accept a mortality risk that was higher than 10%.22 Yet while information provision and clinical support were central to treatment experience and participants’ ability to manage adverse effects, the quality of these supports varied widely. A recent joint advisory emphasized that nutritional and medical management of gastrointestinal tract adverse effects is critical to support GLP-1 RA use, including comprehensive counseling regarding likely adverse effects and mitigation strategies.8 Yet there are currently no standardized guidelines for GLP-1 RA initiation counseling. Standardized guidelines for patient education, including clear recommendations on what should be discussed during clinical encounters when initiating GLP-1 RA therapies, such as expectations, duration of treatment, potential risks, and long-term management planning, could support patients with treatment maintenance. Indeed, maintaining weight loss remains a key challenge in obesity treatment. A 2026 systematic review found that after patients stop taking weight management medications, they typically regain weight rapidly, at a mean of 0.4 kg per month, with all cardiometabolic markers projected to return to baseline within 1.4 years after treatment cessation.23 It is likely that prolonged treatment with GLP-1 RAs may be required to sustain health benefits. A 2023 randomized clinical trial demonstrated successful weight loss maintenance over 4 years with continuous semaglutide treatment.23,24 Further long-term safety and efficacy data will be important in supporting both clinicians and patients to make informed maintenance treatment plans, with clinical trials ongoing.25

Individuals reported feeling motivated by the weight loss that they experienced to make lifestyle changes, which in turn supported their weight loss journey. This concept reflects existing literature, with previous studies proposing that combining healthy diet and physical activity with GLP-1 RA–based pharmacotherapy may enhance endogenous GLP-1 secretion, thereby augmenting treatment response.26 It is also consistent with current guidance stressing the importance of holistic treatment for patients living with obesity. In December 2025, the World Health Organization published a landmark guideline on the use of GLP-1 RA therapies in the treatment of obesity in adults. The guideline recommends that GLP-1 RA treatment should be combined with behavioral therapy addressing lifestyle changes, including physical activity and healthy dietary practices, to maximize and sustain benefits.27

Many participants found valued emotional support and practical advice from online communities, friends, and relatives, complementing formal clinical care. This is consistent with large-scale analyses of social media discussions around GLP-1 RAs, which demonstrate highly active, community-driven engagement. One study identified nearly 400 000 GLP-1 RA-related discussions, with conversations focused on shared experiences of weight loss, adverse effects, access challenges, and psychological impact.28 However, fear of stigma may limit individuals’ access to peer support, whereas institutional framing of GLP-1 RAs through strict eligibility criteria—often anchored to diabetes-related thresholds—appeared to reinforce perceptions that weight loss alone was not a legitimate indication for treatment.

Participants’ accounts underscored that cost and access barriers were not only logistical challenges but also shaped who could realistically benefit from GLP-1 RAs, validating existing concerns about equity and differential access.29 The high expense of therapy has, in turn, driven some patients to seek medications from nonlicensed or unregulated sources, exposing them to heightened risks of serious complications.30 Limited production capacity and distribution challenges also remain major obstacles to equitable access to GLP-1 RAs. However, with Novo Nordisk’s patent on semaglutide expiring in several countries in 2026, including India and China, semaglutide-based drugs could become cheaper and more accessible in the near term.31

Strengths and Limitations

Our multidisciplinary team represents an important strength of the study, with our analysis team including an expert patient with lived experience of GLP-1 RA therapy alongside experienced qualitative researchers and clinicians. This study also has limitations. Participants’ accounts of taking GLP-1 RAs were often described in markedly polarized terms. As with all interview-based qualitative research, selection bias is likely, as individuals with particularly positive or negative experiences may be more motivated to participate, and participants were recruited from ResearchMatch, a pool of individuals already motivated to participate in health studies. This bias may have been amplified by the high public visibility of GLP-1 RAs in the media and the inherently emotive nature of weight and weight loss, which may have shaped both participants’ willingness to engage in the study and the intensity of perspectives expressed. Importantly, the inclusion of 7 participants who had discontinued GLP-1 RA therapy and from individuals living across the US contributed to the diversity of perspectives captured to minimize selection bias.

Conclusions

In this qualitative study of patient experiences with GLP-1 RA therapy, we found that GLP-1 RAs functioned as a facilitator rather than a replacement for personal effort and lifestyle change, highlighting the importance of a holistic approach to weight management. Standardized guidelines for patient education and clinical support with GLP-1 RAs may help improve quality and consistency of care, leading to better expectation management around likely adverse effects, mitigation strategies, and long-term management. Future research could also explore the role of digital support, for example, a digital health companion, for adverse effect counseling, adherence, and health care coaching in acknowledgment of expanding health care needs and limited physician capacity. Further, we emphasize the need not only for equitable access to GLP-1 RAs and comprehensive medical support but for long-term health promotion efforts and obesity prevention policies. Finally, anchoring of GLP-1 RA eligibility criteria to diabetes-related thresholds appeared to reinforce perceptions that weight loss alone is not a legitimate justification for treatment. Reevaluation of eligibility frameworks may help to mitigate societal stigma surrounding the use of pharmacological therapies for weight management.
