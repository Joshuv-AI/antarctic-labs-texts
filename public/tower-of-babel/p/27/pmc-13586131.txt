Ablation of the renal tubular gluconeogenic enzyme PCK1 drives AKI-to-CKD transition by negatively regulating the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway
Authors: Jing-Xuan Gan, Meng-Fan Li, Hai-Hua Deng, Qiu-Tong Liu, Zhi-Xiao Chen, Chun-Ling Liang, Li-Xin Wang, Xu-Sheng Liu, Yue-Yu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe transition from acute kidney injury (AKI) to chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by progressive renal fibrosis, yet the key tubular regulatory factors driving this maladaptive repair remain inadequately defined. In this study, we investigated the role of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1), the rate-limiting enzyme of gluconeogenesis, in the AKI-to-CKD transition. Analysis of human single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets revealed a profound downregulation of PCK1 in injured proximal tubule cells, which correlated with the upregulation of pro-fibrotic markers. In rodent models, we demonstrated that the loss of PCK1 precedes significant collagen deposition and acts as a primary driver of fibrogenesis. Mechanistically, PCK1 deficiency enhanced TGF-β1-induced Smad3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, whereas PCK1 overexpression suppressed Smad3 activation as well as the expression of fibrosis-related genes and proteins. Our functional assays supported that PCK1 serves as an endogenous negative regulator and antagonist of Smad3, its depletion releases the restraint on Smad3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, leading to the loss of tubular epithelial identity. Significantly, the pharmacological or genetic restoration of PCK1 effectively blunted Smad3 activation and attenuated renal fibrosis in vitro. These findings identify PCK1 as a critical metabolic regulator that maintains tubular homeostasis and suggest that PCK1-targeted modulation of Smad3 signaling may be a promising therapeutic strategy for arresting the progression of chronic kidney disease following acute injury.Graphical abstract PCK1 inhibits tubular Smad3 signaling and fibrotic progression during AKI-to-CKD transition. Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a prevalent clinical syndrome characterized by a sudden decline in renal function, with diagnosis standardized by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria based on serum creatinine elevations and urine output [1, 2]. While historically viewed as a transient event, AKI is now recognized as a primary catalyst for chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [3]. This deleterious AKI-to-CKD transition is fundamentally driven by maladaptive repair mechanisms, following the initial insult, persistent cellular stress triggers a pathological cascade of tubular atrophy, chronic inflammation, and progressive fibrosis, ultimately leading to permanent structural failure. The hallmark of this transition is tubulointerstitial fibrosis (TIF) [4], which represents the final common pathway for nearly all forms of progressive kidney disease and serves as a primary determinant of long-term renal prognosis [5, 6]. Consequently, elucidating the molecular drivers of TIF is of paramount importance for developing targeted therapeutic interventions to halt the AKI-to-CKD transition.

Proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTCs), the most metabolically active cells in the kidney, are both the primary targets of initial injury and the central epigenetic and signaling regulators of pro-fibrotic signaling during failed repair [7, 8]. Under physiological conditions, PTCs rely heavily on fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) to meet their high energy demands [9, 10]. However, injured PTCs undergo a profound metabolic shift characterized by impaired FAO and a pathological switch from gluconeogenesis toward glycolysis [11]. This bioenergetic collapse not only leads to a persistent energy crisis but also directly facilitates the acquisition of a partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) phenotype and cellular senescence, thereby fueling the fibrotic program [12]. Yet, while research has focused extensively on FAO and glycolysis, the role of gluconeogenesis, another metabolic hallmark exclusively localized to the proximal tubule [13], remains understudied in the context of renal fibrosis.

With the proximal tubule serving as a critical site for gluconeogenesis, the kidney possesses a unique metabolic signature. While traditionally considered a liver-centric process, the kidneys are now recognized to contribute up to 28% of systemic glucose in the post-absorptive state and nearly 50% under stress conditions [13, 14]. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1) serves as the primary rate-limiting enzyme of this pathway. Although PCK1 is characteristically downregulated across various forms of renal injury, its biological significance has historically been viewed through the narrow lens of metabolic flux [15]. Kidney proximal tubule cells display high expression of this enzyme, whose importance is currently not well defined. The TGF-β/Smad3 signaling is the master regulator of fibrogenesis [16]. However, whether PCK1 functions as a molecular bridge to repress the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling remains an unexplored frontier. Elucidating this potential metabolic mechanism is essential for understanding how tubular identity is maintained and how its loss drives progressive renal failure.

In the present study, we sought to define the protective role of PCK1 in the AKI-to-CKD transition and unravel its underlying molecular mechanisms. By leveraging a human single-cell RNA sequencing data [17], we first characterized the PTC-specific loss of PCK1 in clinical AKI samples and its correlation with fibrotic markers. We then utilized a cisplatin-induced AKI-to-CKD mouse model to establish the spatiotemporal relationship between PCK1 depletion, hemodynamic impairment, and the progression of fibrosis. Finally, through co-IP, bidirectional genetic modulation and pharmacological rescue experiments using the Smad3 inhibitor SIS3, we demonstrated that PCK1 preserves the tubular phenotype by specifically antagonizing Smad3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. These results suggest that targeting PCK1 may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent progressive renal failure.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals suppressed gluconeogenesis and PCK1 loss in human AKI

To decipher the transcriptomic landscape and metabolic reprogramming in the clinical context of renal injury, we analyzed a scRNA-seq dataset (GSE183276) from human kidney biopsies. Rigorous quality control (QC) was performed to ensure data integrity, with consistent distributions of mitochondrial gene percentages, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, and gene detection across all samples (Fig. S1). UMAP clustering identified major renal cell populations, including PT, TAL, DCT, IMM, EC, PC, IC, and POD clusters (Fig. 1A). The volcano plot highlighted a significant downregulation of key gluconeogenic enzymes (PCK1, PCK2, and G6PC), alongside a marked upregulation of pro-fibrotic and injury markers (VIM and TIMP1) (Fig. 1B). Consistent with these expression changes, GSEA demonstrated a robust suppression of the gluconeogenesis pathway (NES = -1.74, p.adj < 0.01), indicating a marked suppression of the gluconeogenic transcriptional program in injured renal epithelial cells (Fig. 1C and Fig. S2). Statistical evaluation of PTCs confirmed the extreme significance of these alterations, with gluconeogenesis-related genes exhibiting a reciprocal relationship with activated fibrosis-associated genes (Fig. 1D and Fig. S3). Notably, PTC-specific analysis of PCK1 within the PT population revealed a dramatic dual decline in both expression intensity and frequency, with the proportion of PCK1-positive PTCs plummeting from 64.2% to 23.9% (Fig. 1E). These clinical transcriptomic findings identify PCK1 loss as a prominent metabolic feature of injured proximal tubular cells in human AKI.

A cisplatin-induced mouse model recapitulates the progressive renal dysfunction

Building upon the clinical evidence of metabolic collapse, we established a cisplatin-induced mouse model to investigate the progressive nature of renal injury (Fig. 2A). Following cisplatin administration, mice exhibited a steady decline in body weight (Fig. 2C) and a concomitant deterioration of renal function, serum UREA and SCr levels significantly increased from week 3 (Fig. 2D, E). Representative Doppler ultrasound images of the kidneys displayed the progressive morphological and hemodynamic alterations following cisplatin administration (Fig. 2B, the upper panel). Notably, a gradual reduction in renal volume was observed over the 4-week course, characterized by gross morphological atrophy and increased echogenicity of the renal parenchyma as the injury transitioned toward fibrosis (Fig. 2B, the lower panel). The Resistive Index (RI) and Pulsatility Index (PI) exhibited an increase throughout the 4-week course, reflecting increased vascular resistance associated with advancing tubular remodeling (Fig. 2F, G). The model was accompanied by a time-dependent accumulation of serum and urinary injury biomarkers, including NGAL and KIM-1 (Fig. 2H-K). Collectively, these data confirm that the cisplatin model effectively recapitulates the progressive injury observed in human patients.

Progression of renal fibrosis is accompanied by a sustained loss of PCK1 and PCK1 downregulation is an early and persistent event during TGF-β1-induced pro-fibrotic transition

To define the spatiotemporal correlation between metabolic failure and fibrogenesis, we performed a comprehensive analysis of kidney tissues from cisplatin-treated mice. Masson’s staining and IHC revealed progressive interstitial collagen deposition and ECM accumulation, with marked elevations observed during the later stages (week 3–4) (Fig. 3A-C). In striking contrast, IHC analysis showed a rapid and steady decline in PCK1 protein within the tubular compartment, with significant loss detectable as early as week 1 (Fig. 3D). This reciprocal relationship was further validated at the transcriptional level, where the early downregulation of gluconeogenic genes (Fbp1, G6pc, and Pck1) closely mirrored the induction of pro-fibrotic markers (Col1a1, Col3a1, and Fn1) (Fig. 3E-J). Heatmap analysis highlighted this systematic collapse of the metabolic machinery alongside the activation of the fibrotic program (Fig. 3K). The robust increase in mesenchymal markers (Vimentin, α-SMA, and COL1A1) and the marked reduction of PCK1, G6PC, and p-FBP1 was confirmed by western blot (Fig. 3L). These findings suggest that the loss of PCK1 is an early event that is tightly synchronized with the development of renal fibrosis in vivo.

IF staining of whole kidneys sections revealed a drastic reduction in PCK1 signal primarily within the renal cortex of CP-4 W mice, which was accompanied by massive interstitial fibronectin accumulation (Fig. 4A-C). Remarkably, merged images exhibited a striking reciprocal spatial distribution between the loss of PCK1 and the deposition of fibronectin. Notably, a robust negative correlation was observed between PCK1 and Fibronectin expression (R2 = 0.589, p < 0.0001), suggesting that PCK1 depletion was statistically associated with the fibrotic burden (Fig. 4D). In vitro, TGF-β1 stimulation induced a rapid and time-dependent decline in PCK1 protein, inversely mirroring the upregulation of Vimentin and Fibronectin (Fig. 4E). Densitometric analysis confirmed that PCK1 reduction began as early as 2 h post-treatment and reached its nadir by 72 h (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that PCK1 downregulation is not merely a consequence but an early event in the pro-fibrotic transition.

To validate this finding in an independent AKI-to-CKD model, we also assessed renal PCK1 expression in mice subjected to bilateral renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (bIRI). One month post-surgery, bIRI induced progressive tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis, with significant collagen deposition in the bIRI-35 min group (Fig. S4A, B). α-SMA immunostaining was significantly elevated in the 30- and 35-min ischemia groups, indicating myofibroblast activation during chronic post-ischemic remodeling (Fig. S4A, C). Conversely, PCK1 immunostaining declined with increasing ischemia duration, reaching significance in the bIRI-35 min group (Fig. S4A, D). Western blot analysis confirmed a significant reduction in renal PCK1 protein expression in the bIRI-35 min group (Fig. S4E). Together, these findings indicate that PCK1 downregulation is a common feature of AKI-to-CKD progression, occurring across both cisplatin-induced nephrotoxic and ischemic renal injury models.

Pharmacological modulation of PCK1 activity regulates TGF-β1-induced pro-fibrotic responses

To determine whether PCK1 plays a causal role in driving renal fibrosis, we employed a pharmacological approach to either inhibit or stimulate its activity in the presence of TGF-β1. Human renal tubular cells were treated with SKF34288 (a specific PCK1 inhibitor) or glucagon (a stimulator of gluconeogenesis and PCK1 expression). IF staining revealed that SKF34288 significantly exacerbated the TGF-β1-induced increase in Vimentin expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). Conversely, the administration of glucagon (100 and 200 nM) effectively attenuated the fibrotic response and partially restored PCK1 protein levels, as evidenced by reduced Vimentin intensity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A).

These findings were further corroborated by protein and mRNA expression analyses. Immunoblotting confirmed that the inhibition of PCK1 by SKF34288 further elevated the levels of Vimentin, COL3A1, and α-SMA in a dose-responsive manner (0.25 µM and 1 µM), while glucagon treatment dose-dependently suppressed these markers and simultaneously replenishing PCK1 expression (Fig. 5B). At the transcriptional level, RT-qPCR analysis showed that the TGF-β1-induced mRNA expression of COL1A1, COL3A1, VIM, and CTGF was consistently enhanced by SKF34288 but markedly reduced by glucagon (Fig. 5C-F).

To further examine whether PCK1 loss occurred within proximal tubular regions showing pro-fibrotic Smad3 activation, we performed supplementary multiplex immunofluorescence staining for PCK1, p-Smad3, LTL, and DAPI. In CP-4 W kidneys, PCK1 signal was reduced in LTL-positive proximal tubular areas, whereas p-Smad3 signal was markedly increased in the same regions. Quantitative analysis confirmed decreased PCK1 expression and significantly elevated p-Smad3 expression specifically in LTL-positive regions of CP-4 W kidneys, while p-Smad3 levels in LTL-negative regions remained comparable to control levels (Fig. 5G, H). These data provide additional spatial evidence linking PCK1 loss to Smad3 activation in injured proximal tubules.

Taken together, these data provide functional evidence that pharmacological preservation of PCK1 activity can mitigate the pro-fibrotic program in renal tubular cells, positioning PCK1 as a potential therapeutic target for preventing the AKI-to-CKD transition.

Bidirectional regulation of PCK1 modulates TGF-β1-induced renal tubular fibrosis via the Smad3 pathway

To establish the functional necessity and sufficiency of PCK1 in renal tubular fibrosis, we performed loss and gain-of-function experiments in human renal tubular cells. Notably, immunofluorescence staining revealed increased α-SMA and p-Smad3 expression in PCK1-deficient cells (Fig. 6A). Crucially, fluorescence intensity profiling showed that shPCK1 markedly enhanced the nuclear accumulation of p-Smad3 in response to TGF-β1 (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the p-Smad3 nuclear translocation ratio in the shPCK1 group compared to the control vector group (Fig. 6C). Stable knockdown of PCK1 (shPCK1) significantly exacerbated the pro-fibrotic response triggered by TGF-β1, as shown by increased expression of multiple fibrotic markers, including protein markers (Fibronectin, α-SMA, Vimentin, COL1A1) and mRNAs (FN1, ACTA2, VIM, COL4A1, COL1A1, COL3A1, CTGF) (Fig. 6D-K). Vimentin expression was also elevated after PCK1 knockdown under TGF-β1 stimulation; however, the magnitude of change at the protein level was not completely proportional to that observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 6D and G). This difference may reflect post-transcriptional regulation, differences in protein stability, or assay-dependent dynamic ranges between western blotting and RT-qPCR. Importantly, both readouts supported the same overall trend that PCK1 deficiency promotes a pro-fibrotic phenotypic shift, and this conclusion was further supported by the coordinated changes in other fibrotic markers and by enhanced p-Smad3 nuclear translocation.

Conversely, stable overexpression of PCK1 (OE-PCK1) conferred a robust protective effect against TGF-β1-induced injury. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that OE-PCK1 effectively suppressed the accumulation of mesenchymal markers and extracellular matrix proteins (Fig. 7A). Line scan analysis of fluorescence intensity revealed that the TGF-β1-induced nuclear translocation of p-Smad3 was substantially diminished by PCK1 overexpression (Fig. 7B). This was further supported by statistical quantification, which showed that OE-PCK1 significantly reduced the p-Smad3 nuclear translocation ratio (Fig.7C). Consistent with these findings, the level of fibrotic genes and proteins were significantly attenuated in cells overexpressing PCK1 (Fig. 7D-K). These results suggest that PCK1 serves as a critical endogenous antagonist of TGF-β/Smad3 mediated fibrogenesis by restricting the nuclear translocation of p-Smad3.

In parallel with these signaling and fibrotic alterations, ATP and lactate release measurements showed that PCK1 knockdown decreased intracellular ATP levels and increased lactate release, whereas PCK1 overexpression increased ATP levels and reduced lactate release in HK-2 cells treated with or without TGF-β1 (Figs. 6L and M and 7L and M). These results indicate that PCK1 helps maintain cellular energy metabolism and lactate homeostasis during TGF-β1-induced tubular injury. Collectively, these bidirectional findings confirm that PCK1 is essential for maintaining the tubular phenotype and its loss is a key driver of the pro-fibrotic transition.

PCK1 regulates the pro-fibrotic transition of renal tubular cells through the Smad3 signaling

To verify whether Smad3 is the definitive downstream mediator of PCK1-regulated renal fibrosis, we employed SIS3, a specific pharmacological inhibitor of Smad3 phosphorylation, in both PCK1 overexpressing and PCK1 knockdown cell models.

In the gain-of-function model, both OE-PCK1 and SIS3 treatment significantly blunted the TGF-β1 induced upregulation of fibrotic markers. Western blot analysis showed that while both interventions independently reduced the protein levels of Fibronectin, α-SMA, Vimentin, and p-Smad3. OE-PCK1 + SIS3 treatment resulted in an additive inhibitory effect, yielding the lowest expression levels of mesenchymal proteins (Fig. 8A). This synergy was further reflected at the transcriptional level, where the mRNA expression of ACTA2, VIM, FN1, and COL1A1 was most robustly suppressed in the combined treatment group compared to either OE-PCK1 or SIS3 alone (Fig. 8B-E).

Conversely, in the loss-of-function model, the pro-fibrotic phenotype induced by PCK1 deficiency was effectively rescued by pharmacological Smad3 inhibition. As previously observed, shPCK1 significantly enhanced the TGF-β1-induced expression of Fibronectin, α-SMA, Vimentin, and the activation of p-Smad3 (Fig. 8F). However, the administration of SIS3 abrogated this exacerbation, restoring the expression of these markers to levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 8F). RT-qPCR analysis corroborated this functional rescue, showing that SIS3 treatment significantly reversed the high-magnitude induction of FN1, ACTA2, VIM, CTGF, and collagen genes subtypes caused by PCK1 knockdown (Fig. 8G-M). Collectively, these data provide compelling mechanistic evidence that PCK1 maintains the tubular epithelial phenotype and inhibits fibrosis specifically by repressing the activation of the Smad3 signaling pathway.

To determine whether PCK1 associates with Smad3, FLAG-tagged PCK1 was overexpressed in HK-2 cells and co-immunoprecipitation was performed. Smad3 was detected in anti-FLAG immunoprecipitates from OE-PCK1 cells but not in those from OE-Vec cells or the IgG control, demonstrating a specific physical association between PCK1 and Smad3 (Fig. 8N). Collectively, these findings suggest that PCK1 suppresses TGF-β1-induced pro-fibrotic signaling by interacting with Smad3 and inhibiting its downstream activation, thereby maintaining the tubular epithelial phenotype.

Discussion

The maintenance of renal gluconeogenesis is paramount for glucose homeostasis under stress conditions such as AKI [18]. In the present study, we systematically characterized the spatiotemporal dynamics of PCK1 loss, the rate-limiting enzyme of gluconeogenesis, and established its causal role in the AKI-to-CKD transition. Consistent with the framework proposed by Faivre et al. [19], our data show that PCK1 downregulation occurs early after kidney injury. By integrating human scRNA-seq analysis, a longitudinal cisplatin-induced AKI-to-CKD model, and supplementary evidence from bilateral ischemia-reperfusion injury, we found that PCK1 loss was predominantly localized to injured proximal tubular regions and preceded overt extracellular matrix deposition and the widespread induction of fibrotic markers. This temporal pattern suggests that PCK1 loss is unlikely to be merely a passive consequence of advanced fibrosis. Rather, it may represent an upstream tubular event accompanying the transition from adaptive repair to maladaptive remodeling.

Beyond its role in basal metabolic homeostasis, PCK1 has recently attracted attention as a protective factor in kidney disease. Restoration of PCK1-related pathways has been reported to attenuate inflammation and fibrotic progression in IgA nephropathy [20], whereas promotion of PGC-1α/PCK1-mediated renal gluconeogenesis has been shown to suppress renal fibrosis [21]. These findings are consistent with the role of PCK1 in CKD-associated metabolic remodeling, mitochondrial maintenance, and tubular homeostasis [13–15, 22]. Outside the kidney, PCK1 dysregulation has also been implicated in cancer metabolism, neuronal injury, hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, liver fibrosis, and fasting-associated protection against NASH and hepatocarcinogenesis [23–27]. Thus, PCK1 may function not only as a metabolic enzyme but also as a broader regulator of cellular quiescence and tissue homeostasis.

From the classical metabolic perspective, PCK1 catalyzes the conversion of oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate and participates in gluconeogenesis and cataplerosis [22, 28, 29]. Accordingly, PCK1 loss may be associated with reduced ATP generation, altered lactate handling, disrupted tricarboxylic acid cycle homeostasis, and mitochondrial dysfunction. However, recent studies of PCK1 indicate that its functions are not limited to the regulation of gluconeogenic metabolic flux. Several reviews and mechanistic studies have proposed non-canonical functions of PCK1 beyond gluconeogenesis, including participation in protein-protein interactions, regulation of signaling complexes, lipid metabolic remodeling, gene expression, and epigenetic regulation [23, 30, 31]. Of note, PCK1 has been reported to function as a protein kinase that uses GTP as a phosphate donor to phosphorylate INSIG1/2 and thereby regulate lipogenesis, providing an important molecular paradigm for non-metabolic functions of PCK1 through direct protein interactions or signaling regulation [30]. These findings suggest that PCK1 may act not only as an effector of proximal tubular metabolic homeostasis but also as a signaling regulator involved in cell fate determination.

In this context, the major mechanistic advance of the present study is that the protective effect of PCK1 is not confined to its metabolic role. We found that PCK1 functions as a negative regulator of the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway, a central pathway driving renal fibrosis, loss of tubular epithelial phenotype, myofibroblast activation, inflammation, and extracellular matrix deposition [16]. In vitro, PCK1 knockdown enhanced TGF-β1-induced Smad3 phosphorylation and p-Smad3 nuclear translocation, accompanied by increased expression of fibronectin, α-SMA, vimentin, CTGF, and multiple collagen subtypes. Conversely, PCK1 overexpression attenuated Smad3 activation, reduced nuclear accumulation of p-Smad3, and suppressed pro-fibrotic transcriptional and protein responses. These bidirectional genetic data indicate that PCK1 is not merely a marker of proximal tubular metabolic status but also an endogenous brake on Smad3 signaling that helps preserve the tubular epithelial phenotype. The co-immunoprecipitation data provide further molecular support for the regulatory relationship between PCK1 and Smad3. In HK-2 cells overexpressing FLAG-tagged PCK1, Smad3 was detected in anti-FLAG immunoprecipitates, whereas little Smad3 signal was observed in empty vector or IgG control groups. These findings indicate that PCK1 associates with Smad3, or at least resides in the same Smad3-containing protein complex.

The SIS3 rescue experiments further demonstrate that Smad3 is a key downstream mediator of the pro-fibrotic effects caused by PCK1 deficiency. SIS3 was initially identified as a selective inhibitor of Smad3 function that attenuates TGF-β1-induced Smad3 phosphorylation and Smad3-Smad4 interaction [32]. Previous studies have shown that SIS3 ameliorates fibrosis, apoptosis, and inflammation in UUO kidneys by inhibiting TGF-β/Smad3 signaling [33], and that combined blockade of Smad3 and JNK pathways suppresses progressive interstitial fibrosis in folic acid nephropathy [34]. In this study, SIS3 effectively reversed the excessive induction of fibronectin, α-SMA, vimentin, CTGF, and collagen genes caused by PCK1 knockdown under TGF-β1 stimulation. These results indicate that the pro-fibrotic phenotype induced by PCK1 loss is largely dependent on Smad3 activation. Therefore, the co-immunoprecipitation and SIS3 rescue experiments together support a working model in which PCK1 associates with Smad3-containing protein complexes and limits Smad3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, whereas PCK1 loss may weaken this endogenous restraint and amplify TGF-β1-induced tubular pro-fibrotic remodeling.

Recent work on PCK1 and liver fibrosis also provides a useful reference for understanding the anti-fibrotic role of PCK1. Novoa et al. reported that PCK1 loss in hepatic stellate cells promotes cell activation, glycolytic remodeling, enhanced tricarboxylic acid cycle fueling, and collagen production, thereby aggravating liver fibrosis [26]. This finding suggests that PCK1 downregulation may represent a shared metabolic abnormality across fibrotic processes in different organs. In contrast to hepatic stellate cells, which acquire a pro-fibrotic activated state mainly through metabolic remodeling, the present study focuses on renal proximal tubular epithelial cells and further shows that PCK1 loss not only reflects impaired tubular metabolic homeostasis but also releases an intrinsic restraint on TGF-β/Smad3 signaling, thereby promoting tubular pro-fibrotic phenotypic transition. Thus, findings from the liver and kidney are complementary rather than contradictory. The former emphasizes a PCK1 loss-metabolic remodeling-stellate cell activation axis, whereas the latter highlights a PCK1 loss-Smad3 disinhibition-tubular pro-fibrotic transition axis. Therefore, these studies suggest that PCK1 may limit fibrotic progression through both metabolism-dependent and signaling-regulatory mechanisms.

On this basis, whether PCK1 loss further contributes to tubular cell senescence remains an important question. The AKI-to-CKD transition is frequently accompanied by G2/M cell-cycle arrest, DNA damage responses, and cellular senescence in tubular epithelial cells. A landmark study demonstrated a causal relationship between epithelial G2/M arrest after injury and fibrotic outcomes [35]. In addition, recent evidence indicates that persistent tubular senescence after repeated low-dose cisplatin nephrotoxicity promotes maladaptive repair and CKD progression [36]. Given that Smad3 signaling may intersect with cell-cycle regulation and senescence-associated pathways, our study shows enhanced Smad3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation under PCK1 deficiency, it is reasonable to speculate that PCK1 loss may not only promote tubular pro-fibrotic phenotypic transition but also participate in G2/M arrest or cellular senescence through Smad3-dependent mechanisms. However, this possibility should be interpreted cautiously because the present study did not directly assess senescence- or cell-cycle-related markers such as p16, p21, SA-β-gal, γH2AX, Cyclin B1, or p-H3. Future studies combining PCK1 gain- and loss-of-function models, SIS3 intervention, and senescence marker analysis will help clarify whether the PCK1-Smad3 axis participates in tubular senescence and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) formation during maladaptive repair after AKI.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we integrated human scRNA-seq analysis, a longitudinal cisplatin-induced AKI-to-CKD model, supplementary bilateral ischemia-reperfusion injury data, and in vitro gain- and loss-of-function experiments, the causal role of PCK1 remains to be further validated in vivo. In particular, proximal tubule-specific Pck1 conditional knockout and overexpression models will be required to determine whether tubular PCK1 is sufficient and necessary to regulate fibrotic remodeling within the intact renal microenvironment. Additional AKI-to-CKD and CKD models, such as UUO and folic acid nephropathy will also help establish whether the protective effect of PCK1 is generalizable across different etiologies of renal fibrosis. Second, the human evidence in this study is mainly based on public single-cell transcriptomic data and therefore remains correlative. Future studies using independent clinical cohorts, spatial transcriptomics, immunostaining of human biopsy samples, and longitudinal patient data would strengthen the clinical relevance of tubular PCK1 loss during AKI-to-CKD progression. Third, although our results suggest that PCK1 loss is associated with metabolic dysfunction, the metabolic mechanism was not fully resolved. ATP and lactate measurements provide useful functional readouts but cannot define carbon flux, mitochondrial substrate utilization, or specific metabolic intermediates linking PCK1 depletion to Smad3 activation. Targeted metabolomics, LC-MS-based flux analysis, stable isotope tracing, and mitochondrial functional assays are needed to clarify how PCK1-dependent gluconeogenesis or cataplerosis contributes to tubular homeostasis and fibrotic signaling. Fourth, although co-immunoprecipitation supports an association between PCK1 and Smad3, this result does not prove a direct physical interaction. Endogenous and reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation, domain-mapping experiments, GST pull-down assays, purified protein binding assays, and structural analyses will be required to determine whether PCK1 directly binds Smad3 and how this interaction affects Smad3 phosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and transcriptional activity. Finally, it remains unclear whether the inhibitory effect of PCK1 on Smad3 signaling depends on its enzymatic activity, cataplerotic function, subcellular localization, or non-canonical protein-interaction capacity. Future studies using catalytically inactive PCK1 mutants, localization-defective mutants, and Smad3-interaction-deficient mutants will be important for distinguishing the metabolic and non-metabolic mechanisms by which PCK1 suppresses renal fibrogenesis. Together, addressing these issues will further refine the mechanistic model proposed here and help determine whether the PCK1-Smad3 axis can be translated into a therapeutic strategy for preventing maladaptive repair after AKI.

In summary, this study suggests that PCK1 loss is an early proximal tubular event during the AKI-to-CKD transition. Although PCK1 has well-established roles in gluconeogenesis and cataplerosis, and its loss is associated with altered energy- and lactate-related metabolic states, our findings further support a non-canonical anti-fibrotic mechanism in which PCK1 may act as an endogenous restraint on TGF-β/Smad3 signaling. By associating with Smad3-containing protein complexes and limiting Smad3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, PCK1 preserves the tubular epithelial phenotype and suppresses pro-fibrotic remodeling. Therefore, PCK1 should not be viewed solely as a proximal tubular metabolic enzyme or gluconeogenic marker, but also as a molecular node linking tubular metabolic status to fibrotic signal transduction. Targeting the PCK1-Smad3 regulatory axis may provide a potential strategy to limit maladaptive tubular repair and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, although further in vivo and translational validation is needed before implications for CKD prevention after AKI can be established.

Conclusion

PCK1 acts as a metabolic regulator preventing the AKI-to-CKD transition by antagonizing the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling. Our study establishes PCK1 loss as a primary driver of renal fibrosis linking tubular metabolic status to Smad3 signaling and identifies tubular PCK1 loss as an early and functionally relevant event during AKI-to-CKD transition. Restoring PCK1 levels abrogates pro-fibrotic signaling and preserves tubular epithelial identity. These findings highlight PCK1 as a potent therapeutic target and suggest that metabolic restoration represents a promising paradigm for arresting CKD progression following acute injury.

Materials and methods

Single-Cell RNA sequencing data acquisition and pre-processing

The human single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE183276 was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. This dataset contains kidney biopsy samples from patients with acute kidney injury (AKI, n = 12) and healthy controls (HC, n = 20) [17]; Chronic kidney disease samples were not included in the present study. Data processing was carried out using the Seurat package (v5.4.0) in R (v4.4.2). A Seurat object was created from the raw count matrix with parameters min.cells = 3 and min. features = 200. For the whole kidney cell atlas analysis, genes detected in fewer than 100 cells were removed to reduce noise before further processing. Quality control (QC) retained cells with 500–5000 detected genes and a mitochondrial gene percentage below 25%. Five proximal tubule (PT) subtypes (PT-S1/2, PT-S3, cycPT, dPT, aPT) were extracted based on the original annotation (subclass.l3).

Dimensionality reduction and cell clustering

For the PT-focused pseudo-bulk analysis, the gene expression matrix was normalized using the NormalizeData function (scale factor = 10,000). Highly variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures (selection.method = “vst”, nfeatures = 2000) and the data were scaled using ScaleData. For the whole kidney cell atlas, normalization and variance stabilization were performed with the SCTransform function (v2), which simultaneously models UMI counts and mitigates technical noise; the top 3000 variable features were retained. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was run on the variable genes, and the first 30 principal components were used as input for Harmony to correct for batch effects arising from patient, library, experiment, and specimen (θ = 2 for each batch variable). After correction, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) were applied to the Harmony-corrected embeddings. Batch correction efficacy was quantified using the Local Inverse Simpson Index (LISI) for disease groups (cLISI) and for batch variables (iLISI).

Differential expression analysis (Pseudo-bulk)

To identify transcriptional alterations in AKI, pseudo-bulk differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Raw counts were aggregated per patient and disease group to construct pseudo-bulk expression matrices; patients with fewer than five cells were excluded. The design formula ~ disease_group was used, with HC as the reference level. Genes with an absolute log₂fold change > 0.25 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed (DEGs). Analyses were conducted for three cell sets: (i) all kidney cells, (ii) combined PT cells (all five subtypes), and (iii) each PT subtype separately.

Functional enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To delineate pathways dysregulated in AKI, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the DEG lists using the clusterProfiler package. For GSEA, all genes were pre-ranked by their log₂fold change and analyzed against gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, v25.1.1), including Hallmark, GO, and KEGG collections. In addition, custom gene sets for gluconeogenesis (12 genes) and fibrosis (32 genes) were compiled from the literature. Enrichment significance was determined by an adjusted p < 0.05.

Visualization of key genes

Expression patterns of gluconeogenic and fibrotic genes were visualized across cell types. Volcano plots highlighted DEGs in all kidney cells and in combined PT cells. Bar charts displayed log₂fold changes for target genes in each PT subtype, with significance indicated by asterisks. Violin plots illustrated the distribution of PCK1 expression and the proportion of expressing cells in HC and AKI groups. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 (adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method).

Animals and experimental protocol

Male specific-pathogen-free C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 20–25 g were purchased from Guangdong Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (license no. SCXK 2022-0063). Animals were housed at the Guangdong Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard chow and sterilized water.

To induce the AKI-to-CKD transition model, mice were randomly assigned after acclimatization to five groups (n = 6 per group): Control, CP-1 W, CP-2 W, CP-3 W, and CP-4 W. All mice were maintained for the same 28-day experimental period and received intraperitoneal injections once weekly on days 0, 7, 14, and 21. The Control group received an equal volume of saline at all four time points. The CP-1 W group received cisplatin only on day 21 and saline on days 0, 7, and 14; the CP-2 W group received cisplatin on days 14 and 21 and saline on days 0 and 7; the CP-3 W group received cisplatin on days 7, 14, and 21 and saline on day 0; and the CP-4 W group received cisplatin on days 0, 7, 14, and 21. Cisplatin (Cayman Chemical, 13119) was dissolved in saline and administered intraperitoneally at 8 mg/kg. All groups were sacrificed on day 28, one week after the final injection, and blood and kidney tissues were collected at this common endpoint (Fig. 2A). Body weight was recorded on each injection day and again at the time of sample collection. Part of each kidney was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, whereas the remainder was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃.

A bilateral renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (bIRI) model was used for supplementary validation (Fig S4). A separate cohort of male specific-pathogen-free C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 20–25 g were randomly assigned to the Sham group or bIRI groups subjected to 20, 25, 30, or 35 min of bilateral renal ischemia, with n = 4 mice per group. Under isoflurane anesthesia and body temperature maintenance, both renal pedicles were exposed and clamped with non-traumatic microvascular clamps for the indicated durations, followed by reperfusion after clamp removal. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical exposure without renal pedicle clamping. Kidney tissues were harvested one month after surgery and processed using the same procedures described above for the cisplatin-induced AKI-to-CKD model.

Renal function and injury markers assessment

Before endpoint, mice were housed individually in metabolic cages equipped with grid bottoms and feces separators with free access to food and water. After 1–2 h of adaptation, 24 h urine collection was initiated. Urine was transferred into centrifuge tubes, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10–15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C. Serum SCr and UREA, were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer or commercial assay kits (Ray Biotech, ELM-TIM1-1, ELM-Lipocalin2-1). Concentrations of the renal injury markers neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) and kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) were quantified with specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions .

Renal ultrasound imaging

Following previously established protocols [37], renal blood flow was assessed using a Vevo 2100 imaging system (Fujifilm Visual Sonics) equipped with a high-frequency linear-array transducer (21–40 MHz). Under isoflurane anesthesia and thermostatic control, two-dimensional B-mode imaging was optimized to delineate the renal parenchyma. Renal resistive index (RI) and pulsatility index (PI) were derived from pulsed-wave Doppler spectra. To ensure data representativeness, measurements were averaged over at least three noise-free cardiac cycles for each subject.

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Fixed kidney tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were stained with Masson’s trichrome to assess collagen deposition and fibrosis. For immunohistochemistry, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval sodium citrate antigen-retrieval solution. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COL1A1 (CST, 72026, 1:200), fibronectin (Affinity, AF5335, 1:200), and PCK1 (Affinity, DF6770, 1:200). Sections were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit. Images were captured under a light microscope and positive staining areas were semi-quantified using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using sodium citrate antigen-retrieval solution. After cooling to room temperature, sections were washed with PBS and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature.

For PCK1/fibronectin co-staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-PCK1 antibody (Affinity, DF6770, 1:200), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary reagent and TSA signal amplification. After antibody stripping by reheating in sodium citrate buffer, sections were re-blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-fibronectin antibody (Affinity, AF5335, 1:200). Fibronectin was then visualized using a different TSA fluorophore. For PCK1/p-Smad3/LTL multiplex staining, sections were first stained with anti-PCK1 antibody (Affinity, DF6770, 1:200) using TSA amplification. After antibody stripping and re-blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-phospho-Smad3 antibody (Affinity, AF8315, 1:200), followed by detection with another TSA fluorophore. Proximal tubules were labeled with FITC-conjugated lotus tetragonolobus lectin (LTL; Invitrogen, L32480, 1:200). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Images were acquired using an Olympus IX83 + DP80.

Cell culture and treatments

HK-2 human proximal tubular epithelial cells (Procell, CL-0109) were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in 5% CO2. When cells reached approximately 70%–80% confluence, they were passaged with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, and cells in logarithmic growth phase were used for subsequent experiments. To establish an in vitro fibrotic microenvironment, cells were seeded into the required culture plates and, after attachment, synchronized by serum starvation or low-serum culture for 24 h. Recombinant human TGF-β1 was then added to a final concentration of 10 ng/mL for different durations (10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h) to determine the optimal modeling window for subsequent intervention experiments and to dynamically evaluate expression changes in key gluconeogenic enzymes and profibrotic markers.

Pharmacological interventions

To evaluate the direct effect of PCK1 functional status on fibrosis progression, the PCK1-specific inhibitor SKF34288 (MedChemExpress, HY-139357) and the PCK1 activator glucagon (MedChemExpress, HY-P0082A) were used for intervention. HK-2 cells in logarithmic growth phase were randomly assigned to the following groups: normal control (NC), TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL), TGF-β1-stimulated cells treated with SKF34288 and glucagon. After 24 h of treatment, cells were harvested for subsequent molecular analyses.

Lentiviral overexpression and knockdown of PCK1

Lentiviral constructs for PCK1 overexpression and knockdown were generated using the pHBLV-CMV-MCS-3flag-EF1-ZsGreen-T2A-PURO and pHBLV-U6-MCS-CMV-ZsGreen-PGK-PURO vectors, respectively. The full-length human PCK1 overexpression construct and the corresponding empty vector control (OE-Vec), as well as a lentiviral shRNA targeting human PCK1 (shPCK1; 5’-CAGTGAGGGAGTTTCTCGAGAATAA-3’) and a negative control shRNA (sh-Vec; 5’-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTAA-3’), were designed, packaged, titrated, and sequence-verified by Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. HK-2 cells at approximately 70%–80% confluence were infected with the indicated lentiviruses at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 30. At 72 h post-infection, the medium was replaced with complete medium containing 5 µg/mL puromycin to select stable cell lines.

SIS3 Rescue experiments

To determine whether regulation of TGF-β1-induced fibrosis by PCK1 depends on Smad3 signaling, HK-2 cells with PCK1 knockdown or overexpression were treated with the Smad3-specific inhibitor SIS3 (Selleck Chemicals, S7959; final concentration 5 µM) under TGF-β1 stimulation. The concentration was selected based on preliminary dose-response experiments conducted in our laboratory [38]. After 24 h of treatment, cells were collected and lysed. Western blotting and RT-qPCR were applied.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from kidney tissues or cultured cells using SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co.,Ltd, AG21017) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (Accurate Biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd, AG11728). RT-qPCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR®Green Supermix (BIORAD, 1725121) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex system or ViiA 7 system. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplemental Table S1. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with ACTB (β-actin) serving as the internal reference.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from renal cortex tissue or cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo, 23225). Equal amounts of protein were loaded per lane, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PCK1 (Affinity, DF6770), phospho-Smad3 (Affinity, AF8315), Smad3 (Affinity, AF6362), α-SMA (Affinity, AF1032), Vimentin (Cell Signaling Technology, 5741), COL1A1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 72026 S), Fibronectin (Affinity, AF5335), COL3A1 (Affinity, AF5457), p-FBP1 (Affinity, AF7000), G6PC (Affinity, DF12610) and β-actin (Affinity, AF7018), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 7074). All primary antibodies used for Western blotting were diluted 1:1000. Bands were visualized using an ECL detection system and quantified by densitometry with ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence staining of cell coverslips

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 3% BSA. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against α-SMA and phospho-Smad3 or Vimentin, followed by reagents from the four-color multiplex immunohistochemistry kit. After visualization using the TSA amplification system according to the manufacturer’s protocol, coverslips were mounted with DAPI-containing medium and imaged under an inverted fluorescence microscope. All primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence were diluted 1:200. Images were acquired using an Olympus IX83 + DP80 inverted fluorescence imaging system.

Measurement of intracellular ATP levels and lactate release

HK-2 cells stably overexpressing PCK1 (OE-PCK1) or harboring the empty vector control (OE-Vec), and cells with stable PCK1 knockdown (shPCK1) or the scramble control (sh-Vec), were treated with or without TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) for 24 h. Culture supernatants were collected for lactate quantification using an L-lactate assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, cat. no. A019-2-1), with absorbance measured at 530 nm. Intracellular ATP levels were determined using the Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, cat. no. S0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Both lactate release and intracellular ATP levels were normalized to total protein content of the corresponding cell lysates.

Co-immunoprecipitation

HK-2 cells stably expressing FLAG-tagged PCK1 (OE-PCK1-FLAG) or the empty vector control (OE-Vec) were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of total protein lysate were incubated with Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 10007D) pre-coupled with an anti-DYKDDDDK tag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 14793 S) or normal rabbit IgG at 4 °C for 4 h with gentle rotation. Immunocomplexes were recovered using a magnetic separator, washed four times with ice-cold IP lysis buffer, and eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. Input lysates and immunoprecipitated fractions were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by western blotting with antibodies against FLAG and Smad3.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Quantitative data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using the Brown-Forsythe or Levene test. Data with normal distribution and equal variance are presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests: Dunnett’s test for multiple groups versus a single control, and Tukey’s test for all pairwise comparisons. Data that were not normally distributed or showed unequal variances are presented as median and were analyzed using the corresponding nonparametric tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test). For all animal experiments, n refers to the number of individual mice. For all in vitro experiments, n refers to the number of independent cell-culture experiments. Technical replicates, when performed for assays such as RT-qPCR, were averaged before statistical analysis and were not counted as independent n values. For image-based quantification, multiple fields, sections, or cells from the same mouse or the same independent cell-culture experiment were averaged to generate one value for statistical analysis. Each dot in the bar graphs represents one n value unless otherwise stated. Exact sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In figures, asterisks (*) indicate significant differences between experimental groups, while the pound sign (#) represents significant differences compared to the control group (* or # p < 0.05, ** or ## p < 0.01, *** or ### p < 0.001, and **** or #### p < 0.0001).
