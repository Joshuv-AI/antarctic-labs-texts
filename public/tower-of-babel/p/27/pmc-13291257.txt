Assessing the relationships between migraine and driving risks, challenges and coping strategies
Authors: Hamit Genc, Reza Ghouri, Asena Ayca Ozdemir, Aynur Ozge, Pinar Yalinay Dikmen, Esme Ekizoglu, Ergun Y. Uc, Bahar Tasdelen, Hayrunnisa Bolay, Betul Baykan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Driving is a multitasking activity that requires sustained attention, multisensory integration, and rapid reaction capacity. This study aims to assess the under-recognized disability experienced during driving in individuals with migraine, the outcomes of symptoms triggered during driving on safety, and the coping strategies employed. This cross-sectional study included individuals between the ages of 18 and 65 diagnosed with migraine according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders-3 criteria. A structured form, based on the "Driving Habits Questionnaire," was applied to patients by 57 neurologists face-to-face. It covered parameters such as demographics, headache characteristics during driving, sensitivity to driving, coping strategies, the Headache Impact Test-6, driving cessation, and traffic accidents. The patients were divided into diagnostic subgroups: those with/without aura, episodic/chronic, and those with and without vestibular migraine by headache experts. Additionally, we employed a Structural Equation Model (SEM) to identify the variables that influence driving cessation and accidents. Of the 2,522 patients evaluated, 1,333 (mean age: 36.75 ± 9.53 years; 64.4% female) had an active driving history. Patients experienced headaches in 13% of their active driving sessions, and 70.6% had experienced a headache while driving at least once during their active driving sessions. Due to migraine, 1.7% of the patients ceased driving, and 9.2% reduced their driving frequency. The mean age of the drivers who had an accident was younger [35.3133; 95% CI: 34.1677–36.4589, p = 0.007]. Headaches during driving were mainly associated with decreased attention (69%), restlessness (66%), and nervousness (56%). To cope with headaches, drivers most often preferred to use analgesics/triptans (58.8%), turn off the music (51.9%), and wear sunglasses (47.7%). SEM revealed causal relationships between migraine subtypes, symptoms, road conditions affecting driving sensitivity, the impact of headaches on driving, direct and indirect cessation of driving, and accident probability. Our findings demonstrated that driving is an underrecognized factor that can cause insidious disability in patients with migraine and may pose a threat to driving safety, often underappreciated in clinical care and traffic regulations. Raising awareness about migraine-related driving impairments and enhancing coping mechanisms may reduce preventable risks and improve the quality of life for affected individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53327-4.

Introductıon

Migraine impacts various aspects of daily life, including driving1. In a study conducted in Italy, approximately 20% of migraine patients reported severe limitations in driving due to migraine, while approximately 15% reported serious problems with driving performance2. Considering the cognitive, visual, and vestibular symptoms associated with migraine3, it is not unexpected that driving ability may be affected4. Migraine headache is associated with central sensory processing and multisensory integration dysfunction. Sensory processing, particularly in the visual system of the brain, is disturbed in more than one domain in migraine patients5–8. The integration of incoming somatosensory input with descending motor output is disrupted during migraine attacks, revealing ictal facilitation in the sensorimotor cortex beginning from the preictal phase9. Another subtype that may affect driving is vestibular migraine (VM), whose lifetime prevalence is estimated to be 1%10. Patients may experience spontaneous, positional, visual, or head movement-triggered vertigo, as well as transient auditory symptoms11. Moreover, visual and postural motion and a visually busy environment evoked vestibular symptoms, which are predominant in migraine patients12. Another factor that may have an impact on driving is chronic migraine (CM), and it was reported that some people with migraine avoid driving as the pain intensity increases13. Nevertheless, all this information, the dimensions, determinants, and effects of migraine on driving have not been sufficiently investigated and documented, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

The primary purposes of this study were to evaluate driving cessation and accident rates in patients with migraine. Secondary purposes included determining factors such as road conditions that trigger or exacerbate headaches in patients, the effects of attacks on patients while driving, and how patients cope with headaches.

Methods

This study employed a hospital-based, multicenter, cross-sectional design from May to July 2024. A structured questionnaire form based on the "Driving Habit Questionnaire"14 was used by 57 neurologists working in 40 different clinics to collect the data [Supplementary files 1 and 2]. Physicians administered the questionnaires via face-to-face interviews after obtaining consent. The entire questionnaire application time was approximately 30 min.

The first section consisted of 21 questions that evaluated the patients’ demographic characteristics, migraine and aura subtypes, headache characteristics, and treatment. The second section included 53 questions regarding the individuals’ experiences with driving, preferences and behaviors, possible migraine triggers, headache coping strategies, driving cessation, and traffic accident history. During driving, patients’ sensitivity to road conditions, whether they had a headache or not, was rated on a Likert-type scale from 0 (I have no difficulty) to 5 (I have significant difficulty). The parameters questioned consisted of questions regarding weather conditions (rainy, snowy, icy, and foggy), ground characteristics (asphalt, path, muddy, intersections, highways or intercity, roads without traffic lights/warning signs), driving time (daytime, nighttime, and work hours), and driving maneuvers (left turn, parallel parking). Again, the effects of factors likely to cause or increase headaches during the journey were questioned on the same scale. The parameters included light sources in road conditions that may trigger a migraine attack or increase the severity of pain (sunlight setting between rows of trees, light-emitting diode (LED) headlights, signal lights, brake lights, etc.), sound sources (heavy traffic, listening to loud music, siren sounds,), flicker sources, odor sources (vehicle-fuel smell, cigarette smell, etc.) and other conditions (heavy traffic (stress factor), rough roads).

The disability level of drivers with migraine was assessed in the third section using the Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6)15.

Based on a prevalence rate of 24.9% in neurology outpatient clinics16, the G*Power analysis calculated that a minimum of 1337 patients should be included in the study with a 2% margin of error. All volunteer patients with migraine were included in the study period, not emergency referrals—90,7% of patients were primarily admitted to the clinic with headache complaints. The primary complaint of the rest of them usually became an accompanying symptom to headache, such as headache, vertigo, dizziness, or neck pain. A total of 2,548 migraine patients were screened, and twenty-six individuals whose data were questionable in cross-checks were excluded from the study. Headache impact test (HIT-6)

The HIT-6 is a valid, brief, and structured questionnaire designed to measure the impact of migraine, including the time lost from daily activities from the patient’s perspective17,18.

It comprises six items that cover various life domains, and each item is scored on a five-point Likert-type scale. Increasing scores indicate an increased impact of headaches on the patient. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the test was conducted by Dikmen et al.19.

Statistical methods

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric tests were applied for normally distributed variables, while non-parametric tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Group comparisons between migraine subtypes were performed using an Independent-Samples t-test or a Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To explore the direct and indirect relationships among migraine subtypes (MwA, VM, CM), accompanying symptoms, driving-related headache, sensitivity to road conditions, traffic accident history, and driving cessation, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was conducted.

SEM is a powerful multivariate analysis technique used for many purposes. One of them is causal modelling or path analysis. In path analysis, causal relationships between variables were investigated. In structural equation modeling, path diagrams illustrate which variables cause changes in other variables. In the path diagram, variables are endogenous or exogenous. While exogenous variables are measured outside the model, endogenous variables can be thought of as dependent variables and their measure are determined in the model. They can be either manifest or latent variables. Manifest variables are represented in squares or rectangles, and latent variables are in ovals or circles. Lines and arrows show direct and indirect relationships between these variables.

In this study, structural equation modeling was used to identify the factors directly and indirectly associated with accidents and driving release. Since the assumption of multivariate normality was not met, parameter estimates were obtained using the asymptotic distribution-free (ADF) Gramian method. The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Jöreskog Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) were used to assess the model’s goodness of fit. RMSEA values between 0.05 and 0.08 indicate an acceptable fit, and less than 0.05 indicates a good fit. A GFI value closer to 1 indicates a better fit of the data.

Migraine characteristics and accompanying symptoms were modeled as exogenous latent variables, while road condition sensitivity, driving-related headache, traffic accidents, and driving cessation were considered endogenous latent variables. The strength and direction of the associations were reported using standardized beta coefficients (β), and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. RMSA value regarding accident ratio was 0.066, and regarding driving cessation was 0.069. Path diagrams were used to illustrate the structural relationships and mediation effects within the model. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Results

Headache attacks during driving lasted an average of 3.36 h, and the pain intensity was 6.0 (overall pain intensity: 7.5) (Table 1). Headaches during driving caused 31% of the patients to worry about being involved in an accident. 17.6% of drivers with migraine had been involved in at least one traffic accident in the previous year. The average age of those who had an accident was 35.3 [95% CI: 34.1677–36.4589, p = 0.007]. 18.2% of patients experienced motion sickness while driving, and the accident rate was higher among these patients (24.2% of those with an accident) (p = 0.008). Individuals who experienced motion sickness while driving had a higher rate of ceasing or reducing driving due to migraine (33.3% of those who ceased/reduced driving reported motion sickness while driving, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Our results highlighted that migraine has a remarkable impact on driving; patients with migraine experienced headache in an average of one out of every 10 drives. While 1.7% of the patients ceased driving due to migraine, 9.2% had reduced their driving frequency. Although there were differences in the MwA, VM, and CM subgroups, the results were generally similar, with road conditions causing mild to moderate disability in drivers. The most striking factor was the weather conditions (Figs. 2, 3, 5, and 6). Headaches experienced while driving could often cause to factors such as a lapse in attention, feelings of restlessness, and increased irritability among drivers (Table 2). While drivers mostly preferred to use medications to cope with the attack, turning off the music and wearing sunglasses were the other most preferred methods (Table 3).

Only a handful of studies from different countries provide feedback on the negative effects of migraine on driving2,20–22. Although clear scientific evidence is limited, these results reflect personal experiences about the effects of migraine on driving because many symptoms accompanying a migraine attack can directly affect driving skills23.

Probably secondary to a lack of habituation24 and problems in processing sensory stimuli25, individuals with migraine have lower discomfort thresholds for various stimuli such as light, sound, odor, thermal, and mechanical stimulation26, all of which can be experienced during driving. Visual inputs, which play a crucial role in driving and initiating a migraine attack, are important in this respect27.

The most prominent light-related factors that can provoke migraine are bright sunlight28–31. Sources of flicker that may be encountered while driving are also numerous32,33. In our study, bright sunlight from the front was the most common light source that caused or increased headaches. This effect intensity was observed most in CM patients, followed by VM and MwA patients (Fig. 4). As in the study showing that migraine was triggered by the effect of sunlight in a small sample group, light exposure lasting for minutes can lead to a migraine attack in a short time34,35. Greater sensitivity to glare, flicker, contrast patterns, fluorescent lights, road markings, and colors was reported in the MwA group than in the MwoA group36. No differences were found between the MwA and MwoA groups in our study; this discrepancy in the results may be due to the methodology of the previous study. In one study, drivers rated white lights around headlights and red brake lights as distracting or problematic, and headaches occurred more frequently at night33. However, the effect of the red brake lights was less pronounced in our study than that of the headlights. In contrast, a study by Salvania et al. found that drivers rated the red brake lights as more dazzling than the white lights surrounding the headlights33. This may be due to the intermittent but repeated exposure caused by the driver repeatedly pressing and releasing the brakes while driving. In the same study, glare was the most frequently reported problem symptom after LED light exposure, followed by blindness or visual impairment, blurring, and double vision. Another feature of the latter study is that it suggests road lighting and warning signs, in addition to vehicle lighting, may be disturbing by causing intermittent and repetitive warnings33. Indeed, our study showed that road conditions lead to neglected and low-effect results in individuals with migraines (Fig. 4).

Prolonged driving may lead to direct or indirect cumulative perceptual and cognitive problems, such as distraction, irritation, loss of concentration, and avoidance behaviors, which can impact driving performance33. Our study found that headaches while driving primarily cause decreased attention in drivers with migraine (Table 2). Therefore, to mitigate the risks that may arise, vehicle and road lighting should be balanced to provide optimal visibility while minimizing the impact on the driver.

Consistent with the literature, motion sickness was detected more in patients with VM than in those without it in this study. Motion sensitivity stands out as a prominent feature of migraine, especially in patients with VM37. Previous studies examining motion sickness triggered by activities such as reading in vehicles have found that this sensitivity is 4.3 times more specific in individuals with VM compared to those with benign paroxysmal positional vertigo38. Vertigo symptoms can lead to impaired motion perception and spatial orientation, as well as sudden falls, which can affect the ability to operate a motor vehicle safely39. Individuals with impaired vestibular systems had fourfold more driving difficulties in a study40. In our study, patients with VM were significantly more sensitive to all road conditions in the presence of a headache. Vuralli et al. showed that visual motion, postural motion, and a high spatial frequency environment are frequent triggers in VM patients12. Migraineurs, especially those with comorbid motion sickness, experience significantly more severe headaches, nausea, imbalance, and even hospitalizations following caloric vestibular testing, likely reflecting maladaptive cortical responses to vestibular stimulation41. This exaggerated sensory reactivity may explain the increased driving-related discomfort and accident risk observed in the VM subgroup. In the study by Cohen et al., people with vestibular disorders were aware that their driving performance was not optimal, and they drove less40.

In the OVERCOME study, which examined 5,485 patients with migraine and a valid driver’s license, the rate of not driving increased in correlation with the intensity of the headache13, a finding similar to our results. Chronic migraineurs reported more visual discomfort in driving and looking at bright screens compared to controls42. The increased light sensitivity can explain the relationship with sunlight.

In a study conducted among healthy workers in Italy, headache was the most common symptom after odor exposure (7.3%)43. In our study, the odor sources that caused or exacerbated headaches in drivers with migraines were cigarette odor and vehicle-fuel smell (Fig. 4). On the other hand, sensitivity to odor increased in the presence of a headache in all groups (Figs. 2, 3, 5, and 6).

During a migraine attack, neck or occipital pain may accompany the headache and is observed at the onset of the attack in 46.8% of physical activities44. Neck pain has the potential to restrict head movement while driving. Activities that can cause neck pain and require intense visual focus, such as reading and driving, were found to be associated with both more frequent pain and more serious deterioration in the quality of life in individuals with headaches45. In our study, neck pain was reported more in CM drivers (Table 1). Similarly, pain severity and HIT-6 scores were higher in this group (Table 1); headaches caused more significant restlessness, irritability, impairment in quick decision-making, and visual impairment in drivers (Table 2).

Of the drivers, 18.1% had been involved in at least one accident in the past year. A study in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) done in the emergency room found that 61.3% of migraineurs had been involved in a previous accident, and 53.7% had suffered a serious injury46. In another study assessing migraineurs with sleep disorders, the rate of crashes and near-crashes was 11.21%47. Young drivers have been associated with inadequate safety maneuvers and higher severity crash outcomes in crashes or crash risk situations47. Engagement in distractions was highest among young drivers and lowest among middle-aged drivers48. In general, young drivers—especially males—tend to overestimate their driving skills. They were also observed to be more willing to underestimate the risk of an accident and to exhibit behaviors that they know are risky49,50. In our study, individuals over 65 were not included to exclude age-related confounding factors. The average age of those who had an accident was lower, consistent with the literature, and motion sickness increased the risk. Notably, in our large study, visual aura was associated with an increased accident rate in patients with EM. At the same time, headaches experienced during driving were also associated with accidents, and this association was more pronounced in patients with CM. This relationship was not direct; it was an indirect effect through the sensitivity of drivers to road conditions. Again, the presence of persistent postural-perceptual dizziness and/or chronic migraine with paroxysmal vestibular symptoms increased the risk of accidents.

Remarkably, we found that 1.7% of individuals with migraine ceased driving due to headaches, and 9.2% reduced driving. Drivers with CM had higher rates of both reducing and ceasing driving, which is understandable. One in three who reduced or ceased driving reported experiencing motion sickness while driving. These patients were advised by their doctors not to drive40. The driving competence of individuals with migraine should be assessed. However, this assessment should consider the individual variable nature of the disease.

Patient-based studies in the USA suggest that the presence of vestibular disorders in migraine patients alone does not warrant suspension of the driver’s license and that regulations should be restructured to reflect physician–patient collaboration51. Similarly, it is emphasized that common standards should be established in Europe, considering the impact on quality of life and occupational functioning52. Since individuals with migraine are generally aware of the symptoms while driving, they avoid driving before an attack, and during an attack, they actively tend to stop the vehicle safely.

Individuals with migraine have a lower tolerance to environmental sensory stimuli compared to healthy controls, and this often results in avoidance behaviors53,54. In our study, drivers with migraine developed various individual coping strategies during attacks. However, 17.7% of the participants reported that they had continued driving without taking any precautions.

The most common coping method was the use of analgesics or specific attack medications (e.g., triptans) by 59% (Table 3). It is thought that individual experiences or physician recommendations shape this preference. Because it is known that early use of triptans provides better attack control and significantly improves patient satisfaction55.

The second most common strategy in our study was turning off the music volume, which was employed by 51.9% of participants. This behavior was observed significantly more frequently in individuals with VM. In line with previous studies, it is also known that sensitivity to light, sound, odor, and thermal or mechanical stimuli is higher in individuals with migraine26. These findings necessitate developing individualized management approaches to sensory stimulation during driving in individuals with migraine.

To cope with light sensitivity, 47.7% of our participants tried wearing sunglasses; this rate was higher in MwA (Table 3). In this regard, polarized sunglasses and windshield sun visors may be recommended to reduce photic triggers. It is essential for public health that other drivers consider this sensitivity and use high-beam LED headlights only under necessary conditions. Additionally, it has been reported that "Blue Cut for Night" glasses, developed to reduce photic sensitivity, effectively reduce nighttime headaches in individuals with migraine56. In the design of vehicle and road lighting systems, light distribution should be provided in a spectrum and intensity that will not trigger excessive sensory stimulation in individuals with migraine while continuing to provide safe vision. In this direction, a balance should be established between lighting engineering and clinical neurological sensitivities. In the event of a migraine attack that may develop while driving, it is essential to have a safe area where driving can be interrupted quickly and easily accessible. Equipping vehicles with dark, quiet, well-ventilated compartments and isolating them from external stimuli, particularly in situations that may trigger migraine attacks, can help patients manage their migraine symptoms more effectively.

A detailed scientific investigation of all the coping strategies listed in our research and the development of specific approaches to ensure that individuals with migraine can drive safely without missing out on life will only be possible through increased awareness and further research. Our study represents an important step in exploring coping strategies in this context.

Conclusions

These findings indicate that migraine should be considered not only as a painful syndrome but also as a neurosensory disorder affecting complex cognitive and sensorimotor activities, such as driving. These effects can limit individuals’ independence and social participation in daily life; they also constitute a public risk area, albeit minimal, in terms of traffic safety. Therefore, the driving competence of individuals with migraine should be assessed and improved based on their personal symptom profile and neurological evaluation. Mapping the functional areas affecting driving performance according to migraine subtypes can be guiding for both health professionals and regulatory authorities.

Our results are a novel contribution to the intersection between headache research and traffic safety. The findings provide a scientific basis that can be used in public awareness studies and future management developments.
