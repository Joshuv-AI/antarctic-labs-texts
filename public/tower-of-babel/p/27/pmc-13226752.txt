Evaluation of various obturation techniques with AH Plus Jet sealer in simulated C-shaped canals
Authors: Maryam Gharechahi, Mina Mehrjouei, Saeed Moradi, Ali Kazemian, Melika Hoseinzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study compared the quality and efficiency of three obturation techniques for simulated 3D-printed C-shaped canals filled with an epoxy resin–based sealer. A mandibular molar with a C-shaped root canal (C1 configuration) was prepared to size #30/0.04, and 30 replicas were 3D-printed. The canals were obturated with AH Plus Jet sealer using cold lateral compaction (CLC), warm vertical compaction (WVC), or warm vertical hybrid compaction (HC) (n = 10). Cross-sections at the apical, middle, and coronal thirds were examined under a digital microscope to determine the proportions of gutta-percha, sealer, and voids. Obturation time was also recorded. Data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA and LSD post-hoc tests (α = 0.05). In the apical third, void percentages were significantly lower for CLC (1.00±0.21%) and HC (1.00±0.43%) compared with WVC (P<0.001), although the effect size indicated only a modest difference (η2 = 0.218). In the middle third, WVC produced the lowest void percentage (1.03 ± 0.37%; P < 0.05). In the coronal third, void percentages in WVC and HC were significantly lower than in CLC (P < 0.05). Among the techniques, WVC required the shortest obturation time (168.00 ± 24.29 s; P < 0.001). Considering void percentage and treatment time, WVC appears to be the most efficient technique for obturating C-shaped canals with AH Plus Jet sealer.Keywords: Root canal obturation, Epoxy resin-based root canal sealer, Three-dimensional printing, Root canal filling materials

Introduction

C-shaped root canal systems, most frequently observed in mandibular first and second molars, represent a significant challenge in endodontics because of their unusual and complex morphology (Pataer et al. 2025). These canals are characterized by fins, webs, and irregular cross-sections that change from the pulp chamber to the apex, making disinfection and three-dimensional obturation technically challenging (Pataer et al. 2025).

A critical determinant of endodontic success is the quality of obturation. Voids compromise the seal and provide pathways for bacterial leakage, which is a primary cause of treatment failure (Kim et al. 2018a, b; Jasrasaria et al. 2023). In C-shaped canals, the anatomical complexity predisposes to incomplete filling and increased void formation (Kim et al. 2018a, b). Voids allow bacterial contamination, impairing periapical healing and predisposing to reinfection. Clinical studies have linked poorly adapted obturation with persistence of apical periodontitis and less favorable long-term outcomes (Jasrasaria et al. 2023). Therefore, minimizing voids is a clinically significant objective that directly impacts long-term treatment success and prevention of reinfection.

Among endodontic sealers, epoxy resin–based formulations are widely accepted because of their strong adhesion to dentin, dimensional stability, long working time, and effective sealing ability (Kim et al. 2022). AH Plus (Dentsply De Trey, Germany) has long been considered the gold standard, due to its handling properties and long-term stability (Sonntag et al. 2015; Zordan-Bronzel et al. 2019; Donnermeyer et al. 2020). A newer formulation, AH Plus Jet, is packaged in premixed syringes with adjustable tips, which simplifies delivery, enhances infection control, and reduces operator variability (Mahajan and Kamra 2007). A preliminary investigation suggests that AH Plus Jet demonstrates reduced microleakage compared with AH Plus (Akhavan et al. 2011); however, evidence regarding its performance in C-shaped canals remains limited.

The obturation technique also exerts a crucial influence on sealing quality. Warm vertical compaction (WVC), warm vertical hybrid compaction (HC), and cold lateral compaction (CLC) differ in their capacity to adapt gutta-percha and sealer to complex canal surfaces. WVC softens gutta-percha and promotes flow into fins, isthmuses, and lateral spaces, while compaction pressure helps reduce sealer thickness and minimize voids. HC aim to combine the better flow of thermal methods with the control of lateral compaction, and lateral compaction remains a clinically conventional method due to its simplicity and predictability. In C-shaped canals, where cross-sections are broad and irregular, the influence of obturation method becomes even more pronounced. Wisawawatin et al. (Wisawawatin et al. 2022) compared sealer penetration and gap volumes using different sealers and techniques in C-shaped canals and found no significant differences. However, their study did not include AH Plus Jet, and the differences in viscosity, flow behavior, heat responsiveness, and handling of this sealer may lead to altered adaptation and void formation under the same techniques. Therefore, in the present investigation, we assessed both the quality of obturation using a digital optical microscope and the obturation time-a clinically relevant metric reflecting procedural efficiency-using AH Plus Jet with three techniques. Employing standardized 3D-printed replicas of C1-configuration canals allowed us to isolate the effects of obturation method while minimizing anatomical variability (Karatekin et al. 2019). Digital microscopy offers advantages of rapid imaging, ease of specimen preparation, and clear visualization at moderate magnification (Almuderis and Al-Gharrawi 2022; Al-Rayesse et al. 2024; Gharechahi et al. 2024). The null hypothesis was that no significant differences would be observed among the three techniques in either sealing quality or time.

Methods

Sample selection

Fifteen human mandibular molars with fused roots were obtained. To identify teeth with C1 anatomy as per the categorization proposed by Fan et al. (Fan et al. 2004), a cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was taken using the Planmeca ProMax® 3D Classic system (Planmeca OY, Helsinki, Finland). The axial sections of the scans were examined using MicroDicom software (MicroDicom Ltd., Sofia, Bulgaria), with a thickness of 0.125 mm and pixel size of 0.125. Ultimately, a tooth exhibiting a C1 shape across its canal was chosen (Fig. 1).

A) selected tooth; B) axial view of the tooth on CBCT image to ensure the configuration; C) the reconstructed image of the configuration; and D) the 3D printed resin model

Root canal preparation

Following access cavity preparation, three K-files #10 (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan) were introduced into the mesial, distal, and middle regions of the canal in order to determine the working length. The sample was decoronated, resulting in a remaining root length of 10 mm. The canal was prepared with M3 Pro Gold rotary systems (United Dental, Shanghai, China) up to size #30/0.04 utilizing an Endomotor (NSK, Tokyo, Japan) at a speed of 350 rpm and a torque of 1–2 Ncm. To assure patency, a K-file size #10 (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan) was applied by passive pressure.

The anti-curvature approach, as described by Abou-Rass et al. (Abou-Rass et al. 1980), was employed in the isthmus. H-files sizes #15–25 (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan) were inserted into the canal and extracted by applying pressure on the buccal wall to avoid strip perforation. After each file, 2 mL NaOCl 1% (Nik Darman, Tehran, Iran) was used. Final irrigation was done with 10 mL EDTA 17% (Nik Darman, Tehran, Iran) and 2 mL Saline (Samen, Mashhad, Iran). The canal was dried with Paper points (Diadent, Seoul, Korea).

3D Printing of the C-shaped canal replicas

The sample underwent CBCT scanning in the high-resolution mode (75 μm). The tooth structure was reconstructed using Planmeca Romexis 6.2 software (Planmeca OY, Helsinki, Finland) and saved in.stl format. The 3D model was printed using an AccuFab-L4K 3D printer (Shining 3D Tech, Hangzhou, China) with SG100 resin (Esun, Shenzhen, China).

The sample size was determined through a power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.8), resulting in 10 specimens per group, in accordance with the findings of Karatekin et al. (Karatekin et al. 2019). A total of thirty replicas were produced at 4 K resolution.

C-shaped replicas obturation

The replicas were filled with AH Plus Jet sealer (Dentsply De Trey, Konstanz, Germany) using:

Warm vertical compaction (WVC) (Kim et al. 2018a, b): Two master gutta-percha cones were coated with sealer and placed at the working length within the mesiodistal and distobuccal regions of the canal. The gutta-percha cones were packed using a Fast Pack device (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) with a medium-sized 50/0.05 tip within 4 mm of the working length. The apical plug was packed using a medium-sized plugger tip #50 (Perfect, Shanghai, China). The canal was filled with Fast Fill (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) in two 4–5 mm layers and compacted using a plugger (Perfect, China).

Warm vertical compaction (WVC) (Kim et al. 2018a, b): Two master gutta-percha cones were coated with sealer and placed at the working length within the mesiodistal and distobuccal regions of the canal. The gutta-percha cones were packed using a Fast Pack device (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) with a medium-sized 50/0.05 tip within 4 mm of the working length. The apical plug was packed using a medium-sized plugger tip #50 (Perfect, Shanghai, China). The canal was filled with Fast Fill (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) in two 4–5 mm layers and compacted using a plugger (Perfect, China).

Cold lateral compaction (CLC) (Soo et al. 2015): Gutta-percha cones size 35/0.02 (Sure Dent, Seoul, Korea) were coated with sealer and placed at working length. A Nickel–titanium finger spreader (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan) was used for compaction, with auxiliary Gutta-percha cones size 20/0.02 (Sure Dent, Seoul, Korea) added. Excess gutta-percha was removed with a Gutta-percha cutter (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) and compacted with Pluggers (Perfect, Shanghai, China).

Cold lateral compaction (CLC) (Soo et al. 2015): Gutta-percha cones size 35/0.02 (Sure Dent, Seoul, Korea) were coated with sealer and placed at working length. A Nickel–titanium finger spreader (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan) was used for compaction, with auxiliary Gutta-percha cones size 20/0.02 (Sure Dent, Seoul, Korea) added. Excess gutta-percha was removed with a Gutta-percha cutter (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) and compacted with Pluggers (Perfect, Shanghai, China).

Warm vertical hybrid compaction (HC) (Wisawawatin et al. 2022): Two master gutta-percha cones were coated with sealer and placed at the working length within the mesiodistal and distobuccal portions of the canal. The auxiliary gutta-percha cones of 20/0.02 were inserted into the canals. Lateral compaction was continued until the spreader size #25 could penetrate 4 mm into the canal. The excess of the gutta-percha cones was moved using the medium-size 50/0.05 tip of the Fast Pack device. The apical plug was packed using a medium-sized plugger. The remaining portion of the root canal was incrementally back-filled with thermoplasticised injectable gutta-percha using an obturation device (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China). Each increment of gutta-percha was compacted using pluggers size #50 and #70 (Perfect, Shanghai, China).

Warm vertical hybrid compaction (HC) (Wisawawatin et al. 2022): Two master gutta-percha cones were coated with sealer and placed at the working length within the mesiodistal and distobuccal portions of the canal. The auxiliary gutta-percha cones of 20/0.02 were inserted into the canals. Lateral compaction was continued until the spreader size #25 could penetrate 4 mm into the canal. The excess of the gutta-percha cones was moved using the medium-size 50/0.05 tip of the Fast Pack device. The apical plug was packed using a medium-sized plugger. The remaining portion of the root canal was incrementally back-filled with thermoplasticised injectable gutta-percha using an obturation device (Eighteeth, Changzhou, China). Each increment of gutta-percha was compacted using pluggers size #50 and #70 (Perfect, Shanghai, China).

Root canal preparation and obturation were performed by an endodontist (M.M.) who had undergone calibration sessions to standardize the clinical protocol, under the supervision of a senior endodontist with more than 10 years of clinical experience (M.G.). The duration for filling each replica was documented. Subsequently, all the samples were incubated at a temperature of 37° under conditions of 100% humidity for a week, to ensure the sealer setting.

Measuring sealer and void percentage

The samples were sectioned into thirds using a 0.3 mm disc from a CNC cutting machine (Delta Electronics Inc., Taipei, Taiwan) at distances of 2 mm, 5 mm, and 7 mm from the apex. The sections were then imaged using a stereomicroscope (Bluelight Industry USA Inc., Fremont, CA, USA) at a magnification 32x. The images were analyzed using DinoCapture 2.0 software (Dino-Lite Digital Microscope, Taiwan). The areas occupied by the filling materials and the voids were represented as percentages of the total area (Fig. 2) (Gharechahi et al. 2024).

Measurements of the gutta-percha (G), sealer (S), and void (V) percentages in the apical section of a replica obturated with AH Plus Jet sealer and warm vertical compaction technique in 32 × magnification

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Comparisons of void percentage, filling material percentage, and treatment time among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were conducted with the LSD post-hoc test. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was adopted for all analyses.

Regarding the void percentage, the CLC group and the HC group showed a significantly lower void percentage in the apical section than the WVC group (P < 0.05). However, the effect size suggests only a modest impact (effect size = 0.218). In the middle section, the void percentage in the CLC group was significantly higher than that of the other techniques (P < 0.05; effect size = 0.652). Additionally, the HC group had a higher void percentage compared to the WVC group (P < 0.05). In the coronal section, the CLC group displayed a significantly higher void percentage than the other groups (P < 0.05; effect size = 0.940).

Treatment time for CLC (482.50 ± 6.20 s) was significantly higher compared to other groups (P < 0.001). The treatment time for the HC technique (341.00 ± 19.12 s) was also significantly higher compared to the WVC technique (168.00 ± 24.29 s; P < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study evaluated three established obturation techniques- CLC, WVC, and HC-in 3D-printed replicas of C-shaped canals with C1 configuration filled with AH Plus Jet sealer. The main outcomes were void percentage, distribution of filling materials, and obturation time. The C1 configuration was selected because it is the most frequently observed among C-shaped morphologies (approximately 40–54% at the orifice level) (Kato et al. 2014; Shaikh et al. 2024), and its broad ribbon-like, uninterrupted canal anatomy presents considerable challenges for achieving a homogenous, void-free obturation (Jidewar et al. 2024).

Methodological standardization is a prerequisite for valid comparison of obturation techniques, particularly in teeth with complex morphologies such as C-shaped canals (Gharechahi et al. 2024). Natural teeth exhibit significant variability in root canal configuration, and the micromechanical properties of dentin further complicate reproducibility (Ivancik et al. 2014; Hülsmann 2022). The use of 3D-printed replicas in this study facilitated standardization and ensured an adequate and uniform sample size (De‐Deus et al. 2020).

Voids in the apical third are particularly critical, as they compromise the apical seal-the primary barrier against periapical contamination. Even small discontinuities here can permit bacterial or fluid ingress, and experimental models consistently link apical voids with treatment failure (Jafari and Jafari 2017; Gulabivala and Ng 2023). In the present study, the void percentage within the apical third were statistically significant; however, the effect size analysis revealed that the clinical impact was negligible. The relatively low void percentage in the WVC group may be attributed to the compaction with the Fast Pack device. However, the predictability of heat conduction and adaptation to the canal walls remains limited in this method, particularly in wide ribbon-shaped canals (Soo et al. 2015; Karatekin et al. 2019). Soo et al. (Soo et al. 2015) similarly noted that in C-shaped canals, apical gutta-percha cones failed to consolidate into a homogeneous mass due to insufficient heat transmission. In contrast, the utilization of injectable gutta-percha in WVC contributed to the significantly lower void percentage and higher gutta-percha content observed in the middle and coronal thirds. These findings corroborate previous reports (Soo et al. 2015; Gok et al. 2017; Karatekin et al. 2019). To enhance apical seal using WVC technique, Karaketin et al. (Karatekin et al. 2019) recommended repeating thermal compaction at multiple levels to minimize voids. The use of auxiliary gutta-percha cones in the HC technique may help improve the consistency of warm compaction protocols (Wisawawatin et al. 2022). However, in the present study this approach yielded only negligible improvement in apical obturation quality.

Voids in the middle and coronal thirds also contribute to reinfection risk by facilitating coronal leakage, allowing microbial ingress from the oral cavity, and undermining the filling. The integrity of the coronal seal is therefore essential for long-term success (Mehta et al. 2025). Moreover, several authors emphasize that failure often results from the combined effect of apical and coronal leakage over time (De Moor and Hommez 2000). The lowest void percentages in the middle and coronal thirds were observed with the WVC technique, and in the coronal section its performance was comparable to that of HC. The HC technique yielded a void percentage comparable to CLC in the apical third, while showing superior performance in other sections. This observation aligns with the results of Wisawawatin et al. (Wisawawatin et al. 2022) who reported similar void percentages between HC and CLC, although their study involved natural teeth with both C1 and CIII configurations. In contrast, samples obturated using CLC exhibited a progressive increase in void percentage from apical to coronal regions. This trend is consistent with the observations of Gok et al. (Gok et al. 2017) and Wu and Wesselink (Wu and Wesselink 2001), who reported inferior filling quality with CLC, particularly in canals prepared with greater taper. The wide canal dimensions necessitated the use of multiple auxiliary gutta-percha cones, prolonging the obturation process and increasing the likelihood of interfacial voids (Soo et al. 2015).

Across all groups, the gutta-percha percentage in the apical third was consistently lower than in the middle and coronal regions. Optimal obturation is characterized by a substantial proportion of gutta-percha with a minimal sealer layer, as gutta-percha is dimensionally stable whereas sealer is more prone to dissolution and shrinkage over time (Soo et al. 2015). In the present study, the apical third consistently showed lower gutta-percha percentages and thicker sealer layers compared with the middle and coronal regions, which may help explain the higher void incidence observed apically. Furthermore, it also may be related to the anatomical disparity between the mesiodistal and buccolingual dimensions of C-shaped canals. Ordinola-Zapata et al. (Ordinola-Zapata et al. 2009) similarly reported inferior obturation quality in the apical third, with an average gutta-percha percentage of 74.5%, closely matching the present findings. Karatekin et al. (Karatekin et al. 2019) also observed a progressive apical-to-coronal increase in gutta-percha percentage in both CLC and WVC groups, consistent with our results. This pattern highlights that a reduced gutta-percha-to-sealer ratio, particularly in the apical region, may predispose the filling to microleakage and compromise long-term seal.

This study has several limitations. First, it was an in vitro investigation, which precludes assessment of the long-term clinical performance of sealers under dynamic physiological conditions. Second, only C1 configurations were evaluated, limiting the generalizability of the results to other canal morphologies such as C2 and C3. Moreover, periodontal ligament and periapical tissue pressures were not simulated; thus, the sealer extrusion was not evaluated. Future studies employing experimental models that replicate periapical resistance may improve clinical relevance. Third, while 3D-printed resin replicas facilitate methodological standardization, their physical properties differ from dentin; the absence of dentinal tubules, altered thermal conductivity, and distinct mechanical characteristics may influence sealer penetration, setting behavior and heat transfer during warm compaction and the tendency of void formation. A previous study indicated that while replicas produced comparable results to natural teeth in simple anatomies but not in complex teeth (molars) (Kooanantkul et al. 2023). Finally, biological responses such as periapical tissue irritation or inflammatory reactions were not assessed. Therefore, while the present results provide valuable comparative insights under controlled conditions, caution is required when extrapolating to the clinical setting. Further clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings and to assess the long-term outcomes of root canal treatment in molars with C-shaped anatomy using AH Plus Jet sealer, particularly considering how material interactions under physiological conditions may influence obturation quality.

Conclusion

The findings showed that differences in void percentage among the groups were negligible in the apical third. In contrast, significant differences were observed in the middle and coronal thirds, leading to rejection of the null hypothesis. The WVC technique achieved the lowest void percentages in these regions while also requiring the shortest treatment time. Nonetheless, these results should be validated in long-term clinical trials.
