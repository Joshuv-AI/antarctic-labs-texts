Integrated Estimates of Forest Expansion Opportunity in New York State
Authors: Andrea Armstrong, Richard C Stedman, David Richardson, Kathryn Bills Walsh, Christopher Zimmerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForest expansion is an important climate mitigation strategy in support of decarbonization efforts. Estimates of land-based potential for forest expansion and other natural climate solutions typically neglect key social dimensions that shape land conversion opportunity. Building on our previous work that examines landowner willingness to expand forest, our analysis integrates estimates of potential for converting land into forest with social preferences of private landowners in New York, United States. We estimate that, when accounting for landowner willingness and excluding land in active agriculture, the maximum amount of land where there is opportunity in New York is 352,000 acres, about one-fifth of the State’s 1.7-million-acre goal. Support for local conservation practitioners and landowner-tailored programming must be expanded to meet climate mitigation goals.

Introduction

Natural Climate Solutions (NCS) are one critical element of climate mitigation (Dooley et al. 2022; Mariani et al. 2024; Fargione et al. 2018). NCS often involve the restoration or enhancement of biophysical processes and ecosystems that provide carbon sequestration and enable other ecosystem outcomes such as improved water and air quality (Griscom et al. 2017). Some NCS strategies involve the conversion of land into new uses such as bioenergy cropping, restored wetland systems (Dooley et al. 2024). Global or large-scale assessments of NCS potential identified forest expansion (whether through reforestation or afforestation) as one crucial carbon sequestration pathway (Cook-Patton et al. 2020; Fargione et al. 2018; Griscom et al. 2017), particularly where forests were the native and ecologically appropriate landcover (Briske et al. 2024). Forest expansion implies a conversion of one land use into that of forest cover; forest expansion strategies include afforestation and natural expansion, which often involve planting trees or managing land to encourage the establishment of young forests, respectively (Food and Agriculture Organization 2020). Other strategies, such as reforestation, enhance carbon sequestration within existing, disturbed forested areas but typically do not involve extending forest cover to new areas (US Forest Service 2022).

Climate mitigation models for the contiguous United States suggest that forest expansion offers the greatest carbon sequestration potential of the prevailing NCS strategies—more than improved forest management, avoided deforestation, or agricultural conservation practices (Fargione et al. 2018). Such climate mitigation models are key to understanding the most effective strategies to meet climate pledges and—critical to forest expansion—the extent and distribution of associated land cover conversion (or restoration) (Dooley et al. 2024). However, understanding the true potential for carbon sequestration via forest expansion requires estimates of land suitability as well as landowners’ willingness to engage in land conversion activities (Stedman et al. 2024). Land cover, land use governance, and decision-making are all social processes that shape climate mitigation prospects. Schulte et al. (2024) found that large scale NCS models lacked information on social preferences and political opportunity—two social aspects that, once integrated with information about biophysical conditions, could lead to more practical and realistic understandings of climate mitigation strategies.

Our analysis addresses this crucial need to estimate ‘on the ground’ opportunity for forest expansion as a climate mitigation strategy. This requires integrating landowners’ attitudes and willingness to take on forest expansion with existing landcover conditions. We ask, when considering landowners’ attitudes about converting their land into forest, what is the opportunity and how does that compare to statewide tree planting goals? Building on our previous work (Stedman et al. 2024), we integrate social science data on landowners’ willingness to convert their land into mature forest with estimates of landcover suitability within New York, USA. New York is an ideal laboratory for this work because the State includes forest expansion as a strategy to meet its goal of net zero by 2050 (New York State 2024). Our findings provide an integrated understanding of forest expansion opportunity in the service of climate mitigation strategies.

Estimates of Forest Expansion Potential

Climate mitigation models are an important tool to estimate the extent, effect, and appropriateness of potential nature-based mitigation strategies (Schulte et al. 2024). Estimates of forest expansion’s climate mitigation potential vary based upon a model’s analytical decisions or assumptions and the inclusion of certain factors such as albedo effects or afforestation of lands that do not naturally support tree cover (Hasler et al. 2024; Veldman et al. 2015). Models that estimate forest expansion potential are typically based on landcover data and identify the number of acres that could potentially be converted into forest. These estimates often exclude productive agricultural lands, urbanized areas, or areas where maximum carbon storage is already present (Bastin et al. 2019; Griscom et al. 2017; Walker et al. 2022).

NCS carbon sequestration models suggest that forest expansion can play an important role in mitigating climate change (Dooley et al. 2022; Fargione et al. 2018; Seng et al. 2020). Cook-Patton et al. (2020) estimated the amount and location of land suitable for forest expansion for the contiguous U.S. Their model estimated that the U.S. potential was 51.6 Mha (127.5 Macres), much of which was in the Southeast and Midwest USA regions. In an update to the 2020 study, they estimated that in New York state, there were 2.17 Mha (5.35 M acres) of pasture land with forest expansion potential (1.48 Mha (3.66 Macres)) (e.g., pasture, hay, and alfalfa), with the next largest opportunity category as urban open space (505,857 hectares, 1.25 Macres) (The Nature Conservancy 2023). According to these estimates, 97% of land in New York state with forest expansion potential was in private ownership (The Nature Conservancy 2023).

Two other estimates of forest expansion potential in New York addressed concerns about land use competition with agricultural production. Wightman et al. (2015) estimated that there are approximately 667,000 ha (1.65 M acres) of unmanaged grassland and shrubland in New York that could be converted into bioenergy crops or forest. Additionally, our earlier work (The Nature Conservancy 2023) used land cover data and USDA Census of Agriculture data to estimate forest expansion potential while accounting for lands that were in active production. In this work, we estimated that there are approximately 676,000 thousand hectares (1.67 M acres) of post-agricultural lands that could be suitable for conversion into forest within New York state. In both estimates, land in active agriculture (including pasture and cropland currently in production) was not considered suitable and thereby excluded from the total potential forest expansion estimate.

Overall, the findings above suggest that there is enough land that could potentially be converted into forest to meet New York State climate goals. However, these estimates do not account for landowner willingness or other critical aspects of the forest conversion process. To accurately estimate forest expansion opportunity—rather than just potential—assessments must integrate the social aspects of land conversion with landcover potential (Stedman et al. 2024). Some social considerations such as land tenure security and existing policy goals were considered within a global model of forest expansion potential (Fesenmyer et al. 2025) while other estimates considered land ownership classifications (e.g., federal, state, etc.) (Cook-Patton et al. 2020; Shanley et al. 2024). In terms of landowner attitudes and decisions, only one study, to our knowledge, adjusted their estimates to reflect landowner participation rates (Wightman et al. 2015); most assessments of land potential for climate mitigation have not accounted for the mechanisms and decision-making processes that shape forest expansion opportunity (Schulte et al. 2024). Forest expansion opportunity estimates are incomplete if they do not account for critical social dimensions, such as landowner willingness.

The Social Aspects of Forest Expansion

There are many social forces that shape forest expansion opportunities and constraints, some of which are individual (e.g., landowner attitudes, motivation, and norms) and others that are systemic (e.g., funding, seedling availability, capacity of conservation organizations). In the U.S., most private landowners have authority over land uses on their property meaning that landowners shape their land’s forest expansion potential (i.e., the land cover conditions that may enable or prohibit new forest) and make decisions about the possible conversion of land into new forest (i.e., planting trees or assisting the natural regeneration of trees with active management). To date, much research about forest expansion on private lands focused on landowner decision-making or behaviors. This focus on individual-scale social forces often engaged the Theory of Planned Behavior, which identified three drivers of behavioral intentions--attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control—that ultimately shape behavior (Ajzen 1991). Attitudes, or a landowner’s evaluation of performing forest expansion behaviors, alongside concerns about climate change encouraged landowner willingness to convert their land (Stedman et al. 2024). Subjective norms, or the perceived expectations and social acceptance from others, increased landowner participation in forest regeneration programs and practices, particularly where there was strong social acceptability of planting trees (Schirmer and Bull 2014), especially when friends, family, or neighbors were involved (Kragt et al. 2017; Ruseva et al. 2015) or where landowners were motivated by the cultural and economic aspects of family legacy (Fischer and Charnley 2010). In Australia, participation in forest regeneration programs was spatially concentrated, suggesting that local peer-learning networks were critical information pathways to engage with social expectations that encouraged participation and ultimately forest expansion (Evans et al. 2024). A study that used the Theory of Planned Behavior to study landowner willingness to participate in a federal, agricultural land conversion program (the Conservation Reserve Program), found that social norms were the strongest predictors of willingness to enroll (Thapa et al. 2024).

The third element of the Theory of Planned Behavior is perceived behavioral control, or the perceived ability to carry out a behavior. Because landowners may experience numerous constraints against converting their land into forest cover (e.g., physical ability, technical knowledge, up front project costs, among many others) (Stedman et al. 2024), public and private tree planting programs support and enhance forest expansion on private lands. Current land use can create practical and perceived barriers to converting land into forest. Landowners’ willingness to expand forest was discouraged by ongoing agricultural production activities, particularly where agriculture was a basis of income, as found in Brazil (Tavares et al. 2024). Adherence to current land uses and the perceived constraint of forest uses deter landowners’ willingness (Stedman et al. 2024). Willingness to establish forest on agricultural land can also be due to cultural and aesthetic factors that stem from land use, as evident among older residents in Norway that preferred agricultural landscapes over converted areas (Liu et al. 2022).

Systemic conditions, such as the capacity of local conservation organizations to support landowners with their forest expansion interests, the availability of tree nursery stock for tree planting, or funding that enables the planning and implementation of forest expansion projects all influence the distance between landowner willingness and the actual rates of forest expansion. Systemic forces, such as the actions of local conservation groups, play an essential role in increasing landowner knowledge, changing the conditions that shift social norms (often via communicating success stories and technical information) (Schulte et al. 2021), and demonstrating forest expansion on the landscape. There are also concerns that solar energy development could diminish local agricultural systems (Nilson and Stedman 2023), as 84% of the land suitable for utility-scale solar in New York state is in agricultural uses (Katkar et al. 2021). Systemic factors are critical to the overall success of tree planting initiatives and often have direct ties to landowner willingness to engage with forest expansion (Pienkowski et al. 2025).

Integrating Landowner Willingness with Landcover Suitability

Our analysis addresses a major gap in forest expansion estimates and climate mitigation modeling more broadly. Here, we couple landcover-based forest expansion potential estimates with social science data from our ongoing work with landowners in New York (Stedman et al. 2024) to approximate the amount of land that could be converted into mature forest for carbon sequestration. These integrated estimates offer a preliminary snapshot of how much land could potentially be converted into forest with the current level of landowner willingness.

Methods

Study Area

Our study area is New York state where, in 2019, the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA) was passed to reduce carbon emissions and decarbonize electricity (New York State 2024). Increased forest carbon sequestration must increase substantially to meet mitigation targets, and the CLCPA Scoping Plan set a goal for 1.7 million acres to be converted into forest by 2040 (New York State 2022). This acreage goal is similar the amount of post-agricultural land, or land that was previously cultivated or pastured and remains to be unforested. In 2024, the state governor also announced an intermediary goal of 25 million trees planted by 2033. This intermediary goal does not involve an associated acreage ambition as it is intended to include tree planting efforts in all landscape settings. As noted above, land use in New York is compatible with forest expansion intentions. Due to a history of shifting land uses, including the consolidation of the agricultural industry, many properties throughout the state have heterogeneous land uses that may be comprised of mature forest, former agricultural fields, some areas in cultivation, and residences.

Data

Our analysis combines two studies that were conducted independently, but in parallel. The first study and its dataset are from our social science survey of landowners in two regions of Upstate New York (n = 979), collected in Fall 2021 (Stedman et al. 2024). In that survey, we asked respondents to indicate their willingness to convert their land into mature forest for five land uses: agriculture (crop fields, pasture, Christmas trees, hay fields mowed at least once annually), grassland or field mowed every 1–3 years, fallow fields that have not been grazed, mowed, or planted in more than 3 years (less than 25% brush), shrubland (more than 25% brush), and young forest (most trees with trunks less than 4” in diameter) (Table 1). To measure landowner willingness, we asked, “Which of the land types that you own would you be willing to convert some of to mature forest as a way to store carbon?” Willingness was measured on a four-point scale from ‘not at all willing’ to ‘very willing’, with a response option to indicate they ‘do not own this type’ of land. We recognize that the focus on carbon storage via tree planting may have influenced landowner willingness to plant trees or engage in forest expansion for broader reasons; however, the overall level of landowner familiarity and knowledge about carbon storage or carbon sequestration programs is very low (Stedman et al. 2024) and we suspect that this clause did not have a major bearing on the findings presented herein. A nonresponse bias analysis found that respondents tended to be more interested in the conversion of their land into mature forest compared to nonrespondents, suggesting that the levels of landowner willingness presented herein are the upper boundaries of landowner willingness.

We used landowner survey data to calculate deduction factors that, when applied to corresponding land cover estimates, yielded an estimate of forest expansion opportunity that is inclusive of landowner willingness. We reported landowner willingness to convert their land into mature forest in Stedman et al. (2024) using different methods for calculating the proportion of ‘not at all willing’ responses; here, we report findings that account for item non-response due to the survey skip patterns. We first calculated the deduction factor to estimate land ‘off the table,’ or the amount of land that landowners are not at all willing to convert into mature forest. Within the survey, respondents could indicate that they were ‘not at all willing’ to convert their land at two locations: (1) within each land use willingness type described above, or (2) prior to specific questions about land conversion, if respondents had, ‘no interest at all in growing mature forest for carbon storage no matter what incentives or assistance you may get’; both types of responses were considered as ‘not at all willing.’ The ‘no opportunity’ deduction factors are the proportion of ‘not at all willing’ responses out of the total completed survey responses for each land cover category.

The second set of deduction factors represent the proportion of respondents that are most likely to convert a portion of their land into mature forest. This deduction factor was estimated by the proportion of all completed survey responses that were ‘very willing’ among landowners owning that type of land cover. Willingness is an expression of a preference or possible intention rather than a confirmation to act. There are many steps and challenges that prevent the most willing landowners from enacting conservation practices (discussed above), and overall participation rates in conservation programming and outreach efforts tend to be low (Butler et al. 2018). Accordingly, we only include the ‘very willing’ response to represent landowners who most realistically consider converting their land into mature forest.

Lastly, we estimated the proportion of landowners that were ‘slightly’ or ‘somewhat’ willing to convert their land into mature forest. This segment of landowners was not completely opposed to converting their land; however, willingness must increase to bring landowners closer to action. We therefore consider this proportion of respondents to represent an area of growth opportunity, meaning the proportion of landowners are not yet ‘very willing’ to convert their land, but may be open to forest expansion on their property. This growth opportunity was estimated by combining the proportion of respondents that were ‘slightly’ or ‘somewhat willing’ to convert their land.

The landcover data that we draw upon herein was compiled within a separate analysis that estimated land suitability for forest expansion in New York state (The Nature Conservancy 2023) based on 2016 National Landcover Database (NLCD), 2016 USDA Cropland Data Layers (CDL), and the 2017 USDA Census of Agriculture (CoA). In this study, suitable land constituted lands in post-agricultural cover that were not actively managed or harvested for economic value. The NLCD was used to distinguish between categories of forested vegetation, grasslands, and developed areas while the CDL was used to analyze over 100 crop types. Both the NLCD and the CDL are available at 30 m resolution, and the CoA data are available at the county level.

Our estimates of forest expansion suitability integrated social and landcover data for three land cover categories: agricultural, post-agricultural, and shrubland. Shrubland acreage was equivalent to the NLCD shrubland category (Table 1). The agriculture and post-agriculture land cover estimates were derived in a three-step process. First, we estimated the number of agricultural acres on the NLCD data (for both row crops and pasture) and cross tabulated these acres with acreages reported in the CDL based upon eight simplified categories: row crops, alfalfa hay, other hay, fallow/idle cropland, orchards and vineyards, Christmas trees, pasture, and unknown/non-agricultural land (i.e., the CDL marked as grassland but NLCD marked as agricultural). These estimates, which we refer to as ‘satellite-observed acres’ are the number of total NLCD agricultural acres broken into, and proportionate with, the number of agricultural acres observed for these eight simplified agricultural CDL data categories. All pasture and hay acres were included in the satellite-observed acres.

Second, we compared the satellite-observed acres and the CoA data, or ‘reported acres’ provided by producers. Where the number of satellite-observed acres were greater than the number of reported acres, all reported acres were categorized as ‘in-production agriculture’, and the remaining satellite-observed acres were reserved for step three. If the reported acres were greater than the satellite-observed acres, we assigned all satellite-observed acres as ‘in production agricultural’ acres and the remaining reported acres were reserved for step three.

In step three, the remaining uncategorized, reported acres were subtracted from the remaining uncategorized, satellite-observed acres; this difference represents post-agricultural lands that were observed in satellite imagery but not recorded as in-production in the CoA. This step assured that all lands reported as in production in the CoA were accounted for within the estimated number of ‘in production acres’. For more detailed methods, please see The Nature Conservancy (2023) .

Findings

Landowner willingness to convert their land into mature forest was lowest for agricultural lands, with nearly two-thirds (64.7%) of agricultural owners ‘not at all willing’ (Table 2). Half (50.6%) of post-agricultural landowners were ‘not at all willing’ to convert their post-agricultural land. Shrubland owners were the most receptive to converting their land into mature forest, with 26.8% of them ‘very willing’ while 17% of post-agricultural landowners were ‘very willing’ to convert. Agricultural landowners had the lowest level of willingness, with 9.8% of respondents ‘very willing’ to convert their agricultural land into mature forest.

Agricultural land was the most widespread of the three land covers analyzed herein, with approximately 6,018,036 ha (14,870,892 acres) of active agricultural land throughout the state. The second most common land cover type was post-agricultural land, which made up 675,211 ha (1,668,484 acres) statewide. Our estimates of forest expansion opportunity, which combine land cover estimates with landowner willingness, suggest that there is realistic opportunity to expand forest on approximately 334,956 ha (827,694 acres) of agricultural land, post-agricultural land, and shrubland in New York state for which landowners are ‘very willing’ to convert into mature forest (Table 3). This estimate of realistic forest expansion opportunity is less than half (48.7%) of New York State’s 1.7-million-acre goal (or 688,000 ha) and constituted 12.2% of the total agricultural land, post-agricultural land, and shrubland in the state.

Agricultural land constituted a significant proportion (more than half (57.5%) at 192,584 ha ((475,886 acres)) of the total forest expansion opportunity. One-third of the realistic opportunity was post-agricultural land (114,786 ha (283,642 acres), 34.3%), and there was a proportionately small (8.2% of realistic opportunity lands) but noteworthy amount of shrubland (27,586 ha (68,166 acres)), again with over a quarter (26.8%) of shrubland-owning respondents ‘very willing’ to convert their land.

Based on our estimates, there is very little possibility that over four million acres (4,102,587 acres, 1,660,258 ha) would be converted into forest. Three-quarters (76.8%) of this ‘no opportunity’ land (1,275,353 ha, 3,151,467 acres) was agricultural, while one-fifth (20.6%) was post-agricultural land (341,657 ha, 844,253 acres).

If agricultural lands were excluded from the opportunity estimates, then the realistic opportunity (consisting only of post-agricultural land and shrubland) was 142,372 ha (351,808 acres), or 20.7% of the State forest expansion goal and 18.3% of statewide post-agricultural land and shrubland that is currently suitable for conversion into mature forest.

We also estimated the forest expansion growth opportunity, which corresponded to the estimated number of acres of which landowners currently have some level of willingness to convert land into mature forest, but who are neither ‘very willing’ nor ‘not at all willing’. The results suggested that there are approximately 1.8 million acres of agricultural land, post-agricultural land, and shrubland that could be converted into mature forest, if landowner willingness increased. Excluding agricultural lands, there could be approximately 250,905 ha (620,001 acres) of post-agricultural land and shrubland with future conversion opportunity in addition to the 142,372 ha (351,808 acres) noted above with realistic opportunity.

Discussion

Our analysis contributes to the growing realization that the social aspects of climate mitigation strategies—here, landowner willingness—can substantially reshape scientific understandings of opportunity. The estimates of forest expansion opportunity herein are substantially lower than prior forest expansion opportunity assessments that did not account for social constraints (Cook-Patton et al. 2020). Excluding productive agricultural lands, there are roughly 142,000 ha (352,000 acres) of forest expansion opportunity currently in New York state, or roughly one-fifth of the 1.7-million-acre (688,000 ha) goal. If productive agricultural land is included in these estimates, then there may be up to an additional 192,584 ha (475,886 acres) of opportunity. We also found that there is potential for landowner willingness to increase—and thereby increase the acreage with genuine opportunity—under the right circumstances: there are roughly half a million acres (200,000 ha) of post-agricultural lands and another 1.2 M acres (486,000 ha) of agricultural land that landowners have low or moderate levels of willingness to convert into mature forest. The estimates for forest expansion opportunity presented herein should be interpreted as an “upper bound” because survey respondents were more interested in the conversion of their land than nonrespondents Stedman et al. (2024) and because deduction factors were calculated based on an unknown proportion of land by land type and were then applied to all of that land type statewide.

Our findings also highlight the critical tension—and opportunity—regarding agriculture and forest expansion. According to our estimates, state forest expansion goals will only be met if agricultural landowners are engaged in a manner that is consistent with their land use expectations. While only 10% of agricultural landowners were ‘very willing’ to convert their agricultural land, this seemingly low proportion of willingness translates into many thousands of agricultural acres with forest expansion opportunity. We suspect that the actual number of agricultural acres about which landowners are ‘very willing’ to reforest is less than what we estimate here as early evidence suggests that producers may be willing to convert marginal areas that are hard to reach or difficult to farm with modern equipment—lands that make up a small proportion of the agricultural land throughout the state (Lauber et al. 2025). Very little is known about the landowners that are willing to expand forest on agricultural lands, and questions such as the location of tree plantings (e.g., edge of field or stream, row breaks), management techniques (e.g., assisted natural regeneration measures), the production potential of tree plantings (e.g., agroforestry or silvopasture), and the broader compatibilities surrounding local food production vis-a-vis forest expansion for climate mitigation should be further explored. We recommend that practitioners and policymakers design forest expansion programs that are compatible with current agricultural production interests and that leverage social norms around land productivity (e.g., ‘the land should be used’). Programming that supports forest expansion on active agricultural lands would also be applicable to post-agricultural landowners, as many of the post-agricultural land in New York is owned by current or past farm operators. Key considerations such as property taxes and social norms around land productivity (e.g., Liu et al. 2022), could be engaging and effective for owners of these lands.

Increasing landowner willingness to convert land into forest is dependent upon, in part, promoting narratives and examples that activate underlying behavioral drivers—attitudes, social norms, and perceived behavioral control regarding forest expansion. Increasing landowner willingness will require activating landowners’ pro-environmental attitudes, including attitudes about climate action, wildlife habitat, and land stewardship. Demonstrating how forest expansion could be compatible with current uses (e.g., edge of field plantings or timber-compatible species) and generating interest and excitement for land conversion via peer-to-peer observation will shift social norms in support of forest expansion (Evans et al. 2024; Schirmer and Bull 2014; Stedman et al. 2024). We recommend that landowner outreach highlights attitudinal and peer-based reasons for forest expansion to increase landowner willingness.

Increased landowner willingness alone will not lead to forest expansion. Landowners, by and large, need assistance to successfully expand forest on their properties. Stedman et al. (2024) found that landowners perceive major barriers to converting their land—barriers such as equipment, upfront costs, and knowledge. These barriers to perceived behavioral control, and the resulting gap between willingness and behavior, can be mitigated by access to conservation practitioners. Forest expansion programs, typically implemented at the local scale, minimize barriers to learning, information, resources, and program enrollment. Systemic and targeted investments to accelerate forest expansion should include financially meaningful incentive programs to compensate landowners for their monetary costs, time, and labor required to carry out forest expansion. However, financial incentives are not the only—or even primary—consideration for some landowners (Kragt et al. 2017; Ruseva et al. 2015), as many seek out tree planting or tree growing opportunities for intrinsic reasons. Landowners may also be effectively engaged with non-monetary programmatic aspects such as technical assistance, planting and maintenance labor, and access to demonstration sites.

While landowners ultimately decide what occurs on their land, land conversion efforts including forest expansion are often enabled or encouraged by other people and groups such as conservation service providers, neighbors, and private sector interests (Evans et al. 2024). Local conservation staff, including soil and water conservation districts, watershed groups, invasive species units, and extension offices are the gatekeepers of conservation resources. Conservation professionals have technical expertise and knowledge of local environmental conditions that inform project design, and connect landowners to financial, labor, and equipment. Professional assistance is necessary to accelerate tree planting on private lands, yet local conservation organizations rarely have the capacity to implement forest expansion projects at the rate needed to meet tree planting goals (Dobrowski et al. 2024). Technical assistance providers are positioned to provide these services; however, they too need support to implement programs (Schulte et al. 2021), particularly in areas of upland tree planting techniques, landowner outreach, program administration, and fundraising. Investments that support forest expansion goals must include funding for practices on private lands and expanded support practice-based conservation organizations.

Our estimates should be interpreted with a few caveats. First, our estimates do not control for other drivers of landowner willingness such as property size or landowner characteristics. We strongly encourage future integrated, social-landcover models to develop spatialized and multi-factor models, which we suspect will reveal key patterns in conversion opportunity. We also note that our analyses combine data collected at multiple scales (e.g., pixel, county, landowner) that, when aggregated, limit the precision of our work. In fact, our estimates are preliminary and course, both in terms of raw values and spatial location—our intention here is to, in a very general manner, identify the approximate gap between the realistic opportunity and intended amount of forest expansion, not to pinpoint the extent of that gap or to identify spatially explicit priorities. Studies that wish to refine forest expansion opportunity estimates would require a broader consideration of social actors, drivers, and constraints. Lastly, there may be opportunities for forest expansion that our analyses did not include because they exist outside of carbon sequestration programming and are potentially underestimated, such as lands that are typically categorized in remote sensing studies as ‘urban open space’ and that make up 1.5 million acres (607,000 ha) in New York state (Cook-Patton et al. 2020), or forest expansion related to other programmatic areas of focus, such as riparian buffer plantings or forest restoration.

If forest expansion is to occur at the extent and timeline needed for climate mitigation, dramatic and effective changes must be implemented soon. Strategic expansion of federal, state, non-governmental organizations, and municipal programs that incorporate local perspectives and address barriers will bring forest expansion goals within reach. Without policy support and straightforward programs, even the most willing landowners will face challenges in planting and growing trees. The lands that are highly suitable for conversion into mature forest are also sought after for conversion into other uses including solar development, housing, grassland habitats, preserved open space, and others. The conversion of lands for climate mitigation can involve conflict, particularly if local benefits and services fail to outweigh the cost of landscape disruption (Nilson and Stedman 2023). When forest expansion is envisioned, enacted, and legitimated by local people and organizations, the strategy will stand a chance of meeting climate mitigation needs.

Conclusion

Conversion of land into forest for carbon sequestration is a compilation of human actions including active management, transformative policy, and financial investments. This analysis is an early attempt to reevaluate forest expansion opportunity estimates using social science and land use information. Our study is a beginning towards integrating the social aspects of forest expansion with biophysical drivers of climate mitigation opportunity. We encourage more work to refine forest expansion and other NCS opportunity estimates using coupled social-ecological systems approaches. More critically, we encourage policymakers, philanthropists, and private sector partners to support climate mitigation efforts, including those that engage private landowners and local practitioners, in an unprecedented manner.
