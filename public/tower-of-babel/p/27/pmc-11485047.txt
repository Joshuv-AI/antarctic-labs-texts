Structure-based pose prediction: Non-cognate docking extended to macrocyclic ligands
Authors: Ann E. Cleves, Himani Tandon, Ajay N. Jain
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
So-called “cross-docking” is the prediction of the bound configuration of small-molecule ligands that differ from the cognate ligand of a protein co-crystal structure. This is a much more challenging problem than re-docking the cognate ligand, particularly when the new ligand is structurally dissimilar from prior known ones. We have updated the previously introduced PINC (“PINC Is Not Cognate”) benchmark which introduced the idea of temporal segregation to measure cross-docking performance. The temporal set encompasses 846 future ligands for ten targets based on information from the earliest 25% of X-ray co-crystal structures known for each target. Here, we extend the benchmark to include thirteen targets where the bound poses of 128 macrocyclic ligands are to be predicted based on knowledge from structures of bound non-macrocyclic ligands. Performance was roughly equivalent for both the temporally-split non-macrocyclic ligand set and the macrocycle prediction set. Using standard and fully automatic protocols for the Surflex-Dock and ForceGen methods, across the combined 974 non-macrocyclic and macrocyclic ligands, the top-scoring pose family was correct 68% of the time, with the top-two pose families achieving a 79% success rate. Correct poses among all those predicted were identified 92% of the time. These success rates far exceeded those observed for the alternative methods AutoDock Vina and Gnina on both sets.

Introduction

Docking of small molecules to protein binding sites by computational means is now a mature field that was established on rigid ligands in the 1980’s [1]. The first practical methods that addressed ligand flexibility in an automatic fashion appeared in the 1990’s, with AutoDock [2], GOLD [3, 4], Hammerhead [5–7] (the antecedent of Surflex-Dock [8]), and FlexX [9, 10]. These early reports shared a common validation strategy: re-docking of ligands into their cognate protein binding pockets, with success rates typically defined as symmetry-corrected RMSD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document}≤ 2.0 Å. On benchmark sets containing dozens of diverse targets, success rates in the late 1990’s to early 2000’s for top-scoring cognate pose prediction were roughly 70–80% [4, 8, 11]. The focus of pose-prediction assessment then shifted toward non-cognate docking, for two reasons. First, re-docking a ligand from its cognate protein structure is not a real-world problem, as it embeds knowing the correct answer (and the precise adapted protein binding-pocket conformation) in the construction of the prediction task. Second, as a consequence, nominal cognate-docking success rates can be improved by 10–20 percentage points through manipulation of the starting conditions for proteins and ligands [23, 24]. Real-world applications of docking are always non-cognate: predicting the binding mode of a candidate ligand during a design process or of a newly reported chemical series. Influential studies of the cross-docking problem, published in the 2006–2013 time-frame [25–28], generally agreed that performance was variable and target-dependent, with typical success rates of 20–35% at the 2.0 Å RMSD success threshold.Fig. 2Scaffold-type cross-docking: known bound ligand poses for 40/330 BACE-1-bound non-macrocyclic ligands (top left); a binding-pocket alignment tree for all 330 structures (top-middle); structures of the five ligands of the automatically selected binding pockets (top right); the five automatically selected protein/ligand variants (middle left); prediction of the bound pose of the macrocyclic ligand of 3DV1 based on information from non-macrocyclic structures (bottom)

In 2015, the PINC Benchmark [29] introduced the idea of temporal segregation for cross-docking assessment, making use of a 25/75% split for known/future ligand poses. Figure 1 shows a snapshot in time of what was known about CDK2 inhibition using X-ray crystallography as of June, 2003. These represent the earliest one-quarter of CDK2 structures with active-site ligands at the time of curation of the PINC Benchmark. One can see the characteristic, and largely invariant, hinge binding motif of the ligands (red arrows). Fewer than half of the ligands whose bound structures were deposited in the PDB after those shown in Fig. 1 had significant 2D similarity to ligands of the early CDK2 structures. Curation was done automatically (see Methods and Data for details), including all aspects of protein and ligand preparation, mutual alignment of binding sites, and selection of five pocket variants for docking. Prediction on new ligands proceeded from completely memory-free ligand conformations, and accuracy was assessed on ranked and clustered pose families. Figure 1 shows the top-scoring pose family of a “future” ligand (cyan sticks, with the top-scoring pose in thick sticks) compared with the deposited crystallographic coordinates (green sticks).

Figure 2 illustrates the non-macrocycle/macrocycle split for BACE-1, which was an extremely well-populated target, including 330 non-macrocyclic co-crystal structures along with 16 macrocycles. As with the temporal-split curation, the macrocyclic-split was done using automated procedures (see Methods and Data for details). This identified 128 macrocyclic test cases in total, with many examples of synthetic design, as in Fig. 2, as well as non-peptidic natural products (e.g. complex and challenging cases such as rapamycin and FK506). Figure 2 illustrates a typical case where a synthetic BACE-1 inhibitor was designed to rigidify and simultaneously mimic the binding mode of non-macrocyclic inhibitors. The core challenge for macrocyclic ligands lies in the ability of search methods to uncover conformations close to those that bind target proteins [30–34], in addition to the inherent challenges of the cross-docking pose prediction problem.

In addition to alignments being generated based on matches to the protomol [8], similarity to known bound ligand poses is also used where such information is available. This procedure replaces substructural matching [29] for pose guidance based on prior knowledge, and it takes advantage of the eSim method’s accuracy in pose prediction [35]. The eSim method also replaces the Surflex-Sim approach for adjusting docking scores in pose-family ranking, as illustrated in Fig. 3 (bottom middle). The degree of similarity of a predicted pose is compared with the distribution of such similarities of all predicted poses against all known bound ligand poses to provide an adjustment value. The protomol concept began with Hammerhead [5] and has been the primary driver for alignment generation since the introduction of Surflex-Dock [8]. Binding site definition, particularly size and extent, was generally based on a provided ligand or pseudo-ligand from a pocket-finding algorithm along with user-controlled parameters governing how far a protomol would extend beyond the provided scoping information. This has been replaced with a new method that calculates buriedness on a regular 1 Å grid that identifies highly-buried volume (yellow spheres in Fig. 3), moderately-buried volume (orange spheres), and weakly-buried volume (pink spheres). In the CDK2 example shown, the highly-buried region corresponds to the kinase hinge-binding area, with the moderately-buried region moving toward solvent, and the weakly-buried region reaching into the solvent-exposed area proximal to the ATP binding site. The buriedness values are used to adaptively define the scope/coverage of the protomol, which extends well beyond the binding site. The docking search procedure ensures that the more deeply-buried parts of the binding site form part of the geometric alignments that are generated based on the protomol (magenta sticks (bottom right) in Fig. 3). Additional details, including the new Coulombic term in the docking scoring function, are provided in the Methods and Data section.

The combination of ForceGen conformational search and the enhanced Surflex-Dock methods yielded excellent and equivalent performance for both the temporal-split and the macrocycle-split sets, despite the additional challenges presented by macrocyclic ligands. Using standard and fully automatic protocols, across the combined 974 non-macrocyclic and macrocyclic ligands, the top-scoring pose family was correct 68% of the time, with the top-two pose families achieving a 79% success rate. Correct poses among all those predicted were identified 92% of the time. These success rates far exceeded those observed for the alternative methods AutoDock Vina [39] and Gnina [40] on both sets, particularly for macrocyclic ligands.

Temporal split PINC set

The ligands are spread across ten extensively studied pharmaceutically relevant targets: beta secretase 1 (BACE1), carbonic anhydrase II (CA-II), cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), HIV protease (HIV-PR), the non-nucleoside site of HIV reverse transcriptase (HIV-RT), HSP90\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α, map kinase 14 (MAPK14), PPAR\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}γ, protein tyrosine phosphatase 1b (PTP1b), and thrombin (see Methods and Data for additional details). The newly curated set consists of 846 protein-ligand complexes, which was reduced from the original 949 due to elimination of duplicate ligands, removal of ligands from alternative binding sites, and automated quality control in the PDB processing procedure.

The challenge in cross-docking stems from three key issues. First, the protein binding site conformation(s) available for docking may change on binding a ligand that is different from the cognate one, especially if the new ligand is substantially different in structure. Here, because we have employed a 25%-known / 75%-future split, the ligands to be docked contain a large fraction that are very different from the prior known ligands. A second and related issue is that conformational elaboration becomes increasingly challenging, especially for template- and library-based methods, as the structural diversity of the ligands to be docked increases. Because template-based conformational search methods are fit to bound ligand structures that are known at a particular time-point, more recently disclosed molecules present difficulties. Third, binding site definition that is narrowly scoped to prior known binders can bias a docking algorithm against generating even close-to-correct poses, but excessively broadly scoped binding-sites make the search space more challenging.

Figure 4 shows the performance of Surflex-Dock on the full set of ten targets (lower right) and on the seven most representative individual targets. Plots include cumulative histograms for the top 1 (violet), 2 (green), 5 (cyan), and 10 (goldenrod) pose families. The procedure made use of the prior known early ligands during the pose generation process as well as for constructing pose families. Figure 1 shows the full top-scoring pose family for 2XNB-Y8L (lower left). Figure 4 shows the top 20 poses from that pose family. Note that there is some left-to-right variation at the hinge-binding area, with greater heterogeneity seen as the piperazine extends into the solvent (left side). Qualitatively, this type of variation is common across the different targets, with strong protein-ligand interactions (e.g. well-buried polar contacts) exhibiting low variability within pose families and higher variability as ligands extend toward solvent.

The cumulative histograms reflect the minimum automorph-corrected RMSD value for the full set of poses in each of the top N families to the PDB reference structure (note that alternative performance metrics will be discussed below). For the single top-scoring pose family, performance over the entire set at the 2.0 Å RMSD threshold was roughly 66%, generally varying between 60–75%. For the top 2 pose families (red circles in Fig. 4), overall performance was nearly 80%, varying between 75–90%. Performance increased for the top 5 families and less so for the top 10. Note that for HIVRT, the Top 5 and Top 10 were identical and thus appear as the same line.

As mentioned above, one of the new approaches in Surflex-Dock is to make use of the ForceGen conformational search procedure prior to docking. To test if the conformational search beginning from randomized input conformers was adequate, the RMSD of the best pose from the ForceGen conformer ensembles relative to the respective crystallographic pose was determined for each of the 846 future ligands. Figure 4 (lower right, dashed red curve) shows the cumulative histogram of these RMSD values. Overall, 80% of ligands have matches to the crystallographic pose at the 1.0 Å threshold and 99% at the 2.0 Å threshold. Clearly, conformational search was not a limitation on docking performance. In the vast majority of cases, numerous conformations were present in the input ensembles to docking that were close to the experimentally observed, offering many “shots-on-goal” for alignment and further conformational refinement.

Measurement of conformational sampling adequacy provides an optimistic upper bound on possible docking performance. The RMSD values are calculated for each conformer in a pool by identifying the optimal minimum-RMSD alignment of that conformer to the crystallographic reference ligand, which removes a major aspect of the docking search problem. Also, the reference conformer itself is just a model [41], and it generally has been optimized with respect to density fit at the expense of ligand energetics, and without formal contributions from considerations of protein-ligand interactions. Given any docking scoring function and a means to restrain ligand conformational energy, pose optimization will move away from the crystallographic model, typically by 0.1–0.5 Å RMSD. Such optimization within aligned variant non-cognate binding pockets will generally produce larger shifts. Further, scoring functions are not perfect, and clearly incorrect poses may score nominally higher than correct ones. Solutions to the docking problem, especially non-cognate docking, must ensure adequate search to avoid hard failures where no close-to-correct solution is explored. The problem of soft failures, where scoring functions incorrectly rank alternative ligand poses, is also significant. As we will see later, poor cross-docking performance for some methods cannot be rescued by giving the correct answer—the crystallographic pose—as input. Non-macrocycle/macrocycle split set

For this set, the known structures were all bound non-macrocycles, and the ligands whose bound pose was to be predicted were all macrocycles. The set is much smaller (128 ligands across 13 targets) than for the temporal split, owing to the relative scarcity of deposited macrocyclic protein-ligand complexes (see the Methods and Data section for a detailed breakdown). The targets with the largest number of macrocyclic test ligands were: heat shock protein 82 (HSP82), BACE1, factor 11a (FA-11a), FK506 binding protein (FKBP12/5), HSP90, HCV NS3-4A serine protease (HCVP), and myeloid leukemia cell differentiation protein (MCL1).

One can characterize the conformational complexity of a macrocycle based on its “total flexibility”, which includes the total number of exocyclic rotatable bonds plus the total number of rotatable bonds within the macrocyclic ring systems. In our previous work [34], we established that total flexibility of roughly 21–23 rotatable bonds was tractable, yielding the same conformational search accuracy as for non-macrocycles. For the set curated here, all targets but FKBP12/5 fell within the tractable flexibility range.

Figure 5 shows pose prediction performance for Surflex-Dock for four representative individual targets, the specific example of 3DV1, and performance over the full set. The specific case of 3DV1 exhibited a common motif, where conformational variability within pose families was observed within alkyl macrocyclic linkers as well as more solvent-exposed portions of ligands. Strong protein-ligand interactions (e.g. the polar interactions seen in the 3DV1 case) exhibited less variability. Overall pose prediction performance was very similar to that observed for the non-macrocyclic temporal set, with the docking success rate for the top 2 pose families being slightly over 80% at the 2.0 Å threshold.

Pure conformational search effectiveness was also similar between the two sets (dotted red curve, Figs. 4, 5), with 80% of macrocyclic ligands having matches to the crystallographic pose at the 1.0 Å threshold and 100% at the 2.0 Å threshold. Note that there was no statistically significant difference in conformational search performance between the temporal and macrocyclic sets. Surflex-Dock: Macrocyclic challenges

The issue of conformational search adequacy was introduced above. Overall, as shown in Fig. 5 (red dashed line, lower right), macrocyclic sampling was excellent. However, there were two targets, FKBP12/5 and HSP82, for which the default ForceGen conformational search procedure was not sufficient to support the success rates illustrated for the four targets (and overall) in Fig. 5. Figure 6 illustrates the complexities involved with those two cases.

FKBP12/5 was the only target for which macrocyclic ligand flexibility exceeded what has been shown to be tractable for the ForceGen method in prior work [34]. Using the standard search approach, the docking of rapamycin into the macrocycle-naïve ensemble of protein pockets produced a solution among the top 2 with an RMSD of 3.1 Å. The deepest binding pipecolic acid moiety was correctly placed (cyan versus green conformers, Fig. 6 (top)), but the triene responsible for binding mTOR was not correct [42]. However, making use of the deep ForceGen search variant [36, 42] brought performance for the FKBP12/5 targets up to 100% success for the top 2 scoring pose families at 2.0 Å RMSD.

The case of geldanamycin binding HSP82 (and HSP90) is characterized by an unexpected and energetically unfavorable cis amide in the macrocyclic ring system [43] (see red arrows in Fig. 6 (bottom)). Standard ForceGen search does not discover the cis form, resulting in a top-scoring solution with an RMSD of 3.1 Å. Docking results in cases such as this can be improved by making use of prior knowledge of the unexpected conformational behavior of the amide, using a torsional restraint to force the cis amide conformation. Effects of prior knowledge

We introduced the idea of systematic exploitation of prior knowledge of bound ligands in the study that described the PINC Benchmark [29]. There were two methods: 1) substructural matches of a ligand to be docked to prior ligands were used during docking to add to the pool of alignments for further refinement using the docking scoring function; and 2) ligand similarity to prior known ligand poses to refine the ranking of pose families. In that work, knowledge to guide alignments improved performance roughly 5–10 percentage points in docking success rates, and knowledge used to refine pose family rankings improved performance roughly 10–15 percentage points.

As summarized in Fig. 3, here we replace the substructure-based alignments with those generated using the eSim approach [35]. The method is fast enough that it adds negligible computational cost, and it is able to produce quality alignments without perfect substructural matches. The eSim method has also replaced the prior ligand similarity approach in the pose family rank-refinement procedure, which relies on the same statistical physics approach introduced previously (additional details are provided in the Methods and Data section) [29].

Figure 7 shows the effects on performance for top-2 pose family success rates (top) along with an example of the typical types of improvements seen using an HIV protease ligand as an example (bottom). Use of eSim-based alignments to add to those derived from protomol matching increased performance by roughly 7 percentage points for the non-macrocyclic temporal set and roughly 3 points for the macrocycle set. The effect of pose family rank-refinement using eSim was larger, with a nearly 10 point increase for the temporal set and a 5 point increase for the macrocycle set.

Note also that the previously reported performance of the prior Surflex-Dock version using both types of prior knowledge (red dotted lines in Fig. 7) was roughly 5 percentage points worse than for the new approach. The collection of Surflex-Dock improvements resulted in an overall increase in docking accuracy, a reduction in the degree to which prior knowledge was required to achieve high accuracy levels, and similar levels of performance for docking-based prediction of macrocyclic ligand binding modes to those for non-macrocycles.

Conformational search sufficiency

As we saw in Figs. 4, 5, the conformational search adequacy for the Surflex-Dock approach was not the bottleneck in terms of docking performance (bottom right, red dotted lines, in both Figures). The approach relied on the ForceGen methodology to go from a memory-free starting conformation to a pool of diverse low-energy conformational minima. For both sets, over 90% of the time, conformers were identified that were 1.5 Å RMSD or better compared with the crystallographic poses.

With the exception of Gnina’s performance on the temporal set having a success rate of about 60% for the top 2 pose families (upper right, Fig. 8), Vina and Gnina performed poorly, with success rates of 25–40% (top 2 pose families at a 2.0 Å RMSD success threshold). To understand the source of the large performance gap, we re-ran the Vina procedure, but instead of beginning from memory-free conformations, the ligands were docked beginning with their bound conformations. Figure 9 shows the difference in performance between the two conditions for the top 2 pose families. There was a substantial increase in nominal performance when Vina docking began from the right answer.

The performance gain on the temporal non-macrocyclic set was roughly 5–10 percentage points within the 1.0–2.5 Å RMSD success thresholds, with the gap highlighted at the 2.0 Å RMSD threshold with red circles. For the macrocyclic set, the performance gains were over 20 percentage points (again highlighted with red circles), suggesting that conformational search adequacy on macrocycles is a particular limitation for AutoDock Vina. Cognate re-docking of macrocycles

These results parallel a recent study from the developers of the macrocyclic search aspect of Vina [44]. The subject of the study was cognate re-docking of macrocyclic ligands, where ligands were docked beginning from their crystallographic poses. When macrocyclic ring systems were held in their native bound geometries, performance was roughly 20 percentage points better than when macrocyclic flexibility was allowed. Vina’s macrocyclic search functionality degraded docking performance.

Similar results were reported in an early study of macrocyclic docking by Bajorath’s group [45]. They reported cognate re-docking performance for 48 macrocycles using MOE LowModeMD [30] for conformational search (beginning from randomized starting conformers) and MOE’s internal docking method. Overall, at the 2.5 Å RMSD success threshold, they achieved a 52% success rate when docking pre-searched conformational ensembles into the cognate protein binding pockets, roughly matching the 53% reported by the Vina macrocyclic docking study [44] when allowing for ring flexibility.

Note that in the Bajorath group study [45], in 47/48 cases, conformational ensembles contained conformers within 2.5 Å RMSD to the bound crystallographic form, so the LowModeMD search was reasonably effective. The authors suggested that a search protocol be developed for macrocycles that combined the generation of conformational ensembles and subsequent ensemble docking, noting that the identifying bioactive conformations is less challenging than predicting accurate compound binding modes that depend on both conformation and alignment. They noted that a limitation of the docking techniques they studied was the inability to treat extended ring systems in a flexible manner and further optimize conformations during docking.

One of the key aspects of the Surflex-Dock approach, introduced many years ago [23], has been the refinement of ligands poses using all-atom Cartesian optimization. This affects the ability of large molecules to be adapted from initial conformations into those that can fit into a protein binding pocket. In general, scoring functions for docking have necessarily bumpy and rugged energy surfaces that stem largely from repulsive terms to prevent protein-ligand clashes. Surflex-Dock treats input conformer pools with additional flexibility during the docking process, including optimization using internal-coordinate alignment/torsional parameters as well as full Cartesian optimization of atomic coordinates. By doing so, it is possible to ameliorate otherwise unrecoverable problems with close-to-correct docked poses.

Limitations of PDB ground-truth

In this work, following common practice including our own prior work, we have used the deposited PDB coordinates of the ligand as “ground-truth” and have used the first set of coordinates in cases where alternates were present. Figure 10 illustrates the impact of this choice using the 2XNB and 3DV1 examples used throughout the foregoing.

In the case of 2XNB, the deposited PDB structure contained two poses for the ligand Y8L, exhibiting variation in the orientation of the thiazole (methyl pointed left vs. right, see top-left of Fig. 10) as well as the piperazine that extends into solvent. This was discussed in the original PINC Benchmark paper [29], where we speculated that additional conformational heterogeneity may fit the X-ray density better. Subsequent to the original work, we developed an approach to fitting conformational ensembles into X-ray density as an alternative to modeling atomic uncertainty with atom-specific B-factors [41], demonstrating better real-space fits as well as producing lower estimates for bound-ligand strain [46, 47]. The ligand of 2XNB when re-fit into density is modeled better by the collection of six conformers shown in orange (middle top of Fig. 10) than by the two alternate B-factor-weighted conformers in the PDB deposition (left top). The characteristics of the top-scoring docked pose family qualitatively match the xGen conformational ensemble (note that both orientations of the thiazole are present within the top 2 docked pose families).

In the case of 3DV1, the deposited PDB structure contained a single pose for the ligand AR9, with high B-factors on the macrocyclic alkyl linker and the aliphatic left-hand-side (left bottom, lighter blue, Fig. 10). As we have previously noted, variation in macrocyclic alkyl linkers is a commonly observed feature when fitting conformational ensembles into X-ray density [41, 46, 47], and the case of 3DV1 is no exception (bottom middle). As with 2XNB, the 3DV1 ligand ensemble fits the experimentally determined density better than the original B-factor-weighted atomic coordinates. Also, the top-scoring docked pose family exhibits heterogeneity in the same manner as the fitted X-ray ensemble. It is also worth noting that bound strain estimates of the re-fit ensembles for both ligands are significantly lower, by nearly 4 kcal/mol for 2XNB-Y8L and just under 3 kcal/mol for 3DV1-AR9.

We have argued that conformational ensembles are the correct model for bound ligands in X-ray structures, and we believe that a more informative measure of docking accuracy for pose prediction than the traditional approach would measure the concordance of a predicted pose family to an X-ray fitted conformational ensemble. One way to do this would be to compute the automorph-corrected RMSD for each pose within a docked pose family to each conformer of the X-ray fitted ensemble. For each predicted pose, the minimum would be taken as the representative value, and the average over all poses within a family would be reported as the final RMSD. In practice, this would not produce large changes in most cases. However, there has been some degree of controversy in the docking field over the most appropriate measure, and we plan to quantitatively explore this in a future study.

Conclusions

Accurately docking small molecules into protein binding sites, despite being a subject of research and development for over 40 years, remains challenging. This is true both with respect to virtual screening and pose prediction, which is the primary subject of this study. This is especially true for highly flexible molecules and macrocyclic ligands. For the latter, relatively recent studies of cognate re-docking of macrocycles reported limited success [44, 45, 48].

We have argued for many years that cognate ligand re-docking is a relevant problem only as a baseline “sanity-check” primarily for methods developers. Success on that problem is not sufficient to predict good performance on the relevant real-world problem of predicting the binding mode and/or relative rank of novel ligands of a protein target. Failure on cognate-ligand re-docking is diagnostic of a method that is very unlikely to work in a prospective application. There are many issues relating to best practices for measuring docking performance. These have special relevance in the cognate re-docking problem, and they have been discussed in a number of prior studies [23–28, 49, 50].

Here, we have focused exclusively on the cross-docking problem, and we have emphasized two strategies to limit the extent to which predictions to be made could be contaminated by the presence of near-neighbor cases within the information used as part of the prediction. The first strategy is temporal splitting, making use of a limited proportion of early deposited PDB protein-ligand complexes to predict the bound poses of a much larger number of future ligands. The second strategy is scaffold-type splitting, making use of non-macrocyclic protein-ligand complexes to predict the bound poses of macrocyclic ligands. For both the temporal and macrocycle sets, curation of complexes, preparation, alignment, and selection of ensembles was fully automatic and used no knowledge of the prediction sets for selection/preparation of the known sets. Also, test ligand starting poses were completely memory-free.

Results for Surflex-Dock were encouraging, with success rates for top-scoring pose family (at the 2.0 Å RMSD threshold) being 66% for the temporal-split set and 80% for the macrocyclic set, increasing to 78% and 85%, respectively, for top-2 pose families, and roughly 90% for top-10 for both sets. Explicit use of known bound ligand poses enhanced performance. Prior knowledge was used to aid in generating alignments during the docking process as well as in re-ranking pose families following docking. Both uses of prior known bound ligands resulted in a roughly 5-10 percentage point improvement in performance, though the impact was less significant for macrocycles than for non-macrocycles.

Conformational search, driven by the ForceGen approach, was not a bottleneck in terms of docking performance for either set. However, a deeper search variant was required for optimal results on the most flexible macrocyclic ligands in this study, and a torsional restraint was helpful in the case of an energetically unfavored amide conformation in a small number of macrocycles. Automatic selection of five protein binding pocket variants for ensemble docking was sufficient for accurate pose prediction. It was enough to allow ligands to flex in Cartesian space rather than explicitly modeling small atomic movements in binding sites, which is more computationally intensive.

The results for the alternative methods, AutoDock Vina and Gnina, were uniformly inferior, especially for macrocycles. Care was taken to make use of these methods appropriately, and controls were run on multiple platforms under multiple conditions. Results for both Vina and Gnina for the top 100 pose families approached 90% success for non-macrocycles, providing some confidence that the docking procedures were carried out properly. The weakness in terms of top-1 or top-2 pose family performance for non-macrocycles appeared to have been driven by a soft search failure relating to scoring rather than a hard search failure stemming from not generating correct poses at all. For macrocycles, however, docking failures appeared to stem primarily from conformational search limitations, evidenced by the very large improvement observed when beginning from the cognate ligand conformation.

As we have argued before, the ideal situation is for experts with particular methods to report results for those methods on common public benchmarks. We would encourage others who have deep expertise with AutoDock variants like Vina and Gnina to make use of the data archive associated with this study and report their findings. We would also hope that methods developers, particularly for widely used commercial docking approaches such as Glide [11], Gold [4], and FlexX [9], would assess those methods on a challenging cross-docking problem by making use of the temporal and macrocyclic benchmark data described here. This is particularly important due to explicit limitations often placed on publication of independent benchmarking results for commercial methods.

Prior studies have reported limited performance for cognate re-docking of macrocycles [44, 45], even with the simplification of a compliant protein conformation and input ligand conformers being held close to their bound ring geometries. Given that context, the most surprising aspect of the results presented here is that the docking of macrocycles into macrocycle-naïve ensembles of binding pockets, making no use of prior knowledge of bound macrocyclic ligands and beginning from memory-free, completely agnostic, input conformations, performed as well as cross-docking of non-macrocycles. Overall, the combination of the Surflex-Dock and ForceGen approaches yielded cross-docking performance for both sets that rivals other reports of cognate ligand re-docking of non-macrocyclic small molecules. As a practical matter, in the vast majority of cases, correct dockings were returned within the top one or two pose families.

From a theoretical standpoint, we believe that both experimentally determined models of bound ligands and the results of docking studies should move from contemplating single ligand poses to ensembles of poses. Ensembles of poses can offer a better model of ligand fit into X-ray density, both in terms of fit quality and ligand energetics. Results from cross-docking are an orthogonal means for predicting bound ligand configurations. The conformational heterogeneity seen with both approaches is qualitatively highly similar, providing support for the experimental relevance of heterogeneous pools of poses rather than the model of a bound ligand as a single set of atomic coordinates.

Temporal data set

The filtering criteria were designed to preserve the ligand binding sites in the Astex Diverse cognate-docking benchmark of 85 complexes [12]. Macrocycle split data set

Curation of the non-macrocycle/macrocycle split set was done by processing the full set of SMILES structures associated with HET codes in the RCSB PDB LigandExpo (www.ligand-expo.rcsb.org/) to segregate macrocycles from non-macrocycles. Note that peptidic macrocycles are not indexed as “ligands” in the PDB LigandExpo, so this set contains very few examples of macrocycles within significant peptidic character.

Enhanced computational methods

There were four primary enhancements to Surflex-Dock between the original PINC Benchmark introduction and the present study.

Conformational search prior to docking

Beginning with the Hammerhead method and continuing with the introduction of Surflex-Dock, conformational search had been performed dynamically, with a single conformation serving as the input to the docking calculation. With the development of the ForceGen conformational search method, a hybrid approach is now employed that fully addresses both small molecules and macrocycles. A ligand conformer pool is provided as input to Surflex-Dock, with further refinement of conformations taking place during the docking/scoring process.

The ForceGen method, and its deeper iterative search variant (termed “deep ForceGen search”) have been previously reported [33, 34, 36, 42], including extensive comparative benchmarking to alternative methods, especially on peptidic macrocycles. Briefly, the method is non-stochastic, requires no templates, and does not make use of torsional libraries. For ring systems, physical movements are made that are analogous to the ways in which such structures move. For non-macrocyclic systems, forcible “bends” are employed at topologically determined locations, followed by energy minimization and iteration [33, 34]. For macrocycles, “twists” are employed in a similar fashion. Search of exocyclic bonds is done with conventional sampling, which is exhaustive for small molecules and systematic for more flexible cases. The approach produces conformers that represent diverse local energetic minima within an energetic window above the discovered estimate of a global minimum.

For small drug-like molecules, ForceGen conformational elaboration is able to identify close-to-crystallographic conformers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document}≤ 1.5 Å RMSD) beginning from random starting conformations with a roughly 98% success rate [34]. For small to medium-sized synthetic and non-peptidic natural product macrocycles, performance is comparable. The components of the search algorithm can be applied in parallel on common multicore computing hardware, so conformer elaboration for small molecules requires seconds and small- to medium-sized macrocycles of the type represented in this study typically requires just minutes [34].

Large macrocycles (including typical peptidic examples) present additional challenges, and the deep ForceGen search approach was introduced to increase search thoroughness adaptively based on the observed conformational complexity of each given macrocycle [36, 42]. Beginning from a single input conformer, the procedure performs a standard ForceGen search, with the resulting conformer pool being clustered by RMSD. If the resulting lowest-energy clusters contain new conformations compared with prior rounds, search is iterated. Multiple rounds of search are carried out, each time consolidating the full set of conformers into a non-redundant set within a specified energetic window prior to clustering.

The deep ForceGen search variant yields a 91% success rate of identifying close-to-crystallographic conformers (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document}≤ 1.5 Å RMSD) beginning from random starting conformations on the Prime MCS set of 208 macrocycles [32], which is focused largely on peptidic exemplars. Widely used alternative methods yielded performance ranging from 60–80% at the 1.5 Å RMSD success threshold [36].

Use of eSim for exploitation of prior known ligand poses

Prior bound ligands were used in two ways previously. One was to focus on generating alignments based on substructural matches, and the other to re-rank pose families based on molecular similarity to prior known poses using an approach akin to a potential of mean force (PMF) statistical physics method [51]. The new approach makes use of the eSim methodology [35] for both tasks. The eSim method combines surface-shape, directional hydrogen-bonding, and Coulombic fields to compare molecules, and its speed with respect to pose optimization and accuracy with respect to pose prediction offer synergistic benefits for docking.

For the alignment generation process, we have simply made use of the eSim alignment engine rather than substructural matching against known bound ligands. Because the eSim procedure is extremely fast relative to docking, this adds a negligible burden to the overall computational cost. The advantage over substructural matching is that poses generated by eSim based on similarity optimization to prior known ligands can be very close to correct binding modes without exact sub-scaffold matches. The poses generated in this step are in addition to those produced by the protomol-based matching process, and subsequent calculations on the eSim-generated poses during docking are done using the docking scoring function without regard to similarity scores.

Following docking, pose families are produced, which group final docked poses together based on automorph-corrected RMSD. Scores for pose families are Boltzmann weighted average docking scores of family member poses. Pose family rank adjustment based on similarity is now done substituting eSim for the prior Surflex-Sim method. Adaptive scoping of binding sites using buriedness

Generation of the “protomols” [5, 8] that are used to drive alignments of input ligand conformers has been enhanced to exploit calculations of buriedness proximal to protein binding sites, allowing for an automatic adaptive procedure to scope the site to be searched. In addition, the core of the binding site is automatically identified, with the docking search procedure focusing on placing a portion of a ligand into the deepest parts of a binding site. This procedure builds upon an approach to identifying buried pockets that was introduced as an enhancement to the PSIM protein similarity methodology [54].

Following calculation of buriedness on a 1.0 Å voxelized grid, a threshold is identified such that a minimum number of voxels (default 600) have buriedness values exceeding that threshold. The largest connected component of voxels so identified forms the set of core voxels. Within that set, thresholds are set to identify deeply buried and moderately deeply buried (see Fig. 3) voxel locations. When the protomol is used for generating alignment candidates for each conformer from the input ForceGen pool during docking, only those alignments that place the ligand proximal to or penetrating the most deeply buried core voxels are considered.

Because the most deeply buried part of the binding site is a required aspect of generating alignments, the overall size of the protomol may be large. So, rather than relying on user judgment, protomols are adaptively sized in order to reach into solvent-exposed protein surface areas that have a minimal degree of nominal burial. This strategy accommodates sensible docking of larger ligands while not considering unproductive alignments for smaller ligands.
