Underwater biological target detection algorithm based on DVS-YOLO11
Authors: Caiqin Jia, Ling Wang, Shengwen Gao, Guoxiang Wang, Xiao Wang, Xingcheng Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The underwater environment is complex and ever-changing, with light being easily absorbed and scattered, resulting in blurred and color-shifted images. This poses challenges for target detection, often leading to false positives and missed detections. To enhance the accuracy and stability of underwater target detection, an improved YOLO11 algorithm is proposed. First, the C3k2 module is enhanced by introducing a Dynamic Feature Fusion (DFF) mechanism, resulting in the C3k2-DFF module. Leveraging its dynamic weighting capability, this module enhances the representation of multi-scale features, enabling more precise localization of underwater targets. Second, by introducing a Global Context (GC) mechanism to enhance the Visual Feature Enhancement (VFE) module, the VFE-GC module is designed to adaptively recalibrate features using global information, suppressing background noise while amplifying key semantic information of targets. Finally, the Shape-IoU loss function is improved by incorporating the Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD), resulting in the Shape-NWD loss function. The NWD loss function optimizes bounding box regression by enforcing shape consistency constraints, thereby enhancing localization accuracy for irregular or elongated underwater targets. Results show that DVS-YOLO11 achieves an mAP@0.5 of 84.78% and an mAP@0.5:0.95 of 64.67%, along with 87.44% Precision and 75.57% Recall, while maintaining low computational costs. Notably, for scallop targets characterized by blurry edges and irregular shapes, the mAP@0.5 improved from 63.9% to 68.6%. Compared with the baseline YOLO11, all metrics show improvements, indicating that the proposed method works well for underwater target detection.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of global energy transition and surging resource demands, marine resource development has garnered increasing attention1. Underwater target detection and recognition, as critical technologies for military defense, deep-sea scientific research, and cultural heritage preservation, face the dual challenges of insufficient detection accuracy and robustness, constrained by complex target characteristics and harsh underwater environments2.

Currently, deep learning-based object detection algorithms have emerged as the mainstream solutions. Their technical pipelines are primarily categorized into two major paradigms: single-stage algorithms represented by the YOLO (You Only Look Once) series3, SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector)4, and RetinaNet5, as well as two-stage algorithms represented by the R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network) series6.

To address the challenge of underwater target detection, researchers have proposed various methods. Fang Zhenbo et al.7 proposed improvements based on YOLO11: they introduced a Context-Guided Block-Down (CGBD) and multi-scale feature extractor to enhance feature capture capability; designed a lightweight and efficient aggregation module (Reparameterized Ghost Convolution Spatial Pyramid Efficient Layer Aggregation Network, RGCSPELAN) to replace the C3K2 structure, thereby reducing model computational burden; and developed a Detail-Enhanced Convolution Head (DEC-Head) integrating reparameterization and detail-enhanced convolution. This module improves the localization and recognition ability of the detection head while minimizing computational overhead. For YOLOv8-based methods, Hu et al.8 incorporated programmable gradient information and eliminated skip connections to mitigate the feature degradation issue in underwater images. Guo Wenhao et al.9 built upon the YOLO11v real-time detection architecture by introducing the C3k2 with Deformable Convolutional Networks (C3k2-DCN) module to enhance deformable feature extraction. They designed an Attentional Scale Fusion (ASF) mechanism to optimize multi-scale information interaction, and replaced the standard downsampling operation with Space-to-Depth Convolution (SPD-Conv) to preserve fine-grained details of small objects. This improvement reduces the model’s parameter count and computational overhead while boosting the detection accuracy and real-time performance for biological targets in complex underwater environments. Chen et al.10 leveraged the YOLO real-time detection framework to reconstruct the backbone network using deformable convolution v3, optimized the neck network with a unified feature fusion framework, and proposed a dynamic ReLU-decoupled detection head. These modifications improve the detection accuracy of small underwater targets, reduce the number of model parameters, and enhance the overall model performance. Beyond structural modifications, recent studies validate vanilla YOLOv8m for underwater fish detection. Bajpai et al.11 established a robust baseline on specialized datasets, outperforming Faster-RCNN and SSD with an F1 score of 64.31%. However, their method focuses solely on fish detection and relies on a single optimization dimension, without addressing the broader challenges of multi-class underwater target detection with irregular shapes, small sizes, and severe degradation. This motivates our joint optimization strategy, which simultaneously addresses feature enhancement, multi-scale fusion, and bounding-box regression for diverse underwater targets. Zhao et al.12 proposed DETR, which leverages a Transformer encoder-decoder with bipartite matching to achieve anchor-free and NMS-free detection. However, despite its end-to-end elegance, DETR suffers from high computational cost, slow training convergence, and degraded performance on small objects – limitations that are particularly problematic for underwater scenarios characterized by densely distributed small targets. Liu et al.13 built upon the DECO convolution architecture by introducing a hybrid encoder to fuse multi-scale features and designed a deformable convolution to mitigate query misalignment. They also proposed an underwater color denoising query and a separate contrastive denoising training strategy. This approach preserves the end-to-end merits while enhancing detection accuracy and embedded deployment efficiency under underwater color shift and noise. Although these models achieve high detection accuracy, they still suffer from excessive parameter counts, and their robustness in extreme environments and generalization ability across wider scenarios remain to be verified.

Despite the proliferation of YOLO-based underwater detection methods, most existing improvements focus on a single aspect of the detection pipeline. For instance, some works enhance feature extraction through attention mechanisms, others improve multi-scale feature fusion, and a few modify the loss function for better localization. However, underwater degradation–characterized by light scattering, low contrast, and small target blurring–is inherently multifaceted. Improving only one dimension leaves other bottlenecks untouched, limiting overall performance gains. In contrast, this work simultaneously addresses three critical components: feature enhancement (via a VFE-GC module), feature fusion (via a C3k2-DFF module), and loss function optimization (via a Shape-NWD loss). This joint optimization strategy is designed to tackle underwater challenges in a more comprehensive manner. It is important to emphasize that these contributions represent not merely a naive combination or trivial engineering fine-tuning of existing off-the-shelf vision modules. Instead, each component undergoes targeted architectural redesign and dedicated computational flow reconfiguration tailored to the unique challenges of underwater detection, rather than directly transplanting universal attention, feature fusion, or loss structures from general open-domain detection tasks. Methods

The YOLO11 algorithm released by Ultralytics in September 2024 achieves more efficient model design while maintaining the real-time performance of YOLOv8 through architectural innovation, attention mechanism reconstruction, and loss function optimization, with support for multi-platform deployment. To address typical challenges in underwater target detection, such as low detection rates for small targets and false positives in complex backgrounds, the proposed DVS-YOLO11 improved framework undergoes targeted optimization for underwater complex environments, Firstly, we introduce the Dynamic Feature Fusion (DFF) module into the backbone network to refine the C3k2 module14, thereby designing the C3k2-DFF module. This architecture replaces the original standard C3k2 block; through dynamic feature fusion mechanisms, it enhances the expression of multi-scale features, boosting the model’s accuracy in extracting underwater fuzzy and small targets. Secondly, we embed a Vision Feature Enhancement (VFE)-based Global Context module (VFE-GC) into the feature fusion path of the neck network15. Leveraging global contextual information, this module performs weighted enhancement on features to further suppress background noise interference while amplifying the key semantic information of targets. Thirdly, based on the shape-based Intersection over Union (IoU) loss function (Shape-IoU)16, we introduce a Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD) metric17. By replacing the traditional IoU with Shape-NWD and incorporating shape consistency constraints to optimize bounding box regression, the proposed method shows improved performance in detecting irregular or elongated underwater targets. VFE-GC module

Light scattering and absorption effects are prevalent in underwater imaging environments, leading to degraded image contrast, blurred details, and attenuated semantic information–a problem that becomes particularly severe after multiple rounds of feature downsampling18. To address the disconnect between the physical modeling of underwater imaging and module design, we first establish the physical model governing underwater image formation, providing a theoretical foundation for the VFE-GC module. The observed degraded underwater image I(x) can be described by the following formula:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I(x) = J(x) \cdot t(x) + B(x) \cdot (1 - t(x)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, J(x) denotes the clear irradiance of the scene, t(x) represents the medium’s transmittance reflecting light absorption-induced attenuation, while B(x) indicates background light contributing to scattering interference. To reconstruct the clear image J(x), these two components must be processed sequentially: first, eliminating the scattering term B(x) · (1 - t(x)), then compensating for attenuation caused by the absorption term t(x). This physical decomposition directly underpins the design principle of “first mitigating scattering, then compensating absorption” proposed in this paper. In real underwater optical propagation, light first encounters scattering interference from suspended particles and then suffers energy attenuation due to medium absorption, making this sequential processing strategy consistent with the natural degradation order of underwater images.

It should be clarified at the outset that the proposed VFE-GC does not perform explicit pixel-wise inversion of the underwater imaging model, nor does it claim to numerically estimate the physical parameters t(x) or B(x). Rather, the physical model in Equation (1) serves as a structural design prior that constrains the topological layout, branch functionality, and execution sequence of the attention-based feature enhancement module. Specifically, we impose a fixed two-stage forward pass—backscatter suppression first, absorption compensation second—that mirrors the natural causal order of underwater degradation. Each computational branch is semantically assigned to one of the two degradation terms in Equation (1), ensuring that the module operates under degradation-prior guidance rather than as an unconstrained black-box attention refiner. This design philosophy positions our method as physics-inspired feature enhancement, distinguished from both pixel-level physical restoration and purely data-driven attention refinement.

Existing mainstream schemes for mitigating such underwater degradation mostly adopt independent pre-enhancement pipelines. A typical representative work constructs a DDPM-based diffusion restoration framework, combined with an AECB-enhanced Transformer19, to recover degraded underwater images in a separate preprocessing stage prior to detection. Nevertheless, this two-stage enhancement-detection paradigm inevitably accumulates preprocessing errors and introduces extra computational overhead for independent image restoration. Unlike such a separate enhancement strategy, our VFE-GC incorporates underwater imaging priors and embeds feature enhancement within the detection backbone, thereby enabling end-to-end inline noise suppression and feature optimization without additional standalone preprocessing.

Guided by this principle, we introduce an improved Visual Feature Enhancement module (VFE-GC) at critical nodes of the Path Aggregation Network (PANet) in YOLO11. Unlike conventional methods that only conduct generic feature extraction in the backbone network, we strategically deploy VFE-GC after the downsampling of small-target (P3) and medium-target (P4) features along the top-down transmission path of PANet. This module draws inspiration from the physical modeling logic of “first mitigate scattering, then compensate for absorption effects,” aiming to restore the original visual representations corrupted by water interference via a staged compensation mechanism before features enter multi-scale fusion (Concat). This proactive optimization paradigm of “enhance first, then fuse” not only eliminates reliance on additional depth information but also blocks the accumulation of noise during multi-scale feature propagation. Working principle of the VFE-GC module.

The VFE-GC module is designed to suppress low-frequency noise in underwater images and enhance high-frequency details. The module receives a 4-dimensional tensor [B, C, H, W], where B represents the batch size, C denotes the number of channels, and H and W indicate the spatial dimensions (height and width). First, the input feature map is flattened and transposed into a 3-dimensional tensor [B, N, C], where N = H × W denotes the number of spatial tokens. This transforms the spatial structural features into sequential features for attention calculation.

The core of VFE-GC is a cross-attention mechanism implemented via multi-head attention layers. Specifically, the module employs a fixed global query vector (dimension: [1, 1, C]) that is broadcast across the batch dimension to [1, B, C]. The flattened spatial feature [B, N, C] is then transposed to [N, B, C] and used as both the key and value inputs. The cross-attention mechanism uses the global query as the query input and the transposed spatial features as both the key and value for computation. This avoids the high computational overhead associated with global spatial self-attention. The attention output dimension is [1, B, C]. After transformation, the noise-degraded component is extracted and subtracted from the original flattened features, yielding clean features [B, N, C]. Subsequently, an MLP with \tanh activation function generates an enhanced weight map of the same dimension [B, N, C], applying exponential-weighted adaptive enhancement to the clean features. Finally, the enhanced features are reshaped into the original 4-dimensional structure [B, C, H, W] and passed to subsequent detection layers.

This design reflects the two-stage physical degradation order discussed above. During the scattering mitigation phase, the cross-attention mechanism estimates the global scattering component S, corresponding to B(x) · (1 - t(x)) in the physical model. In the absorption compensation phase, the MLP predicts the light absorption weight matrix W to characterize the medium’s transmittance t(x). Through this staged compensation mechanism, the original visual representation distorted by water bodies is restored. This “mitigate scattering first, then compensate for absorption” optimization approach not only eliminates reliance on additional depth information but also effectively prevents noise accumulation during multi-scale feature propagation.

Compared with the original VFE module, VFE-GC first discards the reliance on external depth features d and instead constructs an endogenous spatial context representation by flattening the raw visual features v and applying global average pooling. In summary, the VFE-GC module distinguishes itself from conventional attention-based feature refinement approaches in a fundamental way: the attention mechanism serves merely as an implementation tool for global context modeling, whereas the core design principle and structural constraints are entirely derived from the underwater imaging physics. The module’s deployment position, forward execution order, branch functionality, and feature post-processing are all explicitly prescribed by the physical degradation sequence rather than being empirically determined through blind search. The degradation-aware design of our method, therefore, originates from these explicit structural constraints imposed by the imaging model, rather than from any inherent property of the attention operator itself. This design ensures that the enhanced features passed to subsequent detection layers carry not only high signal-to-noise ratios but also degradation-adaptive representations that are sensitive to underwater imaging distortions.

C3k2-DFF module

In the native architecture of YOLO11, the C3k2 module serves as the core building block for both the backbone and neck networks20. By employing the Cross Stage Partial (CSP) strategy, it efficiently aggregates multi-scale features while maintaining a lightweight design and achieving strong feature extraction capability21. However, the bottleneck layer within the standard C3k2 module still adopts a static residual fusion method based on element-wise summation, which defaults to equal feature contributions and aligned feature distributions between the main path and the residual path. In complex underwater scenarios, semantic gaps and noise interference exist between deep semantic features and shallow detail features. This simplistic additive operation tends to dilute or obscure critical target information, limiting the model’s discriminative ability for hard samples such as small underwater targets and objects with blurred boundaries. To this end, we propose an improved C3k2-DFF module by embedding a Dynamic Feature Fusion (DFF) mechanism into the Bottleneck unit of C3k2, enabling adaptive recalibration of residual connections. This preserves the efficiency of the CSP topology while enhancing feature coordination capabilities. Working principle of the C3k2-DFF module.

C3k2-DFF inherits the dual-convolution CSP structure and block design of the native C3k2. It uses 1×1 convolutions for channel splitting and dimensionality reduction, preserving the original advantages of computational efficiency and gradient propagation, while carrying out critical restructuring inside the core Bottleneck. Specifically, it replaces the traditional fixed-weight residual addition with DFF dynamic fusion operations: first, it performs channel concatenation on the input features and convolution-transformed features, extracts global context via adaptive average pooling, and generates channel gating weights through 1×1 convolution and Sigmoid activation to suppress noise responses in invalid channels. Subsequently, it employs two parallel 1×1 convolution branches to model the spatial saliency of the input and convolved features, respectively, generating pixel-level dynamic masks via Sigmoid activation to further highlight key features in target regions. Finally, it applies channel gating and spatial masking sequentially to the concatenated features, outputting recalibrated fusion results that replace the original element-wise addition and achieve dynamic coordination of multi-source feature conflicts.

Existing mainstream dynamic feature fusion modules, such as BiFPN, ASFF, and AFF/iAFF, are predominantly deployed in the network neck region, representing cross-scale inter-layer fusion approaches. Specifically, BiFPN facilitates multi-scale feature interaction through bidirectional information flow and learnable weights22; ASFF focuses on multi-scale spatial feature alignment; while AFF/iAFF performs channel and spatial joint weighting solely at the feature output stage. None of these methods integrates into the internal feature transformation process within the backbone network. The proposed C3k2-DFF embeds the DFF module into the residual path of the C3k bottleneck, enabling intra-layer feature fusion. During feature generation, this module integrates channel-level global attention with spatial pixel-level attention, applying dual adaptive weighting to features from both the main and residual branches. This enhances spatial weight learning across both branches and modulates the interaction between deep semantic representations and shallow detail features. Compared to the native DFF and mainstream fusion modules, the C3k2-DFF features differentiated designs in fusion position, operational granularity, and attention combination strategy. Additionally, addressing the characteristics of underwater images–where high-frequency details are easily lost, and low-frequency noise is prominent–the module incorporates a frequency-aware feature selection mechanism. In summary, this solution represents more than a simple combination and engineering optimization of existing modules; it differs from them in its structural design and its adaptability to the underwater scenario. Unlike BiFPN and ASFF, which perform cross-scale fusion at the neck after feature extraction, our intra-layer fusion within the residual path preserves high-frequency details essential for small underwater targets, as these details are otherwise smoothed during multi-scale aggregation. Moreover, compared to AFF/iAFF, which applies weighting only at the output stage, our frequency-aware selection operates during feature generation, suppressing low-frequency underwater noise before it propagates to subsequent layers. This early-stage intervention is specifically suited for underwater scenarios, where noise dominates, and detail preservation is critical for reliable detection.

Furthermore, to balance feature fusion capability and model inference efficiency, the DFF module adopts a lightweight overall design. Its attention branch is constructed solely using 1×1 pointwise convolutions and adaptive global average pooling, without incorporating computationally intensive operators such as 3×3 standard convolutions or deep convolutions. These lightweight operators require few parameters and low floating-point operations; thus, embedding the DFF into the Bottleneck and C3k2 architectures does not significantly increase the network’s parameter size or computational load. As a result, the model achieves improved accuracy while maintaining its lightweight architecture and fast inference speed.

This enhancement enables the native C3k2 architecture of YOLO11 to maintain lightweight performance while achieving improved dynamic feature representation capabilities. It mitigates challenges such as uneven feature distribution and noise interference in underwater scenarios, improving the model’s detection accuracy for small underwater targets and blurred boundaries. Consequently, it provides more precise feature support for four-category underwater biometric recognition tasks.

Loss function shape-NWD

In underwater target detection tasks, targets often appear as “weak-feature objects” with small sizes, blurred contours, and irregular shapes due to light attenuation and interference from suspended particles. Traditional IoU-based loss functions struggle to constrain the geometric alignment between predicted bounding boxes and ground-truth bounding boxes, leading to localization drift or missed detections, especially for small targets. To address this, we introduce the Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD) mechanism based on Shape-IoU, constructing a Shape-NWD loss function tailored for underwater scenarios to enhance the stability and accuracy of bounding box regression for small underwater organisms such as sea cucumbers, starfish, and scallops.

Shape-IoU calculates the loss by focusing on the shape and aspect ratio of the bounding boxes themselves, demonstrating advantages in handling complex, irregularly shaped targets. It accurately quantifies the shape overlap between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes, capturing the true contours of underwater target regions, thereby improving detection precision.

In this paper, we combine Shape-IoU with the Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD) to replace the original bounding box regression loss of YOLO11 for training integration. During model training, both predicted boxes and ground-truth boxes are simultaneously fed into the Shape-IoU and NWD calculation modules. The final localization loss is constructed as a weighted fusion, with the formula:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Loss} = 0.8 \times (1 - \text {Shape-IoU}) + 0.2 \times (1 - \text {NWD}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This combined loss leverages both the shape-aware modeling capability of Shape-IoU and the robustness of NWD to small objects and noisy annotations. It is fully embedded into the network’s regression branch and participates in backpropagation throughout training, guiding the model to learn more precise bounding box features.

The proposed Shape-NWD loss replaces the original CIoU loss in YOLO11’s regression branch. The total training loss is formulated as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathscr {L}_{\text {total}} = \mathscr {L}_{\text {cls}} + \mathscr {L}_{\text {loc}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{cls}$$\end{document} is the original classification loss (BCE), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {L}_{loc}$$\end{document} is our Shape-NWD loss defined above. The weighted coefficients α = 0.8 for Shape-IoU and (1-α ) = 0.2 for NWD are empirically determined based on preliminary experiments on the DUO validation set, where we observed that Shape-IoU contributes more significantly to overall localization accuracy. During training, for each forward pass, the predicted boxes and ground-truth boxes of foreground anchors are simultaneously fed into both the Shape-IoU and NWD modules. The two loss components are computed in parallel and then fused with the fixed weights. The combined loss is back-propagated through the network to update the regression head parameters. Robustness evaluation under complex underwater conditions

To further validate the proposed method’s generalization capability in real-world underwater scenarios, this paper conducts robustness evaluations under various typical underwater imaging degradation conditions. Specifically, considering common challenges in practical applications–including water turbidity, insufficient lighting, and complex background textures–the test set is automatically divided into multiple subsets with varying degradation levels based on objective image metrics, thereby simulating diverse underwater environments.

First, to evaluate detection performance under varying turbidity levels, this paper employs Laplacian variance to characterize image blurring, combined with the mean variance across RGB channels. This combined metric automatically divides the test set into three categories: clear, moderately turbid, and severely turbid. Second, based on the average brightness of grayscale images, test samples are classified into high-light, normal-light, and low-light conditions to assess the model’s stability under different illumination scenarios. Finally, image information entropy is used to quantify background complexity and interference intensity, automatically classifying the test set into low, medium, and high background interference levels.

The degradation factors considered in our robustness evaluation–water turbidity, illumination variation, and background interference–are representative of the most common challenges encountered in real-world underwater imaging. Crucially, our evaluation is based on objective image quality metrics (Laplacian variance for turbidity, grayscale brightness for illumination, and information entropy for background clutter) applied to real test images, rather than relying on synthetic degradation generation. This ensures that the evaluated degradations reflect authentic underwater imaging conditions rather than artificially generated distortions. Moreover, the three degradation dimensions correspond to physically meaningful causes: turbidity arises from suspended particles causing scattering, illumination variation results from light attenuation in water, and background interference reflects environmental complexity. Thus, our dataset-based degradation simulation provides a credible proxy for real-world domain variation.

The classification boundaries and determination criteria for each sample group are as follows. For water turbidity grouping, this study employs a fixed threshold: samples are classified as clear when the image Laplacian variance is \ge 100 and the mean RGB three-channel variance is \ge 25; as severely turbid when the Laplacian variance is łe 30 or the mean RGB three-channel variance is łe 10; all remaining samples are categorized as moderately turbid. For illumination and background interference grouping, a global data percentile-based adaptive partitioning method is applied by calculating the brightness and information entropy values across all test set samples. The 33rd and 66th percentiles are selected as the binary classification thresholds. Samples with brightness values below the 33rd percentile are classified as the low-brightness group; those between the 33rd and 66th percentiles as the normal-brightness group; and those above the 66th percentile as the high-brightness group. For the information entropy metric, samples below the 33rd percentile are categorized as the low background interference group, those between the 33rd and 66th percentiles as the moderate background interference group, and those above the 66th percentile as the high background interference group. The data in Table 6 demonstrate that the gradual incorporation of the VFE-GC module, C3k2-DFF module, and Shape-NWD loss function improves model performance. The detailed analysis is as follows:

First, we examined the improvement effects of each individual module. After embedding the VFE-GC module into the baseline model (Model 2), Precision increased from 85.35% to 87.38% (+2.03%), Recall improved from 74.71% to 75.08% (+0.37%), mAP@0.5 rose to 83.98% and mAP@0.5:0.95, which reflects localization accuracy, rose from 63.81% to 64.77% (+0.96%). By establishing a dedicated visual feature enhancement pipeline, this module mitigates feature loss caused by light scattering and object blurring in underwater environments and improves the stability of feature extraction for small, low-contrast targets. It primarily enhances Precision by suppressing false positives through improved feature robustness, while also contributing modest gains to recall and localization accuracy by providing cleaner, more reliable input features for subsequent modules.

Second, incorporating only the Shape-NWD loss function into the baseline model (Model 3) improved Recall from 74.71% to 77.05% (+2.34%), while Precision decreased from 85.35% to 83.68% (−1.67%) and mAP@0.5:0.95 slightly decreased from 63.81% to 63.76% (−0.05%). Unlike conventional methods, this loss function treats bounding boxes as Gaussian distributions and measures box similarity using the normalized Wasserstein distance. It overcomes the gradient vanishing limitation of traditional IoU loss in small target overlap regions, reducing missed detections of irregularly shaped underwater targets. The trade-off between Recall gain and Precision loss indicates that Shape-NWD primarily targets the recall side, with localization accuracy maintained at a level comparable to the baseline.

Third, when the C3k2-DFF module is added alone (Model 4), Precision improves from 85.35% to 87.51% (+2.16%), Recall slightly decreases from 74.71% to 74.48% (−0.23%), and mAP@0.5:0.95 increases from 63.81% to 64.71% (+0.90%). This module facilitates interaction between deep semantic information and shallow detail features, enhancing the model’s ability to distinguish targets in cluttered underwater backgrounds. It primarily improves Precision and localization accuracy by dynamically fusing multi-scale features, strengthening the representation of target boundary details, effectively suppressing background interference, and reducing false positives and duplicate detections.

Finally, when all three modules are combined (Model 6), the components exhibit synergistic effects. Quantitatively, compared to the baseline, the individual contributions of VFE-GC, Shape-NWD, and C3k2-DFF to mAP@0.5 improvement were +0.61%, +0.20%, and +0.29%, respectively. When all three modules were integrated, the overall mAP@0.5 improved by 1.41% relative to the baseline, outperforming any single module alone. Beyond the overall mAP improvement, the synergistic combination also yields balanced gains across the three key metrics: Precision reaches 87.44% (baseline: 85.35%, +2.09%), Recall reaches 75.57% (baseline: 74.71%, +0.86%), and mAP@0.5:0.95 (localization accuracy) reaches 64.67% (baseline: 63.81%, +0.86%). Notably, the combined model achieves the highest Precision among all configurations (87.44%), while maintaining competitive Recall (75.57%) and localization accuracy (64.67%). This balanced improvement across all three metrics indicates that the synergistic combination effectively integrates the complementary strengths of each module without sacrificing performance on any single metric.

Conclusion

Given the limitations of traditional target detection models in terms of adaptability and detection accuracy in complex underwater environments, this paper proposes an improved underwater target detection model based on YOLO11. First, the C3k2-DFF module is introduced into the backbone network to replace the original structure, enhancing the detection precision of small targets through dynamic fusion. Second, the VFE-GC module is embedded in the neck network to leverage global context for feature enhancement and background noise suppression. Finally, the Shape-NWD loss function is adopted to optimize bounding box regression, improving the localization ability for targets with irregular shapes. The experimental results indicate that the proposed improved model achieves competitive detection performance on underwater datasets compared with existing methods. Nevertheless, this study still has certain limitations. The experiments are primarily conducted on publicly available underwater datasets with relatively limited scene diversity, and the model’s generalization in real, complex underwater environments (e.g., extremely turbid and low-illumination conditions) requires further verification. Future work will focus on two aspects: on the one hand, expanding the experimental scenarios by collecting real underwater images to verify the model’s generalization ability; on the other hand, conducting model compression and acceleration to facilitate its eventual deployment in practical underwater scenarios. Specifically, we plan to explore knowledge distillation, where a larger teacher model is used to guide a lightweight student network, reducing model size and inference latency while preserving detection accuracy. Meanwhile, structured pruning will be studied to remove redundant weights for further lightweight implementation on embedded devices.
