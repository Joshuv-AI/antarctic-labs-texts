Cadaveric dissection studies in regional anesthesia – A comprehensive bibliographic analysis
Authors: Abhijit S. Nair, P. M. Navveen, Reshma Nath, Sandeep Diwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The anatomical basis and safety optimization of regional anesthesia techniques are supported by cadaveric studies. However, the development of this field of study has not been thoroughly documented. To map publication trends, collaborative networks, and emerging themes in cadaveric studies on regional anesthesia, we conducted a bibliometric analysis. A Scopus database search using the keywords ‘cadaveric studies’ and ‘regional anesthesia’ revealed 298 articles from inception till May 2025. The comma-separated values (CSV) file was uploaded to the VOS viewer and CiteSpace software for analyzing various bibliometric parameters like bibliographic coupling, citation, co-authorship, co-occurrence, and co-citation. The results revealed that most of the work was done and published in high-income countries (the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada), in established universities (University of Toronto, Canada), and in high-impact journals (Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine). Future studies should prioritize multicenter, cross-cultural cadaveric validations, standardized anatomical nomenclature, and stronger collaboration among anatomists and anesthesiologists to translate cadaveric insights into clinical protocols and enhance patient safety.

Introduction

Cadaveric dissection remains the gold standard for understanding three-dimensional relationships of nerves, muscles, fascial planes, and vascular structures. Handling real tissue provides tactile feedback on fascial compliance and needle-tissue interaction that no simulation can fully replicate. In the practice of regional anesthesia, where precise needle placement determines block success, cadavers serve as “silent teachers” to bridge textbook anatomy and live-patient variability.[123]

There are various embalming techniques, each with its advantages and disadvantages. The techniques are Thiel soft embalming, formalin-based embalming, fresh-frozen cadavers, saturated salt solution, and plastination.[4] Thiel or soft-embalmed cadavers offer realistic ultrasound image quality, enabling trainees to master sonoanatomy before live-patient practice. The various peripheral nerve blocks and fascial plane blocks have been validated and implemented into practice only after understanding the spread dynamics done in cadavers. These studies help in understanding the extent of spread, the direction of the spread, and the possible dermatomal coverage.

Bibliometric analysis, a form of quantitative research that examines networks, patterns, and trends within a particular field of study, is used to assess various aspects of published academic literature. To assess research productivity, citation dynamics, and collaborations between researchers, institutions, and nations, this approach makes use of tools and validated software. Bibliometric studies identify influential works, point out research gaps, and offer insightful information about how knowledge has evolved—all of which help to direct future research.[56]

To date, no bibliometric analysis has been done on cadaveric studies related to regional anesthesia. This article aims for a bibliometric analysis to know the various research trends, prolific authors, institutes, countries, journals, and the citation metrics of publications related to cadaveric studies pertaining to regional anesthesia.

Methods

We searched the literature from its inception to May 2025. We conducted a comprehensive search on the Scopus database using the following search strategy: (TITLE-ABS-KEY (cadaveric AND study) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (cadaveric AND dissection) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (cadaveric AND experiments) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (regional AND anesthesia) OR TITLE-ABS-KEY (nerve AND block)) AND (EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “VETE”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “AGRI”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “ENGI”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “MATE”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “COMP”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “SOCI”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “MATH”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “ENVI”) OR EXCLUDE (SUBJAREA, “EART”))

The Scopus file was stored in comma-separated values (CSV) format. The CSV file was systematically screened by two authors (PMN and RN). Articles in which there were no details about cadaveric studies related to regional anesthesia, peripheral nerve block, or fascial plane block were removed. The final articles were processed for bibliometric analysis.

Data processing

After removing the ineligible articles and cited references, the included hits were exported for analysis. For bibliometric analysis, VOSviewer (Version 1.6.20, Leiden University, the Netherlands) was used.[7] We planned five different kinds of analysis using the VOS viewer. These analyses included bibliographic coupling, citation, co-authorship, co-occurrence, and co-citation. The units of analysis for co-authorship analysis were countries, organizations, and authors. The units of analysis for co-occurrence were index, author, and all keywords. Documents, sources, authors, organizations, and countries served as the units of analysis for citation and bibliographic coupling. The units of analysis for co-citation were cited authors, cited sources, and cited references. We also used CiteSpace (version 6.3), a Java-based application developed by Chaomei Chen for visualizing and analyzing trends and patterns in scientific literature.[8]

Results

The initial search of the Scopus database resulted in a total of 437 hits. After screening all the titles and subsequent details, we excluded 139 titles that did not fulfill the inclusion criteria. Thus, a total of 298 titles were subjected to the bibliometric analysis. The CSV file was then uploaded to the VOS viewer software for bibliometric analysis in various categories. For CiteSpace analysis, the CSV file was converted into a Web of Science-compatible format using CiteSpace’s built-in conversion tool, which enables the breakdown of bibliographic records. Discussion

This bibliometric analysis has utilized several quantitative tools, like publication counts, citation networks, and co-word clustering, to identify research trends and the influence of authors, organizations, and various sources publishing the various categories of these documents.

Cadaveric studies in regional anesthesia provide a controlled environment to visualize injectate spread, improve sonoanatomy, and test mechanistic hypotheses that would be unethical or impossible to perform prospectively in patients. Cadaveric dye-dissection studies provide the three-dimensional roadmap necessary to refine block techniques, understand the flow dynamics, and prevent complications.[111] Quantitative imaging in embalmed specimens uncovers variability that can inform clinical volume and needle-placement guidelines. High-fidelity cadaver simulators bridge the gap between synthetic phantoms and live-patient scenarios, enhancing trainee confidence and safety. The results of the various cadaveric studies can be useful for practical refinements across technique, safety, training, and quality assurance in regional anesthesia. These articles help in refining the block technique, improving the needle trajectory, facilitating competency, and increasing confidence in performing various blocks, better safety profile of the blocks due to better understanding, all leading to better analgesic efficacy and successful blocks.

McLeod et al.[12] assessed the characteristics of the US-guided axillary approach to brachial plexus block in two Thiel embalmed cadavers. The authors mentioned that it was easy to identify the nerves, track the needles, perceive the fluid surrounding the nerves after injection, and detect intraneural injections by accident. They recommended Thiel-based cadavers for training in US-guided regional anesthesia. Benkhadra et al.[13] compared characteristics of fresh versus Thiel embalmed cadavers for US-guided RA of the cervical region. Although the two groups were comparable in terms of sex, age, and body mass index, the visibility of muscles, especially the sternocleidomastoid, and pop perceived and nerve swelling were better visualized in Thiel embalmed cadavers.

In a paper published in 2007, Tsui et al.[1415] emphasized that training programs for regional anesthesia for upper and lower extremities need to be improved by using cadavers more often as training models, which could help teach the pertinent anatomy and methodology of both ultrasound-guided techniques (through static and real-time imaging and needling practice) and traditional blocks (through dissections and needling practice). In an article published in 2011, Hocking and McIntyre emphasized real-time nerve blocks on cadavers to teach and master ultrasound-guided nerve blocks.[16] They mentioned that the success and safety of ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia depend on correct image acquisition, accurate needle guidance, and appropriate spread of the injected local anesthetic. Kessler et al.[17] published the findings of their US-guided nerve block teaching (brachial, lumbar, and sacral plexus) in 12 optimized embalmed cadavers. The authors mentioned that all the desired nerves and the adjacent structures were visible as clearly as in the living, and therefore, suggested using cadavers for teaching purposes.

Cadaveric studies comparing anterior, lateral, and posterior quadratus lumborum block revealed approach-dependent interfacial and cranio-caudal spread, which contributes to the explanation of variable clinical dermatomal coverage.[18] Several cadaveric studies described a possible mechanism of action of the erector spinae plane block. These mechanistic results highlight the distinct significance of cadaveric research.[919] The purpose was not only to demonstrate effectiveness but also to assess anatomical plausibility and pinpoint areas where clinical results could differ due to the injectate pathway’s lack of reliability. Around the knee, various cadaveric studies unraveled the distinct properties and spectrum of fascia iliaca compartment block, adductor canal block, pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block, and iPACK techniques. Studies showed how the injectate spread and also helped in understanding the mechanism of action of these blocks.[20212223]

While cadaveric dissection offers unrivaled three-dimensional anatomical fidelity, a multimodal approach to anesthesia training enhances skill acquisition, retention, and patient safety. Key complementary methods include ultrasound phantoms and gel models, high-fidelity manikins and simulators, animal tissue models, virtual and augmented reality platforms, and 3D-printed models.

This bibliometric analysis has several limitations. We searched only the Scopus database. The Web of Science, PubMed, Lens, and Dimensions databases are additional resources that can be utilized for bibliometric analysis. The software does, however, allow analysis of the output produced by a single database. As a result, the analysis could have overlooked some articles. Because new articles were continually added to the literature after this analysis was submitted for review, those published after the date of our data collection were not included. We did not analyze the studies based on the type of cadavers used (Thiel, fresh) or the type of dye used (methylene blue, latex), or the use of radiography in studying the cadavers.

Conclusions

This bibliometric analysis analyzes the research trends, most cited authors and articles, and also the relationship between authors and their cited work. It appears that the literature remains concentrated in a few high-income academic centers, with limited representation from resource-limited settings. Enhanced collaboration between anatomists, anaesthesiologists, and imaging specialists will be essential to translate cadaveric findings into safer, more effective regional anesthesia protocols. Future research could prioritize multicenter cadaveric studies, especially in less-explored anatomical regions and populations.
