Role of the AmvAR efflux system on the pathogenesis of Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Marc Gaona, Jordi Corral, Miquel Sánchez-Osuna, Carla Millán-Román, Carlos Balsalobre, Susana Campoy, Jordi Barbé, Jesús Aranda, María Pérez-Varela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Active extrusion is a key mechanism by which efflux pumps enable nosocomial pathogens to withstand exposure to antimicrobial compounds. In Acinetobacter baumannii, the AmvA efflux pump, belonging to the Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) and regulated by the TetR-family repressor AmvR, reduces susceptibility to disinfectants. Here, we investigated the broader functions of the AmvAR system beyond antimicrobial efflux. Our findings demonstrate that, in addition to conferring reduced susceptibility to disinfectants, the AmvAR system regulates the expression of cell surface components linked to pathogenic traits. Specifically, the A. baumannii AmvAR system contributes to motility and cell adhesion, thereby influencing biofilm formation. Loss of either the AmvA efflux pump or its transcriptional regulator AmvR significantly reduced biofilm development. Furthermore, disruption of regulatory control within this system led to a significant upregulation of the csuA pilus gene. Collectively, these results highlight the role of the AmvAR system in the pathogenesis of A. baumannii, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46007-w.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance represents one of the most critical global health threats of this century. This phenomenon reduces the effectiveness of the treatments leading to prolonged illnesses, increased mortality rates and a higher burden on healthcare systems1. Of particular concern are the ESKAPE pathogens, an acronym which refers to Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species2. These microorganisms are responsible for a significant number of nosocomial infections and possess the ability to evade the action of antimicrobial agents3. Among the ESKAPE pathogens responsible for most hospital-acquired infections, the World Health Organization’s (WHO) 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List includes carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii as a critical priority pathogen due to its extensive drug resistance and persistence in healthcare settings4.

A. baumannii is a Gram-negative, opportunistic pathogen responsible for a variety of infections including pneumonia, sepsis, wound and urinary tract infections5. Its clinical significance has risen due to its remarkable ability to survive and persist in hospital environments6. This opportunistic bacterium is noteworthy for exhibiting a diverse array of adaptative mechanisms that facilitate their survival in the healthcare settings, while also typically exhibiting high resistance to antimicrobial agents, complicating treatment options5.

Several mechanisms contribute to antimicrobial resistance, with efflux pumps playing a significant role7. Efflux pumps are membrane proteins that actively extrude toxic substances, including antimicrobials and biocides, out of bacterial cells. Reduced susceptibility to biocides allows nosocomial pathogens to persist for extended periods, increasing the likelihood of their spread, facilitating their dissemination within healthcare facilities and thereby raising the risk of infection7.

Bacteria have developed diverse mechanisms to ensure their success. During infection, the bacterium undergoes several sequential processes, including contact, colonization, invasion, evasion of host defenses, and ultimately, establishment of infection8. Traits such as motility, biofilm formation and cell adhesion are essential factors in bacterial pathogenicity9. Efflux pumps are also important in these processes, contributing to bacterial pathogenesis beyond antimicrobial resistance10–12. Their expression is often controlled by intricate regulatory networks. Among these regulatory systems, TetR-type transcriptional factors form a large and widely distributed family in bacteria, modulating the expression of certain genes that allow bacteria to adapt to changing environments13. Understanding the roles of efflux pumps in pathogenesis along with their regulatory mechanisms represents a promising approach to combat not only antimicrobial resistance but also bacterial virulence.

In A. baumannii, the AmvA efflux pump, a member of the MFS (Major Facilitator Superfamily), plays a crucial role in exporting disinfectants such as chlorhexidine, as well as dyes, detergents, and erythromycin14. More recently, AmvA has also been associated with tolerance to long-chain polyamines, including spermine and spermidine15. AmvR, a TetR-type transcription regulator, has been reported to repress amvA expression in A. baumannii16,17, and Wang and collaborators recently identified AmvR binding sites within the intergenic region between amvR and amvA in A. baumannii15.

In this study, we investigated the AmvAR system in A. baumannii, focusing on its role in pathogenesis to further elucidate its function and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target.

Results and discussion

The AmvAR efflux system in A. baumannii ATCC 17978 strain

In A. baumannii, the AmvA and AmvR proteins were initially described in the AC0037 and AB5075 strains, respectively14,16. To further investigate the role of the AmvAR system in this pathogen, we identified their homologs using reciprocal BLASTP in A. baumannii strain ATCC 17978 (NCBI GenBank Identifier: NZ_CP018664). In this strain, AmvA is encoded by the AUO97_RS18385 gene, which is 1,479 bp long, whereas the AUO97_RS18390 gene encodes the AmvR regulator and spans 579 bp. These genes are transcribed in opposite directions, with a 124 bp intergenic region between them (Fig. 1A). The efflux pump AmvA (NCBI Protein Identifier: WP_001170327) and the transcriptional regulator AmvR (NCBI Protein Identifier: WP_000323813) consist of 492 and 192 amino acids, respectively. Sequence analysis revealed that both proteins share 99% amino acid identity with their counterparts from the A. baumannii strains AC0037 and AB5075, where this system was first identified14,16.

The divergently transcribed arrangement of the AmvA–AmvR efflux pump–regulator pair is a common organizational feature of TetR-type regulators and their target genes13, including other TetR-regulated efflux pump systems, which are crucial for antimicrobial resistance in various ESKAPE pathogens. The MexZ TetR regulator, which controls the expression of the RND MexXY efflux pump in P. aeruginosa and the QacR TetR regulator that controls the QacA MFS efflux pump involved in the extrusion of biocides in S. aureus are examples of this genomic organization18,19.

Additionally, a comprehensive in silico analysis was performed using genomic data from the NCBI database to assess the distribution of AmvA and AmvR genes across 837 available A. baumannii genomes. The analysis revealed a high prevalence of AmvA and AmvR across A. baumannii genomes, with the regulator detected in all strains and the efflux pump present in over 98% of genomes (Supplementary Table 1). The widespread presence of this system in A. baumannii suggests important roles in bacterial survival, likely contributing to antimicrobial resistance and the enhanced adaptability of this microorganism.

Furthermore, to investigate the role of this system in pathogenesis-related phenotypes, we generated individual knockout mutants for each system component in A. baumannii ATCC 17978 (Fig. 1B and C). As previously mentioned, reduced susceptibility to chlorhexidine has been linked to the AmvA and AmvR proteins in A. baumannii14. To validate the generated knockout mutants, we assessed their chlorhexidine susceptibility as well as amvA expression in the amvR mutant (2-fold induction). The wild-type strain as well as the amvA knockout exhibited a MIC of 2 mg/L. In contrast, deletion of the regulator resulted in a MIC of 4 mg/L. The amvR knockout showed reduced susceptibility, whereas the amvA knockout remained unchanged relative to the parental ATCC 17978 strain. In the clinical A. baumannii strain MAR00219, we used the same constructs as in the ATCC 17978 strain to generate the amvA and amvR mutants. The parental strain showed a MIC of 4 mg/L whereas the amvA mutant showed a reduced MIC of 2 mg/L. By contrast, the amvR mutant displayed an increased MIC of 8 mg/L, reflecting the role of AmvR as a repressor of AmvA as previously described16. In the AC0037 strain, where this efflux pump was first described, a 2-fold MIC difference was observed between the amvA deletion mutant and wild-type14. These discrepancies likely reflect strain-specific differences in efflux pump contribution to chlorhexidine tolerance. In ATCC 17978, other efflux pumps involved in chlorhexidine susceptibility such as AdeABC and AceI may play a more prominent role than AmvA, masking the susceptibility phenotype of the amvA knockout, similar to what has been observed in ATCC 1960620.

The A. baumannii amvR knockout mutant exhibits an impaired motility pattern

To determine whether inactivation of the genes encoding the AmvA efflux pump and the AmvR regulator affects A. baumannii motility, assays were performed using the corresponding mutant strains, with the wild-type parental strain serving as a control. No significant differences were observed in the amvA knockout (Fig. 2). While some efflux pumps are known to influence motility, others do not contribute to this phenotype. For example, PACE family efflux pumps in A. baumannii do not affect motility21. Similarly, studies in Escherichia coli indicate that many TolC-dependent efflux pumps participate in motility, with the exceptions of EmrY and AcrZ, which are not associated with this trait22. However, a significant reduction in motility was observed in the amvR knockout mutant compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 2). Complementation of the transcriptional regulator mutant restored motility to levels comparable with the wild type (Fig. 2). The results obtained suggest that this TetR-type transcriptional regulator could be controlling other genes involved in motility in this pathogen. To our knowledge, no motility-related genes regulated by AmvR have been identified in other A. baumannii strains, nor in other pathogens. However, other TetR-type repressors, such as AcrR in Acinetobacter nosocomialis appears to influence motility, although its targets remain unclear23, suggesting that AmvR could have a similar role affecting motility-related genes such as dat and ddc, which are involved in the synthesis of the surfactant 1,3-diaminopropane24.

Inactivation of amvA and amvR genes results in altered biofilm formation

The biofilm-forming capacity of the amvA and amvR knockout mutants of A. baumannii was assessed. Both mutants showed a significant reduction in biofilm formation compared to the parental strain (Fig. 3A). Consistently, microscopy revealed decreased biofilm biomass and diminished bacterial aggregates in both mutants, especially in the amvR knockout, indicating impaired biofilm development and suggesting altered cell-cell interactions (Fig. 3B). Genetic complementation of the amvR ATCC 17978 mutant restored biofilm formation to levels similar to those of the parental strain, whereas complementation of the amvA mutant resulted in enhanced biofilm formation (Fig. 3A and B). The increase in biofilm observed upon amvA complementation shows that the presence of multiple copies of the vector carrying amvA gene promotes biofilm development, similar to observations reported for overexpression of other efflux pumps such as AdeFGH25. Additionally, we confirmed this phenotype in the clinical MAR002 strain, a hyper-biofilm producing strain19, where both mutants similarly exhibited reduced biofilm formation compared to the parental strain (Fig. 3C). In this pathogen several efflux pumps have been described to contribute to biofilm formation such as the AdeABC, AdeFGH, AdeIJK RND efflux pumps26. Efflux pump regulators also influence this phenotype; for instance, AnoR modulates the AcrAB pump in A. nosocomialis27, while the AdeRS system in the A. baumannii AYE strain regulates both adeABC and genes involved in biofilm matrix synthesis28. Thus, the inactivation of the regulator exerts a more pronounced effect on biofilm formation than the pump itself, likely because the regulator influences the expression of multiple genes, including those potentially involved in biofilm-associated processes. This broader regulatory role also may explain why, despite AmvR repressing AmvA, the amvA and amvR mutant phenotypes do not perfectly mirror each other, suggesting that AmvR influences multiple pathways beyond amvA expression.

The A. baumannii AmvAR efflux system contributes to adhesion to eukaryotic cells

Given the altered biofilm formation observed in the amvR and amvA mutants, their interaction with host cells was examined through adhesion assays using T24 bladder epithelial cells, a relevant model for A. baumannii urinary tract infections29. Specifically, we assessed the attachment of A. baumannii mutants lacking the AmvA efflux pump or the AmvR regulator. These experiments were designed to determine whether the deletion of this efflux pump or TetR-type regulator affects the ability of A. baumannii to adhere to eukaryotic cells, a critical step in colonization and pathogenesis. Results obtained show that the adhesion ability of the amvA and amvR knockout mutants was significantly reduced compared to the wild-type strain. Complementation of amvR mutant restored adhesion to levels comparable to the parental strain while complementation of the amvA mutant partially restored adhesion levels (Fig. 4). Interestingly, the amvA mutant showed a stronger reduction in adhesion than the amvR mutant, which may explain why its complementation is incomplete, as an imbalance in AmvA production could disrupt surface-associated traits required for attachment. The observed effect in the amvR mutant appears to be primarily associated with biofilm regulation. AmvA may be overproduced in the amvR mutant due to loss of repression, suggesting pleiotropic effects of AmvR on surface-associated traits. This phenomenon has been reported for efflux pump regulators in other pathogens, such as P. aeruginosa, where mexZ mutations not only alter the expression of the MexXY efflux pump but also affect quorum-sensing genes involved in pathogenesis30.

These results suggest that the AmvAR system in A. baumannii contributes to the bacterial attachment to host cells, similar to findings in Salmonella enterica, where the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump has been implicated in pathogenesis, with tolC mutants showing significantly reduced adhesion to human intestinal epithelial cells31. Similarly, in enteroaggregative E. coli, AcrAB is required for biofilm formation, aggregative adherence to epithelial cells, and virulence32. Furthermore, the impaired biofilm formation observed in amvA and amvR knockout mutants may lead to reduced adhesion to T24 epithelial cells, as biofilm formation is closely linked to adhesion capacity. This phenomenon has been demonstrated in A. baumannii, particularly in the MAR002 strain, a hyper-biofilm-producing clinical isolate33. A mutant defective in the LH92_11085 gene, which is involved in pilus biogenesis, exhibits excessive biofilm production and altered adhesion to eukaryotic cells, highlighting the connection between biofilm formation and cell adhesion33.

The csuA gene is deregulated in the A. baumannii amvR knockout

Our results show that, in A. baumannii, motility, biofilm formation and cell adhesion was significantly reduced in the amvR knockout mutant. To further investigate this, RT-qPCRs were performed to analyze the expression of well-known genes related to motility and biofilm formation in A. baumannii.

Deletion of amvR gene in A. baumannii, resulted in a dramatic upregulation (227.2-fold) of csuA (protein id: ABO12644) (Fig. 5), which encodes a subunit of the CsuA/BABCDE chaperone-usher pili system involved in biofilm formation and surface attachment34. Despite this, biofilm formation was reduced, likely due to altered cell surface properties affecting adhesion and aggregate stability. Similar phenomena have been observed in other bacteria: type IV pilus activity regulates surface attachment, biofilm formation, and dispersal in Vibrio cholerae35,36 and modulates motility and biofilm development in P. aeruginosa37, while pilus retraction influences biofilm structure and detachment38. Taken together, we hypothesize that, in the A. baumannii amvR mutant, elevated csuA expression may interfere with proper pilus-mediated dynamics, adhesion, or motility, leading to reduced biofilm formation despite the upregulation of a biofilm-associated gene. In fact, previous work has shown that overexpression of the csu operon can alter the pili assembly process in derivative strains due to the potentially unbalanced production of some assembly components39. Interestingly, csuA gene has also been implicated in motility, with previous studies showing an inverse correlation: deletion of csuA enhances motility in A. baumannii40, suggesting that its overexpression may have the opposite effect. This aligns with our findings in the A. baumannii amvR knockout, in which elevated csuA expression may contribute to the observed reduction in motility.

Other genes analyzed in the amvR A. baumannii knockout, such as dat (protein id: ABO12872) and A1S_0114 (protein id: ABO10601), which are involved in motility and pathogenicity41,42, showed no significant changes in expression (Fig. 5), indicating that their regulation is not directly influenced by AmvR.

Overall, our findings suggest that AmvR may play a role in the regulation of genes associated with structural components, as indicated by the significant deregulation of a gene involved in pilus biogenesis. We did not identify any previously characterized DNA-binding sites for this regulator15 in the promoter region of the csuA gene. We suggest that the TetR-family repressor coordinates the expression of cell envelope appendages, structures essential for pathogenic traits such as motility, biofilm formation and cell adhesion.

Virulence impact of the A. baumannii AmvAR system in the Galleria mellonella infection model

The effect of inactivating the genes encoding the AmvA efflux pump and AmvR regulator on virulence was assessed in A. baumannii using the G. mellonella wax-worm model43. Our results show that in A. baumannii, the survival rate of G. mellonella wax-worms significantly increased, rising from 40% to 90% when infected with the amvR knockout mutant compared to those injected with the wild-type strain. Complementation of the amvR mutant restored virulence to levels similar to the wild-type strain, demonstrating the contribution of AmvR in A. baumannii virulence. However, no significant differences were observed in the virulence of the amvA knockout mutant (Fig. 6). The fact that the amvA knockout mutant behaves similarly to the wild-type strain, despite reduced biofilm formation and adhesion to epithelial cells, may be explained by the retention of motility, which likely allows effective colonization and dissemination in the host. Previous studies in A. baumannii have shown that motility contributes significantly to virulence in invertebrate models such as Caenorhabditis elegans and G. mellonella44,45, and deletion of certain efflux pumps does not necessarily impair motility or virulence. In contrast, the reduced virulence of the amvR mutant might be due to the abolition of motility and likely reflects its broader impact on biofilm, adhesion, and motility-related phenotypes, suggesting that AmvR regulates additional genes contributing to pathogenicity in G. mellonella larvae.

The involvement of TetR-type transcriptional regulators in virulence has been documented in various pathogens. For instance, CifR in P. aeruginosa acts as a repressor of the PA2934 gene, which encodes a virulence factor secreted by this pathogen46. Additionally, in A. baumannii it has been recently described that the stochastic activation of different TetR regulators mediates a virulence switch in this bacterium47. Notably, a knockout mutant of adeN, encoding the TetR repressor of AdeIJK, exhibits increased virulence in a G. mellonella model48. The lack of effect on virulence in the amvA efflux pump mutant in A. baumannii suggests that this efflux pump may be redundant with other systems. These findings suggest that the reduced virulence observed in the amvR mutant is likely due to dysregulated overproduction of AmvA and/or other AmvR-regulated pathways. Further investigation into the compensatory pathways or redundant systems could provide a deeper understanding of the complexity of A. baumannii’s virulence mechanisms.

Concluding remarks

This study provides new insights into the A. baumannii AmvAR system, revealing that beyond its established role in antimicrobial resistance, this efflux pump system also influences key pathogenic traits. Our findings demonstrate that AmvAR in A. baumannii affects motility, biofilm formation, and cell adhesion. The regulator AmvR appears to modulate the expression of cell envelope appendages, which are essential virulence determinants in this microorganism, and contributes to virulence in the G. mellonella infection model by coordinating multiple factors required for effective host colonization and survival. Collectively, these results underscore the functional complexity of TetR-family efflux pump regulators and highlight their potential as targets for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this work are listed in Supplementary Table 2. A. baumannii ATCC 17978, A. baumannii MAR002 and E. coli DH5α were routinely grown at 37 °C in Lysogeny Broth (LB) agar and, when necessary, in LB broth with shaking at 180 rpm. When required, culture media were supplemented with kanamycin (50 mg/L) and gentamicin (20 mg/L for E. coli and 40 mg/mL for A. baumannii).

A. baumannii mutants obtaining and complementation

In A. baumannii gene inactivation was carried out using the pCR-BluntII-TOPO method as previously described49. Briefly, an internal fragment of the target gene was PCR amplified using genomic DNA from A. baumannii ATCC 17978 and cloned into the non-replicative plasmid in this bacteria pCR-BluntII-TOPO. Recombinant plasmids were propagated in E. coli DH5α and introduced into A. baumannii by electroporation. Transformants were selected on kanamycin plates and confirmed by PCR and sequencing (Macrogen). Mutant stability was assessed after serial passages without antibiotic selection by comparing growth on LB agar with and without kanamycin. All the oligonucleotides used for mutant obtaining and verification are listed on Supplementary Table 3. Regarding mutant complementation, the pBAV-GFP-Gm plasmid was used for A. baumannii experiments. The target genes were cloned into the XbaI site of the vector using the appropriate oligonucleotides (Supplementary Table 3). The plasmid containing the insert was introduced by electroporation, once verified in E. coli DH5α, into the corresponding A. baumannii knockout mutant. The complemented mutants were selected on gentamicin-containing plates. All mutants and constructions were confirmed by PCR and sequencing (Macrogen).

Motility assays

Motility assays were conducted as previously described45. Fresh LB agar motility plates (1%tryptone, 0.5%yeast extract, 0.5%NaCl, 0.5%glucose, and 0.5%Difco agar) were prepared the day of the assay. In both cases, a 5-µl drop of an exponential-phase culture of each studied strain was placed in the center of the plate and plates incubated at 30 °C until the wild-type strain reached the plate border. All assays were performed in triplicate and at a minimum of three independent times. Images were obtained using a ChemiDoc XRS+ System (Bio-Rad).

Biofilm formation assay and structure analysis

Biofilm formation quantification was performed using the crystal violet assay previously described with slight modifications50. Briefly, strains of interest were cultured overnight in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) broth and next day adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1. In a 96-well polystyrene microplate, 95 µl of TSB and 5 µl of each bacterial suspension were added in each well. Plates were incubated without shaking at 30 °C during 24 h. After incubation, biomass growth was measured in a multiplate reader (Sunrise, Tecan) at OD600. Next, wells were washed twice with MQ water and incubated with 100 µl of 1% crystal violet stain for 30 min. Finally, wells were washed three times with MQ water, and the biofilm solubilized with 100 µl of 95% ethanol. Biofilm formation was read at OD580 and results were normalized according to the previously obtained biomass absorbance.

For biofilm structure analysis, bacterial cultures were grown overnight in TSB, adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1, and further diluted 1:20 in fresh TSB, with antibiotics added if required. The diluted suspensions were transferred into six-well polystyrene microplates (Sarstedt) containing glass coverslips, followed by static incubation at 37 °C for 72 hours51. After incubation, biofilms formed on the glass coverslips were visualized using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) to assess biofilm structure.

T24 eukaryotic cell adhesion assay

The ability of the A. baumannii strains to adhere to eukaryotic cells was evaluated using the human T24 (HTB-4) bladder cells (ATCC). The assay for quantifying bacterial adhesion was performed following the methodology previously described52. Briefly, 105 eukaryotic cells/wells were seeded into 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). Then, cells were washed with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) and bacterial suspensions of the studied strains were adjusted to the required concentration in DMEM/FBS to obtain a eukaryotic cell/bacteria ratio of 1:50. Bacterial suspensions were added into the wells to allow their contact with the T24 cells for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following the incubation, non-adhered bacteria were removed by washing five times with PBS and those remaining were quantified. Cells were recovered by disrupting the host cells by 15 min of incubation with 0.1% Triton X-100 PBS (Phosphate-Buffered Saline) shaking at 300 x g. After homogenization, the lysates were serially diluted and plated onto LB agar plates for colony counting. Data results were obtained from at least three independent experiments, and relative adhesion was calculated from the ratio of the recovered bacteria between the initially added bacteria.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative PCR

Cultures of the studied strains were grown in LB until OD600 0.5 and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen) and following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA depletion was carried out using the DNase Turbo Kit (Ambion) and the absence of DNA was confirmed by PCR. Gene expression was quantified using RT-qPCR with the Lightcycler RNA Master SYBR Green I (Roche) on a Lightcycler 480 instrument (LC480, Roche), following the recommended protocol. RNA concentrations were normalized, and specific primers were designed to amplify a 200 bp fragment of the target genes (Supplementary Table 3). Gene expression levels were calculated relative to the housekeeping gene (gyrB) using the threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCT) method53 and three biological replicates were carried out for each experiment.

G. mellonella virulence assays

The G. mellonella larvae were supplied by the Department of Animal Biology, Plant Biology, and Ecology at the Faculty of Biosciences, Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona (Barcelona, Spain). The virulence assays were performed using the wax worm model following the methodology previously described43, with some modifications. Briefly, 10 caterpillars of approximately 250 mg of weight, were used for each strain. The inoculum was prepared from bacterial cultures at appropriate concentration, then washed with PBS and adjusted to the same optical density. The same inoculum concentrations were confirmed by plated dilutions for colony counting on LB agar plates. G. mellonella worms were inoculated with 10 µl of the corresponding strain or PBS (negative control) and incubated at 37 °C in the dark. Survival was checked every 24 h. All experiments were performed at least two independent times and survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained in the biofilm formation assay and eukaryotic cell adhesion assays were analyzed in a two-tailed, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey test for post-hoc multiple-group comparisons. For RT-qPCR analysis, unpaired t-test was performed to assess statistical significance. Survival curves obtained in the G. mellonella killing assays were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and differences in survival were calculated using the log-rank test. In all cases, a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
