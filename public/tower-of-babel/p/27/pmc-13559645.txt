Reduced Arpc1b expression in astrocytes is associated with cytoskeletal remodeling, ALDOA redistribution, glycolysis, and neuroinflammation in a mouse model of bone cancer pain
Authors: Ying Liang, Jiacheng Yu, Mei Li, Yinan Jin, Yue Yao, Kunyu Han, Yulin Huang, Rui Xu, Yanting Mao, Zhengliang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone cancer pain (BCP) frequently develops following cancerous lesions in the bone, severely affecting patients’ daily activities and long-term quality of life. However, effective therapeutic options remain limited. Therefore, it is essential to explore its underlying mechanisms to identify new therapeutic targets.MethodsA mouse model of BCP was established to investigate pain development using the von Frey test. Immunofluorescence staining and Western blotting were performed to detect neuroinflammation and astrocyte activation. Transcriptome sequencing and targeted metabolomics were conducted to identify significantly altered genes and pathways in BCP mice, and the results were validated by Western blotting, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and immunofluorescence staining. Small interfering RNA was used to examine the relationship between Arpc1b and ALDOA using the aforementioned methods.ResultsNeuroinflammation in the spinal cord of BCP mice was accompanied by activation of C3-positive reactive astrocytes. Sequencing analysis revealed that Arpc1b and glycolysis were associated with C3-positive astrocyte activation. Further experiments demonstrated that BCP-induced neuroinflammation downregulated Arpc1b and increased the expression of glycolytic enzymes in spinal cord astrocytes. Additionally, increased soluble ALDOA appeared to contribute to BCP-induced glycolysis in astrocytes, while Arpc1b-mediated cytoskeletal remodeling increased free ALDOA levels.ConclusionsThe significant reduction of Arpc1b in C3-positive astrocytes within the spinal cord of BCP mice mediates cytoskeletal remodeling, causing dissociation of ALDOA from F-actin. This process promotes excessive glycolysis in astrocytes and persistent neuroinflammation, which may underlie BCP.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Arpc1b is downregulated in C3 positive reactive astrocytes in the spinal cord of BCP mice, causing cytoskeletal remodeling and releasing ALDOA from F actin. This increases soluble ALDOA, drives astrocytic glycolysis, and sustains neuroinflammation associated with pain hypersensitivity.

What is known and what is new?

• Astrocyte activation and neuroinflammation contribute to bone cancer pain (BCP), and glycolysis supports reactive astrocyte phenotypes.

• Arpc1b links cytoskeletal remodeling to astrocytic glycolysis in BCP by promoting ALDOA dissociation from F‑actin—a mechanism not previously described in this context.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Arpc1b and ALDOA‑mediated glycolysis are potential therapeutic targets for BCP. Modulating cytoskeleton–glycolysis coupling in astrocytes may reduce neuroinflammation and pain. Future studies should validate this mechanism in adult spinal astrocytes and test whether targeting Arpc1b or ALDOA produces analgesic effects.

Introduction

Many tumors develop bone metastases in the advanced stage of disease. Bone cancer pain (BCP) frequently occurs following cancerous lesions in the bone and is a common form of chronic pain characterized by severe discomfort at the site of bone metastasis. The pain gradually worsens as the tumor grows within the bone, severely impairing patients’ daily activities and long-term quality of life (1,2). The World Health Organization and the International Association for the Study of Pain have identified cancer-related bone pain as a major global public health concern. Owing to the unclear mechanisms underlying BCP and the limitations of current clinical treatments, approximately 50% of patients experience inadequate pain control (3,4). At present, opioid analgesics such as morphine remain the mainstay of treatment for advanced-stage BCP, despite their severe side effects. Therefore, further exploration of the mechanisms of BCP and identification of novel therapeutic targets and analgesic agents are of great clinical significance.

The mechanism of BCP is highly complex. Owing to both central and peripheral sensitization, BCP involves the pathological mechanisms of inflammatory and neuropathic pain; however, it is not a simple combination of the two (5-8). Our previous research demonstrated that endoplasmic reticulum stress in spinal cord neurons, triggered by inflammatory mediators, contributes to the development of BCP (9). Furthermore, the imbalance of Treg/Th17 cells infiltrating the spinal cord influences the occurrence and progression of BCP, which is essentially caused by neuroinflammation (10). Therefore, we propose that the persistent inflammatory response in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord is the primary cause of BCP. In the central nervous system, more than half of the cells are glial cells, which constitute the major inflammatory population in the dorsal horn. Astrocytes account for approximately 20–40% of glial cells and participate in various neurophysiological and neuropathological processes, including neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, and central nervous system disorders (11,12). Under normal conditions, astrocytes remain in a resting state; however, under pathological conditions, they become activated and transform into a reactive state characterized by changes in morphology, molecular expression, and function (13). Reactive astrocytes constituting a major component in neuroinflammation and chronic pain modulation (14-17). However, the specific mechanisms underlying reactive astrocyte activation remain unclear.

In the present study, a mouse model of BCP was established to investigate the neuroinflammatory mechanisms within the spinal cord that contribute to bone cancer-induced chronic pain. This study aims to identify new therapeutic targets for the treatment of BCP. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0836/rc).

Methods

Animals

This investigation employed 78 male C3H/HeN mice (Charles River Laboratories, Shanghai, China) aged five weeks, with body weights ranging between 20 and 25 grams. All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. 2020AE01025) granted by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, Affiliated with Nanjing University Medical School, in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals. The rodents were maintained in pathogen-free facilities with standardized environmental parameters (ambient temperature maintained at 22±2 ℃, relative humidity at 55%±5%) and subjected to a 12-hour light/dark photoperiod. Food and water were provided ad libitum. Subjects were randomly divided into two experimental cohorts: sham-operated controls (SHAM) and tumor-bearing animals (BCP).

Establishment of the mouse BCP model

The NCTC 2472 sarcoma cell line (ATCC, VA, USA) was maintained in NCTC 135 culture medium enriched with 10% equine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) under standard incubation conditions (37 ℃, 5% CO2, humidified environment). The cell line was authenticated and tested for mycoplasma. For surgical procedures, mice received anesthesia through inhalation of 2% isoflurane administered via a veterinary anesthesia system (Midmark VMR, OH, USA), with the anesthetic gas flow maintained at 0.2 L/min. Following anesthesia induction, the right knee area was surgically accessed, and either 20 µL of α-MEM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) containing 2×105 NCTC 2472 tumor cells or control α-MEM without cells was introduced into the medullary space of the right femoral distal region. The injection portal was subsequently closed using bone wax, followed by wound closure through standard suturing techniques.

Paw withdrawal mechanical threshold (PWMT)

The evaluation of mechanical allodynia was conducted utilizing calibrated von Frey filaments (Stoelting, IL, USA) following established protocols (18-20). Experimental animals were individually housed in clear Plexiglas enclosures measuring 10×10×15 cm, which were elevated on a metallic mesh platform with 0.5×0.5 cm openings. Following a minimum 30-minute habituation period, calibrated filaments with varying forces (0.16, 0.4, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4, and 2.0 g) were applied perpendicularly to the ventral aspect of the right hind limb. Each application was maintained for 6–8 seconds, with five repetitions per stimulus intensity. The threshold for paw withdrawal was determined as the minimal force required to elicit at least three withdrawal responses out of five consecutive applications.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The femoral specimens were preserved in a 10% paraformaldehyde solution and underwent decalcification using EDTA-based solution for 7–14 days. Following dehydration procedures, the samples were paraffin-embedded and sliced into 5-micron sections. These sections subsequently underwent H&E staining, with microscopic visualization performed using an Olympus laser-scanning confocal system (Tokyo, Japan).

Cell culture of primary astrocytes

Neonatal C57BL/6 mice (postnatal days 1–3) were humanely sacrificed via decapitation. Cerebral tissues were carefully excised, with meningeal membranes meticulously removed under stereomicroscopic guidance (Olympus). Cortical regions were specifically harvested for astrocyte cultivation. Following a 14-day differentiation period, primary astrocytes were separated from contaminating microglia through orbital shaking at 250 revolutions per minute for 12 hours at physiological temperature. The purified cell populations were then maintained in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS for one day.

For gene silencing experiments, LipofectamineTM RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, MA, USA) was employed to deliver si-CON and si-Arpc1b (RiboBio, Guangdong, China) following the standard protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Astrocyte sorting

Astrocytes were purified from spinal cord tissues obtained from both SHAM and BCP model mice on D21 through magnetic-activated cell sorting with Anti-ACSA-2 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), strictly adhering to the established procedures.

Transcriptome sequencing

Astrocytes isolated from both SHAM (n=6) and BCP (n=6) experimental groups on D21 were processed for analysis. Following RNA concentration measurement and quality evaluation, sequencing libraries were constructed with the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, MA, USA) in strict adherence to the provided instructions, with unique identifiers incorporated for sample differentiation. The cBot Cluster Generation System, utilizing the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina, USA), was employed for cluster formation following standard procedures. Subsequently, the prepared libraries underwent sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument, producing 150 base-pair paired-end sequence reads. Reference genome and gene model annotation files were downloaded from genome website directly. Index of the reference genome was built using Hisat2 v2.0.5 and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled by StringTie (v1.3.3b) in a reference-based approach. StringTie uses a novel network flow algorithm as well as an optional de novo assembly step to assemble and quantitate fulllength transcripts representing multiple splice variants for each gene locus. featureCounts v1.5.0-p3 was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene. And then gene expression levels were calculated as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), which accounts for both sequencing depth and transcript length, and remains one of the most commonly used metrics for quantifying gene expression. Prior to differential expression analysis, raw read counts were normalized using the edgeR package via a scaling factor. Differential expression between the two groups was assessed using the edgeR package (version 3.18.1), and the resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with an adjusted P value <0.05 and an absolute fold change >2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional characterization was conducted through Gene Ontology (GO) classification and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis to evaluate biological enrichment patterns.

Targeted metabolomics

Astrocytes extracted from both D21 SHAM and BCP model mice were gathered for analysis. To ensure sufficient cell quantities, spinal cord-derived astrocytes from two animals were combined into a single experimental unit. Following thawing and ice-cold homogenization, 500 µL of chilled 70% methanol/water solution (pre-cooled to −20 ℃) was added to each sample. The samples underwent vortex mixing at 280 × g for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 16,260 × g for 10 min under 4 ℃ conditions. The clarified liquid phase was carefully pipetted into fresh centrifuge tubes, subjected to −20 ℃ incubation for 30 min, and subsequently recentrifuged under identical parameters. The processed supernatant was then loaded onto a protein precipitation plate for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry examination. Metabolite profiling was carried out employing an LC-ESI-MS/MS platform (supplied by Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). MWY242426a_R was served as quality control sample. Qualitative identification was performed using MetWare Database (MWDB) constructed from authentic standards. Identification of each metabolite relied on matching five parameters: precursor ion mass (Q1), characteristic product ion (Q3), retention time (RT), collision energy (CE), and declustering potential (DP), combined with MS/MS fragmentation patterns and isotopic distribution. Intelligent spectral matching was applied with a mass tolerance of ≤20 ppm for MS/MS and ≤0.2 min for RT, enabling high‑confidence identification. All 80 energy‑related metabolites were verified by reference standards. Quantitative analysis was conducted on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (e.g., AB Sciex QTRAP) in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The first quadrupole (Q1) selected precursor ions, which were then subjected to collision‑induced dissociation (CID) to generate product ions, and the third quadrupole (Q3) monitored characteristic fragment ions for specific detection. This approach effectively eliminated matrix interference, thereby enhancing sensitivity and reproducibility. Absolute quantification was achieved using an external standard method combined with isotope‑labeled internal standard correction, with standard curves constructed for each analyte. The method yielded accurate and stable results, representing the gold standard for quantitative metabolomics. Mass spectral data interpretation was performed against the MetWare reference database, with initial data treatment executed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex, USA). Principal component analysis (PCA) analysis and heat maps are normalized using unit variance scaling (UV).

Immunofluorescence staining

Spinal cord tissue from the lumbosacral region was harvested from both SHAM and BCP model mice, then preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde solution before being processed through 30% sucrose for dehydration. Transverse sections measuring 25 µm in thickness were prepared from lumbar spinal cord segments L3 through L5 and subsequently affixed to microscope slides. These tissue sections underwent blocking procedures followed by overnight exposure at 4 ℃ to primary antibodies targeting specific markers: GFAP (diluted 1:500, sourced from Abcam, Cambridge, UK), C3 (1:200 dilution, obtained from Proteintech, Hubei, China), S100A1 (1:200, Proteintech), and Arpc1b (1:100, Abcam). Following phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes, the sections were treated with fluorescent secondary antibodies in light-protected conditions, including Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1,000, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Three representative sections per animal were selected for detailed examination. In parallel experiments for cellular analysis, primary astrocyte cultures were plated onto specialized confocal imaging dishes (35 mm diameter with 14 mm glass viewing window, manufactured by Cellvis, CA, USA).

Following a 24-hour period, the culture medium was discarded, and cellular specimens were immobilized and permeabilized using methanol. Subsequently, the samples were treated with phalloidin (1:500 dilution, Abcam) and primary antibodies targeting ALDOA (1:500 dilution, Abcam). Following PBS rinsing, the cells were exposed to secondary antibodies in light-protected conditions: Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1,000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative assessments were performed using triplicate samples for each experimental condition. Microscopic visualization was achieved through an Olympus laser-scanning confocal system (Tokyo, Japan). Digital image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) to measure fluorescence signals corresponding to GFAP, C3, S100A1, Arpc1b, phalloidin, and ALDOA markers. Colocalization analysis was performed using the Coloc 2 plugin in ImageJ. Fluorescence quantification was performed using identical imaging settings across groups.

Western blotting

Biological samples including tissues and cellular materials were harvested, with total protein extracts being obtained through the utilization of chilled RIPA extraction solution containing a mixture of protease inhibitors. To isolate cytoplasmic proteins, cellular specimens underwent triple washing with PBS followed by treatment with 30 µg/mL digitonin dissolved in PBS for a 5-minute period at 4 ℃. Following this incubation step, the liquid phase was carefully separated, while the residual cellular material underwent disruption using 200 µL of RIPA solution per culture well. The collected supernatants were subjected to centrifugation at 2000 revolutions per minute to eliminate particulate matter. For immunoblotting procedures, the following rabbit-derived monoclonal primary antibodies were employed: those targeting interleukin-6 (IL-6; CAT: ab290735, 1:1,000 dilution), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; CAT: ab215188, 1:500 dilution), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; CAT: ab283818, 1:1,000 dilution), actin-related protein 2/3 complex subunit 1B (Arpc1b; CAT: ab99314, 1:500 dilution), and aldolase A (ALDOA; CAT: ab252953, 1:1,000 dilution), all procured from Abcam.

The primary antibodies employed in this study targeted pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2; CAT: AF7764, 1:1,000 dilution), enolase 1 (ENO1; CAT: AF5162, 1:1,000 dilution), lactate dehydrogenase α (LDHA; CAT: AF0216, 1:500 dilution), and β-Tublin (CAT: AF2839, 1:2,000 dilution), all sourced from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). For secondary antibody detection, a goat-derived anti-rabbit IgG (CAT: A0208, 1:2,000 dilution, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was utilized. Protein detection was achieved through chemiluminescent western blotting with substrates obtained from Vazyme Biotech (Jiangsu), while visualization was accomplished using a cooled CCD imaging system manufactured by Tanon (Shanghai). All immunoblotting experiments were performed with three independent replicates. Band intensity quantification was carried out using ImageJ analysis software (NIH).

ATP level detection

Fresh astrocytes isolated from spinal cord were collected as described previously. Cells was added to 100 µL lysis solution and homogenized with grinder. After incubating on ice for 30 min, lysed cells were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 ℃. An ATP detection kit (Beyotime) was used to detect the ATP levels of the supernatant according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 22.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Numerical results are expressed as means accompanied by their standard deviations. Behavioral evaluation modifications were examined through two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measurements. For intergroup comparisons, normality and equality of variance were tested and then an independent two-sample t-test was employed or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, supplemented by Bonferroni’s multiple comparison adjustment. Statistical significance was determined at the threshold of P<0.05.

Results

Neuroinflammation in the spinal cord of BCP mice is accompanied by activation of C3-positive reactive astrocytes

Pain behavior in BCP mice was assessed using the von Frey test (Figure 1A). The baseline PWMT did not differ significantly between the SHAM and BCP groups. Compared with the SHAM group, the BCP group showed a statistically significant reduction in PWMT from day 14 after modeling. The PWMT continued to decrease until day 28 post-surgery, indicating that the BCP model successfully induced chronic pain in mice. HE staining demonstrated that bone cancer destroyed femoral tissue compared with the SHAM group on day 21 (Figure 1B). The levels of proinflammatory cytokines in the spinal cord were subsequently examined on day 21 after modeling. The concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly higher in the BCP group than in the SHAM group (Figure 1C-1F), indicating that BCP induced neuroinflammation in the spinal cord. Because A1-like reactive astrocytes are key contributors to neuroinflammation, we measured the expression of their characteristic marker, C3, on day 21 after modeling. The proportion of C3-positive cells among GFAP-positive cells was markedly increased in the BCP group (Figure 1G,1H). Conversely, expression of S100A1, a marker of A2-type reactive astrocytes, was decreased in GFAP-positive cells of BCP mice (Figure 1I,1J). These results suggest that activation of C3-positive reactive astrocytes may mediate BCP-induced neuroinflammation.

BCP-induced neuroinflammation alters Arpc1b expression and upregulates glycolytic enzymes in spinal cord astrocytes

The RNA sequencing results were further validated experimentally. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that Arpc1b expression was decreased in astrocytes within the spinal cords of BCP mice on day 21 (Figure 3A,3B). Western blot analysis of sorted astrocytes confirmed a consistent reduction in Arpc1b protein levels (Figure 3C,3D). As the final product of glycolysis, the concentration of lactic acid in astrocytes isolated from the BCP group was higher than that in the SHAM group (Figure 3E). Meanwhile, the ATP concentration was also detected and astrocytes isolated from the BCP group had lower ATP level than SHAM group (Figure 3F). Furthermore, expression levels of key glycolytic enzymes were elevated in BCP mice (Figure 3G-3J). Collectively, these findings indicate that BCP induces cytoskeletal remodeling and activation of glycolysis in spinal cord astrocytes.

Activation of ALDOA may underlie BCP-induced glycolysis in astrocytes

The mechanisms underlying cytoskeletal remodeling and glycolytic activation in astrocytes remained unclear. Primary astrocytes were treated with the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) to suppress glycolytic activity. Different concentration was added to treat astrocytes for 48 h, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay was performed to test the effect of 2-DG on cell viability. As shown in Figure 4A, 1 mM 2-DG was chosen as the experimental concentration. Following 2-DG treatment for 48 h in same medium, levels of proinflammatory cytokines were reduced (Figure 4B-4E), suggesting that BCP-induced neuroinflammation may result from glycolytic activation. To further elucidate the mechanism of glycolysis activation, targeted metabolomic analysis was performed. PCA is presented in Figure 4F. In addition to lactic acid, the glycolytic intermediates glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G-3-P) and fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F-1,6-BP) were also increased in the BCP group (Figure 4G,4H). ALDO is a key enzyme in this process; it catalyzes the cleavage of F-1,6-BP into G-3-P and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (22). Therefore, the protein expression of ALDOA—the predominant aldolase isozyme—was examined in astrocytes, and the BCP group showed markedly higher levels (Figure 4I,4J).

Discussion

It is important to acknowledge that astrocyte reactivity is a heterogeneous and contextdependent process that cannot be fully captured by the conventional A1/A2 classification based solely on a limited set of markers such as C3 and S100A1 (23,24). While C3 is commonly used as a marker of neuroinflammatory (A1‑like) astrocytes and S100A1 as a pan‑astrocytic or homeostatic marker, accumulating evidence indicates that astrocyte phenotypes exist along a continuum, with regional, temporal, and diseasespecific variations (25,26). Therefore, our use of these two markers was intended as a preliminary assessment of astrocyte reactivity, rather than a definitive classification. Astrocytes provide metabolic and nutritional support to neurons, and glucose serves as a major energy source for both cell types. In astrocytes, the primary pathway for glucose utilization is glycolysis. Multiple studies have demonstrated that L-lactic acid, produced through astrocytic glycolysis, can be exported to neurons via monocarboxylate transporters, supplying energy to sustain synaptic activity and neuronal survival (27,28). An increased release of L-lactate from the spinal cord is an important response associated with the maintenance of neuropathic pain (29). Wang et al. (30) reported that inflammatory pain induced by complete Freund’s adjuvant led to significantly higher extracellular lactic acid levels in the anterior cingulate cortex and that disruption of the lactate shuttle between astrocytes and neurons reduced neuronal energy supply, thereby influencing the development and persistence of chronic pain. Consistent with these findings, we observed activation of glycolysis in spinal cord astrocytes of BCP mice, accompanied by elevated levels of astrocyte-derived lactic acid. Of course, elevated lactate could also represent a general feature of astrocyte reactivity, a cellular stress response, or a metabolic adaptation to the local inflammatory environment (31,32). Therefore, while our data suggest a potential link between glycolytic flux and astrocyte‑mediated pain signaling, the functional contribution of lactate to pain maintenance and the relative impact of these alternative pathways require further investigation.

In the present study, Arpc1b was identified as the most significantly altered gene in astrocytes from the BCP group compared with the SHAM group. The actin-related protein 2/3 (Arp2/3) complex is a crucial regulator of the cytoskeleton and is involved in various biological processes, including cell differentiation, migration, adhesion, and intracellular transport (33). This complex is composed of seven polypeptides, including Arp2 and Arp3, and five scaffold subunits, namely Arpc1 through Arpc5. Unlike the other four Arpc subunits, Arpc1 is a WD-repeat-containing protein with two isoforms, Arpc1a and Arpc1b (34). Alterations in Arpc1b have been shown to induce cytoskeletal remodeling (35). The primary component of microfilaments regulated by Arpc1b is actin, which exists in two forms: G-actin and F-actin. F-actin is formed through the reversible polymerization of multiple G-actin monomers (36). Under physiological conditions, cells maintain a dynamic equilibrium between actin monomers and filaments. Environmental stimuli can disrupt this balance, resulting in microfilament reorganization and functional changes, leading to cytoskeletal remodeling (37). Consistent with this mechanism, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Arpc1b in primary astrocytes resulted in pronounced cytoskeletal reorganization, as demonstrated by phalloidin staining.

Although limited research has explored the relationship between Arpc1b and glycolysis, previous studies have shown that Arpc1b is essential for maintaining membrane proteins such as CD8 and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) on CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (38). We hypothesize that a regulatory relationship exists between Arpc1b and glycolysis in astrocytes. To determine which stage of glycolysis is affected by Arpc1b, we examined the metabolites produced at different steps of the pathway. The levels of F-1,6-BP and G-3-P were elevated in astrocytes from BCP mice. As the key enzyme catalyzing the conversion of F-1,6-BP to G-3-P, ALDOA has been shown to accelerate glycolytic flux and increase lactic acid production (39). In addition to its role in glycolysis, ALDOA participates in several other cellular processes, including muscle tissue regulation, modulation of cell shape and motility, and organization of the actin cytoskeleton. It may also contribute to cell proliferation. In its inactive form, ALDOA binds to F-actin (40). When F-actin depolymerizes, ALDOA is released from the filaments and becomes activated, thereby enhancing glycolytic activity and increasing lactic acid production (41). In the present study, increased soluble ALDOA was observed in spinal cord astrocytes of BCP mice. Moreover, Arpc1b knockdown-induced cytoskeletal remodeling led to an increase in free cytoplasmic ALDOA, confirming our hypothesis that reduced Arpc1b expression promotes cytoskeletal remodeling, ALDOA redistribution to the cytosol, and subsequent lactic acid overproduction in C3-positive astrocytes, which contributes to neuroinflammation in the spinal cords of BCP mice.

This study has several limitations. Due to the stability of body weight, only male mice were used for the study. The impact of gender on BCP mechanism needs further investigation. In vivo experiments manipulating Arpc1b and ALDOA expression in astrocytes of BCP mice were not conducted. Future studies using astrocyte-specific in vivo manipulation of Arpc1b and ALDOA are required to establish causality. Our colocalization data are quantitative but do not provide direct biochemical evidence for ALDOA-actin binding. Direct biochemical assays (e.g., F‑actin/G‑actin fractionation or co‑IP) are required to confirm the proposed mechanism. The in vitro astrocytes were derived from neonatal C57BL/6 mouse cortex, whereas the BCP model uses adult C3H/HeN mouse spinal cord. Astrocyte phenotype and function can differ by strain, age, and anatomical region (42,43). Therefore, our in vitro findings are not direct proof of the mechanisms in spinal astrocytes of BCP mice but rather suggest conserved pathways requiring future validation using adult, spinal cord-derived, same-strain astrocytes. Nevertheless, our findings reveal a potential regulatory mechanism linking Arpc1b and ALDOA that integrates cytoskeletal dynamics with glycolytic activity in astrocytes, providing new insights into the neuroinflammatory mechanisms underlying BCP.

Conclusions

The marked reduction of Arpc1b in C3-positive astrocytes within the spinal cords of BCP mice mediates cytoskeletal remodeling, resulting in the dissociation of ALDOA from F-actin. This process enhances glycolytic activity in astrocytes, leading to excessive lactic acid production and sustained neuroinflammation, which may contribute to the development of BCP.
