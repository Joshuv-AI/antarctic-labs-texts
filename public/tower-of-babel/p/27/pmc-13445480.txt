Expanded virome analysis of metatranscriptomes from grapevine downy mildew lesions reveals novel viral segments, novel viruses and viroid-like RNAs
Authors: Stefania Daghino, Marco Forgia, Francesco Di Serio, Marcos de la Peña, María A. Ayllón, Beatriz Navarro, Massimo Turina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Grapevine downy mildew, caused by the obligate biotrophic oomycete Plasmopara viticola, represents a major constraint to viticulture worldwide. Previous metatranscriptomic analyses revealed an unexpectedly rich viral diversity associated with downy mildew leaf lesions and in this study we re-analyzed the same RNA-seq libraries to complete viral genomes and identify additional RNA infectious elements. Using homology-based searches, RNA structural motif detection, coverage-based segment association and targeted experimental validation, we reconstructed the presumed complete genomes of multiple viruses belonging to the Splipalmiviridae, the proposed ‘Orfanplasmoviridae’ and ‘Viviviridae’ families. Specifically, we completed the genomes of seven known and three novel splipalmiviruses, five known and two novel orfanplasmoviruses, and four viviviruses, substantially expanding their genomic characterization.We further identified six novel ambiviruses, designated “downy mildew lesion-associated ambivirus 1–6” (DmlaAV1–6), which have a circular genome encoding two open reading frames on opposite polarities, including a putative RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. One DmlaAV contains paired delta-like ribozymes for which the self-cleavage activity was demonstrated in vitro, providing the first biochemical evidence of functional delta-like ribozymes in ambiviruses.In addition, a 767-nt circular RNA element lacking coding capacity and homology to known sequences was identified. This RNA adopts a quasi-rod-like structure, contains twister ribozymes in both polarity strands and lacks a DNA counterpart, supporting its classification as an infectious viroid-like RNA.Overall, this work refines and extends the virome associated with grapevine downy mildew leaf lesions, highlighting the remarkable diversity of RNA infectious agents inhabiting this ecological niche and underscoring the value of re-analyzing existing transcriptomic datasets.

Introduction

Downy mildew disease, one of the major threats for grapevines, affects green leaves, twigs, and fruit tissues, causing huge losses in yield worldwide, especially in areas with relatively warm and humid climate conditions (Peng et al., 2024), with up to 40–90% destroyed field plants under optimal humidity and temperature conditions (Toffolatti et al., 2018). This disease is caused by Plasmopara viticola (P. viticola, Berk. & M. A. Curtis; Berl. & De Toni), an obligate biotrophic oomycete belonging to the Peronosporales order. P. viticola spreads by releasing zoospores which move in the water film on the plant surface and encyst in the stomata of green tissues, germinate in hyphae that colonize the parenchyma and form haustoria, the cellular structures that allow the pathogen to obtain nutrients, suppress defense mechanisms, and redirect the host’s metabolism (Gouveia et al., 2024). Several strategies have been developed for the management of grapevine downy mildew disease, including the use of chemical fungicides (Battiston et al., 2018), of beneficial microorganisms as biological control agents (Lakkis et al., 2019), as well as breeding of disease-resistant grapevine varieties (Vezzulli et al., 2019). All these approaches cannot escape the development of P. viticola strains able to overcome the natural plant defense or human-driven chemical therapy. An envisaged alternative strategy would be the use of hypovirulence-inducing mycoviruses, for which some successful examples are available, such as the use of Cryphonectria hypovirus 1 (CHV1) for reducing the virulence of the fungal pathogenic agent of chestnut blight disease (Choi and Nuss, 1992) or the use of Sclerotinia sclerotiorum hypovirulence-associated DNA virus 1 (SsHADV-1) for suppressing the development of Brassica napus leaf lesions caused by its host, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Yu et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2020). Furthermore, a recent paper demonstrated that the infection by mycoviruses not only can attenuate the host pathogenicity, but can also enhance its antagonistic properties, ultimately leading to the suppression of co-occurring pathogens (Liu et al., 2026), which is of particular interest for the biocontrol in a mycobiota-rich niche such as the downy mildew leaf lesion. It is therefore important to fully characterize the infectious elements present in the downy mildew lesions to have a complete library of virus/viroid-like elements to look at the direct and indirect effect on P. viticola virulence. Few viruses from oomycetes have been described so far, mainly focused on isolated cultivable strains, in particular from Phytophthora species (Botella et al., 2022, 2025; Botella and Jung, 2021; Cai and Hillman, 2013; Poimala et al., 2022; Raco et al., 2023), and also few Globisporangium splendens (Shiba et al., 2019) and Halophytophthora spp. (Botella et al., 2020) strains, all suggesting that multiple infections may occur in the same strain. Mycoviruses from non-cultivable oomycetes, Plasmopara halstedii (Heller-Dohmen et al., 2011), and Sclerophthora macrospora (Yokoi et al., 1999, 2003) have been described, but for this group of obligate biotrophs only two comprehensive descriptions of their associated virome, based on RNA high-throughput sequencing, are available, in particular for Bremia lactucae (Forgia et al., 2024) and Plasmopara viticola (Chiapello et al., 2020).

Chiapello et al. (2020) identified 283 new RNA viruses associated with the downy mildew lesions, that include both P. viticola mycelia and epi/endophytic fungal mycelia from other species, encompassing positive, negative and double-stranded RNA viruses. Among these viruses, for the first time five viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) sequences, that cluster as monophyletic sister clade of characterized narnavirids and show conservation of the A, B, and C palm subdomains, were discovered. They have been proposed with the name of orfanplasmovirus. Later Jia et al. (2024) characterized a bisegmented orfanplasmovirus in Exserohilum rostratum and few bisegmented viruses deposited as narna-like or narnavirus show actually a high similarity with the RdRP of orfanplasmovirus (Jia et al., 2021), suggesting that the monosegmented genomes of the orfanplasmovirus previously reported from P. viticola are not complete.

Among the viruses in the phylum Lenarviricota, Chiapello and colleagues (2020) also found a group of mycovirus segments distantly related to narnaviruses, and characterized by a polymerase palm domain lacking the putative GDD catalytic triad. Viral RdRPs divided into two proteins, encoded by two genomic segments, one of which contained the conserved RdRp motifs A and B, and the other the catalytic site with GDD, have been found later by the same authors in the ericoid mycorrhizal fungus Oidiodendron maius (Sutela et al., 2020), and by other authors in Magnaporthe oryzae (Lin et al., 2020), Aspergillus fumigatus (Chiba et al., 2020), Botrytis cinerea (Ruiz-Padilla et al., 2021), Cryphonectria naterciae (Sato et al., 2022), Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Jia et al., 2021), Monilinia fructicola (De Miccolis Angelini et al., 2022), Beauveria bassiana (ncbitaxon: 3096631), Diplodia seriata (Khan et al., 2022) and Suillus luteus (Liu et al., 2023), and they have been grouped in three genera (Jakapalmivirus, Divipalmivirus and Delepalmivirus) in the family Splipalmiviridae (ICTV accepted proposal 2024.003F, Sabanadzovic et al., 2025). In addition, Chiba and colleagues (Chiba, et al., 2021), characterized the Aspergillus tennesseensis narnavirus 1 (AteNV1), infecting Aspergillus tennesseensis and characterized by the RdRP palm domain encoded by two different genomic segments (like splipamivirids, but with the motif B on the ORFb, together with the motif C, and not on the ORFa like in the splipalmiviruses described above). The AteNV1 RdRP was called by the authors “type-II-divided RdRP”, with the “type-I” referring to the Splipalmiviridae RdRP. All these viral genomes have been reported at least as bisegmented, and in many cases with three or four segments; it is therefore conceivable that the monosegmented viruses encoding for a RdRP without GDD motif characterized by Chiapello et al. (2020), require the association to a second segment.

Furthermore, Chiapello et al. (2020) found for the first time among the positive strand viruses, a group of tobamo/virga-like viruses, including eight viral contigs with a length between 2 and 3.5 kb, that were attributed to four bisegmented viral genomes, with the proposed genus name of ‘Vivivirus’. Following papers built on this initial report characterized new viruses attributed to this genus, two from Aspergillus flavus (Degola et al., 2021; Kuroki et al., 2023; Yoshioka et al., 2025) and one from Fusarium asiaticum (Huang et al., 2025), with genomes comprising three, twelve and ten segments, respectively. Besides, one ‘vivivirus’ isolate from Aspergillus fumigatus was deposited as NCBI record (NCBI: txid2747487). At the time of the initial analysis of the downy mildew lesions virome, the presence of circular RNA infectious elements was unknown. Recent studies based on RNA structural bioinformatic approaches identified viroid-like RNA elements in many other environments and organisms, including plant-associated fungi, characterized by a circular RNA genome and, in many cases, by the presence of self-cleaving ribozymes (Forgia et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023; Zheludev et al., 2024). According to their size, they have been classified in four groups: I) ∼5 Kb circular genomes including ambiviruses or genomes with homology to ambiviruses, II) mitochondrial RNA virus members of the Mitoviridae family, III) elements with sizes ranging from 1000 to 2200 nt that encode for a protein (HDVag and Dag-like, including hepatitis delta virus and delta like agents), IV) RNA elements with sizes similar to those of viroids (200–800 nt) with or without coding capacity (Navarro and Turina, 2024). In addition, a new class of circular RNAs called obelisks, with size ranging from ∼0.5–1 Kb, has been detected from a human gut bacterial commensal and from environmental samples (López-Simón et al., 2025; Zheludev et al., 2024). Ambiviruses have been recently classified in the new phylum Ambiviricota (ICTV accepted proposal 2023.007F), in the kingdom Orthornavirae (Kuhn et al., 2024b). They contain two ORFs, one in each polarity strand, encoding a putative viral RdRP and a protein of unknown function, ORFa and ORFb, respectively. They also have properties typical of viroids such a circular RNA genome, presence of self-cleaving ribozymes in both polarity strands and replication through a symmetric rolling circle mechanism. The ambiviruses identified in silico by Forgia and colleagues (2023) have been grouped in six major clades, showing diverse ribozyme usage: most of them harbour paired hammerhead ribozymes (HHRz), while one-third-contain either two hairpin ribozymes (HPRz) or a HHRz/HPRz mix, and few carry deltavirus ribozymes (DVRz) typical of deltaviruses, either paired or a mix with HHRz or a Twister ribozyme paired with a HHRz. Ambiviruses were first identified and characterized in vivo in orchid mycorrhizal fungi belonging to Tulasnella genus (Sutela et al., 2020), and in the chestnut pathogen Cryphonectria parasitica (Forgia et al., 2021). In the latter, Cryphonectria parasitica ambivirus 1 has been demonstrated to cause hypovirulence (Forgia et al., 2023). Viroid-like RNAs have been recently identified in the fungus Botryosphaeria dothidea (Dong et al., 2023), and it has been demonstrated that they replicate autonomously via a symmetric rolling-circle mechanism and can modulate the host phenotype (e.g., change the morphology, decrease growth rate and virulence, and change the tolerance to abiotic stress).

In this study, the virome associated with downy mildew lesions of grapevine leaves presented by Chiapello et al. (2020) was re-analyzed both by seeking the missing segments of mycoviruses belonging to the taxa tentatively named splipalmivirus, orfanplasmovirus and vivivirus, and by looking for new viruses and viroid-like RNAs. The presumed complete genome of seven known and three new splipalmiviruses, five known and two new orfanplasmovirus and four known viviviruses was obtained. In addition, we identified six new ambiviruses, five of them carrying HHRz and one carrying DVRz, the first of this type experimentally characterized. Morever, a viroid-like element harboring Twister ribozymes was identified.

Materials and methods

Data and bioinformatic analyses

The detection of new viruses and new segments of known viral genomes has been done from RNAseq libraries obtained by Chiapello and colleagues (2020). All the raw reads were available in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), and the de novo assembly, also available because the work was performed in our group, has been searched for new ambiviruses and other circular RNAs, and to complete the genome of viruses already identified.

We used published sequences of ambiviruses (Table 1S) as queries to perform a tblastn search into the assembled contigs. The retrieved contigs were filtered by length of the sequence (>4000 nt) and of the alignment (>200 nt). The ORFs were predicted by ORFinder, and the obtained proteins used as blastp queries and checked for the presence of typical palm domain motifs. The contigs with no overlapping ends, were completed in vitro by RT-PCR (Table 2S) or in silico by looking for reads covering the 5′ and 3′ terminal sequences.

A similar approach has been followed to look for missing fragments of the putative splipalmiviruses: we used published sequences from complete splipalmiviruses genomes (Table 1S) to search by tblastn into the libraries. For RNA2, the obtained contigs were filtered by length (>1800 nt) and aligned with themselves to eliminate duplicates and subsequences of larger ones. The obtained non-redundant contigs were used as queries for a blastx search in GenBank to confirm them as putative RNA2. We also searched for possibly missed RNA1, by using the published RNA1 (Chiapello et al., 2020) as queries for a tblastn analysis into the assembly. To match the published and new RNA1 with the newly found RNA2, the untranslated 5′ and 3′ ends have been aligned and the contigs have been tracked through the different libraries by reads mapping for co-expression patterns. With the same approach, we found the RNA3 and RNA4 segments and paired them with RNA1/RNA2 segments to complete the genomes. Alphafold 3 (Abramson et al., 2024) has been used to predict the tertiary structures of the ORFc from the splipalmivirus genomes.

Orfanplasmovirus genomes have been completed by using published RNA2 of close putative orfanplasmoviruses (Table 1S) as queries for a tblastn search into our libraries. The obtained contigs have been filtered by length of the sequence (>1800 nt), by length of the alignment (>600 nt), and variants over 90% of identity have been discarded, retaining the longer sequence available. Also new RNA1 have been searched, by using published ones as queries, including those associated with P. viticola. The conservation of RNA1 and RNA2 5′ and 3′ ends verified through alignment and co-expression in the libraries verified through reads mapping allowed the attribution to a single genome.

Vivivirus new viral segments have been searched by using the published proteins from Aspergillus flavus vivivirus 1 and Fusarium asiaticum vivivirus 1 (two multisegmented genomes presumed complete) as queries for a tblastn search into our libraries. The obtained contigs have been filtered by length close to the length of the query and aligned with each other to keep only the largest one among those coding for identical proteins. The attribution of the contigs to a single viral entity has been checked based on the presence and abundance of RNA-seq reads mapping onto the genomic segments in the different libraries. ColabFold v1.5.5 was used to predict the 3D structure of DmlaVV proteins, based on the single protein sequence, with default settings. The predicted structures were searched by the Foldseek (van Kempen et al., 2024) and DALI (Holm et al., 2023) online tools. The 3D structures have been visualized and aligned in PyMol (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0 Schrödinger, LLC).

Assembled contigs were also searched for the presence of circular RNAs containing ribozymes applying the homology independent bioinformatics tools based on RNA structural motifs InferRNAl (Nawrocki and Eddy, 2013) and using all the covariance models previously developed (Forgia et al., 2023), https://github.com/ababaian/serratus/wiki/ambivirus_extended_data/).

The circularity of the putative viroid-like molecules has been checked in silico by looking for reads spanning the 5′ and the 3′ of the predicted linear contig and in vitro by an inverse RT-PCR using a pair of adjacent primers with opposite orientation (Table 2S).

All the obtained putative viral sequences have been annotated and deposited in GenBank (Table 1S). The detected viroid-like element has been deposited under the accession number PPZ382280.

Phylogenetic analyses

Sequences of RdRPs from all viral species belonging to the viral taxa whose genomes have been completed in this work, together with their closest homologues were aligned using the online version of MAFFT (version 7) or ClustalOmega software with default parameters (Katoh et al., 2018; Madeira et al., 2024). Retrieved alignments were then submitted to IQ-TREE software online (Trifinopoulos et al., 2016) to produce phylogenetic trees under maximum likelihood model with default parameters. The obtained trees were then visualized and formatted with iTOL v. 6.9.1 (Letunic and Bork, 2024). The sequences used for the RdRP phylogenetic analyses are listed in Table 1S. For splipalmiviruses a ORFb-based phylogenetic tree was generated, with the same method, and for viviviruses trees were generated for the ORFc-d-e-f proteins.

Pairwise percent identity matrices have been generated from the protein alignments, by the tool generating distance matrices implemented in the web Clustal Omega (v. 1.2.4; Madeira et al., 2024). The matrix was visualized using the R package corrplot (v. 0.92, Wei and Simko, 2024).

Analysis of in vitro self-cleavage of the circular RNAs

Self-cleavage activity of DVRz and twister ribozymes was tested in vitro by transcription of recombinant plasmids containing the cDNA of the respective ribozymes in the case of the ambivirus DmlaAV5 or the full-length genome of the viroid-like RNA. The cDNAs containing the DVRz of the plus and minus strand of the DmlaAV5 were amplified with the primer pairs PvaAV5_4564F/PvaAV5_118R and PvaAV5_99F/PvaAV5_439R (Table 2S), respectively, and cloned in pCR-Blunt vector in the right orientation. The full-length genome cDNA of the downy mildew lesion-associated viroid-like 1 was amplified with a pair of adjacent primers of opposite polarity, NODE33416_rt223R and NODE33416_224F and cloned in pGemT-easy vector in both orientations. The recombinant plasmids, linearized with SpeI restriction enzyme, were transcribed in vitro with T7 RNA polymerase (Thermo-Fisher Scientific). A transcript of 350 nt containing the plus twister ribozyme region of DmlaVdlRNA1 was generated by in vitro transcription of a PCR amplicon obtained using primers N33416_MiniRbz_For_T7 and N33416_MiniRbz_Rev and T7 RNA polymerase. In vitro transcription products were separated by denaturing 5% PAGE 8 M urea and 1 × TBE (89 mM Tris, 89 mM boric acid, 2.5 mM EDTA, pH 8.3), stained with ethidium bromide and UV visualized. The 5′ terminal nucleotide generated by the DVRz ribozymatic activity of both polarity strands was determined by 5′RACE of the 3′ self-cleavage respective RNA fragments eluted from the PAGE gel. The eluted RNAs of plus and minus polarity were reverse transcribed with ‘Superscript IV’ (Invitrogen™, Carlsbad, CA) with the primers PvaAV5_RACEp1 and PvaAV5_RACEn3 (Table 2S), respectively. Then, a poly(dG) tail was added by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and the tailed cDNAs were PCR amplified using ‘GoTaq DNA polymerase’ (Promega, Madison, WI, United States) with the primers polyC and PvaAV5_RACEpNes2 for the DVRZ of plus polarity and PvaAV5_RACEnNes4 (Table 2S) for the DVRZ of minus polarity. 5’RACE amplicons were gel-purified, cloned in pGEM-T Easy and Sanger sequenced.

Results

Ambiviruses and one viroid-like RNA

Six new ambiviruses have been found, for which we propose the name downy mildew lesion-associated ambivirus (DmlaAV) 1 to 6 (Table 1). Two variants were detected for DmlaAV1 (99% identity of the RdRP amino acid sequence) in two different libraries from Italy. DmlaAV6 was detected in the libraries of samples from Spain, the DmlaAV1, 3, 4 and 5 were detected in samples from Italy, and DmlaAV2 was detected in samples from both countries (Table 3S). DmlaAV5 was the least abundant with a mean coverage of 15 reads (average number of aligned reads overlapping each position on the genome), while DmlaAV3 was the most abundant with a mean coverage of 2671 reads in one single library. For all of them we could infer the circularity in silico or experimentally: circularity of DmlaAV5 and 6 has been checked by RT-PCR (Fig. 1S), and the product was sequenced to check for possible nucleotides missing from the assembly. The circularity of the other 4 ambiviruses has been hypothesized in silico, by the identification of reads spanning both the 5′ and the 3′ ends of the contig that was initially predicted as linear. Five ambiviruses carry paired hammerhead ribozymes (HHRz; Fig. 2S), while DmlaAV5 has a delta-like ribozyme (DVRz) in each polarity strand (Fig. 1A, upper panel). All the contigs have been predicted to encode two putative proteins on the plus and minus polarities, respectively called ORFa and ORFb. The ORFa is a putative RdRP, containing the typical C-motif with GDD conserved residues, together with motifs conserved in putative RdRPs from other ambiviruses (Fig. 3S). The ORFb domain analysis does not show known annotated conserved protein domains and its function is so far unknown. The ORfb amino acid sequence alignment reveals a high conservation among the different ambiviruses, with five conserved domains (Fig. 4S).

The phylogenetic analysis based on RdRP (Fig. 2) shows that all 6 ambiviruses associated with downy mildew lesions can be classified in the recently proposed family Dumbiviridae, in three different and well-supported clades. Notably, DmlaAV5 clusters separately from the others and it groups with other ambiviruses carrying a paired DVRz on both polarities. The RdRP protein similarity matrix (Fig. 5S) supports the phylogenetic analysis, highlighting that, except for few cases, the putative RdRP sequences of known ambiviruses exhibit low similarity to one another. A putative circular RNA of 767 base pair (bp), with a predicted quasi rod-like secondary structure of minimum free energy (Fig. 3A) and carrying a bona fide twister ribozyme (Fig. 3B) in each polarity strand (plus and minus polarity Infernal e-values 3e-11 and 6.3e-08, respectively) was also identified associated to downy mildew lesions. Neither similarity with sequences in the database nor ORFs longer than 53 amino acids were found. This RNA was detected by RT-qPCR in a single lesion sample collected in Italy in 2018. The existence of reads covering the 3′ and 5′ ends of the monomeric linear contig and the full-length amplification by RT-PCR using a pair of adjacent primers in opposite orientation (Table 2S) strongly suggested its circularity, although the experimental proof of the circularity could not be achieved by standard procedures such as northern blot and RNase R treatment because of the very limited amount of available biological material. The absence of DNA counterpart of this RNA, to exclude that is originated from the host genome, was confirmed by PCR of a total nucleic acid preparation from a single sample (DMG116) of grapevine infected by downy mildew using the specific primers (Fig. 6S, Table 2S). This putative infectious circular RNA has been tentatively named downy mildew lesion-associated viroid-like 1 (DmlaVdlRNA1). The self-cleavage activity of twister ribozymes on both polarity strands of DmlaVdlRNA1 was assessed by in vitro transcription of the full-length genomic minus strand and of a 350- nt region encompassing the twister ribozyme of the plus strand. Electrophoretic analysis of the transcription products revealed that, in addition to the transcript, two smaller RNA fragments were produced. Their sizes were consistent with those expected from self-cleavage mediated by the predicted twister ribozyme, thereby confirming the autocatalytic activity inferred from bioinformatic analyses (Fig. 3C-D). Splipalmiviruses

Chiapello and colleagues (2020) published 9 sequences under the name of Plasmopara viticola lesion-associated narnavirus (PVaNarna 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 19 and 20) that lack both a typical palm domain and the GDD triplet in their RdRPs and formed a statistically well supported clade that was proposed as a new taxon with the name of ‘GDDminusnarnaviridae’. Recently published findings of similar sequences linked to more complex, segmented genomes, some comprising up to four segments, prompted us to complete those genomes associated to P. viticola lesions (Table 2). By using known narnavirus sequences as queries for searching, we could find in our libraries three new RNA1 segments, ten RNA2 and nine sequences among RNA3 and RNA4. According to the alignment of the 5′ and 3′ terminal regions (Fig. 7S) and the mapping data (Table 4S) we found two genomes with four segments, five trisegmented genomes and three bisegmented (Fig. 8S), while we could not complete the genomes of the PvaNarna5 and 19. The species demarcation criterion for splipalmiviruses has been recently defined at 70% of identity of both the ORFa and ORFb proteins (ICTV accepted proposal 2024.003F). The identity matrices calculated with the protein sequences of the ORFa (Fig 9Sa), ORFb (Fig. 9Sb) and ORFc (Fig. 9Sc) suggest that the DmlaSV can be considered different species. DmlaSV3 and 4 are very close, but distinct. Besides, the high identity percentage between DmlaSV2 and Bremia lactucae associated splipalmivirus 1, over the three examined ORFs (respectively 98% of the ORFa, 100% of the ORFb and 84% of the ORFc) suggests that they can be attributed to the same species. DmlaSV1 was related to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum narnavirus 1, Botritys cinerea binarnavirus 1 and Botrytis cinerea binarnavirus 3, with 75%, 80% and 75% identity of the ORFa, and 73%, 77% and 73% identity of the ORFb, respectively. DmlaSV6 showed a 74% and 72% identity with the ORFa and ORFb of Aspergillus fumigatus narnavirus 2, allowing to attribute both viruses to the same species. The phylogenetic analysis performed on the ORFa and of the ORFb protein sequences (Fig. 4A and B) confirms that the DmlaSVs belong to the Splipalmiviridae family, and attribute them to three different genera, namely DmlaSV1, 2, 3, 4 and 19 fall in the Jakapalmivirus clade, DmlaSV5, 6, 7, 44, 45 and 46 fall in Divipalmivirus clade and DmlaSV20 fall in the Delepalmivirus clade. The amino acid sequence similarity percentage of the ORFc of the RNA3, if present, parallels the one observed for the RNA1 and RNA2-encoded ORFs (Fig. 9Sc), and is partially mirrored by the degree of conservation of the predicted tertiary structures (Table 7S), with the ORFc from DmlaSV3 and 4 almost perfectly matching (RMSD = 0.53), but with DmlaSV1 ORFc structure more similar to DmlaSV3 (RMSD = 1.354) and 4 (RMSD = 1.038) than to DmlaSV2 (RMSD = 2.833; Table 7S). Wolframvirales clade of viruses with type-II divided RdRPs

We also completed the genome of Plasmopara viticola lesion-associated narnavirus 8 (PVaNarna8), by finding a second segment, that revealed that this virus belongs to the group of the so called “type-II-divided RdRP”. We could confirm that the two segments belong to the same virus by aligning the 5′ end, while the 3′ end is poorly conserved (Fig. 7S-k) or incomplete. We deposited the coding complete genome with the name of downy mildew lesion-associated narnavirus 8. Both the ORFa and the ORFb-based phylogenetic trees show that DmlaNV8 and AteNV1 belong to the same clade, within the Wolframvirales order but distinct from the Splipalmiviridae and narna-like viruses (Fig. 4A and 4B).

Orfanplasmoviruses

Here we completed the genomes of four out of five orfanplasmovirus (Table 3, Fig. 10S), and the genomic segments pairing was inferred based on the alignment of the 5′ and/or 3′ ends (Fig. 11S) and on the reads mapping data (Table 5S). Viviviruses

Four bisegmented virga-like viruses have been found in the metatranscriptomic libraries from P. viticola leaf lesions material, for which the name of Plasmopara viticola lesion-associated vivivirus 1 to 4 has been proposed at the time of publications. The more recent finding of two vivivirus with genomes with 10 to 12 segments in two filamentous fungi prompted us to look for the other genomic segments of the PvlaVivivirus1–4 (Table 4). We found five new segments for PvlaVivivirus1 (Fig. 6) for which the association was supported by both the correlation of mapping read abundance in the different libraries (Table 6S) and by the 5′ and 3′ UTR alignment (Fig. 13S). Three, four and three additional segments have been found for PvlaVivivirus2, PvlaVivivirus3 and PvlaVivivirus4, respectively, whose association was mainly based on the read-mapping data (Table 6S). One of these associations was not only confirmed as library correlation but also as a correlation among single samples within one positive library. In particular, we were able to check by RT-PCR the presence of all the proposed segments of DmlaVV2 in the library DMS5 from which it has been detected (Chiapello et al., 2020): six out of seven samples were positive for all the five segments and one sample was negative for all (Fig. 14S). We propose for these genomes the name of downy mildew lesion-associated vivivirus 1–4 (DmlaVV1–4). The blastX analyses showed that DmlaVV3 has been also found to be associated with Erysiphe necator, although deposited under a different name. The prediction of the DmlaVV3-ORFg 3D structure resulted in a model with very low confidence (pLLDT= 28.2), for which any further comparison would not be reliable.

Discussion

The analysis of the virome associated with grapevine downy mildew lesions revealed a high level of complexity (Chiapello et al., 2019, 2020). The mixed nature of the RNA libraries, comprising transcripts from the host plant, the oomycete, and various epi‑ or endophytic microorganisms, precludes the definitive assignment of each virus to a specific host, not only because of the rich diversity in these samples/libraries of samples, but also because the abundance of the host not necessarily mirrors the abundance of the infecting virus, that depends also on its replication efficiency and on the number of infected samples in the pool. The previous paper (Chiapello et al., 2020) described the relative abundance of the holobiont components based on the respective read abundance, suggesting that in some libraries, namely those from Italy, P. viticola represented from 20 to 40% of the total, and that the ascomycetes A. alternata was also very abundant. The isolation of single-strain cultures would not only confirm the attribution of the infectious elements to their hosts but also allow to explore the phenotype of the infected host and the virus potential as a biocontrol agent of phytopathogenic species.

By adopting the term 'downy mildew lesion-associated' for the proposed taxa, we acknowledge a methodological limitation but also the high complexity and richness of diversity within the lesion, where viruses from diverse kingdoms coexist and potentially interact among them and with their hosts.

Indeed, this paper gives an extended and more precise picture of the virome associated with grapevine downy mildew leaf lesions holobiont, implementing the one already given by Chiapello et al. (2020), by re-analyzing the same RNA libraries, that were obtained from in-field collected leaves, and by focusing on viruses that, based on their occurrence in other samples, are likely associated with fungal-like organisms and not with the plant, the latter described previously (Chiapello et al., 2019). The newly identified and putatively complete multisegmented viral genomes were reconstructed based on segment co-occurrence and relative abundance across RNA-seq libraries, the presence of identical or highly conserved 5′ and 3′ terminal sequences when available, and their similarity to previously characterized viral genomes. Nevertheless, these methods cannot entirely exclude the possibility of the presence of different variants of the same virus which may harbor distinct segment complements, potentially as a result of reassortment events occurring in mixed infections (McDonald et al., 2016). Furthermore, the attribution of new segments to known viral genomes in this paper suggests the importance of considering genomes reconstructed from metatranscriptomic data as provisional, since additional genomic segments may be identified in further studies (based on virion purification), also demonstrating the importance of re-analysing existing transcriptomic datasets to uncover overlooked diversity, leveraging both the knowledge accumulated in recent years (enriched databases) and the development of novel bioinformatic tools (Charon et al., 2024).

Ambiviruses have been detected in samples collected either in Italy or in Spain, and only DmlaAV2 was detected in samples from both countries. Five of them are characterized by the presence in their genome of HHRz on both polarity strands. This kind of ambiviruses have been already detected in fungi, both Asco- and Basidiomycetes, and the activity in vitro and in vivo of the HHRz ribozymes has been demonstrated for Chryphonectria ambivirus 1 and for Tulasnella ambivirus 1 and 4 (Forgia et al., 2023). This is the first characterization of ambiviruses associated with the downy mildew lesion holobiont. Here we also identified one ambivirus, DmlaAV5, that carries a DVRz on both polarity strands. DVRzs are characteristics of family Kolmioviridae members, which include the human hepatitis delta virus (HDV) and hepatitis-delta like agents identified in several vertebrate and invertebrate species (Kuhn et al., 2024a). Although ambiviruses with these characteristics have already been detected by mining metatrascriptomic data (Forgia et al., 2023), this is the first experimental detection from a single biological sample that allowed to demonstrate the self-cleavage activity of the ambivirus DVRz during in vitro transcription and to confirm the predicted self-cleavage site (Fig. 1). Our in vitro self-cleavage assay provides the first direct biochemical evidence of functional DVRz activity within the Ambiviricota, suggesting that the use of DVRz for rolling-circle replication is not limited to Kolmioviridae, but is a more widespread strategy in the RNA virosphere, and supporting the relevance of the DVRz detection from metatrascriptomic data.

The phylogenetic analysis based on the RdRP sequences of the DmlaAVs and of the ambiviruses currently accepted by the ICTV, showed that the putative ambiviruses included in this analysis distribute in four main clades, each one proposed as a different family and genus, and harboring sequences from different hosts, with no correlation with their taxonomy. All DmlaAVs belong to the family Dumbiviridae, but fall in distinct subclades, each characterized by a different usage of ribozymes (Fig. 2). Further analysis will be needed to define whether such clades might represent distinct genera, but the protein sequence similarity within the group DmlaAV1–2–6 and between DmlaAV3 and 4 might support this hypothesis, since it is higher compared to the one observed for other ambiviruses in other clades (around 50% vs 20–30%; Fig. 5S).

The metagenomic data mining performed in the last years revealed a vast diversity of circular RNA genetic elements, expanding also the range of possible hosts (Koonin and Lee, 2025). Hundreds of viroid-like RNAs harboring a symmetric or asymmetric combination of twister ribozymes were identified in silico, the latter in combination with hairpin-type ribozymes, as detected so far (Weinberg et al., 2021), but also in combination with HHRz (Lee et al., 2023). Twister ribozymes have been identified in silico to be widespread among microbial, plant and animal genomes and extensively studied in rice plants (Panja et al., 2017). The twister ribozyme has been discovered and characterized by Roth and colleagues (2014), that found it in the genome of many different bacteria and eukaryotic species, including the fungus Phaeosphaeria nodorum, and later it has been detected in 11 additional fungal species (McKinley et al., 2024). However, DmlaVdlRNA1 identified in this work is likely an infectious agent given the absence of any detectable DNA form. The in vitro self-cleavage activity of DmlaVdlRNA1 has been confirmed for the twister ribozymes present in both polarity strands (Fig. 3). Although several viroid-like RNAs have been reported associated with grapevine (Raza et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2012) none among them harbored a twister ribozyme. However, a viroid-like RNA identified in peach plants and containing symmetric twister ribozymes has been experimentally validated for its self-cleavage activity in vitro (Raza et al., 2025). No mycoviroid-like RNAs infection has been demonstrated in oomycetes so far, but the potato spindle tuber viroid (PSTVd) has been found to persist in pure cultures of Phytophthora infestans (Afanasenko et al., 2022), supporting the possibility of bidirectional viroid transfer between plants and oomycetes.

The completion of several splipalmivirus genomes associated with P. viticola lesions provides further evidence for the remarkable genomic plasticity of the Splipalmiviridae. Splipalmiviruses have been so far discovered in fungi or associated with oomycetes (Forgia et al., 2024), and reported as associated with an insect, though not as primary host (Etebari et al., 2025), suggesting that the splipalmiviruses reported here are likely infecting a fungal host within the holobiont. Here we found three new splipalmiviruses (DmlaSV44, 45, 46) and we completed the genomes of seven splipalmiviruses for which only RNA1 was previously detected and published, while we could not complete two genomes among them, possibly because of their low abundance in the samples (Chiapello et al., 2020). They are distributed in the three genera of the family Splipalmiviridae (Fig. 4). A defining feature of this family is the fragmentation of the RdRP into two distinct polypeptides, the first including the motifs A and B of the palm domain, the second including the motifs C and D, which, based on the 3D structure modeling, likely reassemble post-translationally to form a functional polymerase (Chiba et al., 2020). We identified tri- and quadri-segmented splipalmivirus genomes in our samples. Although the proteins encoded by these additional segments lack known protein domains, their conservation across different species, such as the high structural similarity between DmlaSV3 and DmlaSV4 ORFc, points toward an essential biological function, such as replication or host immune response evasion (Rodriguez Coy et al., 2022).

Here we also completed the genome of a narnavirus (DmlaNV8) that exhibits a different split site in RdRP, like the Aspergillus tennesseensis narnavirus 1 (Chiba et al., 2021). They formed a distinct phylogenetic clade within Wolframvirales that includes three more narnaviruses with incomplete RdRP (Leptosphaeria biglobosa narnavirus 10, Streptophyte associated narna like virus 15 and Zhangzhou Narna tick virus 5). This observation suggests that RdRP fragmentation is an evolutionary trait that has emerged independently across different lineages of the Wolframvirales and we cannot exclude that other kinds of split RdRPs exist.

The taxonomic organization of the splipalmiviruses suggests a correlation between clade assignment and the size of the encoded proteins. Specifically, viruses belonging to the clade that includes both Jakapalmiviruses and Delepalmiviruses encode larger proteins than those in the Divipalmivirus-only clade, which are characterized by smaller proteins, with the ORFb larger than ORFa. Additionally, we observed that Plasmopara viticola lesion associated narnavirus 10 possesses an RdRP (1146 aa) whose size closely matches the combined length of the two parts of the type-II divided RdRPs (DmlaNV8: 551 aa / 468 aa and AteNV1: 556 aa / 478 aa). Notably, these two viruses exhibit very similar protein sizes to each other, which are distinctly different from those encoded by splipalmiviruses.

The identification and genomic completion of multiple orfanplasmoviruses (DmlaOV) in our libraries reinforce the emergence of the proposed ‘Orfanplasmoviridae’ as a distinct and ecologically widespread family within the Wolframvirales. Orfanplasmoviruses have been first detected from downy mildew leaf-lesions caused by P. viticola, identified as ORFan-encoding sequences likely viral, with very abundant mapping reads on both strands. The encoded proteins carried the GDD motif typical of the RdRP palm-domain, and they have been attributed to a clade basal to Narnaviridae, because they show a somewhat confident alignment of the palm-domain motifs with levi-like and narna-like viruses (Chiapello et al., 2020). Later, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum narnavirus 3 and 4 (SsNV3 and SsNV4) have been reported as phylogenetically close to the first discovered but with a bisegmented genome (Jia et al., 2021). More recently few new orfanplasmoviruses and few close sequences defined as narnaviruses or narna-like have been found, and the new family ‘Orfanplasmoviridae’ was proposed, grouping these viral genomes with two monocistronic segments, encoding respectively for a RdRP close but distinct from Narnaviridae and for an hypotetical protein with no known function but with six conserved motifs, suggesting a conserved function (Jia et al., 2024). The tertiary structures of these proteins have been predicted with low confidence, hence we could neither make hypothesis about their functional role, nor confirm the coat protein function suggested by Jia and colleagues for two orfanplasmovirus from Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Jia et al., 2021). Jia and colleagues (2024) concluded that ErOV1 exists in a “naked” RNA form since they could not observe either viral or viral-like particles in the virion fractions purified from E. rostratum.

The proposed ‘Orfanplasmoviridae’ family accommodates all the DmlaOVs, in at least two subclades, and also other sequences deposited as narna-like or narnaviruses (Fig. 5). A species demarcation criterion has not been defined yet for this group, but the high similarity over both encoded proteins might suggest that DmlaOV3, DmlaOV6 and Exserohilum rostratum orfanplasmovirus 1 represent the same or very close species. Given that the latter has been experimentally shown to undergo horizontal transmission between different fungal species (Jia et al., 2024), this might suggest that DmlaOVs may not be host-specific to P. viticola. Instead, they may circulate among the diverse components of the lesion holobiont, potentially acting as shared viral agents.

The proposed taxon “Vivivirus” represents an emerging group of viruses originally identified in downy mildew lesions (Chiapello et al., 2020) and subsequently detected in various filamentous fungi, including Aspergillus flavus (Degola et al., 2021; Kuroki et al., 2023), A. fumigatus (NCBI: txid2747487), Ophiocordyceps sinensis (Kang et al., 2024), and Fusarium asiaticum (Huang et al., 2025). A defining milestone in the study of this group is the recent characterization of Fusarium asiaticum vivivirus 1 (FaVV1), a +ssRNA mycovirus with a complex 10-segment genome and rod-shaped virions. This discovery, along with the identification of 29 related "vivivirids" has established a conserved genomic architecture characterized by specific protein domains: the RdRP domain and/or a novel methyltransferase (Mtr) domain on the first segment, the canonical Mtr from martellivirales and/or the superfamily I helicase (SF1H) on the second segment, a CSPro and a RNaseIII domain on the third segment, the SF2H on the fifth segment and the capsid protein domain on the eight segment, whose structure is close to the one of potyvirids.

In this study, we significantly expanded the genomic characterization of four downy mildew lesion-associated viviviruses (DmlaVV1–4) by retrieving several previously missing segments. Specifically, we identified the fifth segment (encoding the SF2H helicase) and the eighth segment (encoding the putative CP) for all four viruses. The third segment was found for DmlaVV1 and 2, while the fourth and the sixth segments were found for DmlaVV1 and 3. Consequently, DmlaVV1 appears to possess a nearly complete genome, while the others likely remain partially sequenced. The protein alignments of all available DmlaVV ORFs with sequences from characterized or putative viviviruses consistently supported their classification within the proposed ‘Viviviridae’ family (Fig. 7; 16S; 17S; 18S).

Nonetheless, the identification of these highly divergent segments was challenging, as standard sequence-based searches (NCBI-CDD) often failed to detect conserved domains. To overcome this, we employed protein folding predictions (AlphaFold) and structural alignments. Although some models, such as the DmlaVV1-ORFc, exhibited relatively low confidence scores, the overall folding architecture showed significant structural conservation when aligned with the CSPro putative domain of FaVV1-VP3 (Fig. 15S). Furthermore, the putative CPs of DmlaVV1, 2, and 4 showed high structural homology with the FaVV1-CP (Fig. 19S), like the capsid protein of sweet potato mild mottle, further supporting the evolutionary link between the ‘Viviviridae’ and the Potyviridae lineage (Huang et al., 2025). Such structural congruence, even in the presence of low-confidence regions that might represent intrinsically disordered domains (Ruff and Pappu, 2021), provides crucial evidence for the homology of these viral proteins, also underscoring how structural bioinformatics can bridge the gap in viral discovery when primary sequences are too divergent for traditional homology searches (Hou et al., 2024).

In conclusion, this study completes and extends the current understanding of the RNA virosphere associated with grapevine downy mildew leaf lesions, adding new viruses and one viroid-like RNA to the already known and complex viral diversity of an ecological niche representing a rich reservoir of unexplored RNA genetic diversity that might be further explored in the future.
