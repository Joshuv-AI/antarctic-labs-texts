Care transition from a pediatric intestinal rehabilitation program to adult care and the risk of all‐cause mortality: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Gillian R Goddard, Stephanie Oliveira, Crystal Slaughter, Kim Klotz, Marilyn Stoops, Julie Schletker, Jackie Wessel, Michael Helmrath, Samuel Kocoshis, Monique Goldschmidt, Paul W Wales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs patients with intestinal failure reach adulthood, transitioning to adult care remains challenging because of a lack of specialized programs. This study evaluated outcomes of patients who transitioned care from a pediatric intestinal rehabilitation program.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted of intestinal failure patients who turned 20 years old between 2013 and 2022 with follow‐up through February 2025. Patient characteristics, including demographics, anatomy, nutrition, and comorbidities, were collected for all patients at the age of 18 years. Patients were classified into exposure groups based on whether they transitioned to adult care or continued follow‐up at our institution. Univariate analysis comparing groups was performed with Mann‐Whitney U test or chi‐square/Fisher's exact test, with P < 0.05 being significant.ResultsForty‐six patients were included, with 68% having short bowel syndrome. Nine patients (20%) had an ostomy and 14 of 46 (30%) continued to receive parenteral nutrition. Twenty‐seven patients (59%) transitioned to adult care at a median age of 20.8 (interquartile range [IQR], 3.8) years. No demographic, nutrition, or comorbidity differences were observed between groups. Higher mortality rates occurred among patients who transitioned care compared with patients continuing care at our institution (33% vs 5%; P = 0.02). The median time duration to death was 12 months (IQR, 43.5).ConclusionPatients transitioned to adult care have a higher mortality rate, despite no obvious difference in medical complexity based on medical devices or comorbidities. Additional studies are needed to understand this difference. Increased awareness and structured transition programs are essential to ensuring optimal long‐term outcomes.Keywords: intestinal failure, mortality, transition to adult care

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric intestinal failure is defined as the reduction of functional intestinal mass below that which can sustain life, resulting in dependence on supplemental parenteral support for a minimum of 60 days within a 74–consecutive day interval. 1 The last two decades have seen a transformational change in outcomes for children with intestinal failure, in large part because of the implementation of multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation programs. 2 , 3 , 4 In well‐established programs, overall survival exceeds 90%. The rate of intestinal failure–associated liver disease and sepsis—historically the leading causes of death in this population—has declined significantly with the implementation of alternative intravenous lipid management strategies and the introduction of central venous catheter locking solutions. In addition, the number of intestinal transplants performed in pediatrics has decreased since 2008 because of the success of intestinal rehabilitation. As a result of improved survival, intestinal rehabilitation programs are managing patients with comorbidities that were previously underappreciated because of historically limited life expectancy in this population. A large proportion of patients are surviving to adult age and will eventually need to transition to adult care. There is a general paucity of adult programs to support transition from pediatric intestinal rehabilitation services, which understandably creates anxiety for families and healthcare providers alike. 5

As these patients transition into adulthood, a critical gap in care becomes apparent. Adult gastroenterologists and general surgeons are typically not trained in the complexities of managing conditions rooted in pediatric intestinal failure. In the United States, most adult patients are not managed within a formal multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation program but rather by sole practitioners with limited or no allied health support. Although the improved survival of these patients during childhood is a remarkable achievement, it is accompanied by a pressing concern: how to ensure continuity of care and maintain quality of life when patients transition away from the specialized teams that have managed them, sometimes since birth. This dual reality of clinical progress and systemic uncertainty underscores the urgent need for structured, adult multidisciplinary transition programs to address the long‐term care needs of this growing population.

The impact of transitioning care to adult care providers is not yet clear in patients with intestinal failure. We aimed to study all‐cause mortality in patients with intestinal failure who transitioned care away from a quaternary pediatric intestinal rehabilitation program and compared them with patients who continued to follow‐up at our institution. We hypothesize that care transition is associated with increased all‐cause mortality in patients with intestinal failure compared with continued care.

METHODS

We performed a retrospective cohort study of 46 patients with intestinal failure followed by our multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation program at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center who turned 20 years old between 2013 and 2022. Follow‐up for these patients was until February 2025. Patient characteristics, including demographics, intestinal anatomy, nutrition, and comorbidities, were collected for each patient at 18 years of age. Our exposure of interest was care transition defined as a patient transitioning care to an adult facility after 18 years of age. Our comparator was continued care at our institution after the patient turned 18 years of age. Our primary outcome was all‐cause mortality. Death was defined as all‐cause mortality during the follow‐up period and was determined through our hospital record. The length of follow‐up and number of hospital admissions, emergency room visits, central line–associated bloodstream infections (CLABSIs), and surgical procedures were counted from each patient's 18th birthday. For the survival analysis (Kaplan‐Meier curve, log rank test, and hazard ratio), time zero was defined as the date of transition for patients in the care transition group and was 20.8 years for the patients who did not transition care and continued to follow‐up at our institution. The age of 20.8 years was used because this was the median time of transition for patients in the care transition group.

Univariate analysis comparing groups was performed with Mann‐Whitney U test or chi‐square/Fisher's exact test, with the Mann‐Whitney U test used for continuous variable and chi‐square/Fisher's exact tests used for categorical variables. Significance testing was performed with the use of a two‐sided alpha level of 0.05. Data were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (version 29) was used for the analysis, with the exception of the Kaplan‐Meier curve, hazard ratio, and log rank test, which was done using Graphpad Prism 10.4.1.

Our study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center (protocol #2024‐0289), adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was waived because the research poses no more than minimal risks to participants.

RESULTS

This study included 46 patients with a history of intestinal failure who turned 20 years old between 2013 and 2022. Initial screening revealed a potential study cohort of 60 patients; however, 14 patients were excluded from our study because of transitioning their intestinal rehabilitation care away from our institution before their 18th birthday (n = 10), not actually having a diagnosis of intestinal failure (n = 2), not establishing care with our institution until after age 20 (n = 1), and not enough information to be included in the study (n = 1).

Of the 46 patients included in this study, 31 (68%) had short bowel syndrome as their category of intestinal failure. Nine patients (20%) had an ostomy, and 14 (30%) patients were still receiving parenteral nutrition (PN) at the age of 18 years old. For patients with a diagnosis of short bowel syndrome, we were unable to calculate the percentage of remaining expected bowel length 6 because this information was often not documented in operative reports or was inaccessible because of surgeries performed before the implementation of the electronic medical record at our institution.

Twenty‐seven patients (59%) transitioned to adult care at a median age of 20.8 (IQR, 3.8) years. We found a higher mortality rate among those patients who transitioned to adult care compared with patients who continued care at our institution (33% vs 5%; P = 0.02), with the median time to death following transfer being 12 months (IQR, 43.5). Death within this patient population was attributed to a range of diverse etiologies (Figure 1). In the care transition group (n = 9), two patients died from a respiratory event (acute respiratory distress syndrome), one patient died from sepsis secondary to CLABSI, one patient died because of cancer recurrence, one patient died from an overdose, and four patients died from unknown causes. The single patient who died in the noncare transition group had Down syndrome and presented to our emergency department in active cardiac arrest of unknown etiology. Per report, this patient had been asymptomatic before the event and at their baseline health. Kaplan‐Meier analysis of survival after transition to adult care is significant (Figure 2), with a log rank P value of 0.04 and a survival hazard ratio of 6.3 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.8–22.2).

We did not observe any statistically significant differences between patients who transitioned care from and those who remained in our intestinal rehabilitation program with respect to emergency department visits, hospital admissions, CLABSIs per 1000 catheter days, or surgical interventions (Table 2). Following their 18th birthday, patients who transitioned to adult care had a median of four emergency department visits and two hospitalizations, compared with a median of five emergency department visits and six hospitalizations among patients who continued care within our institution. Additionally, both groups had a median of one surgical procedure and zero CLABSIs per 1000 catheter days during our follow‐up period, which was a median of 5 vs 5.2 years (P = 0.63) for those who transitioned care and those who remained, respectively.

Although the number of patients receiving PN at age 18 years did not differ significantly between groups, 55% (5 of 9) of the patients who transitioned care and then subsequently died were receiving PN at the time of transfer (P = 0.02). Among patients who transitioned care, 71% (5 of 7) of those receiving PN at the time of transfer died compared with a 20% (4 out of 20) mortality rate among those receiving full enteral nutrition which has a relative risk of 3.2 (95% CI, 1.2–8.9).

DISCUSSION

This study identified a higher all‐cause mortality rate among patients with intestinal failure who transitioned to adult care compared with those who continued to follow‐up with our intestinal rehabilitation program in a quaternary free‐standing pediatric hospital (33% vs 5%), despite not having any identified differences regarding their demographics, nutrition support, or medical complexity as defined by the presence of comorbidities and number of medical devices at the age of 18 years. Among patients who transitioned care, those who remained dependent on PN at the time of transfer were three times more likely to die compared with those who had weaned off PN before their transition. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report an increased mortality rate among patients with intestinal failure transitioning from pediatric to adult care, with the risk being even greater for those still dependent on PN at the time of transition. This study was done to bring insight to a largely unaddressed issue, and additional multi‐institutional studies will need to be done to fully determine the effect of transitioning from pediatric to adult care providers has on patients with intestinal failure in the United States as well as identify factors to help make the transition to adult care providers successful.

The relatively short survival time posttransition is noteworthy as the median time of death was 1 year following transfer to an adult care center. One possible contributing factor may be the lack of multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation programs in the adult care setting. A multidisciplinary approach, typically consisting of a gastroenterologist, surgeon, advanced practice providers, pharmacist, dietitian, social worker, and physiotherapists and occupational therapists, is well‐established in pediatric centers for managing patients with intestinal failure and is known to improve outcomes. 3 , 4 Because of the historically low numbers of patients with intestinal failure reaching adulthood, the use of multidisciplinary teams to manage patients with intestinal failure has not yet been routinely established in adult institutions, with few adult centers caring for patients with intestinal failure globally. Not uncommonly in the United States, adult patients with intestinal failure are typically managed by a single physician collaborating with a commercial specialty pharmacy that provides PN supplies and contributes to the management of the patient. 7

As more and more patients with intestinal failure reach adulthood, there is a greater need for adult centers to adequately care for this unique patient population. Neumann et al found that only 21% (3 of 14) of adult patients with intestinal failure were currently receiving multidisciplinary care from an intestinal rehabilitation program and highlighted that many adult patients faced challenges in accessing adult providers with the necessary expertise in managing intestinal failure. 8

Other possible negative contributors to outcomes in the adult care setting include the shift in care dynamics between pediatric and adult healthcare systems. In pediatric care, medical teams are generally more proactive in follow‐up efforts after missed appointments or tests, whereas adult care systems place a greater responsibility on patients to independently manage their care. In pediatric care, parents and caregivers are used to participating in visits and being actively involved in decision‐making. After adult transition, parents often lose this role, leaving the young adult patient to convey information and receive instructions themselves. Some patients have a degree of cognitive delay, often related to comorbidities like prematurity, and their capacity to communicate fully may be impaired. In addition, because there are fewer adult programs to provide intestinal failure follow‐up, patients may need to travel further distances to receive care, limiting frequency of visits. Insurance preauthorization for care with a provider in a different state or change of insurance coverage within a family for children as they age out of their parent's healthcare plans may also make follow‐up impossible. Psychosocial factors such as the patient's and families’ perceptions, feelings, and level of support during the transition from pediatric to adult care should also be considered because these factors likely also affect patient outcomes and need to be further studied.

Currently, our institution does not have a formal protocol for transitioning care to adult centers, and therefore, the age and timing of transitioning patients is done at the discretion of the intestinal rehabilitation team on an individual basis. Our patients are widely distributed around the United States, making identifying a local adult provider challenging. Recent publications by Diamanti, Demiork, and Kinberg outline their own protocols and steps to optimize transition from pediatric to adult centers. 5 , 9 , 10 Overall, it is recommended to begin discussing and preparing patients for transitioning to adult care early, around the ages of 13–15 years during a period when they are medically stable. Education and transition of responsibility is gradual based on the patient's capacity, with this process taking several years. Ideally, during this time, patients become more independent in managing their own care, with at least a 6‐month overlap between pediatric and adult providers. 9 , 10 One method to potentially help determine when patients are ready to transition from pediatric to adult care is to use the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire. This is a validated tool that helps adolescents and young adults with chronic conditions evaluate their preparedness for the shift from pediatric to adult care. 11 , 12 This patient‐reported self‐assessment has been applied across a variety of chronic disease populations but has not yet been examined in individuals with intestinal failure.

This study is not without limitations. As a retrospective cohort study of medical records, it is prone to bias inherent in this type of study design. We were unable to determine the cause of death for four of the nine care transition patients who died. Interestingly, four of the five causes of death we do know are not related directly to their intestinal failure. Although our electronic medical record system is integrated with many other hospitals, outcomes for patients who transitioned to adult care may still be underestimated. Another limitation is the sample size of this study, albeit a reasonable size for this rare condition. We recognize that our sample size is small and thus limits the statistical power to detect small to moderate effects. As a result, nonsignificant findings should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect limited statistical power rather than the absence of an association.

CONCLUSION

Patients transitioning to adult care experience higher mortality rates, despite having similar levels of medical complexity at the time of transition, compared with patients who did not transition to adult care providers. Among patients who transitioned care, those who remained dependent on PN had a mortality risk three times higher than those who had achieved enteral autonomy before transition. These findings underscore the critical need for specialized adult care programs tailored to this patient population, particularly as the number of adolescents with intestinal failure reaching adulthood continues to grow. We advocate increased awareness and the development of structured transition programs to support optimal long‐term outcomes. Furthermore, prospective studies focused on patients with intestinal failure transitioning from pediatric to adult care are essential, along with collaborative efforts to establish multidisciplinary adult intestinal rehabilitation centers.
