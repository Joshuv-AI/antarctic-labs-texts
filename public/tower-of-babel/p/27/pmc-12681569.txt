Age-structured vaccination strategies in mathematical modeling of malaria transmission
Authors: Andualem Tekle Haringo, Legesse Lemecha Obsu, Feyissa Kebede Bushu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria is a global infectious disease, causing hundreds of thousands of deaths annually. This study presents an age-structured mathematical model for malaria vaccination that captures human and mosquito host interactions. Fundamental mathematical properties were established, and the stability of equilibria was analyzed in relation to the effective reproduction number (Re). The system admits two equilibrium points: a malaria-free equilibrium and a malaria-endemic equilibrium. Analytical results showed that the malaria-free equilibrium is locally and globally asymptotically stable when Re<1. Conversely, the malaria-endemic equilibrium is globally asymptotically stable when Re>1. A sensitivity analysis of Re was performed to identify parameters influencing malaria transmission dynamics for prioritization in control strategies. The numerical results corroborate theoretical findings, illustrating that the model solutions converge to equilibrium states over time. This implies that with effective vaccinations, treatments, and insecticide vector control, malaria transmission could be reduced to nearly zero, potentially leading to complete eradication of the disease. This study offers key insights on the integration of vaccination with complementary malaria control strategies, informing public health planning for sustainable malaria control in endemic regions.Keywords: Age-structured mathematical model, Malaria transmission, Stability analysis, Simulation

Introduction

Malaria is a perilous disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Plasmodium. It is a significant contributor to affliction and fatalities in many developing countries (Haile et al., 2024). The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that the number of global malaria cases increased by five million from 2021 to 2022, reaching 249 million cases (WHO, 2021). The African region is the hardest hit; 95% of cases and 96% of deaths in the region were related to it, with 234 cases and 593,000 deaths (Kassie et al., 2024). Recent advancements in malaria control have led to significant improvements, including the development of highly effective therapies like artemisinin combination therapy and the introduction of rapid diagnostic tests. The broader adoption of various vector control strategies, including insecticide-treated bednets and preventive intermittent chemotherapy for vulnerable populations, has significantly contributed to the decline in malaria incidence. However, this progress has recently stagnated, raising the possibility that malaria incidence may start to increase again (Wenger and Eckhoff, 2013; Sougoufara et al., 2020; WHO, 2020; Okumu et al., 2025). In addition to the mentioned control efforts, the WHO emphasizes the development of malaria vaccines to reduce transmission and enhance protection against infection (Handari et al., 2022; El-Moamly and El-Sweify, 2023).

Malaria incidence varies significantly across different age groups due to differences in immunity. Children under five years old are one of the most vulnerable groups, experiencing the highest rates of severe malaria due to a lack of prior exposure and immature immune systems. As children transition into the 6–14-year age group, they begin to acquire greater immunity, which reduces their susceptibility to infection (Gardner, 1980; Ranjha et al., 2023). This transition underscores the importance of age-specific strategies in malaria prevention and control strategies.

Currently, among the various malaria prevention strategies, vaccinating children under five years old is a critical and effective approach in the ongoing fight against malaria. The investigation by Sauboin et al. (2015) showed that the RTS,S vaccination significantly lowers the incidence of clinical malaria, severe cases, hospitalizations, and deaths in 42 countries across sub-Saharan Africa. Malaria vaccines are categorized based on the three distinct stages of Plasmodium life cycles within the human host: pre-erythrocytic stage, blood-stage, and transmission-blocking stage vaccines (Zheng et al., 2019). The pre-erythrocytic vaccine targets sporozoites immediately after mosquito injection, effectively preventing Plasmodium from entering the liver or causing hepatocyte destruction. According to a WHO report, the RTS,S/AS01, RTS,S/AS02, and R21/Matrix-M vaccines are notable pre-erythrocytic vaccines recommended for children to combat P. falciparum-caused malaria (Hammershaimb and Berry, 2023; WHO, 2024).

Several mathematical models have been developed to investigate the dynamics of malaria transmission (Diekmann et al., 1990; Feng et al., 2015; Ibrahim et al., 2023; Gizaw and Deressa, 2024; Haringo et al., 2024). In 1911, Sir Ronald Ross became the first scientist to develop a comprehensive model of malaria transmission dynamics (Haile et al., 2024). His groundbreaking research explored the intricate interactions between infected humans and mosquito hosts, establishing a crucial framework in epidemic modeling. Building on Ross’s foundational work, later researchers expanded his model by integrating various factors related to potential control interventions, enhancing understanding of malaria transmission and management (Anderson, 2013; Bakary et al., 2018; Ibrahim et al., 2020; Aldila, 2021; Rehman et al., 2022; Ranjha et al., 2023; Dinsa et al., 2024; Helikumi et al., 2024; Haringo et al., 2025).

Korsah et al. (2024) formulated a malaria transmission dynamical model to assess the effect of insecticidal nets, indoor residual spraying, and individualized protective measures, and suggested that the integrated use of insecticidal nets, indoor residual spraying, and individualized protective measures can significantly reduce malaria prevalence. Olutimo et al. (2024) developed a mathematical model of malaria transmission to examine the role of environmental immunity in humans. Their findings indicate that increased environmental immunity in recovered individuals significantly reduces the number of infected humans, which in turn lowers the number of infected mosquitoes due to fewer transmission opportunities. A sensitivity analysis of a mathematical model for malaria transmission, which incorporates relapse dynamics and unaware-infected individuals, is proposed in Rehman et al. (2022). The findings indicate that mosquito protection measures are significantly more effective than treatment alone in reducing disease spread. Zhao and Mohammed-Awel (2014) proposed a mathematical model that includes mosquito-stage transmission-blocking vaccines, demonstrating that increased vaccination coverage can significantly reduce and potentially eliminate the disease, while insufficient rates may allow it to persist. White et al. (2015) formulated a malaria transmission dynamics model considering hypothetical vaccination for the general population. Their findings indicate that malaria eradication is achievable by maintaining the reproduction number below unity. Ouifki et al. (2024) developed a mathematical model of malaria transmission to analyze the effects of different demographic growths and the use of transmission-blocking drugs in humans. Their key finding revealed that one specific demographic growth pattern resulted in a considerably higher disease burden compared to others. However, the elimination of malaria is achieved when the high-level treatment and transmission-blocking vaccine administration are implemented simultaneously. Ducrot et al. (2009) developed a two-species malaria model that considers susceptibility, exposure, and infectivity of human hosts. This model identifies two distinct types of hosts within the human population, i.e. non-immune and semi-immune individuals. Non-immune individuals are particularly vulnerable to malaria, as they have never developed any immunity to the disease. In contrast, semi-immune individuals have experienced at least one episode of malaria, allowing them to acquire some level of immunity throughout their lives. A deterministic analysis of an age-sex-structured model of malaria transmission was proposed and studied for the Rwanda case (Uwineza et al., 2024). This study highlighted the need for women to increase awareness and implement preventive measures, such as educating older children on safe play areas and ensuring younger ones use bed nets. Tchoumi et al. (2022) proposed a malaria transmission dynamical model that incorporates a two-group structure. The study revealed that interventions aimed at reducing contact between mosquitoes and individuals under five years old have a significantly greater positive effect on overall endemicity levels compared to similar measures targeting individuals over five years old. This finding underscores the critical public health implication that efforts to combat malaria should be intensified among children under five, to effectively mitigate its spread throughout the population. Forouzannia and Gumel (2014) examined a deterministic mathematical model to assess the impact of age structure on malaria transmission in a community without vaccination or treatment interventions. Their findings indicated that the cumulative incidence of new infections and malaria-related mortality increases with rising average mosquito lifespan and birth rate.

Recently, age-structured vaccination strategies have received significant attention in modeling malaria transmission, emphasizing key parameters such as vaccine efficacy, waning immunity, and their role in control interventions. Tchoumi et al. (2023) incorporated optimal control of a two-group malaria transmission model with vaccination for children under five years old to address the recently approved children vaccine. Their results showed that the simultaneous implementation of vaccination with other control and prevention measures for children under five achieves the highest effectiveness. In the related avenue, Uwineza et al. (2024) proposed a two-age-class malaria model with age-structured vaccination and recovery, showing that the malaria-free equilibrium is globally stable when the basic reproduction number is below one, and an endemic equilibrium is stable when it exceeds one. Glèlè Kakaï and Djomatin (2025) developed a deterministic age-structured model to evaluate the impact of vaccination on malaria transmission, burden, and severity. The model stratifies individuals by vaccination status (unvaccinated, primary doses only, and booster recipients) and incorporates the synergistic effects of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs). Calibrated with demographic and epidemiological data from low- (Ethiopia) and high-transmission (Nigeria) settings, the study demonstrates that combining vaccination with existing interventions can substantially reduce incidence and mortality, offering insights for optimizing vaccination strategies and informing malaria control policies.

Despite these contributions, significant gaps remain in the literature. Few studies have systematically investigated age-structured vaccination and its integration with long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs). Moreover, the combined effects of age-structured vaccination strategies with insecticide application and drug treatment on malaria infection remain underexplored. To address these gaps, this study develops a deterministic age-structured mathematical model to evaluate the combined effect of malaria vaccination, insecticide application, and drug treatment interventions on malaria infection. The model divides the human population into two age-based epidemiological groups, vaccinated individuals under five and unvaccinated individuals aged five and above. It integrates the combined effects of malaria vaccination, insecticide application, and antimalarial treatment. Using numerical simulations over a 50-year horizon, the study assesses how the combined administration of malaria vaccination, insecticide use, and antimalarial treatment impacts malaria morbidity and mortality, providing actionable insights to optimize malaria control strategies across age groups. Using Ethiopian malaria incidence data, numerical simulations over a 50-year horizon evaluate the impact of these interventions on malaria morbidity and mortality, providing actionable insights to optimize age-specific malaria control strategies.

Materials and methods

Model formulation

In the study of malaria transmission, it has been shown that susceptibility and infectivity of the human host mainly depend on the age difference. These two clinical states depend on whether the host has lost immunity or has not yet acquired it (Gardner, 1980). Individuals who have never acquired immunity are at significantly higher risk of malaria than those who have experienced infection at least once. This heightened vulnerability is a primary factor in the high mortality rates among children aged under five who live in endemic areas. Therefore, categorizing human hosts by age is essential for understanding malaria transmission dynamics and developing more effective control strategies (Ranjha et al., 2023). While existing age-structured vaccine models have advanced understanding of malaria transmission, key gaps persist. We extended the two-group malaria model of Tchoumi et al. (2022), which accounts for age-dependent risk but excludes age-structured vaccination, under-five recovery classes, treatment compartments, and mosquito insecticide application. Our enhanced framework incorporates age-specific vaccination, treatment classes, recovery and waning immunity in the under-five population, and insecticide use for mosquito control. Thus, we categorized the human population into two broad age groups: individuals under 5 years old, who are most vulnerable due to their low acquired immunity, and individuals aged 5 and older, who are less vulnerable to malaria as a result of their high acquired immunity. We subdivided individuals under five years old into five epidemiologically distinct classes: susceptible (Sc); vaccinated (Vc); infected (Ic); treated (Tc); and recovered (Rc). The susceptible (Sc) class increases at a constant recruitment rate (Πc), matures at a rate (θc), experiences an increase due to waning immunity at a rate (ϵc), and decreases due to vaccination at a rate (νc) or by becoming infected with a force of infection (λc). The infected individuals (Ic) transition to the treated class (Tc) at a treatment rate (ρc), die at a disease-induced death rate (δc , or recover at a rate (ηc). Due to loss of vaccine efficacy, vaccinated individuals (Vc) may transition back to the susceptible class (Sc) at a rate h. The treated class (Tc) decreases at the recovery rate (ηc) or dies due to treatment failure-induced death rate (φc). The immune individuals (Rc) increase at the recovery rate (ηc) and decrease due to waning immunity at a rate (ϵc). Analogously, individuals aged five and older are categorized into four epidemiologically distinct classes: susceptible (Sa); infected (Ia); treated (Ta); and recovered (Ra). Susceptible individuals (Sa) increase due to maturation rate (θc) from the susceptible class (Sc) and waning immunity from the recovered class (Ra) at a rate (ϵa). They become infected with a force of infection (λa). Infected individuals (Ia) transition to the treated class (Ta) at a rate (ρa) or die from disease-induced mortality at a rate (δa). Treated individuals (Ta) recover at a rate (ηa) and may die from treatment failure at a rate (φa). Recovered individuals (Ra) can revert to the susceptible class (Sa) due to waning immunity at a rate (ϵa). It is assumed that individuals in each age group of distinct epidemiological classes decrease by experiencing natural mortality at a rate (μh). To simplify the model, we assume a constant recruitment rate Πm for the susceptible mosquito population, enhancing analytical tractability and facilitating clearer interpretation. The force of infection for mosquitoes, λm, depends on the proportion of infected humans (Ic) and (Im) relative to the total human population (Nh). This formulation assumes that transmission is primarily driven by contacts between infected humans and susceptible mosquitoes, rather than by the total mosquito population size. Implicitly, the mosquito population is considered sufficiently abundant compared to humans, such that its total size does not significantly affect transmission dynamics. It is assumed that mosquitoes do not recover from malaria due to their short lifespan; they die either from insecticidal application at rate (γm) or naturally at rate (μm). This approach ensures that the most relevant Ethiopian demographic parameters are accounted for to provide a comprehensive understanding of malaria dynamics in the country. Unfortunately, we lacked explicit data on malaria incidence proportions for individuals under 5 years old (Ic) and those aged 5 and older (Ic). To address this limitation, we utilized the implicit data of combined incidence (Ic+Ia) for validation and parameter estimation. This robust methodology enhances analysis and prediction within the age-structured model framework, effectively mitigating the limitations posed by the available data. Moreover, we assumed the following initial human and mosquito populations based on incidence data from the World Bank, ranging from the years 2000–2022 (WHO, 2023): Sc(0)=120, Vc(0)=0, Ic(0)=22, Tc(0)=0, Rc(0)=0, Sa(0)=557.3, Ia(0)=300.7, Ta(0)=0, Ra(0)=0, Sm(0)=800, Im(0)=200. The constant influx of malaria-prone humans Πc and the natural mortality rate in humans μh were estimated from Ethiopian demographic data. The value for μh is calculated by taking the reciprocal of the average life expectancy in Ethiopia, which is approximately 67.8 years (WHO, 2024). Thus, we have μh=167.8 deaths per year. To calculate the annual constant influx of malaria-susceptible humans (Πc) and mosquitoes (Πm), we explored a scenario introduced in Madueme and Chirove (2023) where human population under age five has reached its steady state. Thus, we considered total malaria incidence in the country of about 1000 humans, such that Πc=1000×μh. For the annual constant influx of malaria-susceptible mosquitoes, we assumed a hypothetical average population of 1000, leading to Πc=1000×μm, where μm=36521year−1 represents the average lifespan of mosquitoes. This assumption is necessary due to the absence of quantified estimates for the actual annual mosquito population. Based on an empirical study (Jaleta et al., 2025), a treatment rate ρc=0.27 (bound in [0,1]) was assumed for children under five years. This rate indicates that young children are often prioritized in treatment protocols due to their vulnerability and elevated risk of morbidity. The parameters φc, φa, δc, δa, ηc and ηa were adopted from a recently published mathematical modeling study on malaria by Haringo et al. (2025), which utilized the same dataset for parameter fitting. Given the relevance and reliability of this source, these parameters were used directly rather than re-estimated. Furthermore, parameters β1, β2, β3, β4, γm, h, ϵc, ϵa, ρa and νc were accurately fitted (Table 2). We also found that the fitted parameters for insecticide usage rate and transmission rates closely align with the results reported by Haringo et al. (2025). The fitted values for the rate of loss of vaccine efficacy are realistic when compared to similar analyses in Tchoumi et al. (2022). Additionally, the parameters for waning immunity closely match those highlighted in the analysis presented in Tchoumi et al. (2023). This close alignment confirms that our fitted parameters exhibit biological realism and validity in relation to prior empirical studies. The model fit presented in Table 2 shows strong alignment between our age-structured vaccine model and incidence data in Ethiopia over the past 23 years. This close correspondence underscores the robustness and accuracy of our model in capturing the trends and patterns of malaria incidence in the region.

Here, we present numerical solutions of model (1) to support the theoretical findings obtained from previous sections. The simulations were executed using scipy.integrate.solve_ivp function, which is well-suited for solving ordinary differential equations in Python software. To illustrate the convergence of model equilibria, we aimed to show that the solutions of the model approach a specific equilibrium point as time goes. Fig. 6, Fig. 7 depict the time-course behavior of the model solution for the cases when Re<1 and Re>1, respectively. To demonstrate convergence to E0, we used parameter values from Table 2 and set the transmission rate β1=0.0021. Substituting these values into equation (10), we get Re=0.415. In this scenario, the model exhibits an MFE given by E0=(257,675,0,0,0,48,0,0,0,158557.8,0), which is globally asymptotically stable as depicted in Fig. 6. In particular, the number of non-infected humans in Fig. 6A and non-infected mosquitoes in Fig. 6D continues to rise. This trend indicates that an increasing proportion of individuals in both species remain in malaria-free state, while the populations of infected and treated individuals decline. As a consequence, the human population of recovered individuals also decreases, as expected. This is further supported by the data presented in Fig. 6B and C, which demonstrates that the number of infected and treated humans continue to diminish, approaching zero over time. This suggests that the disease will eventually be brought under control when Re<1.

Discussion

Malaria remains a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, driving high morbidity, mortality, and socio-economic disruption. In Ethiopia, the disease continues to impose a considerable burden on the public health system and impede sustainable development. In this study, we developed a deterministic age-structured vaccination model to investigate malaria transmission dynamics across two human age-structures. The model assumes that human susceptibility and infectivity to malaria are primarily influenced by age-structure, reflecting the level of an individual’s acquired immunity. Based on this assumption, we categorized human hosts by their age-structure to elucidate malaria transmission dynamics and inform the development of more effective control strategies. This approach, utilized by Glèlè Kakaï and Djomatin (2025) in their age-structured model, stratifies the human population into monthly cohorts and advances the system in discrete one-month time steps, while Forouzannia and Gumel (2014) applies it to evaluate the impact of age structure on transmission dynamics. This framework is essential for capturing variations in immunity and susceptibility, vaccine efficacy, contact rates, and epidemiological transitions across age groups. Alongside the age-structured vaccination strategy, we assumed non-vaccine interventions, namely insecticide-based vector control and antimalarial drug treatment, were implemented. Incorporating these integrated strategies into our malaria model improves understanding of transmission dynamics, supports more accurate predictions, and guides robust public health policies, including targeted vaccine deployment in high-burden regions such as Ethiopia.

The investigation of fundamental mathematical properties such as positivity, boundedness, feasibility, and stability of equilibria ensures the rigor and validity of our model. Positivity guarantees that the population in each compartment remains non-negative and biologically interpretable throughout the simulation period. Boundedness ensures that those populations remain within the realistic limited region defined by the initial population, preventing unrealistic model behaviors. From an epidemiological perspective, these properties affirm that the model outputs are meaningful for representing real-world malaria dynamics. Stability analysis of the equilibria malaria-free and malaria-endemic states provides insight into the long-term behavior of the infection within the population. Stable equilibria convergence tells us predictable outcomes, either malaria elimination or persistence, depending on the average number of secondary infections. Thus, this analytical framework lays a solid foundation for interpreting the impacts of interventions and guiding effective malaria control strategies. By setting the vaccination rate to zero, we obtain Re≤R0. This indicates that there is a need for enhanced interventions to suppress malaria transmission. On the other hand, increasing vaccination rates are central to public health, directly reducing infectious cases and treatment demand. This decline curbs transmission, eases healthcare burdens, and lowers costs, while expanded coverage strengthens herd immunity and indirectly protects unvaccinated and vulnerable groups as the effective reproductive number falls well below one. Further, our model was applied in the Ethiopian context to estimate key rates influencing malaria dynamics, including vaccination, loss of vaccine efficacy, waning immunity, malaria transmission, insecticide application, and treatment. The fitted insecticide usage and transmission rates closely align with those reported by Haringo et al. (2025), while the estimated values for loss of vaccine efficacy, treatment, and malaria transmission rates nearly match those obtained by Tchoumi et al. (2022) and Glèlè Kakaï and Djomatin (2025), reinforcing the reliability of the model calibration. The close correspondence of the fitted parameters with prior studies substantiates the robustness and validity of the model calibration, thereby confirming its epidemiological relevance for guiding malaria intervention strategies. Furthermore, the model fit illustrated in Fig. 2A demonstrates strong alignment between the malaria model and the incidence data from Ethiopia over the past 23 years. However, graphical analysis of residuals displayed in Fig. 2B revealed a nonlinear pattern, suggesting that the model may have omitted key environmental drivers influencing malaria dynamics. Such omissions constrain the model’s ability to fully capture underlying transmission factors, limiting its predictive accuracy. Future research should focus on integrating these environmental and temporal factors to refine the model and enhance its utility for informing effective control and intervention strategies.

The sensitivity indices analysis indicated that increases in vaccination rate, insecticide application rates, recruitment of children under five, and malaria treatment rates contribute significantly to reducing malaria transmission within a population. As shown in Fig. 9, increasing vaccination rates leads to a marked decline in the number of infectious and treated individuals who directly receive the vaccine, while the populations of susceptible and vaccinated individuals increase. This finding, supported by Glèlè Kakaï and Djomatin (2025), reflects the strong efficacy of vaccination in young children, especially in high-transmission settings. These results highlight the importance of considering long-term immunity dynamics and underscore the need for complementary malaria control interventions. The higher negative sensitivity index of the insecticide application rate underscores its critical role in reducing malaria transmission by lowering mosquito abundance and subsequent transmission. The finding suggests that public health strategies should prioritize effective insecticide application in combination with complementary interventions to achieve sustainable malaria control initiatives. This is consistent with previous studies, such as Wang et al. (2021), which demonstrated that vector control measures, including insecticide-treated bednets and indoor residual spraying substantially reduce malaria incidence. Similarly, enhancing drug treatment improves human recovery and reduces infectiousness in high-transmission areas. Conversely, parameters like malaria transmission rates between humans and mosquitoes and juvenile mosquito recruitment show positive sensitivity indices, indicating they exacerbate malaria transmission. As shown in Fig. 12, Fig. 13, higher transmission rates lead to marked increases in infected and infectious individuals, resulting in elevated malaria incidence. In parallel, the numbers of susceptible and vaccinated individuals decline as the transmission rates rise. These patterns emphasize the central role of controlling transmission rate in shaping disease burden and support the need for integrated interventions, such as the Attractive Targeted Sugar Bait (ATSB) strategy suggested by Moghaddas et al. (2025).

Lastly, we identified some shortcomings that require immediate attention. The assumptions of time-invariant recruitment rates and the exclusion of environmental drivers constrain the model’s realism and predictive accuracy. Integrating time-varying recruitment rates and environmental variables, alongside age-structured vaccination, will better capture the dynamic interplay among human populations, mosquito vectors, and environmental conditions. This approach aims to enhance the accuracy of transmission dynamics and inform more effective malaria control strategies in public health.

Conclusions

This study developed and analyzed an age-structured vaccination model to assess the impact of malaria vaccination on disease dynamics and to guide effective prevention and control strategies. The model assumes that susceptibility and infectivity in human hosts vary with age. Our findings indicate that, with effective implementation of vaccinations, treatments, and vector control strategies, the model solutions converge toward a malaria-free state over time, suggesting the potential for eradication of the disease. Conversely, if these strategies are implemented inadequately, the model solutions converge to an endemic state. This highlights the need of robust public health policies, including targeted vaccine deployment in high-burden regions such as Ethiopia. Future research could enhance the model by incorporating additional heterogeneities, such as time-varying recruitment rates and environmental variables. In addition, the current study relies on implicit incidence data from two age groups rather than explicitly stratified incidence by distinct age categories. This limitation can be addressed by incorporating age-specific incidence data, enabling a more robust methodological framework and improving predictive accuracy.
