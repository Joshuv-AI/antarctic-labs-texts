Distinct molecular and tumor microenvironment characteristics of mucinous adenocarcinoma in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jianxia Li, Yang Fu, Fan Bai, Zehua Wu, Ge Qin, Yanhong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMucinous colorectal adenocarcinoma (MAC) is characterized by poor prognosis and therapy resistance, yet its molecular and tumor microenvironment (TME) features remain inadequately understood, limiting the identification of effective therapeutic targets. Here, we performed a comprehensive analysis of MAC tumor characteristics and the TME using large-scale genetic and transcriptomic sequencing datasets. Our findings reveal that MAC tumor cells harbor a higher frequency of canonical mutations yet exhibit lower chromosomal instability. Additionally, we observed an increased infiltration of natural killer (NK) cells and SPP1 highly expressed macrophages (Mac-SPP1) within the TME, along with heightened fibroblastic and myeloid inflammatory signals. Mac-SPP1, characterized by M2 macrophages, was associated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, we identified key prognosis-related genes, including FSCN1, SLC11A1, and PLXND1, and proposed potential therapeutic agents for overcoming treatment resistance. Our findings offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying MAC and highlight the critical need for novel therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Mucinous colorectal adenocarcinoma cancer (MAC) is a distinct subtype of colorectal cancer (CRC), characterized by extracellular mucin comprising more than 50% of the tumor volume.1 It accounts for approximately 5%–15% of CRC cases and is associated with elevated expression of mucins (MUC2 and MUC5AC), increased microsatellite instability frequency, and common mutations in KRAS and BRAF, as well as the CpG island methylation phenotype compared with conventional colorectal adenocarcinoma (CAC).2,3

The prognosis of MAC remains controversial. While mucinous adenocarcinoma is generally considered a poor prognostic factor in CRC, particularly in advanced stages.1,2,4 Studies have also linked the mucinous histological subtype to resistance against chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).5,6,7,8 This resistance is attributed to multiple mechanisms, including excessive mucus secretion, which acts as a physical barrier to chemotherapeutic agents9; oncogenic mutations that activate multiple signaling pathways, promoting tumor progression and drug resistance2; and the overexpression of MUC2 and MUC5AC, which has been linked to enhanced tumor progression.10

Regarding the tumor microenvironment (TME), studies have yielded mixed findings. Some report that rectal MAC exhibits higher cytotoxic T cell and regulatory T cell infiltration, alongside significantly increased PD-1 expression.11 Others, however, suggest a negative correlation between PD-1 expression and MAC in intestinal cancers.12 Additionally, MAC is characterized by an abundance of immunosuppressive components, including tumor-associated fibroblasts and M2 macrophages,13 which may further influence prognosis.

Despite these unique pathological and microenvironmental features, MAC histology has not yet been incorporated into treatment decision-making, and no tailored therapeutic approach has been approved. The standard of care for microsatellite-instable (MSI) CRC has been established, however, many prior studies have not accounted for microsatellite status when assessing MAC prognosis and biological characteristics. Therefore, further investigation into microsatellite-stable (MSS) MAC is necessary to identify novel therapeutic targets and improve patient outcomes. In this study, we integrated somatic variant and transcriptome data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics, along with gene expression datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), to elucidate the tumor and microenvironmental characteristics, as well as the therapeutic targets of MAC.

Results

Mucinous adenocarcinoma is associated with poor prognosis

To illustrate the clinical and prognostic features of MAC, we integrated multiple datasets from TCGA colorectal cancer cohorts (colon adenocarcinoma [COAD] and rectum adenocarcinoma [READ]), the MSK-IMPACT cohort (Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets; Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center), and GEO (Figure 1A). Our analysis revealed common features associated with mucinous histology in CRC, including advanced tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) staging, a predominant localization in the right-sided colon, and a higher prevalence of MSI (Tables S1 and S2). Since different microsatellite stability statuses are recognized as distinct CRC subtypes, we stratified the cohort accordingly. Notably, in the MSS subtype, MAC exhibited a significantly poorer overall survival (OS) compared to CAC (log rank test p = 0.037, Figure 1B). To validate these findings, we integrated data from the GEO database and conducted survival analysis for MSI and MSS subtypes. The classification of MSI and MSS samples was performed as described in STAR Methods. Notably, the immune scores of samples predicted to be MSI or MSS were comparable to those of the annotated cases within the same subtype (Figure S1A), supporting the reliability of this prediction approach. In the absence of available pathology information, we assigned a mucinous adenocarcinoma label score to each sample to categorize the mucus phenotype (see STAR Methods for details). This analysis revealed that CRC samples with high mucinous scores were associated with significantly worse OS in MSS CRC, but not in MSI CRC (Figure 1C). A similar trend was observed for relapse-free survival (RFS), whereas disease-free survival (DFS) did not differ by mucinous status (Figures S1B and S1C). These findings suggest that the adverse prognosis of MAC is largely restricted to the MSS subset, underscoring the need for a deeper molecular characterization of MAC, particularly within MSS tumors.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma is a chromosomal stable tumor

We first sought to characterize the genomic alterations associated with the mucinous histology of CRC. Compared to CAC, MAC exhibited a higher whole-exome tumor mutation burden (TMB) and indel ratio (Figures 2A and S2A). However, when stratified by microsatellite status, these differences were not statistically significant (Figures 2A and S2A). Mutational-signature analysis showed that MSI tumors were enriched for signatures 1, 6, and 15, whereas no distinct pattern was observed in MAC (Figure S2B). Interestingly, in the MSK-IMPACT cohort, the number of mutations of MSS-MAC remained higher than in MSS-CAC (p = 0.013, Figure 2A). Because the MSK-IMPACT cohort employed targeted sequencing of 421 cancer-related genes (379 after filtering by histology) rather than whole-exome sequencing, we analyzed tumor-associated genes from the TCGA dataset to validate this finding. The results confirmed that MSS-MAC indeed exhibited higher tumor-associated gene mutations compared to MSS-CAC (p = 0.014, Figure 2A). Among the high-frequency mutations in MAC, notable genes included SMAD4, GLUD2, BRAF, KIAA0825, and KMT2D (Figures 2B and 2C). We next examined differences in copy number variation (CNV) profiles between MAC and CAC. Compared to CAC, MAC exhibited a significantly lower CNV burden, encompassing both amplifications and deletions (Figures 2D and S2D). Given that MSI tumors are generally associated with low CNV levels,14 we adjusted for microsatellite status and found that MAC still exhibited a lower CNV burden (Figures 2D and S2D). Consensus molecular subtype (CMS) classification showed that CMS2—the chromosomal-instable (CIN) phenotype,15,16 was almost absent in MAC (0% vs. 35% in CAC, p < 0.001), whereas the CMS3 subtype was markedly over-represented (41% vs. 6%, p < 0.001) (Figure 2E). The same inverse relationship between high mucin score and CMS2 prevalence was confirmed in the independent GEO cohort, irrespective of MSI/MSS status (Figure S2E). To further assess whether MAC exhibits chromosomal stability, we quantified γH2AX, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks.17 MAC tumors displayed significantly lower γH2AX levels than CAC (Figure 2F), supporting a chromosomally stable (CS) phenotype.

Characteristics of epithelial cells in mucinous adenocarcinoma

To investigate the characteristics of tumor cells in MAC, we analyzed tumor cell heterogeneity using single-cell transcriptome data. We identified 25,451 epithelial cells, marked by EPCAM expression, which were clustered for further analysis (Figures S3A, S3B, and 3A). Comparison between epithelial cells from MSS-MAC and MSS-CAC revealed substantial heterogeneity. Clusters C6, C7, C10, and C11 were predominantly associated with MSS-CAC, while C9 was primarily found in MSS-MAC (Figure 3B). The cell ratio analysis indicated that cluster C1 was more frequent in MSS-CAC, whereas clusters C2, C3, and C4 showed a tendency to be more prevalent in MSS-MAC (Figure 3C). In-depth analysis of the epithelial composition from three MSS-MAC cases revealed distinct distributions: C126 predominantly consisted of C2 and C3; C133 included C2, C3, and C4; and C145 was mainly C2, C3, and C9 (Figure S3B). Thus, C2, C3, and C9 are primarily associated with MAC epithelium, while C1, C6, C7, C10, and C11 are characteristic of CAC epithelium. To further validate these findings, we applied Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) to calculate characteristic scores for various epithelial subtypes in the TCGA and GEO datasets, and then assessed the relationship between these scores and the mucinous phenotype. Notably, the mucinous phenotype exhibited a negative correlation with clusters C1, C6, C7, and C10, while showing a positive correlation with C2, C4, and C9 (Figures S3D and S3E). These findings suggest that specific epithelial subtypes are preferentially associated with either MAC or CAC, further highlighting the distinct molecular landscape of MAC. Functional annotation of cell subtypes using ClusterGVis revealed that MAC epithelium was enriched in pathways related to glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, tumor cell central carbon metabolism (C2), antigen processing and presentation (C2 and C4), gastrointestinal epithelial maintenance, and interleukin 17 signaling (C4), and phagosome pathways (C4 and C9). On the contrary, CAC epithelium showed enrichment in pathways such as positive regulation of amide metabolism, regulation of ubiquitin protein ligase activity (C1), protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, cell matrix adhesion, and fatty acid metabolism (C6); interferon-mediated signaling, cell cycle regulation, and PI3K signaling (C7); and response to glucose starvation, Wnt signaling, and endoplasmic reticulum activation (C10) (Figure 3D). Given their respective enrichment for glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism, we compared the expression of metabolic pathway genes between MAC and CAC using TCGA data. MAC tumors exhibited significantly stronger glycolytic signatures (Figures 3F and 3G), whereas no significant difference in fatty acid metabolism was observed (Figure S3C).

In conclusion, these findings suggest that MAC epithelium exhibits lower CNV characteristics and distinct epithelial subtypes. Although MAC partially overlaps with the CMS3 metabolic subtype, its specific metabolic subprograms and functional implications remain to be elucidated.

Specific TME characteristics of mucinous adenocarcinoma

We next analyzed the TME landscape of MAC. Tumor-infiltrating immune and stromal cells were clustered into distinct subsets: T cells (10 subsets), B cells (7 subsets), myeloid cells (11 subsets), and stromal cells (5 subsets) (Figure 4A). We compared the TME differences between MAC and CAC in MSS subtype to identify MAC-specific features. We found that MAC exhibited an enrichment of natural killer (NK) cells and SPP1 macrophages (Mac-SPP1) but a reduced presence of IgA plasma cells compared to CAC (Figure 4B). Analysis of the TCGA cohort further confirmed that genes highly expressed in MAC were significantly associated with NK cells and SPP1 macrophages. NK-FGFBP2 cells displayed characteristics of CD16+NK cells, marked by expression of cytotoxic genes such as GNLY, GZMB, PRF1, and chemokines XCL1 and XCL2, as well as low expression of both activating and inhibiting receptors of NK cells (KLRC1, KLRK1, CD16, KLR2DL4, Figures 4E and S4C). SPP1 macrophages were characterized by high expression of M2-type genes, including SPP1, CD163, and MSR1 (CD206) (Figure 4C). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) further confirmed differences in SPP1 and TYROBP expression between MAC and CAC (Figure 4D). In addition, although there was no significant difference in overall cell abundance, MAC exhibited stronger fibroblast and myeloid inflammatory signals, irrespective of microsatellite status (Figures 4B, S4A, S4B, and S4D).

These findings highlight distinct TME features of MAC, including an enriched NK and SPP1 macrophage presence, along with stronger fibroblast and myeloid inflammatory signals, which may contribute to its unique tumor biology.

Potential therapeutic targets for mucinous adenocarcinoma

To further explore potential therapeutic targets for MAC, we examined the unique characteristics of MAC tumor cells and their TME in relation to prognosis. Our initial analysis focused on identifying therapeutic targets associated with the epithelial features specific to MAC. A comparison between the epithelial cells of MAC (MAC-ep) and CAC (CAC-ep) revealed 2,187 genes significantly upregulated in MAC-ep. Among these, 276 genes also showed significant high expression in MAC within the TCGA data. Cox analysis revealed that 29 of these genes were associated with poor prognosis in TCGA cohort, and 24 genes were similarly linked to poor prognosis in GEO cohort (Figure 5A). Five genes (FSCN1, SLC11A1, HOXB4, PLXND1, and RAMP1) were identified as having prognostic significance in both databases (Figure 5C). Since these genes could serve as key targets to improve the prognosis of MAC, we then conducted drug sensitivity analyses using data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) and the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, which provided data for four of the identified genes. This analysis revealed that high expression of PLXND1 was linked to resistance to 50 drugs, including 5-FU, a standard chemotherapeutic agent for CRC (Figure 5D). Conversely, FSCN1-high-expressing cells were sensitive to 18 drugs, including ulixertinib and niraparib (Figures 5D and 5E). Interestingly, these drugs also showed efficacy against SLC11A1-high-expressing cells. Additionally, mirin was found to be effective for FSCN1-high-expressing and RAMP1-high-expressing cells (Figures 5D and 5E). However, there were no sensitive drugs identified for PLXND1-high-expressing cells. These findings suggest that targeting PLXND1 may provide a promising strategy to overcome drug resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes.

To further explore the role of the TME in prognosis, we analyzed the cell abundances in the prognostic cohort based on cell types identified via single-cell RNA sequencing. Our analysis revealed that Mac-SPP1, fibroblasts, and monocytes were associated with poorer prognosis in MAC (Figure 5F). Moreover, key marker genes for these cell types were markedly overexpressed in MAC, including SPP1, TREM2, and ANPEP for macrophages, as well as CRIP1, FRZB, and CCDC80 for fibroblasts (Figure 5G). These results underscore the crucial role of TME components in shaping MAC prognosis and suggest that targeting specific TME cell populations, such as SPP1 macrophages and fibroblasts, may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for improving patient outcomes.

Discussion

MAC is a distinct pathological subtype of CRC with poor treatment response and adverse survival outcomes. This study integrates over 3,000 CRC samples to uncover key tumor and microenvironment characteristics specific to MAC, and identifies potential prognosis-related genes and therapeutic targets. We observed that MAC was significantly associated with OS and RFS but showed no clear association with DFS—findings consistent with previous retrospective studies and meta-analyses.18 DFS duration may be confounded by early metastasis and treatment resistance; however, the absence of adjuvant therapy data precludes definitive elucidation of these differences. The divergent relationships of MAC with OS, RFS, and DFS underscore the complexity of MAC’s prognostic impact and highlight its greater significance in advanced-stage disease.

We found that MAC is a CS tumor, characterized by low CNV in the genome and the mutual exclusion of CMS2 in the transcriptome. While previous studies have reported that MAC exhibits lower genomic variation19 and CMS2 mutual exclusion,20 the potential influence of its high MSI rate on these characteristics remains unclear. This study addresses this gap, suggesting that CS is an intrinsic feature of MAC, regardless of MSI status. Although CNV levels were low, frequent alterations in canonical oncogenic pathways were observed in MAC, whereas mutational signature analysis revealed no distinct patterns. These findings highlight the unique molecular landscape of MAC, which appears to be driven primarily by alterations in canonical oncogenic pathways rather than by MSI or chromosomal instability. The CS phenotype of MAC may be attributed to the rarity of TP53 mutations, as TP53 is a key driver of genomic instability in the conventional adenoma pathway.21 The rarity of TP53 mutations, together with the low CNV burden in MAC, points to an alternative evolutionary pathway. According to the study by Joanito et al.,22 CRC epithelial cells can be classified into iCMS2 and iCMS3 subtypes, corresponding to CMS2 and CMS3: iCMS2 tumors, driven by chromosomal instability, arise from conventional adenomas, whereas iCMS3 tumors, which exhibit near-diploid genomes, include all MSI and about one-third of MSS cases, likely originating from serrated lesions. The alignment between MAC/CAC molecular patterns and iCMS3-MSS/iCMS2 profiles suggests that transcriptional subtypes may mirror distinct histopathological origins. The molecular similarity between iCMS3-MSS and MSI cancers22 raises intriguing questions about the relationship between MAC and MSI. Although MSI is frequently associated with MAC, the timing of MSI acquisition remains uncertain. After MSI develops, both molecular and TME features appear largely independent of mucinous differentiation; nevertheless, MSI and MSS subsets within MAC differ substantially. This paradox raises the possibility that MSI may emerge as a late event during serrated adenoma progression, and its temporal trajectory and contribution to tumor evolution warrant further investigation.

Regarding the TME, our analysis revealed that MAC is enriched in NK cells and M2 macrophages (Mac-SPP1), consistent with previous studies.13,23 However, survival analysis showed no significant association between NK cell abundance and poor prognosis, indicating that the role of NK cells in MAC remains unclear. Studies have quantified NK-cell infiltration in solid tumors, consistently reporting low intratumoral densities that are insufficient for robust prognostication.24 Although their prognostic value remains unresolved, NK cells constitute a promising therapeutic target. In the CRC microenvironment, NK cells undergo phenotypic alterations and functional impairment, characterized by upregulated inhibitory receptors and downregulated activating receptors.24 Thus, the enrichment of NK cells in MAC offers a potential leverage point for improving therapeutic efficacy; whether these cells retain functionality in MAC and how to reactivate them warrant further investigation. M2 macrophages (MS4A4A+ macrophages) have been reported to be enriched in MAC tissues, and blocking MS4A4A has been shown to restore CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity.13 Additionally, we observed stronger fibroblastic and myeloid inflammatory signals in MAC. Our previous studies have suggested that myeloid inflammation is associated with reduced efficacy of ICI.25 Clinically, the relationship between MAC and ICI efficacy remains inconclusive. While case reports suggest poorer responses to ICI in MAC,8 further investigation is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Moreover, we identified several prognosis-related genes and potential drug sensitivities in MAC. Among them, FSCN1, SLC11A1, and PLXND1 are the most promising targets. Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of FSCN1 was associated with high tumor invasiveness and poor prognosis, and the protein modulates key oncogenic pathways, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition, PI3K/AKT, Wnt signaling pathway mediated by β-catenin, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling.26,27 In CRC, high SLC11A1 expression correlated to infiltration of cancer-associated fibroblasts and macrophages positively and associated with shorter OS.28 PLXND1 induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition in CRC cells by activating the PI3K/AKT axis.29 Collectively, these molecules represent promising anti-tumor targets. Ongoing clinical trials for ulixertinib and niraparib in CRC have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential.30,31 Future studies using patient-derived organoids or animal models will be critical to unravel the mechanisms underlying chemoresistance and to evaluate targeted interventions for MAC.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of MAC was relatively small, requiring further validation in larger, independent cohorts. Second, epithelial cells exhibit significant heterogeneity. Although our analysis consistently identified CS characteristics in MAC epithelial cells at both the bulk-tissue and single-cell levels, more detailed investigations at the cellular level are needed to confirm these findings. Third, while we identified potential therapeutic targets, their clinical relevance requires further preclinical validation and experimental studies to assess their efficacy and translational potential. Moreover, the impact of other molecular features, such as RAS/RAF mutations, on MAC survival remains unclear. The small sample size and low mutation frequency limited the ability to perform further subclassification, underscoring the need for larger, independent cohorts in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patients and tissue specimens

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) slides of 32 primary CRC tumor tissue (16 males and 16 females, aged 30–80 years) were obtained from surgical resection specimens for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis to assess gene expression. Histological classification as CAC or MAC was determined by experienced pathologists, and all specimens were derived from patients who had not received preoperative treatment at the time of initial diagnosis. This study was approved by the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University Institutional Review Board and ethics committee before the start of the project (2023ZSLYEC-002).

Method details

Data retrieval, preprocessing and Batch effect correction

We included a total of 3,267 CRC samples from three data sources: the Gene Expression Omnibus (Database: GEO), The Cancer Genome Atlas (Database: TCGA-COAD, TCGA-READ), and the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (Database: MSK-IMPACT, Integrated Mutational Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets; Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center). For the TCGA dataset, gene expression data from bulk RNA sequencing (STAR–TPM, Count), somatic mutation data in MAF format, and masked copy number segment data based on Affymetrix SNP 6.0 (SNP6) array data for the TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ cohorts were downloaded from the NCI’s Genomic Data Commons (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/).

For MSK-IMAPCT dataset, genomic results and associated clinical data for colorectal cancer sequenced using MSK-IMAPCT was downloaded from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=crc_msk_2017). Patients in the MSK-Colorectal cohort had been analyzed and described in a previous publication.31 For the validation dataset, we identified CRC studies with detailed follow-up information and larger sample sizes from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Twelve Affymetrix-based cohorts were included (GSE13294, GSE39582, GSE81980, GSE18088, GSE92921, GSE35896, GSE37892, GSE72970, GSE17536, GSE33114, GSE14333, and GSE21510). Raw data were downloaded and reanalyzed using Affymetrix arrays to generate robust multi-array averaging (RMA) normalized data for each dataset.42 Batch effect correction was then performed using the ‘sva’ R package,32 yielding a combined GEO-CRC cohort (n = 1,973). For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, we retrieved scRNA-seq profiles from 62 human CRC samples from the GEO database (accession number: GSE178341).42 The workflow, along with the allocation of discovery and validation cohorts, is presented in Figure 1A.

Clinicopathological and molecular data collation

Histological classification for the TCGA, MSK-IMPACT, and scRNA-seq datasets was determined based on recorded pathological diagnoses, retaining only cases with clearly documented classifications. Eligible cases included adenocarcinoma and mucinous adenocarcinoma of the colon and rectum, while other histological subtypes or cases with unknown classifications were excluded. The subgroup sample sizes for each cohort are presented in Figure 1A. For the validated GEO dataset, a Mucinous Score was calculated for each sample to assess mucinous histology as described below.

Microsatellite instability classification for the TCGA, MSK-IMPACT, and scRNA-seq datasets was obtained from recorded data and previously published studies.43 Samples recorded as microsatellite instability high or deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) are considered to be microsatellite instable (MSI) subtypes. For the validated GEO dataset, MSI features were extracted from the GEO datasets and associated publications. For patients without MSI annotation, an estimation algorithm was applied to predict microsatellite status. Detailed methodological information is provided below. Other clinical and follow-up data were obtained from recorded data and published studies.

Derivation of the mucinous signature and Muc.Score calculation

To develop mucinous signatures, TPM data from TCGA colorectal cancer samples were analyzed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MSS-MAC and MSS-CAC were identified using DESeq2.35 Muc.Scores were derived from DEG sets obtained at fold-change thresholds of 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3, with receiver operating characteristic curve analysis determining the optimal set for final signature construction. The Muc.Score was calculated as the mean expression of upregulated minus downregulated genes.

Estimate algorism to predicted microsatellite status

For the validated GEO dataset, microsatellite status was available for 1,143 of 1,973 patients. For the remaining 830 patients, MSI status was predicted using an estimation algorithm based on immune scores calculated by the estimate R package.38 Patients were ranked by immune score and stratified into deciles. Within each decile, the proportion of MSI cases in the known cohort (α%) was used to classify the top α% of samples in the unknown cohort as MSI, with the remainder designated as MSS. The predicted classification results are shown in Figure S1A, where no significant differences were observed within the same categories, supporting the validity of the predictions.

Differential gene expression analysis between MAC and CAC

Differential gene expression analysis between MAC and CAC was performed using a generalized linear model with the Wald statistical test, assuming that the underlying gene expression count data followed a negative binomial distribution, as implemented in DESeq2.35 DEGs with a q-value < 0.05 were considered for further analysis. The adjusted p-value for multiple testing was calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Estimation of the microenvironment cell population

The consensus molecular subtype (CMS) classification was performed using the CMScaller R package37 and determined with the nearest template predictor algorithm, applying a false discovery rate threshold of <0.05. ESTIMATE algorithm was used to calculate the fraction of stromal and immune cells via the estimate R package.38 To obtain a detailed characterization of immune cell infiltration, the the microenvironment cell populations-counter (MCP-counter) R package was employed to quantify the relative abundances of immune cell types.39 Additionally, we used the gsva function from the GSVA R package to perform single-sample gene set enrichment analysis and evaluate immune cell infiltration levels.40 Genes with a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 and an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 were considered marker genes for generating cell type-specific signatures.

Somatic mutation and CNV analysis

Somatic mutation data from the TCGA and MSK-IMPACT cohorts were analyzed using the maftools R package.41 Tumor mutational burden was defined as the number of somatic mutations per million bases, considering only the following functional classifications: Frame_Shift_Del, Frame_Shift_Ins, In_Frame_Del, In_Frame_Ins, Missense_Mutation, Nonsense_Mutation, Nonstop_Mutation, and Splice_Site.

The deconvolution of COSMIC mutational signatures was performed using the musicatk R package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/musicatk).Masked Copy Number Segment files were obtained from the TCGA data portal and processed using GISTIC2 (https://cloud.genepattern.org/) to generate focal somatic copy number alteration (SCNA) values. SCNA were categorized as -2, -1, 0, 1, 2 where -2 refers to homozygous deletions, 2 refers to high-level amplifications, -1 hemizygous i.e., gene loss, with 1 referring to copy number gain and 0: no SCNA.CNV burden was calculated as the proportion of genes with focal SCNAs relative to the total number of genes.

Processing, quality control, and dimensionality reduction of scRNA-seq data

The scRNA-seq dataset was obtained from GSE178341, which includes tumor and normal samples collected from Massachusetts General Hospital.42 Only tumor samples were retained for analysis. The combined data were downloaded and converted into a Seurat object using the Seurat R package (V5.0.1).36 Single-cell data were processed using Seurat V5. Cells with <200 or >2,500 detected genes or >20% mitochondrial content were excluded, as were genes detected in <3 cells. Data were normalized using NormalizeData. For dimensionality reduction, the top 2,000 variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, followed by scaling with ScaleData. t-SNE was applied using 20 principal components (PCs) for cluster visualization. For cell type clustering, PCA was performed on 2,000 variable genes (20 components), with UMI counts regressed out.

Cell type annotation of the scRNA-seq data

The FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in the Seurat package were utilized with 20 PCs to identify cell clusters and subpopulations, setting the resolution parameter between 0.1 and 0.5. Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical gene markers, including: T cells (CD3D, CD4, CD8A); B cells (CD79A, CD79B, MS4A1); plasma cells (MZB1, JCHAIN, XBP1); Myeloid cells (CD14, CD68); Epithelial cells (EPCAM, CD24); Fibroblast (COL1A2, COL3A1, MYH11, ACTA2); Endothelial cells (VWF, PECAM1, RAMP2). Cell subpopulations were manually annotated using gene markers extracted from the literature: CD4 subsets (CD4, FOXP3, IL2RA, CCR7, IL7R, KLRB1, CD40LG, RORA) ; CD8 subsets (CD8A, GZMK, IFNG, GZMB, CXCL13, PDCD1, HAVCR2, MKI67 ); NK cells (TYROBP, FGFBP2, KLRF1) ; Monocyte-like/Macrophages subsets (FCN1, S100A8, S100A9, IL1B, APOE, C1QC, SPP1, CD68, CD163); Dendritic cells (CCL19, LAMP3,CLIC3, LILRA4, CLEC10A, CD1C); Mast cell (CPA3,TPSB2, KIT).

Enrichment analysis and CNV analysis for epithelial clusters

Enrichment analysis for epithelial clusters was conducted using the enrichCluster function from the ClusterGVis R package (https://github.com/junjunlab/ClusterGVis), which integrates with clusterProfiler. Initial copy number variations (CNVs) for each region were estimated using the inferCNV R package (https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV, accessed on September 5, 2022) with a cutoff of 0.1, Hidden Markov Model smoothing, and fibroblasts/endothelial cells as reference. The CNV score was calculated by determining the median expression value of each gene across all cells and then summing the squared differences between gene expression values and their medians for each cell, reflecting the variation in gene expression within individual cells.44

Tumor cell line drug sensitivity analysis

Gene expression data and IC50 values of tumor cell lines were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (http://www.cancerrxgene.org/, accessed on October 29, 2024). Only colorectal cancer MSS cell line data were retained to investigate the correlation between selected gene expression and drug sensitivity. Gene expression data (RMA-normalized) and drug sensitivity data (LN_IC50) were matched by cell identifiers (COSMIC_ID) from the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC). Cell lines were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on median gene expression, and IC50 differences were compared between groups. Higher IC50 indicated drug resistance, while lower IC50 indicated sensitivity.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

FFPE tissue sections were sequentially deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and visualized using a DAB color solution. The primary antibodies utilized included γH2AX (Cell Signaling Technology, #9718), TYROBP (Cusabio, #PA699424), FCGR3A (Proteintech, #16559-1-AP), SPP1 (Proteintech, #22952-1-AP), CCL18 (Proteintech, #22303-1-AP), TIMP1 (Proteintech, #16644-1-AP), FST (Proteintech, #60060-1-Ig), RAMP1 (Proteintech, #10327-1-AP).

Images were captured using a BX53 upright microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For each IHC sample of γH2AX, the tumor area were selected based on morphological features. The total number of cancer cells and the number of γH2AX-positive cells were counted in each field, then the global percentage of γH2AX-positive cells was calculated. IHC counting and scoring was independently quantified by two blinded, experienced scorers. Immunostaining intensity (A) was classified into four grades: 0 (negative), 1 (weakly positive), 2 (positive), and 3 (strongly positive). The proportion of staining-positive cells (B) was graded into five categories: 0 (<5%), 1 (6%–25%), 2 (26%–50%), 3 (51%–75%), and 4 (>75%). The final IHC score was calculated as A × B.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Categorical variable differences were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The Wilcoxon test was used to compare the nonparametric data sets. Correlation analysis between continuous variables was conducted using Pearson’s correlation test. For survival analysis, Cox proportional hazards regression models and the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test were applied as appropriate. All P-values were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05, unless otherwise specified. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.
