Performance of compressed-sensing cardiac accelerated cine MR in evaluating left ventricular function: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhaozhao Wang, Huihui Kong, Zekun Zhou, Anqi Yang, Zien Zhou, Chi Wang, Jing An, Yi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the consistency of left ventricular (LV) characteristics on cine cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) images acquired from compressed-sensing (CS) accelerated cine versus standard segmented cine (Std-cine) sequences, both utilizing a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) readout.Materials & methodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis. Electronic databases were searched for studies where both CS-cine and Std-cine sequences were performed for individual participants and LV characteristic data from the two sequences were reported separately. Two investigators independently assessed the eligibility of the retrieved studies and extracted data. Key outcomes included LV ejection fraction (LVEF), volume, mass, and image quality. We performed subgroup analyses for children, breath control, arrhythmia, and MRI field intensity.ResultsThirty-five studies were included. The LVEF (%) obtained from CS-cine imaging did not differ from that obtained from Std-cine imaging (standardized mean difference [SMD]: −0.05; 95% confidence interval [95%CI]: −0.11, 0.01; p = 0.12; I2 = 0.0%). No other LV parameters differed (I2 = 0.0% to 0.0%), nor did heterogeneity differ across subgroups by MR field intensity, breath-hold requirements, children, or arrhythmia (for inter-subgroup heterogeneity, all p < 0.05). The overall image quality was comparable between the two sequences (SMD: −0.36; 95%CI: −0.65, 0.08; p < 0.05; I2 = 37%). The difference across participants with arrhythmias (SMD: −0.04; 95%CI: −1.26, 1.18; p = 0.95; I2 = 87%) and without arrhythmias (SMD: −0.63; 95%CI: −1.05, −0.21; p = 0.003; I2 = 91%) or with no relevant information on arrhythmias (SMD: −0.17; 95%CI: −0.51, 0.18; p = 0.35; I2 = 87%) may have accounted for the high heterogeneity.ConclusionsCS-accelerated cine imaging and standard segmented cine imaging did not differ in their ability to evaluate LV characteristics. The CS-cine images quality was acceptable and superior to that of conventional cine images for arrhythmias.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022328219.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02522-w.Keywords: Cardiac magnetic resonance, Cine, Compressed-sensing, Left ventricular function, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Changes in left ventricular (LV) morphology and function are key indicators in cardiac disease evaluation, prognosis prediction, and treatment decisions [1, 2]. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is the gold standard for evaluating LV structure and function [3]. In current clinical practice, the steady-state free precession cine CMR sequence is widely used to assess LV characteristics, including LV ejection fraction (LVEF), end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), and mass [4]. As an essential component of any comprehensive CMR protocol, cine imaging is indispensable for functional assessment. However, the long acquisition time of Std-cine (approximately 10 minutes, including both scanning and multiple breath-holds) adds substantially to the overall length of a CMR protocol. This, in turn, limits the broader applicability of CMR, particularly in patients with poor breath-hold tolerance or arrhythmias.

Compressed-sensing (CS) accelerated cine CMR, a fast imaging technique, was recently developed to reduce acquisition time through k-space sparse sampling and iterative reconstruction [5]. Several studies have evaluated the accuracy of CS-cine; however, its accuracy and applicability remain uncertain [6, 7]. Because the included study populations, MRI scanner field strengths, and sample size differed among previous studies, we performed this systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the consistency of LV characteristics measured via CS-accelerated cine (CS-cine) sequences versus standard segmented cine (Std-cine) sequences, both of which employ a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) readout.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [8]. The protocol was registered with the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42022328219). One principal investigator (ZW) conducted the literature search in the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials electronic databases from inception to 31st Dec 2023 using the following combinations of medical subject heading terms or keywords: (‘Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Cine’ OR ‘Cine MRI’) AND (‘Sparse Sampling’ OR ‘real time’ OR ‘accelerate’ OR ‘motion corrected’ OR ‘compressed sens*’ OR ‘single breath hold’ OR ‘fast’) AND (‘Heart’ OR ‘Cardiac’ OR ‘left ventricular’ OR ‘ventricular function’) (Online supplemental material S1). We did not set language restrictions. Reference lists of all retrieved studies and related review articles were manually searched for further relevant studies until no further publications were found.

It should be noted that the term ‘real time’ was included in the search strategy solely to maximize sensitivity. All included studies employed ECG-gated or ECG-triggered acquisitions (retrospective or prospective) for both CS-accelerated and standard cine sequences. Studies utilizing purely non-gated continuous real-time imaging without cardiac synchronization were not included, as they are beyond the scope of this comparative analysis.

Study selection

Two investigators (ZZ and AY) independently checked the retrieved studies. After screening titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible articles, full texts were read to confirm eligibility according to pre-specified criteria. We included observational studies in which CMR was performed with both CS-accelerated cine (CS-cine) and standard segmented cine (Std-cine) sequences for each participant, and data on LV characteristics from the two sequences were presented separately. We excluded 1) studies where the primary aim was to assess the image quality of CS-cine sequences; 2) reviews, editorials, letters, cases (or case series with samples < 20 participants), reports, guidelines, technical notes, and books; and 3) studies with missing outcome data and no reply from the authors after contacting them. Conference abstracts were included if the data could be extracted. If the same population was presented in more than one publication, the article or abstract with the largest sample was included (judged by overlapping study dates, grant funding numbers, and similar or identical reported patient characteristics). Any disagreements were resolved in consultation with a third investigator (YH).

For the purpose of this meta-analysis, CS-cine refers to compressed sensing-accelerated cine imaging, and Std-cine refers to the conventional multi-breath-hold segmented cine imaging used as the reference standard. Both sequences utilize a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) readout.

Data extraction

We used a standardized data extraction spreadsheet. Extracted data included the following: 1) first author, year of publication, country or region, study design, and sample size; 2) participant characteristics such as age, sex, and CMR indications (or healthy volunteers); 3) CMR parameters including MR field intensity, repetition time (TR), echo time (TE), image matrix, reconstructed spatial resolution, temporal resolution, slice thickness/gap, flip angle, bandwidth, acceleration factor, mean acquisition time, reconstruction time, electrocardiogram (ECG)-gating mode, cardiac scanning plan, and breath-holding requirement (no/single/multiple); 4) key LV characteristics such as LVEF, EDV, ESV, and mass; 5) image quality scale; and 6) intra- and interobserver agreements for LV function according to the Bland-Altman analysis or the intraclass correlation coefficient. The quality of each included study was judged using a revised tool for quality assessment of diagnostic accuracy studies (QUADAS-2).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was LVEF. Other outcomes were LVEDV, LVESV, LV mass, image quality, and intra- and interobserver agreements for LV function measurement.

Statistical analysis

Mean values with standard deviations (SD) were collated for continuous outcomes, and the number (percentage) of dichotomous outcomes was summarized. Continuous outcomes that were reported as medians (interquartile range [IQR]) were converted to mean (SD) as previously described [9, 10]. Pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence interval (CIs) were estimated for LVEF and other continuous outcomes between CS-cine and Std-cine CMR sequences using the random-effects model weighted by inverse variance. This model also pooled the image quality defined by a unified 5-point scale (ordinal, with higher points representing better quality). The proportion of insufficient image quality (<3 points) was compared between the two sequences as a sensitivity analysis. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistics. I2 of < 50%, 50%-75%, or >75% was regarded as low, substantial, or high heterogeneity, respectively. In studies with a high likelihood of heterogeneity, sensitivity or subgroup analyses were performed by excluding individual studies or stratifying potential confounding factors (MR field strength, breath-control requirement, age, and arrhythmia) to determine reasons for the differences. We provided a narrative summary of LV function measurement precision on CS-cine and Std-cine sequences separately because a meta-analysis was not applicable to these parameters. Evidence of publication bias was determined using Egger’s regression test for funnel asymmetry and visual inspection of the funnel plots. A two-sided P of ≤0.05 was deemed significant in all cases. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 15.1.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The literature search yielded 1,971 potentially eligible articles or conference abstracts, of which 134 were read in full (Fig. 1). Finally, 35 observational studies [6, 7, 11–43] involving 1,969 participants met the inclusion criteria. Many articles were excluded because they did not apply the CS-accelerated cine sequence (n = 31) or report of LV characteristics (n = 29; Online Supplementary Table S1). The included studies were published from 2014 to 2023, with sample sizes of 20 [6, 16, 26] to 154 [36] participants, a mean participant age of 6.27 years [31] to 74.7 years [6], and the proportion of male participants from 45% [6] to 82% [11] (Table 1). Most studies required single breath-holding during CS-cine sequence scanning; 14 studies allowed free-breathing [12, 14, 15, 18–20, 22, 31, 38, 39, 41–43]. Among these free-breathing studies, three studies dedicated respiratory motion correction techniques—specifically fully automated, non-rigid registration-based retrospective motion correction algorithms—to minimize respiratory motion artifacts during free-breathing acquisition [38, 41, 43]. All studies recruited patients with regular heart rhythms or healthy volunteers, except three that recruited patients with arrhythmia [6, 7, 36]. Six studies specifically enrolled pediatric and adolescent populations [22, 30–32, 35, 39]. The age range across these pediatric studies spanned from 0.5 to 21 years, with mean ages ranging from 6.3 to 16.7 years. Among these pediatric studies, general anesthesia or sedation was employed in a subset of patients in three studies: Naresh et al. 2021 [30] (11/28 patients), Zou et al. 2021 [31] (low-weight or young children in the free-breathing cohort), and Xu et al. 2023 [39] (11 patients in the younger cohort). In sedated patients, free-breathing acquisition was typically used, whereas older cooperative children were generally able to comply with breath-hold instructions. One study [35] utilized an ultra-short single breath-hold (6–12 seconds) that was well tolerated by all pediatric participants.

Most studies used Siemens MRI systems with various CS-cine sequence parameters: 3.0T and 1.5 T systems in 22 and 13 studies, respectively; prospective and retrospective ECG-gating in 23 and 11 studies, respectively, TR from 1.64 ms [20] to 4.23 ms [25], TE from 1.06 ms [35] to 1.54 ms [16], spatial resolutions from 1.07 × 1.07 mm2 [40] to 2.0 × 2.0 mm2 [18] and temporal resolutions from 30 ms [11, 17] to 49 ms [7, 23, 28] (Online Supplementary Table S2). Notably, among these prospectively triggered studies, several incorporated strategies to avoid truncation of end-diastole—such as adaptive triggering with continuous acquisition until the next R-wave [7, 23, 31, 38, 39, 41, 43], data acquisition over two heartbeats [13, 15, 26, 27], or retrospective phase interpolation [31, 41, 43]—effectively achieving coverage comparable to retrospective gating. One study did not explicitly specify the ECG synchronization method [36]. The standard reference cine sequences employed parallel imaging acceleration in the majority of studies (33/35), with GRAPPA (acceleration factor 2–3) being the most common technique, followed by SENSE (acceleration factor 1.5–2). For CS-accelerated cine acquisitions, the number of excitations per slice varied: 22 studies utilized single-shot acquisition (net acceleration factor 8–12.8), 7 studies employed a 2-shot approach (net acceleration factor 5.6–8) [21, 22, 24, 26, 29, 33, 40], and 2 studies used a 3-shot technique [17, 37]. Detailed acquisition parameters for each included study are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Online Supplementary Figure S1 shows the QUADAS-2 quality assessment characteristics.

Scan time and breath-hold reduction

Across the 35 included studies, CS-accelerated cine sequences substantially reduced acquisition times compared with conventional segmented cine. The total scan time, including breath-hold rest intervals, was reduced by approximately 80%, from a mean of 254 ± 153 seconds for conventional cine to 52 ± 57 seconds for CS-cine. The number of required breath-holds was reduced by approximately 50%, from a mean of 9.7 ± 4.1 for conventional cine to 5.3 ± 3.5 for CS-cine. Detailed scan time data for each individual study are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Image quality for CS-cine vs SSFP-cine sequences

Image quality was assessed in 30 studies: 15 [13, 15, 19, 20, 22, 24, 25, 31, 33, 36, 39–43] by the 5-point Likert scale, 11 [6, 7, 11, 17, 21, 23, 28, 29, 34, 35, 38] by the 4-point Likert scale, 1 [30] by the 3-point scale, and 3 [14, 26, 27] by the dichotomies of diagnosis adequate) (Online Supplementary Table S4). Overall, the image quality was comparable between the CS-cine and Std-cine sequences (unified ordinal scale SMD: −0.36; 95%CI: −0.65, −0.08; p = 0.01; I2 = 90%; pooled relative risk of dichotomous insufficient image quality: 1.00; 95%CI: 0.99, 1.01; p = 0.93; I2 = 0.0%; Online Supplementary Figures S3, S4). The differences across participants with arrhythmia (unified ordinal scale SMD: −0.04; 95% CI: −1.26, 1.18; p = 0.95; I2 = 87%), without arrhythmias (SMD: −0.63; 95% CI: −1.05, −0.21; p = 0.003; I2 = 91%) or with no relevant information on arrhythmias (SMD: −0.17; 95%CI: −0.51, 0.18; p = 0.35; I2 = 87%) accounted for the high heterogeneity (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3), whereas the differences across age groups did not (p = 0.35; Online Supplementary Figures S5).

Summary of LV function evaluation precision on CS-cine and Std-cine sequences

Discussion

The major findings of our meta-analysis can be summarized as follows. (1) CS-accelerated cine (CS-cine) and standard segmented cine (Std-cine) CMR imaging did not differ in their evaluations of LVEF, and LV volume and mass, regardless of MR field intensity, breath-holding, participants’ age, and presence of arrhythmia. (2) The image quality was comparable between the two sequences, with some indication that the CS-cine CMR sequence had better image quality than did the SSFP-cine sequence in patients with arrhythmia.

Conventional MRI techniques sequentially sample k-space according to the Nyquist criterion to ensure that the reconstruction process is simple and solid [44]. To achieve high spatial/temporal resolution and whole heart coverage, CMR cine imaging is currently performed using retrospective ECG-gating for two-dimensional segmented acquisition under breath-holding [41, 45]. That is, the entire k-space segment is collected through multiple cardiac cycles. However, in patients with poor breath-holding ability or arrhythmia, inconsistent data acquired through multiple cardiac cycles can lead to blurring or artifacts of recombinant images [44]. It should be acknowledged that conventional segmented SSFP cine can also be performed during free breathing by employing multiple signal averages to suppress respiratory motion artifacts. However, this approach substantially prolongs acquisition time and may still yield suboptimal image quality in patients with irregular breathing patterns or severe dyspnea. Consequently, breath-hold acquisition remains the clinical standard for conventional cine imaging.

CS-accelerated cine sequences were developed to overcome two challenges of traditional segmented cine sequences. First, many cardiac patients have difficulty holding their breath, and second, ECG synchronization for data segmentation may be ineffective in patients with arrhythmias. CS acquires fewer data through k-space undersampling and iterative reconstruction, which dramatically decreases CMR data acquisition time and accelerates image acquisition [44, 46]. In 2006, Candès et al. proposed the CS theory, using signal sparsity to sparse sample the k-space at less than the Nyquist sampling rate and perform algorithmic reconstruction to achieve fast sampling without sacrificing image resolution [44]. In 2007, Lustig et al. first applied CS technology to MR and proposed three application requirements of CS; signal sparsity or sparse transformation, incoherence of undersampling artifacts, and nonlinear image reconstruction that enforces sparsity [47]. Since then, MR CS technology has been introduced to a variety of clinical applications, including CMR, whole-body imaging, neuroimaging, and spectral imaging [47, 48]. CS-cine imaging obtains similar spatial/temporal resolution images with fewer cardiac cycles, thus shortening scanning time, and is especially beneficial for patients with poor breath-holding ability or arrhythmia [41, 49].

CS technology reduces scanning time

Our meta-analysis confirms that CS-accelerated cine imaging substantially reduces both total scan time and the number of required breath-holds compared with conventional segmented cine. This reduction is primarily attributable to the high undersampling factors employed in CS acquisitions, which allow multiple slices to be acquired per breath-hold or enable free-breathing acquisitions altogether. The shortened acquisition time not only improves patient comfort, but also extends the applicability of CMR to populations with limited breath-hold capacity, including patients with heart failure, pediatric patients, and those requiring sedation.

CS technology yields accurate LV function parameters

We found that the CS-cine and Std-cine assessments were comparable in terms of LV function accuracy. Accurate assessment of LV function helps guide treatment and judge prognosis and has great clinical significance [1, 50]. Current recommendations or guidelines for CMR examinations from the Society for CMR do not specify the conditions for use of CS-cine [50]. The undetermined accuracy of LV evaluations, along with relevant studies having small sizes (N = 155 was the largest [50]) and differing scanning protocols, has caused concern. Meta-analyses of existing studies help reduce the possibility of chance findings in individual studies. Our results showed that the CS-cine sequence retained all essential MR signals facilitating CMR images with sufficient sharpness and acceptable noise levels after Fourier transform, despite its rapidly incomplete MRI data acquisition by k-space undersampling [47, 51]. CS-cine can be applied to MR machines with different field strengths. We found no inferiorities in the 1.5T to 3.0T machines in LV evaluation or image quality on CS-cine images, which is consistent with corresponding results from SSFP-cine CMR imaging from previous reports [52, 53]. This suggests the stability of CS-cine in accurately assessing LV function.

CS technology yielded better image quality in some patients

CS-cine can also be used for patients with arrhythmias or poor breath-holding ability. In patients with arrhythmias, the image quality was better with the CS-cine sequence than with the Std-cine sequence. Accurate endocardial and epicardial contours are key to obtaining accurate LVEF and LV volume. Arrhythmias lead to irregular R-R intervals on ECG and cause incorrect MRI data acquisition owing to abnormal ECG-gating [31]. CS-cine acquires MRI data faster with fewer inconsistent MR signals for k-space filling across irregular cardiac cycles.

The number of excitations (shots) employed in CS-cine acquisitions also influences image quality. Among the included studies, 24 utilized single-shot acquisitions, 8 employed 2-shot techniques, and 2 used 3-shot acquisitions (Supplementary Table S2). Single-shot CS achieves the highest acceleration factors (typically 8–12.8) but is more susceptible to image smoothing and reduced edge sharpness due to sparser k-space sampling and greater reliance on iterative reconstruction. In contrast, 2-shot and 3-shot approaches distribute k-space sampling across multiple heartbeats, increasing the effective sampling density and improving spatial resolution and boundary delineation at the cost of slightly longer breath-hold durations. This trade-off explains why several studies employing 2-shot CS reported image quality scores comparable to conventional cine [21, 24, 29, 40], whereas single-shot CS studies more frequently noted mild image degradation [7, 23, 28]. Nevertheless, even single-shot CS consistently provided diagnostic image quality across all patient populations, and in arrhythmic patients, its robustness to R-R variability outweighed the modest reduction in edge sharpness.

Poor breath-holding ability is common in patients with chronic heart failure [54], pediatric populations, and those with cognitive or hearing impairments. Our subgroup analyses demonstrated that CS-cine imaging—whether performed with breath-holding or during free breathing—achieves comparable LV functional assessment to standard cine across these populations without compromising image quality. Thus, when CS-cine sequences are employed, breath-holding may be safely exempted, offering a more comfortable examination experience for these patients. It should be noted, however, that the number of studies specifically evaluating free-breathing CS-cine in pediatric cohorts remains limited [22, 31, 39], and further dedicated studies in this population are warranted to definitively establish its accuracy and reliability.

Study limitations

Our study had some limitations. First, different CS-cine sequence parameters, cardiac scanning plans, MRI machines, and image analysis software were used in the included studies. Therefore, we cannot provide management recommendations. A standardized scanning protocol and unified image post-processing method for CS-cine CMR sequence are required. Second, we focused only on key LV functional and structural parameters. LV global longitudinal strain is another important index, which is more sensitive than is LVEF for detecting subclinical LV systolic dysfunction and to predicting unfavorable prognoses [55]. However, we did not investigate LV global longitudinal strain because few reports have been published on it for CS-cine imaging. Additionally, the performance of CS-cine imaging in evaluating right ventricular function is unknown. Third, CMR examinations usually involve other sequences (e.g.,T1-mapping, T2-mapping, T2-star, late gadolinium enhancement, extracellular volume, and diffusion) in addition to the cine sequence. Our study did not refer to an optimal scanning order of the CS-cine sequence, which can reduce the overall CMR examination time, as shown in one recent study [55]. Finally, although our findings suggest that CS-cine imaging may be an option for those with poor breath-hold control or arrhythmias, generalizing the application of CS-cine requires high-quality randomized control trials concerning associations between CS-cine sourced LV parameters and long-term outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CS-accelerated cine CMR technology significantly reduces scanning times by reducing the overall examination time and number of breath-holds, while providing LV functional assessments comparable to standard segmented cine imaging. It is especially important for children and patients with poor breath-hold control or arrhythmia because it may improve patient compliance.
