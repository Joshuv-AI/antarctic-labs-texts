From policy to practice: feasibility and acceptability of training ASHAs to deliver birth preparedness and complication readiness in North Karnataka, India: a pre–post study
Authors: Annapurna Kari, Mubashir Angolkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal mortality remains a major public health concern in low-resource settings. Birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) is an established strategy to improve timely care-seeking and reduce preventable maternal and neonatal complications. Utilizing Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) to deliver BPCR interventions may strengthen community-level implementation; however, evidence on feasibility and acceptability is limited.ObjectiveTo assess the feasibility and acceptability of an ASHA training strategy for community-level delivery of BPCR interventions in North Karnataka, India.MethodsA quasi-experimental pre–post interventional study was conducted among 100 ASHAs from two Primary Health Centres (PHCs) and one Community Health Centre (CHC). All eligible ASHAs who provided informed consent were included using complete enumeration sampling. Baseline BPCR-related knowledge and practice data were collected through face-to-face interviews. ASHAs then received structured BPCR training delivered through a Birth Planner—a pictorial educational pamphlet—over three interactive sessions at 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals. Post-intervention assessment was conducted using the same questionnaire. Pre- and post-intervention scores were compared using paired t-tests, and effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s dz.ResultsMean baseline knowledge scores for pregnancy, labour and childbirth, and postpartum danger signs were 31.55, 27.11, and 27.27, respectively, increasing to 73.45, 53.22, and 59.27 following the intervention. Self-reported practice scores for birth preparedness improved from 49.33 at baseline to 99.67 post-intervention. All improvements were statistically significant (p < 0.001) with large to very large effect sizes (Cohen’s dz ≥ 1.31), indicating feasibility and high acceptance of BPCR implementation through structured ASHA training.ConclusionsStructured BPCR training using a simple, pictorial Birth Planner was feasible and acceptable among ASHAs in a rural district of North Karnataka, resulting in significant improvements in self-reported BPCR knowledge and practices. These findings, while limited by the pre–post design and single-district context, support ASHA-led BPCR integration within the National Health Mission. Future multi-site controlled studies are warranted to assess generalizability and long-term impact on maternal and neonatal health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14986-1.

Introduction

Maternal mortality remains a major public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where health system constraints and inequities in access to quality care persist. Globally, an estimated 287,000 maternal deaths occurred in 2020 despite substantial declines over recent decades, representing approximately 94% of all maternal deaths in LMICs and underscoring the unfinished agenda of improving maternal health outcomes [1]. India has made notable progress, reducing its maternal mortality ratio (MMR) from 556 per 100,000 live births in 1990 to 97 per 100,000 live births in 2019–21, a decline of more than 80% [2]. Nevertheless, India continues to contribute substantially to the global burden, accounting for an estimated 8–9% of global maternal deaths in 2020, with approximately 24,000 maternal deaths recorded that year [1]. Although several Indian states have approached or achieved the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target—a globally agreed benchmark of fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030—preventable maternal and neonatal deaths remain concentrated around labour, delivery, and the immediate postpartum period [3, 4].

Pregnancy-related complications are often unpredictable, and many maternal deaths are linked to systemic delays in recognizing complications, deciding to seek care, reaching appropriate facilities, and receiving timely and adequate treatment [5]. Addressing these delays requires strengthening not only facility-based services but also community-level preparedness and linkages within the health system. Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness (BPCR), endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a key component of antenatal care, promotes proactive planning for childbirth and obstetric emergencies through identification of skilled birth attendants, facility selection, financial and transport planning, and community support mechanisms [6, 7].

In India, Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) serve as frontline community health workers and play a central role in maternal health promotion and service linkage. Their proximity to households positions them well to support BPCR delivery. However, evidence on the feasibility and acceptability of structured training approaches enabling ASHAs to implement BPCR at the community level remains limited, particularly in specific local contexts. Therefore, this study aimed to assess ASHAs’ BPCR-related knowledge and practices and to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of training ASHAs to deliver BPCR strategies to pregnant women in a rural district of North Karnataka, India.

Methods

Study design and setting

A quasi-experimental pre–post study design was employed. The study was conducted between February 2020 and March 2023 in a rural district of North Karnataka, India. The district had a total of 28 Primary Health Centres (PHCs) and 4 Community Health Centres (CHCs). Two PHCs and one CHC were randomly selected using simple random sampling as study sites.

Participants and sampling

The study population comprised all ASHAs working in the selected health facilities. Of the 112 ASHAs registered across the three selected facilities, 108 met the eligibility criteria (at least six months of work experience and willingness to provide informed consent). Of these, 100 ASHAs consented to participate, yielding a response rate of 92.6%. ASHAs who declined participation or had fewer than six months of experience were excluded. Complete enumeration sampling was used to ensure full representation of all consenting eligible ASHAs across the selected centres.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using two structured instruments. BPCR-related knowledge and self-reported practice data were assessed using a questionnaire adapted from the JHPIEGO (Johns Hopkins Program for International Education in Gynecology and Obstetrics, an affiliate of Johns Hopkins University) Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness monitoring tools [8]. The instrument was contextually modified to reflect local health system practices and the service delivery roles of ASHAs. The adapted questionnaire underwent local validation through expert review and pilot testing with ten ASHAs from a non-study facility, conducted prior to the main study, to ensure content relevance, clarity, and feasibility.

Feasibility and acceptability of the Birth Planner were evaluated using a standardized tool adapted from the System Usability Scale (SUS), a widely validated instrument for assessing usability [9]. The SUS uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) across seven items each for feasibility and acceptability. Items were modified to align with the BPCR training context. The feasibility and acceptability tool was administered immediately following the final training session. Scores were aggregated and analyzed descriptively using frequencies and percentages to characterize participants’ perceptions. The tool was reviewed for contextual appropriateness before administration.

The final English-language versions of both instruments have been provided as supplementary files: the adapted ASHA interview questionnaire (Supplementary File 1) and the feasibility and acceptability questionnaire (Supplementary File 2).

Study implementation and the Birth Planner

The intervention consisted of BPCR training delivered using a structured Birth Planner — a pictorial pamphlet developed by the research team as a low-literacy, context-specific educational tool covering key maternal and neonatal practices and components of birth preparedness. The planner was distributed to all participants and used both as a training aid and as a community education resource for pregnant women (Fig. 1). All interviews and training sessions were conducted at the respective PHC or CHC premises to maintain standardization of setting.

Pre-test BPCR knowledge and self-reported practice data were collected through face-to-face interviews prior to the intervention. ASHAs received three interactive training sessions at 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals (approximately three hours each), supported by the Birth Planner and focused on recognition of maternal danger signs and development of a family-centred birth plan. Knowledge reinforcement was assessed through oral questioning with refresher inputs as needed. Post-test data were collected one month after the final session using the same questionnaire.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Paired t-tests were applied to compare pre- and post-intervention knowledge and self-reported practice scores. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s dz (mean difference divided by the standard deviation of the difference scores for paired samples) to quantify the magnitude of pre–post changes. Effect sizes were interpreted as: small (dz = 0.20), medium (dz = 0.50), large (dz = 0.80), and very large (dz ≥ 1.30), following conventional benchmarks. Feasibility and acceptability responses were analysed descriptively using frequencies and percentages. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Committee (Human) for Ph.D. Research, KAHER, Belagavi (Ref. No. KAHER/EC/19–20/290619002). Administrative permission to collect data was obtained from the Department of Health and Family Welfare Services (RCH), Government of Karnataka. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was entirely voluntary, and ASHAs were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without penalty. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout.

Table 2 shows statistically significant pre–post improvements in all BPCR knowledge and self-reported practice domains among ASHAs (n = 100; all p < 0.001). Knowledge gains ranged from 26.11 (labour and childbirth danger signs; 95% CI: 22.91–29.31) to 51.75 (newborn care; 95% CI: 47.01–56.49), with substantial increases also observed in pregnancy danger signs, antenatal care, information provision, birth preparedness, postpartum care, and breastfeeding. Very large effects were observed for antenatal care (dz = 3.34), information provided to pregnant women (dz = 4.60), birth preparedness (dz = 2.95), postpartum care (dz = 2.57), newborn care (dz = 2.16), pregnancy danger signs (dz = 2.05), and birth preparedness services (dz = 3.02). All effect sizes exceeded the conventional threshold for large effects (dz ≥ 0.80), demonstrating educationally and programmatically meaningful improvements. The exceptionally high effect sizes observed in certain domains may reflect cumulative gains from repeated training, practical reinforcement, and ceiling effects in domains where post-test scores approached maximum attainable values.

Responses of ASHA workers towards acceptability of the Birth Planner (n = 100)

Table 5 indicates high acceptability of the Birth Planner among ASHA workers. All participants expressed positive experience: 72% strongly agreed and 28% agreed that they liked using it, while 83% agreed and 17% strongly agreed that it was easy to use. Rapid learnability was reported by all participants (75% agree; 25% strongly agree), and universal confidence in use was observed (84% agree; 16% strongly agree). Perceived barriers were minimal, with 95% disagreeing that skilled assistance was required, 100% rejecting that the planner was cumbersome, and 85% disagreeing that it was inconsistent, collectively supporting its acceptability for routine BPCR delivery.

Discussion

This quasi-experimental pre–post study evaluated structured BPCR training delivered through a Birth Planner among ASHAs in a rural district of North Karnataka, India. Findings demonstrated statistically significant improvements in BPCR-related knowledge and self-reported practice domains, accompanied by large to very large effect sizes, and high feasibility and acceptability of the Birth Planner as a training tool.

The sociodemographic profile of participating ASHA workers—predominantly married women aged 30–39 years with secondary-level education—mirrors findings from Indian studies conducted in Karnataka, Uttar Pradesh, and Bihar [10–12]. Similar to NFHS-5 data, most ASHAs belonged to lower or lower-middle socioeconomic strata and held BPL cards, reflecting national recruitment norms that prioritize community embeddedness [13, 14]. Despite substantial work experience and prior training exposure, baseline knowledge gaps were consistent with Indian evaluations reporting limited retention of BPCR concepts under routine training models [11, 15]. These findings reinforce the need for simplified, skill-oriented BPCR tools adapted to ASHAs’ sociodemographic context.

Structured BPCR training resulted in statistically significant improvements across all knowledge and self-reported practice domains (p < 0.001), accompanied by large to very large standardized effects (Cohen’s dz ≥ 1.31). The most pronounced gains were observed in information provision to pregnant women, antenatal care knowledge, and birth preparedness services. The exceptionally large effect sizes observed (some exceeding dz = 3.0) likely reflect the cumulative impact of repeated training, practical reinforcement during service delivery, and the use of a simple, pictorial, context-specific Birth Planner that enhanced knowledge retention and routine application. Ceiling effects may also have contributed, particularly in domains where post-test scores approached maximum attainable values. These findings align with earlier Community Health Worker (CHW) interventions in Nepal and Ethiopia, which reported improved identification of maternal danger signs and better birth preparedness after repeated, structured training. Overall, better performance was observed in CHW and TBA interventions that combined incentives, regular supervision, and continuous training. Evidence also suggests that such community-based intervention packages can reduce maternal morbidity and improve neonatal outcomes [16–19].

Feasibility was high, with all ASHAs expressing willingness to use the Birth Planner frequently and none reporting complexity or a need for prior learning. Most participants found it easy to use, well integrated, and suitable for routine service delivery. These results are consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that the feasibility of job aids is closely linked to simplicity and contextual relevance: pictorial, low-literacy tools have been shown to enhance usability among community health workers in resource-constrained settings [20]. The findings suggest that the Birth Planner can be adopted without substantially increasing workload or disrupting routine ASHA activities.

Acceptability was equally high: the majority of ASHA workers reported liking the Birth Planner, learning to use it rapidly, and feeling confident applying it independently. Few perceived inconsistencies or required assistance, and none found it cumbersome. These results mirror findings from community health worker research demonstrating that acceptance of training tools is closely associated with perceived usefulness and ease of integration into routine practice [21, 22]. A study of community health worker decision-support tools in India found that perceived relevance and ease of use were key predictors of sustained utilization beyond initial training [22].

In light of these findings, structured and repeated BPCR training supported by a simple birth preparedness planner should be considered for integration into routine ASHA capacity-building activities under the National Health Mission. Ongoing supportive supervision and periodic refresher sessions are essential to sustain improvements in knowledge and self-reported service delivery practices [23, 24]. Formal incorporation of the Birth Planner into ASHA job aid packages and review mechanisms—alongside potential digital adaptation within existing mHealth platforms—may enhance scalability and monitoring across diverse health system contexts.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the quasi-experimental pre–post design without a control group limits causal inference. Observed improvements in knowledge and self-reported practices may partly reflect testing effects, Hawthorne effects, or influences external to the intervention. Second, self-reported practice outcomes may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly in a post-training context; objective assessment of ASHA field practices was not undertaken. Third, long-term retention and sustainability of behavioural change were not assessed, and maternal or neonatal health outcomes were not measured, limiting conclusions on clinical impact. Fourth, as the study was conducted in selected facilities within a single rural district of North Karnataka, findings should be interpreted within this geographic and contextual scope; generalizability to other districts, states, or health system settings requires further investigation. Nonetheless, key strengths include the implementation of a structured, repeated training intervention embedded within routine service delivery, the use of complete enumeration sampling, and the reporting of standardized effect size estimates to quantify the magnitude of change.

Conclusion

The observed improvements in BPCR-related knowledge and self-reported service delivery practices, together with large effect sizes and high feasibility and acceptability ratings, indicate that the Birth Planner-based training intervention was well received and operationally practical within the rural health system context of North Karnataka. Although the quasi-experimental design precludes definitive causal conclusions, the magnitude and consistency of the changes suggest that structured, context-sensitive ASHA training combined with a simple pictorial job aid may enhance preparedness to support birth planning and complication readiness under routine programme conditions within the National Health Mission. Future multi-site controlled studies are recommended to strengthen causal inference, assess long-term retention and sustainability, evaluate the impact on maternal and neonatal health outcomes, and determine the generalizability of these findings to other geographic and health system contexts.
