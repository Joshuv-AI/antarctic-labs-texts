Characterization of a transmembrane-activating STING agonist using genetically humanized mice
Authors: Nobuyo Mizuno, Jinu Abraham, Kevin Jimenez-Perez, Ian Rose, Laura Springgay, Dylan Boehm, Takeshi Ando, Daniel Streblow, Janine Ward, Shannon Miller, Uddav Pandey, Ahmad Junaid, David Joyner, Roshell Muir, Elias K. Haddad, David Burkhart, Omer Rasheed, Victor R. DeFilippis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYStimulator of interferon genes (STING) is a pattern recognition receptor that activates type I interferon and proinflammatory responses following cytosolic DNA exposure. Its pharmacologic stimulation enhances vaccine potency and generates anti-tumor responses, but clinical trials evaluating STING agonists have not supported human use. STING activation can occur through the engagement of cytosolic or transmembrane protein domains, processes to which distinct phenotypes are attributed. However, transmembrane agonists are human selective, and conventional in vivo testing is not feasible. We synthesized human-selective STING agonists and describe genetically humanized STING mice as an in vivo model useful for examining these agonists. Experiments suggest that the lead molecule functions through binding to the STING transmembrane region, and its comparison with conventional agonists reveals differences in molecular and immune effects. This work both represents a thorough in vivo immune characterization of the effects of transmembrane STING agonism and demonstrates the efficacy of a potential vaccine adjuvant and oncological therapeutic.

INTRODUCTION

The innate immune response is defined by rapid intracellular reactions to microbe- or danger-associated molecular patterns (i.e., M/DAMPs). These factors are detected by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that initiate signal cascades culminating in transcriptomic changes, inflammatory cytokine maturation, and increased antigen uptake and presentation.1,2 In this way, innate immunity both generates tissue states resistant to microbial growth and potentiates adaptive immune responses. Stimulator of interferon genes (STING) is both a PRR reactive to cyclic purine dinucleotides (CDNs) and a signal adaptor for cytosolic DNA-sensing PRRs.3–5 The PRR cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) is a nucleotidyl transferase that produces 2′-5′ and 3′-5′ phosphodiester-linked GMP-AMP (cGAMP)3,6 in response to cytosolic DNA originating from infection7,8 as well as transformation-associated processes such as genotoxicity,9 DNA damage responses,10 mitochondrial stress,11 or tumor cell phagocytosis.12–14 Like other PRRs, STING stimulates synthesis of type I interferons (IFN-I), proinflammatory cytokines, co-stimulatory molecules, and antimicrobial effectors.15,16 However, STING is unusual for reasons related to its diverse molecular functions, stimulus-driven induction, and phenotypic effects.17,18 First, STING elicits transcriptomes and cytokine profiles (largely through TBK1-mediated activation of transcription factors IRF3 and NF-κB) that are cell type specific yet functionally undercharacterized in vivo.19 Next, STING can activate numerous TBK1-independent processes such as autophagy,19–21 lysosomal biogenesis,22,23 translational shutoff,24 inflammasome activation,25 and programmed cell death.26 Collectively, these integrate to shape antigen-directed adaptive immunity, and the magnitude, proportions, and contexts by which these processes occur vary by stimulus and cell type in ways that require deeper understanding.

The cGAS-STING pathway is crucial to protective innate and adaptive immune processes against infections and cancers (reviewed in Motwani et al.17). For instance, mice lacking cGAS, STING, or IRF3 are highly susceptible to viral infection and are unable to clear multiple tumor types.12,27–31 Tumor cell-derived DNA induces IFN-I responses that stimulate dendritic cell (DC) antigen presentation and subsequent tumor cytotoxic CD8+ T cells.12 Moreover, STING activation triggers expression of CXCR3 ligands that recruit CD8+ cells to the tumor microenvironment (TME).32 These findings facilitated demonstration that activation of STING using CDNs or small molecules is capable of remarkable tumor clearance and even abscopal responses.33,34 Additionally, STING-inducing adjuvants enhance the potency of antimicrobial vaccines (reviewed in Shen et al., Tian et al., and Rezabakhsh et al.35–37), including the generation of both humoral and cell-mediated protection against viral,38–40 bacterial,41,42 and parasitic43,44 infection. Unfortunately, successes observed in mice have not been replicated in clinical trials,45 and the diversity of STING agonist types that have failed as such suggests that phenomena other than pharmacokinetics may be relevant. For instance, binding affinity for STING orthologs varies greatly between ligands, and this, along with diversity in STING expression between cell types, further influences the immune processes that agonists stimulate.46,47 Additionally, how TBK1-independent functions vary between agonists and orthologs is largely unexplored, especially in relation to the adaptive responses to which they are linked.48 This is particularly evident following engagement of the canonical ligand-binding domain and the transmembrane domain (LBD and TMD), since these agonism modes induce divergent molecular processes.22,23,46,49 Thus, mechanisms connecting the contextual diversity of agonist-induced phenotypes and ensuing adaptive immune responses are lacking, yet this has crucial implications for clinical targeting of STING. Surprisingly, immune-associated cellular responses elicited by activated STING can vary by the chemical structure of the activating ligand.46,48,50,51 As such, the incentive is high to uncover novel STING agonists and to characterize the spectrum of effects they elicit in the context of clinically useful models.

Our group previously identified a molecule that activates human, but not mouse, STING.52 We now describe characterization of more potent analogs of this molecule obtained through molecular modeling and structure-activity relationship (SAR) approaches.53 To explore the translational applications of these, we constructed C57BL/6 mice in which the endogenous STING coding region was replaced with the human wild-type (WT) allele (huSTING mice). These are capable of reacting ex vivo and in vivo to human-selective but not mouse-selective STING inducers and display enhanced innate and adaptive immune responses when administered antigens are adjuvanted with human STING agonists. We now show that a derivative termed INI3069 can enhance humoral responses to co-delivered antigens and impair tumor growth in these mice, and it thus represents a human ortholog-selective agonist with clinically useful properties.

RESULTS

Synthesis of G10-derived human-selective STING agonists

G10 is a small molecule that activates human, but not mouse, STING.52 The potency of this molecule is low, and as such, we used in silico modeling and SAR-based medicinal chemistry to synthesize more effective analogs (Figure S1).53 This led to the identification of derivatives designated INI3067, INI3069, INI3070, and INI3071 (Figure 1A) that displayed improved peak innate induction and potency as indicated by their ability to stimulate IRF3/IFN-dependent reporter gene expression in telomerized human fibroblast (THF) cells that express STING but lack the IRF3-terminal adaptor proteins MAVS and TRIF (Figure 1B). Cytotoxicity was also examined, and concentration-dependent decreases in cell viability were found to associate with the presence of STING (Figure 1C). We next examined induction by the compounds of endogenous mRNAs responsive to IRF3 and IFN-I using semi-quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR). As shown in Figure 1D, the synthesis of CXCL10, Viperin, and IFIT1 mRNAs was stimulated only in cells that express STING, whereas stimuli such as Sendai virus (SeV)54 and AV-C,55 inducing MAVS- and TRIF-dependent responses, respectively, are able to activate these genes in STING−/− cells. As an additional control, we included the diamidobenzimidazole STING agonist diABZI,56 which also displayed activity only in STING-expressing cells. We next used immunoblotting to verify that the canonical STING signaling cascade is activated as expected. As shown in Figure 1E, each compound led to phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 but not in cells from which STING was deleted. It is worth noting that INI3069 elicited phosphorylation of TBK1 that was enhanced in STING+/+ and measurable in STING−/− cells. This result suggests that the compound may trigger residual activation of this protein via an unknown STING-independent mechanism and in a manner that does not lead to IRF3 phosphorylation in the absence of STING.

While these experiments demonstrate that STING is essential for these activation processes, the sufficiency of the protein cannot be determined from them. Moreover, multiple alleles of human STING are described that may display differential reactivity to these ligands (reviewed in Patel and Jin57). To examine the sufficiency of STING in the response to the analogs and to compare analog efficacy across alleles, we stably transduced HEK293T cells (naturally deficient in cGAS and STING58) with lentivectors that express one of three common human STING variants (WT [RGRR], HAQ, and R232H57). We next exposed these cells to each analog at a high concentration to enhance the likelihood that nonspecific (STING-independent) responses would be detected in the event that any are elicited. As shown in Figure 1F, no compounds induced IRF3 phosphorylation in the absence of STING. However, all analogs stimulated IRF3 phosphorylation in the presence of each allele. We next transiently transfected these cells with a luciferase reporter vector responsive to activation of the IRF3/IFN-I-dependent IFIT1 promoter to allow comparative measurement of allele-specific analog potencies. For this, we calculated relative luminescence units (RLUs) for each analog and concentration in relation to signal from dose-matched DMSO control cells. As shown in Figure 1G, the compounds exhibited no activity in the absence of STING but displayed dose-dependent activation in the presence of each allele. For each analog and allele, we also determined the maximum RLU induction (Figure 1H) as well as the effective concentration leading to a 50% maximal response (EC50; Figure 1I). While each analog was able to activate responses across alleles, it is interesting to note that the allele R232H displayed the lowest activity to all analogs, a result that confirms previous work showing low agonist responsiveness of this allele.59 These observations suggest that STING is necessary and sufficient for G10 analog-mediated IRF3/IFN-I induction in vitro and that they are able to activate the three most common alleles of the protein.

G10 analogs induce STING- and IRF3-dependent but ATG5-independent antiviral activity

A hallmark of STING activation involves establishment of cellular states that impair viral replication through IFN-I-dependent and autophagic processes.7,21,60–64 We therefore examined whether the analogs can elicit antiviral activity in vitro and aimed to identify cellular signaling proteins required for this. We pretreated parental THF cells with a dosage range of the four molecules. These were then infected with the (+) RNA chikungunya virus (CHIKV) engineered to express a nanoluciferase (nLuc) reporter open reading frame (ORF).65 Virus-encoded luminescence levels were measured and compared across treatments. As shown in Figure 2A, each compound was capable of impairing viral growth with inhibitory concentrations leading to 50% viral growth inhibition (IC50) of 4.895 (INI3067), 3.022 (INI3069), 5.404 (INI3070), and 1.196 μM (INI3071). These relative potencies resemble what was observed for allele-specific activity of each agonist (Figure 1I). Since all four analogs potently elicit STING-associated processes, we decided to focus on INI3069 due to the simplicity of its synthesis, high innate induction capacity, and low EC50. We thus similarly examined the dependence of INI3069 antiviral activity in THF cells lacking MAVS/TRIF, STING, IRF3 (to discern the role of TBK1/IFN-I pathways), or ATG5 (to discern the role of autophagy). Control stimuli included DMSO (negative) and IFN-β (positive). As shown in Figure 2B, IFN-β was able to suppress nLuc signal in all cells as expected since the IFN-α receptor (IFNAR) and JAK/STAT pathways are operational. INI3069 suppressed CHIKV in THF-MAVS/TRIF−/− (IC50 = 0.6758 μM) and THF-ATG5−/− cells (Figure 2C; IC50 = 0.5171 μM) but failed to do so in the others, indicating that STING and IRF3 are required for this response.

To determine the breadth of virus types sensitive to the STING-dependent cellular state induced by INI3069, we next examined its effects on the growth of herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), a DNA-based virus that is fundamentally dissimilar from CHIKV. For this we exposed THF, THF-STING−/−, and THF-ATG5−/− cells overnight to DMSO, diABZI, or INI3069 and then infected them with HSV-1. At 24 h, we harvested media and determined viral titers using a serial dilution plaque assay. As shown in Figure 2D, treatment with diABZI and INI3069 diminished HSV-1 production in cells that express STING, including ATG5−/− cells, by multiple logs, but this effect was lost in the absence of STING. From these results, we conclude that INI3069 stimulates cellular states refractory to replication of highly divergent virus types in a manner that requires STING and IRF3 but not ATG5 and is thus likely IFN-I dependent and autophagy independent.

Activity of INI3069 on primary human cells

We next examined whether INI3069 can trigger innate reactivity in primary human cells. For this, we obtained peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from six healthy donors homozygous for the WT allele and first asked whether INI3069 could induce maturation of DCs from these cells. For this, we exposed the PBMCs ex vivo to INI3069 at two concentrations or dose-matched DMSO. Flow cytometry was then used to quantify surface expression of maturation markers CD86 and CD83. Significant increases were detectable for both markers at both concentrations (Figure 3A). We next used a multiplex immunoassay to quantify the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines/chemokines following similar treatment. As shown in Figure 3B, significant induction of eighteen cytokines/chemokines was detected following treatment with either one or both concentrations examined. Based on this, we conclude that INI3069 activates innate processes in primary human cells and thus may be suitable for clinical applications.

INI3069 does not bind to the STING cytosolic domain and requires an intact TMD oligomerization domain for activation

STING includes a four-pass TMD that anchors it to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi as well as a cytosolic region that contains a CDN LBD and a C-terminal tail (CTT) required for recruitment of TBK1 and IRF3.66 While ligands that engage the LBD67 such as diABZI and cGAMP are most common, human-selective agonists such as C53 and NVS-STG2 have been described that activate via engagement of the TMD.68,69 Importantly, it has been recently shown that the agonist-binding region directs the cellular processes stimulated by activated STING.22,23,46,70 To explore the domains required for engagement of INI3069, we first purified the STING C-terminal cytosolic domain (CTD) and used a fluorescence-based thermal shift assay.71 As shown in Figure 4A, combining STING-CTD with cGAMP led to a substantial change in thermal stability as indicated by the shift in fluorescence emission with increasing temperature. However, INI3069 did not affect thermal stability, as emission resembled that observed with STING alone. To determine whether INI3069-mediated STING activation requires amino acids in the TMD oligomerization domain, we constructed plasmids expressing versions of the protein that contain previously described point mutations in TMDs 1 (L30A) and 3 (Y104A) known to be relevant for both oligomerization and C53 activity.67,68 These, along with a control vector expressing WT STING, were then transfected into HEK293T cells and treated with DMSO, diABZI, C53, or INI3069. Whole-cell lysates were then harvested, and immunoblot analysis was used to determine the phosphorylation status of IRF3 as an indicator of active STING signaling. As shown in Figure 4B, all stimuli triggered phosphorylation of IRF3 in the presence of WT STING. However, expression of TMD mutants L30A or Y104A abrogated the ability of C53 and INI3069, but not diABZI, to induce this response. Based on these observations, we conclude that INI3069 activation of STING requires an intact TMD oligomerization interface but does not engage the cytosolic region. Furthermore, these observations are consistent with results presented in a companion publication that describes the combining of computational profiling with synthetic SAR optimization to guide the design and synthesis of the analogs described here.53 Specifically, comparative in silico docking against both the LBD and TMD regions was performed, and SAR patterns revealed steric constraints consistent with a defined TMD pocket to which INI3069 binds. These outcomes can be used in future SAR modeling studies to develop improved analogs.

INI3069 inhibits trafficking of STING to the Golgi

Conventional LBD-mediated activation of STING induces a 180° rotation of the CTD relative to the TMD and oligomerization on the membrane.67 It then exits the ER through coat protein II (COPII)-mediated vesicular transport, translocates through the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), and accumulates in the trans-Golgi network.72 We first used indirect immunofluorescence microscopy to ask whether INI3069 leads to normal Golgi accumulation of activated STING. For this, we treated THF-MAVS/TRIF−/− cells with DMSO, diABZI, C53, or INI3069 and stained for STING as well as the Golgi marker GM130. As shown in Figure 4C, treatment with diABZI led to distinct colocalization of STING with GM130. However, intracellular STING distribution remained diffuse following treatment with C53 or INI3069. Furthermore, diABZI-mediated Golgi localization of STING could also be impaired by pretreating cells with either C53 or INI3069, suggesting that these compounds actively restrict this process (Figure 4C). Once activated, the STING CTT recruits TBK1, which autophosphorylates and phosphorylates STING at residue S366 (S365 in mice).73 STING subsequently recruits IRF3, which is also phosphorylated by TBK1.74 Previous work shows that STING-mediated TBK1/IRF3 phosphorylation in response to diABZI, but not C53, is inhibited when ER-Golgi trafficking is blocked by brefeldin A (BFA).22,70 We therefore asked whether Golgi transport also affects INI3069-mediated IRF3 activation. For this, we left THF-MAVS/TRIF−/− cells untreated or exposed them to BFA and then treated them with DMSO, diABZI, C53, or INI3069 and examined IRF3 phosphorylation status using immunoblot. As shown in Figure 4D, while diABZI was unable to activate IRF3 during BFA-mediated blockade of ER-Golgi transport, both C53 and INI3069 could induce IRF3 phosphorylation during this. Based on these results, we conclude that INI3069 does not lead to, and indeed prevents, Golgi trafficking of STING yet is able to stimulate TBK1-mediated IRF3 activation without translocation.

INI3069 blocks STING-mediated TFEB dephosphorylation and autophagy

STING oligomerization following LBD-mediated activation can form a proton channel independently of TBK1 that raises Golgi pH and diminishes cytosolic pH.46 This, in turn, leads to GABARAP lipidation and inhibition of mTORC1, a kinase that maintains steady-state phosphorylation of the transcription factor TFEB.22,23 Dephosphorylated TFEB translocates to the nucleus where it upregulates genes necessary for lysosomal biogenesis, a process that enhances direct antigen presentation.75 C53 binding to the TMD is known to block proton leakage and in this way fails to activate TFEB.22,23,49,70 Based on its ability to inhibit STING translocation to the Golgi, we hypothesized that INI3069 also fails to activate TFEB dephosphorylation. To test this, we treated THF-MAVS/TRIF−/− cells with DMSO, diABZI, C53, or INI3069 and performed immunoblotting to measure TFEB phosphorylation status. As shown in Figure 4D, TFEB in THF-MAVS/TRIF−/− cells is unphosphorylated following treatment with diABZI as expected. However, TFEB remained phosphorylated following treatment with C53 or INI3069 despite their ability to induce IRF3 phosphorylation, suggesting that INI3069 and C53 fail to stimulate the TBK1-independent effects observed for diABZI. To determine whether INI3069 could actively inhibit the ability of LBD-induced TFEB dephosphorylation, we next pretreated cells with C53 or INI3069 and then treated them with diABZI. These treatments prevented diABZI-mediated TFEB dephosphorylation, suggesting an active block to mTORC inhibition.

STING-mediated proton leakage is also linked to induction of noncanonical autophagy through sensing of vesicle deacidification by vacuolar ATPase (V-ATPase), which recruits ATG16L1 and catalyzes LC3 lipidation.21,76 To address whether this process is also blocked by INI3069, we first used immunoblotting on cell lysate samples obtained from treatment of THF-MAVS/TRIF−/− cells described earlier to measure LC3 lipidation (LC3-II) in response to STING agonists. As shown in Figure 4E, LC3 lipidation was highest following singular treatment with diABZI and similarly diminished after other treatments. To obtain orthogonal validation of this, we also constructed HEK293T cell clones that stably express a protein chimera comprising mCherry, GFP, and LC3 to quantify the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes (autophagic flux).77 Sensitivity of GFP fluorescence to lysosomal degradation allows flow cytometry to be used to determine the relative signal of mCherry and GFP, the ratio of which increases with autophagic flux. These cells were transfected with a plasmid expressing an ORF that encodes human STING and blue fluorescent protein (BFP) separated by a P2A cleavage site as described78 or left untransfected and exposed to DMSO, diABZI, C53, or INI3069. We next used flow cytometry to quantify changes in the GFP and mCherry MFI in BFP+ cells. This allowed discernment of a GFP:mCherry ratio indicative of high autophagic flux as shown in Figures 4F and S2. These results indicate that no stimuli led to high autophagy responses in the absence of STING. However, in the presence of STING, the fraction of cells displaying a high autophagic response increased significantly following exposure to diABZI but not C53 or INI3069 (Figure 4F). Moreover, pretreatment of cells with INI3069 followed by treatment with diABZI also failed to significantly induce high autophagy levels. Interestingly, C53 pretreatment followed by diABZI led to slightly higher autophagy relative to untreated cells but not relative to singular diABZI treatment. These results indicate that while INI3069 activates STING-mediated IRF3 phosphorylation, it fails to induce TFEB dephosphorylation or autophagy and, indeed, blocks these processes since subsequent diABZI treatment is unable to stimulate them. This finding is also consistent with the absence of impact of ATG5 on INI3069-mediated antiviral responses and its engagement of the STING TMD and likely blockade of proton leakage.

Replacement of the endogenous coding region for STING with the human ortholog yields viable animals and functional in vivo expression of the ectopic ORF

G10 analogs fail to activate murine STING, and, as such, examining their in vivo efficacy in conventional models is not feasible. Since STING-deficient mice are healthy79 and previous work has demonstrated the capacity of ectopic human STING to activate innate signaling in mouse cells,80,81 we hypothesized that replacement of the mouse coding region with an ORF that encodes the human ortholog would lead to the generation of viable animals responsive at molecular and immunologic levels to human-selective STING inducers. To accomplish this, embryonic cell homologous recombination was performed by electroporation of a vector containing the sequence for the coding portion of human Tmem173 as illustrated in Figure S3A. The presence of the transgenic insert was confirmed by PCR using primers that bind internally to the engineered locus as well as externally in the targeted chromosomal region isolated from animal tissues (Figures S3A and S3B). Animals were bred to homozygosity, and absolute levels of human STING mRNA transcripts were compared across tissue types to levels of endogenous STING mRNA from parental C57BL/6 mice using qPCR. As shown in Figure S3C, significant differences in the respective mRNA levels were only detected between parental and huSTING mice in heart muscle (higher in huSTING mice) and bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs; lower in huSTING mice). These data suggest that across cellular environments, homeostatic regulation of transcription and mRNA turnover of the huSTING transgene largely align with that observed for the endogenous WT allele.

Comparative molecular function of transgenic huSTING in response to activating ligands

Regulation of STING is complex, involving multifaceted feedback mechanisms.82 Moreover, whether the huSTING protein signals in a biologically appropriate manner in the context of murine cells, especially following ligand engagement, is crucial to exploring in vivo systemic STING-mediated phenotypes. To characterize this, we began by examining activation of transgenic STING-mediated molecular signaling. Murine embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were prepared from C57BL/6, huSTING, and STING-deficient (Goldenticket; STINGGt/Gt) mice.79 These were then exposed to SeV as a STING-independent IRF3-activating control and agonists of STING that are either species selective or cross-activating and harvested for immunoblotting. As shown in Figure 5A, SeV induced phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 in all cells. STING, TBK1, and IRF3 from all but STINGGt/Gt cell types were phosphorylated as expected following treatment with diABZI. Small molecules that induce species-selective activation of STING proteins include DMXAA (mouse)83 and INI3069. As shown in Figure 5A, the compounds only activated phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 in a manner consistent with their known species specificity, and none triggered these responses in STINGGt/Gt MEFs. However, in line with observations made in STING−/− human cells (Figure 1D), INI3069 was also able to stimulate residual phosphorylation of TBK1 in C57Bl/6 and STINGGt/Gt MEFs, suggesting off-target or noncanonical activation of this response. Interestingly, INI3069 also led to detectable phosphorylation of IRF3 in C57BL/6 but not STINGGt/Gt MEFs, and thus a noncanonical STING-dependent mechanism may be involved.

We next examined whether these agonists can induce convergent transcriptional outcomes in primary immune cells. For this, we isolated splenocytes from C57BL/6, huSTING, and STINGGt/Gt mice. Secretion of the chemokine CXCL10 was first examined using a Luminex assay. As shown in Figure 5B, diABZI treatment led to secretion of CXCL10 in WT and huSTING but not STINGGt/Gt splenocytes, whereas DMXAA only induced CXCL10 secretion in splenocytes from WT mice. While G10 induced detectable CXCL10 secretion in huSTING (but not WT or STINGGt/Gt) splenocytes, INI3069 induced high secretion in huSTING and lower, but measurable, secretion in WT and STINGGt/Gt splenocytes consistent with observed residual induction of STING-independent TBK1 activity. qPCR was next used to more precisely validate the induction of IRF3/IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs; CXCL10, Viperin, and IFIT1) following ex vivo stimulation with the STING inducers. As shown in Figure 5C, diABZI led to ISG induction in WT and huSTING but not STINGGt/Gt splenocytes. As expected, DMXAA only induced ISG expression in splenocytes from WT mice. Interestingly, while G10 and INI3069 stimulated substantial ISG expression in splenocytes from huSTING mice, some induction was also detected by these compounds in C57BL/6 and STINGGt/Gt splenocytes. This may indicate a STING-independent mechanism of IRF3 activation and correlates with observed TBK1 activation by INI3069 in the absence of human STING (Figure 1E). Additionally, while G10 and INI3069 induced comparable induction levels of CXCL10 and IFIT1 in huSTING mice, INI3069 stimulated higher levels of Viperin mRNA than did G10. This may be linked to differential factors responsible for Viperin transcription that are in some way inhibited by G10. Collectively, however, these data indicate that molecular function of exogenously stimulated transgenic human STING in the context of murine cells reacts to ligand engagement in a manner that resembles that seen in cells from WT mice.

Transcriptomes of DCs from huSTING and C57BL/6 mice in response to innate stimuli

Data presented in Figure S4 suggest that agonist-mediated STING signaling by transgenic huSTING resembles that seen in the presence of endogenous murine STING. However, myriad mRNAs are known to be regulated in response to STING activation, perhaps even in the absence of ligand engagement.84 Moreover, since immunologically critical BMDCs appear to synthesize huSTING mRNA at lower levels than their WT counterparts (Figure S3C), we examined the implications of both the ortholog dissimilarity and differential expression levels in C57BL/6 and huSTING mice on agonist-induced activity in BMDCs treated ex vivo. For this, BMDCs from WT and huSTING were cultured and treated with cGAMP and diABZI as well LPS as a STING-independent IRF3-activating control stimulus. Total RNA was then harvested at 6 h post treatment, an early time point chosen to diminish the impact of autocrine/paracrine effects of secreted cytokines.85 Hybridization array was then used to quantify levels of >20,000 individual transcripts. With the exception of Tmem173 (STING; not shown), which expectedly does not cross-hybridize to the mouse array probe, no transcripts were identified as significantly differentially expressed in control DMSO-treated BMDCs between huSTING and WT cells (Table S1). Furthermore, principal-component analysis (PCA) of absolute mRNA levels revealed that transcriptomes segregate predominantly by stimulus rather than by mouse strain (Figure S4A). Fold changes of transcripts induced by each stimulus were also highly correlated between WT and huSTING cells as shown in Figure S4B. Importantly, mock cells displayed similarly grouped transcriptomes, suggesting that the human ortholog does not lead to homeostatic gene expression that is substantially different from that of the endogenous protein. Likewise, predicted regulation of biologically characterized pathways (Table S2) was also highly similar between the strains for the different STING agonists as illustrated in Figure S4C. These results indicate that cells from huSTING and WT mice do not react in a transcriptionally dissimilar manner to exogenous activators of STING or STING-independent IRF-activating stimuli. In addition, they also illustrate that STING-dependent transcription can vary in relation to the activating ligand (Figure S4A).

STING agonist-induced activity on huSTING BMDCs

Antigen-directed adaptive immune responses require activation and maturation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as DCs in response to innate stimuli. These are particularly relevant to humoral and cell-mediated responses for which STING activation has been shown to be a potentiator.86 We therefore examined whether immature BMDCs from huSTING mice respond as such through the expression of conventional markers of APC maturation following exposure to diABZI and INI3069. Immature BMDCs from WT and huSTING mice were harvested and treated as described earlier. Flow cytometry was then used to measure surface expression of maturation markers CD40, CD80, and CD86. As shown in Figures 5D and S5, diABZI was able to significantly increase expression of all three markers in BMDCs from both mouse strains. Alternatively, INI3069 only induced maturation in cells from huSTING mice, consistent with results described earlier. These data indicate species-predicted, STING-dependent innate immune responsiveness at molecular and transcriptional levels in cells from huSTING mice and suggest that the observed decrease in the expression of STING mRNA in BMDCs relative to WT mice (Figure S3C) has no substantial impact.

Cells from huSTING mice react expectedly to activating ligands by generating canonical TBK1/IRF3-dependent and innate cell maturation responses. However, the TBK1-independent proton channel function of STING also elicits intracellular processes that likely affect immune activity, although this has not been examined in vivo. Whether the protein behaves similarly in the context of mouse innate cells is important for both establishing the suitability of these animals as a model and validating the effects of INI3069 observed in human cells. We therefore examined STING agonist-mediated dephosphorylation of TFEB in BMDCs. As shown in Figure 5E, dephosphorylation of TFEB following treatment of BMDCs harvested from huSTING mice with cGAMP occurs as expected. However, while C53 and INI3069 are active in these cells as indicated by their ability to induce IRF3 phosphorylation, they fail to stimulate dephosphorylation of TFEB as was observed in human cells (Figure 5E). These results suggest that INI3069 resembles C53 in that it activates STING-dependent IRF3 phosphorylation but not TFEB. Moreover, huSTING behaves similarly when expressed as a transgene in mouse cells to endogenous STING in human cells when activated by LBD- versus TMD-binding agonists and thus strengthening this model.

INI3069-associated enhancement of antigen-reactive humoral responses in huSTING mice

Agonists of STING can augment the immunogenicity of co-administered protein antigens, which manifests as an increase in titers of antigen-reactive serum antibodies (reviewed in Tian et al. and Herck et al.36,87). This has been shown to elicit anti-tumor immune effects when STING agonists are admixed with tumor-expressing antigens including ectopic ovalbumin (OVA).88–90 We therefore examined whether subcutaneous (s.c.) injection of OVA paired with INI3069, CDA, diABZI, or alum as control adjuvants using a prime-boost schedule could elicit OVA-specific IgG in huSTING and WT mice. As shown in Figures 6A and 6B, diABZI, ML-RR-S2 (a CDA derivative modified for in vivo use91), and alum expectedly led to significant increases in titers of OVA-reactive total IgG relative to the antigen adjuvanted with formulation vehicle alone in both huSTING and WT mice. INI3069 led to significantly enhanced IgG titers in huSTING but not WT mice, a finding consistent with the compound’s capacity for ortholog-specific innate induction and proper functioning of the protein. T helper (TH) polarization can also be affected by adjuvant class with STING agonists having demonstrated a range of TH1, TH2, and TH17 responses largely in relation to the route of administration, target species and strain used, and chemical structure (reviewed in Tian et al.36). As an indicator of TH polarization induced by INI3069 relative to control agonists, we quantified OVA-reactive IgG1 and IgG2c isotypes. As shown in Figure 6C, all adjuvants led to significant and similar induction of IgG1 isotypes. However, alum and INI3069 elicited substantially lower IgG2c isotype levels than did CDA or diABZI. These results suggest a more balanced TH1:TH2 polarization for CDA and diABZI and higher TH2 polarization for alum and INI3069, underscoring key immunological differences between STING agonist classes. Whether this is functionally linked to the different TBK1-independent phenotypes induced by these agonist classes will require additional tools and studies to elucidate. Recently published work by our group additionally demonstrates INI3069-associated enhancement of humoral responses to influenza antigens, confirming efficacy across antigen platforms.53

Class-switch recombination and affinity maturation of antibodies take place in the lymph node germinal centers and are facilitated by T follicular helper (TFH) cells in antigen- and adjuvant-specific manners. We next examined the capacity of STING agonists to induce antigen-associated TFH development in huSTING mice using an activation-induced marker (AIM) assay.92,93 Animals vaccinated with OVA plus formulation vehicle, diABZI, or INI3069 as described earlier were euthanized, and splenocyte single-cell suspensions were cultured ex vivo and exposed to a peptide pool that spans the OVA protein. Flow cytometry was then used to quantify expression of OX40, CD25, Bcl6, and PD-1 on CD3/4+ cells. As shown in Figures 6D and S6, inclusion of diABZI and INI3069 led to a significant increase in the numbers of OVA-reactive TFH cells relative to vehicle alone. Based on these results, we conclude that the humoral response in huSTING mice as indicated by antibody formation and antigen-specific TFH activation can be induced by human-selective, TMD-engaging, and LBD-engaging STING agonists.

Next, we examined whether INI3069 can enhance the generation of antigen-reactive T cells. For this, OVA was coadministered with vehicle alone, diABZI, or formulated INI3069 as described earlier. Splenocytes from these mice were either directly stimulated with an OVA-spanning peptide pool or mixed with DC2.4 cells stably transduced with full-length OVA to skew toward direct antigen presentation. As shown in Figure 6E, pairing diABZI with OVA led to antigen-specific expression of IFN-γ in splenocytes from these mice. However, the use of INI3069 did not (Figure 6E). Collectively, these results suggest that INI3069 can enhance antigen-directed IgG1 humoral responses and TFH activation but not IgG2c or cell-mediated responses, in contrast to the LBD-engaging agonist diABZI. As such, despite the abilities of INI3069 and diABZI to similarly activate STING-mediated TBK1-dependent processes, the immune responses they elicit appear divergent.

Anti-tumor activity of INI3069 in huSTING mice

Peritumoral injection of STING agonists can lead to immune-mediated clearance of transplanted tumors in murine models (reviewed in Huang et al.94). Demonstrating that syngeneic tumors grow similarly in huSTING and C57BL/6 mice is essential to establish these animals as suitable for investigating STING-mediated anti-cancer therapies. We therefore subcutaneously injected B16 melanoma and MC38 colon adenocarcinoma cells into C57BL/6 and huSTING mice and monitored tumor volumes through time. As shown in Figure 7A, growth rates of B16 and MC38 tumors did not differ significantly when left untreated between C57BL/6 and huSTING mice. We additionally compared anti-tumor activity of diABZI in these models to verify that a STING agonist known to induce tumor clearance in mice is similarly effective between strains. We therefore similarly established MC38 or B16 tumors in C57BL/6 or huSTING mice, injected diABZI peritumorally three times, and monitored tumor growth. Tumor growth was suppressed similarly in both strains (Figure 7A), indicating that STING agonist-mediated anti-tumor responses are active in huSTING mice and that the animals should be appropriate for examining anti-tumor treatments.

We next asked whether direct administration of INI3069 could impair tumor growth in huSTING mice. B16 cells are largely insensitive to anti-PD-1-based immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) therapies and thus represent an immunologically “cold” tumor type.95–97 Transplanted B16 tumors were peritumorally treated three times with formulation vehicle alone or formulated INI3069, and tumor volume was monitored (Figure 7B). In this approach, INI3069 alone did not significantly diminish tumor growth. We next examined whether INI3069 could enhance tumor clearance during ICI by co-administering anti-PD1 or isotype control antibodies. As shown in Figure 7B, this led to profound impairment of tumor growth. Whether CD8+ T cells play a role in this outcome was next examined by depleting these prior to and during treatment with INI3069 and anti-PD-1. As shown in Figure 7B, depletion of CD8+ T cells alone led to more rapid growth of untreated tumors. Moreover, CD8+ T cells were essential for the synergistic INI3069 plus anti-PD1 effect as tumor growth was largely restored during this treatment scheme in their absence.

To determine whether INI3069 conferred similar effects on the growth of an unrelated tumor type, we examined MC38 cells as a model that is more sensitive to ICI. For this, we administered only formulation vehicle alone or formulated INI3069 as described earlier. As shown in Figure 7C, INI3069 alone led to a significant impairment of MC38 tumor growth. Based on these results, we conclude that huSTING mice are a suitable model for examining the anti-tumor responses of human-selective STING agonists. Moreover, INI3069 is capable of impairing growth of an ICI-insensitive tumor in a manner dependent on CD8+ T cells as well as impairing the growth of an ICI-sensitive tumor. Importantly, INI3069-mediated effects are unlikely to be conferred through a function involving innate activation of the transplanted tumor cells as these are not transcriptionally responsive to the compound as indicated by qPCR and illustrated in Figure 7D.

DISCUSSION

Transient innate induction by STING agonists can generate useful outcomes in multiple clinically important areas including antimicrobial vaccine enhancement,36 antiviral responses,63 anti-tumor responses,98 and pain suppression.99 While clinical trials involving STING agonists are both planned and ongoing, thus far no STING-targeting drugs have received FDA approval.45 Importantly, all STING agonists for which clinical trials have been completed and unsuccessful engage the LBD as their mechanism of activation. Whether their failure is due to the TBK1-independent phenotypes they elicit requires understanding to allow predictions on the potential superiority of TMD agonists and to guide future drug development. Moreover, the evolutionary divergence of STING likely results in ortholog- and allele-specific ligand activity that manifests as dissimilar downstream effects. As such, examination of activity against the human ortholog in an in vivo model may represent a more useful approach to evaluation of novel agonists. Surprisingly, the preclinical criteria used to advance candidate compounds have involved limited molecular phenotypic evaluation of them and conventionally rely on induction of IFN-I-dependent responses as a metric. Yet it is likely that engagement of STING orthologs and alleles by specific agonists elicits aggregate response profiles that differ in ways that are not examined.48 How these processes associate qualitatively with agonist structure, ortholog, and immune activation has been minimally explored.

STING agonists known to activate the protein by binding to the TMD include only human-selective compounds.68,69 As such, investigation of their in vivo effects is not feasible in conventional models. Genetically humanized STING mice, while examined previously to assess anti-tumor responses of NVS-STG2,69 have been minimally characterized with respect to the general molecular and immunological responses they display, and thus their suitability as a preclinical model is not fully known. By examining transcriptomics, signaling pathway activation, vaccination responses, and anti-tumor responses, this work demonstrates that at molecular and immunological levels, huSTING mice respond to cross-reactive STING agonists in ways that resemble those in the parental strain. Based on this, until agonists are identified that similarly activate mouse and human STING TMD-specific responses, we believe they are a useful model for exploring the mechanisms and clinical value of human-selective agonists such as those engaging the TMD. Indeed, given the unknown differences in phenotypes induced by STING orthologs, they may also be more appropriate for characterizing any STING agonist for which human applications are being pursued.

While STING-mediated TBK1-independent phenotypes linked to proton channel formation are actively being defined at the molecular level, the implications these have on clinically relevant processes such as innate and adaptive immunity remain uncharacterized. This work begins to explore this by contrasting the effects of LBD-activating compounds such as diABZI and CDNs with TMD activators such as C53 and INI3069 using models of vaccination and anti-tumor activity. While similarities between them were identified, including the ability to simulate the production of antigen-reactive IgG1 (Figure 6B) and induce TFH responses (Figure 6D), differences were also observed including differential generation of IgG2c (Figure 6C) and T cell responses (Figure 6E). While this suggests that TMD and LBD agonists may elicit distinct TH polarization profiles, a useful understanding will require both additional validation and mechanistic explanations that link the dissimilarity in molecular phenotypes to immune outcomes. This highlights a critical knowledge gap regarding the respective contributions of TBK1-dependent and independent processes to immune polarization. Processes such as autophagy, proinflammatory gene synthesis, lysosomal biogenesis, and inflammasome activation are individually known to affect antigen presentation and immune cell recruitment and differentiation. Functional linkage of domain-specific STING induction to T helper polarization via differential stimulation of these activities would facilitate rational design of future analogs to achieve desired purposes. In addition, LBD agonists display cytotoxicity toward immune cells and especially T cells in many contexts, and this has been suggested to impact their lack of success in clinical trials.100 The extent to which this is linked to proton channel-dependent processes, activation of which is known to be cytotoxic, is an underexplored but relevant phenomenon and may lead to the discovery that use of LBD agonists or a combination of LBD and TMD agonists is a more promising approach.

Interestingly, administration using NVS-STG2 was shown to associate with anti-tumor responses and an increase in tumor antigen-reactive T cells. This contrasts with what we observed following intramuscular (i.m.) administration of INI3069 paired with OVA (Figure 6E). Whether these differences are due to dosage used, route of injection, or phenotypic dissimilarity between the agonists will require additional scrutiny. Furthermore, the anti-tumor responses elicited by INI3069 appear less potent than those induced by diABZI (Figure 7). Unknown issues regarding this observation include the extent to which this difference relates to INI3069 and diABZI specifically versus TMD and LBD agonists more broadly and the functional role of TBK1-dependent and independent processes activated in both cases. Moreover, the inability of vaccination using INI3069 as an adjuvant to induce T cell reactivity yet observed essentiality of CD8+ T cells for INI3069-mediated anti-tumor responses represents an apparent paradox that requires exploration and may also inform us about novel phenotypes induced by these agonist classes.

This work demonstrates that INI3069 does not engage the CTD of STING and that amino acid mutations in TM1 and TM3 regions abrogate the compound’s activity. Moreover, INI3069’s mode of activation prevents trafficking of STING to the Golgi as well as processes induced by proton channel formation. However, the physical basis of the proton channel blockade by INI3069 and C53 is not known. Since the amino acids that comprise the actual pore have not been explicitly mapped, it is possible that TM1 and TM3 represent formative components and INI3069 may physically obstruct proton movement by engaging here. It is also possible that INI3069 binding alters protein structure such that pore formation by these or other transmembrane domains is impaired. Understanding this phenomenon will require other methodologies such as cryogenic electron microscopy and may lead to important insight regarding proton channel function that could guide future analog development.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of this study is the lack of mechanistic links between the differential molecular phenotypes induced by TMD- and LBD-engaging agonists and the demonstrable differences in the immune responses they induce. An important future question is the extent to which these are mediated or affected by processes such as lysosomal biogenesis, inflammasome activation, or autophagy as induced by LBD- but not TMD-engaging agonists. To address this, future work could attempt to replicate the TH polarization observed for INI3069 by using diABZI in the presence of specific inhibitors of these processes. This may allow definition of roles for these as induced by LBD agonists. Another limitation of the study is the lack of structural data formally demonstrating the region of STING engagement by INI3069. While lack of binding between INI3069 and the STING CTD is clear, only mutational analysis and phenotype consistency data indicate a role for the TMD in INI3069-mediated activity. This could be accomplished using cryo-electron microscopy with the full-length STING protein as described.68 Finally, the differential avidity and protective capacity of antibodies generated following vaccination with INI3069 and diABZI were not examined. It is possible that agonist-mediated vaccine enhancement differs to an extent that correlates with clinical value as indicated by the ability to neutralize pathogen targets. To explore this, viral proteins containing neutralizing epitopes could be used as vaccine antigens in the presence of INI3069 or diABZI in huSTING mice. Antibody avidity could then be quantified using bio-layer interferometry and neutralization quantified by viral plaque reduction following serum serial dilution. Moreover, protective capacity could also be compared by monitoring the survival of vaccinated animals following viral challenge.

METHOD DETAILS

Luciferase reporter and cytotoxicity assays

THF reporter or HEK293T cells were plated at 20,000 cells per well in a white 96-well plate 24 h before stimulation. Treatments were performed in duplicate overnight in 50 μL DMEM plus 2% FCS. One-GLO lysis/luciferin reagent was added 1:1 to each well, and luminescence measured on a Bio-Tek Synergy plate reader. Reporter assays using HEK293T cells stably transduced with STING alleles involved plating 500,000 cells in 35 mm dishes and transfecting plasmids encoding IFIT1-dependent luciferase (IFIT1-Luc) using Lipofetamine 3000 according to manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h cells were trypsinized and plated at 20,000/well in 96 well plates and exposed in duplicate to indicated stimuli eluted in 50 μL DMEM plus 2% FCS for 24 h and luminescence read as above. Nonlinear regression using a four-parameter logistic to determine EC50 was performed using Graphpad Prism. Cytotoxicity was measured by treating THF cells in duplicate for 24 h with analogs at indicated dose. Measurement of ATP was then performed using directly added Cell Titer GLO reagent (Promega) and luminescence quantified on a Bio-Tek Synergy plate reader. Nonlinear regression using a four-parameter logistic to determine EC50 was performed using Graphpad Prism.

Immunoblotting

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) immunoblotting was performed as follows. After trypsinization and cell pelleting at 2,000 × g for 10 min, whole-cell lysates were harvested in RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were electrophoresed in 8% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using semidry transfer at 400 mA for 1 h. The blots were blocked at room temperature for 2 h or overnight using 10% nonfat milk in 1 x PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. The blots were exposed to primary antibody in 5% nonfat milk in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 18 h at 4°C. The blots were then washed in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 20, 15, and 5 min, followed by deionized water for 5 min. A 1 h exposure to horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and subsequent washes were performed as described for the primary antibodies. The antibody was visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence.

Construction of stable cell lines and genome editing

Stable overexpression of STING alleles and mCherry-GFP-LC3 fusion protein in HEK293T cells and OVA in DC2.4 cells or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene deletion of THF cells was performed using lentiviral transduction. DNA encoding WT, HAQ, and R232H human STING alleles, WT STING-P2A-BFP fusion (using identical codon optimization patterns), mCherry-GFP-LC3, or OVA sequences were synthesized by Twist Biosciences. These were cloned into the pLVX-EF1a-Puro vector (Takara Bio). For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of ATG5, targeting gRNA (GATGGACAGTTGCACACACT) was cloned into LentiCRISPRv2 as described.102 Lentivirus for these constructs was made by transfecting them along with pMD2.g and psPAX2 into LentiX cells using Lipofectamine 3000. At 24 h media was harvested, cleared by centrifugation, and filtered through 0.45 μm filter. This was then added to target cells for transduction in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene. Transduced cells were selected by exposing to 1 μg/mL puromycin. Single cell clones were then obtained either by serial dilution or fluorescence activated cell sorting (mCherry-GFP-LC3) and target protein expression or deletion verified using immunoblot.

Ratiometric flow cytometry

HEK293T cell clones stably transduced with mCherry-GFP-LC3 were plated at 125,000 cells per well and transiently transfected for 24 h with empty pLVX-EF1α plasmid or plasmid encoding STING-P2A-BFP (WT). Cells were exposed to 1 μM diABZI, 10 μM C53, or 10 μM INI3069 for 4 h. Flow cytometry was then performed using A5 Symphony 28 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using Flowjo v10.10.0 (Tree Star). The ratio of mCherry to GFP MFI was determined by gating on BFP+ cells. Degree of autophagic flux was assigned based on the distribution of ratios observed in BFP+ cell populations as shown in Figure 5B and as described in.77,103 Gating and autophagy induction designation based on mCherry:GFP signal for representatives of all sample treatments is presented in Figure S2. Experiments were performed in two independent clonal cell lines in duplicate.

In vitro virus infection

Stocks of Nanoluciferase expressing CHIKV (CHIKV-nLuc) was derived from an infectious clone as previously described.65 Live virus was generated from in vitro transcribed RNA that was then transfected via Lipofectamine into BHK-21 cells and media collected. Resultant virus was titered as above and propagated in C6/36 insect cells for 48 h to produce viral stocks. Virus was pelleted through a 20% sucrose cushion by ultracentrifugation (22,000 rpm [825,206 × g] for 1.5 h). Experimental infections of indicated cells were carried out in triplicate on 15,000 cells per well in a 96 well plate using a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 PFU per cell. CHIKV-nLuc was quantified by adding Nano-Glo luciferin lysis buffer (Promega) to cells at a 1:1 ratio and reading luminescence as described above. Stocks of the KOS strain of HSV-1 were grown on Vero cells. Supernatant was clarified by centrifugation and titered by serial dilution plaque assay on Vero cells. For experimental infection, indicated THF were plated at 125,000 cells per well in a 24 well dish and pretreated overnight in a 12 well dish at with 0.5% DMSO, 100 nM diABZI, or 10 μM INI3069. Infections of were carried out in duplicate using MOI of 0.1 PFU per cell. Media was harvested at 24 h post infection and titered on Vero cells.

Hybridization array transcriptomic analysis

Microarray assays were performed in the OHSU Integrated Genomics Laboratory. RNA sample quantity and purity were measured by UV absorbance with a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer. RNA integrity and size distribution were determined by running total RNA on a Nano chip instrument (Agilent Technologies). RNA was prepared for array hybridization by labeling 100 ng aliquots using the 3’IVT Express kit (Affymetrix). RNA was reverse transcribed to generate first-strand cDNA containing a T7 promoter sequence. A second-strand cDNA synthesis step was performed that converted the single-stranded cDNA into a dsDNA template for transcription. Amplified and biotin-labeled cRNA was generated during the in vitro transcription step. After a magnetic bead purification step the cRNA was fragmented. Labeled and fragmented cRNA was combined with hybridization cocktail components and hybridization controls, and 130 μL of each hybridization cocktail containing 6.5 μg of labeled target was injected into a cartridge containing the Clariom S murine array (Affymetrix) interrogating over 150,000 transcripts from >22,100 genes. Arrays were incubated for 18 h at 45°C, followed by washing and staining on a GeneChip Fluidics Station 450 (Affymetrix) and the associated hybridization wash and stain kit. Arrays were scanned using the GeneChip Scanner 3000 7G. Image processing of sample .DAT files to generate probe intensity .CEL files was performed using the Affymetrix GeneChip Command Console (AGCC) software. Each array file was then analyzed using Transcriptome Analysis Console (TAC; Version 4.0.3) to obtain array performance metrics and calculate transcript fold changes. To identify probe sets that were significantly regulated in treated versus untreated (mock) cells, we employed a traditional unpaired one-way (single-factor) ANOVA for each pair of condition groups as implemented in TAC. Probe sets were considered differentially regulated if the ANOVA P value was <0.05 and fold change >2. Pathway analysis of induced transcripts was performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Qiagen).

Construction of transgenic knock in mice

Genetically humanized STING mice were constructed by Cyagen Biomodels, LLC. Briefly, the sequence encompassing exon 3 to exon 8 of mouse Sting1 was replaced by the Human STING1 CDS-polyA-sdNeo cassette shown in Figure 6. The targeting vector was electroporated into C57BL/6N embryonic stem (ES) cells. Cells were then cultured in a medium containing neomycin. PCR genotyping was then used to assess acquisition of the desired mutation through homologous recombination. Targeted ES cell clone 1H2 was injected into C57BL/6 embryos, which were then re-implanted into CD-1 pseudo-pregnant female chimeras. The resulting progeny (F0) were then bred with wildtype animals and selected for germline transmission. Among the next generation (F1), humanized mice were verified by PCR screening of extracted genomic DNA using primers CATAGAAAAGCCTTGACTTGAGGTT (F3) and AGCCCTTGTAATGAAAACAGAGGT (R1) to detect the wild type mouse allele, primers CATAGAAAAGCCTTGACTTGAGGTT (F1) and R1 to detect insertion of the neomycin cassette, and ACACGCTCTGTTTACTATGAACCT (F2) and CCCGTTTAACAGCAGTCCCA (R2) to detect insertion of the human STING coding region. The heterozygous F1 were further intercrossed for homologous mutant F2. These mice express the human STING1 allele under the control of endogenous mouse Sting1 promoter with no mouse Sting1 expressed.

In vivo studies

C57Bl/6 were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. huSTING mice were bred in house. All animals were used experimentally at 6 – 8 weeks of age. For vaccination experiments animals were injected subcutaneously (SQ) with 5 μg endotoxin free OVA admixed with 200 μg INI3069 formulated in DMA:PEG, 10 μg diABZI, 10 μg ML-RR-S2 (CDA), 300 μg Alhydrogel, or DMA:PEG vehicle alone in 100 μL total volume and boosted identically at 2 w. For INI3069 formulation a 20 mg/mL stock in DMA was combined with 10 volume parts DMA was combined with 30 volume parts PEG, and 60 volume parts PBS. For serum antibody studies animals were euthanized at 2 w post boost and for ELISPOT studies animals were euthanized at 1 w post boost. For tumor studies huSTING mice were injected SQ in the rear flank with 5 × 105 B16F10 or MC38 cells and tumor volume (V) monitored every other day using calipers (V = 0.5 × L × W2). When tumor sizes reached 100 mm3 three peritumoral injections of vehicle alone, 30 μg diABZI, or 100 μg formulated INI3069 were performed every other day. Immune checkpoint inhibition was induced by intraperitumorally (IP) injected 200 μg anti-PD1 antibody three times every other day at the start of tumor treatment. CD8+ T cell depletion was performed by IP injection of 100 μg anti-CD8a antibody every four days starting two days before tumor inoculation. CD8+ cell depletion was validated by flow cytometry performed on peripheral blood collected by submandibular bleed.

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells or tissues, treated with DNase provided in a DNA-free RNA isolation kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and quantified using UV spectrometry. Single-stranded cDNA for use as a PCR template was made from total RNA and random hexamers to prime first-strand synthesis via reverse transcriptase. Relative fold change of mRNA expression between treated and mock-treated samples was calculated using semi-quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qPCR) with the Applied Biosystems sequence detection system according to the ΔΔCT method104 with GAPDH as a control. mRNA-specific pre-validated Prime-Time 6-carboxyfluorescein qPCR primer/probe sets obtained from IDT were used for all genes. Absolute quantitation of viral DNA copies or mRNA transcripts from mouse tissues was performed by isolating RNA and synthesizing cDNA as above. A serial dilution of plasmid encoding the virus-specific target region was included to establish a Ct value standard curve which allowed regression analysis to establish absolute mRNA copy numbers per μg total RNA.

STING thermal shift assays

Engagement of purified human STING C-terminal domain and INI3069 was performed as described.105 Briefly, an open reading frame encoding 6xHIS tagged STING amino acids 137 to 379 was cloned into pRSET-B and used to transform the E. coli strain BL21 (DE3). Liquid cultures of transformed E. coli were then induced to express the protein using 1 mM isopropyl-D-thiogalactopyranoside at 16°C for 18 h. STING protein was purified by nickel-affinity chromatography (Clontech Laboratories) and purified by gel filtration chromatography (HiPrep 16/60Sephacryl S-100 HR column; GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Eluted proteins were concentrated using Amicon centrifugal filters (10-kDa cutoff). For thermal shift assay, 1 μg of recombinant protein was combined 100 μM 2’3’-cGAMP or 50 μM INI3069 along SYPRO Orange dye at 1:1000 dilution in a 20μL reaction (in triplicate). A StepOne Plus Real-time PCR system was used to acquire fluorescence over a temperature gradient of 25 to 99°C. The melting curves were plotted as fluorescence signal using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

MEF isolation, bone marrow cell harvest, and DC maturation

Murine embryonic fibroblasts (MEF) were harvested from C57Bl/6, huSTING, and C57Bl/6Gt/Gt and cultured as described.106 Immature bone marrow derived dendritic cells (BMDC) were obtained by collecting marrow from mouse femurs using syringe flushing followed by red blood cell lysis in ACK buffer. Cells were cultured in RPMI + 10% FCS in the presence of 20 ng/mL GM-CSF and 10 ng/mL IL-4 (Peprotech). Cytokines and media were replaced on day 3. Nonadherent cells were removed and media similarly replaced on day 6. On day 8 cells were then exposed to indicated stimuli at approximately 400,000 per well for 24 h and flow cytometry performed. Agonist stimulation was performed for 24 h with 0.5% DMSO, 10 nM diABZI, or 10 μM INI3069.

BMDC flow cytometry

Cells were collected by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 minutes and washed with FACS buffer (0.5% BSA, 5 mM EDTA in PBS). Cells were blocked with 5 μg/mL FC block and dead cells were stained with Live/Dead Fixable Near IR at 4°C for 15 minutes. Staining antibodies CD11c, MHC-II, CD80, CD86, and CD40 were diluted and incubated with cells at 4°C for 30 minutes and washed. Data acquisition was performed using A5 Symphony 28 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using Flowjo v10.10.0 (Tree Star). Median MFI of activation markers were compared to DMSO control.

Activation induced marker (AIM) assay

Splenocytes from vaccinated mice were incubated with 2 μg/mL 15-mer OVA peptide pool (Miltenyi) at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 18h. Stimulation with 5 μg/mL concanamycin A was included as a positive control. After stimulation, cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Near IR and blocked with mouse Fc block CD16/CD32 (BD Biosciences) at 4°C for 15 minutes. Cells were then stained for 30 min at 4°C with the following fluorochrome-conjugated anti-mouse Abs: CD3, CD4, CD45R/B220, CD25, PD-1, and OX40. Intracellular staining was performed for 45 min at 4°C with anti-mouse Bcl6 antibody using Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization kit (BD Biosciences), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cell events were collected on A5 Symphony 28 (BD Biosciences) flow cytometer, and data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.10.0 software.

IFNγ ELISPot

Splenocytes were harvested from mice one week after vaccination boost and maintained in prewashed mouse IFNγ ELISPot plates (MabTech) at 250,000/well in RPMI + 10% FBS following red blood cell lysis. They were either stimulated with a pool of 15-mer OVA inclusive peptides at 10 μg/well or mixed with DC2.4 cells that stably overexpress full length OVA at a splenocyte:DC2.4 ratio of 1:10 for 18 h. Plates were then stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, plates were washed and incubated with anti-mouse IFNγ biotin antibody for 2 h. Steptavidin-ALP secondary antibody was added for 1 h after plates were washed. Spots were detected using BCIP/NPT-plus substrate, rinsed with water, and dried before counting with an AID ELISpot plate reader.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To measure levels of antigen-specific total IgG in mouse serum sandwich ELISA was used. OVA-reactive antibodies were measured by first coating 96 well plates with OVA eluted in 100 μL PBS at 10 μg/mL at 4°C for 24 h. Plates were blotted dry and blocked for 1 h with 200 μL of 5% milk in PBS with 0.05% Tween-20 (ELISA buffer). Plates were then washed three times with 200 μL of ELISA buffer. Serum was heat-inactivated at 56°C for 0.5 h, diluted 1:50 in ELISA buffer, and serially diluted 1:5 seven times. 100 μL of each dilution was added to wells in duplicate and incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature (RT). Plates were washed three times with ELISA buffer and blotted dry. HRP conjugated secondary antibodies were diluted 1:10,000 in ELISA buffer and 100 μL was added to wells and incubated for 1 h at RT. Plates were then washed three times with ELISA buffer, blotted dry and developed with HRP substrate. The reaction was stopped with 1 M HCl 10 minutes after exposure. Absorbance was then read at 490 nm on a BioTek Synergy plate reader. Total IgG titers were calculated by determining area under the curve for serum-specific absorbance and log transformed. Absolute levels for antigen-reactive IgG1 and IgG2c were determined by using linear regression based on a standard curve of control isotype specific mouse antibodies.

Human PBMC flow cytometry analysis

Thawed PBMCs were transferred to 50 mL polypropylene tubes, spun down and suspended in cultured RPMI medium with 10% FBS and 1X (50U) and penicillin-streptomycin. For stimulation, PBMCs were plated at 106 cells/well in 96-well polypropylene deep well plates. Monocyte-derived immature DCs were generated from PBMCs. Monocytes were enriched from leukapheresed blood using a CD14+ monocyte magnetic bead separation kit without CD16 depletion (Stemcell). Enriched monocytes were differentiated into immature DCs using 100 ng/mL GM-CSF (Gemini) and 20 ng/mL IL-4 in CellGenix GMP DC medium, and harvested after differentiating for 36–48 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. For stimulation of immature DCs, cells were plated at 500,000 cells/well in 96-well polypropylene deep well plates. PBMCs or immature DCs were plated with INI3069 (5, 10 μM) or dose matched DMSO overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. Supernatant and cells were harvested for subsequent multiplex immunoassay analysis flow cytometric analysis, respectively.

For ex vivo analysis of activation and maturation marker expression, 106 PBMCs or immature DCs stimulated with varying concentrations of INI3069, were incubated with Live/Dead Fixable Aqua viability stain 405 to discriminate dead from live cells then stained with incubated in 1X Annexin-V buffer with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for at least 15–20 minutes at 4°C or on ice, protected from light. The following fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human antibodies from Biolegend or BD were used: CD40-APC, PD-1 PE-CF594, ICOSL-BV421, CD83-PE, CD86-BV605, CD80-PECy5, HLA-DR-AF700, CD14, BV650, CD16-PECy7, Annexin-V-FITC, and a DUMP (CD3, CD19) channel on APC-Cy7. Flow cytometry measurements were made on a BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) and collected data analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.7.1.
