Ribbon-like Electron-Dense Deposits in the Subepithelial Layer of Diffuse and Global Glomerular Basement Membrane in Neural Epidermal Growth Factor-like 1-Positive Membranous Nephropathy: A Case Report
Authors: Takehito Yamamoto, Mashiro Katsuyama, Kenji Kasuno, Sho Nishikawa, Yuki Watanabe, Kazuhisa Nishimori, Sachiko Fukushima, Mamiko Kobayashi, Yudai Nishikawa, Naoki Takahashi, Hideki Kimura, Tadashi Toyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
An 88-year-old man was referred with peripheral edema, pleural effusion and nephrotic syndrome that had developed 3 months prior. Based on a kidney biopsy, the majority of glomeruli exhibited capillary wall thickening and the slight area of glomeruli exhibited spike formations and bubbly appearances. Fluorescent immunostaining showed global deposition of neural epidermal growth factor-like 1 (NELL-1), immunoglobulin (Ig) G1 and complement (C) 3c within the glomerular capillary wall. Electron microscopy showed the presence of unique subepithelial electron-dense deposits distributed in a ribbon-like manner along more than 75% of glomerular capillary walls. Fluorescent immunostaining showed no positivity for other recently identified antigens associated with membranous nephropathy, including M-type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R), thrombospondin type 1 domain-containing 7A (THSD7A), and exostosin 1 (EXT1). A comprehensive medical examination for malignant diseases yielded negative results, and there was no discernible change in κ/λ staining. Additionally, serum complement levels were within the normal range. The patient was therefore diagnosed with NELL-1-positive membranous nephropathy and has been refractory to the treatment with prednisolone, cyclosporine (CyA) and rituximab for 10 months. According to previous reports, segmental or incomplete IgG capillary loop staining have been observed in 93.4% of cases of NELL-1-positive membranous nephropathy. Diffuse and global ribbon-like deposits, as observed in this case, are exceedingly rare.

Neural epidermal growth factor-like 1 (NELL-1)-positive membranous nephropathy (MN) was recently characterized by Sanjeev Sethi et al.1 NELL-1 positivity is seen in about 8% of primary MN and is characterized by segmental electron-dense deposits and frequently associated with malignant disease (30%).2 In a case series study of 91 NELL-1-positive MN, the segmental (involvement of <50% of glomerular tuft) to incomplete global (segmental loops uninvolved by staining) capillary loop staining pattern was seen in 93.4% of cases of NELL1-associated MN (85/91) and in no cases of PLA2R (0/181) or THSD7A-associated MN (0/10). Complete (100% tuft involvement) capillary loop staining was seen in 6 of 91 (6.6%) of NELL1-associated MN, compared with 100% of PLA2R-positive MN and 100% of THSD7A-positive MN control cases. The segmental pattern of immune deposits was also seen by the distribution of electron-dense immune-type deposits by electron microscopy.3 Diffuse and global ribbon-like electron-dense deposits have not been previously reported in patients with NELL-1-positive MN.

Case Report

The patient had been followed up by his primary physician for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction as well as mitral regurgitation and peripheral arterial disease. He was referred to our hospital in April 2023 after the onset of leg edema and right pleural effusion. He presented with bilateral hard edema from his lower legs to his thighs. There was no history of malignant disease or exposure to hydrargyrum; thiol-containing drugs such as lipoic acid, bucillamine or tiotropium; traditional indigenous drugs, especially those with a high mercury content; or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs except for aspirin. There was also no evidence of human immunodeficiency virus infection, purpura or skin rash. Because urine analysis showed urinary protein at 4-6 g/day and serum albumin at 1.6 mg/dL, the patient was suspected of having nephrotic syndrome. Subsequent kidney biopsy showed a histology typical of elderly patients, with 22% global sclerosis in the glomeruli and regional tubular atrophy and fibrosis in the interstitium. The remaining glomeruli exhibited global thickening of the capillary walls and trivial spike formations and bubbly appearances (Fig 1). Immunofluorescence microscopy showed granular and partly linear deposition of immunoglobulin (Ig) G1, complement (C) 3c, and NELL-1 on the glomerular walls. There was no change in the intensity of κ/λ staining (Fig 1N and O). Both PLA2R, THSD7A were negative (Fig 2A-D). Electron microscopy showed amorphous and finely granular electron-dense deposits along the subepithelial outer aspect of more than 75% of the global glomerular capillary walls. The electron-dense deposits were uniquely thick and linear in a ribbon-like manner, lacking the separation by intervening spikes of basement membrane material characteristic of conventional MN (Fig 2I-L). The electron-dense deposits did not show microspherular substructure, and no organized deposits were observed.4 Extensive evaluations were carried out beyond the age-based screening for malignant diseases to exclude secondary MN. Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, computed tomography scan from neck to pelvis, and abdominal ultrasound were performed, but no obvious malignancy was found. Various tumor markers, including prostate-specific antigen, were negative, and no M-protein was detected by immunofixation electrophoresis of blood and urine. The patient was therefore diagnosed with NELL-1-positive MN with nephrosclerosis and has been refractory to treatment with prednisolone, CyA and rituximab for 10 months. Despite these treatments, the patient died of congestive heart failure 1 year later in another hospital to which he had been transferred. No autopsy was conducted in accordance with the family's wishes; therefore, details concerning the patient's death and the kidney tissue at the time of death are not available. Discussion

The novel feature in this case is the very rare global ribbon-like linear electron-dense deposits. These electron-dense deposits cannot be accurately classified as stage I or any other stage, which Churg and Ehrenreich described as the typical ultrastructure of MN.5 Furthermore, the deposits showed no characteristics of stage II and stage III, dominant spike formations by protrusions or surroundings of new basement membrane material. The diagnosis of NELL-1-positive MN was made based on immunohistochemistry using frozen sections. We did not use paraffin sections, which may cause artifact signals in podocytes and serum traps. Consequently, the positivity of NELL-1 is not an artifact, but is consistent with NELL-1-related MN, in which NELL-1-positive stain along the glomerular basement membrane. NELL-1-positive MN is reportedly associated with malignant diseases such as breast or prostate cancer.6 Our patient, however, was not complicated by malignant disease. Other recent studies have reported associations with bucillamine administration, lipoic acid supplementation, or intake of traditional indigenous medicines containing mercury.7 Moreover, NELL-1 MN has also been associated with autoimmune diseases, hepatitis C, hepatitis B, sarcoidosis and transplantation.6 However, our patient was negative for any history of these drugs or diseases.

A differential diagnosis was therefore made as follows. The electron-dense deposits seen in this case are typically characteristic of monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease,8 which presents with global linear depositions around glomerular basement membranes.9 However, this patient was deemed negative for monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease, as there was no change in κ/λ staining, and he was negative for urinary Bence–Jones protein. Systemic monoclonal gammopathy was also excluded as there was no M-protein detection in the immunofixation electrophoresis of serum and urine, and no typical immunofluorescence microscopy of κ/λ staining and electron microscopic findings. Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis type II is characterized by the presence of electron-dense deposits in the subendothelial or the central layer of the lamina densa along the glomerular basement membrane.10 Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis type II is often accompanied by hypocomplementemia.10 In contrast, in the present case, the electron-dense deposits were found in the subepithelial layer outside the lamina densa (Fig 2L) and the complement level was within the normal range, which were completely different from membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis type II.

The relationship between new antigens of MN and characteristics of electron-dense deposits is rarely investigated. In a review of 26 cases of MN, microspherical deposits were found in 19 cases. Global microspherular deposits were associated with IgG1 subclass predominance and negative staining for PLA2R and THSD7A, suggesting secondary MN. The segmental microspherular deposits had mixed staining patterns for IgG subclasses and PLA2R.4 However, the electron microscopic findings of our patient were negative for microspherical deposits.

In terms of the prognosis, past reports have shown that NELL-1-positive MN, which is characterized by segmental electron-dense deposits, is favorable.11 However, in this case, the response to treatment was not favorable. The relationship between the presence of global ribbon-like electron-dense deposits, and treatment response is an interesting topic for further research.
