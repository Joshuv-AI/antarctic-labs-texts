Unique phenotypic and T cell receptor characteristics of CD8+ T cells accumulated in the brains of Alzheimer’s disease mice
Authors: Wang Zhihuan, Emi Furusawa-Nishii, Sachiko Miyake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the involvement of CD8⁺ tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) have attracted growing interest. During AD pathogenesis, age-associated and AD-associated CD8⁺ T cells may cooperatively influence disease progression; however, their phenotypic and functional differences remain poorly understood. Here, we reanalyzed public single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of brain-derived CD8⁺ T cells from normally aged, wild-type AD, and Cxcr6-deficient AD mice. We identified two major Trm populations: a C-X-C chemokine receptor type 6 (CXCR6)-related immunosuppressive cluster present in aged and wild-type AD mice, and an AD-associated cluster with stem-like features. These clusters were completely phenotypically and clonotypically distinct. Given the proposed role of CXCR6-related CD8⁺ T cells in reducing amyloid-β pathology, their presence in aged mice suggests a protective role. By contrast, the AD-associated cluster exhibited high Tcf7 and Bcl2 expression alongside upregulated immediate-early genes (IEGs), and showed strong clonal overlap with other IEG–enriched clusters. CellChat analysis revealed selective activation of the galectin-9-prolyl 4-hydroxylase pathway in AD-associated Trm-microglia interactions, implicating altered redox regulation. Structural modeling of expanded T cell receptors predicted stable binding to amyloid-β42 peptides, suggesting potential antigen specificity. These findings reveal two transcriptionally and clonotypically distinct CD8+ Trm populations with potentially opposing functions in AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38351-8.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder and is a leading cause of dementia worldwide1,2. There is growing interest in the role of immune responses in AD pathogenesis3,4. Genetic studies have implicated microglia in the pathogenesis of AD5–8, and more recently, CD8+ T cells have emerged as an important player in modulating microglial function9,10. The clonal expansion of CD8⁺ T cells has been observed in the cerebrospinal fluid of AD patients3, and both human and mouse studies have demonstrated the accumulation of CD8⁺ T cells—with or without a tissue-resident phenotype—at sites of amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau deposition9–11. Experimental models further indicate that the chemokine-mediated recruitment of specific subsets of CD8⁺ T cells ameliorates AD pathology9, thereby highlighting the communication between adaptive immunity and glial cells within the central nervous system (CNS).

Under homeostatic conditions, the CNS parenchyma expresses few MHC class I molecules and contains few peripheral immune cells, whereas immune cells are readily found in the border regions and perivascular spaces12. This compartmentalized organization forms the basis of CNS immune regulation, which becomes further activated under neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative conditions 3,12. Although alterations in the immune environment of the brain have traditionally been investigated in the context of CNS disorders, recent studies have revealed that the CNS immune landscape also undergoes dynamic remodeling during normal aging; this is characterized by a marked accumulation of CD8⁺ T cells13–15. Previous studies have mainly focused on cytotoxic subsets of CD8⁺ tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) that emerge during aging and promote neurodegeneration. However, the recent recognition of Trm heterogeneity suggests that brain Trm populations are functionally diverse rather than uniformly pathogenic.

During CNS disorders such as AD, both age-associated and disease-associated CD8⁺ T cells may play heterogeneous roles in disease progression. Distinguishing between these two populations is therefore essential for elucidating their antigen specificity and functional diversity. Building on these observations, recent work has identified the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 6 (CXCR6)–C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 16 (CXCL16) axis as a key pathway that recruits CD8⁺ T cells into the brain, where they acquire an immunosuppressive programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)⁺ tissue-resident phenotype9. These CXCR6-related CD8⁺ T cells suppress microglial inflammatory activity, thereby reducing Aβ pathology; however, it remains unclear whether CD8⁺ T cells with similar immunosuppressive features also arise during normal aging.

In the present study, to determine whether CXCR6-related immunosuppressive CD8⁺ T cells also exist during normal aging, we reanalyzed integrated public single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets of brain-derived CD8⁺ T cells from normally aged mice, wild-type (WT) AD mice, and Cxcr6 deficient AD mice. Importantly, we revealed that a large CXCR6-related CD8⁺ T cell cluster exhibiting an immunosuppressive resident memory phenotype was also present in normally aged mice. Given the proposed role of CXCR6-related immunosuppressive CD8⁺ T cells in reducing Aβ pathology, these findings suggest that CD8⁺ T cells influenced by AD independent factors, such as aging, may include populations capable of contributing to the suppression of Aβ pathology. In addition, we identified a distinct AD-associated Trm population with stem-like features. We further dissected the clonal relationships, microglial interaction pathways, and candidate antigen specificities toward Aβ42 peptides through structure-based modeling of T cell receptor (TCR)–peptide–major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) complexes.

Collectively, the present study revealed two distinct major populations of brain CD8⁺ Trm with different origins: one that was associated with AD and another that was CXCR6-related. Understanding the differences between these two Trm populations will be essential for elucidating their functional diversity as well as their antigen specificity.

Results

Integrated single-cell transcriptomic analysis identifies a CXCR6-related CD8+ T cell cluster present in both normal aging and AD models

It has been reported that Cxcr6 deficiency results in decreased Aβ deposition in an AD model9. To determine whether this cell population is specific to AD or is also present in normal aged mice, we reanalyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset (GSE207702)9 derived from the brains of 8-month-old 5xFAD mice and non-transgenic (non-Tg) controls. The 5xFAD group included both WT and Cxcr6 knockout (KO) genotypes. In total, we identified 7,088 CD8⁺ T cells (4,734 from 5xFAD_WT, 993 from 5xFAD_Cxcr6_KO, and 1361 from non-Tg controls), which segregated into 15 clusters (Fig. 1A).

Cluster 0 (cd8_c0) represented the largest population, whereas cd8_c1 was the second largest (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Notably, cd8_c0 consisted of CD8⁺ T cells from 5xFAD_WT and non-Tg controls but not from Cxcr6_KO mice (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1A), although Cxcr6 expression itself was detectable in several clusters other than cd8_c0 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). These observations indicate that cluster 0, among the Cxcr6-expressing clusters, had the strongest reliance on CXCR6 signaling for its maintenance or entry. Interestingly, in non-Tg controls, cd8_c0 accounted for 53.8% of all CD8⁺ T cells, indicating that CXCR6-related CD8⁺ T cells constitute a major population, even in the normally aged brain.

By contrast, cd8_c1 comprised CD8⁺ T cells from both 5xFAD_WT and 5xFAD_Cxcr6_KO mice, but not from non-Tg controls, suggesting AD pathology–driven recruitment. The remaining clusters (cd8_c2–c14) contained a mixture of cells from all three genotypes. The emergence of clusters restricted to specific genotypes was observed exclusively among CD8⁺ T cells and was not evident in CD4⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1C–E).

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that the largest cluster of brain CD8⁺ T cells is recruited in a CXCR6-related manner and is also present in non-Tg controls. In addition, the second-largest cluster appears to be associated with AD pathology.

Phenotypic characterization of CXCR6-related and AD-associated CD8+ T cell clusters

We then subdivided the 15 CD8⁺ T cell clusters into five phenotypic groups (Fig. 1D): circulating central memory (cd8_c3 and c6; Tcf7, Lef1, Il7r, Ccr7, Sell, Satb1, Bach2, S1pr1, and Klf2), proliferative (cd8_c11 and c12; Mki67, Ccna2, Birc5, Kif18b, Pimreg, Prr11, Ankle1, and Iqgap3), effector lineage (cd8_c7 and c8; Gzma, Gzmb, Klrg1, Klrd1, Fgl2, Fcer1g, Klra1, Klra5, Klra7, Ncr1, Zeb2, Tbx21, and Runx3), immediate-early gene (IEG)–enriched (cd8_c1, c2, c4, c9, c10, and c14; Fos, Fosb, Jun, Egr1, Btg1, Dusp2, Nfkbia, and Socs1), and immunosuppressive (cd8_c0, c5, and c13; Pdcd1, Tox, Lag3, and Ctla4). Expression of the tissue-egress–associated genes Klf2 and S1pr1 was markedly elevated in cd8_c3, c6, and c8 but remained low in the other clusters (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that cd8_c3, c6, and c8 represent circulating CD8⁺ T cells, whereas the remaining clusters correspond to CD8⁺ Trm, albeit with differential Cd69 expression.

Consistent with previous reports9, the CXCR6-related cluster (cd8_c0) was categorized into the immunosuppressive phenotype group, in which cells expressed checkpoint molecules such as Pdcd1, Tox, Lag3, and Ctla4, indicating an immunosuppressive nature (Fig. 1D). However, the expression levels of these checkpoint molecules were lower in cd8_c0 than in cd8_c5. In addition, unlike in cd8_c5, Eomes (listed at the end of “Effector lineage” in Fig. 1D), which is expressed in terminally exhausted CD8⁺ T cells16 as well as in effector cells, was barely detectable in cd8_c0. These observations indicate that cd8_c0 likely represents an immunosuppressive Trm subset that has not yet reached terminal exhaustion. Furthermore, given that CXCR6-related cd8_c0 cells were also detected in the brains of normally aged mice (Fig. 1C), and that previous studies have reported that such Trm subsets contribute to a reduction in Aβ pathology9, it is plausible that factors independent of AD may play a protective role in suppressing Aβ pathology.

By contrast, the AD-associated cluster (cd8_c1) was classified within the IEG-enriched group (Fig. 1D), indicating that AD-associated CD8⁺ T cells exhibit a phenotype that is distinct from the CXCR6-related Trm subset. The IEG enriched group exhibited a non-circulating phenotype characterized by reduced expressions of Klf2 and S1pr1 (listed in “Tissue egress” in Fig. 1D). In addition, cd8_c1, c2, c4, c9, and c10 shared another common feature beyond IEG expression, namely moderate to high levels of Bcl2 (listed first under “Stemness/Memory” in Fig. 1D) and Ifng (listed first under “Cytokines/Gzms” in Fig. 1D), whereas cd8_c4 alone showed an upregulated IFN-related gene signature. Beyond these common features, cd8_c1 was further distinguished from all other IEG enriched clusters by its maintained expression of Tcf7 (listed second under “Stemness/Memory” in Fig. 1D). This characteristic signature was not observed in any other Trm clusters. Together, these features suggest that cd8_c1 may be a potential reservoir of long-lived, stem-like CD8⁺ T cells with intermediate Ifng expression that may persist and influence AD pathology.

Galectin-9 (LGALS9)–prolyl 4-hydroxylase (P4HB) pathway is selectively active in AD-associated CD8+ T cell–microglia interactions

The Trm phenotype is reportedly shaped by environmental niches, including interactions with neighboring cells, the cytokine milieu, and chemokine signaling17,18. Because brain Trm frequently interact with microglia9,19,20, we investigated CD8⁺ T cell–microglia interaction pathways that may contribute to shaping the CXCR6-related cluster (cd8_c0) and the AD-associated cluster (cd8_c1).

Using the same dataset (GSE207702) as we did for the CD8⁺ T cells, we performed an integrated scRNA-seq reanalysis of Ptprc⁺Tmem119⁺P2ry12⁺Cd3e⁻Cd19⁻ microglia derived from 5xFAD_WT mice (4,913 cells), 5xFAD_Cxcr6 KO mice (2,444 cells), and non-Tg controls (1,142 cells); these segregated into eight distinct clusters (Fig. 2A).

Microglia cluster 0 (mg_c0), cluster 2 (mg_c2), and cluster 5 (mg_c5) were markedly enriched in 5xFAD_WT and 5xFAD_Cxcr6 KO mice but were detected only at very low frequencies in non-Tg controls (Fig. 2B–D), indicating that they represent AD-associated clusters. Cluster mg_c0 was characterized by the high expression of IEGs (Fos, Fosb, Jun, Junb, Jund, Dusp1, Dusp2, Dusp5, Atf3, Atf4, Egr1, and Egr2), whereas mg_c2 was enriched in the signature genes of disease-associated microglia (Apoe, Trem2, Lpl, Spp1, Cst7, Cd68, Ctsb, Ctss, Tyrobp, Itgax, and Axl) (Fig. 2E).

By contrast, mg_c1 was absent in Cxcr6 KO mice (Fig. 2C,D), suggesting that its formation depends on CXCR6-mediated chemokine signaling. Although it was difficult to identify mg_c1–specific gene expression patterns, this cluster retained the expression of several homeostatic microglia genes, including P2ry12, Tmem119, and Cx3cr1, but exhibited only low-level expression of inflammatory genes (Fig. 2E). Among the other microglial clusters, mg_c3 and c4 were characterized by the high expression of homeostatic microglia genes (P2ry12, Tmem119, Cx3cr1), although mg_c3 also showed a modest upregulation of disease-associated microglia–related genes. In addition, mg_c5 exhibited elevated expression of inflammatory and interferon-related genes, whereas Irf1, a type II IFN responsive gene, was expressed in mg_c5 as well as in mg_c0 and mg_c2 (Fig. 2E). Among the eight microglial clusters, mg_c6 was the only one that expressed Mrc1 (listed last under “Microglia/macrophage marker genes” in Fig. 2E), with low expressions of the homeostatic microglia genes P2ry12, Tmem119, and Cx3cr1, suggesting that this cluster may represent perivascular macrophages21.

We next analyzed Trm–microglia interactions using CellChat and compared the signaling pathways (Fig. 3A). Trm clusters included cd8_c0, c1, c2, c4, c5, c7, and c9–14 (Fig. 1D). Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure 2 show signaling from microglia (source) to Trm (target) and Supplementary Fig. 3 shows the reverse. An analysis of the number of interactions revealed that each Trm cluster engaged in more signaling pathways with mg_c0, c1, c2, c5, and c6 than with mg_c3, c4, and c7. This result was consistent in both directions (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3A). On the basis of these results, we selected the major contributing microglial clusters (mg_c0, c1, c2, c5, and c6) and analyzed the signaling landscape of Trm–microglia interactions in greater detail. In the microglia-to-Trm direction, we identified 91 significantly active pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2), whereas 68 pathways were identified in the reverse direction (Supplementary Fig. 3B). This difference was largely related to 20 MHC class I–CD8 interactions present in the microglia-to-Trm pathways, likely reflecting the MHC–TCR immune synapse. We therefore focused our analysis on functional pathways other than the MHC class I–CD8 interactions.

Unfortunately, we were unable to identify signaling pathways that were uniquely active in the cd8_c0 cluster alone (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3B). However, the Sema4d-Cd72 pathway detected in the CD8⁺ T cell to microglia direction (Supplementary Fig. 3B, indicated by the red arrow) was relatively more active in cd8_c0 than in the other CD8⁺ T cell clusters. Notably, Sema4D-mediated signaling, which has been implicated in suppressing microglial inflammatory activation22, was particularly enriched between cd8_c0 and the AD-associated microglial cluster mg_c2. These observations suggest that immunosuppressive CD8⁺ T cells may negatively regulate excessive microglial activation by delivering inhibitory cues to AD-associated microglia.

In the AD-associated CD8+ cluster (cd8_c1), the LGALS9–P4HB pathway, which has been implicated in regulating the redox environment of the plasma membrane in T cells23, was more active and exhibited a relatively high communication probability compared with the other Trm clusters (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Figs. 2, 3B). Redox changes at the plasma membrane reduce linker for activation of T cells family member 1 (LAT) localization and suppress TCR signaling24, suggesting that LGALS9–P4HB–mediated alterations in membrane redox status might also downregulate TCR signaling strength. In addition, CellChat analysis of the Trm-to-microglia direction revealed that Ifng–(Ifngr1/Ifngr2) was significantly active. Notably, this pathway showed a slightly higher communication probability between cd8_c1 and the microglial clusters compared with other Trm clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3B, indicated by blue arrow). Microglial clusters that interacted with cd8_c1 included Irf1-expressing clusters (mg_c0, mg_c2, and mg_c5), suggesting that the Ifng–(Ifngr1/Ifngr2) pathway may contribute to shaping the type II IFN responsiveness of these clusters.

Taken together, the unique interaction pathways between the AD-associated Trm cluster and microglia may contribute to shaping the AD-associated CD8⁺ T cell or microglial phenotypes.

Lineage continuity of AD-associated CD8+ T cells with other CD8+ T cell clusters

We next evaluated whether AD-associated CD8⁺ T cells (cd8_c1) with a long-lived, stem-like phenotype are able to differentiate into other clusters. We first analyzed the lineage continuity of AD-associated CD8⁺ T cells with other CD8⁺ T cell clusters by performing paired scRNA-seq and TCR-seq analyses, integrating TCR repertoire information with gene expression profiles.

As shown in Fig. 4A,B, and Supplementary Fig. 4, immunosuppressive clusters (cd8_c0, c5, and c13) contained a relatively high proportion of clonally expanded CD8⁺ T cells with medium or large clone sizes. By contrast, the circulating central memory group (cd8_c3 and c6) contained very few such cells. Clonal expansion in the TCR-driven, IEG-enriched group, including cd8_c1, was moderate (Fig. 4B).

We next assessed the repertoire overlap among frequent clones using the Morisita–Horn (MH) index25. The MH scores were highest between cd8_c1 and c2 and between cd8_c2 and c4, followed by those between cd8_c1 and c4 and between cd8_c1 and c10 (Fig. 4C,D). Clusters cd8_c2, c4, and c10, which belong to the IEG-enriched group, shared highly similar clonal compositions with cd8_c1. By contrast, cd8_c0 showed minimal overlap with other clusters and exhibited no repertoire sharing with cd8_c1 (MH score = 0, Fig. 4C), indicating a distinct clonal architecture. Although cd8_c0 and c1 represent the two largest clusters among brain CD8⁺ T cells and both display features of Trm, their complete lack of TCR overlap suggests that they are induced and maintained under distinct antigenic environments or stimulation histories.

We next analyzed the numbers of shared TCR repertoires among cd8_c1, c2, c4, and 10. Clusters cd8_c1 and c4, cd8_c1 and c2, and cd8_c2 and c4 shared 61, 58, and 53 clones, respectively, with 38 clones common to all three clusters (Fig. 4E). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization further confirmed that the top two expanded TCRs in cd8_c1 were also distributed across cd8_c2 and c4 (Fig. 4F). Together, these findings demonstrate that the major TCR clones enriched in cd8_c1 are broadly represented in cd8_c2 and c4, thus supporting a lineage continuum within the IEG-enriched group.

Unique biophysical profiles of clonally expanded TCRs in AD-associated cluster 1

Given the extensive heterogeneity of TCR repertoires that arise from the cooperative involvement of age-associated and AD-associated CD8⁺ T cells in AD pathology, the identification of AD-related antigen-specific T cells remains a formidable and essential objective. In silico analyses of the biophysical properties of clonally expanded TCRs within the AD-associated cluster, as well as predictions of their complex formation with Aβ peptide–presenting pMHC, represent a powerful approach for identifying AD-related antigen-specific T cells. Therefore, we first investigated whether the clonally expanded CD8⁺ T cells in cd8_c1 express TCRs with unique biophysical properties using the Automated Immune Molecule Separator (AIMS) algorithm26. The fundamental biophysical properties of the amino acids (aa) in each TCR complementarity-determining region (CDR) loop were encoded into a high-dimensional matrix (Fig. 5A). Each sequence was then projected into a lower-dimensional space and clustered by biophysical similarity (Fig. 5B). We identified 36 AIMS clusters, in which the number of distinct TCR repertoires per cluster ranged from a maximum of 76 to a minimum of 3. Among these, AIMS cluster 20 (AIMS_c20), AIMS_c27, and AIMS_c28 were dominated by a single TCR, with more than 95% of the cells within each cluster originating from the same TCR. This observation suggests that the dominant TCR in these clusters may exhibit unique biophysical properties (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, the most expanded (TRAV9D-3.TRAJ22.TRAC_TRBV16.NA.TRBJ2-1.TRBC2) and the second most expanded (TRAV6N-7.TRAJ17.TRAC_TRBV17.TRBD2.TRBJ2-7.TRBC2) TCRs in AD-associated cluster (cd8_c1) belonged to AIMS_c28 and AIMS_c20, where these top two TCRs accounted for 98.7% and 95.0% of cells within AIMS_c28 and AIMS_c20, respectively (Figs. 5C,D). In contrast, the dominant TCR in AIMS_c27 corresponded to the most clonally expanded TCR in CXCR6-related CD8⁺ T cell cluster (cd8_c0). Based on the exclusivity of a single TCR composition in each AIMS-based cluster, there were almost no other TCRs with similar biophysical properties to the most or second most expanded TCRs in the AD-associated cluster. Moreover, AIMS_c20 and AIMS _c28 exhibited distinct characteristics in terms of charge and hydropathy (Fig. 5E). Together, these findings suggest that the top two clonally expanded TCRs in cd8_c1possess unique biophysical properties that distinguish them from other TCRs.

Predicted structural binding mode of the highly expanded TCR with Aβ peptide–MHC class I

We next analyzed whether the highly expanded TCRs in the AD-associated CD8⁺ T cell cluster (cd8_c1) adopt a structurally plausible binding mode with Aβ peptide–pMHC complexes using TCRmodel2. This tool predicts whether a given TCR can form a valid complex with a pMHC presenting a selected epitope, and assigns a confidence score to each model27. A score > 0.85 is considered high confidence, indicating a structurally coherent complex and a strong likelihood of interaction.

Among the TCRs with high clonal expansion in the AD-associated CD8⁺ T cell cluster (cd8_c1), the most expanded TCR (TCR No. 1) and the second most expanded TCR (TCR No. 2) exhibited particularly high enrichment in cd8_c1, with more than 70% of their clones present in this cluster (Supplementary Table 1). We therefore focused on the top two TCRs (No. 1 and No. 2) for the subsequent structural modeling of their binding modes with Aβ peptide–presenting MHC class I.

The predicted binding affinity of Aβ42 peptides to H-2Kb, which was analyzed using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) tool, was highest for the aa8–17 (SGYEVHHQKL) 10-mer Aβ peptide, with a percentile rank of 0.16 (Supplementary Table 2). However, the confidence scores of the No. 1 and No. 2 TCR–pMHC complexes were 0.54 and 0.72, respectively, suggesting that neither TCR reliably interacts with pMHC presenting the aa8–17 Aβ peptide (Supplementary Table 3).

Next, we investigated the interactions of the top two TCRs with pMHC complexes presenting different 10-mer Aβ peptides. The predicted affinity of each Aβ peptide with H-2Kb, as calculated by the IEDB tool, is shown in Supplementary Table 2. Corresponding confidence scores for the modeled TCR–pMHC complexes are presented in Supplementary Table 3. TCR No. 2 consistently exhibited confidence scores < 0.85 across overlapping Aβ42 peptides, indicating poor structural compatibility. By contrast, TCR No. 1 achieved high-confidence predictions (≥ 0.85) with several but not all Aβ42 peptides. Confidence scores of 0.85 were observed for aa6–15, aa7–16, aa21–30, aa27–36, aa28–37, aa29–38, aa31–40, and aa37–42. Additionally, scores of 0.86 were obtained for aa14–23, aa18–27, and aa22–31. In addition to these 10-mer Aβ peptides, we also calculated confidence scores for 9-mer Aβ peptides and APP-derived peptides predicted to have high affinity for MHC class I; however, none had confidence scores ≥ 0.85 (Supplementary Tables 4, 5).

We then performed a more detailed structural analysis of the pMHC–TCR complexes, focusing on the aa14–23, aa18–27, and aa22–31 peptides. In structural studies, atomic contacts within 4 Å are generally considered to contribute to pMHC–TCR recognition28. For the aa14–23 peptide, nine contact sites within 4 Å were identified, including three with the MHC and six with the peptide (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 6). When the aa18–27 peptide was presented, 13 contact sites were detected, comprising 10 with the MHC and three with the peptide (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Table 7). Similarly, analysis with the aa22–31 peptide revealed 13 contact sites, including eight with the MHC and five with the peptide (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Table 8). In TCR–pMHC interactions, contacts with the peptide were primarily mediated by the CDR3 loops, and partial contributions from CDR1 and CDR2 were also observed. Together, these results suggest that the most clonally expanded TCRs in the AD-associated cluster have the potential to interact with pMHC complexes presenting Aβ peptides.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified two distinct major populations of brain CD8⁺ Trm: one was related to CXCR6 and the other was associated with AD pathology. Notably, these two clusters were completely distinct not only phenotypically but also in terms of TCR repertoire sharing. The CXCR6-related cluster, which exhibited an immunosuppressive phenotype, was also present in normally aged mice, indicating that factors independent of AD pathology, such as aging, might contribute to its appearance. By contrast, the AD-associated cluster displayed a stem-like Trm phenotype that was characterized by high Tcf7 and Bcl2 expression and preferentially engaged in the LGALS9–P4HB interaction pathway with microglia. Furthermore, in silico TCR–pMHC structural analyses using highly expanded TCRs in the AD-associated cluster identified candidate TCRs with the potential to recognize Aβ peptides. This finding may offer hints for the discovery of Aβ-specific TCRs in AD.

Aging represents a major underlying factor for AD, and both age-associated and disease-associated CD8⁺ T cells may play heterogeneous roles in disease progression. To address the functional relevance of CD8⁺ T cells in the aged brain, we performed a reanalysis of integrated scRNA-seq data from brain CD8⁺ T cells of 8-month-old WT AD mice, Cxcr6 KO AD mice, and non-Tg controls. This analysis indicated that the generation of the CXCR6-related immunosuppressive Trm cluster, which was characterized by the expression of checkpoint molecules such as Pdcd1, Tox, Lag3, and Ctla4, is not specific to AD pathology but can also be induced in the aged brain. Although previous flow cytometric studies have reported the presence of CXCR6⁺ Trm in the brains of normally aged mice9, our transcriptomic analysis, which extended beyond simple expression profiling, demonstrated that a cell population whose phenotype and function are defined by the CXCR6 pathway was also present in the brains of normally aged mice. This supports the notion that AD-independent factors such as natural aging, rather than Aβ pathology, may contribute to the generation of CXCR6-related immunosuppressive Trm; however, direct causal evidence remains to be established. In addition, given the proposed role of CXRC6-related CD8⁺ T cells in reducing Aβ pathology9, the presence of the CXCR6-related cluster in normally aged mice suggests that AD pathology-independent CD8⁺ T cells may play a protective role in suppressing Aβ pathology. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that the treatment of 18-month-old mice with anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4 antibodies increases inflammatory oligodendrocytes and microglia in the white matter, thereby exacerbating white matter aging. These findings support the idea that immunosuppressive CD8⁺ T cells play an immunoregulatory role in the aged brain15. The CXCR6-related immunosuppressive CD8⁺ T cells identified in this study may contribute to the reduction of Aβ pathology by modulating microglial functions via CD8⁺ T cell to microglia signaling. For example, Sema4d-Cd72 signaling, which was relatively active between cd8_c0 and the AD-associated microglial cluster, may suppress excessive inflammatory responses in AD-associated microglia. Future functional studies will be important to test these observations directly.

Although the CXCR6-related cluster represented the largest population in the normally aged brain, we also identified several Trm clusters that exhibited effector or cytotoxic phenotypes. Specifically, cd8_c2 showed high expression of Ifng, and cd8_c4 expressed both Ifng and Gzmb, resembling the CD8⁺ T cells that were previously reported to be involved in neurotoxicity in aged brains13,14. Together, these findings suggest that the brain contains a heterogeneous population of Trm; some subsets contribute to inflammatory responses, whereas the majority may play a role in maintaining tissue homeostasis in the brain.

In the brains of WT AD mice, the second largest cluster (cd8_c1) was an AD-associated population. Given the complete lack of TCR overlap between the AD-associated and CXCR6-related clusters, these two populations likely have distinct origins and are induced by different antigens. Interestingly, AD-associated Trm exhibited high Tcf7 and Bcl2 expression, which is indicative of a stem-like, long-lived state. Recent studies of tumors and mucosal tissues have identified the presence of transcription factor 1 (TCF1)⁺ Trm, suggesting stem-like features17,18,28,29. In tumors, the abundance of stem-like Trm is a key factor for better prognosis and treatment response28,29. Although the functional role of stem-like Trm in AD remains unclear, our TCR analysis revealed a strong clonal overlap between the Tcf7 high AD-associated cluster and the cd8_c2 and cd8_c4 clusters that exhibited effector or cytotoxic phenotypes. Elucidating whether the AD-associated stem-like cluster serves as a progenitor source for effector or cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells, and contributes to the exacerbation of neuroinflammation represents an important future challenge.

Recent studies have underscored that the generation of TCF1⁺ Trm is strongly influenced by the surrounding microenvironment, including interactions with neighboring cells, the cytokine milieu, and chemokine signaling. Our CellChat-based analysis of Trm–microglia crosstalk revealed that the LGALS9–P4HB pathway was selectively activated in the AD-associated Trm cluster. Prior work has implicated this pathway in regulating the redox balance at the T cell plasma membrane23, and redox perturbations are known to alter LAT localization and reduce TCR signaling24. These observations support the notion that LGALS9–P4HB–driven redox remodeling could diminish TCR signal intensity and facilitate the development of Tcf7⁺ stem-like Trm.

Our structural modeling of TCR with pMHC demonstrated that the predominantly expanded TCR within the AD-associated cluster formed high-confidence complexes with Aβ42 peptide–presenting pMHC, even though these candidate peptides had low predicted binding affinity to MHC class I. These findings highlight the importance of future investigations that delineate the functional effects of the AD-associated Trm population on AD pathology, as well as its antigen recognition mechanisms.

Collectively, our findings highlight the presence of two major brain CD8⁺ Trm populations with distinct origins: one closely linked to AD pathology and the other related to CXCR6. Dissecting the differences between these two Trm subsets will be crucial for clarifying their functional roles and antigen specificities.

Materials and methods

scRNA-seq expression profile analysis

The filtered count matrices from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database GSE207702 were downloaded and read into the R (v.4.4.2) package Seurat (v.5). Samples were merged into a single Seurat object for consistent filtering, and features detected in fewer than three cells were removed from the dataset. Cells with low features (< 750) or unique molecular identifier counts (< 500) or high mitochondrial read percentages (> 10%) were excluded from further analysis. The R package DoubletFinder30 was used to drop out potential doublets using default settings (artificial doublet rate = 7.5%). After quality control, libraries were normalized using the NormalizeData function (scale.factor = 106). For the analysis of CD8+ T cells, Ptprc+Cd3e+Cd8a+Cd4−Cd19−P2ry12− cells were isolated. In total, 7,088 CD8⁺ T cells (4,734 from 5xFAD_WT, 993 from 5xFAD_Cxcr6 KO, and 1,361 from Non-Tg controls) were selected and reclustered using the Seurat workflow. The R package Harmony was used for eliminating batch effects among libraries, with the following settings: RunUMAP (dims = 1:30), FindNeighbors (dims = 1:30), and FindClusters (resolution = 0.4). This generated 15 CD8+ T cell subclusters. For the analysis of microglia, Ptprc+Tmem119+P2ry12+Cd3e−Cd19− cells were isolated. In total, 8499 microglia (4,913 from 5xFAD_WT, 2,444 from 5xFAD_Cxcr6 KO, and 1,142 from Non-Tg controls) were selected and underwent similar processing pipelines to the CD8+ T cells using Seurat. Eight clusters of microglia were generated. For the analysis of CD4+ T cells, Ptprc+Cd3e+Cd4+Cd8a−Cd19−P2ry12− cells were isolated. In total, 1,229 CD4+ T cells (612 from 5xFAD_WT, 322 from 5xFAD_Cxcr6 KO, and 295 from Non-Tg controls) were selected. Five clusters of CD4+ T cells were generated.

scV(D)J-seq data processing and analysis

The R package scRepertoire was used to combine filtered contig_annotation from different samples into a single list object (function combineTCR). The combined TCR contig list was then integrated with the corresponding Seurat object of the scRNA-seq data using the function combineExpression (cloneCall=“gene”). The clone type was defined according to the genes comprising the TCR and the aa sequence of CDR3. After isolating CD8+ T cells, the clone size of each clone type was calculated, and clone frequencies were categorized as follows: single(1), small(2~5)2–4, medium(6~20)6–19and large (> 20).

Intercellular communication analysis

To explore the potential interaction between microglia and CD8+ T cells, the intercellular communication of cell clusters was calculated and analyzed using the R package CellChat (v2.1.0)31. First, CellChat objects were generated using the creatCellChat function. The overall cell–cell interaction strength between microglia and CD8+ T cells was then calculated using the default workflow based on ligand–receptor databases in the CellChat package. The workflow included the following commands in sequential order, using default parameters unless otherwise specified: indentifyOverExpressedGenes, indentifyOverExpressedInteractions, smoothData, computeCommunProb, filterCommunication (min.cells = 10), aggregateData, subsetCommunication, and netAnalysis_computeCentrality. Finally, visualizations that described cellular communication were generated using the embedded functions in the CellChat and ggplot2 packages.

AIMS analysis

To acquire information about the full-length TCR sequence, original VDJseq FASTQ files were downloaded and processed using Cellranger (v9.0, using the mouse VDJ reference genome mm10). All six CDR loops (CDR1–3 of the α and β chains) of the gene expression–matched TCR sequences were encoded into an AIMS-compatible matrix for biophysical characterization, which generated large biophysical property matrices26,32. Next, these property matrices were projected into three dimensions using UMAP before being clustered using the Ordering Points to Identify the Clustering Structure (OPTICS) algorithm, with a minimum of 30 sequences per cluster. The TCR sequences were divided into 36 meaningful clusters (AIMS_cluster 0–35), whereas TCR sequences, which were unable to be appropriately gathered as clusters, were consistently labeled as “AIMS_cluster − 1”. Information about the AIMS clusters was then integrated into the metadata of the Seurat object using matched cell barcodes. For additional information about the AIMS analysis or to download the analysis scripts, see the AIMS GitHub page (github.com/ctboughter/AIMS). For the exact analysis scripts that were used to create the analysis in the present manuscript, see https://github.com/ctboughter/AIMS_manuscripts.

TCRmodel2 analysis

To evaluate whether TCR can form a stable structure with MHC-I–presented Aβ42 peptide, we used TCRmodel2, a tool that is designed to predict TCR recognition based on AlphaFold v2.3.027. First, we used MHC-I epitope prediction tools on IEDB (http://tools.iedb.org/mhci, choosing “NetMHCpan 4.1 EL” as the prediction method) to predict the potential binding of H2-Kb (MHC-I of C57B6J mice) and the Aβ42 peptide. Candidate TCR sequences and pMHC-I complexes were then submitted to the TCRmodel2 web server (https://tcrmodel.ibbr.umd.edu). Models with model confidence ≥ 0.85 are likely good, whereas those with model confidence ≤ 0.49 are likely not good. After model generation, the most confident model (rank_0) was used in PyMOL to evaluate potential interactions on the interface of TCR and pMHC. Atomic spacing between TCR CDR loops and peptide or MHC were measured; those that were less than 4 Å are presented using dashed lines.

Statistical analysis and visualization

To identify significantly enriched signaling pathways between Trm and microglia, the significance of signaling pathways was assessed using the embedded statistical framework in the computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway functions of CellChat, which estimate P-values through permutation-based testing. Pathways with P < 0.05 were considered significant. Visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and the R package ggplot2. The visualization of data generated by AIMS was processed using the Python package matplotlib.
