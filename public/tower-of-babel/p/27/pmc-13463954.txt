Transcriptome graph transformer: a graph transformer-based unsupervised model for transcriptome data analysis
Authors: Teng Long, Sachit Satyal, Jean Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapidly growing transcriptomic datasets pose challenges for traditional analytical methods, which struggle with high dimensionality, heterogeneity, and nonlinear gene relationships. Existing deep learning models often require fixed-length inputs and fail to integrate biological network information.MethodsWe introduce Transcriptome Graph Transformer (TGT), an unsupervised graph Transformer framework that constructs a heterogeneous gene–pathway graph using expression data, STRING interactions, and GO/KEGG/Reactome pathway annotations. TGT is pretrained with a masked-node prediction task and fine-tuned for disease classification, biomarker discovery, and zero-shot clustering of single-cell and spatial transcriptomics.ResultsTGT achieves superior performance across Alzheimer’s disease, cancer, acute kidney injury, and COVID-19 datasets, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines. The model generalizes well across platforms and yields biologically meaningful gene and pathway importance scores consistent with known disease mechanisms.ConclusionsTGT provides an effective and generalizable approach for transcriptomic representation learning by integrating biological network knowledge with graph Transformer architectures. Its strong performance highlights its utility for broad transcriptomic applications and precision medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06458-4.Keywords: Graph transformer, Gene expression analysis, Unsupervised learning, Biomarker discovery, Disease classification

Introduction

In transcriptomic studies, expression matrix analysis is a critical step for understanding gene expression patterns, identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and uncovering underlying biological mechanisms. Traditional analytical methods such as differential expression analysis and enrichment analysis have been widely used for quantifying gene expression and annotating gene functions. However, as the scale and complexity of data increase, these conventional approaches face significant challenges when handling high-dimensional, nonlinear, and heterogeneous transcriptomic datasets [1].

Differential expression analysis (DEA) aims to identify genes whose expression levels vary significantly under different experimental conditions, tissue types, or disease states. Commonly used tools include DESeq2, edgeR, and limma. However, these methods primarily rely on linear models, which struggle to capture the complex nonlinear and interactive relationships between genes [2]. Additionally, transcriptomic data often comprise tens of thousands of gene features, and traditional statistical techniques may encounter the “curse of dimensionality," which adversely affects analytical performance. Technical variations such as batch effects and differences in sequencing depth further compromise the stability and reproducibility of results.

Enrichment analysis is employed to identify overrepresented biological processes, molecular functions, or signaling pathways among the DEGs, thereby revealing potential biological mechanisms. Popular approaches involve annotations from databases such as GO [3] and KEGG [4]. However, traditional enrichment analyses depend on predefined gene sets and may overlook novel or unknown biological pathways. For example, the hierarchical structure of GO terms–with varying depths across different branches–may introduce ambiguity or inaccuracy in the analysis [5]. Furthermore, conventional methods often disregard gene-gene interactions and regulatory networks, limiting our understanding of complex biological processes.

With the advancement of artificial intelligence, deep learning (DL) has increasingly been applied to omics data analysis due to its powerful capabilities in feature extraction and pattern recognition. For instance, the Biologically Informed Neural Network (BINN) [6] utilized hierarchical signaling pathways from the Reactome database to process protein expression profiles derived from sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (AKI), COVID-19 patients, and benign plasma samples. It is then applied for sample classification and biomarker discovery. Current applications of deep learning in transcriptomic data primarily focus on biomarker identification and sample classification at the cellular and tissue levels. At the tissue level, DeepGene Transformer [7] processes gene expression profiles of cancer and normal tissues from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) using 1D-CNN followed by a Transformer, and finally a multilayer perceptron (MLP) for classification. Trans2D-CNN [8] rearranges gene expression profiles according to chromosomal order and stacks them into a 2D image for convolution using a 2D CNN. Other approaches such as simple artificial neural networks, support vector machines, and random forests have also been used for transcriptome-based classification [9]. However, these methods share a common limitation: they require fixed-length expression matrices, rendering them unsuitable for handling samples from multiple sequencing platforms. Additionally, these methods typically rely solely on raw gene expression values, without incorporating biologically informed structural or relational information (e.g., pathway structures or gene–gene interactions), which may limit model performance. DeepGene Transformer [7] also suffers from computational inefficiency and noise due to long-range attention mechanisms.

At the cellular level, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have recently gained significant attention, with numerous graph neural network (GNN) methods being developed for analyzing such data. In single-cell sequencing, scGraphformer [10] computes Euclidean distances between cell expression profiles to construct a k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph, followed by cell classification using a graph-based Transformer. In spatial transcriptomics, CCST [11] treats gene expression profiles as node features and determines cell-cell edges based on a truncation distance, then applies a graph convolutional network (GCN) to extract latent variables, followed by clustering methods such as K-means. However, current methods heavily rely on expression profiles as embedding features for cells and lack effective alternative feature engineering strategies. The same issue of requiring fixed-length expression vectors also persists. While earlier deep learning models have shown promise, recent advances in transcriptomic representation learning have further demonstrated the value of integrating multimodal biological data to infer complex gene interactions. For instance, scMultiomeGRN [12]leverages deep learning on single-cell multiome data to infer cell-specific regulatory networks, while LEGEND [13] integrates scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics to identify co-expressed gene modules across tissue domains, outperforming traditional co-expression analyses. Similarly, SIGEL [14] utilizes spatial genomics to learn context-aware representations, improving gene-gene interaction modeling.

Inspired by the knowledge-guided pre-training framework (KPGT) [15], which uses unsupervised learning to model atomic interactions among molecules with varying atomic counts, we propose Transcriptome Graph Transformer (TGT), an unsupervised, graph Transformer-based pretraining model for transcriptomic expression profiles. TGT does not rely on fixed-length expression matrices. We utilize the STRING database [16] to construct gene-gene edges and generate node features from expression data. In consideration of the valuable information contained in GO [3] and KEGG [4] pathways, we incorporate both pathway nodes and virtual pathway nodes to enhance connectivity between genes and biological pathways. Finally, we apply a heterogeneous graph Transformer to perform masked node prediction, allowing the model to comprehensively learn gene interaction information under various transcriptomic contexts.

Our pretrained TGT model can be fine-tuned for classification and regression tasks at the tissue level and can also perform zero-shot embedding of expression profiles at the cellular level for downstream tasks such as clustering and classification. These capabilities highlight the broad applicability of our method in transcriptomic research.

Model

A gene expression graph was constructed based on the transcriptomic gene expression matrices. Specifically, a gene expression graph G = (V,E,X) was constructed, where V is the set of gene nodes, with each node representing a gene. E is the set of edges between nodes, with connections provided by the STRING database [16]. An undirected edge was established between two genes i and j if their interaction confidence score (i.e., combined score) exceeds 700. A combined score above 700 reflects high-confidence interactions in the STRING database. X represents the node feature matrix, where each gene node’s features comprise its normalized expression value and its tokenized identity. In this manner, a gene expression graph incorporating both gene interaction and expression information was constructed.

According to annotations from Gene Ontology (972 pathways) [3], Reactome (634 pathways) [17], and KEGG (363 pathways) databases [4], a pathway node was added if at least three gene nodes from a pathway are present in the expression matrix, and undirected edges were established between the pathway node and each corresponding gene node.

To enhance information flow between distant nodes, a virtual node was incorporated. This node exclusively connects to all pathway nodes (not directly to gene nodes), with its initial feature set as the mean of all pathway node features.

Two strategies were employed to construct the graph Transformer architecture. The first was based on a homogeneous graph design. The second strategy utilized a heterogeneous graph framework, in which separate Transformer convolution layers were defined for each interaction type: gene-to-gene (g2g), gene-to-pathway (g2p), pathway-to-gene (p2g), pathway-to-pathway (p2p), virtual-to-pathway (v2p), and pathway-to-virtual (p2v). Unlike conventional Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT) models, which compute cross-attention only between neighboring nodes, the virtual node in our model attends to all pathway nodes. Specifically, in the v2p and p2v layers, cross-attention is calculated between the virtual node and all pathway nodes, enabling global information exchange within the pathway space.

Pre-training

In this study, we adopted a node masking strategy for unsupervised pretraining on transcriptomic data. Specifically, 15% of gene nodes were randomly selected for masking. Among them, 80% were replaced with blank nodes, 10% were substituted with features from randomly sampled other nodes, and the remaining 10% were left unchanged. The model was trained to predict the true identity of each masked gene node. To achieve this, we employed a cross-entropy loss function to compute the discrepancy between the predicted outputs and the true labels of the masked nodes.

The loss function used was cross-entropy loss, which measures the difference between the predicted and true labels of the masked nodes. The training process was optimized using the AdamW optimizer, with the learning rate following a warm-up strategy that gradually increased from 0.00001 to 0.001, followed by a linear decay schedule to ensure stable convergence. Fine-tuning

To adapt the model for graph-level classification tasks, we replaced the pretrained node classification head with a graph readout function, and appended the same MLP classification layer to the readout graph representation to predict the label type for each sample. During fine-tuning, we used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 0.005. The dataset was split into training and validation sets at a 4:1 ratio, with the validation set containing 285 samples. Early stopping was employed with a patience of 7 epochs to prevent overfitting and enhance model generalization. Similar to pretraining, the cross-entropy loss function was used. Result

Case study 1: AD transcriptomic classification fine-tuning and biomarkers & pathways discovery

In this study, we constructed a graph-structured input based on transcriptomic expression profiles and fine-tuned a Graph Transformer model on a single-cell RNA-seq Alzheimer’s disease (AD) dataset [18] to perform classification between AD patients and normal control samples. The model’s performance on the test set is illustrated in Fig. 3. As shown in the confusion matrix, the model effectively distinguished between disease and normal states, correctly identifying 132 normal samples (True Negatives) and 81 AD samples (True Positives), with 21 false positives and 51 false negatives.

ROC–AUC curve and confusion matrix on the AD test dataset

Based on these results, we calculated several classification performance metrics: the sensitivity (recall) was 0.61, indicating that there is room for improvement in detecting AD samples; the specificity was 0.86, suggesting strong discriminative power in identifying non-AD samples. The overall accuracy reached 0.75, with a precision of 0.79, an F-score of 0.69, and a Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) of 0.50. Figure 3 presents the model’s ROC curve, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.74, further demonstrating the model’s robust performance in binary classification tasks. To further assess the model’s capability, we compared TGT with several baseline frameworks, including BINN [6], the hybrid CNN-Transformer model DeepGene Transformer [7], and the 2D convolution-based Trans2D-CNN [8]. The performance comparison of different models on the Alzheimer’s disease dataset is summarized in Table 1. As shown in Fig. 4, TGT outperformed all baselines in accuracy, precision, and specificity, highlighting its strength in capturing nonlinear relationships and multimodal biological dependencies among genes. While BINN benefits from hierarchical pathway information, its reliance on fixed gene sets and linear assumptions limits its expressiveness. DeepGene Transformer and Trans2D-CNN showed moderate performance but lacked the ability to jointly model gene–gene and pathway-level interactions.

During AD progression, inflammatory pathways in microglia–including the NLRP3 inflammasome, TLR4, and NF-κ B–are activated, resulting in excessive secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL?1β , IL?18, and TNF?α . This creates a positive feedback loop that inhibits Aβ clearance and exacerbates Tau pathology [28]. Notably, several of these processes are captured by high-scoring pathways in our model, including the TLR4 cascade (R-HSA-9013149), Interleukin-1 family signaling (R-HSA-6785807), and the TNFR2 non-canonical NF- κ B pathway (R-HSA-5661231).

At the same time, mitochondrial-associated genes in AD brains, such as the members of the NDUFA1, COX6C, and SLC25 families, are downregulated. This impairs oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and electron transport chain activity, leading to reduced ATP synthesis and increased oxidative stress [29]. These pathological features are further reflected in enriched pathways such as the Cellular response to chemical stress (R-HSA-8957275) and the RHO GTPase cycle (R-HSA-5610787). Mitochondrial dysfunction also promotes the release of mtROS and mtDNA, which further activate the NLRP3 and cGAS–STING pathways, amplifying inflammation and oxidative damage and ultimately establishing a self-reinforcing loop of Aβ /Tau pathology [28]. These findings not only confirm the model’s effectiveness in disease classification but also provide a systems-level insight into the potential molecular mechanisms of AD, offering valuable guidance for future biomarker discovery and targeted therapeutic research.

Case study 2: tumor transcriptomic classification fine-tuning

In the tumor classification task, TGT demonstrated superior performance when applied to gene expression matrices, achieving an accuracy of 97%, which significantly outperforms the convolutional neural network (CNN) approaches used in previous studies [30], where the reported accuracy was 95.59%, shown in Fig. 7. In this setting, each sample corresponds to a tumor tissue profile from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas dataset, and the model is trained to predict the tumor type. The classification labels represent different cancer types–such as breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), and others–based on transcriptomic profiles.

Confusion matrix of TGT under five-fold cross-validation on the TCGA dataset

By constructing a graph structure from gene expression data, TGT effectively captures gene–gene interactions and expression patterns, thereby enhancing classification performance. In contrast, the CNN-based method embeds high-dimensional RNA-Seq data into two-dimensional images for processing. While this approach has shown promising results in certain tasks, it may suffer from limitations inherent in the image transformation process when dealing with complex gene expression profiles.

Therefore, TGT exhibits a stronger advantage in tumor classification tasks, offering a novel and effective strategy for cancer type identification based on gene expression data.

Case study 3: zero-shot clustering on transcriptomic data

In addition to its outstanding performance in tumor type classification, TGT also demonstrates strong capability in handling gene expression-based subtype classification. We utilized the pre-trained hidden layer outputs of TGT for dimensionality reduction, followed by unsupervised clustering analysis on four osteoarthritis (OA) subtypes [31]. In Fig. 8, the results showed that TGT effectively separated the four subtypes into distinct clusters in the low-dimensional embedding space, indicating good spatial separability and subtype discriminative power. These findings suggest that TGT possesses high sensitivity to phenotype-associated expression patterns and a strong awareness of data structure, enabling it to capture latent features relevant to disease heterogeneity. This capability holds promise for supporting molecular subtyping studies of complex diseases such as OA, offering a novel technical approach and interpretive perspective for understanding intra-disease variation.

Performance comparison with BINN and ablation study

To evaluate the impact of different architectural components and justify the choice of a Transformer-based backbone, we conducted comprehensive comparison experiments on the AKI [25] and COVID [26] datasets. We compared the proposed TGT model with several GNN variants, including GAT, GIN, and a vanilla Graph Transformer (GT) backbone, alongside the baseline BINN [6] and ablated TGT versions. As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 10A, TGT achieved the best performance across all evaluation metrics, with an accuracy of 0.97, an AUC of 0.99, an F-score of 0.94, and a precision of 0.97. Compared with the BINN [6], TGT maintained the same AUC while achieving higher F and precision scores, indicating stronger discriminative ability under class imbalance conditions. Furthermore, the ablation study highlights the critical contributions of both the virtual node and pathway information. Removing the virtual node (TGT-noVirtualNode) led to a slight drop in F-score to 0.94 and precision to 0.96. Removing the pathway information (TGT-noPathway) caused a further decrease in precision to 0.95. Notably, TGT consistently outperformed other GNN backbones such as GAT and GIN, which yielded lower F1-scores of 0.93 and precision of 0.95. Although all ablated models still outperformed or approached the performance of BINN [6], the slight reductions confirm the positive impact of these structural components on model performance. Overall, TGT outperforms the comparison models in both accuracy and stability, and its architectural design–incorporating virtual nodes and pathway information–plays a crucial role in boosting model effectiveness.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed TGT, an unsupervised transcriptomic language model based on a graph Transformer architecture. By integrating gene expression data with rich biological network information, TGT constructs a heterogeneous graph comprising gene nodes, pathway nodes, and a virtual pathway node, thereby enhancing long-range upstream and downstream signal propagation between distant genes.

The core innovation of the TGT model lies in its graph Transformer framework, which can simultaneously handle multiple node and edge types in heterogeneous graphs, enabling a more comprehensive representation of complex gene–gene relationships. During the pretraining phase, the use of a node masking task encourages the model to capture deep semantic features within transcriptomic profiles, facilitating accurate gene identity recovery.

TGT shares similarities with recent approaches such as DeepMAPS [32], which learns joint representations of cells and genes via heterogeneous graph learning to improve gene regulatory network inference. Likewise, our use of a masking-and-recovery task is inspired by GexBERT [33], which applies masked language modeling to gene expression data to learn contextual embeddings, thereby enhancing downstream performance in cancer research.

Compared to traditional methods, TGT demonstrates superior representation learning capacity and generalization performance across multiple downstream tasks. In particular, its strong performance in zero-shot transcriptomic embedding suggests promising applicability in future single-cell and spatial transcriptomics analysis.

Nonetheless, several limitations remain in the current design. First, TGT relies heavily on high-quality and complete gene interaction and pathway databases. Missing or incorrect annotations could compromise the model’s performance. Second, the computational complexity of the heterogeneous graph structure and Transformer-based architecture results in high training costs, limiting its scalability to large-scale or real-time scenarios. Moreover, the current model does not explicitly incorporate temporal dynamics in gene expression or cellular heterogeneity, both of which are crucial in certain biological processes and disease studies.

Future work could address these challenges from several directions. On one hand, incorporating additional multi-omics data types–such as epigenetic modifications, protein-protein interactions, and spatial localization–may enrich both node features and graph topology, thereby enhancing biological interpretability. On the other hand, architectural optimizations such as lightweight Transformer designs and graph sampling techniques could reduce computational overhead and improve efficiency during training and inference. Furthermore, developing dynamic graph models tailored for time-series transcriptomic data may help capture the evolving nature of gene regulation. Finally, integrating fine-grained cellular subpopulation information may improve the model’s ability to predict gene expression and functional annotations at single-cell resolution.

In summary, the TGT model opens a new avenue for unsupervised feature learning on transcriptomic data. By effectively leveraging biological knowledge and advanced deep learning techniques, TGT holds strong potential for broad applications. Its utility in disease mechanism analysis, drug target discovery, and precision medicine warrants further exploration and validation.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed TGT, a model that effectively integrates gene expression data with functional pathway information to enable unsupervised pretraining on transcriptomic profiles. By introducing a heterogeneous graph Transformer architecture, TGT is capable of capturing complex interactions between genes and biological pathways, thereby enhancing the richness of learned representations.

The pretrained TGT model demonstrated strong performance across a range of downstream tasks, including transcriptome classification, and feature embedding of cell-level expression profiles. Experimental results show that TGT overcomes the limitations of traditional methods in handling high-dimensional and heterogeneous data, and exhibits strong generalization ability.

Overall, TGT provides a novel framework for deep analysis of transcriptomic data, offering broad applicability and significant potential for extension in various biological and biomedical research contexts.
