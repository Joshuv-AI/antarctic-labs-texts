Association between near visual display device usage and the eye’s accommodation and vergence parameters: a cross-sectional study of high school children in Cape Coast, Ghana
Authors: Charles Darko-Takyi, Thomas Welbeck Mills, Samuel Bert Boadi-Kusi, Michael Ntodie, Carl Halladay Abraham, Emmanuel Kwasi Abu, Sedinam Amewuzah, Emmanuel Essien, Bright Osei Wiafe, Stephen Ocansey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding the link between near visual display device (NVDD) use and the parameters of the eye’s accommodation and vergence (PEAV) can impact vision therapy and optometric advice. This study examined the relationship between the duration of NVDD use and PEAV among high school students in Cape Coast, Ghana.MethodsThis school-based cross-sectional study employed multistage sampling to administer reliable questionnaires (on the frequency of NVDD usage, ergonomic factors and computer vision syndrome symptoms) to 326 high school students. The PEAV were assessed over maximum plus for the best-corrected visual acuity refractive correction.ResultsThe duration of usage of NVDD correlated with deteriorations in: measures of latent ocular squints (near heterophoria (rs=0.360, p=0.0001)); eye’s ability to converge (near point of convergence (rs=0.369, p=0.0001)); ability to change between convergence and divergence (vergence facility (rs=−0.340, p=0.0001)); ability to compensate for near squints (negative fusional vergence break (rs=−0.352, p=0.0001) and positive fusional vergence break (rs=−0.322, p=0.0001) at near); refractive power to accommodate (accommodative amplitude (rs=−0.340, p=0.0001)); ability to switch between near and distance targets (monocular accommodative facility (rs=−0.377, p=0.0001)) and response to focusing a target (accommodative response (rs=0.382, p=0.0001)).ConclusionUsing NVDD for more than two hours a day correlated with clinically significant deterioration in the PEAV.

Introduction

Following technological advancements, near visual display devices (NVDDs), such as computing and communication tools, have become crucial in our modern-day activities. These devices have evolved from immobile desktops to mobile laptops and pocket devices, such as mobile phones and tablets. It was projected that by the end of the year 2018, 84% of people worldwide would be using computers and mobile phones.1 Again, 69.4% of people worldwide specifically use mobile phones, as stated in a digital 2024 global overview report.2 In Ghana, 54.1% of citizens who are 5 years and older own a functional mobile phone, and 7.9% use different forms of computers.3

The key factors influencing the comfortable usage of near digital devices include computer vision syndrome (CVS), ergonomic practice (EP) and binocularity. CVS (characterised as a public health challenge)4 describes a myriad of visual symptoms associated with the usage of near digital devices.5 The digital eyestrain associated with the usage of these near digital devices might be brought on by variables connected to visual and workplace ergonomics.6 Studies7 8 report adverse ocular consequences caused by using near digital devices with high-resolution screens and wrong viewing distances. Again, the parameters of the eye’s accommodation and vergence (PEAV), key components of the binocular vision system (BVS), must function optimally to enhance comfortable usage of NVDDs. The vergence system aligns both eyes together on the target on the device. The accommodative system resolves the image on both retinas for interpretation. Accommodative and vergence disorders are non-strabismic binocular vision anomalies (NSBVA) that adversely affect comfort and the effectiveness of visual performance with near work.9 The asthenopia complaints by near digital device users have underlying visual functional aetiologies, specifically instability of the BVS.10

Some studies1113 reported a significant increase in lag of accommodation after the use of near digital devices. Other studies found a decrease in accommodative amplitude (AA),11 14 15 fusional convergence,11 12 near point of convergence (NPC),11 12 16 relative accommodation,12 15 vergence facility (VF)12 and accommodative facility15 17 18 after periods of near digital device use. All the studies1218 were conducted in non-African populations. The review article11 recommended that more studies be performed among high near digital device users, like teenagers, especially considering accommodative and binocular vision anomalies.

The association between the use of NVDDs, CVS and EP, with NSBVA, however, remains to be investigated. Again, despite the BVS being significantly important in NVDDs use, the relationship between the usage of these devices and associated factors (CVS and EP) and NSBVA needs clarification. Furthermore, the correlation between the duration of usage of NVDDs and the PEAV needs elucidation to inform patient advice and guide interventions in the Ghanaian population. As the previous studies1218 were conducted in non-African populations, there is a need to investigate these associations in African populations as well. This is especially so as there are known population differences in vision parameters19 due to the variances in the eye’s anatomy.20 The present study sought to determine the association between the usage of NVDDs and their associated factors (namely CVS and EP), and the PEAV of junior high school (JHS) children in Cape Coast, Ghana.

Materials and methods

Ethical consideration

The study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki on the use of human research subjects. It was approved by the University of Cape Coast Institutional Review Board (reference: UCCIRB/CHAS/2023/49). Parents or guardians of students gave written informed consent, and students gave assent to participate in the study.

Study design, population and sampling

This school-based cross-sectional study was conducted among JHS students aged 13–17 years old in the Cape Coast metropolis, Ghana, from September 2023 to January 2024. Estimating from a pilot study that a proportion, p of 75% of the high school students used NVDDs, and a deviation, d of 5% at a 95% CI and using the Cochran formula, n=(Z²xp (1−p))/d² and considering an attrition rate of 10%, the minimum sample size was determined to be 317. The multistage sampling technique adopted involved the following stages: (1) clustering high schools into two, namely Cape Coast North and Cape Coast South. (2) Simple random sampling of two mixed schools from within each cluster. (3) Stratified random sampling of JHS children 13–17 years old in each school by gender. (4) Simple random sampling (picking folded sheets with yes inscriptions) of not <50 JHS children in each of eight (8) strata for the study.

Data collection procedures

Oculo-visual examination: this involved measuring visual acuity using the Bailey-Lovie logMAR chart to determine how well the eyes can see, determining ocular deviation using the unilateral cover tests, examining the external and internal eye using a handheld slit lamp and a direct ophthalmoscope, respectively, and determining the refractive status using trial lens sets in subjective refraction.

Exclusion criteria: participants with ocular diseases, unilateral blindness, squint, amblyopia (lazy eye), nystagmus (eyes with uncontrolled repetitive movements) and unco-operative or unreliable responses were excluded from the study.

Questionnaire administration: a structured, reliable questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha, 0.807) with four sections (demographics, usage of visual display terminals, EPs and a validated CVS questionnaire (CVS-Q))21 was administered to participants who passed the oculovisual examination. On the questionnaire, participants who indicated they used NVDDs were asked to indicate the number of hours, categorised into low (1–3 hours), moderate (4–6 hours) and high (≥7 hours), and the specific NVDDs (desktops, laptops, tablets and phones) they mostly used within a day. The section of the questionnaire on EP focused primarily on the principles of ergonomics that the NVDD users practised while using the near digital device. Participants who correctly practised >6 and ≤6 out of 9 ergonomic parameters measured were classified into the ‘good EP’ and ‘poor EP’ categories, respectively.22 The CVS-Q contained a total of 16 symptoms associated with CVS.21 The intensity of each symptom was graded as moderate (1) and intense (2); the frequency was graded as never (0), occasionally (1) and always (2). The score was obtained by multiplying the sum of the 16 grades of frequency of occurrence by the sum of the 16 grades of intensity of symptoms.14 Any NVDD user who scored ≥6 points was classified into the ‘CVS Present’ group as indicated by the ‘CVS-Q’.21

Assessing non-strabismic BVS: binocular vision assessments were performed over the participant’s optimum refractive correction that gives the best visual acuity (Maximun Plus for Best Corrected Visual Acuity). The examination included measuring: (1) the heterophorias (latent ocular squints) using alternating cover test with prism bar neutralisation at near distance; (2) the two eyes’ ability to compensate for near squints and fuse to maintain single vision (positive fusional vergence (PFV) and negative fusional vergence (NFV) reserves) at near using prism bar step vergence testing; (3) the two eyes’ ability to converge on near target and diverge on distance target (VF) using 3 base-in /12 base-out flipper prisms; (4) the eye’s maximum ability to converge (NPC) using the ‘line with dot target’ of the Royal Air Force (RAF) rule; (5) the eye’s maximum refractive power to focus images at near distance onto the retina (AA) using N.6 letter target on the RAF rule; (6) the eye’s ability to change focus to see near and distance targets (accommodative facility testing) using ±2D flipper lenses; (7) the eye’s response to seeing a target at near distance (accommodative response) using the dynamic retinoscope monocular estimation method. To avoid interexaminer variability in results, one examiner performed only one procedure for all participants throughout the study. Again, each examiner used standardised instructional sets in examining the participants to prevent intraexaminer variability. NSBVA was diagnosed based on Scheiman and Wick’s integrative analysis criteria.23 The measured PEAV were compared with normative values for high school children in Ghana.24 25

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Windows V.22.0.1 software was used to analyse the data. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk p values. All descriptive data were summarised into tables using frequencies, mean±1 SD and percentiles with interquartile ranges. Multivariate binary logistic regression was used to determine associations between categorical data. Spearman’s ρ correlation was used to determine the relationship between continuous variables. Statistically significant associations were determined if the p value was <0.05.

Patient or public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

A total of 390 JHS children were recruited into the study. Of this number, 42 with conditions, namely strabismus (6), ocular diseases (22), unilateral blindness (3), amblyopia (2), nystagmus (2) and uncooperative or unreliable responses (7) were excluded. Some 22 participants did not complete the testing. The final sample (326), comprising 141 (43.3%) males and 185 (56.7%) females, with a mean age of 15.23±1.31 years, answered the questionnaires and underwent the complete binocular vision assessment. The frequency of participants who use the NVDD was 188 (57.7%). Of the 188 participants, 63 (19.3%), 73 (22.4%) and 52 (16.0%) indicated they used the near digital devices within 1–3 hours (low), 4 to 6 hours (moderate) and more than 6 hours (high), respectively, in a day. The prevalence of CVS and poor EP among participants who use near digital devices was 82.4% and 64.4%, respectively. The prevalence of NSBVA was 34.7% among study participants (326) and comprised convergence insufficiency (10.4%), pseudo convergence insufficiency (8.6%), fusional vergence dysfunction (6.1%), accommodative insufficiency (4.9%), accommodative infacility (3.1%) and accommodative excess (1.2%). Among only participants (188) who use NVDDs, the prevalence of NSBVA was 46.3% (convergence insufficiency (13.4%), pseudo-convergence insufficiency (11.6%), fusional vergence dysfunction (8.1%), accommodative insufficiency (6.9%), accommodative infacility (4.1%) and accommodative excess (1.8%)). Discussion

The increased duration of use of NVDDs within the day was associated with a recession in the PEAV. As the near heterophorias increased with the prolonged use of NVDDs, the fusional vergence reserves (expected to compensate for these deviations) decreased. This may be associated with a decompensated heterophoria, increasing demand for the BVS and causing visual strain and asthenopia. The asthenopia may worsen with the reduced ability to converge (receded NPC) and decreased ability to stimulate vergence dynamics (decreased VF) in focusing a target on an NVDD screen. In the accommodative system, an increased lag of accommodation, reduced AA and AF, and decreased relative accommodation may impede the visual system’s ability to resolve and focus a target on NVDD screens. Any effort to stimulate accommodation may put a strain on the visual system, resulting in visual fatigue and asthenopia.

The recession in the PEAV observed among JHS students in the present study is similar to that in an experimental study on Korean University students,15 which found significant declines in AF and AA after 2 hours of using a visual display terminal. Several other experimental studies121416 reported declines in PEAV after participants actively used near digital devices for periods ranging from 20 min to 1 hour within a day. All these previous studies were conducted among young adults111416 from different populations, namely, Spain,13 Iran,27 India,12 28 Korea,14 16 17 Australia18 and Thailand.26 Also, previous studies1214 16 18 2931 found declines in PEAV for specific devices—such as smartphones, tablets or computers. This present study, however, defines NVDD use as engaging with one or more devices such as smartphones, tablets, laptops or desktop computers. This was because most participants reported using multiple near digital devices in a single day. When this occurred, the durations for using each device within the day were combined. Although there are differences in target distances between NVDDs, some studies13 14 17 30 observed no significant difference in increased accommodative lag between smartphone and tablet use. Other research26 31 reported no differences in parameters such as accommodative lag, NRA, PRA and AA when comparing reading on tablets versus printed tasks31 or using tablets versus computers.26 The symptoms experienced with computer usage often overlap with those reported by mobile phone and tablet users.11

The present study justifies clinically significant deteriorations in PEAV after more than 2 hours of use of NVDDs. The significant recession in the various parameters after 4 hours of use of NVDDs doubled after 8 hours each day (table 2B). For example, differences in latent ocular deviations of 2∆ and 4∆ after 4 hours and 8 hours of NVDD use, respectively, are clinically meaningful. Again, a reduction in PFV reserves at near of 3∆ and 6∆ after 4 hours and 8 hours of NVDDs use, respectively, is clinically meaningful. Furthermore, a recession in NPC of 2 cm and 4 cm after 4 hours and 8 hours of NVDD use, respectively, is clinically meaningful. These changes are clinically meaningful as the differences are more than the SD for the specific normative data for the Ghanaian children.24 25 Moreover, these changes can lead to decompensation of the fusional reserves, leading to eye fatigue and eyestrain. The recession in the PEAV within <2 hours of use of NVDDs was not clinically meaningful (table 2B) and comparable to other studies.14 29 32 This may suggest that using NVDDs for <2 hours may not be associated with strain on the BVS. It is, however, likely that the cumulative effect of the prolonged use of NVDDs each day for several days may negatively impact the BVS. This may be so as a study33 reported negative correlations between binocular AF with accruing duration of near work over months, whether NVDDs-related or print-related.

The recession in the PEAV after prolonged use of NVDDs was further supported when participants with NSBVA were found to have used the NVDDs for significantly longer periods than those without anomalies (table 2A). Again, participants with CVS and those with poor EP were significantly associated with NSBVA. Persons with NSBVA would experience visual fatigue with prolonged NVDD usage and may likely show signs and symptoms of CVS. Adverse EP would simply be an additive negative factor. The poor lighting conditions, improper positioning, poor viewing angle, intense glare, low-resolution NVDD screens and prolonged exposure to NVDDs may expose the visual system to frequent blur. This may put a greater demand on the BVS and may result in ocular fatigue and subsequent recession of the PEAV. The strain to focus clearly may exacerbate postural problems and CVS-related asthenopia.

The questionnaire used in this study gathered information about participants’ ongoing habitual use of NVDDs and the duration of use each day. The changes in the parameters may reflect alterations in the visual system due to continuous digital near stress over the years of NVDD use. Longer durations of NVDD use are associated with frequent long saccadic movements, ongoing accommodation adjustments and persistent vergence changes.34 These factors have been linked to symptoms of asthenopia, such as headaches.6 34 The neurophysiological mechanism behind the prolonged near stress caused by NVDD use may involve the parasympathetic nervous system, which mediates excessive contraction of the ciliary muscles, making it difficult to relax the crystalline lens during accommodation.35 The accommodative-convergence reflex becomes overactive and fixed, leading to pseudomyopia (where the eye adapts to near vision and struggles to relax for distance focus), spasms of near reflexes and binocular vision problems.36 Overstimulation of this near vision system may cause asthenopia symptoms and negatively affect academic performance.

The authors, however, acknowledge the limitations of administering questionnaires to elicit information on NVDD usage. There may have been recall bias in the self-reported screen time or symptoms. Again, various optometric tests performed required participants’ subjective responses. There may be participant response biases during such subjective testing. These tests, however, are standard optometric investigative tests for NSBVA routinely performed in clinics. Furthermore, the cross-sectional study design sought to determine correlations, and the results do not imply causation. This protocol adopted (validated questionnaire administration and vision examination) is novel as it elicited answers to the continuous use of near digital devices and their associated factors, namely CVS and poor EP, and the association with the parameters of the visual system. Future confirmatory studies for this population should adopt an experimental design to measure these parameters over time to imply causation.

Conclusion

NVDD use with CVS and poor EP was associated with the presence of NSBVA in JHS children in Cape Coast, Ghana. More than 2 hours of use of NVDDs within the day was associated with clinically significant deterioration or recession in the PEAV. Good EP and reducing time for NVDDs use within the day may relax the muscles of the visual system, prevent deterioration of the PEAV and relieve asthenopia resulting from CVS and NSBVA.
