Mechanobiology of CAR-T cell therapy: regulatory mechanisms and strategies for enhanced antitumor immunity
Authors: Ya Li, Chensi Zeng, Sanxiu He, Qing Xiao, Yi Liu, Xuejiao Shu, Xiaoqing Xie, Yaxiao Lu, Huihui Fu, Jun Li, Chunyan Xiao, Jing Wu, Yao Liu, Xiaomei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy is a next generation precision immunotherapy that engineers a patient’s own T cells to express synthetic CARs, thereby augmenting tumour cell recognition and cytotoxic activity. Despite transformative clinical success in haematologic cancers, nearly half of treated patients relapse or fail to respond, and the translation of CAR-T therapy to solid tumours remains substantially more challenging. Increasing evidence shows that biomechanical forces, including stretch, compression, shear stress, and extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness, shape immune activation, trafficking, and effector function through mechanotransduction pathways, ultimately modulating immune responses and disease evolution. Biomechanical cues critically influence CAR-T function, governing target recognition, activation dynamics, and cytotoxic engagement. Furthermore, the mechanical landscape of the tumour microenvironment shapes T cell infiltration, persistence, and exhaustion, thereby constraining CAR-T efficacy. These insights have fueled growing interest in biomechanically informed strategies to optimize CAR-T therapies. Here, we review the biomechanical principles governing CAR-T antitumour responses and highlight how ECM rigidity, shear forces, and other mechanical cues shape CAR-T performance. Biomechanics informed strategies that enhance CAR-T cell antitumour immunity offer a novel conceptual framework for advancing precision cancer immunotherapy. Elucidating how CAR-T cells sense and adapt to the mechanical tumour microenvironment will guide the design of next generation products and accelerate their translation into solid tumour indications.

Introduction

Cancer is a major cause of mortality worldwide, and the treatment of hematologic malignancies continues to face substantial clinical challenges. Conventional modalities, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, have yielded therapeutic benefit but are often limited by significant toxicity and the development of drug resistance. In recent years, CAR-T cell immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative approach, demonstrating striking efficacy and delivering breakthrough outcomes in hematologic cancers. CAR-T therapy operates by genetically modifying a patient’s T cells ex vivo to express synthetic receptors capable of recognizing tumour-associated antigens with high specificity. The engineered receptors permit T cells to identify tumour associated antigens, triggering robust immune activation and targeted cytotoxicity. By endowing T cells with antigen-specific recognition, CAR-T therapy achieves potent and selective elimination of malignant cells [1, 2]. At the heart of CAR-T therapy is the synthetic chimeric antigen receptor, a modular construct composed of several essential functional domains [3, 4]: (1) Antigen binding ectodomain: typically derived from a monoclonal antibody variable region, enabling precise recognition of tumour antigens such as CD19 or BCMA (B-cell maturation antigen). Its MHC (Major Histocompatibility Complex) independent recognition circumvents mechanisms of immune escape driven by tumoural MHC downregulation. (2) Transmembrane domain: stabilizes CAR anchoring within the T cell membrane and may heterodimerize with endogenous partners, affecting receptor expression, rigidity, and signalling behaviour. (3) Intracellular signalling module: consisting of CD3ζ activation motifs and co-stimulatory elements such as CD28 or 4-1BB, incorporating several immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) that initiate and amplify downstream signalling. Engagement of cognate antigen triggers ITAM phosphorylation and downstream signalling cascades, leading to robust T cell activation, expansion, and acquisition of effector functions [5, 6]. CAR-T cells eradicate malignant cells through coordinated mechanisms involving direct cytotoxic engagement, cytokine mediated amplification, and long term immunological memory. Upon activation, CAR-T cells secrete perforin, granzymes, and other cytotoxic mediators that initiate programmed death of tumour cells. A fraction of activated CAR-T cells transitions into long lived memory states, enabling durable persistence and rapid reactivation. Upon antigen re-encounter, memory CAR-T cells mount rapid activation and proliferative responses, generating potent effector populations that confer durable, antigen specific tumour immunity [7–10].

Since the FDA’s approval of the first CAR-T therapy in 2017, the field has advanced rapidly, yielding complete remission rates exceeding 40% in hematologic malignancies such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia and diffuse large B cell lymphoma [11–14]. Nevertheless, substantial challenges remain regarding both the safety and durability of CAR-T therapy. The principal safety concerns stem from excessive immune activation, which can precipitate severe and potentially life threatening toxicities. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS), the most frequent CAR-T associated toxicity, results from explosive T cell expansion and massive cytokine production such as IL-6, IFN-γ, TNF-α, leading to fever, hypotension, hypoxia, and, in severe cases, multi-organ failure [15]. Excessive cytokine release and disruption of the blood–brain barrier can give rise to immune effector cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [16, 17]. Furthermore, CAR-T therapy can induce prolonged cytopenias, including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. CD19 directed CAR-T products further eliminate normal B cells, leading to B cell aplasia and heightened susceptibility to infection and relapse [18, 19]. Additional toxicities include tumor lysis syndrome, hypersensitivity reactions, and the potential development of secondary malignancies [20, 21]. Therapeutically, limited CAR-T persistence and the onset of T-cell exhaustion compromise antitumour efficacy and increase the risk of relapse [22]. These factors likely contribute to the approximately 50% complete remission rate observed in real world clinical cohorts [23]. In solid tumors, CAR-T effectiveness is further hindered by the scarcity of optimal antigens, profound hypoxia within tumor cores, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, elevated oxidative stress, and reduced T cell infiltration. The solid tumor microenvironment, characterized by disorganized vasculature, a rigid and fibrotic ECM, increased interstitial pressure, hypoxia, and abundant immunosuppressive cells, presents formidable barriers to CAR-T infiltration and activity [24–26]. Moreover, pronounced antigenic heterogeneity and the scarcity of uniformly expressed tumour antigens further limit CAR-T applicability in solid cancers [27]. Improving therapeutic potency while minimizing toxicity continues to be a major priority in CAR-T research.

Genetic engineering strategies that enable CAR-T cells to express specific cytokines such as IL-12, IL-15 and IL-18, downregulate immune checkpoint pathways or knock out key molecules interfering with therapeutic efficacy, as well as optimization of ex vivo expansion systems to regulate epigenetics [28] and signaling pathways [29], can increase the proportion of stem-like memory CAR-T subsets and reduce exhaustion, thereby enhancing their antitumor activity in vivo [30–34]. These efforts largely centre on biochemical cues that shape CAR-T cell behaviour. Beyond biochemical signalling, physiological and pathological processes are governed by multiscale biomechanical regulation operating from the molecular to the organ level. The key biomechanical features of the tumour microenvironment encompass ECM deposition induced matrix stiffening and topographical remodelling, shear forces and interstitial fluid pressure generated by blood and tissue fluid flow, and solid stress or mechanical strain resulting from excessive proliferative activity. These biomechanical cues not only shape tumour cell invasiveness but also affect immune cell survival, infiltration and effector function. Moreover, the mechanical properties of cytotoxic T cells and tumour cells, such as stiffness and viscoelasticity, critically modulate T cell cytolytic performance [35–37]. Based on these insights, this review delineates the biomechanical principles governing CAR-T cell antitumour function and highlights emerging strategies that exploit mechanical regulation to enhance CAR-T therapy. This review particularly focuses on the mechanisms by which CAR-T cells sense extracellular mechanical signals, the molecular pathways that convert mechanical stimuli into intracellular functional programs, and the impact of these processes on CAR-T cell migration, persistence, functional exhaustion, and tumor cytotoxicity.

Biomechanics in CAR-T cell-mediated tumor elimination

During CAR-T cell mediated antitumor responses, both the physical interactions between CAR-T cells and target cells and the mechanical properties of the tumor microenvironment are critical determinants of therapeutic efficacy. The antitumor process mediated by CAR-T cells mainly comprises four stages: cell migration, target cell recognition, cellular activation, and tumor cell killing [38] (Fig. 1). The structural components of the CAR, including the single-chain variable fragment (scFv), hinge region, transmembrane domain, costimulatory domains, and CD3ζ ITAM modules, not only define the specificity of antigen recognition, but also regulate the mode of force transmission after receptor antigen binding, the architecture of the immune synapse, and downstream signaling initiation. Accordingly, the CAR may be considered a key mechanoregulatory module that couples external mechanical cues with intracellular functional responses.

At the migratory stage, T cells predominantly employ an amoeboid mode of migration within dense extracellular matrices, characterized by membrane protrusion, cortical actomyosin contraction, and transient traction forces independent of matrix degradation [39, 40]. This mode of migration critically depends on cellular deformability, particularly nuclear deformability, as the nucleus represents the major mechanical constraint and its stiffness substantially influences migration efficiency. Previous studies demonstrated that elevated nuclear stiffness impairs rapid migration in three dimensional spaces while enhancing cell adhesion, spreading, and stress fiber assembly, ultimately diminishing cellular motility [41, 42]. In solid tumors, excessive deposition and crosslinking of ECM components, particularly collagen, enhance tissue compactness and generate physical barriers to immune-cell infiltration. For instance, in pancreatic cancer, dense collagen architectures remain capable of restricting T cell infiltration even in the presence of high Chemokine Ligand 10 (CXCL10) or Chemokine Ligand 4 (CCL4) expression [43]. Collectively, the migratory behavior of T cells is governed not only by external mechanical confinement but also by their intrinsic biomechanical adaptability.

During the target cell recognition stage, CAR-T cells directly recognize tumor-associated surface antigens through CARs independently of MHC presentation [44]. Unlike the native TCR (T Cell Receptor), the modular architecture of the CAR not only transduces biochemical signals but also determines the force transmission pathway following antigen engagement. The affinity and binding kinetics between the scFv and antigen influence the stability of the initial mechanical coupling; the length and flexibility of the hinge region regulate synaptic spacing; and the transmembrane and intracellular domains affect the transmission of external forces to intracellular signaling machinery. From a biomechanical perspective, intracellular domains may represent critical regulatory nodes in CAR signal transduction. Using DNA-based tension probes, interaction forces of approximately 8–19 pN with a duration of around 1 s have been detected during CAR–antigen engagement [45]. This mechanical interaction is jointly regulated by scFv affinity and costimulatory domains and depends on the integrity of the CD3ζITAM motifs; deletion of the costimulatory sequence or ITAM modules markedly reduces force transmission [45, 46]. These findings suggest that intracellular CAR modules not only initiate phosphorylation signaling following receptor clustering but also directly participate in the early mechanical responses during antigen recognition. The CAR mediated recognition process may also partially follow the“kinetic segregation model”whereby spatial exclusion at the synaptic interface removes inhibitory molecules with large extracellular domains, such as CD45, from the contact region, thereby lowering the activation threshold [47]. In this context, the length and flexibility of the hinge region influence synaptic gap dimensions and determine whether inhibitory phosphatases can be efficiently excluded. Further studies demonstrated that CAR molecules containing only costimulatory domains are capable of transmitting detectable forces of 4.7–19 pN, and that this mechanical event occurs prior to intracellular Ca²⁺ release and is highly colocalized with phosphorylation sites of canonical TCR signaling machinery [46].

At the stage of T cell activation, mechanical cues are further integrated with biochemical signaling pathways. At the level of the immune synapse (IS), CAR-T cells typically form nonclassical synaptic architectures distinct from those of native T cells. Unlike the central supramolecular activation cluster (cSMAC) surrounded by an LFA-1 (Lymphocyte Function-associated Antigen 1) ring in conventional TCR synapses, CAR-T cell synapses are more dispersed and lack the characteristic LFA-1 ring; CAR clusters are often organized into multifocal Lck (Lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinase) microclusters, resulting in faster initiation and attenuation of CAR signaling compared with endogenous TCR signaling [48, 49]. Intracellular design elements, particularly the number and type of ITAMs, are also important determinants regulating CAR-T cell fate. The 4-1BB costimulatory domain contributes to enhanced F-actin (Filamentous actin) polarization and improved synapse stability [50]. ITAMs are essential for maintaining basal force transmission, whereas moderate reduction in ITAM number can attenuate signaling strength, thereby regulating T cell activation thresholds and differentiation fate [45, 51, 52]. The long term functionality of CAR-T cells is also regulated by the biophysical properties of the receptor. CD28ζ CARs typically induce faster and stronger phosphorylation responses and are associated with effector like transcriptional profiles, whereas 4-1BBζ CAR signaling is more moderate and preferentially maintains memory-associated gene expression [53]. In terms of membrane organization, CD28ζ CARs tend to form larger and denser microclusters, whereas 4-1BB CARs generate smaller but more numerous and rapidly diffusing oligomeric microclusters, thereby affecting CD3ζ and pLck phosphorylation efficiency, immune synapse stability, and signaling fidelity [54]. In addition, instability within the scFv structure may trigger spontaneous CAR aggregation and tonic signaling, ultimately driving functional exhaustion of CAR-T cells [55]. Moderate reduction in ITAM number can attenuate signaling strength and improve CAR-T cell persistence [52].

During the effector phase, CAR-T cells eliminate target cells by forming cytotoxic immune synapses and releasing perforin and granzymes. Cytotoxic efficiency depends on the quality of the synapse established during the preceding recognition and activation stages, and the influence of CAR design on synaptic mechanical stability is ultimately translated into functional output. Studies have shown that inhibition of the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1 enhances T cell traction force and increases cytotoxicity, suggesting that mechanosensory pathways participate in regulating killing function [56]. The membrane insertion efficiency of perforin is also closely associated with the mechanical state of the cell [57]. To enhance synaptic mechanical stability, studies have introduced PDZ (PSD-95 / Dlg / ZO-1 domain) binding motifs into CAR constructs, thereby strengthening receptor interactions with intracellular scaffold proteins, increasing synaptic polarization and force transmission, and ultimately improving cytotoxic efficiency [58]. Meanwhile, activated T cells downregulate Filamin A to maintain lower cellular stiffness and avoid self inflicted damage [59]. However, tumor cells can also evade immune surveillance by reducing their own stiffness [60, 61], and can further restrict immune cell infiltration by promoting (Discoidin Domain Receptor 1) DDR1-mediated collagen fiber alignment to form dense physical barriers [62].

In summary, CAR-T cell-mediated antitumor responses are dynamic processes jointly regulated by multilevel mechanical factors. Cell migration determines whether CAR-T cells can traverse the solid tumor matrix and reach target cells; the target recognition stage is governed by CAR architecture, which determines force transmission and activation thresholds; the activation stage is characterized by nonclassical immune synapse organization, microcluster dynamics, and regulation of signaling strength; and the tumor killing stage translates these events into perforin/granzyme release and target cell elimination. The specificity of CARs is not limited to antigen recognition but extends across force sensing, synaptic architecture, activation dynamics, exhaustion propensity, and cytotoxic efficiency. Future optimization of CAR structural design and mechanosensory properties is expected to enhance CAR-T cell infiltration into solid tumors, improve cytotoxic efficacy, and sustain long term functional persistence.

Mechanical cues in tumor microenvironment shaping CAR-T cell function

The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment constitutes a critical barrier to the efficacy of CAR-T cell immunotherapy. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem comprising all nonmalignant cellular components and their secreted products within tumor tissues. The TME encompasses diverse cellular constituents, including immune cells, fibroblasts, pericytes, adipocytes, and peripheral nerve cells, together with vascular networks (blood and lymphatic vessels) and noncellular components such as the extracellular matrix, cytokines, and metabolic metabolites [63]. Rather than serving as a passive bystander, the TME dynamically interacts with tumor cells and actively participates in the regulation of tumorigenesis, progression, invasion, metastasis, and treatment responsiveness [64]. Earlier investigations of the TME primarily emphasized biochemical characteristics, including hypoxia, acidic pH, inflammation, and immunosuppressive signaling, while the contribution of biomechanical cues has remained relatively underexplored. Biomechanical factors within the TME encompass enhanced extracellular matrix stiffness, topological remodeling of the extracellular matrix, solid stress and tensile strain caused by rapid tumor proliferation, and shear stress arising from interstitial fluid flow and vascular systems [65]. Accumulating evidence indicates that T cells actively perceive physical and mechanical cues from their microenvironment and adapt their biological behaviors accordingly, including proliferation, migration, activation, and effector responses [66]. Elucidating how diverse biomechanical factors within the TME regulate CAR-T cell behavior, together with the underlying mechanosensory mechanisms of CAR-T cells, will be essential for the development of biomechanically engineered interventions aimed at overcoming current bottlenecks in CAR-T therapy. Matrix stiffness

In the tumor microenvironment (TME), the extracellular matrix (ECM) not only provides structural support for tissues but also plays important roles in immune cell infiltration and tumor immune regulation [67]. The biomechanical properties of the ECM primarily encompass matrix stiffness and topological organization, with stiffness being largely determined by the density, spatial arrangement, and crosslinking of collagen, fibronectin, proteoglycans, and related components, and typically quantified by Young’s modulus [68, 69]. In multiple solid tumors, ECM stiffening is a characteristic pathological feature mainly caused by increased collagen deposition and enhanced crosslinking mediated by the lysyl oxidase (LOX) family, and it undergoes continuous remodeling during tumor progression. Previous studies have shown that increased matrix stiffness not only promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance, but also profoundly affects immune cell infiltration, activation, and functional maintenance [70, 71].

Matrix stiffness-mediated immunosuppression is mainly manifested at two levels: on the one hand, it acts as a physical barrier restricting immune cell infiltration into tumor tissues; on the other hand, it serves as a mechanical signal input that alters immune cell functional states through mechanosensing and signal transduction. First, as a physical barrier, aberrant ECM deposition and elevated solid stress in solid tumors together constitute an important structural basis restricting immune cell infiltration [72]. Dense collagen networks and enhanced crosslinking can directly impede the migration of effector cells such as T cells into tumor tissues, while simultaneously promoting the release of multiple inhibitory signals that further establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment [73–75]. Salvador et al. found that Serpine1 maintains matrix density by inhibiting ECM degradation, and its high expression in colorectal cancer is negatively correlated with T cell infiltration, suggesting a close association between ECM remodeling and immune exclusion [76]. To address this issue, studies have attempted to weaken the matrix barrier through engineered approaches. Tang et al. used digital light processing (DLP)-based bioprinting to construct three dimensional glioblastoma (GBM) models with varying stiffness, and found that increased material stiffness significantly reduced the penetration capacity and cytotoxicity of CAR-T cells [77]. Furthermore, thermally inducible glycoprotein 2 (GD2)-targeted CAR-T cells were engineered to upregulate CAR expression under controllable heating conditions, partially overcoming the suppressive effects of high stiffness environments on CAR-T function, as evidenced by enhanced tumor penetration together with downregulation of mTORC1 (mammalian Target Of Rapamycin Complex 1) and hypoxia related signaling. In a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) model, Xiao et al. employed fibroblast activation protein (FAP)-targeted CAR-T cells to eliminate FAP⁺cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which not only disrupted the dense stromal structure and alleviated barriers to T cell infiltration, but also enhanced the antitumor activity of Meso-CAR T cells and PD-1 (Programmed cell death protein 1) antibodies, accompanied by increased recruitment of CD8⁺T cells and NK cells, reduced immunosuppressive myeloid cells, and decreased TGF-βlevels [78]. Beyond macroscopic structural barriers, mechanical confinement within three-dimensional microenvironments can directly affect immune synapse formation and impair cytotoxic function [79]. Meanwhile, matrix components themselves also possess immunomodulatory properties. Studies have shown that type I and III collagen as well as elastin can suppress NK cell cytotoxicity and alter their secretory profiles, whereas reducing tumor collagen deposition can restore NK cell killing activity against MHC-I-deficient melanoma [80].

Beyond serving as a physical obstacle to T-cell infiltration, matrix stiffness itself acts as an autonomous biomechanical signal that directly modulates the activation, differentiation, and long term functional maintenance of both T cells and CAR-T cells. During immune synapse formation, the mechanical properties of the antigen-presenting interface can be directly sensed by the TCR/CD3 complex, CD28, and integrin receptors such as LFA-1 [81]. Upon contact with substrates of elevated or physiologically optimal stiffness, T cells experience increased resistance to actomyosin-generated contractile forces, which enhances the mechanical tension applied to receptor ligand complexes such as TCR–pMHC, prolongs bond lifetime, and facilitates the stable formation of catch bonds [82]. This mechanical force generated by the interplay between cytoskeletal traction and matrix resistance further induces conformational changes in mechanosensitive adaptor proteins such as Talin and Crk-associated substrate lymphocyte-type (CasL), promotes immune synapse microcluster stabilization and phosphorylation of proximal kinases including Lck and ZAP-70 (Zeta-chain-associated protein kinase 70 (kDa)), and subsequently amplifies Ca²⁺influx and the nuclear translocation signaling of transcription factors such as NFAT (Nuclear Factor of Activated T cells) and AP-1 (Activator protein 1) [83]. Ultimately, this mechanotransduction process can significantly upregulate IL-2 expression, enhance the proliferation and effector differentiation of T cells and CAR-T cells, and promote the differentiation of CD4⁺T cells toward an IFN-γ secreting Th1-like phenotype [81]. Notably, T cell responses to matrix stiffness exhibit a pronounced biphasic effect: at intermediate stiffness levels close to the physiological range, cytoskeletal tension and T cell activation are optimal, whereas both proliferation and effector functions decline under extremely soft or excessively stiff conditions [82]. In addition, YAP (Yes-associated protein), as a key mechanosensitive transcriptional regulator, also participates in the mechanical regulation of T cell metabolism and activation, and its activation enhances the metabolic adaptation and functional maintenance of effector T cells [84].

However, under pathological conditions such as solid tumors, persistently elevated ECM stiffness often converts this physiological mechanosensing process into an immunosuppressive signal. The high stiffness environment generated by aberrant collagen deposition and enhanced crosslinking can be continuously sensed by integrins and the TCR complex, thereby inducing increased actomyosin cytoskeletal contraction and traction force generation in T cells, which subsequently activates mechanotransduction pathways such as RhoA/ROCK (Ras homolog family member A/Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase) [68, 85, 86]. Previous studies have demonstrated that hyperactivated YAP signaling within the tumor microenvironment suppresses T cell infiltration and cytotoxic function, disrupts immune synapse stability, and ultimately promotes T cell exhaustion [68, 87]. Similarly, Zhang et al. further demonstrated that high stiffness matrices induce Piezo1-mediated Ca²⁺influx, activate CaMKII/CREB (Calcium-dependent protein kinase II/cAMP response element-binding protein) signaling, and promote Odd-skipped related transcription factor 2 (Osr2) expression; subsequently, Osr2 suppresses the transcription of cytotoxicity-associated genes through recruitment of HDAC3 (Histone deacetylase 3), thereby driving CD8⁺T cells toward a terminally exhausted state [88]. These findings suggest that tumor associated mechanical signals not only directly impair the cytotoxic function of T cells and CAR-T cells, but also drive transcriptional and epigenetic reprogramming through persistent mechanical stimulation, thereby establishing a stable exhausted state.

In addition to directly affecting T cells, pathological matrix stiffening can further restrict T cell function by reshaping immunosuppressive myeloid cells. In highly fibrotic tumors such as breast cancer, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), upon sensing ECM stiffening, can initiate a mechano-metabolic reprogramming pathway characterized by enhanced collagen synthesis and dysregulated amino acid metabolism. Specifically, TAMs consume large amounts of arginine and release ornithine, which not only further promotes collagen deposition and stromal fibrosis, but also establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by nutrient deprivation and accumulation of inhibitory metabolites, ultimately leading to CD8⁺T cell dysfunction and immune exclusion [89].

Collectively, elevated matrix stiffness in the tumor microenvironment may impair CAR-T cell antitumor efficacy through two major mechanisms: first, by establishing a physical barrier that restricts CAR-T cell infiltration; and second, by activating mechanosensory signaling pathways mediated by integrins, Piezo1, YAP, and related molecules, thereby directly driving metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming of T cells and promoting their activation-associated exhaustion. In addition, elevated ECM stiffness can indirectly modulate T cell effector functions through macrophage mediated immunoregulatory mechanisms. Given these multifaceted effects, therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating pathological matrix stiffness are increasingly being explored as promising approaches to enhance CAR-T cell efficacy.

Matrix topography

The ECM topology within the tumor microenvironment, characterized by features such as fiber organization, pore dimensions, and network density, is highly heterogeneous. Distinct tumor types, as well as different regions within the same tumor, frequently exhibit diverse patterns of fiber alignment and matrix compactness, and these spatial variations directly regulate T cell migration, positioning, and activation states.

Accumulating evidence indicates that densely packed and highly crosslinked collagen fibers establish durable physical barriers that impede T cell infiltration into tumor cores, thereby limiting direct interactions between T cells and tumor cells [90, 91]. Regarding migration, two photon intravital microscopy further confirmed that T cell migration within the TME is strongly guided by ECM structures such as blood vessels and collagen fibers, with collagen fibers serving as important migratory tracks. To recapitulate this process, investigators employed nanogrooved substrates to mimic the spatial organization of fibrotic ECM. Compared with flat substrates, nanogrooved surfaces promoted more directional T cell migration, and under confined conditions, this guidance effect was found to depend predominantly on topological cues rather than integrin-mediated adhesion [92]. Additional investigations using nanogroove substrates of different stiffness demonstrated that soft grooves promote microtubule instability and cortical contractility, driving T cells toward an “in-groove” amoeboid migration pattern characterized by enhanced migratory speed and directionality, whereas stiff grooves stabilize microtubules and favor an“on-ridge”mesenchymal-like migration mode associated with impaired migratory efficiency [93]. These observations indicate that T cell migration in tumors is governed by a biomechanically optimal state that is co-regulated by microtubule dynamics and cellular contractile forces. In addition to linear fiber architectures, local geometric configurations, including nanoscale zigzag patterns, markedly influence T cell motility primarily through turning angles rather than segment length [94]. Song and colleagues further demonstrated that T cells preferentially migrate along undulating concave topographies, a behavior coordinately controlled by integrin-dependent adhesion and myosin II-driven contractility. Moreover, cellular organization itself constitutes an important topological signal. Relative to highly aligned endothelial monolayers, randomly oriented endothelial cell arrangements promote more rapid and frequent T cell transendothelial migration. Consistently, hydrogel based 3D culture systems have shown that decreasing ECM network density enhances the infiltrative capacity of CAR-T cells [95].

Emerging evidence indicates that ECM topology not only guides T cell migratory behavior but also acts as an autonomous biomechanical signal capable of directly modulating the activation threshold and effector function of both T cells and CAR-T cells. Nanoscale pore confinement spatially restricts microvilli and induces size-dependent segregation of TCR from CD45, thereby promoting localized TCR clustering and the formation of signaling hotspots that activate ERK/MAPK (Extracellular signal-regulated kinase/Mitogen-activated protein kinase) and NF-κB (Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells) pathways and enhance expression of activation-associated genes including CD69 and IL-2, ultimately enabling spontaneous T cell activation and expansion even without exogenous antigen stimulation. In parallel, nanoscale confinement promotes polarized F-actin accumulation while excluding microtubules from microvillus-associated regions, thereby sustaining tyrosine phosphorylation signaling and enhancing the infiltrative and proliferative capacity of CAR-T cells [96]. Likewise, microscale topographic cues are detected by the TCR/CD3 complex through changes in spatial depth, leading to activation of Rh–ROCK–myosin-dependent contractile signaling and Actin-related protein 2/3 complex (Arp2/3)-mediated branched F-actin remodeling, ultimately facilitating the formation of F-actin- and hematopoietic lineage cell-specific protein 1 (HS1)-rich invadosome-like protrusions (ILPs). Traction forces generated by nonmuscle myosin-II subsequently further reduce the T cell activation threshold and potentiate IL-2 and IFN-γ production [97]. In addition to its direct effects on T cells, ECM topology can indirectly modulate antitumor immunity through functional reprogramming of antigen-presenting cells. Through spatial confinement and cell matrix mechanical coupling, aligned collagen fibers drive metabolic reprogramming of dendritic cells, manifested by suppressed oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), reduced ATP (Adenosine triphosphate) generation, impaired migratory function, and increased secretion of protumor chemokines including CXCL8 and CCL2, ultimately compromising their T cell priming capacity [98]. Concurrently, CRATER niches arising at the tumor stroma boundary promote stable immune synapse formation by CD8⁺T cells through upregulation of MHC-I/B2M (Beta-2-microglobulin) expression, while IFN-γ dependent positive feedback signaling further amplifies local antigen presentation and cytotoxic immune responses [99].

Notably, abnormally dense and aligned collagen networks in tumors often convert physiological topological sensing into immunosuppressive signals. Increased collagen fiber density and alignment can promote YAP nuclear translocation via the“Rho–cytoskeletal tension–YAP” mechanotransduction axis, subsequently suppressing the expression of CD25, CD44, and CD69 as well as the secretion of IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, accompanied by aberrant F-actin remodeling and reduced migration directionality, ultimately limiting T cell infiltration into tumor cores [100]. At the immune synapse level, shorter pMHC–TCR or CAR–antigen binding distances can exclude large phosphatases such as CD45 via local membrane curvature, promoting exposure of CD3 ITAMs and activation of the Lck–ZAP70–LAT/SLP76 (Linker for activation of T cells/SH2 domain-containing leukocyte protein of 76 kDa) signaling cascade, thereby enhancing Ca²⁺mobilization and NFAT/MAPK/NF-κB transcriptional programs; in contrast, distal positioning of epitopes from the cell membrane weakens mechanical force transmission and reduces CAR-T cytotoxicity [101]. In addition, topology related mechanical stimulation can regulate LFA-1 polarization and immune synapse stability through Piezo1 mediated Ca²⁺influx and calpain-dependent F-actin remodeling; however, sustained high pressure or excessive confinement may lead to nuclear envelope damage, impaired TCR clustering, and reduced cytotoxic function [102, 103].

In summary, ECM topology does not merely function as a physical barrier; rather, it continuously regulates the migration, immune synapse formation, and antitumor effector functions of T cells and CAR-T cells by influencing membrane receptor sensing, cytoskeletal remodeling, mechanotransduction, and downstream transcriptional and metabolic states, constituting an important mechanical regulatory factor within the tumor microenvironment.

Fluid shear stress

Fluid shear stress (FSS) is ubiquitously present in blood vessels, the lymphatic system, and interstitial fluid flow, representing a mechanical force generated by frictional interactions between moving fluid and the cell membrane. Elevated interstitial fluid pressure and increased shear stress within the circulation represent hallmark hemodynamic features of solid tumors. FSS not only directly contributes to immune regulation during tumor progression but also profoundly modulates T cell migration, activation, and functional persistence [104, 105].

Fluid shear stress (FSS) plays a critical and multifaceted regulatory role during T cell transendothelial extravasation. Interstitial fluid flow (IFF) has been shown to remodel matrix architecture and modulate the endothelial microenvironment, thereby reducing T cell extravasation and promoting their local retention in lymph node models [106]. Within the circulation, T cells can only overcome blood flow induced shear forces when sufficiently strong transient interactions are established between selectins and their ligands on endothelial cells, enabling initial capture and rolling along the endothelial surface. In high shear environments, both effector and regulatory T cells can sustain stable rolling and adhesion through the formation of dynamic“tethers and slings”structures [107]. Shear stress subsequently promotes integrin affinity maturation, enhancing adhesion strength between T cells and endothelial cells, allowing them to withstand persistent hemodynamic forces and complete transendothelial migration [108]. Functionally, this process serves as a biomechanical selection mechanism acting on circulating T cells. Although these observations are primarily derived from studies of endogenous T cells, the same fundamental physical constraints are expected to govern the in vivo circulation and homing behavior of CAR-T cells.

Emerging evidence indicates that fluid shear stress is involved not only in T cell circulation and homing but also functions as an autonomous biomechanical stimulus that directly modulates T cell and CAR-T cell activation, immune synapse stability, and cytotoxic activity [85]. At the receptor recognition interface, TCR–pMHC interactions generate piconewton-scale mechanical forces through cytoskeletal coupling, while cooperative signaling by CD8 and LFA-1 further amplifies TCR force loading and spatial clustering. Insufficient receptor tension or disruption of cytoskeletal coupling markedly compromises antigen recognition specificity, indicating that T cell activation relies on a biomechanical proofreading mechanism involving receptor tension thresholds, cytoskeletal dynamics, and immune synapse stabilization [109]. Moderate shear stress can further amplify TCR/CD3 signaling. It has been demonstrated that fluid shear stress at 5.0 dyn/cm²induces Piezo1 dependent Ca²⁺influx, thereby promoting ZAP70 phosphorylation and activating NFAT-, NF-κB-, and AP-1-mediated transcriptional programs, ultimately enhancing IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α expression; this response is significantly impaired following Piezo1 inhibition or deletion [104]. Long term studies further revealed that sustained shear stress combined with CD3/CD28 stimulation persistently augments NF-κB, cellular FBJ murine osteosarcoma viral oncogene homolog (c-Fos), and NFAT signaling, maintains CD69/CD25 expression, cellular proliferation, and secretion of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2, while also increasing expression of cytotoxic mediators including CD107a, granzyme B, and perforin, suggesting that controlled fluidic stimulation may represent an ex vivo mechanical priming strategy to enhance CAR-T cell expansion, persistence, and cytotoxic capacity [110]. However, chronic exposure to abnormal fluid mechanical stimulation may shift these responses toward immunosuppressive signaling pathways. Tumor-associated increases in shear stress, tissue pressure, and matrix stiffness can chronically activate Piezo1 and induce intracellular Ca²⁺overload, which subsequently promotes calpain-dependent (Phospholipase C gamma 1) PLCγ1 cleavage, attenuates CAR signaling, and drives exhaustion-associated phenotypes including PD-1 and lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) expression, ultimately impairing CAR-T cell infiltration, persistence, and cytotoxic activity [111].

Beyond functional regulation, the application value of FSS in cell manufacturing and quality control has also attracted increasing attention. Ashby et al. established a microfluidic shear stress based affinity sorting system that enabled efficient recovery of high affinity T cells under physiologically simulated shear conditions [112]. This concept may also provide important implications for CAR-T manufacturing, namely, improving the functional consistency of infused cells through the introduction of a mechanical selection dimension. Furthermore, during large scale CAR-T production, shear stimulation generated by bioreactors can directly influence cellular expansion efficiency and differentiation trajectories.

Overall, fluid shear stress is not only an important regulator of T cell migration and activation, but also a critical variable that cannot be overlooked during CAR-T cell manufacturing, enrichment, and quality control. In the future, further elucidation of FSS regulated networks through integrated multi-omics approaches, together with the systematic incorporation of FSS modulation into clinical grade CAR-T manufacturing workflows, may provide new opportunities to improve cellular product quality and therapeutic efficacy against solid tumors.

Other biomechanical cues

The remodeling effects of the tumor physical microenvironment on the effector functions of T cells and CAR-T cells have become a key dimension for understanding immune resistance in solid tumors. The multidimensional physical barriers formed by solid stress, viscoelasticity, interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), and spatial microarchitecture constrain the infiltration potential, survival fitness, and cytotoxic efficacy of CAR-T cells [113, 114].

More specifically, multidimensional physical barriers composed of solid stress, viscoelasticity, interstitial fluid pressure, and spatial confinement within solid tumors continuously reshape the migratory, metabolic, and effector states of T cells and CAR-T cells. Rapid tumor expansion together with CAF activation promotes collagen deposition, ECM crosslinking, and matrix stiffening via TGF-β-dependent signaling, resulting in accumulation of solid stress and compression of blood and lymphatic vessels, ultimately inducing hypoperfusion, hypoxia, and elevated IFP. These aberrant biomechanical cues are detected by mechanosensory pathways including Piezo1 channels, integrins, and YAP/TAZ (Yes-associated protein/Transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif), and are subsequently translated into intracellular Ca²⁺signaling, cytoskeletal tension, and transcriptional reprogramming; meanwhile, dense ECM together with abnormal fluid pressure synergistically impedes T cell infiltration into the tumor core [115]. During CAR-T cell therapy, CAF-driven type I collagen deposition and crosslinking substantially compress matrix pore architecture, thereby restricting CAR-T cells to peripheral tumor regions. Concurrently, chronic mechanical stress together with acidic conditions and immunosuppressive mediators such as TGF-β and IL-10 can activate the Piezo1–Ca²⁺ mechanotransduction pathway, thereby inducing exhaustion-associated molecules including PD-1 and LAG-3, ultimately resulting in impaired CAR-T cell infiltration, reduced persistence, and diminished cytotoxicity [111]. Beyond stiffness and spatial confinement, tumor viscoelasticity itself independently modulates CAR-T cell function, as rapidly stress-relaxing matrices can suppress CAR-T cell expansion, cytotoxic activity, and in vivo tumor control even under comparable stiffness conditions [116]. Sustained solid stress may additionally compress or collapse tumor vasculature and lymphatic networks, thereby exacerbating hypoxia, acidosis, and lactate accumulation. Hypoxia-inducible factor(HIF)-associated signaling pathways subsequently enhance PD-L1 (Programmed death-ligand 1) expression and activate MDSCs (Myeloid-derived suppressor cells), thereby confining CAR-T cells to peripheral immunosuppressive niches and promoting reduced cytokine production together with increased susceptibility to exhaustion. Concurrently, increased IFP resulting from vascular hyperpermeability and defective lymphatic drainage establishes an outward pressure gradient from the tumor core to the periphery, driving interstitial fluid flow that reduces the retention and distribution of CAR-T cells and therapeutic agents within central tumor regions [113]. Furthermore, dense and anisotropically aligned collagen networks reshape T cell migratory behavior through micropore confinement and contact guidance mechanisms. Specifically, microtubule instability enhances cortical contractility via GEF-H1/Rho (Guanine nucleotide exchange factor-H1/Ras homolog) signaling, thereby promoting an amoeboid migratory phenotype optimized for navigation through three dimensional matrices, whereas microtubule stabilization diminishes migratory velocity and directional persistence [93]. Under conditions of severe spatial confinement, CAR-T cells become more mechanically constrained than conventional T cells due to their requirement for greater cellular deformation, leading to impaired migration through narrow microchannels and dense ECM. Stimulation with chemokines or chimeric cytokine receptors can partially rescue confined migratory capacity, indicating that CAR-T cell infiltration is coordinately governed by chemotactic signaling, cellular deformability, and matrix pore architecture [117].

In summary, solid stress, interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), viscoelasticity, and spatial microarchitecture are not isolated physical barriers; rather, they collectively participate in a continuous process within the tumor microenvironment that spans from physical restriction to mechanotransduction and ultimately to immunosuppression, thereby influencing T cell infiltration, functional maintenance, and cytotoxic activity [118]. Further elucidation of how these physical cues regulate immune cell function through specific mechanosensory checkpoints will provide a stronger theoretical foundation for engineering T cells to overcome tumor-associated physical barriers.

Biomechanics-based strategies for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy

The clinical translation of CAR-T-cell therapy in solid tumors remains hindered by multiple challenges, including treatment-associated toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), antigen escape resulting from target antigen loss, immunosuppressive microenvironments dominated by Tregs and MDSCs, tumor heterogeneity, and limited CAR-T cell infiltration into dense tumor tissues [119]. To overcome these limitations, a range of multidimensional engineering strategies has been proposed, including the development of bispecific CARs or bispecific T cell engager (BiTE)-secreting CAR-T cells to address antigen heterogeneity, as well as universal adaptor-based systems. Engineering chemokine receptors such as C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 1/2 (CXCR1/2) and C-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CCR4), together with localized delivery approaches, has also been explored to improve CAR-T cell trafficking and infiltration within tumors. Furthermore, strategies including CRISPR (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats)-mediated deletion of immunosuppressive pathways such as PD-1 or TGF-β receptors, ectopic expression of immunostimulatory factors, and combination therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors have been applied to reshape the suppressive TME [120]. Wang and colleagues further highlighted the limitations of conventional CAR-T cell therapy, including high manufacturing costs, TME-associated barriers such as dense stroma, immunosuppressive signaling, and hypoxic/acidic conditions, as well as the lack of precise post infusion monitoring. They proposed that nanotechnology offers distinct advantages in targeted delivery, functional potentiation, and imaging surveillance; however, successful clinical translation still depends on overcoming challenges related to delivery efficiency, long term biosafety, and compatibility with T cell function [121]. In this context, inflammation-related nanotoxicity should also be considered, because nanocarrier physicochemical properties may influence protein corona formation, immune recognition, mitochondrial stress, reactive oxygen species generation, and pro-inflammatory signaling, all of which are relevant to the safe design of nanomaterial-assisted CAR-T platforms [122]. The limited efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors is fundamentally driven by the complex nature of the tumor microenvironment, where stromal barriers and immunosuppressive signaling cooperatively establish major therapeutic obstacles. Regulation of tumor stromal mechanics may not only enhance CAR-T cell infiltration and persistence but also synergistically mitigate immunosuppressive signaling pathways. Moreover, biomechanical cues may function as reprogramming stimuli capable of modulating CAR-T cell activity, thereby broadening their therapeutic applicability in solid tumors. The following sections focus on strategies aimed at optimizing CAR-T cell immunotherapy through targeting biomechanical factors within the tumor microenvironment and engineering CAR-T manufacturing systems based on mechanobiological principles. Mechanics targeting strategies for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy in the tumor microenvironment

In recent years, the tumor microenvironment has emerged as a central determinant of solid tumor progression and resistance to therapy. The TME consists of extracellular matrix components, stromal cells, immune cells, vascular networks, and multiple signaling mediators, and its aberrant biomechanical characteristics are increasingly recognized as major barriers restricting CAR-T cell efficacy in solid tumors.The excessive ECM deposition, increased collagen crosslinking, matrix stiffening, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, and aberrant vascularization commonly observed in solid tumors not only facilitate tumor growth and metastasis, but also compromise CAR-T cell infiltration and effector activity by restricting T cell migration, impairing immune synapse formation, and inducing metabolic suppression. Highly crosslinked ECM architectures function as physical barriers that limit T cell infiltration and migration, and this phenomenon has been confirmed in three dimensional in vitro culture systems [123, 124].

To address these issues, researchers initially attempted to improve CAR-T cell infiltration in solid tumors by remodeling ECM structure. One such strategy involves engineering CAR-T cells to locally secrete matrix-degrading enzymes, thereby indirectly modulating the mechanical properties of the tumor microenvironment. Zheng et al. engineered CAR-T cells using synthetic Notch receptors to achieve localized and specific secretion of ECM-degrading enzymes, including MMP9, MMP12, and HPSE (Heparanase), effectively reducing ECM density and viscosity and significantly enhancing CAR-T cell infiltration into ECM-rich solid tumors [125]. Similarly, Ogawa et al. designed relaxin-2 (RLN2)-secreting CAR-T cells that upregulated MMP (Matrix metalloproteinase) expression, including MMP-1, -7, and − 9, thereby not only effectively degrading collagen fibers but also polarizing immunosuppressive M2 macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, resulting in significant antitumor effects in stroma-rich pancreatic cancer models [126]. Although these approaches employ CAR-T cell engineering strategies, their primary objective remains the modulation of ECM structure to alleviate the mechanical barriers within the TME, and thus they fundamentally represent a “tumor microenvironment-targeting” optimization strategy. In addition to directly regulating the ECM, targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) represents another important strategy for remodeling the tumor mechanical microenvironment. CAFs are major contributors to ECM deposition and matrix stiffening; therefore, FAP-targeted CAR-T cells can weaken CAF mediated barrier functions and enhance antitumor activity across multiple models. Notably, in multiple myeloma, bispecific CAR-T strategies simultaneously targeting CAFs and malignant B cells have demonstrated synergistically enhanced therapeutic efficacy [127]. However, in solid tumors, heterogeneity of target antigen expression, antigen loss, and the immunosuppressive microenvironment continue to collectively limit CAR-T cell efficacy. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), for example, is characterized by a dense stroma predominantly generated by CAFs, and this structural barrier is considered one of the major reasons for the limited efficacy of previous mesothelin-targeted CAR-T therapies. To address this issue, investigators introduced secreted T cell engaging antibody molecules (TEAMs) into mesothelin-targeted CAR constructs, enabling engineered T cells to recognize CAFs via FAP and recruit T cells through CD3 engagement, thereby simultaneously targeting tumor cells and stromal components. Across multiple models containing both tumor cells and CAFs, including patient-derived organoids and organotypic tumor spheroids, this strategy exhibited enhanced T cell activation and cytotoxicity. Such three dimensional translational models are particularly valuable because organoids and tumor spheroids can better preserve tissue architecture, cellular interactions, and microenvironmental complexity than conventional two dimensional cultures, thereby providing more physiologically relevant platforms for evaluating engineered immune cell therapies [128]. In vivo pancreatic cancer models further demonstrated superior clearance of both tumor cells and CAFs compared with single target engineered T cells [129]. Further studies revealed that this strategy could additionally induce partial remodeling of the tumor stroma, thereby creating a more favorable microenvironment for sustained T cell infiltration and functional maintenance. In addition to biological strategies, physical intervention approaches have also been applied to improve the mechanical microenvironment of solid tumors. Local microwave ablation (MWA) can reduce hyaluronic acid levels, improve blood perfusion and tissue oxygenation, and alleviate interstitial pressure, thereby enhancing the metabolic adaptability and persistence of CAR-T cells, promoting differentiation toward a memory phenotype, and reducing the expression of exhaustion-associated molecules such as PD-1, TIM-3 (T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3), and CTLA-4 (Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte Antigen 4), ultimately demonstrating favorable efficacy and safety in immunodeficient mouse models [130]. Wang et al. loaded gold precursors and L-arginine into thermosensitive hydrogels and injected them into tumors, where microwave heating induced the in situ formation of gold nanoparticles (Au NPs). Au NPs can disperse heat and improve energy utilization efficiency, thereby expanding the effective treatment area of microwave ablation and overcoming the problem of incomplete ablation. Moreover, Au bioreactor-based microwave ablation significantly reduced major ECM components within the TME, including hyaluronic acid and collagen I, achieving ECM viscosity reprogramming and markedly improving CAR-T cell penetration [131]. Abnormal tumor vasculature also represents an important mechanical and immunosuppressive barrier within the TME. Low dose anti-angiogenic therapy can induce vascular normalization, improve perfusion, reduce hypoxia, and enhance immune cell infiltration [132]. Using an epidermal growth factor receptor variant III (EGFRvIII)-specific CAR-T model, Dong et al. demonstrated that anti-VEGF (Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor) therapy enhanced CAR-T cell delivery efficiency and antitumor activity, primarily through restoration of vascular structure and function rather than through direct effects on tumor cells or endothelial cells [133].

Overall, current optimization strategies targeting the tumor mechanical microenvironment are primarily focused on ECM degradation, CAF depletion, local physical interventions, and vascular normalization. Their central objective is to reduce the physical barriers and immunosuppressive mechanical conditions within solid tumors, thereby creating a more favorable environment for CAR-T cell infiltration, migration, and sustained function. These approaches primarily focus on remodeling the tumor microenvironment itself, thereby indirectly enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cells. Strategies leveraging biomechanics to optimize CAR-T cell manufacturing

In addition to directly remodeling the tumor microenvironment, another optimization strategy focuses on CAR-T cells themselves, aiming to improve their functional quality and mechanical adaptability by mimicking physiological mechanical environments, modulating mechanosignaling, and optimizing ex vivo expansion conditions. In recent years, although CAR-T therapy has achieved remarkable success in hematological malignancies, its manufacturing process still faces challenges such as low expansion efficiency, difficulty in maintaining function, and insufficient adaptability to solid tumors. Conventional CAR-T expansion typically relies on rigid anti-CD3/CD28 antibody-coated magnetic beads (Dynabeads) for activation, followed by culture in two dimensional suspension systems. However, this system fails to recapitulate the complex mechanical and spatial cues experienced by T cells in vivo; therefore, an increasing number of studies have attempted to develop biomechanically relevant culture environments that more closely resemble physiological conditions. First, investigators have sought to employ three dimensional biomaterials to recapitulate the spatial organization and biomechanical properties of lymphoid tissue. Lizana and colleagues developed a three dimensional macroscopic scaffold based on a stiffness-tunable thermoresponsive terpolymer for CAR-T cell expansion [134]. The results demonstrated that softer matrices supported CAR-T cell expansion comparable to conventional suspension culture, whereas stiffer matrices (17 kPa) reduced viability and expansion efficiency, potentially due to restricted oxygen/nutrient diffusion and lactate accumulation-associated metabolic stress. To overcome these limitations, the materials were further fabricated into mesh like structures. Delalat and colleagues employed melt electrowriting (MEW) to generate ordered polycaprolactone (PCL) microstructured meshes and covalently immobilized anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies on their surface, thereby establishing a three dimensional T cell activation platform. This platform, characterized by high surface area and porosity, enabled over 100-fold expansion of human CD4⁺T cells within 8–10 days in a 10 cm²G-Rex 10 bioreactor system [135]. Lv et al. further found that three-dimensional fibrin gels can regulate T cell proliferation and differentiation programs via specific mechanical signals, thereby efficiently expanding stem like CAR-T cells with significantly higher efficiency than conventional flat and other matrix-based culture systems [136]. Castellote and colleagues developed a polyethylene glycol–heparin (PEG-Hep) hydrogel platform, in which polymer concentration was tuned to achieve an approximately 120 μm pore size and 3.1 kPa stiffness to recapitulate the physical microenvironment of lymph nodes [137]. This system markedly improved T cell expansion efficiency and CAR transduction efficiency without the need for exogenous cytokines, while heparin further enhanced viral clustering through electrostatic interactions with viral envelope proteins, thereby increasing transduction efficiency. Jie and colleagues developed a three dimensional immune cell matrix scaffold based on self-assembling peptides; this nanoscale integrated platform optimized CAR-T cell expansion by tuning matrix stiffness and adhesion ligand density to enhance the integration of mechanical and biochemical cues [138]. Within this system, CAR-T cell production time was shortened to 3 days, with expansion efficiency improved by over 10 fold. To further overcome the limitations of static systems, researchers have begun developing platforms with dynamic mechanical feedback to better recapitulate the force–strain environment experienced during T cell activation. Liu and colleagues developed alginate-based microfluidic synthetic Vaccine-like Artificial Antigen-Presenting cells (SynVACs), whose viscoelasticity and stiffness closely recapitulate the biomechanical properties of natural antigen presenting cells such as dendritic cells [139]. Compared with clinically used rigid Dynabeads, the APC-like viscoelasticity of SynVACs enables more physiological T cell–APC (Antigen-presenting cell) interactions, thereby enhancing TCR signal transduction efficiency and improving the quality of T cell expansion. SynVACs not only enable higher T cell expansion but also bias differentiation toward CD8⁺T cell subsets, reduce regulatory T cell (Treg) generation, and markedly increase CAR transduction efficiency as well as the proportion of T stem cell memory (TSCM) cells. In vivo lymphoma and solid tumor models, CAR-T cells expanded using SynVACs exhibited enhanced antitumor efficacy and prolonged in vivo persistence. Zhu and colleagues engineered a flexible graphene oxide-based antigen-presenting platform (GO-APP3/28) by immobilizing anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies on graphene oxide surfaces to emulate the mechanical flexibility involved in TCR activation [140]. Beyond their mechanical flexibility, graphene oxide-based interfaces should also be viewed as redox active biomaterials, since their oxidation state, defect density, surface chemistry, and electron transfer capacity may modulate reactive oxygen species production, mitochondrial signaling, and cell fate, which are important considerations for defining an appropriate therapeutic window [141]. This platform enables IL-2 autocrine production in T cells and efficient expansion without exogenous IL-2, while also enhancing CAR transduction efficiency; the resulting CAR-T cells exhibit improved tumor clearance and survival benefits in animal models. In parallel, nano- and micro-topographical regulation of T cell mechanosensing and signal transduction has gained increasing attention. Yassin and colleagues constructed a PDMS (Polydimethylsiloxane)-based elastic micropillar array platform for T cell activation, tuning pillar diameter, aspect ratio, and substrate modulus while coating the surface with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies to recapitulate the force environment during antigen presentation [142]. The study demonstrated that micropillar arrays with 0.5 μm diameter, aspect ratio 4, and 0.5 MPa elastic modulus exhibited optimal performance in enhancing T cell activation, proliferation, CAR transduction efficiency, and alleviating exhaustion phenotypes. This platform markedly promotes CAR-T cell differentiation toward central memory T cell (Tcm) subsets and demonstrates enhanced tumor suppression and prolonged in vivo persistence in mouse xenograft models. Mechanistically, micropillar topography induces local membrane curvature changes that drive spatial reorganization and segregation of TCR and phosphatases such as CD45, thereby strengthening TCR signaling. In addition, the collective mechanical effects of micropillar arrays result in an“effective softening”of the surface by about five orders of magnitude compared with the bulk material, making it closer to in vivo mechanical environments and providing more suitable mechanical conditions for sustained T cell activation. Studies show that CD28 clustering around the TCR–CD3 complex significantly enhances T cell activation and IL-2 secretion [143]. Viscoelastic platforms help balance T cell activation and exhaustion, avoiding functional decline caused by continuous stimulation on rigid substrates [144]. Additionally, nano topographical cues can induce immune synapse-like membrane protrusions that facilitate TCR clustering and may enhance signaling persistence by spatially excluding large phosphatases such as CD45, thereby improving CAR transduction efficiency [96]. These findings suggest that activation processes with dynamic mechanical feedback may themselves serve as a form of“functional training”influencing CAR-T cell fate and long term functionality.

In addition to optimizing CAR-T cell production, biomechanical concepts have increasingly been incorporated into the development of functional evaluation platforms for CAR-T cells. It has been reported that CAR-T cells migrate less efficiently than conventional T cells in narrow microchannels, indicating that they are particularly susceptible to the elevated mechanical stress and dense extracellular matrix characteristic of solid tumors [117]. These findings imply that current ex vivo expansion platforms fail to provide sufficient mechanical adaptation cues, which may contribute to impaired migration and dysfunctional activity of CAR-T cells after entering solid tumor tissues. Building on this insight, Zeming and colleagues established a microfluidics-based, label-free biophysical analysis platform known as Cell Trajectory Modulation (CTM), enabling rapid assessment of CAR-T cell functional states based on cellular size and deformability characteristics [145]. Requiring fewer than 10,000 cells and only 20 µL of unprocessed sample, the assay can be completed within 10 min, markedly outperforming traditional flow cytometric and cytotoxicity-based assays in speed and thereby offering strong potential for real time quality control during CAR-T production. CTM-derived parameters were found to strongly correlate with the CD4:CD8 composition, CD127 expression, and multiple memory related phenotypes of CAR-T cells, while also showing significant positive associations with in vitro killing activity and the secretion of effector molecules such as IL-5, IL-13, and granzyme B.

In summary, biomechanics-based CAR-T engineering and manufacturing optimization strategies primarily focus on modifying CAR-T cells themselves, aiming to improve expansion efficiency, phenotypic quality, long term persistence, and adaptability to solid tumors by recapitulating physiological mechanical environments, optimizing mechanical stimulation, and enhancing mechanoadaptive capacity. These strategies are fundamentally distinct from the aforementioned approaches that remodel the tumor microenvironment in terms of intervention targets, technological pathways, and translational rationale; however, the two approaches may ultimately become complementary and collectively advance the development of CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors. Conclusions and perspectives

Recent advances in tumor mechanobiology have highlighted the growing importance of biomechanical factors in cancer progression and immunotherapeutic responses. Nevertheless, most existing studies are still limited to in vitro systems and mechanistic exploration, leaving a considerable gap between fundamental discoveries and clinical application. In CAR-T cell therapy for solid tumors, the dense extracellular matrix together with the abnormal mechanical microenvironment forms a pronounced physical barrier that impairs T cell infiltration, motility, and durable antitumor function. Accordingly, future studies should overcome the constraints of traditional ex vivo models and convert biomechanical parameters into clinically applicable metrics for disease assessment and therapeutic guidance, ultimately promoting deeper integration between mechanobiology and CAR-T based immunotherapy.

First, future research should promote a transition from in vitro simulation systems toward in situ clinical mechanical assessment. At present, although polyacrylamide (PAA) hydrogels and microfluidic chips are widely used to mimic matrix stiffness, they fail to faithfully recapitulate the complex and dynamically evolving mechanical states present in patients. Therefore, the development of non-invasive or minimally invasive technologies capable of real time assessment of tumor mechanical properties is of considerable importance. At the macroscopic level, magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) can be employed to evaluate the overall stiffness and spatial heterogeneity of solid tumors, thereby identifying lesions with elevated physical stress. The solid stress measurement approach proposed by the team of Rakesh K. Jain has also provided an important framework for studies of tumor mechanopathology [146]. At the microscopic level, atomic force microscopy (AFM) enables nanoscale scanning of biopsy specimens and can be used to identify critical mechanical barriers such as the“stiffened rim”at tumor margins [147]. Collectively, these studies indicate that aberrant tumor mechanical properties may constitute a critical determinant of primary resistance to immunotherapeutic interventions.

Second, a biomarker framework centered on mechanical characteristics should be established. Previous studies have demonstrated that matrix stiffness is closely associated with the acquisition of malignant phenotypes [148]. In the context of CAR-T therapy, the mechanical heterogeneity characterized by a stiff tumor periphery and a relatively softer core may constitute a major basis for T cell exclusion. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce the concept of“mechanomarkers”and, in combination with technologies such as MRE, AFM, and spatial transcriptomics, establish an“immuno-mechanical subtyping”framework. In patients with highly crosslinked matrices and elevated tissue stiffness, simply increasing the CAR-T infusion dose is often insufficient to improve therapeutic efficacy; by contrast, combination strategies involving “matrix-degrading agents plus CAR-T cells”may be more effective in enhancing T cell infiltration and therapeutic responses.

Moreover, integrated therapeutic strategies directed against mechanotransduction signaling and physical stromal barriers may also hold substantial clinical value. Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) functions as a central mechanosensory molecule for extracellular matrix stiffness and has emerged as a clinically promising target for therapeutic modulation. Evidence suggests that inhibition of FAK can attenuate stromal fibrosis while simultaneously decreasing the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [149]. Furthermore, collagen crosslinking mediated by lysyl oxidase (LOX) is recognized as a critical contributor to tissue stiffening [150].

Overall, mechanobiology research should not remain limited to in vitro mechanistic exploration, but should instead be translated into clinical applications for CAR-T cell therapy. In the future, the establishment of clinically applicable biomechanical assessment systems, the development of mechanomarker-based patient stratification models, and the integration of matrix-remodeling and biomechanical intervention strategies may help overcome the physical barriers underlying resistance to CAR-T therapy in solid tumors. The deep integration of mechanobiology and immunotherapy is also expected to provide new directions for the application of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors.
