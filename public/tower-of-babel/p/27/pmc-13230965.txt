Mechanisms of human mitochondrial leaderless mRNA translation initiation
Authors: Shuangjie Shen, Yinghua Xu, Daniel L. Kober, Jinfan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial translation is essential for cellular function, and its dysregulation is associated with mitochondrial disorders and cancer. However, the mechanisms by which human mitochondrial ribosomes initiate translation remain poorly understood, particularly because mitochondrial mRNAs generally lack the 5′ untranslated regions that guide translation initiation in bacterial and cytoplasmic systems. Using real-time single-molecule fluorescence measurements, biochemical assays, and cryo-EM analysis, we show that human mitochondrial translation initiation occurs through two parallel pathways. In one pathway, leaderless mRNA first loads onto the 28S small subunit, followed by recruitment of the 39S large subunit to form the 55S initiation complex. In the second pathway, a preassembled 55S monosome directly loads onto leaderless mRNA. Both pathways require recruitment of mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet before mRNA binding. However, the monosome-loading pathway tolerates non-formylated Met-tRNAMet and is suppressed by mtIF3. Together, these findings define the heterogeneous pathways of human mitochondrial translation initiation on leaderless mRNAs.

Introduction

Human mitochondria possess their own translation system to produce 13 core proteins of the oxidative phosphorylation complexes that are critical for cellular energy production1–5. Defects in mitochondrial translation are linked to a range of human mitochondrial diseases, often caused by mutations in components of the mitochondrial translation machineries6–10. Despite extensive study1, a dynamic and quantitative framework for understanding how these mutations perturb mitochondrial translation remains elusive. A central unresolved question is how mammalian mitochondrial ribosomes are loaded onto mitochondrial mRNAs to initiate protein synthesis1,11.

Mammalian mitochondrial mRNAs lack a 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR) entirely or possess only a very short one (up to three nucleotides)12. Such leaderless mRNAs can also be found in certain bacterial and archaeal species13. In bacteria, translation of leaderless mRNAs initiates directly on monosomes, a mechanism that contrasts with the sequential subunit loading pathway used for 5′UTR-containing mRNAs via Shine-Dalgarno sequence-mediated recruitment14,15. Studies of human mitochondrial translation have suggested that leaderless mRNA translation initiation occurs exclusively on 55S monosomes16,17, thus representing an evolutionarily conserved leaderless mRNA translation initiation pathway. However, earlier evidence indicates that leaderless mRNAs can also associate with the 28S small mitoribosomal subunits both in vitro18,19 and in vivo20, suggesting that sequential loading of the 28S and 39S subunits may also support leaderless mRNA translation initiation18,21.

Although the key components of mitochondrial translation initiation have been identified22, detailed kinetic analyses have been hindered by limited access to mitochondrial material, particularly mitoribosomes1,5, and in vivo genetic approaches using translation reporters remain challenging23. Consequently, the precise mechanisms underlying assembly of elongation-competent 55S initiation complexes (55S ICs) are not fully understood. Single-molecule fluorescence approaches, which require less material than bulk biochemical assays and allow real-time observation of dynamic heterogeneity24,25, offer a powerful alternative.

Here, we reconstituted a human mitochondrial translation initiation system using site-specifically labeled mitoribosomal subunits, mRNAs, tRNAs, and initiation factors. By combining real-time single-molecule spectroscopy with single-particle cryo-EM, we uncovered heterogeneous pathways of leaderless mRNA translation initiation. We defined the factor requirements and the sequence and timing of sub-steps of translation initiation. Furthermore, we show how initiation pathways are modulated by mtIF3 and by the N-formylation of Met-tRNAMet, revealing potential mechanisms for translation regulation in human mitochondria. Collectively, our work provides a dynamic framework for understanding human mitochondrial translation initiation and its regulation.

Results

Reconstitution of human mitochondrial leaderless mRNA translation initiation

Here, we reconstituted human mitochondrial translation initiation in vitro using purified mitoribosomal subunits, initiation factors, and N-formyl-Met-tRNAMet (fMet-tRNAMet, lacking post-transcriptional tRNA modifications)16,26–29 (Supplementary Fig. 1). We first examined whether leaderless mRNAs can associate with the 28S subunit in the absence of the 39S subunit, a topic that remains under debate11,16–20. To test this, we employed a native gel shift assay30 to assess the binding of a 45-nucleotide model leaderless mRNA (hereafter referred to as AUG-model mRNA) to the 28S subunits (Supplementary Fig. 2). The AUG-model mRNA was labeled with a Cy3 fluorophore at the 3′ end and contained a single AUG codon at the 5′-monophosphorylated end, thereby eliminating ambiguity in initiation start site usage.

Reaction mixtures containing 1 μM AUG-model mRNA-Cy3, 100 nM 28S subunits, 400 nM mtIF2, 300 nM mtIF3, and 200 nM fMet-tRNAMet were assembled in various combinations in an initiation buffer28 supplemented with 2 mM GTP, and incubated at 30 °C for 10 min. Complexes were then resolved on a 0.8% agarose gel, and Cy3 fluorescence was visualized to assess mRNA–28S complex formation and the factor requirements for binding.

We observed that the presence of initiation factors and fMet-tRNAMet alone did not alter the electrophoretic mobility of the mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 2b, lanes 1–2). However, further inclusion of the 28S subunits resulted in a slower-migrating mRNA species (Supplementary Fig. 2b, lane 3), consistent with the formation of a leaderless mRNA–28S complex. Quantification of the Cy3 fluorescence indicated that approximately 60% of the 28S subunits were active in mRNA binding (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Omission of mtIF3 had no detectable effect on complex formation efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 2b, lane 4). In contrast, minimal complex formation was observed when mtIF2 or fMet-tRNAMet was omitted, or when the mRNA was incubated with the 28S subunit alone (Supplementary Fig. 2b, lanes 5–7). Together, these findings demonstrate that leaderless mRNAs can be loaded onto the 28S subunit and that mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet, but not mtIF3, are critical for this assembly step. Importantly, subsequent addition of excess 39S subunits resulted in a quantitative shift of the 28S–mRNA complexes to a slower-migrating species, consistent with formation of the 55S initiation complex (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

The 28S small subunit was site-specifically biotinylated via an A1-tag31 fused to the C-terminus of mS26 (biotin-28S, Supplementary Fig. 3) and immobilized on the imaging surface (Fig. 1a). Because the biotin-28S contained no fluorophores for direct visualization, we co-immobilized a biotin-Cy5 DNA oligonucleotide fiducial (at a constant, empirically determined molar ratio) to control for variation in tethering density and ensure single 28S subunit tethering (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Note). Before each experiment, the number of Cy5-fiducials per field of view (FOV) was quantified with 640-nm excitation. Upon start of real-time data acquisition with 532-nm excitation of the same FOV, a mixture containing 1 nM Cy3-labeled leaderless mRNA, 200 nM fMet-tRNAMet, 400 nM mtIF2, 300 nM mtIF3, and excess GTP was delivered into the flow chamber at 30 °C using a stopped-flow pump.

Under these conditions, binding of the AUG-model leaderless mRNA to the 28S subunit was readily detected as the appearance of Cy3 fluorescence upon sample mixing (Fig. 1b), corresponding to stable mRNA binding events (median lifetime ≈ 140 s, Supplementary Fig. 5). The number of observed Cy3 binding molecules per Cy5 fiducial within a given FOV (~0.75 in this case, Fig. 1c) was used as the mRNA binding efficiency. When only biotin-Cy5 fiducials were immobilized, the binding efficiency was decreased ~25-fold (to ~0.03, Fig. 1c), and the remaining events were short-lived (median lifetime ≈ 13 s, Supplementary Fig. 5), confirming that the stable Cy3 signals originated from specific binding of mRNA to tethered 28S subunits.

In line with our native gel shift assays (Supplementary Fig. 2), omission of either fMet-tRNAMet or mtIF2 markedly impaired mRNA binding to the 28S subunit, reducing mRNA binding efficiency (to ~0.16 and ~0.10, respectively, Fig. 1c) and producing short-lived events (median lifetime ≈ 6–10 s; Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, omission of mtIF3 neither reduced mRNA binding nor affected complex stability (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that stable mRNA engagement requires mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet, but not mtIF3.

To test the importance of the 5’ start codon, we replaced the AUG with a non-cognate CAA codon (CAA-model mRNA). Native gel shift assays with the CAA-model mRNA showed substantially reduced mRNA–28S complex formation (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with the single-molecule data showing lower binding efficiency and shorter lifetime (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Similarly, introduction of a 29-nucleotide 5′ UTR upstream of the AUG (UTR-model mRNA) also weakened mRNA binding (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 5). These results demonstrate that the presence of a start codon at the 5′ end is critical for efficient and stable association of leaderless mRNAs with the 28S subunit, in agreement with previous biochemical evidence that mammalian mitoribosomes preferentially recognize 5′-terminal start codons on leaderless transcripts18.

Robust and stable binding was also observed for full-length, naturally occurring leaderless Cox3 and CytB mRNAs labeled with Cy3 at the 3′ end (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that formation of a stable leaderless mRNA–28S complex is a general feature of human mitochondrial translation initiation.

The 28S subunit is preloaded with mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet to recruit mRNAs

Analysis of directly excited Cy5 (mtIF2) and Cy3 (mRNA) fluorescence intensities (Fig. 2b), aligned to the beginning of the Cy5 events, revealed that mtIF2 binding consistently preceded mRNA association (Fig. 2c). Upon mRNA loading, a stable FRET signal emerged, indicating formation of an mRNA–mtIF2–28S complex (Fig. 2b), which was further validated in experiments using a single 532-nm laser excitation (Supplementary Fig. 7). Cryo-EM structure of the 28S PIC

The leaderless mRNAs used in our assays completely lack a 5′UTR, and the structural basis for their stable engagement with the 28S subunit has remained unclear. To the best of our knowledge, the only previously reported 28S PIC-like structure formed on a mature 28S subunit was obtained from native samples of a cell line defective in 39S subunit biogenesis and contained extra density attributed to the 39S protein bL12m11. In that structure, the associated mRNA was assigned to Cox1, which carries a three-nucleotide 5’UTR that stacks against Gly164 and Gly165 of uS7m11. These interactions were proposed to confer exceptional stability to the Cox1–28S association, distinguishing it from other human mitochondrial mRNAs that contain only 0–2 nucleotides upstream of the start codon11,16.

To elucidate the structural basis of 28S PIC formation observed in our assays, we reconstituted the complex with our model leaderless mRNA and visualized it by single-particle cryo-EM (Supplementary Fig. 11). The model mRNA was incubated with the 28S subunit, mtIF2•GTP, mtIF3, and fMet-tRNAMet at room temperature, then applied to a cryo-EM grid and vitrified within ~1 min of mixing. At this pre-steady state timepoint, mRNA binding was expected to occur on most of 28S subunits (Supplementary Fig. 11a), forming a stable complex with mtIF2 (Supplementary Fig. 7e) and fMet-tRNAMet (Supplementary Fig. 10f).

Indeed, after data processing, we identified a subset of 167,631 particles (~57% of total 28S particles) containing well-defined densities for mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet (Supplementary Fig. 11e). Three-dimensional classification of these particles yielded three major classes, two of which, comprising a total of 107,666 particles, were combined and refined to a 2.50 Å reconstruction of the 28S PIC (Supplementary Figs. 11e and 12).

In the 28S PIC map, fMet-tRNAMet and mtIF2 were well resolved in regions contacting the 28S subunit, including the tRNA anticodon arm and mtIF2 domains II and III (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Figs. 12b and 13). The solvent-exposed acceptor stem of tRNAMet and its interacting domain IV of mtIF2 were less well resolved, likely reflecting conformational flexibility in these peripheral regions (Supplementary Fig. 13). Densities that belong to the mRNA can be traced from the P site toward the mS39 region in the 3’ direction (Fig. 4d), with the first three nucleotides corresponding to the AUG codon displaying the strongest density, allowing confident modeling (Fig. 4e). Notably, the mRNA density began with the 5’ monophosphate group followed by the AUG codon, which formed strong base-pairing interactions with the anticodon of fMet-tRNAMet, without any involvement of mRNA-base stacking with the Gly164 and Gly165 residues of uS7m (Fig. 4f).Fig. 4Cryo-EM structure of the 28S PIC.a Cryo-EM density map of the 28S PIC (overall resolution 2.5 Å). These observations indicate that the codon–anticodon interaction is the primary stabilizing force anchoring the leaderless mRNA on the 28S subunit in the absence of a 5′UTR, explaining its strict dependence on 5′ start codon recognition by fMet-tRNAMet. Moreover, the 28S PIC structure reveals how mtIF2 reinforces this interaction through coordinated contacts with both fMet-tRNAMet and the ribosomal subunit, providing a molecular framework for leaderless mRNA recruitment during human mitochondrial translation initiation.

Two parallel translation initiation pathways on leaderless mRNAs

Having established that a 28S PIC can assemble independently of the 39S subunit, we next hypothesized that leaderless mRNAs might recruit the two ribosomal subunits sequentially. To test this, we designed an inter-ribosomal subunit FRET signal to monitor 55S monosome formation in real time (Supplementary Fig. 14). A short S6 peptide tag31 was fused to the C-termini of the endogenous mS31 (28S subunit) and bL31m (39S subunit) proteins, which are within the predicted FRET distance (~40 Å) in structural models of the 55S mitoribosome (Supplementary Fig. 14a). The tagged ribosomes retained normal function in cells (Supplementary Fig. 4), and the purified 28S-S6 and 39S-S6 subunits were site-specifically labeled with Cy3 and Cy5, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14d).

To observe ribosome loading, a biotinylated model leaderless mRNA was immobilized on the imaging surface (Fig. 5a). At the start of data acquisition, a mixture containing 1.5 nM Cy3-28S, 4 nM Cy5-39S, 400 nM mtIF2, 200 nM fMet-tRNAMet, and 300 nM mtIF3 was added to the flow chamber. Under these conditions, the majority of 55S ICs (~90%) assembled via sequential loading–the 28S subunit arrives first, followed by 39S joining (Fig. 5b, d). This demonstrates that the 28S PIC on leaderless mRNA is competent for subunit joining and initiation. A smaller fraction of events (~ 10%) showed simultaneous arrival of both subunits, indicating a direct monosome loading pathway (Fig. 5c,d). Notably, approximately 90% of 55S ICs formed by either route were active in entering translation elongation by accepting the first elongator aminoacyl-tRNA (Supplementary Fig. 15), indicating that both pathways are functionally productive.

Consistent with earlier observations, mtIF2–and likely also fMet-tRNAMet–was preloaded onto the ribosomes prior to mRNA binding (Supplementary Fig. 15d, e, g). The transition from initiation to elongation was further regulated by mtIF2 (Supplementary Fig. 15b, c, f, h, i). Importantly, both sequential and monosome initiation pathways were also detected on full-length Cox3 and CytB mRNAs, though the relative frequencies and kinetics varied among transcripts (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 16).

Together, these results reveal that human mitochondrial translation initiation on leaderless mRNAs can proceed through two parallel pathways, with the mitoribosomal subunits either loaded sequentially or simultaneously as a monosome.

Regulation of the translation initiation pathways by mtIF3 and tRNAMet

Leaderless mRNA translation becomes dysregulated when either mtIF3 or mitochondrial methionyl-tRNA formyltransferase (MTFMT) is ablated in cells20,35,36. To define how these factors influence initiation pathway selection, we first omitted mtIF3 from the single-molecule initiation assay using the model leaderless mRNA (Fig. 5a). Strikingly, the absence of mtIF3 led to a pronounced shift toward the monosome loading pathway, increasing its frequency to ~74% (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 16). This effect was not due to disrupted 28S PIC formation during sequential subunit loading (Fig. 1c), but likely reflected enhanced pre-assembly of 55S monosomes in the absence of mtIF3 prior to mRNA binding37.

We next examined the role of N-formylation of the Met-tRNAMet by substituting fMet-tRNAMet with non-formylated Met-tRNAMet. Under these conditions, nearly all initiation events (~95%) proceeded via direct monosome loading (Fig. 5d), although initiation occurred at a ~2.5-fold slower rate than with fMet-tRNAMet (Supplementary Fig. 16). The reduced sequential pathway usage correlated with weakened interaction between Met-tRNAMet and mtIF2 (Supplementary Fig. 9)34, resulting in compromised 28S PIC formation.

Together, these findings indicate that mtIF3 suppresses premature monosome formation and thereby favors the sequential loading pathway. In parallel, N-formylation of Met-tRNAMet is essential for stable mtIF2 engagement and efficient 28S PIC assembly. Consequently, when either mtIF3 or MTFMT function is lost, translation initiation becomes biased toward the alternative, monosome-driven pathway.

Discussion

Although the core factors required for mammalian mitochondrial translation initiation on leaderless mRNAs have been identified, the precise order, kinetics, and regulation of this process have remained unresolved1,16–20,26,29. A quantitative model of human mitochondrial leaderless mRNA translation initiation.

The mtIF3 protein remains associated with the 28S subunit from the late stages of its biogenesis11. After maturation of the 28S subunit, mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet can bind to the subunit either individually or as a preformed complex. This facilitates rapid recruitment of leaderless mRNA, forming a 28S pre-initiation complex (PIC) that is poised for 39S subunit joining. Alternatively, the 28S subunit can associate with the 39S to form a 55S monosome. When mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet load onto the 55S monosome, the complex becomes competent for leaderless mRNA binding. Once the 55S initiation complex (IC) is formed, GTP hydrolysis by mtIF2 is triggered, promoting its release from the ribosome and allowing the first elongator tRNA to bind, thereby entering the translation elongation phase. In the monosome loading pathway, non-formylated Met-tRNAMet can also initiate translation, although at a slower rate; and this pathway is suppressed by mtIF3.

Our single-molecule and structural analyses (Figs. 1–5) demonstrate that translation initiation proceeds through a sequential subunit loading pathway, in which the 28S subunit preloaded with mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet first recruits the leaderless mRNA, followed by 39S joining to form a 55S IC. Although reminiscent of bacterial and cytosolic translation initiation in its stepwise assembly38,39, this process employs a distinct molecular mechanism: leaderless mRNAs lacking a 5′UTR are stabilized within the 28S PIC primarily through start codon-anticodon pairing with fMet-tRNAMet. Our results further show that mtIF2 and fMet–tRNAMet cooperatively anchor the 5′ region of the mRNA, establishing the molecular basis for start codon–dependent stabilization (Fig. 1c) and underscoring the critical role of N-formylation of the Met-tRNAMet in maintaining this interaction network (Supplementary Fig. 9).

We also observed an alternative monosome loading pathway16,26, in which both mitoribosomal subunits associated with the mRNA simultaneously (Fig. 5c). This pathway is less selective against non-formylated Met-tRNAMet, allowing slower translation initiation with Met-tRNAMet (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 16). When formylation is impaired, such as in Leigh syndrome patients carrying MTFMT mutations35,40, this flexibility in the monosome loading pathway likely helps sustain residual mitochondrial translation and function. Under normal conditions, however, initiation with non-formylated Met-tRNAMet must be limited, since proper assembly of oxidative phosphorylation complexes is disrupted without N-formylation41,42. One mechanism that may restrict the monosome loading pathway involves mtIF3. This factor associates with the 28S subunit from late stages of its biogenesis (PIC-1, Fig. 6)11,17,43, enabling rapid recruitment of mtIF2 and fMet-tRNAMet for leaderless mRNA loading. Additionally, mtIF3 prevents formation of 55S monosomes lacking mRNAs37, e.g., following ribosome recycling44, further suppressing the monosome loading pathway (Fig. 5d).

After catalyzing 55S IC formation, mtIF2 regulates the transition to elongation through its GTP hydrolysis-dependent release from the ribosomal complex (Supplementary Fig. 15). The observed mtIF2 residence time (~13 s, Supplementary Fig. 15i) parallels that of its yeast cytosolic counterpart eIF5B45,46 and may serve a similar proofreading role in ensuring accurate start codon recognition before elongation proceeds46,47. This potential initiation fidelity mechanism warrants future in-depth examinations.

In summary, our results demonstrate that human mitochondrial leaderless mRNA translation initiation is not a single linear process, but a regulated network of parallel pathways tuned by initiation factors and the tRNAMet (Fig. 6). The heterogeneous pathways may ensure both efficiency and adaptability of mitochondrial protein synthesis under varying physiological conditions. The dynamic framework and reconstituted single-molecule system presented here provide a foundation for dissecting how mitochondrial translation is dynamically controlled in health and disease1.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK293S GnTI- cells were obtained from ATCC (CRL-3022). To guard against genomic instability, an aliquot of the cell line was passaged for only 4−6 weeks. Cells were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma using InVivogen MycoStrip kit (#rep-mys-10). Cell lines were not validated further. Cells were thawed in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (high glucose) medium (Gibco #11965118) supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS, GeminiBio #900-108-500), 100 units/ml penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco #15150-122), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco #25030-081), and passaged in monolayer culture at 37 °C in 5% CO2. HEK293S GnTI− cell lines with mitoribosomal protein tagged were created via CRISPR-Cas9 editing (see below). For suspension culture of wild type and edited HEK293S cells, after expanding to eight 15 cm dishes, cells were sloughed off and transferred to suspension culture in FreeStyle 293 medium (Gibco #12338-018) supplemented with 2% v/v FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin and streptomycin. Suspension cells were grown with orbital shaking at 130 rpm in baffled flasks at 37 °C in 8% CO2. Cells were passaged to maintain a suspension density within the range of 0.4 × 106 cells/mL–3 × 106 cells/mL.

CRISPR-Cas9 and homology-directed repair

Sequences

To generate the mS26-A1, mS31-S6, and bL31m-S6 cell lines, the guide sequences GGACTCCTAGGGGCCCAGTA (mS26), ATACAGTTCAATTAAGACCA (mS31), and TCCCGGGGTGGCTGGAGCCA (bL31m) were cloned into the pX458 vector backbone using the published approach48. To insert the tags onto the endogenous copies of the genes, single-stranded DNA ultramer repair templates were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies that contained about 40–60 nucleotides of flanking sequence on either side of the desired insertion. See Supplementary Table 1 for all guide oligo, repair template, and PCR screening oligo sequences.

Transfections, sorting, and screening overview

Approximately 24 h post-seeding in a well of a 6-well plate, wild-type HEK293S cells at ~60% confluency were transiently transfected (Liopfectamine 3000, ThermoFisher #L13000008) with 1 µg of the relevant pX458 plasmid and 2 µg of ssDNA repair template. Cells were allowed to recover for 48 h. Single, eGFP-positive cells were sorted at the UT Southwestern Flow Cytometry Core Facility into a well of a 96-well plate that contained 50% conditioned DMEM (high glucose) medium. Individual colonies were allowed to recover until they were visible by eye (approximately 2 weeks), upon which colonies were transferred to a well of a 24-well plate. Once confluent, colonies were screened via PCR, Sanger sequencing, and Western blot analyses. Successfully edited cell lines were expanded and ultimately stored as stocks in 10% DMSO/ 90% FBS solution.

PCR screening and Sanger sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from candidate cell lines using the QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Lucigen, #QE09050) essentially as described, except 10 µL of resuspended cells were added to 50 µL of QuickExtract solution. Following extraction, 1 µL of genomic DNA was added to a standard 24 µL 2× GoTaq Green PCR (Promega, #M7123), typically ~35 cycles. Sequences for screening oligos are available in Supplementary Table 1. Following amplification, PCR reactions were analyzed using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. PCR products with the desired insertion size were submitted for Sanger sequencing, following their purification.

Western blotting

Whole cell lysates were analyzed via SDS-PAGE followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. All antibodies were diluted in EveryBlot Blocking buffer (BioRad, # 12010020). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-mS26 at 1:2000 from Abcam (ab181863, Lot number EPR14396); anti-mS31 at 1:1000 from Thermo Scientific (PA5-109985, Lot number ZD4280461); and anti-bL31m at 1:2000 from Thermo Scientific (PA5-97981, Lot number ZD4281106A). Incubations with primary antibodies were for 16 h at 4 °C. The secondary antibody was Mouse anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (sc-2357, Lot number D0423) and was used at 1:2000. Blots were imaged via chemiluminescence.

On-gel mito-FUNCAT assays

The on-gel mito-FUNCAT assays were performed as described32. Wild type or edited HEK293S cells were seeded at a density of 2.0 × 106 cells per well in 2 mL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/ml penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco #15150-122), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco #25030-081) in 6-well plates. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 20 h. After washing the cells with PBS, the cells were incubated in methionine-free DMEM (Gibco #21013-024) supplemented with 100 µg/mL anisomycin (ThermoFisher, #502579290) for 15 min at 37 °C in 5% CO2. For the negative controls, to inhibit mitochondrial translation, additional 100 µg/mL chloramphenicol (ThermoFisher, #AC227920250) was included in the medium during incubation. Next, L-homopropargylglycine (HPG; Lumiprobe, #3313-100 mg) was added to the culture at final 50 µM, and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2.5 h.

After the incubation, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, detached from the plate using a cell scraper, and transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 3 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 50 µL lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 10 min. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting clarified lysate was transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube for click chemistry reactions.

The click chemistry reaction was performed by incubating the clarified lysate with 30 µM Cy5-azide (Lumiprobe, #A3330), a premixed solution of 9.1 mM CuSO4 (Fisher Scientific #AA1417836) and 45.45 mM THPTA (Vector Laboratories #CCT1010100), 100 mM sodium ascorbate (Fisher Scientific #AAA1775922), and 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer at 25 °C for 60 min. The reaction was quenched by final 1X Laemmli Sample buffer (BioRad #1610747), and samples were heated at 50 °C for 5 min before analyzed by 20% SDS-PAGE.

Preparation of human mitochondrial ribosomal subunits

The mitochondrial ribosomal subunits were purified essentially as described28,49, with some modifications.

Purification of mitochondria from HEK293S cells

For each purification, 6 L of wild type or edited HEK293S cell cultures were harvest at a density ~3 × 106 cells/mL by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cells were washed in ice cold PBS and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Typically, ~60 g of cell mass could be obtained. The cells were resuspended in 180 mL cold MIB buffer (25 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 20 mM Mg(OAc)2, 2 mM DTT, protease inhibitors) and gently stirred for 20 min at 4 °C. The cell suspension was transferred to a pre-cooled nitrogen cavitation chamber (Parr Instrument Company #4635) on ice, and then 83 mL cold SM4 buffer (25 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 20 mM Mg(OAc)2, 2 mM DTT, 281 mM sucrose, 844 mM mannitol, protease inhibitors) was added. Next, the nitrogen cavitation chamber was filled with nitrogen gas until the pressure reached 500 psi and kept on ice for 20 min. Upon pressure release from the cavitation chamber, the lysate was collected and centrifuged at 800 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was passed through the cheesecloth (Millipore Sigma #475855-1 R) into a beaker kept on ice. To the pellet, 90 mL of MIBSM buffer (3:1 MIB:SM4) was added. The resuspended mixture was homogenized using glass Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 800 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was passed through the cheesecloth into a beaker kept on ice. The clarified supernatant fractions from the above two centrifugation steps were combined and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The tight pellet was resuspended in 10 mL MIBSM buffer, and 200 units of Turbo DNase (ThermoFisher #AM2238) was added. The resuspension was rotated on a roller at 4 °C for 20 min. Collect the crude mitochondria by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and a typical yield was ~2 g wet weight.

Purification of mitochondrial ribosomal subunits

The mitochondria pellet was resuspended in 5 mL lysis buffer (25 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 20 mM Mg(OAc)2, 2% Triton X-100, protease inhibitors, RNase inhibitors) and kept on ice for 30 min, then homogenized using glass Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was kept on ice for another 10 min before centrifugation at 30,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 105,000 × g for 17 h at 4 °C in a type 90 Ti fixed-angle rotor. The pellet that contained crude mitochondrial ribosomes was resuspended in dissociation buffer (50 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitors) and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 min. The clarified solution was mixed with final 1 mM puromycin and incubated at 30 °C for 30 min. The resulting solution was loaded onto a 10-30% sucrose gradient (in dissociation buffer) and centrifuged at 75,416 × g for 18 h in a SW41 rotor. The gradient was fractionated on a BioComp piston gradient fractionator. The fractions corresponding to 28S and 39S subunits were separately concentrated in a 30 kD MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter and buffer exchanged into mitoribosome storage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM DTT, 30 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 6% w/v sucrose, 0.1 mM Spermine, and 1 mM Spermidine) before flash frozen on liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. The concentrations of mitoribosomal subunits were measured by A260nm using Nanodrop and determined using the conversion: 1 A260nm = 77 pmol 28S and 1 A260nm = 55 pmol 39S.

Purification of labeled mitochondrial ribosomal subunits

For labeling of the 28S or 39S subunits through the A1 or S6 tags31, a 200-μl labeling reaction was prepared by mixing 100 μL ribosomal subunits, 5 μM AcpS enzyme (for A1-tagged subunits) or 2 μM SFP synthase (for S6-tagged subunits) and 10 μM biotin-, Cy3-, or Cy5-CoA conjugates in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.3, 1 mM DTT and 15 mM MgCl2 and 50 mM KOAc. The reaction was incubated at 30 °C for 1 h. To purify the labeled ribosomes from free dye or biotin, the 200 μl reaction mixture was loaded on top of a 700-μl sucrose cushion (30 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.3, 1 mM DTT, 50 mM KOAc, 15 mM Mg(OAc)2, and 15% w/v sucrose) for ultracentrifugation at 470,000 × g at 4 °C for 90 min in an MLA-130 rotor. The pellet containing labeled mitoribosomal subunits was washed once with and then resuspended in the mitoribosome storage buffer and stored at −80 °C after flash-freezing with liquid nitrogen. The labeling efficiencies were estimated to be ~40–60%.

Purification of unlabeled and labeled mtIF2

The pET24a plasmid for the expression of mature human mtIF2 protein with a N-terminal His6-tag and a TEV cleavage site was a kind gift from the Ban lab26. For fluorescent labeling of mtIF2, a ybbR-tag (DSLEFIASKLA) was inserted to the plasmid, such that the recombinant protein was expressed as His6-TEV site-GGSGSG-ybbR tag-mtIF2. Plasmids were transformed individually into Escherichia coli Rosetta2 (DE3) pLysS (Novagen #71403-M), and overexpressed separately in LB media by induction at an OD600nm of 0.6–0.8 with 0.15 mM IPTG at 18 °C. For each purification, 3 L of cells were harvested after 16 h by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C in a Fiberlite F9 rotor (ThermoFisher), and resuspended in lysis buffer (200 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM HEPES, 10 mM Imidazole, 10% Glycerol, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.3 at room temperature) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 0.6 µM Leupeptin, 2 µM Pepstatin A, 2 mM Benzamidine. Cells were lysed by sonication (Fisherbrand model 505, 2 s on/2 s off, 6 min total on time at 40% amplitude), and lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 38,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C in an Fiberlite F21 rotor followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. Recombinant proteins were eluted with elution buffer (same as lysis buffer but with 50 mM KCl and 250 mM Imidazole). After a further 5 CV wash with 9:1 Low salt buffer: High salt buffer (same as Low salt buffer but with 800 mM KCl), proteins were eluted as 1 mL fractions using an 80 mL linear gradient to final 100% High salt buffer. Then TEV protease was added to this labeling reaction and further incubated at RT for 2 h. TEV protease, Sfp synthase, and the cleaved His6 tag were removed via a subtractive Ni-NTA gravity column equilibrated in TEV Buffer, with the flow-through collected. Free dye was removed via purification over 10DG-desalting columns (Bio-Rad, # 7322010) equilibrated in TEV Buffer. Dye-labeled mtIF2 proteins were subjected to a final SEC purification as for the unlabeled proteins. Fractions containing mtIF2 were concentrated using a 10 kD MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter (Millipore Sigma #UFC801024), aliquoted, flash frozen on liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Protein concentrations were determined via absorption at 280 nm for total protein, and Cy5 or Cy5.5 concentrations for labeled proteins using a nanodrop, respectively. The dye labeling efficiencies were ~70%.

Preparation of tRNAs

Due to technical challenges in obtaining purified human mitochondrial tRNAs in sufficient quantities for our in vitro studies, we have used an unmodified human mitochondrial tRNAMet in translation initiation assays26,52 and Cy3.5 labeled E. coli Phe-tRNAPhe for translation elongation assays45.

Unlabeled human mitochondrial tRNAMet

The tRNAMet was either purchased from TriLink or transcribed from a DNA template with a 5′-end T7 promoter and hammerhead ribozyme. For transcribed tRNAMet, the plasmid (ordered from Genscript) was linearized via digestion with BstNI for transcriptional termination at the 3′-CCA of tRNAMet. The tRNA was transcribed with the MEGAScript T7 Transcription kit (ThermoFisher #AMB13345). Purified and linearized plasmid was used as the template for in vitro transcription with T7 polymerase at 37 °C for 4 h at 16 mM MgCl2, during which the ribozyme self-cleaved (>80% efficiency). Mature tRNAMet was separated from precursor RNA and cleaved ribozyme via 10% acrylamide gel electrophoresis in the presence of 8 M urea. After excision of the tRNAMet band, the RNA was extracted 3× at room temperature for 12 h with 300 mM ammonium acetate, and ethanol precipitated. tRNAMet was resuspended in 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM Bis-tris, pH 7.0 and stored at −80 °C.

Cy5-labeled human mitochondrial tRNAMet

HPLC purified, synthetic human mitochondrial tRNAMet with U17 replaced with Uridine-C6 Amino linker was purchased from TriLink. The modified tRNAMet was labeled with excess sulfo-Cy5-NHS ester (Lumiprobe, #13320) in a buffer containing 50 mM Hepes-KOH, pH 8.0, and 0.9 M NaCl at 30 °C for 2 h followed by 4 °C for 16 h in the dark. The labeling reaction was stopped by addition of final 0.3 M NaOAc (pH 5.2). The tRNAs were extracted twice with phenol (pH 4.7) and once with chloroform before precipitated in 3 volumes of ethanol. The precipitated tRNAs were pelleted by centrifugation at 20,000 × g at 4 °C for 1 h and dissolved in 75 µL ddH2O. The resuspended tRNA solution was loaded onto a Bio-Spin 6 column (BioRad, # 7326221) pre-equilibrated with ddH2O to remove any excess free dyes. The labeling efficiency was ~100% based on absorbance measurements on a Nanodrop for A260nm (for total RNA) and Cy5. The purified Cy5-tRNAMet was flash-frozen on liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Charging and formylation of labeled and unlabeled tRNAMet

To charge the unlabeled or Cy5-labeled tRNAMet, the tRNAs were heated at 85 °C for 10 min and slowly cooled to RT. Then the tRNAs were added to final 5 µM in a charging reaction buffered with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0 at room temperature), 10 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM Spermine, 0.2 mg/mL BSA, 20 mM KCl, and 5 mM DTT. The charging reaction was initiated with the addition of final 2.5 mM ATP, 2 mM L-methionine, and 1.5 µM MetRS. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. If non-formylated Met-tRNAs were desired, the reactions were stopped by adding final 0.3 M NaOAc pH 5.0 and kept on ice until further purification. To formylate the Met-tRNAs, final 1 mM 10-formyltetrahydrofolate and 1 µM MTFMT26 were added to the charging reaction mixture and further incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Upon stopping the reactions with final 0.3 M NaOAc pH 5.0, the tRNAs were extracted once with phenol (pH 4.7) and once with chloroform. The water phase that contained tRNAs was mixed with 3 volumes of ethanol and kept at −20 °C for 30 min before centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The tRNA pellet was dissolved in 70 µL tRNA storage buffer (2 mM Mg(OAc)2 and 10 mM NaOAc pH 5.2). The charged tRNA was further purified with a Bio-Spin 6 column that were equilibrated with tRNA storage buffer. The charging efficiency was ~100% based on acid urea PAGE analyses of the final tRNA product.

Preparation of leaderless mRNAs

All the mRNA sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The chemically synthesized model leaderless mRNAs were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) or Millipore Sigma. To produce full-length human mitochondrial Cox3 and CytB leaderless mRNAs, the MT-COX3 and MT-CYTB genes were individually cloned into a pUC57 vector with a 36-nucleotide polyA sequence at the 3′ end (Genscript). The mRNA gene sequences were located to the 3′ of a T7 promoter sequence and a hammerhead ribozyme sequence26,52. The DNA templates for in vitro transcription of the mRNAs were amplified by PCR from the plasmids and purified with a Qiagen PCR purification kit. In vitro transcription with the MEGAScript T7 Transcription kit (ThermoFisher #AMB13345) was performed at 37 °C for 5 h and transcripts were incubated for 1 h at 60 °C to complete hammerhead cleavage. The mRNA was separated from the hammerhead ribozyme using 10% PAGE in the presence of 8 M urea. After excision of the appropriate band, the mRNA was extracted over night at 4 °C by shaking the gel pieces in 300 mM ammonium acetate and ethanol precipitated. The mRNA was dissolved in ddH2O and stored at −80 °C. To label the 3′ end of the full-length Cox3 and CytB mRNAs with a Cy3 dye or biotin, the mRNAs were oxidized with potassium periodate and then reacted with Cy3- or biotin-hydrazide, as described53.

Native gel shift assays

Purified 28S subunits (100 nM), mtIF2 (400 nM), mtIF3 (300 nM), unlabeled or Cy5-labeled fMet-tRNAMet (200 nM), and Cy3-mRNA (1 µM) were incubated in the indicated combinations at 30 °C for 10 min in 1X Initiation buffer (30 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM Spermine, 1 mM Spermidine, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5 at room temperature)16 supplemented with 2 mM GTP. Complexes were resolved on 0.8% agarose gels prepared and run in buffer containing 34 mM Tris-acetate pH 7.5, 57 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.3, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 10 mM Mg(OAc)2.

To assess 55S initiation complex formation, purified 28S subunits (50 nM), mtIF2 (400 nM), mtIF3 (300 nM), unlabeled fMet-tRNAMet (200 nM), and Cy3-mRNA (1 µM) were incubated in the indicated combinations at 30 °C for 5 min in 1X Initiation buffer supplemented with 2 mM GTP. Subsequently, 39S subunit (100 nM) or buffer were added, and samples were incubated for an additional 5 min at 30 °C prior to agarose gel electrophoresis as described above.

Gels were imaged to detect Cy3 and Cy5 fluorescence, and the fraction of mRNA bound to 28S subunits was quantified by densitometry using Image J. Three biological replicates were analyzed.

Single-molecule assays

Instrumentation

We performed all single-molecule experiments at 30 °C (or otherwise denoted) using a custom-built, objective-based (Nikon CFI Apochromat TIRF 60X Oil) total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope (Nikon Ti2E inverted microscope), equipped with a MultiSplit V2 system (Cairn Research), a Photometrics Kinetix sCMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics), a LUN-F 488/532/640 nm laser system (maximal output power for the three laser lines are 60/50/40 mW), a Legato 110 syringe pump (KD Scientific), and an H201-enclosure (Okolab) for temperature control. Within the MultiSplit, a dichroic beam splitter separated Cy3/Cy3.5 and Cy5/Cy5.5 emission (T635LPXR, Chroma), a filter cube (ET667/30 m, T685LPXR, ET720/60 m, Chroma) was used to separate Cy5 from Cy5.5 emission signals, and another filter cube (ET615/40 m, T585LPXR, ET572/23 m, Chroma) was used to separate Cy3 from Cy3.5 emission signals. For experiments with a single 532-nm laser excitation, the laser power was used at 30%; for ALEX excitation, the 532-nm laser was set at 30% power while the 640-nm laser at 20%; for simultaneous illumination with 532- and 640-nm lasers, the laser power was 30% and 20% for the two lasers, respectively. Movies were acquired using the Nikon NIS-Elements software package (v 6.02.03).

Imaging surface and buffer conditions

Glass TIRF slides and cover slips were passivated and functionalized as described54. Single-molecule data analysis

Experimental movies that captured fluorescence intensities over time were processed using MATLAB (R2018b or R2024a), and single-molecule traces were extracted using the Spartan software package (v.3.7.0)56, as described previously45,57,58. In Spartan, the channels for the different wavelengths were aligned according to the alignment parameters obtained with a movie that was acquired with a TetraSpeck bead (Thermo Fisher #T7279) slide. Fluorescence traces exported from Spartan were further analyzed with MATLAB using published scripts45,57,58. Spectral bleedthrough between channels was corrected based on parameters pre-determined using individual dye-labeled DNA oligos. Individual binding events were assigned manually based on the appearance and disappearance of the respective fluorescence signals. Experiments were performed with at least two replicates, and unless intractable due to inefficient complex assembly or inhibition, approximately 100–400 single molecules were analyzed.

Arrival times of a particular fluorescent component were defined as the time elapsed from the last molecular event (or the delivery of the labeled component if it was the first binding event in a reaction) until a burst of fluorescence for that component. Lifetimes were defined as the duration of the corresponding fluorescence signal. Kinetic parameters were extracted by fitting the cumulative probability distribution of the observed data to a single- or two-step kinetics model as previously described59:

the single-exponential model (for single-step reactions): \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C\left(t\right)=1-{e}^{-b\times t}$$\end{document}Ct=1−e−b×t

where t is time and b is the reaction rate;

the two-step reaction kinetics model: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C\left(t\right)=1-\frac{b}{b-a}{e}^{-a\times t}-\frac{a}{a-b}{e}^{-b\times t}$$\end{document}Ct=1−bb−ae−a×t−aa−be−b×t

where t is time, a and b are the reaction rates of the two steps.

In concentration titration experiments, the pseudo-first order rate constants were obtained via linear regression model fitting to apparent rate constants in Prism 10 (GraphPad Software Inc.), with reported error ranges denoting 95% confidence intervals. FRET efficiency was defined as EFRET = IA/(IA + ID), where IA and ID represent fluorescence intensities of the acceptor and donor fluorophores. The EFRET distributions were fit to single gaussian models, with error ranges denoting the standard deviation of the mean EFRET.

Statistical details of individual experiments, including number of analyzed molecules (including full and partial complexes) and the definition of error bars are indicated in the corresponding figure panels, legends or the source data file Source Data.

Cryo-EM analysis

Sample preparation

A ribosome mix was prepared by mixing 600 nM mtIF2, 800 nM fMet-tRNAMet, 300 nM mtIF3, and 300 nM 28S subunits in 1X Initiation buffer supplemented with 2 mM GTP and kept on ice. Separately, the model leaderless mRNA (5’pAUGUCAUAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACAACA-Biotin) was diluted to 2 µM in the same buffer and kept on ice. In parallel, in-house carbon-coated (3 nm) 300-mesh Quantifoil R2/2 grids (SPI Supplies, #4430C-XA, Lot# 1280410) were glow-discharged for 30 s in a PELCO EasiGlow glow discharger (Ted Pella, conditions: negative charge, 30 mA, 0.38 mBar, 30 s). After grids were prepared, 1.5 µL of the ribosome mix and 1.5 µL of the mRNA mix (both pre-warmed at room temperature) were combined and well mixed at room temperature before being loaded onto the grid. The sample was vitrified by plunging into liquid ethane on a Vitrobot plunger (setup at 4 °C, 100 % humidity, blotting time 3.5 s, wait time 10 s, blotting force 0). The total time elapsed from sample mixing until freezing in liquid ethane was ~60 s.

Cryo-EM data collection

Movies were collected on a Titan Krios equipped with the post-column BioQuantum energy filter (Gatan) and a K3 direct electron detector operated at 300 kV in the super-resolution mode with a pixel size 0.413 Å per pixel using SerialEM software (version 4.0)60. The dose rate was 19 e−/pixel/s and the total accumulated dose was 50 e−/Å2 over 50 frames. A nominal defocus range of −1.2 and −2.5 μm was used and the energy filter was set to 20 eV.

Cryo-EM image processing

Image processing was done using Cryosparc (v4.5.3)61. Imported movies were motion-corrected using Patch Motion Correction and Fourier-cropped to a pixel size of 0.826 Å. Following Patch CTF estimation, a curated set of 8077 micrographs were selected for subsequent processing. Following rounds of initial manual picking and template picking to generate quality templates from two-dimensional (2D) classes, 1.8 million particles were picked using template picking (Supplementary Fig. 11d). After one round of 2D classification, 521 K particles were selected (Supplementary Fig. 11 d). Three ab initio maps were generated from 100 K particles, and the full dataset was subjected to Heterogenous three-dimensional (3D) Refinement, from which a good 28S subunit class with 296,367 particles was selected (Supplementary Fig. 11e). 293,580 particles were re-extracted in a box of 600 pixels. These particles yielded a map of 3.24 Å with non-uniform 3D refinement62, but which suffered from heterogeneity in the density for the tRNA. To sort 28S particles of different compositions, a mask was generated using the structure of mtIF3 when bound to the 28S (PDB 6RW5)17 and used for masked 3D classification. 33,600 particles were identified containing density for mtIF3 and mtIF2, and these yielded a 28S–mtIF2–mtIF3 map of 3.01 Å (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Three classes contained strong density for mtIF2 and the fMet-tRNAMet; two of these were combined and subjected to further non-uniform 3D refinement and reference-based motion correction for a final set of 107,666 particles and resulted in a 28S PIC map of 2.50 Å (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). FSC curves for the 28S-mtIF2-mtIF3 map or 28S PIC map are shown in Supplementary Fig. 12.

Model building

PDB 7PO211 was used as the starting model for the 28S PIC structure. The models were manually edited using Coot63, ChimeraX64, and ISOLDE65 and refined using Phenix66. Data collection and model statistics are provided in Supplementary Table 3. ChimeraX was used for analysis of the structure and figure making.
