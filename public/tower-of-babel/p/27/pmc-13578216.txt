Efficacy and safety of a novel oral anti-vasculogenic mimicry agent, CVM-1118, in advanced well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors: a Phase IIa trial
Authors: Chia-Jui Yen, Ming-Huang Chen, Yen-Yang Chen, Li-Yuan Bai, Jen-Shi Chen, Jason Chia-Hsun Hsieh, Yu-Hsuan Shih, I-Chen Wu, Yen-Ling Chen, Chun-Man Chen, Ting-Yu Chao, Yi-Wen Chu, Du-Shieng Chien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHighly vascularised neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) are attractive targets for foslinanib (CVM-1118), which disrupts vasculogenic mimicry and induces apoptosis via tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1. We evaluated the efficacy and safety of CVM-1118 in advanced NETs.MethodsPatients with grades 1–2, well-differentiated lung, gastrointestinal, or pancreatic NETs, refractory or intolerant to one or more standard therapies and progressing within 6 months, received CVM-1118 (200–300 mg orally twice daily) in 28-day cycles. Primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS); secondary endpoints included objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), overall survival (OS), and safety.ResultsOf 43 enroled participants, 35 were efficacy-evaluable; most had grade 2 pancreatic (63%) or gastrointestinal (34%) NETs with two prior therapies. Median PFS was 10.5 months (95% CI, 5.6–22.3). ORR was 3%, DCR was 77%, and median OS was not reached (95% CI, 23.8–NR). Sensitivity analysis in the full analysis set (N = 43) showed a PFS estimate broadly aligned with the primary analysis (median, 8.4 months); patients with prior everolimus, sunitinib, or peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (N = 22; median, 8.3 months) showed similar findings. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 44%, mostly grades 1–2, with no serious events.ConclusionsCVM-1118 demonstrates favourable efficacy and safety in advanced NETs.Clinical Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT03600233.

Background

Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) are malignancies of neuroendocrine cells, commonly arising in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, pancreas, and lungs [1]. Current therapeutic algorithms for well-differentiated grade 1/2 NETs include somatostatin analogues (SSAs) as frontline antiproliferative therapy that delays tumour progression; targeted therapies, such as everolimus and sunitinib; peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), which has demonstrated efficacy across various lines of therapy; and chemotherapy. Treatment recommendations from oncology and NET societies vary and are continually refined on the basis of emerging research. Treatment considerations include patient preference, disease status, and accessibility to specific modalities, including specialised infrastructure required for PRRT.

NETs are highly vascularised, and the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factors presents prognostic value in neuroendocrine neoplasms [2]. In pancreatic NETs (pNET), vascular endothelial growth factor-targeted agents achieve an objective response rate of approximately 18%, indicating clinically meaningful antitumor activity [3]; sunitinib and cabozantinib are two such drugs that are Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for pNET. However, angiogenic signalling often diminishes in progressive disease [4], and tumours may maintain perfusion via vasculogenic mimicry (VM)—tumour cell-formed, endothelial-independent channels [5]. These features underscore persistent tumour heterogeneity and treatment resistance, reinforcing the need for new therapeutic strategies. Consistent with the highly vascular nature of NETs, intratumoral vascular alterations and VM have been observed across NET subtypes [6–8], supporting anti-VM as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1), a mitochondrial chaperone implicated in metabolic reprogramming, apoptosis resistance, and metastasis, is overexpressed in pNET tissues [9, 10]. Mechanistically, TRAP1 suppression can open the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, activate caspase-3/9–mediated apoptosis, and modulate succinate dehydrogenase, thereby destabilising hypoxia-inducible factor-1α—a master regulator of angiogenesis and VM [11, 12]. Foslinanib (CVM-1118), a multitargeted anticancer agent, inhibits TRAP1 and induces apoptosis, inhibits proliferation, and disrupts VM networks [13]. This reduces pro-angiogenic factor expression and impairs VM network formation via the Nodal signalling pathway [10]. Collectively, these data support evaluating CVM-1118 in NETs as a TRAP1-targeted approach with potential effects on downstream vascularisation pathways.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated anti-tumour activity of CVM-1118 in NET models in vitro and in vivo. In pancreatic (QGP-1) and bronchial (NCI-H727) NET cell lines, 72-hour incubation with CVM-1118 yielded half-maximal inhibitory concentration values of 34 nM and 145 nM, respectively—approximately two orders of magnitude lower than those observed with everolimus or sunitinib [14, 15]. In xenograft-bearing mice, continuous CVM-1118 dosing for 28 days produced 30–40% tumour reduction versus the control. Early clinical experience from two Phase I studies (CVM-001 and CVM-002) in patients with advanced cancer demonstrated a favourable safety profile of CVM-1118 [16], with drug-related adverse events being largely manageable GI symptoms (e.g., diarrhoea and nausea). Notably, one participant with advanced atypical lung carcinoid achieved prolonged stable disease and progression-free survival (PFS) of 20.9 months with 27% tumour shrinkage. Collectively, these data support further evaluation of CVM-1118 in NETs; therefore, we aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of CVM-1118 in patients with advanced NETs.

Methods

Study design and population

CVM-005 was an open-label, single-arm, multicenter, prospective Phase IIa study conducted at eight clinical centres in Taiwan. Eligible patients had functional or non-functional, histologically or cytologically confirmed unresectable and/or metastatic, well-differentiated, low-grade to intermediate-grade (World Health Organisation grades 1–2) NETs of pancreatic, GI, or lung origin that were refractory to or intolerant of one or more standard therapies. Other key eligibility criteria included age ≥20 years, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0–2, documented progressive disease within 6 months preceding enrolment, measurable disease according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours version 1.1, and adequate organ function.

The trial was approved by the institutional review boards of each of the eight clinical sites and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and local regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

Treatment administration and monitoring

Eligible participants received oral CVM-1118 capsules (200 mg twice daily [BID]) in 28-day cycles. Participants who tolerated the 200 mg BID dose for at least two cycles without intolerable toxicities (defined as any CVM-1118–related GI toxicity >grade 1 or non-GI toxicity >grade 2; prior higher-grade events were permitted if adequately managed with a low risk of recurrence as judged by the investigator and medical monitor) could have their dose increased to 300 mg BID for potential additional therapeutic benefit. In case of adverse events (AEs), dose adjustments in 50-mg decrements (e.g., 150 mg BID) were permitted, with up to two dose reductions allowed to resolve or stabilise the event. Concomitant use of somatostatin analogues (SSAs) was permitted when the dosage of SSAs had remained stable for ≥3 months prior to study entry and disease progression occurred during that period.

Tumour response was evaluated every 12 weeks using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours version 1.1. Safety was assessed via adverse events (AEs), laboratory values, physical examinations, vital signs, body weight, and electrocardiography at predefined intervals. AEs and laboratory values were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Treatment was continued until documented disease progression, death, intolerable toxicity, or consent withdrawal. Following the last dose of CVM-1118, survival follow-up was performed every 3 months for up to 2 years or until initiation of subsequent therapy, whichever occurred first.

Study endpoints and statistical analyses

The primary endpoint, PFS, was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. PFS was defined as the time from the first dose to disease progression or death, whichever came first. Secondary endpoints included the objective response rate (defined as the percentage of patients with a complete response or partial response), disease control rate (defined as the percentage of patients with a complete response, partial response, or stable disease), duration of response, time to progression (defined as the time from the first dose to disease progression), and overall survival (defined as the time from the first dose to death). Safety parameters were summarised using descriptive statistics. The effects of covariates, including tumour grade, primary tumour site, and prior therapy, were analysed using Cox regression models.

Based on pivotal studies of sunitinib and everolimus, which are FDA-approved drugs for pNET and epNET [17–19], the median PFS for participants in the placebo group ranged from 3.9–5.5 months. Therefore, the assumed median PFS for participants without any treatment was 5.5 months (hazard rate λ0 = 0.126027). With the minimum clinical benefit defined as an additional 2.5 months, the target median PFS for CVM-1118-treated participants was set at 8 months (hazard rate λ = 0.086643). Given an estimated 48-month recruitment period and a minimum 12-month treatment period, a sample size of 33 participants was required to achieve at least 80% statistical power at a 5% type I error rate.

The primary efficacy analysis of PFS was conducted in the efficacy-evaluable population, as pre-specified in the protocol, defined as participants who received at least one drug dose and had at least one post-baseline tumour assessment. This population was selected to ensure interpretable Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours-based efficacy evaluation. To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, supportive sensitivity analyses were performed in the full analysis set, which included all enroled participants. For these analyses, available survival data up to the last follow-up were used for time-to-event endpoints, and non-responder imputation was applied for ORR, as appropriate. Survival status was assessed every 3 months for up to 2 years following treatment discontinuation or until initiation of subsequent anticancer therapy, whichever occurred first, in accordance with the protocol.

Primary analysis was conducted using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) on data up to the clinical cutoff date set at 48 weeks after the last participant initiated drug administration (August 22, 2024).

Results

Patient characteristics

From December 15, 2018, to September 22, 2023, 55 patients were screened; 43 met all eligibility criteria and were enroled (Supplementary Table 1). Thirty-five participants who completed at least one post-baseline tumour assessment comprised the efficacy-evaluable population used for primary efficacy analysis (Fig. 1). Reasons for missing post-baseline tumour assessment and premature study discontinuation included treatment-unrelated deaths and withdrawal of consent to receive alternative treatments (Supplementary Table 2). Among the 35 evaluable participants, the median age was 58 years (range: 36–76), and 21 (60%) were men. Most participants had grade 2 tumours (77%), an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 (94%), non-functional tumours (54%), and primary tumours located in the pancreas (63%) or GI tract (34%). Approximately two-thirds were diagnosed more than 36 months before enrolment (Table 1). Participants received a median of two prior systemic therapies. Efficacy in later-line NETs

At a median follow-up of 19.8 months (range: 2.6–53.3 months), the efficacy-evaluable population demonstrated a median PFS of 10.5 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 5.6–22.3 months) (Fig. 2a). Progression-free survival rates at 6 and 12 months were 68% and 39%, respectively. Median time to progression was 10.5 months. In the post hoc sensitivity analysis, the full analysis set (N = 43) showed a median PFS of 8.4 months (95% CI, 5.6–13.9 months) (Supplementary Fig. 1a), despite the inclusion of 8 participants who discontinued treatment early for various reasons (Supplementary Table 2). Median PFS estimates in both analysis populations exceeded the pre-specified target PFS of 8 months defined in the study protocol and showed a consistent directional trend. During survival follow-up in the efficacy-evaluable population, 2 deaths (5.7%) occurred and 33 (94.3%) were censored at the data cutoff. Median overall survival was not reached (95% CI, 23.8 months to not reached). Given the evolving treatment landscape and the availability of everolimus, sunitinib, and PRRT, most participants with NET enroled in trials are likely to have prior exposure to one or more of these therapies. Following this, participants in the CVM-005 study who had at least one prior exposure to everolimus, sunitinib, or Lu-177 dotatate (N = 22) exhibited a median PFS of 8.3 months (95% CI, 3.8–11.1 months) (Fig. 2b). Further subgroup analysis based on the primary tumour site revealed median PFS durations of 8.4 months (95% CI, 5.6–11.1 months) in pNETs (N = 22) and 22.4 months (95% CI, 2.9–25.2 months) in GI or lung NETs (N = 13) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Among 15 (43%) participants receiving concurrent SSA, a longer median PFS of 22.4 months (95% CI, 5.6 months to not reached) was observed (data not shown).

According to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours version 1.1, one participant with pNET achieved a partial response with ≥83% reduction in tumour size, including a complete response of one of the two target lesions. This response lasted ≥27 months, and treatment was ongoing at the time of manuscript preparation. Stable disease was observed in 26 participants (74%) and progressive disease in 8 (23%). The objective response rate and disease control rate were 3% and 77%, respectively (Table 2). Safety profile

Safety was evaluated in all enroled participants (N = 43) who received at least one CVM-1118 dose. At the clinical cutoff date, 40 (95%) participants had discontinued treatment. The primary reasons for discontinuation were radiological disease progression in 22 participants (51%), adverse events unrelated to the study drug in 3 (7%, including 1 [2%] leading to death), treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) in 2 (5%), treatment delays exceeding 14 days leading to withdrawal per protocol in 2 (5%), clinical disease progression according to the investigator in 1 (2%), and withdrawal of consent in 9 (21%, including 6 [14%] due to receipt of other therapies, 2 [5%] due to worsening physical condition, and 1 [2%] due to participant decision). TRAEs occurred in 19 (44%) participants, with grade 3 TRAEs in 6 participants and grade 4 in 1; no grade 5 TRAEs were observed (Table 3). The most frequent grade ≥3 TRAEs (incidence, ≥5%) were anaemia (7%), decreased neutrophil count (5%), and increased alanine aminotransferase (5%). Dose reductions due to TRAEs occurred in 6 (14%) participants (from 200 mg BID to 150 mg BID in 5 participants, and from 200 mg BID to 100 mg BID in 1). Among the 13 participants for whom the dose was increased to 300 mg BID, TRAEs occurred in 3 patients (2 with grade 2 diarrhoea and 1 with grade 3 anaemia); all resolved following dose reduction. Discussion

Despite therapeutic advances, few novel targets for NETs have emerged over the past decade, reinforcing the need for treatment strategies beyond receptor tyrosine kinases, mammalian target of rapamycin, and somatostatin receptors. Given the vascular biology of NETs and the presence of VM, the TRAP1-targeted agent, CVM-1118, may provide a mechanism to inhibit tumour growth and VM formation. In this Phase II CVM-005 study, patients with advanced, well-differentiated, grade 1/2 NETs, refractory to or intolerant of one or more prior standard therapies and with recent progression within 6 months preceding enrolment, received oral CVM-1118 at an initial dose of 200 mg BID. The primary endpoint was met with a median PFS of 10.5 months in the efficacy-evaluable population, exceeding the prespecified efficacy target of 8 months.

To assess the robustness of this finding, sensitivity analyses were performed in the full analysis set, which included all enroled participants regardless of post-baseline tumour assessment. The median PFS in this population was 8.4 months, demonstrating a consistent treatment effect despite the inclusion of participants who discontinued early for non-treatment-related reasons. Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed similar overall patterns between the efficacy-evaluable and full analysis sets, supporting the reliability of the primary PFS result (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Importantly, the objective response rates were minimally affected by these early discontinuations, further reinforcing the consistency of the efficacy signal.

To contextualise these findings, the outcomes were descriptively compared with those of historical Phase III trials in NETs [17–19], where median PFS ranged from 11 to 11.4 months in generally earlier-line populations. The observed median PFS of 10.5 months in this later-line, single-arm study suggests clinically meaningful activity. Such cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in patient selection, prior therapies, and assessment schedules.

Exploratory subgroup analyses further supported the observed efficacy signal. Among 22 participants previously treated with at least one FDA-approved agent (everolimus, sunitinib, or Lu-177-dotatate), median PFS was 8.3 months, meeting the predefined target. Concomitant SSA use was associated with a numerically longer PFS (Supplementary Table 3), an observation that aligns with previous literature [20, 21]; however, this trend likely reflects baseline differences (fewer prior lines) rather than pharmacologic interaction. Similarly, although epNET showed a longer median PFS than pNET (Supplementary Table 3), the difference was not statistically significant and may reflect the more favourable prognosis of GI NETs. Nonetheless, the favourable PFS trend with CVM-1118 was observed regardless of tumour grade, suggesting a consistent exploratory efficacy signal across tumour grades.

CVM-1118 was generally well tolerated. TRAEs occurred in 44%, with the majority (81%) being grades 1–2; dose reductions were required in 21%; and no treatment-related serious AEs or grade 5 AEs were reported. Relative to published experience with approved agents (sunitinib, everolimus, cabozantinib), the apparent AE and dose-reduction rates with CVM-1118 were descriptively lower, noting inherent limitations of cross-trial comparisons [17–19, 22]. Overall, the manageable tolerability minimised dose modifications and supported treatment continuity.

Several limitations should be considered, including the single-arm design, modest sample size, heterogenicity in prior therapies, and missing post-baseline tumour assessment due to non-treatment-related discontinuation. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses across analysis populations yielded consistent efficacy trends, and the favourable tolerability profile supports further clinical evaluation. Taken together, these findings provide preliminary evidence of antitumor activity of CVM-1118 in later-line NETs with a favourable safety profile. However, given the single-arm design and heterogeneity of prior therapies, controlled studies will be required to further characterise its benefit–risk profile and define its role across NET subgroups.

Conclusions

CVM-1118 demonstrates therapeutic potential in patients with advanced, well-differentiated grade 1/2 NETs that have progressed after prior therapies, with evidence of clinically meaningful disease control and a manageable safety profile. These findings support further evaluation of CVM-1118 in controlled confirmatory trials.
