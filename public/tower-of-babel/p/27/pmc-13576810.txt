Care Needs for Patients Screened Positive for Cognitive Impairment and Delirium: A Cross‐Sectional Observational Study
Authors: Alison M Mudge, Sarah Anderson, Aparna Arjunan, Marguerite Byrnes, Margaret Cahill, Alison Craswell, Joel Dulhunty, Amanda Fox, Nicole C Gavin, Peter Lawrenson, Karen Lee‐Steere, Hui‐Shan Lin, Andrew Teodorczuk, Elise Treleaven, Laura White, Chloe Yap

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo describe the point prevalence of cognitive impairment in hospitalised adults and evaluate the association with care needs and perceived risks of complications.DesignMulti‐site cross‐sectional study on a single day in May 2023.MethodsTrained clinician auditors screened adult inpatients in acute medical, surgical, oncology, geriatric, mental health, convalescent, and rehabilitation wards for cognitive impairment using the 4AT in seven healthcare facilities and recorded need for support with basic activities of daily living, incontinence, and perceived risks of complications (falls, pressure injuries, and malnutrition). Introduction

Cognitive impairment is common in hospitalised adults (Bickel et al. 2018; Reynish et al. 2017). Previous cross‐sectional and cohort studies in Australia, the United Kingdom, and Germany have estimated 38%–44% of adult or older adult inpatients screen positive for cognitive impairment (Bickel et al. 2018; Casey et al. 2019; Mudge et al. 2022; Reynish et al. 2017). Cognitive impairment results from conditions including dementia, delirium, intellectual impairment, and acquired brain injury (e.g., stroke or trauma). Cognitive impairment can occur at any age but is much more common in those aged 65 years and older as dementia and delirium both increase with age. Dementia is a chronic progressive clinical syndrome due to a range of brain diseases which can affect memory, speech, cognition, emotions, behaviour, and mobility. Dementia is now the leading cause of death in Australia (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025a). Delirium is a common acute brain disorder which presents with fluctuating impairments of attention, arousal, perceptions, and other aspects of cognition (Wilson et al. 2020). Delirium may be precipitated by many serious acute illnesses, medications, alcohol or drug withdrawal, and surgery. It may be a presenting syndrome at hospital admission and is also the most common hospital‐acquired complication in Australia (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025b). There is a bidirectional relationship between delirium and other cognitive impairment: people with cognitive impairment have an increased risk of delirium (Ormseth et al. 2023), and episodes of delirium are associated with increased future risk of dementia (Gordon et al. 2024; Richardson et al. 2021).

Background

Experiencing cognitive impairment has important implications for the experience, quality, and outcomes of hospital care. Inpatients with cognitive impairment have reduced ability to care for themselves and increased risks of hospital‐associated harm (Fogg et al. 2018). They are more likely to be dependent on assistance from others for self‐care and have higher risks of falls, pressure injuries, malnutrition, incontinence, and functional decline, which contribute to longer stays, higher mortality, and higher post‐hospital care needs. (Bail et al. 2015; Fogg et al. 2018; Fox et al. 2021; Hartley et al. 2017; Reynish et al. 2017). They may also have difficulties with communicating their care needs, understanding and retaining information, interpreting and interacting with the complex hospital setting, and regulating their emotions (Johansson et al. 2018). Unfortunately, acute hospitals are often characterised by unfamiliar routines, noisy and cluttered environments, interactions with numerous unfamiliar people, complex communication and decision‐making exchanges, rushed and task‐focussed care, and uncertain roles for family carers (Lee‐Steere et al. 2020; Rosvik and Rostad 2020). It is therefore not surprising that patients with cognitive impairment often feel frustrated, insecure, and overwhelmed (Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020; Lee‐Steere et al. 2024; Parke and Chappell 2010), which can lead to responses such as avoiding or resisting care, agitation, and aggression directed towards staff and family carers who are trying to assist them (Hendlmeier et al. 2019; Parke and Chappell 2010).

With the rise in dementia prevalence (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2025b), managing the complex care needs of people with cognitive impairment is recognised as a pressing challenge in contemporary hospital care. There is still limited and poor‐quality evidence about specific interventions to improve the experience or outcomes of care for people with cognitive impairment or their carers (Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020). Recent reviews highlight a systemic need for investment in building workforce capacity to recognise and respond to the additional care needs and risks of inpatients with CI (Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020), but the magnitude of this challenge has not been clearly articulated. Although needing assistance with basic activities of daily living (ADL) prior to hospital admission is associated with cognitive impairment (Fox et al. 2021), ADL dependency is dynamic during hospitalisation. Surprisingly, few studies have specifically reported the inpatient care needs of patients with and without cognitive impairment during hospitalisation, and these studies have focussed exclusively on older adults and have not quantified the association (Hendlmeier et al. 2019; Mudge et al. 2022).

The Study

The aim of this study was to describe the point prevalence of cognitive impairment in hospitalised adults of all ages across an entire healthcare service and to quantify the association between patients' cognitive impairment and their physical care needs and risks. This study is part of a larger observational study which aimed to raise organisational awareness of cognitive impairment and measure adherence to the Australian Delirium Clinical Care Standards. Reporting against the standards is reported in a separate paper (manuscript under review).

Methods

Setting

This prospective, multi‐site cross‐sectional study was conducted in seven healthcare facilities that comprise the largest Hospital and Health Service in Australia. Metro North Health provides state‐funded hospital care for approximately 1 million people across metropolitan, regional, and rural areas in northern Brisbane and its surrounds. The study included participants admitted to acute, subacute, and mental health facilities during a 1‐week period in May 2023. Each facility was audited on a single day (except the largest hospital where audits took place over 2 days). Reporting follows Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (von Elm et al. 2007).

Participants

Patients were eligible to take part in this study if they had been admitted to hospital for at least 8 h in an acute (emergency short stay, medical, surgical, or oncology), mental health, geriatric evaluation and management, and convalescence or rehabilitation ward. Intensive care and obstetric wards were excluded as these services use different risk screening processes. Patient exclusions included those in a coma, critically ill (e.g., escalation to medical emergency response or intensive care unit review) or receiving terminal care, those unable to communicate in English (e.g., aphasia, severe uncorrected hearing impairment, and non‐English speaking), those aged < 18 years, and those who required security personnel in attendance at the time of data collection or were otherwise considered inappropriate to approach by the clinical nursing team leader.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved as a quality assurance and evaluation activity by the Chair of the Metro North Health Human Research Ethics Committee in February 2023 (approval number EX/2023/MNHA/94493), providing exemption from requiring individual consent to participation and record access based on negligible risk and public expectation of quality improvement. An information flyer about the evaluation was publicly available on participating wards, and verbal consent was sought from each participant to administer the 4AT on the audit day, as it is in usual clinical practice.

Outcomes and Measures

The primary explanatory variable was cognitive impairment, assessed using the 4AT screening tool administered by clinical auditors on the day of audit. The 4AT was designed as a short, simple tool to detect delirium and general cognitive impairment, suitable for use in hospital practice by any clinician with minimal training (Shenkin et al. 2019; Tieges et al. 2021). The 4AT assesses awareness, attention, orientation, and acute cognitive change, with a maximum (most impaired) score of 12.4AT scores are categorised as 0 (delirium or cognitive impairment unlikely), 1–3 (possible cognitive impairment), and 4 or more (possible delirium, with or without underlying cognitive impairment). A score of 4 or more showed a pooled sensitivity of 82%–93% and pooled specificity of 82%–92% for delirium in adults aged 65 or older in emergency departments, medical, surgical, and stroke wards (Tieges et al. 2021), while a score of 1 or more demonstrated sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 63% for expert diagnosis of dementia in adults aged 70 and older attending an emergency department (O'Sullivan et al. 2018). The 4AT had been implemented as a screening tool at the point of admission at all the study facilities.

The primary outcome variables were nurse‐documented need for supervision or assistance with basic activities of daily living (including mobilising, transferring, toileting, dressing, showering, and eating), incontinence, and nurse‐documented risk of falls, pressure injuries, or malnutrition. These data were collected from structured daily clinical care documentation (on paper or electronic records) maintained by nurses in each site based on their clinical judgement and updated daily.

Data were collected by volunteer clinician auditors (nurses, allied health professionals, and doctors) at each site who were coordinated and supervised by a multidisciplinary clinician investigator team. Data were collected onto a structured paper data collection tool designed by the investigators. Auditors received 1 h of face‐to‐face training in the data collection process, including the use of the 4AT, prior to completing the audit, from the site investigators who were senior medical, nursing, and allied health clinician researchers with clinical experience in delirium care. Auditors identified eligible patients on each ward in collaboration with the nursing team leader and administered the 4AT after obtaining verbal consent. Auditors noted whether one‐to‐one nursing was currently being provided. Basic descriptive demographic and hospitalisation information (date of admission, age, sex, and unit) were extracted from the clinical record.

Analysis

Data were entered by the site investigators into a secure web‐based REDCap database (Harris et al. 2019) hosted by the health service. Data were uploaded into SPSS Statistics, Version 29 for analysis. Participant characteristics were described and compared across 4AT categories (0 versus 1–3 versus 4 or more) using chi‐square test for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. The proportion of participants documented to require assistance or supervision for each ADL, incontinence of urine or faeces, and considered at high risk of falls, pressure injuries, or malnutrition were described by 4AT category. Binary logistic regression models were created for each outcome, adjusted for age, sex, unit type, and severity of illness on the audit day (as indicated by the Q‐ADDS score). The association between audit day 4AT category and each outcome was described using adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence intervals. For the main analysis, missing documentation of ADL and risks were coded as not requiring assistance or low risk, respectively. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore the potential impact of incomplete care needs data: first, a complete case analysis excluding those cases with incomplete care needs documentation for each outcome; and second, recoding cases with incomplete documentation as requiring supervision or assistance. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The investigator team trained 144 auditors across seven facilities including 83 nurses, 35 allied health professionals, 13 doctors, and 13 supervised health students. Of 1549 patients across 68 wards, 1263 were eligible for inclusion and 1145 (90.7%) were screened with the 4AT (Figure 1). The participants' mean age was 68 years (SD = 18 years), and 583 (50.9%) were female; median length of stay from admission was 6 days (IQR 2–17 days). Participants were cared for in acute medical (449, 39.2%), surgical (384, 33.5%) post‐acute rehabilitation or convalescence care (226, 19.7%), oncology or palliative care (50, 4.4%), and mental health (36, 3.1%) units. Screening with the 4AT showed that 167 (14.6%) scored 4 or more and 315 (27.5%) scored 1–3, meaning that 482 (42.1%) participants were identified as likely to have delirium and/or other cognitive impairment on the audit day. These participants were more likely to be male, older, in hospital for longer, and less likely to have a surgical condition compared to those scoring 0 (Table 1). In 408 participants aged less than 65 years, 294 (72.0%) scored 4AT 0, 78 (19.1%) scored 4AT 1–3, and 36 (8.8%) scored 4AT 4 or more. Of 737 participants aged 65 or older, 369 (50.0%) scored 0, 237 (32.1%) scored 1–3, and 131 (17.8%) scored 4 or more. A 4AT 4 or more (likely delirium) was most common in male and older participants and those in medical wards.

Discussion

This multi‐site, cross‐sectional study found that 42.1% of adults hospitalised in a large health service had evidence of possible cognitive impairment (4AT score 1 or more), including 14.6% with likely delirium (4AT score 4 or more). These findings are broadly comparable with previous prevalence studies. (Casey et al. 2019) (Bickel et al. 2018; Mudge et al. 2022; Reynish et al. 2017; Lindroth et al. 2024).

Our study adds to the literature by clearly quantifying the significantly higher support requirements of hospitalised adults with cognitive impairment, with 2–5 times the odds of requiring assistance with each ADL compared to those without cognitive impairment even after adjusting for major confounders. There was a clinically and statistically significant graded association between 4AT category and the need for greater assistance with each ADL and with continence care. For example, 65%–76% of participants with 4AT scores 1–3, and 69%–83% of those scoring 4 or more, required supervision and assistance with toileting, dressing, and washing, and half of participants scoring 4 or more were incontinent. Toileting, washing, dressing, and continence care require substantial nursing time (Jung et al. 2023) and represent intimate situations where it is understandable that a person experiencing challenges interpreting their environment and communicating their needs might react with resistance or aggression. The German General Hospital (GHoSt) study highlighted that both ADL dependency and cognitive impairment were significantly associated with nurse‐reported challenges to providing inpatient care: patients with cognitive impairment required significantly greater time for fundamental care provision and were much more likely to display aggression, agitation, and ‘fending off’ than those without (Hendlmeier et al. 2019).

In the face of complex care demands and limited resourcing, nurses are forced to make rationing choices about prioritising tasks, leading to missed or unfinished nursing cares (Mandal et al. 2019). We observed that 74% of participants with probable cognitive impairment and 82% of those with delirium required assistance with mobilising, which is the most frequently missed nursing care (Kalisch and Xie 2014). This vulnerability may contribute to the higher incidence of functional decline amongst patients with cognitive impairment (Hartley et al. 2017).

Our findings necessitate urgent planning of hospital nursing workforce and models of care in the context of the rising prevalence of dementia with population ageing. They suggest that the prevalence of cognitive impairment may be an important factor to include in nursing workforce planning frameworks. A common mitigating strategy for the imbalance between care needs and skilled nursing resources is ad hoc allocation of additional one‐to‐one nursing roles (‘specials’, ‘sitters’, or ‘close observation’) (Coyle et al. 2020). In our study, almost all (44/50, 88%) participants receiving one‐to‐one nursing had cognitive impairment on 4AT screening, and 30/50 (60%) had likely delirium. One‐on‐one nursing is frequently framed in terms of safety issues, particularly falls risk, rather than explicitly recognising and addressing the intrinsic challenges to meet the complex functional, emotional, and communication needs of the person with cognitive impairment (Coyle et al. 2020; Graham et al. 2021). This creates a paradox in care. Skill development in the expert care for patients with such high care needs is highly reliant on role modelling and experiential learning. However, one‐on‐one nursing roles are traditionally assigned to lesser skilled and inexperienced staff such as nursing assistants or even volunteers, often with poor delegation, leading to a custodial and passive approach that inevitably fails to realise its potential therapeutic benefit (Graham et al. 2021). Our findings highlight the importance of framing these roles as proactive functional support rather than a custodial, risk‐averse ‘watching’ role (Handley et al. 2017). Novel models for training and support of assistants, such as deploying trained and supported student nurses in assistant roles for patients with cognitive impairment, offer promise (Beccaria et al. 2024).

Another potential strategy for meeting the demonstrated high care needs of patients experiencing cognitive impairment is better engagement with family carers, who are important informants, advocates, and partners in care but often find hospitalisation a stressful experience (Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020). Effective engagement of family carers of people with dementia and delirium requires skills in family carer support and knowledge sharing, a welcoming and carer‐inclusive culture and environment, and respect for carers' individual circumstances and preferences (Aggar et al. 2023; Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020; Lee‐Steere et al. 2024).

Our demonstration of a graded association of care needs with higher 4AT scores suggests that preventing hospital‐acquired delirium could mitigate care needs. Effective programs for delirium prevention already exist (Burton et al. 2021), but they require strong policy support, organisational investment, and leadership to be implemented at scale (Bradley et al. 2005). Clinical and organisational leadership is also required to embed evidence‐based principles of enabling environmental design in all buildings caring for adult inpatients (Alzheimer's 2025). Staff, patients, and family carers consistently identify that poorly designed acute hospital environments increase confusion and reduce independence in patients with cognitive impairment (Rosvik and Rostad 2020; Siostrom et al. 2024). Better layouts, wayfinding, and amenities could support both staff and family carers to help patients with cognitive impairment maintain and restore their functional independence and reduce care needs.

There are some limitations in this research. The 4AT is not a gold‐standard diagnostic tool for cognitive impairment, although it has demonstrated good accuracy for delirium (score 4 or more) and reasonable performance for dementia (score > 0) in older adults (O'Sullivan et al. 2018; Shenkin et al. 2019) There may have been misclassification between delirium and other causes of cognitive impairment (Casey et al. 2019). There has been limited validation of the 4AT in adults aged under 65 years or those managed in mental health settings, so findings for these subgroups should be interpreted with caution. We note that point prevalence estimates will underestimate true delirium occurrence as delirium is a fluctuating condition. Thus, some participants may have experienced delirium that had resolved or partly resolved (with potential misclassification as other cognitive impairment). ADL and risk measures were based on existing structured nursing documentation based on clinical judgements, which may have introduced bias. Missing data may also have introduced bias as the primary analysis coded missing data as ‘not requiring assistance’, although missing data were similar across 4AT categories and sensitivity analyses confined to those with complete data for each outcome, or recoding all missing data as ‘requiring assistance’, showed similar findings. We enrolled data collectors from a range of professions and experience levels who were trained and supported on the audit day, but we did not test inter‐rater reliability. We collected a limited number of variables for multivariate adjustment. Data were collected from a single healthcare system in Australia, although included multiple facilities and ward types.

Conclusion

Our study findings quantify both the high prevalence of likely cognitive impairment in unselected hospitalised adults and the significantly greater support for fundamental care needs associated with likely cognitive impairment. These findings have important implications for clinical practice, education and training, and research as the prevalence of dementia rises with the ageing population. With almost half of adult inpatients experiencing cognitive impairment, the additional time and skills required to meet these needs must be accounted for in planning and delivering clinical care and preparing the nursing and broader clinical workforce. Strategies to meet the fundamental care needs for the growing number of patients with cognitive impairment may include adapting nursing workforce planning frameworks, better training and support for nursing assistants, active engagement of family carers, investment in delirium prevention programs, and improving physical design to support physical and cognitive function. Such approaches offer promise in supporting better care and experience for patients with cognitive impairment, carers, and staff, but will require clinical and organisational leadership (Abbott et al. 2021). Comprehensive evaluation of the impact of integrated approaches to cognitive impairment on patients, carers, staff, and health systems is urgently needed to inform the limited evidence base about interventions to improve outcomes (Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020).
