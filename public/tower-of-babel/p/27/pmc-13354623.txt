Comparison of the efficacy and safety of intense pulsed light (IPL), pulsed dye laser (PDL), and Q-switched neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd: YAG) laser in the treatment of moderate to severe inflammatory acne: a three-arm randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Haniyeh Keramati, Alireza Jafarzadeh, Mohammadreza Ghassemi, Mina Khosravi, Hamid Ansari, Azadeh Goodarzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of lasers and light devices in dermatology has significantly increased in recent years due to their ease of treatment, predictable clinical efficacy, and minimal side effects. Numerous studies have explored the use of lasers in acne treatment, including Q-Switched Nd: YAG 1064 nm laser, pulsed dye laser (PDL), intense pulsed light (IPL), diode lasers, and laser-based photodynamic therapy. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and side effects of Q-Switched Nd: YAG 1064 nm laser, PDL, and IPL in the treatment of acne. This pilot study was a three-arm randomized controlled trial involving 36 patients with moderate to severe inflammatory acne. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups. Group 1 received three PDL laser treatments at two-week intervals, Group 2 received three IPL treatments at two-week intervals, and Group 3 received three sessions of Q-Switched Nd: YAG 1064 nm laser treatments at two-week intervals. A total of 36 patients were randomly assigned to three groups (PDL, IPL, and Q-Switched Nd: YAG 1064 nm laser) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of each treatment for inflammatory acne. No significant differences were observed in acne lesion counts or severity scores between the groups at any treatment session (P-values ranging from 0.06 to 0.92). Lesion counts and severity decreased significantly within each group over the course of the study. Acne-related disability, measured using the Cardiff Acne Disability Index (CADI), decreased significantly from baseline to two weeks after the third treatment session in all groups, indicating improved quality of life. No statistically significant difference was observed between the groups (P = 0.45). Adverse effects were minimal, with mild erythema reported by one participant in the PDL group, and the Nd: YAG group reported less pain during treatment compared to the others. All three treatment modalities were associated with improvements in acne lesion count, lesion severity, and CADI scores. The reduction in CADI scores indicated reduced acne-related disability and improved quality of life. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups. Therefore, PDL, IPL, and Nd: YAG may be considered promising short-term treatment options for inflammatory acne.Keywords: IPL, PDL, Nd:YAG, Inflammatory acne, Intense Pulsed Light, Pulsed Dye Laser, Clinical Trial, RCT

Introduction

Acne vulgaris is a chronic inflammatory disease of the pilosebaceous unit, caused by increased sebum production induced by androgens, altered keratinization, inflammation, and bacterial colonization of hair follicles on the face, neck, chest, and back by Propionibacterium acnes [1]. Acne vulgaris affects 85% of adolescents, often beginning in preadolescence and continuing into adulthood [2]. It has a profound negative impact on mental health, including increased rates of mood disorders, psychiatric hospitalizations, school absenteeism, unemployment, and suicide [3]. Studies have shown that treatment improves quality of life [4]. Considering these key factors, effective and rapid reduction of acne lesions requires treatments that target as many of the underlying mechanisms as possible [5].

The main therapeutic categories include topical and systemic retinoids, antimicrobial agents, and systemic hormonal therapies [6]. Benzoyl peroxide is a broad-spectrum bactericidal agent effective in acne due to its oxidizing activity. Its main limitations are concentration-dependent skin irritation, dryness, and bleaching of clothing, hair, and bedding. Topical retinoids currently used in acne treatment include tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene, isotretinoin, and retinaldehyde. The primary adverse effect of topical retinoids is irritant dermatitis.

Topical antibiotics such as erythromycin and clindamycin are widely used and popular in acne management. Although generally mild, their adverse effects include erythema, peeling, pruritus, dryness, and burning. Oral antibiotics are indicated for moderate to severe inflammatory acne [7].

Isotretinoin is the most potent-known inhibitor of sebum production. Its efficacy at doses of 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day in acne treatment is well established and safe, although sometimes poorly tolerated due to cutaneous side effects [8].

Recently, 1726-nm laser systems (e.g., AviClear™ and Accure™) have been introduced as novel therapeutic options for acne vulgaris. These devices selectively target sebaceous glands through the principle of selective photothermolysis, leading to a reduction in sebum production while sparing surrounding tissues. Early clinical studies have demonstrated promising efficacy and safety profiles, suggesting that 1726-nm lasers may represent an effective addition to the current range of laser and light-based treatments for acne [9, 10].

Poor compliance, lack of sustained improvement, and potential adverse effects are common drawbacks of these treatments. In recent years, the use of lasers and light-based devices has increased significantly due to their overall ease of use, predictable clinical efficacy, and minimal side effects [11]. Laser-based acne treatments studied include Nd: YAG, pulsed dye laser (PDL), intense pulsed light (IPL), diode laser, and laser-based photodynamic therapy [7]. In this study, we evaluate the efficacy and side effects of three laser modalities—Nd: YAG, PDL, and IPL—in the treatment of acne.

Methods

Study design and setting

This three-arm clinical trial included patients with moderate to severe inflammatory facial acne. Following ethics approval and registration in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT), eligible participants were assessed during their initial dermatology visit. Baseline evaluations included standardized photography, calculation of the Leeds Acne Grading Score (Leeds score), and lesion counting.

Randomization

Participants were randomly allocated into three treatment groups using restricted randomization and the random allocation rule. Equal numbers of slips labeled 1, 2, and 3 (representing the three study groups) were placed inside identical opaque envelopes. After shuffling, each patient selected one envelope to determine group assignment.

Interventions

Group 1: PDL – Patients received three sessions of pulsed dye laser (PDL) at two-week intervals, with a 5 mm spot size, a fluence of 6.5 J/cm², and a pulse duration of 6 milliseconds.

Group 2: IPL– Patients underwent three sessions of intense pulsed light (IPL) therapy at two-week intervals, with wavelengths of 420–1200 nm, a fluence of 6 J/cm², and a pulse duration of 50 milliseconds.

Group 3: Q-Switched Nd:YAG 1064 nm laser. Patients received three sessions of fractional Q-Switched Nd:YAG 1064 nm laser therapy at two-week intervals, with an energy of 1200 mJ and a pulse width of 10 ns.

Data collection

Patient demographic information and baseline photographs were collected at study entry. Lesion counting and photography were repeated two weeks after each treatment session. Acne severity was assessed by counting inflammatory papules and pustules and applying the Leeds Revised Acne Grading System through blinded evaluation of photographs by a dermatologist. Patients also completed the CADI to assess subjective impact and quality-of-life changes. Lesion improvement percentages were calculated for each group.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 12–50 years, have moderate to severe inflammatory facial acne, and have no history of isotretinoin use. The exclusion criteria excluded individuals who did not provide informed consent, were pregnant or breastfeeding, had photosensitivity or rheumatologic conditions, had a history of hypertrophic scarring or keloids, or were using anticoagulant medications.

Sample size

Although previous studies have compared some of these modalities in pairwise designs, limited evidence is available from randomized trials simultaneously comparing PDL, IPL, and Q-switched Nd: YAG in a three-arm configuration. Therefore, this study was designed as a pilot trial with 12 participants in each group to explore the feasibility, preliminary effectiveness, and safety of this three-arm comparison.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included mean (standard deviation), percentages, and proportions. Group comparisons were performed using the T-test, ANOVA, and Chi-square tests. Trends over time were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA. A significance level of 0.05 was applied. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.

Regarding adverse effects, no long-term side effects were recorded during the 8-week period, and the treatments were well tolerated by the participants. In terms of safety, all three lasers were acceptable, and only one participant in the PDL group reported mild facial erythema for a few days after treatment. However, pain during treatment was lower with Nd: YAG compared to the other two groups.

Discussion

Acne vulgaris is one of the most common dermatologic conditions worldwide and can cause significant psychological and physical distress for patients, underscoring the importance of further advances in therapeutic approaches to enhance efficacy and minimize adverse effects. The studies reviewed in our work indicate that laser- and light-based therapies can be highly effective in the treatment of acne, both as monotherapy and in combination with other treatments. Notably, one limitation is that many of the cited studies had relatively short follow-up periods, which weakens the strength of the evidence, especially when compared with well-established treatments such as isotretinoin, for which stronger and long-term studies exist to evaluate its efficacy and safety [12].

Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that laser irradiation of the skin may generate reactive oxygen species, which can contribute to some of the reported side effects, such as erythema [13, 14]. Additional research is needed to better understand the efficacy and safety of these treatments, and studies with longer follow-up periods are essential to more accurately assess their long-term effectiveness. Nevertheless, current evidence in the literature indicates that laser- and light-based therapies are effective treatment options for acne and warrant further investigation.

Based on the findings of our study, the lesion counts decreased progressively across all three treatment groups (PDL, IPL, and Nd: YAG) from the first to the final treatment sessions. Although each group demonstrated a reduction in lesions over time, the differences between the groups were not statistically significant at any assessment point. By the end of the treatment course and during follow-up, all groups showed continued improvement, yet the overall pattern indicated comparable efficacy among the three laser modalities.

When comparing lesion severity using the Leeds score, all three treatment groups (PDL, IPL, and Nd: YAG) showed a gradual reduction in severity scores across successive treatment sessions and during follow-up. Although the overall trend indicated improvement in each group, the differences between them were not statistically significant at any stage of assessment. This suggests that while each laser modality contributed to a decrease in acne severity, their relative effectiveness was comparable.

When patient satisfaction was assessed using the CADI index, all three treatment groups (PDL, IPL, and Nd: YAG) reported similar levels of satisfaction after the first session, with no significant differences observed. By two weeks after the third session, satisfaction scores had improved across all groups, yet the comparisons again revealed no statistically significant differences, indicating that patient-reported outcomes were comparable among the three laser modalities.

Pulse duration is an important parameter in determining the efficacy and safety of laser and light-based therapies for acne treatment. In our study, the pulse duration varied between the different modalities: 6 milliseconds for PDL, 50 milliseconds for IPL, and 10 ns for Q-Switched Nd: YAG. Previous studies have suggested that shorter pulse durations may lead to more superficial treatment, potentially reducing the risk of side effects such as erythema and hyperpigmentation. On the other hand, longer pulse durations might penetrate deeper into the skin, which could enhance the treatment effect but also increase the likelihood of side effects [9, 15].

Recently, 1726-nm laser systems have emerged as energy-based treatments for acne through selective targeting of sebaceous glands. However, this mechanism should not be extrapolated to the modalities assessed in the present study. PDL, IPL, and Q-switched Nd: YAG act through different vascular, photothermal, and photoacoustic mechanisms and are not considered sebum-selective treatments. Therefore, the 1726-nm literature is mentioned only as background regarding emerging acne therapies [15–18].

Choi et al. investigated the therapeutic effects of PDL versus IPL in a randomized, single-blind clinical trial. Over a 14-week period, 17 patients with active inflammatory facial acne were treated with PDL (585 nm, 40-ms pulse duration, 8–10 J/cm² fluence) and IPL (530–750 nm, 2.5-ms pulse duration, 7.5–8.3 J/cm² fluence). Each modality was applied to one side of the face. Patients received four treatment sessions at two-week intervals and were followed for eight weeks after the final session.

After completion of the interventions, the results showed no significant difference in lesion counts between IPL and PDL treatments. The IPL-treated sides demonstrated up to a 50% reduction from baseline in inflammatory acne lesions after the first treatment and a 34% reduction after the final treatment. However, eight weeks after treatment, 45% of the baseline lesion count had returned.

The PDL-treated sides showed a slower but more sustained improvement, with a 64% reduction after the first session and a 14% reduction in lesions at the end of the eight-week post-treatment period. In both treatment modalities, inflammatory and non-inflammatory acne lesions showed greater improvement following PDL compared with IPL.

In terms of patient satisfaction, scores were higher in the PDL group, indicating greater patient satisfaction with this treatment modality. Histopathological evaluations also demonstrated a more pronounced reduction in inflammation and increased expression of TGF-β during PDL treatment [19].

In our study, no superiority of the PDL group over the IPL group was observed, which may be attributable to the small sample size. Moreover, the study by Choi et al. was conducted using a split-face design, which was not feasible in our three-arm study. However, in terms of higher patient satisfaction in the PDL group compared with the IPL group, our findings were consistent with those of Choi’s study [19].

Across various investigations, PDL has been suggested as a useful modality when combined with other adjunctive acne treatments. In a study by Ibrahim et al., which compared the efficacy of oral isotretinoin alone (ISO) with oral isotretinoin combined with PDL (ISO/PDL), the trial results showed that although both groups demonstrated significant improvements in acne severity, the combination ISO/PDL group exhibited markedly greater improvement at the three- and six-month follow-ups. Furthermore, the ISO/PDL group experienced fewer adverse effects and required a lower cumulative dose of isotretinoin to achieve successful outcomes compared with the ISO-only group [20].

The 1320-nm Nd: YAG laser is a popular non-ablative treatment with clinical benefits in skin rejuvenation and acne scarring. However, several studies have also examined its usefulness in the treatment of active acne. Orringer et al. conducted an interventional study involving 46 patients with facial acne who had a minimum Leeds acne severity score of 2 out of 12. During the trial, participants received non-ablative laser treatment with the 1320-nm Nd: YAG laser at three-week intervals. One half of each patient’s face was evaluated clinically at baseline and at weeks 7 and 14, with assessments including systematic counts of papules, pustules, cysts, and comedones.

According to the study results, laser treatment caused considerable discomfort in patients: 74% reported at least moderate discomfort, 24% rated their discomfort as significant, and 2% described it as severe. Despite the pain experienced during treatment, the procedures were generally well tolerated, with only a few transient adverse effects observed, including two cases of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and two cases of focal blistering [21].

In another clinical trial, Deng et al. evaluated 35 individuals with moderate to severe acne who underwent treatment with a fractional 1320-nm Nd: YAG laser in a regimen consisting of six sessions at two-week intervals. According to the study findings, the laser treatment was well tolerated and resulted in a 57% reduction in inflammatory lesions and a 35% reduction in non-inflammatory lesions. In addition, there was a significant 30% decrease in skin sebum levels following treatment [22].

A randomized clinical trial conducted in 2020 by Monib et al. compared the efficacy of long-pulsed 1064-nm Nd: YAG laser (Synchro HP, DEKA) with IPL treatment in managing inflammatory and non-inflammatory acne lesions. In this study, 30 patients with Fitzpatrick skin types III–V were randomly assigned to either the Nd: YAG or IPL group. Both treatments were administered in three sessions at two-week intervals.

The reduction in lesions was significantly greater in the Nd: YAG group after each session compared with the IPL group. Moreover, the Nd: YAG-treated group showed a markedly greater improvement in non-inflammatory lesions than the IPL group. Patient-reported satisfaction scores also favored the Nd: YAG group, with a statistically significant difference observed after the third session [23].

Adverse effects such as erythema and edema were comparable between the groups; however, crust formation was significantly more common in the IPL group. Overall, although both treatments were effective, Nd: YAG laser therapy demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing acne lesions—particularly non-inflammatory lesions—along with higher patient satisfaction and fewer side effects [23].

Whenever referring to the Nd: YAG laser, it is important to specify that the laser used in our study is the Q-Switched Nd: YAG. This is because the Q-Switched Nd: YAG operates through a different mechanism of action compared to the long-pulse Nd: YAG. The Q-Switched Nd: YAG delivers short, high-energy pulses in nanoseconds, which leads to more targeted tissue disruption and less heat buildup in the surrounding skin. In contrast, long-pulse Nd: YAG lasers deliver energy over a longer duration, allowing for deeper tissue penetration but with a higher risk of thermal damage. Understanding these differences is crucial as they may contribute to varying outcomes in terms of both efficacy and side effects in acne treatment [21–23].

While most acne studies cited in the literature utilize long-pulsed or fractional Nd: YAG lasers, which act primarily via dermal heating and sebaceous gland modulation, we selected the Q-Switched 1064 nm Nd: YAG for its unique mechanism of action. The Q-Switched Nd: YAG is known for its ability to target pigment and induce selective photoacoustic effects, which can potentially aid in reducing hyperpigmentation and inflammatory lesions in acne patients. Although it has minimal thermal diffusion, its ability to selectively target melanin and hemoglobin can be beneficial in treating post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) associated with acne. Additionally, Q-Switched Nd: YAG lasers are well tolerated by patients with darker skin types, as they carry a lower risk of inducing heat-related side effects such as hyperpigmentation or scarring, which can be more prevalent with longer-pulsed lasers. Thus, the choice of Q-Switched Nd: YAG was made to specifically address these concerns and provide an effective and safe treatment option for acne, especially for patients prone to pigmentation issues.

The exact mechanism by which fractional Q-switched Nd: YAG laser may improve active inflammatory acne remains incompletely understood. Unlike long-pulsed lasers or sebaceous gland–selective wavelengths such as 1726 nm, fractional Q-switched Nd: YAG delivers very short pulses and is expected to produce relatively limited bulk dermal heating. Therefore, its effect should not be primarily attributed to direct sebaceous gland destruction or selective sebaceous photothermolysis. A more plausible explanation is that fractionated Q-switched irradiation induces photomechanical microinjury, including laser-induced optical breakdown–like effects, followed by wound-healing responses, dermal remodeling, and modulation of perifollicular inflammation. These changes may indirectly improve the inflammatory microenvironment of acne lesions. Previous studies have reported clinical improvement of active acne using 1064-nm Nd: YAG modalities, including Q-switched and combined long-pulsed/Q-switched systems; however, the precise mechanism remains uncertain. Further histologic, sebumetric, and inflammatory biomarker-based studies are required to clarify the role of fractional Q-switched Nd: YAG laser in active acne [24–26].

In comparing our findings with those of previous studies, all three modalities were associated with reductions in acne lesion count and severity. Although numerical differences were observed among the treatment groups, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, PDL, IPL, and Nd: YAG may be considered comparable short-term treatment options for patients with moderate to severe inflammatory acne.

Limitations

An important limitation of the present pilot study was its short follow-up period. Participants were evaluated up to two weeks after the third treatment session; therefore, the durability of the observed improvements beyond this period cannot be determined. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are required to assess the long-term effectiveness of these modalities.

Because this study did not include histologic evaluation, sebum measurement, sebaceous gland imaging, or inflammatory biomarker assessment, we cannot determine whether fractional Q-switched Nd: YAG laser directly affects sebaceous gland activity. The proposed mechanism should therefore be considered hypothetical.

Conclusion

All three modalities were associated with significant within-group reductions in inflammatory acne lesion counts and severity scores. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the treatment groups. Similarly, CADI scores decreased in all groups, indicating reduced acne-related disability and improved quality of life, without a statistically significant between-group difference. These findings suggest that PDL, IPL, and Q-switched Nd: YAG may have comparable short-term effectiveness and acceptable safety profiles in the treatment of moderate to severe inflammatory facial acne.
