Deep SVDD-based anomaly detection of machine sound signals using active signal refinement
Authors: Jong Hyuk Lee, Min Young Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting abnormal industrial audio is essential but often hindered by irregular background noise and data imbalance. This study proposes a Deep SVDD-based framework integrated with novel preprocessing to enhance detection robustness. The methodology consists of two core modules. First, Shifted Sequential Segmentation applies overlapping segmentation and time-shifting to Log-Mel Spectrograms. This augments training data and ensures temporal invariance by capturing structural features independent of frame boundaries. Second, Active Signal Refinement utilizes the Sobel operator to distinguish signal regions based on gradient magnitude, enabling effective noise subtraction to isolate valid dynamic patterns. Experimental results on the MIMII dataset demonstrate superior performance, achieving an AUC of 0.83 compared to 0.70 for existing Deep SVDD and 0.61 for autoencoders.Keywords: Time series, Anomaly detection, Soft-boundary Deep SVDD, Mel-spectrogram filtering, Industry 4.0Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In modern industrial environments, continuous monitoring of machinery is recognized as an essential element for maintaining process stability, ensuring product quality, and preventing production stoppages and economic losses caused by unexpected equipment failures. Particularly, as the transition to smart factories accelerates, real-time condition diagnosis and early detection of abnormal signs have emerged as increasingly critical challenges1,2. In this context, various sensor-based detection methods are being utilized. Notably, sound signal-based monitoring holds significant advantages due to its non-contact nature, ease of installation, and minimal constraints imposed by equipment structure or operating conditions. Consequently, unlike vibration or thermal sensors, sound-based analysis has the potential to detect subtle anomalies or early defect states more precisely. This is because it comprehensively reflects the frequency and temporal changes in sounds generated during machine operation. However, despite these advantages, sound-based anomaly detection in industrial environments faces several technical constraints. In actual factories, as shown in Fig. 1, complex background noise from the simultaneous operation of various machines and exhaust sounds can distort abnormal signals or degrade detection performance. In addition, anomalies occur infrequently, exhibit irregular patterns and vary widely, making it difficult to obtain sufficient labeled defect data. For these reasons, unsupervised anomaly detection methods are the primary approach. Existing reconstruction-based or traditional clustering methods often struggle with generalization in complex industrial environments. Specifically, autoencoders may overfit to background noise during the reconstruction process, while simple clustering can suffer from unstable convergence when dealing with high-dimensional acoustic features. To address these challenges, this study proposes a Soft-boundary Deep Support Vector Data Description(SVDD) framework tailored for machine sound anomaly detection. Unlike reconstruction-based approaches, this framework directly optimizes the distributional compactness of normal data. The proposed method enhances both the data preprocessing and representation learning stages to improve the robustness and precision of fault detection. First, the raw audio is converted into a Log-Mel spectrogram, followed by the application of shifted sequential segmentation. This technique decomposes the spectrogram into overlapping temporal segments by shifting each segment by a fixed number of frames. This approach enables the model to leverage temporal continuity between segments while enhancing data diversity. In addition, we introduce Active Signal Detection Refinement, a Region of Interest (ROI) extraction technique based on Sobel filtering. Beyond simply filtering out background intervals with low information content, this module actively refines the signal by subtracting residual noise components identified through gradient magnitude analysis. By focusing exclusively on signal regions with significant spectral transitions, the proposed model prevents performance degradation caused by irrelevant acoustic artifacts and enables more discriminative feature learning. This approach ensures the capture of essential dynamic patterns rather than background noise. Furthermore, by guiding the learning process with noise-suppressed data, it facilitates a more flexible representation of normal data. This flexibility is particularly crucial when defining the decision boundary for anomaly detection, as it effectively complements the rigid constraints of traditional hard-boundary methods. While to represent the normal state, Deep SVDD learns to compress normal features toward a center c in the latent space, thereby forming a minimum-volume hypersphere boundary. However, a strictly hard-boundary approach Deep SVDD learns to compress normal features toward a center c in the latent space to form a minimum-volume hypersphere, a strictly hard-boundary approach risks distorting the decision boundary when trained on noisy real-world data. In contrast, the soft-boundary approach adopted in this study employs an outlier-permissive parameter ν, allowing certain data points to fall outside the boundary. This flexibility ensures superior robustness against outliers and noise, particularly in challenging high-noise industrial environments.

Related works

Sound-based anomaly detection in industrial environments

Sound-based anomaly detection3has emerged as a compelling alternative to traditional vibration or current sensors4–7 due to its non-contact nature, ease of installation, and the rich time-spectral information it provides8,9. Early research primarily relied on manual feature engineering, extracting coefficients such as Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC)10,11 and spectral centroids12,13to model steady-state conditions using statistical methods like Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)14,15. However, these approaches often struggled to adapt to the irregular and complex noise environments typical of actual factories. With the advancement of deep learning, models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)16,17 and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)18,19 have been introduced to learn robust representations directly from log-Mel spectrograms. Despite these improvements, reconstruction-based models like autoencoders tend to overfit to background noise, which can contaminate the latent space and degrade the discriminative power between normal and abnormal signals. Consequently, there is a growing academic shift toward one-class classification strategies that directly optimize the distributional compactness of normal data to ensure greater robustness against industrial noise20,21.

Deep SVDD-based anomaly detection

Support Vector Data Description (SVDD)22 , which has played a pivotal role in the field of One-Class Classification, evolved from the theoretical foundation of Support Vector Machines (SVM)23. By extending SVM’s principles to unlabeled data distribution modeling, SVDD effectively identifies anomalies by compressing normal data into a minimum-volume hypersphere centered around a vector c. While traditional kernel-based SVDD faced limitations regarding high computational costs and the constraints of manual kernel selection, Deep SVDD overcame these challenges by employing neural networks to automatically learn nonlinear representations in a low-dimensional latent space24. However, existing Deep SVDD frameworks often utilize fixed-size patches or non-overlapping segments, which fail to fully capture the essential temporal continuity of industrial acoustic signals. Furthermore, the traditional ’hard-boundary’ approach, which forces all training data within a strict radius, is highly sensitive to outliers and background noise common in factory settings25. To address these structural limitations, this study proposes a framework that integrates Shifted Sequential Segmentation to preserve temporal context and a soft-boundary strategy to form more flexible and robust decision boundaries.

Valid signal classification

Among mechanical sound signals, the operating sounds of valves contain both active signal intervals and inactive intervals. These inactive intervals consist of background noise or crosstalk noise generated independently of actual machine operation , which can cause the model to learn irrelevant noise information and potentially degrade anomaly detection performance. To address this issue, existing studies have employed energy-based26 or entropy-based27 classification to select regions of interest based on fixed thresholds. However, these methods often struggle to distinguish valid signals in complex industrial environments with high noise floor. Additionally, some studies have attempted to enhance model learning efficiency by applying Voice Activity Detection techniques to remove non-signal intervals28–31. This study applies a novel effective signal classification method utilizing the Sobel filter, specifically tailored to the characteristics of valve data. The Sobel filter is a powerful operator for detecting abrupt energy shifts in spectrograms , effectively extracting interval components that correspond to actual machine operation in the time-frequency domain32. Furthermore, since industrial acoustic data inherently contains repetitive mechanical mechanisms, preserving the temporal flow is of paramount importance. Accordingly, this study adopts an overlapping segmentation approach to strictly maintain the continuity of time-series signals while simultaneously expanding the dataset volume. Beyond mere data augmentation, this method serves as a foundational step to minimize the loss of features that may occur at segment boundaries, enabling the model to learn the periodic patterns of machine operation with greater precision.

Dataset

This study utilized the Malfunctioning Industrial Machine Investigation and Inspection (MIMII) dataset, which provides acoustic data recorded in real factory environments for four machine types: Fan, Pump, Valve, and Slider25. Each machine is further classified into four distinct equipment IDs (00, 02, 04, 06) to evaluate the model’s generalization performance across varying structural and acoustic patterns. A critical aspect of this research is the evaluation of robustness against background noise, which is facilitated by the dataset’s three Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) conditions: 6 dB, 0 dB, and –6 dB. In our experiments, these SNR levels are treated as independent environmental variables. The model is trained using only normal data from a specific SNR condition and subsequently tested on both normal and abnormal data from the same noise level. This stratified approach allows for a rigorous comparative analysis of how increasing noise intensity particularly at the challenging –6 dB level impacts the precision of anomaly detection. Regarding data composition, we implement an unsupervised anomaly detection scenario where the model learns the steady-state representation of normal operations. The training set consists of the available normal data for each equipment ID. To maintain a balanced and reliable evaluation, the test set is constructed by combining all available abnormal data with an equal number of randomly selected normal data points from the original pool. This random partitioning strategy ensures that the model’s detection capabilities are validated against diverse machine malfunctions while minimizing bias in the evaluation metrics.

Shifted sequential segmentation

Overlapping equal partitioning

All input sound signals are converted into log-Mel spectrograms to extract key features while mimicking the human auditory system. This conversion standardizes the data, enabling deep learning models to learn frequency patterns efficiently. During the conversion process, the Fast Fourier Transform window size was set to 1024, the hop size to 512, and a 64 element mel filter bank was applied. By applying a logarithmic scale at this point, the energy differences in machine noise were mitigated, and the nonlinear frequency perception characteristics were reflected. To maintain the temporal continuity of machine operation signals and enhance data usability, the spectrograms are divided into overlapping segments. Conventional splitting of time-series data without overlap can result in the loss of critical features at segment boundaries. Therefore, by partitioning the data so that adjacent segments overlap by a fixed length, as shown in Fig. 3, information loss is minimized and the model can better learn the features at transition intervals. This approach also effectively increases the volume of practical training data. The data segmentation process is conducted through a series of systematic stages designed to minimize feature loss in time-series signals. The process begins with Base Partitioning, which divides the entire time frame T into three equal parts to define the standard segment length, . Subsequently, in the sequential segment extraction stage, three primary segments (1st, 3rd, 5th) are isolated, corresponding to the start, middle, and end of the signal. To bridge the informational gaps between these primary segments, the intermediate overlap generation stage follows. Rather than simple expansion, two additional segments (2nd, 4th) are generated by calculating specific start and end indices centered around the two internal dividing lines. This ensures a consistent 50% overlap with adjacent segments, maintaining temporal continuity across the partitioned data. This refined indexing method results in a total of five segmented areas of equal length, facilitating both data augmentation and the stable learning of continuous mechanical patterns.

Time shifting augmentation

To further enhance data diversity, a Time Shifting technique is applied to each of the five previously generated segments (Parts 1–5) as shown in Fig. 4. By shifting the time axis by one frame, five additional local windows are constructed for each part, enabling the model to focus on the inherent structural characteristics of the signal rather than relying on precise start points or frame boundaries. Through this process, the final number of samples generated from a single data point increases fivefold to a total of 25 (5 parts × 5 frame shifts). This significant expansion of the training set prevents overfitting and allows the model to efficiently learn frequency variation patterns within short time intervals, ultimately improving generalization performance. Consequently, this approach ensures that the input data maintains local temporal patterns while accurately reflecting the global structural characteristics of the entire segment.

Active signal refinement

The Valve data in the MIMII dataset irregularly contains inactive intervals where only background noise exists between action cycles. These intervals introduce unnecessary noise into the training data, hindering the anomaly detection model from clearly learning normal patterns. To address this issue, this study designed an Active Signal Refinement(ASR) utilizing the Sobel Operator. The Sobel operator is uniquely suited for Log-Mel Spectrograms in this context because it effectively quantifies the rapid energy fluctuations characteristic of mechanical transients. By calculating the gradient magnitude, the ASR module distinguishes between steady-state industrial background noise and the high-contrast edges of valid acoustic events, such as valve operations. This allows the model to prioritize regions with high information density, ensuring that the learned hypersphere boundary is defined by essential structural patterns rather than irrelevant noise artifacts. To maximize information density and guide the model to focus on the core patterns of valve operation, the refinement process is executed in three stages: (1) Global Threshold Estimation, (2) Segment Validation and Masking, and (3) Background Noise Subtraction.

The proposed Soft-Boundary Deep SVDD is compared against four baselines: Basic_AE31, which relies on reconstruction error, Autoencoder model combined with attention mechanism33, which learns inter-frame correlations through a Transformer encoder, Basic_SVDD32, which utilizes non-overlapping 64x64 patches and Basic_Glow34, a flow-based generative model that estimates the exact log-likelihood of data using invertible 1 × 1 convolutions.

As shown in Table 1, the baseline models Basic_AE, Basic_Attention, and Basic_SVDD exhibit poor performance, with AUC values mostly hovering between 0.419 and 0.525. While Basic_GLOW shows a relatively strong AUC of 0.872 at 6dB, its performance drops significantly to 0.701 as the noise increases to -6dB. These results indicate that traditional reconstruction-based or flow-based methods struggle to maintain stability against the complex background noise of valve operations. In contrast, the proposed framework achieves substantial performance gains through a strategic, stepwise integration of the SSS and ASR modules: When the SSS was applied alone (SSS: O, ASR: X), the AUC at 6dB significantly increased to 0.799, which confirms that considering temporal continuity through overlapping segments is crucial for stable feature extraction. The integration of the ASR module with SSS (SSS: O, ASR: O) yielded the highest performance across all Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) conditions. Notably, at 0dB, the pAUC improved significantly from 0.596 to 0.712, demonstrating that ASR’s gradient-based ROI extraction effectively suppresses residual noise and reduces false-positive rates. Furthermore, the proposed model exhibited exceptional robustness under extreme conditions. Even in the most challenging -6dB environment, the model maintained a high AUC of 0.752. This performance at the highest noise level actually outperforms most baseline models tested in much cleaner 6dB conditions, suggesting that the synergy between temporal segmentation and active refinement allows the system to isolate relevant valve signals despite overwhelming acoustic interference.

As shown in Table 2, the proposed framework demonstrates superior performance across various machine IDs compared to traditional methods. The proposed framework reaches its full potential when SSS and ASR work in tandem, delivering the most balanced, stable, and superior performance across the vast majority of metrics and machine IDs. This result definitively confirms that while SSS provides a solid foundation for stable feature extraction, the ASR module is the critical innovation for refining decision boundaries and ensuring the high-precision anomaly detection required for demanding industrial environments. While a direct comparison between SSS and ASR may seem functionally disparate, it highlights a crucial finding: simply increasing data volume via SSS is insufficient for robust anomaly detection in high-noise settings. The superior performance of the ASR-integrated model suggests that active refinement of the signal’s ROI captures discriminative features that data augmentation alone cannot provide. This structural purification is particularly vital for industrial valve monitoring, where the focus shifts from general discriminative power (standard AUC) to the low false-alarm region (pAUC). By optimizing this “sweet spot,” the proposed model ensures the high-precision detection necessary for real-world operations, where minimizing false positives is paramount to maintaining efficiency.

Conclusion

This study proposes a robust anomaly detection framework for industrial machine sounds by integrating SSS and ASR within a Soft-Boundary Deep SVDD architecture. The proposed approach addresses the inherent challenges of unsupervised learning in industrial settings, where irregular background noise and the absence of abnormal labels often lead to performance degradation. By leveraging SSS, the model effectively augments time-series acoustic data while preserving temporal continuity, and the ASR module precisely isolates the ROI by suppressing non-essential acoustic artifacts. Experimental results on the MIMII dataset demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed framework across multiple machine types, including Fan, Pump, Slider, and Valve. The proposed framework significantly outperformed baseline models, achieving an average AUC of 0.829, which represents a performance gain of 12.5pp and 22.0pp over Basic SVDD and Basic AutoEncoder, respectively (see Table 1). Notably, the model demonstrated robust noise-handling capabilities: it achieved a 0.781 AUC on the noise-heavy Valve dataset and maintained a 0.784 AUC for Slider data even at -6dB SNR using the Soft-Boundary strategy. Since this framework can be trained using only normal data without requiring separate abnormal data, it is highly suitable for building cost-effective systems for real-time anomaly detection in actual factory environments. Despite these achievements, this study has certain limitations. The ASR module, while highly effective for impulsive or spectrally transitioning sounds like valves, may offer diminishing returns for machines with static noise profiles, such as fans. Future research will focus on extending this framework into a multimodal sensor fusion system that incorporates both sound and vibration data. We also plan to develop a Spectral-Temporal Attention Network to dynamically emphasize critical frequency bands by combining Sobel-based edge information with global signal features. These advancements are expected to further improve the precision and real-time reliability of predictive maintenance systems in complex industrial environments.
