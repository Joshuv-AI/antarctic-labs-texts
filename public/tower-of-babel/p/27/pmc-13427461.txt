Effect of clinical pharmacist consultation on postoperative pain, quality of life, and patient satisfaction after pacemaker implantation: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Anwar M. Alhaj, Eglal A. Bassiouny, Samar F. Farid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective management of postoperative pain is vital for recovery following pacemaker (PM) implantation. This study evaluated a pain management protocol incorporating clinical pharmacist consultation to improve patient outcomes and healthcare utilization.MethodsIn this randomized, unblinded controlled trial, 60 adults undergoing PM implantation were assigned to either standard care (control) or standard care plus a structured clinical pharmacist consultation (intervention). Pain intensity was measured daily (Days 0–10) using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale. Quality of Life (QoL) was assessed on Days 0, 1, 7, and 10 via European Quality of Life 5-Dimensions 5-Levels (EQ-5D-5L) version and European Quality of Life visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) instruments. Patient satisfaction was measured on Day 10 using the Short Assessment of Patient Satisfaction (SAPS).ResultsPain intensity and QoL scores improved significantly over time in both groups, with no statistically significant differences between groups. Medication adherence, physician contacts, and unresolved adverse effects were comparable between groups. In contrast, patient satisfaction scores were significantly higher in the intervention group, particularly regarding care, communication, and perceived respect.ConclusionClinical pharmacist consultation did not significantly alter postoperative pain intensity or QoL outcomes following PM implantation. However, it was associated with higher patient satisfaction, suggesting a meaningful contribution to the patient experience. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are warranted to clarify the impact of pharmacist involvement on clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), including pacemakers (PMs) and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), are commonly implanted under local anesthesia. Patient experience has become an important indicator of healthcare quality, and effective peri-procedural pain management is a key component of recovery following CIED procedures. The indications for PM and ICD implantation have become increasingly well-defined over time, resulting in a growing number of patients undergoing these procedures.1 The term CIED procedure refers to the surgical implantation of both PMs and ICDs.

It was estimated that approximately 1.14 million PMs were implanted worldwide in 2016, with projections suggesting an increase to 1.43 million units by 2023.2 Several countries currently monitor implantation-related complications through national registries.3, 4, 5, 6 As with any surgical intervention, device implantation carries a risk of complications.7, 8, 9 Although postoperative pain is often considered minor and expected, it is recognized as a procedural complication. Minor wound pain following implantation is anticipated but is typically managed with standard analgesics.10 In recent years, postoperative pain following device implantation has received increased attention, with efforts aimed at improving recognition and management.11, 12, 13

Acute postoperative pain following PM implantation may be influenced by surgical factors, individual pain perception, and perioperative analgesic strategies. Despite available guidance and expert recommendations, postoperative pain is sometimes insufficiently anticipated or undertreated.14 In Africa, inadequate postoperative pain management is still a prevalent issue, often resulting from patients' misconceptions and inadequate assessment of postoperative pain.15 And since the patient is the primary and sole assessor of pain intensity, it appeared to us that they were the core group we could work with.

Poorly controlled acute pain may contribute to chronic pain development,16, 17 functional impairment, or unplanned healthcare utilization after hospital discharge.18 Additionally, even short-term exposure to mild opioids carries a risk of prolonged use in some patients.19 These concerns highlight the importance of optimizing postoperative pain management while supporting appropriate medication use.

Clinical pharmacists have an established role in medication management and patient education and may contribute meaningfully to postoperative care.20 Previous studies have shown that integrating pharmacists into care pathways can improve patient education regarding analgesic use, enhance communication within multidisciplinary teams, facilitate transitions between hospital and community care, and improve medication adherence.21, 22, 23 Janbakhsh et al. reported that pharmacist consultation reduced the proportion of outpatients experiencing pain scores greater than three.24 Similarly, evidence suggests that pharmacist involvement may empower patients through improved understanding of prescribed medications and active engagement in their care.25

Despite this growing body of evidence, data specifically evaluating the impact of clinical pharmacist consultation on postoperative pain management following PM implantation remain limited. To date, no studies have directly examined the role of clinical pharmacist consultation in this specific clinical context. Pharmacist-led counseling may influence pain-related outcomes indirectly through improved understanding of postoperative expectations, reinforcement of analgesic adherence, improved self-management behaviors, reduction of anxiety, and earlier identification of patient concerns.26, 27 Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate a postoperative pain management approach incorporating clinical pharmacist consultation after PM implantation. The primary hypothesis was that pharmacist involvement would reduce postoperative pain severity, while the secondary hypothesis was that this intervention would improve patient satisfaction and quality of life (QoL) with outpatient care.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the research ethics Committee of the Faculty of pharmacy, Cairo University (CL 3704) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the declaration of Helsinki. This study adhered to the consolidated standards of reporting trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Clinical trial registration number: NCT06748482

Study design and settings

This randomized controlled unblinded study was conducted to evaluate the effect of a clinical pharmacist consultation on postoperative outcomes following PM implantation. Eligible patients were recruited from the cardiology department of Mabarra Heart Academy (MHA) hospital and randomized into either an intervention group receiving clinical pharmacist consultation in addition to standard care or a control group receiving standard care, which include physician advice and restrictions regarding the proper position of the left hand and the activities that should be avoided. Additionally, each patient received a printed paper including the medication and advice that was given orally by the physician.

Participants

Adult patients aged more than 18 years who underwent PM implantation were eligible for inclusion in this eleven-day-study.

Patients with a history of chronic pain, on long-term analgesic therapy, suffered from cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders, pregnant, experienced any surgical complications that could interfere with accurate pain assessment, or declined to participate in the study were excluded from that study. The legal criteria were adhered to by obtaining informed oral and written consent from all patients. An explanation of the forms was provided to each patient or their caregiver prior to their signature.

Sample size calculation

A priori sample size (50) participants was calculated using G*Power software and Chi square test to compare proportions. This was based on the primary outcome (the proportions of patients with a pain score > 3) in both groups (0.31 vs 0.14), the effect size = 0.4,25 alpha error = 0.05, power = 80%, number of groups = 2, allocation ratio = 1:1. We decided to increase the total sample size to 60 to take into account a 20% drop out and to increase the confidence and significance level of the results.

Randomization

Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the control or intervention group using a sealed envelope randomization technique. Allocation was performed after patient enrolment to minimize selection bias.26

Intervention

Baseline assessment was conducted on Day 0 prior to surgery for both groups and included demographic data collection, pain assessment using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (0−10) (supplementary material 1a),28 and QoL assessment using the validated Arabic version for Egypt of European Quality of Life 5-Dimensions 5-Levels (EQ-5D-5L) version and European Quality of Life visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) (supplementary material 1b) instruments (registration number: 67013).29 The instructions on how to fill the questionnaire were explained to the patients as the questionnaire was self-administered before the operation and two copies of the questionnaire were given to patients to be self-administered at home. All patients were given a printed visual pain score as well, to report their pain score daily.

Patients randomized to the intervention group received a structured 15-min postoperative consultation delivered by the researcher prior to hospital discharge following PM implantation. The consultation was standardized and focused on postoperative pain management, appropriate use of prescribed analgesics, expected pain course, recognition and management of common adverse drug reactions (ADRs), and clear guidance on when to seek medical attention. Non-pharmacological pain management strategies and medication adherence were also addressed.

Following discharge, both intervention and control groups received follow-up contacts according to the same schedule, daily for 11 consecutive days for data collection purposes. The intervention group received only two additional telephone consultations on days 1 and 7. These follow-up contacts served both data collection and clinical support purposes and were conducted according to a predefined schedule using telephone calls on days1 and 7 and WhatsApp messages on days 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 9. A written information sheet was provided to reinforce verbal counseling.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of the study was postoperative pain intensity during the first 10 days following PM implantation. Pain was assessed using a validated pain rating scale and measured repeatedly to capture changes in pain over time. Pain assessments were conducted for both groups via telephone calls on postoperative days 1, and 7, via structured WhatsApp messages on postoperative days 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 9, and via direct interviews on postoperative day 10. Baseline pain assessment was performed on day 0 prior to hospital discharge.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included QoL, patient satisfaction with healthcare services, medication adherence, adverse drug reactions, and healthcare utilization during the postoperative period.

Quality of life

QoL was assessed in both study groups using the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire and the EQ visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS). Assessments were conducted via direct interviews on days 0 and 10 and via telephone on postoperative days 1 and 7 using standardized administration procedures.

Patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction with pharmaceutical services was evaluated using the Short Assessment of Patient Satisfaction (SAPS) questionnaire (supplementary material 1c).30 SAPS was administered to both study groups on postoperative day 10, and the total SAPS score as well as individual item scores were recorded.

Medication adherence

Medication adherence in the intervention group was assessed on postoperative days 1 and 7 using a validated adherence measurement scale (supplementary material 1d)31 as part of pharmacist follow-up. Tablet counting was performed on postoperative day 10 to for both groups to support adherence assessment.

Adverse drug reactions

The occurrence of ADRs during the postoperative period was recorded for both groups. ADRs were documented as binary outcomes, indicating the presence or absence of reported events.

Healthcare utilization

Healthcare utilization during the postoperative period was assessed by recording the number of contacts with the treating physician for pain- or medication-related concerns. These contacts were documented for both study groups during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was utilized. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Analyses were conducted on participants who completed the study protocol.

Postoperative pain scores were analyzed as continuous variables. Between-group comparisons of pain scores at individual postoperative time points were conducted using the independent-samples t-test. Given that pain was assessed at multiple time points, Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. In addition, changes in pain scores over time between the intervention and control groups were analyzed using a mixed repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), with time as the within-subject factor and study group as the between-subject factor.

QoL outcomes were assessed using the EQ-5D-5L index score based on the utility values for Egypt29 and the EQ visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS), were analyzed using the same approach. Between-group comparisons at individual postoperative time points were performed using independent-samples t-tests with Bonferroni adjustment, and overall changes over time between groups were examined using mixed repeated-measures ANOVA.

Patient satisfaction was assessed using the SAPS questionnaire. The total SAPS score on postoperative day 10 was compared between the intervention and control groups using an independent-samples t-test. Individual SAPS items were also compared between groups using independent-samples t-tests. Because multiple SAPS items were analyzed, Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple item-level comparisons.

Medication adherence was assessed using tablet counting on postoperative day 10. For the intervention group, the mean score of a validated adherence measurement scale was measured on days 1 and 7. When assumptions of normality were not met, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied.

The proportion of patients experiencing ADRs and the number of contacts with the treating physician were compared between groups using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate based on expected cell counts.

As a sensitivity analysis, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to key continuous outcomes, including pain scores, QoL scores, and SAPS scores, to confirm the robustness of results obtained using parametric tests.

A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with Bonferroni-adjusted thresholds applied where appropriate.

Patients' quality of life

At baseline (Day 0), both groups showed comparable EQ-5D-5L utility values (Control = 0.44, Intervention = 0.38; p = 0.717, Independent Sample t-test). Over time, utility values improved in both groups, reaching 0.72 and 0.70 by Day 10, respectively. However, independent sample t-test showed no statistically significant difference between both groups at days 1, 7, and 10 (p > 0.05). After applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, none of the differences showed statistical significance (all adjusted p-values = 1).

The change in utility value from baseline to day 10 was calculated for each participant and compared between groups using Mann-Whitney U test showing no significant difference between groups (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study was conducted to examine whether a structured pharmacist consultation could improve postoperative pain outcomes in a procedural setting where the pharmacist's role is still not well defined, and to provide evidence for integrating clinical pharmacists into procedural cardiology services. Although pain intensity was the primary outcome and the sample size was calculated to detect differences in the proportion of patients experiencing moderate to severe pain, no statistically significant difference was found between the intervention and control groups. This likely reflects the relatively low pain burden observed after PM implantation and the use of standardized postoperative analgesic protocols, which may have reduced the potential added benefit of an educational intervention. In addition, consultation-based pharmacist interventions may be more likely to influence medication understanding, reassurance, confidence in care, and self-management behaviors than to produce immediate changes in short-term physiological outcomes when pharmacotherapy itself is already standardized.

In the present study, pain scores decreased significantly over time from day 0 to day 10 in both groups, with no statistically significant differences observed between the intervention and control groups. This suggests that postoperative pain following PM implantation may follow a predictable recovery course that is not substantially altered by pharmacist consultation alone. In contrast, Decelis et al. evaluated pharmacist intervention in pain control following cardiac surgery and reported reduced pain scores for up to six weeks after surgery in the intervention group.32 Furthermore, Barat et al. studied the effect of pharmacist consultations on postoperative pain in ambulatory surgery and reported a 17% reduction in patients experiencing moderate to severe pain, with an average pain score reduction of 0.9 on a 10-point scale.25 The discrepancy between these findings and our results may be explained by differences in surgical invasiveness, baseline pain severity, patient characteristics, and the intensity and duration of pharmacist-led interventions. It is also possible that the true effect of the intervention on pain was smaller than expected based on previous studies, which would limit the ability to detect differences with the current sample size.

The reduction in pain observed in studies demonstrating benefit from pharmacist involvement has been attributed, in part, to the scheduled administration of analgesics rather than reactive use in response to pain.33 Robaux et al. reported improved pain outcomes when postoperative analgesia was anticipated rather than delayed.34 Although similar principles were applied in our intervention, the relatively mild and transient nature of pain following PM implantation may have limited the potential for detecting between-group differences. In addition, Decelis et al. observed a sustained reduction in pain scores from week 1 to week 6 in the intervention group,32 whereas our follow-up period was limited to 10 days, potentially precluding the detection of longer-term effects. Studies incorporating clinical pharmacists within multidisciplinary pain management teams have reported reductions in both acute and chronic postoperative pain, as well as shorter durations of disability. Slipp et al. demonstrated that such an approach significantly reduced pain and disability duration compared to interventions without pharmacist involvement.35 These findings suggest that larger effects may be observed when pharmacists are embedded within multidisciplinary teams and participate directly in therapeutic decision-making rather than counseling alone.

A significant change in EQ-5D-5L utility values over time (Day 0 to Day 10) was observed with no significant differences between both groups. In contrast, It was reported that the QoL of patients suffering from cancer pain was improved by pharmacists providing patient education or by conducting various combinations of interventions, including medication review, patient education, physician recommendation and pain assessment.36, 37 Furthermore Thapa. reported that pharmacist-led intervention had a mixed impact on the QoL of patients with acute pain.38 This could be explained by the fact that the research was limited to only 10 days after the procedure, which is a short period compared to other studies, given that clinically meaningful changes in QoL often require longer periods to be observed, therefore, this period may not have been sufficient to observe the real effect of the intervention.

In the present study, no difference in medication adherence was observed between the intervention and control groups, with both groups demonstrating complete adherence to prescribed medications. This indicates that clinical pharmacist consultation did not confer an additional benefit in improving medication adherence in this setting. In contrast, Decelis et al. reported improved adherence to analgesic therapy following pharmacist-led education and structured discharge planning.32 The discrepancy between their findings and ours may be explained by differences in study context, including the short duration of postoperative therapy, the relative simplicity of the medication regimens after PM implantation and high baseline adherence to prescribed medications in both groups. These factors further limited the opportunity for the intervention to demonstrate incremental effects on clinical outcomes and suggest a ceiling effect for adherence in this population.

A key finding of this study was the significantly higher patient satisfaction observed in the intervention group compared with the control group. Patient satisfaction is increasingly recognized as an important indicator of healthcare quality and has been linked to medication adherence, engagement with follow-up care, and healthcare utilization. The pharmacist intervention may have improved patients' understanding of their medications, addressed concerns about adverse effects and pain management, and increased reassurance and trust in the care process. These factors may contribute to better adherence and clinical outcomes over a longer period than was captured in this short-term follow-up. Accordingly, the present findings may indicate that the earliest measurable benefit of pharmacist consultation in this setting is improvement in the patient experience, with downstream clinical benefits requiring longer observation periods to emerge.

Prior researches demonstrated that pharmacist consultations positively influence patient satisfaction.23, 35 Renaudin et al. reported enhanced patient satisfaction following preoperative pharmacist consultations in orthopaedic surgery, attributing this effect to improved communication and medication-related counseling.39 The improvement in patient satisfaction observed in the intervention group is more likely attributable to the nature of the pharmacist-led consultation itself, including individualized counseling, structured medication explanations, and direct engagement by a clinical pharmacist, rather than differences in follow-up frequency or mode of contact. Moreover, Lu et al. explored the role of clinical pharmacists in postoperative acute pain services and found that, although most pharmacists believed they should be involved, only 62.6% reported substantial participation in such services.40 This gap highlights an opportunity to expand and formalize the role of clinical pharmacists in postoperative care pathways.

However, not all studies have reported improvements in satisfaction. Barat et al. found no statistically significant difference in patient satisfaction between intervention and control groups.25 This may be explained by high baseline satisfaction levels, limited room for improvement, or the influence of other healthcare providers, such as nurses, anaesthesiologists, and surgeons, whose interactions also shape patient perceptions of care.

This study was designed as a pragmatic evaluation of pharmacist-led peri-procedural counseling in a real-world setting and the findings suggest that pharmacist consultation adds value in patient-centered outcomes, while also indicating that larger studies with longer follow-up and inclusion of higher-risk populations are needed to evaluate potential downstream clinical benefits. Future studies may also benefit from testing pharmacist interventions that include collaborative prescribing authority, medication adjustment, or protocol-driven analgesic optimization.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. The follow-up period was limited to 10 days, which may not capture longer-term effects of clinical pharmacist consultation on pain, QoL, adherence, or recovery trajectories. The absence of statistically significant between-group differences in HRQoL should be interpreted cautiously. HRQoL changes following PM implantation may continue to evolve beyond the 10-day follow-up period used in this study. Therefore, the present findings do not exclude the possibility of longer-term benefits that may become apparent with extended follow-up. However, the primary objective of the study was to evaluate early postoperative outcomes, a period during which pain, medication use, and patient concerns are often most pronounced following pacemaker implantation.

Generalizability of the findings should be interpreted cautiously. The study was conducted at a single tertiary cardiac center and included a relatively small sample of patients undergoing routine PM implantation, most of whom experienced mild postoperative pain and uncomplicated recovery. Consequently, the findings may not be directly applicable to patients undergoing more complex cardiovascular procedures, those experiencing greater postoperative symptom burden, or populations with substantially different clinical characteristics. Nevertheless, the study was conducted in a tertiary cardiac center using routine clinical practice procedures, which may enhance the relevance of the findings to similar clinical environments. Further multicenter studies are warranted to confirm external validity. The lack of blinding introduces the potential for performance and reporting bias, particularly for patient-reported outcomes such as pain, satisfaction, and QoL. Because the intervention consisted of direct pharmacist counseling and education, participant blinding was not feasible. This limitation should therefore be considered when interpreting subjective outcomes, particularly patient satisfaction. The high levels of medication adherence observed in both groups may partly reflect a Hawthorne effect, whereby participants modify their behavior because they are aware of being observed within a clinical study. This phenomenon has been described in clinical research and may lead to improved adherence and symptom reporting in both intervention and control groups, thereby attenuating measurable differences between study arms. The close monitoring and short duration of postoperative therapy may have further contributed to uniformly high adherence across groups, limiting the ability to detect incremental benefits of pharmacist consultation.

However, the increased patient satisfaction observed in the intervention group is less likely to be explained by the Hawthorne effect, as both groups received the same follow-up schedule, reducing the possibility that differences were attributable solely to unequal attention or contact frequency. Therefore, satisfaction was directly related to the quality and content of pharmacist–patient interaction.

All outcomes were self-reported and therefore subject to recall and reporting bias. However, patient-reported outcomes remain the most appropriate approach for evaluating subjective constructs such as pain, QoL, and satisfaction. In addition, the clinical pharmacist did not have independent authority to modify medication regimens without physician approval, which may have limited the measurable clinical impact of the intervention. Nevertheless, the study was designed to evaluate the contribution of pharmacist-led education and consultation within existing multidisciplinary practice rather than independent prescribing. Finally, the study was powered to detect differences in postoperative pain outcomes, and it is possible that the study was underpowered to detect smaller differences in secondary outcomes such as QoL. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences in some secondary outcomes should be interpreted cautiously and should not necessarily be considered evidence of a lack of intervention benefit.

Conclusion

Incorporating clinical pharmacists into the postoperative care pathway for patients undergoing PM implantation did not demonstrate significant improvements in pain intensity or QoL measures during the short follow-up period, but it was associated with a significant improvement in patients' experience of care. Patients who received clinical pharmacist consultations reported higher satisfaction, particularly regarding clarity of explanations, communication, and the sense of being respected and supported by healthcare professionals. These findings indicate that pharmacist-led consultations can improve important patient-centered outcomes even in procedural settings characterized by low baseline pain burden, rapid postoperative recovery, and high adherence rates. Further studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and broader scopes of pharmacist authority are warranted to better define the role of clinical pharmacists in optimizing postoperative outcomes and long-term recovery.
