Interleukin 32 Exacerbates Vitiligo by Promoting Inflammation and Oxidative Stress-induced Injury in Melanocytes
Authors: Tianqi Wei, Xin Huang, Jing Zhu, Guanying Liu, Baiyi Zuo, Zhaokang Zhou, Beilei Xu, Lingling Luo, Chengrang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxidative stress-mediated melanocyte injury and subsequent inflammation constitute essential pathogenic mechanisms underlying vitiligo. The secretion of diverse cytokines plays a crucial role in modifying the inflammatory environment characteristics of skin lesions. Interleukin 32 (IL32) is a multifunctional cytokine recognized for its potent pro-inflammatory effects. Here, we observed significantly enhanced IL32 expression in vitiligo skin lesions. The present study investigates the role of IL32 in oxidative stress-driven melanocyte inflammation and its contribution to vitiligo progression by establishing knockdown and overexpression models in PIG cell lines. Our findings demonstrate that oxidative stress triggers IL32 production in melanocytes, and increased IL32 exacerbates oxidative stress-mediated cellular injury. IL32 suppression activates the Nrf2 pathway and mitigates the oxidative stress response. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis revealed that IL32 may promote the secretion of multiple inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in melanocytes in vitro, especially CXCL9, thereby facilitating the recruitment of CD8+ T cells. Notably, IL32 accelerates depigmentation in the mouse model of vitiligo. These results highlight the therapeutic promise of targeting IL32 to attenuate inflammation and oxidative damage in melanocytes associated with vitiligo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-026-02570-y.

Introduction

Vitiligo is a multifaceted pigmentary autoimmune disorder characterized by the loss of melanocytes in the skin and mucosal surfaces [1]. The therapeutic challenges associated with vitiligo not only impair the quality of patients’ life but also intensify psychological distress [2]. The pathogenesis of vitiligo is associated with oxidative stress responses and aberrant, immune-mediated inflammatory mechanisms [3]. Oxidative stress-induced melanocyte injury serves as the initiating event in depigmentation and represents a crucial component of innate immune activation [4, 5]. Accumulating evidence indicates that the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, triggered by oxidative stress, facilitates the establishment of a localized inflammatory microenvironment [6]. This environment fosters T cell recruitment and activation, which contributes to melanocyte destruction [7]. A recent study revealed that the recruitment of CD8+ T cells targeting melanocytes, mediated by CXCL9 and CXCL10 secretion from dermal fibroblasts exhibiting differential responsiveness to IFN-γ, constituted a critical mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of vitiligo [8]. However, it remains unclear how T cells in the dermis initiate targeted killing of epidermal melanocytes. The functional significance of melanocyte-derived cytokines in bridging innate and adaptive immunity has not yet been established.

The novel cytokine, interleukin 32 (IL32), was originally cloned from human natural killer (NK) cells and first termed natural killer transcript 4 (NK-4) [9]. It was named IL32 due to its potent pro-inflammatory effects. So far, nine isoforms of IL32 have been discovered, among which α, β, and γ are the most extensively studied isoforms [10]. Research has demonstrated that various IL32 isoforms play critical roles in cancer, infections, and chronic inflammatory diseases, participating in multiple biological processes such as antimicrobial responses, immune activation and suppression, cell migration, and cellular metabolism [11]. IL32 expression could be induced or enhanced in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines, infections, and oxidative stress [12]. Although a cognate receptor for IL32 has not been identified, IL32 can induce cytokines through the NF-κB, AP-1, and p38MAPK pathways [13]. In addition, IL32 plays a regulatory role in keratinocyte apoptosis induced by TNF-α and IFN-γ in atopic dermatitis [14]. However, the involvement of IL32 in vitiligo remains unexplored. IL32α has been shown to enhance the migration of human melanoma cells, suggesting it may promote metastatic potential [15]. Whether it participates in inflammation recruitment and the modulation of melanocyte destruction continues to be largely unknown.

In this study, we investigated the role of IL32 in melanocyte-driven inflammation and oxidative stress, as well as its contribution to vitiligo progression both in vitro and in vivo. Through transcriptomic analysis, we elucidated the involvement of IL32 in inducing melanocyte inflammation, which is associated with the cytokine cytokine receptor interaction pathway. Furthermore, IL32 may mediate oxidative stress-induced injury in melanocytes by modulating the Nrf2 pathway. Our study highlighted a pivotal role of the novel pro-inflammatory cytokine IL32 in vitiligo, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for treatment.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Hydrogen peroxide (H792073, Macklin, Shanghai, China), Recombinant Human IL-32 beta Protein (CF 6769-IL, R&D Systems), Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), MelM (Sciencell, Carlsbad, CA, USA), Kerati. Basal media (XRBIO, Hangzhou, China), DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, China), FBS (Gibco, Maryland, USA), penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China), Rabbit monoclonal antibody to Anti-IL32 (Abacm, ab315466, 1:1000, MA, USA), Recombinant antibody rabbit monoclonal antibody to Anti-Nrf2 (PT0855R, 1:2000), Anti-Keap1(PT0444R, 1:2000), Anti-NQO1 (PT0071R, 1:2000), and Anti-HO-1 (PT0511R, 1:2000) were obtained from Immunoway (Suzhou, China), Rb-Melan-A antibody (1:500, ab210546, Abcam), Rat-CD8a antibody (1:300, ab308264, Abcam), InVivoMab anti-mouse CD4 antibody (Bio X cell, Clone GK1.5, Lebanon, NH, USA).

Clinical Sample Collection

Skin specimens of active vitiligo patients were collected from the edge of the depigmented area, all from the Hospital of Skin Diseases, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College. At the same time, skin samples of the same size were collected from healthy donors who underwent other surgical procedures. A total of three active vitiligo patients and three healthy donors were included. All of them underwent skin biopsy. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Skin Diseases, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College (Approval Number: 2025-KY-038).

Cell Culture

The human epidermal melanocytes (HEMs), human epidermal keratinocytes (HEKs), and human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were extracted from adolescent foreskin tissue donated after circumcision and cultured in melanocyte medium MelM or Kerati. Basal media or DMEM supplemented with 1% MGS or 1% KGS and 1% P/S, respectively. The human melanocyte cell line PIG1 and the vitiligo melanocyte cell line PIG3V were sourced from BLUEFBIO (Shanghai, China). Cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To investigate the phenotype of melanocytes under different in vitro treatments, we used HEMs for functional studies to demonstrate changes in human epidermal melanocytes. However, primary melanocytes obtained directly from normal control and vitiligo patients are limited in supply. Thus, we utilized PIG1 and PIG3V for further study, which were cell lines derived from normal human skin and vitiligo patient skin, respectively. These two cell lines were immortalized by transfecting with the HPV16 E6/E7 genes [16, 17].

Cell Transfection

PIG1 or PIG3V cells were seeded in 6- or 24-well plates. Transfection was performed when cell density reached 30%−40%. The knockdown sequences for IL32 (small interfering RNA, siIL32) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Cell transfection was carried out using the siRNA-mate plus (GenePharma). Subsequent experiments were conducted 24–48 h after transfection. All knockdown sequences are shown in Supporting Table S1. Cells were transfected with the lentivirus (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) containing LV-IL32-sh2 for 48 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The overexpression efficiencies of IL32 were identified using Western blotting.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was detected by the CCK8 assay. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells/well and treated with different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) or rhIL32β for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µL CCK8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 1.5 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

RNA-seq

The human epidermal melanocytes were treated with rhIL-32β for 24 h, and the total RNA of cells was isolated using Trizol reagent. RNA sequencing and raw data analysis were executed by LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China).

Lipid Peroxidation Detection

Cellular lipid peroxidation levels were assayed utilizing BODIPY-C11 dye (Beyotime, China). The cells were cultured with PBS supplemented with 1µM BODIPY-C11 dye for 30 min at 37˚C. Subsequently, the cells were harvested and resuspended in 400 µL of PBS for further quantification by flow-cytometry with the FITC channel. The analysis was conducted on BD LSRFortessaTM Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). Analysis of data was executed using FlowJo V10.8.1.

Reactive Oxygen Species Detection

DHE (dihydroethidium) staining was used to detect superoxide anion (O2⁻) in cells. Cells are incubated with DHE staining solution (Beyotime, China). After incubation, cells are washed and examined under a fluorescence microscope. The intensity of the red fluorescence indicates the presence and level of superoxide anion in the cells.

ELISA

ELISA kits (Elabscience, China) were used to measure the concentrations of IL32, CXCL9, and CXCL10 according to the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. Briefly, the primary antibody is coated on a microplate, where each of them in the samples binds. Biotinylated anti-primary antibody and horseradish peroxidase-labeled streptavidin are then added, forming an immune complex. Unbound components are washed away. TMB substrate turns blue under horseradish peroxidase, then yellow after the stop solution. The OD value at 450 nm is measured, and the concentration is calculated using a standard curve.

Cytokine Measurements

The HEMs were grown in 24-well tissue culture plates and then treated with 100ng/mL rhIL-32β for 24 h. The cell culture supernatants were collected and clarified by centrifugation. The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-8, IFN-α, and IFN-γ in the supernatants were determined using the Luminex multiplex assay.

Quantitative Real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Fast total RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, China), and reverse transcribed to cDNA using the Reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China). The qPCR reactions were performed using the qPCR Mix (Vazyme, China) on Roche LightCycler480II (Basel, Switzerland) with ACTB as the internal reference. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Western Blotting

The appropriately treated cells were collected and lysed by homogenization in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), which was supplemented with 1% Protease Inhibitor and 1% Phosphatase Inhibitor cocktails (Beyotime, China). Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were separated using Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, China). The protein samples were then subjected to electrophoresis using Smart PAGE™ Precast Protein Gel (Smart-Lifesciences, Changzhou, China), followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm). After blocking with 20% rapid sealing fluid (Epizyme, Shanghai, China), the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000 or 1:2000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed three times with TBST and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C. The protein band signal intensities were subsequently measured using the Cytiva Imaging System.

Immunofluorescence

Frozen skin sections underwent permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 45 min. Samples were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After removing the primary antibodies and washing the sections three times, secondary antibody staining was performed for 60 min in the dark. Nuclear staining was conducted using DAPI solution (C1005, Beyotime, China) for 10 min in the dark. Fluorescence was visualized using confocal microscopy (FV1000, Olympus, Japan).

Animals

The melanoma-Treg-induced vitiligo mouse model was generated as previously described by Chen et al. [18]. The depigmentation developed through brief inoculation of B16F10 melanoma cells and CD4 + regulatory T cell depletion [19]. Briefly, on day 0, 2 × 10^5 B16F10 melanoma cells were intradermally injected into the dorsal skin of 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Anti-CD4 depleting monoclonal antibody (10 µg/g) was administered intraperitoneally on days 4 and 10. Primary tumors were surgically excised on day 12. On day 33, mice exhibiting vitiligo were stratified into two groups according to the extent of depigmentation. The groups had equal amounts of depigmentation represented in each group. Four mice were taken from each group and treated every other day with either rhIL-32β (10 µg/g) or DPBS. Vitiligo progression was assessed by quantifying the depigmented skin area on day 90.

Whole‑mount Immunostaining

Tail hair was removed using depilatory cream. The skin was excised, flattened, and sectioned into 1 × 1 cm fragments, then incubated with 20 mM EDTA at 37 °C with agitation for 1 h. The epidermis was gently separated from the dermis, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed three times with PBS, permeabilized in 0.3% H₂O₂/methanol at − 20 °C for 20 min, and subsequently blocked at room temperature for 1 h. Tissues were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, fluorescent secondary antibody staining was performed at room temperature in the dark. PBS was used for all wash steps. Fluorescence was visualized by confocal microscopy. CD8⁺ T cell and melanocyte quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

Experimental results were all executed in triplicate or more. GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analyses. The differences between groups were assessed by Student’s t-test. P-values < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

IL32 Expression is Increased in Active Vitiligo

To assess the involvement of IL32 in vitiligo, we examined IL32 expression in the skin of healthy individuals and active vitiligo patients. Immunofluorescence analysis showed a significant increase in IL32 in both the epidermis and dermis of active vitiligo lesions (4-fold; P < 0.01; Fig. 1a and b). Subsequently, we performed bioinformatic analysis for the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of skin samples from 5 healthy donors and 10 patients with vitiligo [8] (PRJCA006797). The samples used for scRNA-seq consisted of epidermis and dermis of the skin tissue. After quality control and cell clustering, we identified and included eight main cell types: keratinocytes, dermal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, melanocytes, smooth muscle cells, monocytes, T cells, and Langerhans cells (Supplementary Figure S1). Consistently, scRNA-seq data also revealed elevated IL32 expression in vitiligo lesions, particularly during the progressive stage (Fig. 1c). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence co-localization with melanocyte markers indicated that IL32 is expressed in melanocytes and is markedly overexpressed in active vitiligo (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis showed that IL32 mRNA expression was increased by 1.8‑fold (P < 0.05) in the vitiligo melanocyte cell line PIG3V (Fig. 1d). In addition, increased IL32 protein levels were also observed in PIG3V relative to the normal human melanocyte cell line PIG1 (Fig. 1e).

Oxidative Stress can Stimulate the Expression of IL32 in Melanocytes

Oxidative stress is an important initiating factor for melanocyte damage in vitiligo. To determine whether IL32 can be stimulated by oxidative stress in melanocytes, we analyzed the RNA expression dataset (GSE216447) of human primary epidermal melanocytes (HEMs) exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [20]. As shown in Fig. 2a, IL32 was significantly upregulated in HEMs under oxidative stress. The heatmap showed that IL32 was among the top 20 upregulated DEGs (Fig. 2b). Correspondingly, the IL32 content in the supernatant of PIG1 and PIG3V cells stimulated by H2O2 also increased to varying degrees (P < 0.001; Fig. 2d). To evaluate the sensitivity of different cell types in skin exposed to H2O2, we treated HEMs, HEKs, and HDFs with H2O2 at different concentration gradients. Under 1000 µM H2O2 treatment of HEKs, 50 µM H2O2 treatment of HEMs, and 800 µM H2O2 treatment of HDFs, cell viability of all cell types decreased by approximately 50% (Supplement Figure S3). Our results showed that HEMs were most sensitive to H2O2. In order to generate comparable stress levels in all the cells, HEKs, HEMs, and HDFs were treated with the aforementioned concentrations of H2O2, respectively, resulting in the same decrease in cell viability compared to the control. Subsequently, qPCR analysis revealed that all cells exhibited minimal IL32 transcriptional activity under baseline conditions, whereas oxidative stress markedly enhanced IL32 transcriptional activity in HEMs (29-fold; P < 0.0001; Fig. 2c). However, H2O2 did not induce a significant increase in IL32 in HEKs or HDFs.

IL32 Mediates Oxidative Stress-induced Injury of Melanocytes

To evaluate the effect of elevated IL32 expression in melanocytes under oxidative stress, a lentiviral vector encoding IL32 was constructed and transfected into PIG1 cells (Fig. 3a). The findings demonstrated that IL32 overexpression heightened the susceptibility of PIG1 cells to oxidative stress and markedly diminished cell viability (0.8-fold; P < 0.001; Fig. 3b). Furthermore, upon exposure to H2O2, PIG1 cells overexpressing IL32 displayed a significant increase in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation (1.4-fold; P < 0.01; Fig. 3c). Melanocytes from individuals with vitiligo exhibit heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress, and IL32 expression was found to be elevated in PIG3V cells relative to PIG1 cells (Fig. 1d and e). To determine whether suppressing IL32 could mitigate oxidative stress-induced melanocyte injury, IL32-knockdown PIG3V cells were generated using siRNA (P < 0.0001; Fig. 3d and e). Our results showed that suppression of IL32 partially alleviated H2O2-induced ROS accumulation (0.5-fold; P < 0.01; Fig. 3f) and lipid peroxidation in PIG3V cells (0.9-fold; P < 0.05; Fig. 3g).

Knockdown of IL32 Enhances the Antioxidant Ability of Vitiligo Melanocytes by Activating Nrf2 Signal Pathway

Activation of the Nrf2 pathway represents a pivotal mechanism underlying melanocyte antioxidant defenses under oxidative stress [21]. In comparison to normal human melanocytes (PIG1), impaired Nrf2 expression could be observed in vitiligo melanocytes (PIG3V) (0.75-fold; P < 0.01; Fig. 4a), which also exhibit elevated IL32 levels (Fig. 1d and e). As shown in Fig. 3, silencing IL32 expression in PIG3V cells ameliorated reactive oxygen species accumulation during oxidative challenge. We further examined the influence of IL32 inhibition on the Nrf2 pathway in PIG3V cells. Our findings revealed that IL32 knockdown in PIG3V cells markedly upregulated Nrf2 expression (2-fold; P < 0.01; Fig. 4c). Given that Nrf2 activity also depends on nuclear translocation, we isolated cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins from IL32 knockdown PIG3V cells and detected the expression levels of Nrf2. After knocking down IL32 in PIG3V cells, Nrf2 protein levels increased in both the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, knockdown of IL32 in PIG3V activated the Nrf2/Keap1 axis (1.3-fold; P < 0.05) and induced the expression of downstream antioxidant effectors NQO1 and HO-1 under oxidative stress (Fig. 4c).

IL32 Induces Inflammatory Cytokines and Chemokines in Melanocytes

As a novel cytokine, IL32 has been shown to promote inflammation or cell death in vitro [22]. To investigate the effect of IL32 on melanocyte function, HEMs were exposed to different concentrations of IL32. However, IL32 could not affect the cell viability of HEMs under different concentrations (Supplementary Figure S4). Subsequently, RNA sequencing was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HEMs treated with or without IL32. KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs revealed a significant enrichment in the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway (Fig. 5a). Concurrently, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further indicated that the DEGs were predominantly associated with cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 5c). A heatmap illustrated that key cytokines, chemokines, and receptors exhibited differential expression between the two groups (Fig. 5d). Additionally, qPCR confirmed a marked upregulation of CXCL9 (9.5-fold; P < 0.01) and CXCL10 (2.3-fold; P < 0.05) in HEMs treated with IL32 (Fig. 5f). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis suggested that CXCL9 served as a key element of the DEGs regulatory network (Fig. 5b). Moreover, significantly increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-8 (1.6-fold) and IL-1β (2-fold) were detected in the supernatant of IL32-treated HEMs (P < 0.01; Fig. 5e). Collectively, our results showed that IL32 induced the production of various inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in melanocytes.

Exogenous IL32 Promotes the Development of Vitiligo in the Mouse Model

Although IL32 is expressed in higher mammals, research found that recombinant IL32 (rIL32) induced pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in mice, implying that rIL32 could activate signaling pathways in mice [23]. To further investigate the function of IL32 in vitiligo, we established a vitiligo mouse model treated with exogenous IL32 administration (Fig. 6a). Among the various IL32 isoforms, comparative analysis revealed that β and δ isoforms were predominantly upregulated in vitiligo melanocytes (Supplementary Figure S2). Therefore, we evaluated the impact of IL32β on depigmentation in the vitiligo mouse model. After injection of rhIL-32β, the mice exhibited a significant increase in depigmentation area compared to the DPBS injection group (Fig. 6b and c). Whole-mount staining analysis showed a significant increase in CD8+T cells (6.4-fold; P < 0.05; Fig. 6d) and a significant decrease in melanocyte content in the tail lesions of mice (0.3-fold; P < 0.01; Fig. 6e). In addition, detection of peripheral serum chemokine levels revealed significant elevation in CXCL9 (2-fold; P < 0.05; Fig. 6g), with a slight elevation in CXCL10 with no significance (1.2-fold; P > 0.05; Fig. 6h).

Discussion

Oxidative stress-induced melanocyte damage and inflammatory cytokine release play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of vitiligo. Our study revealed that elevated expression levels of IL32 were involved in the melanocytes of vitiligo under oxidative stress. In PIG1 cells, increased IL32 expression amplified the accumulation of reactive oxygen species in melanocytes, while inhibiting IL32 expression activated the Nrf2 pathway to enhance antioxidant capacity in PIG3V cells. In addition, IL32 promoted the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in HEMs in vitro, which subsequently induced adaptive immunity. In vivo experiments confirmed that exogenous IL32 could exacerbate the progression of depigmentation in mice.

As a potential biomarker, IL32 is markedly upregulated in various inflammatory skin diseases [24]. Compared to healthy controls, individuals with atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, hidradenitis suppurativa, and alopecia areata exhibit varying degrees of IL32 elevation in both serum and lesional skin, with remission of disease frequently correlating with a reduction in IL32 levels [25–28]. However, the role of IL32 in vitiligo has not been elucidated in prior studies. Our findings demonstrate that IL32 expression is significantly increased in patients’ biopsies of active vitiligo lesions, especially in epidermal keratinocytes, melanocytes, and dermal fibroblasts. Strong fluorescence of IL32 expression was observed in the stratum spinosum of active vitiligo lesions. A previous study has shown that keratinocytes can express IL32, which is elevated in the lesions of atopic dermatitis patients [14]. To clarify whether IL32 is expressed in keratinocytes of vitiligo patients, we further analyzed the IL32 expression levels of keratinocyte subgroups in scRNA-seq data. Our results confirmed that progressive vitiligo patients had increased IL32 expression in keratinocytes (Supplementary Figure S1). This suggests that IL32 produced in keratinocytes in vitiligo lesions might be exogenous to melanocytes. In alignment with these results, Jesus et al., utilizing multi-omics single-cell analysis, observed increased IL32 expression in peripheral blood T cells of vitiligo patients [29]. A recent study found that elevated serum IL32 level in non-segmental vitiligo patients is associated with disease activity, while the expression level of IL32 and its α and β isoforms in peripheral blood cells is also significantly upregulated [30]. These observations implicate IL32 as a potential contributor to the pathogenesis of vitiligo.

The primary initiating event in vitiligo is the oxidative stress-mediated injury of melanocytes driven by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [31]. Subtoxic levels of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) elicit pronounced pro-inflammatory responses within melanocytes, thereby serving as a critical trigger for innate immune activation [6]. The pro-inflammatory cytokine IL32 is constitutively expressed under normal physiological circumstances, while its expression is markedly upregulated in response to stress stimuli [32]. Ischemia-reperfusion injury has been shown to induce a pronounced elevation of IL-32 expression in both myocardial and neuroendocrine cells [33, 34]. In this study, we observed that oxidative stress markedly induced IL32 expression within vitiligo melanocytes in HEMs. Previous study revealed that oxidative stress fails to elicit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in keratinocytes [6]. Correspondingly, IL32 expression levels exhibited no appreciable alterations in HEKs and HDFs exposed to H2O2. This indicates that oxidative stress is an important source of IL32 in melanocytes of vitiligo lesions. In addition to H2O2, other forms of cell stress and pro-inflammatory cytokines have been shown to induce IL32 expression [10]. Given that IFN-γ is an important pro-inflammatory cytokine in the pathogenesis of vitiligo [7], we examined whether IFN-γ could induce IL32 production in HEKs and HDFs in the skin. Unsurprisingly, IFN-γ significantly induced upregulation of IL32 in HEKs and HDFs (Supplementary Figure S5). Therefore, we speculate that the increased IL32 expression in the upper epidermis might originate from the induction of local pro-inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ, especially in keratinocytes. However, the precise mechanisms by which these cells contribute to the pathogenesis of vitiligo remain to be elucidated.

Under conditions of oxidative stress, the levels of ROS were elevated, and augmented IL32 expression further facilitated the intracellular accumulation of ROS, thereby intensifying oxidative injury in PIG1 cells. Suppression of IL32 expression ameliorated such oxidative damage in PIG3V cells. Consequently, IL32 may exert a potent endogenous regulatory influence on oxidative stress-induced injury in melanocytes. Taken together, these findings indicate that IL32 could potentially be involved in the pathogenesis of vitiligo by altering oxidative stress-induced damage to melanocytes.

Nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a pivotal transcription factor governing redox homeostasis, cellular stress responses, and inflammation [35]. In normal melanocytes, Nrf2 predominantly associates with antioxidant response elements (AREs) to modulate the transcription of a repertoire of downstream antioxidant genes, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and quinolone oxidoreductase-1 (NQO1), thereby conferring cytoprotection against oxidative stress-induced injury [36]. Genetic studies have revealed that polymorphisms within the NRF2 gene are linked to an increased susceptibility to vitiligo [37], while impaired Nrf2 signaling has been implicated in the apoptosis of melanocytes in active vitiligo [38]. Previous studies demonstrated that silencing IL32 expression in PC12 cells activated the Nrf2 signaling pathway, conferring protection against damage induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation [34]. In this study, we confirmed that the Nrf2 signaling of vitiligo melanocytes was impaired in PIG cell lines. In PIG3V cells, suppression of IL32 led to an upregulation and nuclear translocation of Nrf2, activating downstream antioxidant targets, HO-1 and NQO1. Thus, under oxidative stress, IL32 inhibition may potentiate the antioxidant defenses of vitiligo melanocytes via activation of the Nrf2 pathway, thereby exerting a protective effect. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear.

The loss of melanocytes is the ultimate result of depigmentation in vitiligo. Our results revealed that IL32 levels were elevated in melanocytes of active vitiligo. Previous studies have demonstrated that IL32 facilitates epithelial cell apoptosis via activation of the caspase-3 and caspase-8 signaling pathways [39]. In addition, IL32 has been shown to induce NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in nasal epithelial cells [40]. Intriguingly, our study found that different concentrations of IL32 did not affect the activity of HEMs in vitro, suggesting that IL32 does not directly induce melanocyte death. To elucidate the mechanism by which IL32 contributes to melanocyte-driven vitiligo pathogenesis, we conducted in vitro experiments treating melanocytes with IL32. Subsequent transcriptomic analysis of HEMs treated with or without IL32 identified CXCL9 as a key molecule within the differentially expressed protein-protein interaction network. Recent studies have confirmed that the IFN-γ–CXCL9/CXCL10 axis is central to the autoimmune response, sustaining vitiligo activity through recruitment of CXCR3 CD8+ T cells [7, 8]. The primary cell source of CXCL9 in vitiligo skin is keratinocytes, though it is also produced by immune cells like T cells and dendritic cells [41]. For CXCL10, the main sources are keratinocytes and fibroblasts [8, 41]. However, the potential contribution of melanocyte‑derived CXCL9/CXCL10 to the vitiligo chemokine network should not be overlooked. Our data demonstrated that IL32 stimulation markedly upregulated the production of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in melanocytes. CXCL9 is mainly responsible for recruiting T cells in the microenvironment, while CXCL10 is mainly responsible for the localization of T cells in skin lesions, which is related to the progression and persistence of vitiligo [42, 43]. Locally, IL32 might initially facilitate the recruitment of CD8+ T cells by enhancing the secretion of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in melanocytes, ultimately contributing to melanocyte destruction. Thus, melanocyte-derived IL32 might be involved in bridging innate and adaptive immunity.

Although IL32 expression is markedly elevated across a spectrum of inflammation-associated disorders, the functional roles ascribed to its various isoforms differ significantly between diseases and might even present opposing effects. In a transgenic mouse model expressing IL32β, systemic administration of LPS resulted in heightened neuroinflammation and sustained NF-κB activation [44]. Conversely, a more recent investigation demonstrated that transgenic overexpression of IL32γ attenuated the progression of atopic dermatitis by downregulating miR-205 and NF-κB within cutaneous tissue [45]. Rodents do not autonomously express IL32, but injecting rhIL32 into the naive C57/BL6 mice joint could lead to swelling, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cartilage damage [23]. This indicates that rhIL32 can induce cytokine secretion and initiate signal transduction in mice. To evaluate the function of IL32 in vitiligo pathogenesis, we developed a melanoma Treg cell-induced vitiligo mouse model and assessed the impact of exogenous rhIL32 administration on disease severity. Notably, IL32 aggravated depigmentation in the vitiligo mice, accompanied by an increase in CD8+T cells and a decrease in melanocytes in the tail. Therefore, IL32 may promote the progression of vitiligo.

This study was subject to several limitations. Owing to the limited availability of IL32 protein isoforms commercially, we assessed only the effects of IL32β on vitiligo. Further experimental investigation is required to elucidate the functions of other isoforms. Moreover, IL32 regulates immune and additional cellular processes through a range of pathways and might influence the progression of inflammatory disorders either directly or indirectly via interaction with other cytokines [10]. In addition to melanocytes, skin lesions contain both inflammatory and non-inflammatory cell populations that serve as significant sources of IL32 in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines. The precise roles these cells play in the pathogenesis of vitiligo warrant further exploration.

In conclusion, the elevated IL32 in melanocytes under oxidative stress may aggravate vitiligo by promoting inflammation and oxidative damage. Targeting IL32 may hold promise in vitiligo management by mitigating oxidative stress and modulating inflammatory response.
