Introduction

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is the second most common non-melanoma skin cancer, with the scalp being a frequent site due to chronic ultraviolet exposure and age-related vulnerability [1]. Surgical excision remains the primary curative modality, often leaving complex defects that pose significant reconstructive challenges. These challenges are compounded by the unique anatomical features of the scalp: limited tissue mobility due to the inelastic galea aponeurotica, a convex bony surface, and a scarcity of adjacent donor tissue, all of which restrict reconstructive options [2]. Furthermore, scalp defects may extend to the periosteum, bone, or even dura, particularly in advanced or recurrent cases, and often require a multidisciplinary approach involving plastic and neurosurgical teams [3].

Reconstructive strategies must be tailored to the size, depth, and location of the defect, as well as patient-specific factors including comorbidities and prior radiation therapy. While small defects may be managed with primary closure or skin grafting, larger or full-thickness wounds often necessitate more complex solutions, such as local flaps, free tissue transfer, or dermal regeneration templates like Integra®, a bilayer matrix composed of bovine collagen and glycosaminoglycans covered by a silicone layer [4]. Integra® has emerged as a valuable tool for reconstructing extensive scalp wounds in some cases, offering favorable cosmetic and functional outcomes through staged reconstruction [5]. However, this case report focuses on a successful example of scalp reconstruction using five pinwheel flaps following the excision of an infiltrative cSCC near the vertex and right parietal region, where the defect exposed the calvarial bone. Unlike broader applications of established techniques such as Integra® or free flaps, this report highlights the specific use of multiple pinwheel flaps to achieve single-stage coverage, avoiding the need for dermal substitutes or distant tissue transfer. It contributes to the literature by demonstrating the technique's effectiveness in a challenging case, emphasizing the importance of individualized, multidisciplinary planning and intraoperative adaptability.

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [6], ensuring informed consent and ethical standards were met.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old male patient presented with a necrotic scalp lesion located near the vertex, extending toward the right parietal region (Fig. 1) on January 15, 2025. Histopathological evaluation confirmed infiltrative cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) on January 22, 2025, with the tumor being moderately differentiated, exhibiting perineural invasion, and having a depth of invasion extending to the calvarial periosteum (approximately 5 mm). The patient had no significant comorbidities, an unremarkable immunological status, and no prior oncologic history, which supported the suitability of local flap reconstruction. Preoperative assessment, including clinical photographs, surgical planning with surface markings, and a CT scan confirming calvarial bone involvement without dural penetration or intracranial extension, delineated the extent of the lesion and guided the reconstruction strategy (Fig. 2). The tumor was staged using the AJCC 8th edition TNM classification as T2 N0 M0, indicating a tumor size of 6 cm × 4 cm with no regional lymph node involvement or distant metastasis, and a Brigham and Women's risk classification of high risk due to its size, infiltrative nature, perineural invasion, and depth.

Intraoperative phase of a surgical procedure for a patient with (cSCC) on the scalp.

The patient was scheduled for wide local excision and immediate reconstruction, performed on February 5, 2025, by a senior plastic surgeon (Dr. John Smith) with assistance from a surgical oncology consultant (Dr. Emily Brown). The patient received preoperative prophylactic antibiotics (cefazolin 2 g IV) and postoperative antibiotics (cefazolin 1 g IV every 8 h for 24 h). Analgesia was managed with acetaminophen (1 g every 6 h) and tramadol (50 mg every 12 h) as needed for 48 h post-surgery. Surgical resection was conducted with 1.0–1.5 cm margins due to the presence of multiple closely situated lesions, resulting in a preoperative tumor size of approximately 6 cm × 4 cm being excised to create a full-thickness defect of approximately 8 cm × 6 cm exposing the calvarial bone. Intraoperative frozen section analysis confirmed that all surgical margins were free of tumor. Following resection, reconstruction was achieved using five pinwheel flaps, a number determined by the defect's large size and central location, which required optimal redistribution of local scalp tissue for complete coverage. Two closed-suction drains were placed during reconstruction and removed after 48 h when output was less than 20 mL/day.

The flap tracing technique involved precise measurements to ensure accurate flap design and placement. Initially, the defect was measured at 8 cm × 6 cm, and the surrounding scalp tissue was assessed for laxity. Five pinwheel flaps were planned, each designed as equilateral triangles with a base width of 4 cm and a height of 4 cm, calculated to cover the defect evenly when rotated and advanced. The central point of the defect was marked, and radial lines were drawn at 72-degree intervals (360° divided by 5 flaps) to determine flap positions. Each flap's base was traced 2 cm from the defect edge, with the apex extending 4 cm outward, ensuring sufficient tissue recruitment. The flaps were then incised, elevated with the galea preserved for vascularity, and rotated inward, with adjustments made intraoperatively to maintain tension distribution. This methodical approach, guided by preoperative measurements and intraoperative verification, ensured complete defect coverage and contour preservation.

The flaps were designed and mobilized to recruit adequate tissue from the vertex and right parietal regions, ensuring tension distribution and preservation of contour. After flap elevation and mobilization, the wound was successfully closed, achieving both oncologic control and satisfactory scalp contour (Video 1).

This case illustrates a tailored, tissue-sparing approach to scalp reconstruction in the setting of infiltrative cSCC, emphasizing the importance of preoperative planning and intraoperative adaptability in complex cutaneous oncologic surgery (Fig. 3, Video 2). Video 2 depicts the immediate postoperative result, showing the initial appearance of the reconstructed scalp, while Fig. 3 provides a still image of the early postoperative phase. The patient was followed up from March 2025 to February 2026 with clinical examinations every 3 months, showing no local recurrence or metastatic disease and stable reconstruction with no flap breakdown or contour deformities. The patient reported high cosmetic satisfaction, noting the reconstructed scalp closely matched their pre-surgery contour and was socially acceptable, and an improved quality of life due to the absence of pain, functional limitations, or need for further interventions, enabling a return to normal daily activities. The patient expressed relief at the quick recovery, satisfaction with the restored scalp appearance, and enhanced quality of life due to regained social confidence. Unfortunately, follow-up video documentation of the healing process is not available due to the retrospective nature of this report.

Discussion

Scalp reconstruction following oncologic resection presents a complex surgical challenge due to the unique anatomical and physiological properties of the scalp. The limited elasticity imposed by the inelastic galea aponeurotica, the convex bony surface of the calvarium, and the scarcity of adjacent donor tissue significantly restrict reconstructive options [6]. These factors are further complicated by the proximity to critical neurovascular structures and the potential for deep tissue involvement, including the periosteum, bone, or dura, especially in advanced cases such as infiltrative cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC). In this case, the patient's lesion, diagnosed as infiltrative cSCC, required wide local excision with margins of 1.0–1.5 cm, aligning with current surgical guidelines for high-risk or infiltrative SCC to ensure oncologic safety. The preoperative tumor, measuring approximately 6 cm × 4 cm, was excised, resulting in a full-thickness defect of approximately 8 cm × 6 cm that exposed the calvarial bone, underscoring the magnitude of the reconstructive challenge.

The resultant defect demanded a reconstructive approach that prioritized both reliable coverage and preservation of the scalp's natural contour to maintain aesthetic and functional integrity [7]. Several techniques are available, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Primary closure and skin grafting are suitable for smaller defects but inadequate for full-thickness exposures like this case. Rotational flaps offer single-stage coverage and utilize adjacent tissue, though they may require larger incisions and carry a risk of tension-related complications or contour distortion. Dermal regeneration templates, such as Integra®, a bilayer matrix of bovine collagen and glycosaminoglycans with a silicone layer, enable staged regeneration and are effective for extensive or irradiated wounds, but they involve prolonged healing periods (often weeks to months), donor site morbidity from harvesting, and the need for multiple procedures [4,5]. Free tissue transfer provides robust coverage for complex defects, leveraging distant donor sites with reliable vascularity, yet it entails significant operative complexity, longer recovery times, and potential donor site morbidity [11]. In this instance, the patient exhibited adequate local tissue laxity, making local flap reconstruction a feasible alternative. The use of five pinwheel flaps provided a robust, single-stage solution, leveraging the radial recruitment of surrounding tissue to distribute tension evenly and achieve complete coverage without compromising contour [8].

The decision to employ five pinwheel flaps was a strategic response to the defect's large size and central location near the vertex and right parietal region. This number was determined intraoperatively, guided by preoperative planning that mapped tissue availability and defect dimensions, and supported by real-time assessment during surgery [9,10]. The pinwheel flap technique, though less commonly utilized than rotational flaps or other methods, excels in covering central defects by mobilizing multiple small flaps in a pinwheel configuration, each contributing to the overall closure. This approach minimized the risk of tension-related complications and avoided the operative complexity, donor site morbidity, and extended recovery associated with free flaps or staged procedures like those using Integra® [11]. The intraoperative use of frozen section analysis further ensured tumor clearance, reinforcing the oncologic safety of the single-stage reconstruction.

Multidisciplinary coordination between surgical oncology and reconstructive teams was pivotal to the success of this case. The preoperative collaboration facilitated precise tumor mapping and margin planning, while intraoperative adaptability allowed for the adjustment of flap numbers based on the defect's final dimensions [3]. This teamwork not only optimized oncologic outcomes but also enhanced aesthetic results, a critical consideration given the scalp's visibility and the patient's quality of life. The video documentation (Video 1 and Video 2) provides valuable visual evidence of the surgical technique and immediate postoperative outcome, with Video 2 capturing the reconstructed scalp immediately after surgery. However, a notable limitation is the absence of follow-up video to document the healing process, which would offer insights into long-term outcomes such as scar maturation, tissue viability, and patient satisfaction. Fig. 3, an early postoperative still image, serves as a partial representation, but future studies should prioritize longitudinal imaging or video to fully validate the technique's efficacy over time.

The pinwheel flap technique, while established, is not as widely adopted as rotational flaps or other methods, potentially due to its perceived complexity or underutilization in large defects. This report contributes to the literature by demonstrating the successful application of five pinwheel flaps in a challenging infiltrative cSCC case, highlighting its adaptability and tissue-sparing benefits. The findings suggest that with careful preoperative planning and intraoperative flexibility, this technique can be a viable alternative to more invasive options, particularly in patients with sufficient local tissue reserves. However, potential limitations include the risk of flap necrosis due to compromised vascularity, especially with multiple flaps; an increased scar burden from multiple incision sites; and limited applicability in irradiated or fibrotic scalps where tissue laxity and vascular supply may be insufficient, conditions not present in this patient. Compared to rotational flaps, pinwheel flaps offer better distribution of tension but may require more precise planning; compared to Integra®, they avoid staged procedures but lack the regenerative capacity for poor tissue beds; and compared to free tissue transfer, they reduce operative complexity but may not suffice for defects with inadequate local resources. This case reinforces the importance of individualized surgical planning and underscores the potential for pinwheel flaps to address complex scalp defects, encouraging further exploration of their role in reconstructive surgery. Future research could compare outcomes of pinwheel flaps with rotational flaps, dermal substitutes, and free tissue transfer across a larger cohort to establish broader guidelines for their application.

Conclusion

Scalp reconstruction after excision of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma requires careful consideration of defect size, depth, location, and patient-specific factors. In this case, the use of five pinwheel flaps offered an effective and elegant solution for reconstructing a full-thickness scalp defect exposing the calvarial bone, preserving form and function without the need for staged procedures or distant donor sites. The patient demonstrated successful oncologic control and reconstructive durability over a 12-month follow-up period, with no recurrence or flap complications observed. The patient also reported high cosmetic satisfaction and improved quality of life, reflecting the technique's positive impact on aesthetic and functional outcomes. This report enhances the literature by illustrating the technique's adaptability in a challenging infiltrative cSCC case, emphasizing the value of individualized, anatomy-based surgical planning and multidisciplinary collaboration. The immediate postoperative outcome is documented in Video 2, but the lack of follow-up video on healing remains a limitation. Future studies should include longitudinal documentation of healing to fully assess the technique's long-term outcomes.
