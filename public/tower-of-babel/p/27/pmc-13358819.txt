Research progress, translation-related challenges, and clinical prospects of surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy in respiratory virus detection: a narrative review
Authors: Jiayang Wang, Lingyi Kong, Boyu Zhao, Shaoshuai Han, Zhenlin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveThe diagnosis of respiratory viral infections has traditionally relied on nucleic acid testing, antigen assays, and viral culture; however, these approaches may be limited by turnaround time, sensitivity, operational complexity, or multiplexing capacity in certain clinical settings. Research interest in surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) as a rapid diagnostic strategy has intensified owing to its high sensitivity, molecular specificity, label-free detection capability, and multiplex analytical potential. This review provides a synthesis of the recently generated evidence related to SERS-based respiratory virus detection and discusses the translation-related challenges and clinical prospects.MethodsA systematic literature search for relevant studies published from January 1, 2005, to April 1, 2026, was conducted in the PubMed, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect databases. The search focused on the application of SERS for respiratory virus detection, specifically label-based and label-free strategies, microfluidics, and integration with machine learning and polymerase chain reaction.Key Content and FindingsInitial studies in the field established the feasibility of SERS in identifying respiratory viruses and distinguishing pathogen-specific molecular signatures. More recent work has expanded applications to encompass label-free detection, immunoassay-based platforms, nucleic acid amplification–coupled SERS, and integrated microfluidic or portable systems. These advances have improved analytical sensitivity, enabled multiplex pathogen discrimination, and broadened potential use across scenarios such as community screening, emergency triage, surveillance, and confirmatory testing. Combinations with deep learning and automated platforms, which may further enhance diagnostic performance and clinical adaptability, have also emerged. However, major barriers remain, including insufficient signal reproducibility, poor substrate uniformity, lack of standardized methodologies, and limited large-scale clinical validation.ConclusionsSERS has demonstrated considerable promise as a complementary diagnostic modality for respiratory virus detection and is progressing from proof-of-concept validation to the assessment of clinical feasibility. The future translation of this technology will depend on reproducible substrates, standardized workflows, robust algorithms, and rigorous clinical validation.Keywords: Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), respiratory viruses, pathogen detection, clinical translation, narrative review

Introduction

Background

Respiratory viruses have long posed a threat to global public health. Viral pathogens such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), influenza viruses, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) have led to widespread outbreaks and substantial morbidity worldwide. These viruses are characterized by rapid transmission and broad infection ranges. Given that the clinical manifestations induced by different respiratory viruses often overlap significantly, etiological differentiation based solely on symptoms remains challenging. Therefore, accurate laboratory-based diagnosis is considered essential for guiding therapeutic decision-making and implementing appropriate isolation measures.

The currently available diagnostic approaches have inherent limitations. Reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) is widely recognized for its high sensitivity and specificity; however, it is equipment-dependent and relatively time-consuming. Antigen-based assays offer operational simplicity and rapid turnaround but have relatively poor sensitivity, particularly in cases with low viral loads. Viral culture and sequencing techniques can provide comprehensive information, but are not suitable for rapid clinical diagnostics. As a result, a tradeoff persists among speed, sensitivity, and accessibility across these existing methodologies, and it is thus difficult to simultaneously satisfy all clinical requirements. Accordingly, the development of novel diagnostic technologies that integrate high sensitivity, rapid response, and point-of-care applicability has become a critical research priority in the field of respiratory virus detection.

Role and development of Raman spectroscopy in biomedical analysis

Raman spectroscopy is based on the inelastic scattering of light arising from interactions between photons and molecular vibrations. The resulting frequency shifts directly correspond to molecular vibrational modes, thereby providing highly specific structural “fingerprint” information (1). Compared with infrared absorption spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy is far less susceptible to interference from water, making it particularly suitable for the direct analysis of aqueous solutions, biological fluids, and hydrated samples (2). In addition, Raman spectroscopy has minimal sample preparation requirements and provides nondestructive detection with high spatial resolution. These features enable in situ measurements on solid surfaces, particulate matter, thin films, and even individual microstructures (3,4). Within biological systems, biomolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids generate distinct Raman signatures, which form the basis for the identification of virus-associated molecular components.

As illustrated in Figure 1, current respiratory virus detection strategies have multiple limitations in practical applications, including low viral load conditions, co-infections, and an insufficiency to meet the increasing demand for rapid diagnostics. Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), by exploiting the localized electromagnetic field enhancement effects generated on noble metal nanostructures, is capable of amplifying Raman signals by factors ranging from 106 to 1014, thereby significantly improving trace-level detection sensitivity.

Although a variety of SERS-based detection strategies have been reported, substantial heterogeneity remains across different approaches in terms of platform design, applicability to real clinical samples, and the level of evidence supporting clinical translation. Notably, a systematic comparison and synthesis of the literature on clinical application scenarios remains lacking. In this context, we conducted a review in order to comprehensively characterize the field of SERS-based respiratory virus detection. The review begins with outlining the fundamental principles of Raman scattering. This is followed by a critical analysis of the major technical strategies, current status of real-sample applications, and key challenges associated with clinical translation. Furthermore, key factors influencing detection performance are summarized, and future development directions are discussed. It is hoped that this review can facilitate further research and promote the clinical implementation of this emerging technology. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1297/rc).

Methods

This narrative review was based on a structured literature search of studies published between January 1, 2005, and April 1, 2026, in the PubMed, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect databases (final search conducted on April 2, 2026). The selection process focused on retrieving pivotal studies that have defined the current diagnostic landscape and key technological concepts (e.g., microfluidics, machine learning, and PCR coupling) of SERS in virology. The aim of this targeted approach was to synthesize a critical, expert perspective that spanned concepts from nanomaterial design to clinical translation. The primary search terms used were “SERS clinical diagnosis virus”, “microfluidic SERS virus detection”, “label-based SERS virus detection”, “label-free SERS virus detection”, “machine learning SERS virus detection”, and “SERS PCR virus detection”.

Conversely, studies focusing on nonrespiratory pathogens, purely theoretical material science research lacking actual biological detection applications, and studies examining non-SERS analytical techniques were excluded (Table 1). Thus, the scope was exclusively respiratory viral infections.

Theoretical basis and technical advantages of SERS in respiratory virus detection

To better elucidate the application potential and technical challenges of Raman spectroscopy in the detection of respiratory disease diagnostics, it is necessary to first clarify its underlying physical mechanisms and enhancement strategies at the molecular level. In this section, the fundamental principles of Raman scattering, the relationship between spectral features and molecular structures, and the enhancement mechanisms of SERS are systematically introduced in order to provide a theoretical framework for subsequent application-oriented analyses.

Physical mechanism of Raman scattering

Raman scattering is an inelastic light-matter interaction process induced by the interaction between incident photons and molecular systems. Its physical origin involves the modulation of the incident light frequency caused by changes in molecular polarizability during vibrational motion. Since the Raman shift is determined solely by molecular vibrational energy levels and is independent of the excitation wavelength, distinct molecular systems exhibit characteristic Raman peak positions (5). These characteristic peaks collectively constitute the molecular “spectral fingerprint” (6), which provides a theoretical basis for both the qualitative identification and quantitative analysis of substances. In a typical Raman scattering process, the scattered light can be classified into Rayleigh scattering, Stokes scattering, and anti-Stokes scattering. Among these, Stokes scattering generally exhibits the strongest signal intensity and is therefore most widely used in practical detection applications.

As illustrated in Figure 2, SERS enhancement is primarily attributed to two mechanisms, electromagnetic enhancement, and chemical enhancement‚ with the former contributing most to this phenomenon. Upon excitation by incident light, free electrons on the surface of metallic nanostructures are coupled with the electromagnetic field, resulting in the generation of surface plasmon resonance (SPR) (7). Among commonly used metals, gold, silver, and copper (8) are widely employed as SERS substrate materials due to their negative real dielectric constants and relatively low optical losses. The spatial distribution of electromagnetic enhancement is typically heterogeneous. Significant localization of electromagnetic fields occurs at nanoparticle tips, interparticle junctions, and rough surface features. These regions, often referred to as “hotspots”, exhibit markedly intensified local electromagnetic fields (9).

In addition to electromagnetic enhancement, chemical enhancement also plays a significant role in the SERS process. When the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) or lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of an adsorbed molecule is energetically aligned with the Fermi level of the metal surface, charge-transfer transitions between the molecule and the metal can occur, leading to a substantial increase in the Raman scattering cross-section (10). The mode of molecular adsorption has a pronounced influence on SERS enhancement efficiency (11). Covalent adsorption generally stabilizes the molecular orientation, facilitating compliance with surface selection rules and thereby enhancing specific vibrational modes (12). In contrast, physically adsorbed molecules tend to exhibit random orientation distributions, resulting in greater variability in enhancement factors (13).

Relationship between Raman spectral features and molecular structure

Respiratory viruses are primarily composed of biomolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids (14). Variations in the structural organization and chemical composition of these components produce distinct Raman spectral features.

Characteristic Raman peaks directly reflect molecular vibrational modes, with different chemical bonds and functional groups exhibiting signals within specific wavenumber ranges. Protein-related signals are mainly associated with amino acid side chains and amide backbone vibrations (15), whereas nucleic acid signals are primarily associated with base ring vibrations and phosphate backbone modes (16). Lipid-associated peaks predominantly arise from fatty acid chain skeletons and C-H stretching vibrations (17). The differences in the composition and relative abundance of these biomolecular components among various viruses form the fundamental basis for spectral discrimination.

In virus detection, the discriminative capability of Raman spectroscopy does not typically rely on a single characteristic peak but rather on an integrated spectral pattern composed of multiple bands. It should be noted that in real-world samples, virus-related spectral signals are often subject to interference from background components such as proteins, salts, metabolites, and cellular debris. In addition, spectral overlap among different components may further complicate signal interpretation. Therefore, in practical applications, full-spectrum analysis combined with signal preprocessing and multivariate statistical methods is generally required to improve both the accuracy and robustness of identification.

However, the intrinsic Raman signals of virus-associated molecules are typically weak and easily affected by background interference and spectral overlap in complex biological matrices. Consequently, molecular fingerprint information alone is insufficient to support reliable detection. Instead, effective signal amplification and robust readout must be achieved through rational platform design.

Influence of platform design on the performance of SERS-based respiratory virus detection

Whether the theoretical advantages of SERS enhancement can be translated into reproducible and controllable performance in practical applications largely depends on the systematic design of platform architecture and interfacial engineering. Factors including the choice of metallic substrates, nanostructure morphology, hotspot construction strategies, and bio-interface regulation all directly influence the distribution of localized electromagnetic fields, molecular adsorption behavior, and the stability of signal output.

Noble metal substrates and nanostructure design

The design of SERS substrates involves a balance among enhancement capability, stability, and reproducibility. Gold-based substrates are widely used in biosensing applications due to their excellent chemical stability, as demonstrated in studies involving the detection of circulating tumor cells in peripheral blood (18). In contrast, silver-based substrates generally provide stronger electromagnetic enhancement; however, the release of silver ions from silver nanoparticles may induce strong oxidative reactions that can damage biological structures (19). Therefore, surface modification or composite design strategies are often required to achieve a balance between enhanced performance and system stability in biological applications.

In addition, nanostructure morphology plays a critical role in determining hotspot distribution. Spherical nanoparticles are relatively easy to fabricate (20), but their local field enhancement capability is limited. In comparison, nanorods and nanoprisms can generate stronger localized electromagnetic fields along their longitudinal axes (21). Core-shell nanostructures (e.g., Au@Ag) can integrate the advantages of different metallic materials (22), enabling high enhancement efficiency while maintaining favorable stability.

Hotspot engineering and signal amplification

Hotspots are the primary source of SERS signal enhancement, and their spatial distribution and intensity directly determine detection sensitivity. Therefore, the construction of controllable and high-density hotspot architectures has become a central focus in platform design.

Nanogap structures are among the most typical sources of hotspots. When the distance between two nanoparticles is reduced to the nanometer or even subnanometer scale, localized electromagnetic fields can be significantly amplified, resulting in substantial enhancement of Raman signals. Precise control of gap size enables effective tuning of the enhancement factor (23). Xu et al. proposed a seed-mediated synthesis strategy (24) to fabricate silver nanostructures with internal nanogaps. By functionalizing the nanoparticle surface with Raman reporters and subsequently forming an outer silver shell via in situ reduction, Raman-active molecules can be successfully confined within the nanogap regions.

Self-assembly strategies provide a convenient approach for hotspot construction. Through molecular linkers or DNA templates, nanoparticles can be guided to form ordered assemblies, generating a large number of uniform hotspots within the system (25). This method offers advantages in operational simplicity and scalability. Kouba et al. developed a gold-coated silicon dioxide nanosphere film substrate using a bottom-up approach (26), in which a closely packed monolayer of silicon dioxide nanospheres is first formed, followed by the deposition of a thin gold layer via magnetron sputtering. This provides a large-area substrate with uniform hotspot distribution.

In recent years, three-dimensional (3D) substrate architectures have attracted heightened research attention. Compared with conventional two-dimensional substrates, 3D structures provide a more extensive spatial distribution of hotspots, thereby improving signal collection efficiency and enhancing detection sensitivity. Jin et al. designed a 3D silver nanorod electrode structure (27) that offers an abundance of spatial hotspots and, when combined with a DNA walker-based signal amplification strategy, enables dual-mode SERS/electrochemical detection. These studies indicate that the core objective of hotspot engineering is not solely to maximize local field intensity but also to achieve an optimal balance among enhancement capability, spatial uniformity, and fabrication reproducibility.

It should be emphasized that hotspot engineering is not merely aimed at achieving higher localized electromagnetic fields. Excessive localization may lead to spatially heterogeneous signal distribution, thereby reducing reproducibility. Consequently, in platform design, enhancement efficiency, spatial uniformity, and manufacturing reproducibility should be balanced to achieve coordinated optimization of sensitivity and stability.

Rational nanostructure design and hotspot engineering provide the physical foundation for SERS signal amplification. However, these structural advantages can only be fully realized when integrated with appropriate detection strategies. The subsequent sections further clarify the various SERS-based detection strategies and their performance in real clinical samples.

Label-based SERS detection

Fundamental principles and probe design

In label-based SERS approaches, nanoprobes are constructed by integrating Raman reporter molecules with specific recognition elements. Small organic molecules such as 4-mercaptobenzoic acid (4-MBA) and 4-aminothiophenol (4-ATP) are commonly employed as signal reporters (28). These reporter molecules are conjugated with antibodies, aptamers, or nucleic acid probes, enabling the specific recognition of target viruses. Upon binding to the target, the nanoprobes are localized within plasmonic hotspot regions, where significantly enhanced Raman signals are generated. Such “sandwich-type” structural designs have been demonstrated to improve detection sensitivity to the pg/mL or even femtomolar (fM) level (29), which is sufficient for detecting low viral loads in early-stage infections.

Applications in respiratory virus detection

Label-based SERS strategies have been widely applied in the detection of various respiratory viruses, with particularly rapid development observed in studies related to severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and influenza viruses. For SARS-CoV-2 antigen detection, Gordon et al. developed a paper-based gold nanoprobe platform (30). In this system, the high surface area of fibrous paper captures viral spike and nucleocapsid proteins (NP). The limits of detection (LODs) for the direct identification of spike (S) and nucleocapsid (N) proteins were determined to be 0.8 and 2.0 pM, respectively. When silica nanoparticles were adopted as synthetic virus surrogates, the LOD targeting the S protein was further lowered to 0.027 pM. Compared with traditional colloidal gold test strips, the proposed SERS platform costs less than 2 US cents per single test, revealing its integrated merits of ultra-high sensitivity and economic feasibility. Kaladharan et al. developed an alternative approach (31), in which nanopillar arrays were integrated into a DVD substrate and coated with a silver film. When this structure was combined with Raman-labeled silver nanoparticles, detection could be completed within 20 minutes without complex washing steps, and a detection limit of 50 pg/mL was achieved. To address portability, Li et al. (32) developed a lateral flow strip platform based on IR808 resonance Raman reporters. When combined with a portable Raman spectrometer, the detection time was reduced to less than 15 minutes.

In influenza virus detection, Wang et al. established a magnetic bead-based enrichment method (33), in which H5N1 viruses were captured using magnetic beads, which was followed by SERS tag-mediated signal amplification; the LOD for virus was as low as 5.0×10−6 TCID50/mL. This system demonstrated high specificity, with no cross-reactivity observed for other influenza subtypes. Zhdanov et al. employed aptamers as recognition elements (34) and optimized the morphology of silver nano-island substrates, achieving a detection limit as low as 190 viral particles per milliliter (VP/mL), and the total detection time was only 6 minutes. Compared with antibodies, aptamers offer advantages in terms of ease of modification, which has become increasingly valuable in sensor design. Nevertheless, neither study was validated with human clinical samples, and their practical performance remains to be assessed.

Multiplex detection represents another key advantage of label-based SERS. Shen et al. developed an SERS-based lateral flow immunoassay capable of simultaneously detecting Streptococcus pneumoniae and influenza A virus (35). Distinct Raman reporter molecules can provide unique spectral encoding, enabling the simultaneous analysis of multiple pathogens in a single test. Importantly, this study employed a ResNet-18 deep learning model to analyze the SERS imaging distribution patterns of the test line (T line), thereby effectively eliminating false-positive results caused by membrane inhomogeneity in conventional intensity-based readout methods. The proposed method achieved 94.52% accuracy, 91.43% sensitivity, and 97.37% specificity on the test dataset. Zhao et al. further incorporated deep learning techniques (36) and introduced a process in which probe distribution patterns near the test line are analyzed to reduce interference from abnormal signals. The limits of detection (LODs) for H1N1 and Streptococcus pneumoniae were 29 PFU/mL and 16 cells/mL, respectively. The sensitivity was 172-fold and 312-fold greater than that of conventional gold nanoparticle (AuNP)-based test strips. Among 21 clinical throat swab samples that tested positive for H1N1, this method achieved a 100% detection rate. In addition, the results showed a strong correlation with those obtained by ELISA (R2=0.98).

Strengths and limitations

Label-based SERS maintains three primary advantages. First, this approach provides high sensitivity, with detection limits reaching picograms-per-milliliter and viral-particles-per-milliliter levels—or even lower—for respiratory viruses. Second, strong specificity is ensured by antibodies or aptamers, which effectively reduce nonspecific background interference. Third, reliable quantitative capability can be achieved due to the stable signals of Raman reporter molecules, facilitating the construction of calibration curves and multiplex encoding.

However, several limitations remain. Probe fabrication typically involves multiple steps, including nanomaterial synthesis, reporter molecule functionalization, and conjugation with recognition elements (37). This multistep process is not only complex and costly but also increases the likelihood of batch-to-batch variability. Functionalized nanoprobes and high-quality enhancement substrates are relatively expensive, limiting large-scale implementation. Furthermore, the detection workflow often includes incubation, washing, and signal readout steps, which hinder its suitability for rapid point-of-care testing (POCT). Finally, the long-term stability and interbatch consistency of nanoprobes, which may directly influence the reproducibility and reliability of detection results, remain to be further validated.

Label-free SERS detection

Fundamental principles and technical characteristics

Label-free SERS detection is based on the direct acquisition of intrinsic Raman signals from viral components. Variations in the composition of viral proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids produce distinct spectral “fingerprint” features (38). In this approach, virus-containing samples are deposited onto plasmonic substrates, where intrinsic vibrational signals are amplified and subsequently used for identification and classification. Since no exogenous Raman reporter molecules are introduced, the original molecular information of the sample is preserved. When target molecules exhibit relatively large Raman scattering cross-sections, label-free SERS substrates can be effectively employed for direct detection (39).

Direct detection of viral proteins and nucleic acids

Compared with label-based strategies that rely on antibodies or aptamers, label-free SERS is more highly dependent on capturing the intrinsic molecular signatures of viruses or the overall biochemical profile of infected samples. This confers unique advantages in rapid screening and multiplex pathogen discrimination. Gao et al. used an H-shaped nanopore array structure (40) to directly record Raman signals of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and IgG antibodies under 785-nm laser excitation. It was demonstrated that this nanostructure could generate reproducible SERS signals in liquid environments, with a detection limit of 0.85 ng/mL. Peng et al. further investigated the stability of viral components (41). By employing gold nanoarrays that enhanced spike protein signals via a “lightning rod” effect, the authors systematically evaluated the influence of temperature on Raman activity and demonstrated that the LOD of the Au nanoarray-based SERS chip was as low as 1 pg/mL. These studies not only enhanced detection capability but also contributed to a deeper understanding of viral structural properties.

Multiplex pathogen discrimination is a characteristic strength of label-free SERS. Yang et al. developed an integrated platform combining both hardware and algorithmic innovations (42). A silica-coated silver nanorod array was used as the substrate, and a deep learning model was established for spectral analysis. This system was capable of identifying co-infections involving multiple respiratory viruses and performing concentration regression analysis. The limits of detection for single-virus samples ranged from 50 to 100 PFU/mL, whereas those for dual- or triple-virus mixtures increased to 100–1,000 PFU/mL, indicating that multiplex detection may compromise detection sensitivity to some extent. Chisanga et al. analyzed serum metabolomic profiles from nonhospitalized adults (43) and successfully identified metabolic signatures associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection, suggesting that host response information can also be leveraged for diagnostic purposes.

Strengths and limitations of label-free SERS

Label-free SERS enables pathogen identification through direct acquisition of intrinsic Raman signals, without the need for specific probes. This approach offers several advantages, including simplified operation, rapid detection, and the ability to capture comprehensive molecular information. Consequently, it has demonstrated considerable potential in the rapid screening and preliminary discrimination of respiratory viruses.

However, several major challenges remain. First, proteins, salts, and metabolites present in clinical samples may competitively adsorb onto the substrate surface, leading to substantial background interference. Second, SERS substrates are highly sensitive to experimental conditions (44), and even minor variations in nanostructure size, surface charge, or sample drying methods can significantly affect signal output.

Third, as the acquired spectra represent composite contributions from multiple components, it is difficult to perform quantitative analysis based on a single peak intensity, as is commonly done in label-based approaches. In samples with low viral loads, target signals are particularly susceptible to being masked by background noise.

In addition, cross-platform reproducibility remains a significant challenge. Variations in instrumentation, substrate fabrication, and preprocessing protocols across different laboratories can result in distribution shifts in spectral data (45), limiting the generalizability of models trained in a single setting. Model interpretability is also a critical issue. Deep learning methods are often regarded as “black boxes”, and their decision-making processes are not readily interpretable by clinicians, which may hinder their acceptance in medical diagnostics.

Therefore, label-free SERS is currently more suited to being a rapid screening and pathogen differentiation tool than a standalone confirmatory diagnostic method. The key issue of detection consistency must be resolved before it can be integrated into routine clinical practice. Once these challenges are overcome, label-free SERS may become an important tool for rapid and large-scale screening.

Combination of SERS and nucleic acid amplification strategies

Technical principles and methodological characteristics

A strategy has been developed that integrates nucleic acid amplification techniques with SERS-based signal readout. Established amplification methods, including PCR, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based systems, are employed to retain high specificity, while SERS is used as an alternative to conventional fluorescence for endpoint or real-time signal detection (46). This combination can provide enhanced signal amplification and shorter detection times. Compared with fluorescence-based readout, SERS exhibits narrower spectral peaks, resistance to photobleaching, and strong multiplex encoding capacity (47). These features confer advantages in multitarget nucleic acid analysis and trace-level detection.

Applications in respiratory virus detection

Given that nucleic acid amplification provides powerful molecular-level signal amplification and that SERS enables highly sensitive detection of amplified products, their integration often results in superior analytical performance as compared with either method used alone.

Wu et al. developed an SERS-PCR platform targeting the E gene and RdRp gene of SARS-CoV-2 (48). Conventional RT-qPCR typically requires approximately 25 thermal cycles to reach the detection threshold, but the SERS-PCR approach achieved detection within only 8 cycles, indicating a significant reduction in assay time. However, its LOD was relatively high at 2.56×107 copies/µL for the concentration after amplification, and no validation using clinical samples was performed.

Wang et al. employed a different amplification system (49), in which CRISPR/Cas13a-based specific recognition was combined with SERS-integrated lateral flow assays. This platform enabled the detection of H1N1 viral RNA over a range of 4.4 to 104 copies/µL, with a 56-fold improvement in sensitivity compared with that of commercial colloidal gold test strips. Importantly, the assay was validated using 51 clinical throat swab samples, including 31 positive and 20 negative specimens. It showed 100% consistency with RT-qPCR results, with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy all reaching 100%, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.0. This work is among the few studies that have conducted a comprehensive clinical performance evaluation of SERS for viral nucleic acid detection, demonstrating the substantial potential of the SERS-CRISPR platform for clinical translation.

Limitations

Despite these advantages, several limitations remain evident. The inherent complexity of nucleic acid amplification workflows has not yet been eliminated, and sample-processing steps, including lysis, nucleic acid extraction, and precise temperature control, are still required (50), thereby limiting operational simplicity. In addition, contamination risk must be carefully considered, as nucleic acid amplification is highly sensitive to trace contamination, which may result in false-positive outcomes and amplification bias. The anticipated advantage in detection speed is also less pronounced than expected. For instance, He et al. developed a label-free SERS approach (51) and combined it with machine learning to directly analyze nasal swab samples, enabling influenza virus detection within seconds without the need for nucleic acid extraction or amplification. In comparison, although SERS-nucleic acid amplification strategies may theoretically achieve lower limits of detection, they are clearly disadvantaged in terms of speed and operational simplicity.

These comparisons suggest that improvements in sensitivity are only achievable at the expense of rapid detection capability. Fundamentally, this strategy should be regarded as an enhancement of existing molecular diagnostic systems rather than a fully independent detection modality. It functions as a “performance augmentation module” rather than as a standalone technique. In practical terms, this approach is particularly valuable in laboratory settings requiring ultrahigh sensitivity. However, in POCT scenarios, it is less competitive compared to label-free SERS or rapid immunoassays in terms of simplicity and speed. Consequently, its future applications are more likely to be focused on high-sensitivity laboratory diagnostics, including confirmatory testing of complex cases and the analysis of low viral load samples.

Microfluidic and portable SERS platforms

Core elements of platform design

Microfluidic platforms integrate the entire detection workflow into a single chip, providing sample processing, reaction control, and signal acquisition within a miniaturized system. As a result, sample and reagent consumption can be reduced to the microliter scale, thereby lowering operational costs. In addition, closed-system fluidic control enhances analytical efficiency and minimizes human-induced variability. Microfluidic SERS platforms can generally be classified into two categories: colloidal nanoparticle-based flow systems and fixed nanostructure substrate-based chip systems (52). In colloidal flow systems, signal enhancement relies on the dispersion and aggregation of nanoparticles within the fluid. In the presence of target viruses, nanoparticle aggregation induces the formation of hotspots, leading to signal amplification (53). In contrast, in fixed-substrate systems, the prefabricated nanostructure arrays are located on chip surfaces (54), where viral samples flowing across the substrate undergo stable and reproducible signal enhancement. Each approach presents distinct strengths and limitations, and the choice between them depends on the application requirements at hand.

Li et al. developed a fiber-optic SERS microfluidic platform, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) controlled within 10% (55). The integration of portable Raman spectrometers enabled the system to operate independently of bulky instrumentation. Yang et al. developed a Y-shaped microchannel SERS microfluidic platform (56), in which Fe3O4@SiO2@Ag magnetic core-shell-satellite nanostructures were combined with immobilized gold nanotriangles to achieve synergistic signal enhancement. This platform exhibited a favorable linear response over the concentration range of 1 pM to 1 µM (R2=0.99). Its LOD was as low as 1 pM, and the platform showed excellent reproducibility, with an RSD below 2%. Specificity tests demonstrated that the platform could effectively distinguish RSV from the H1N1 influenza virus. Nevertheless, this study remains at the proof-of-concept stage and has not yet been validated using authentic clinical samples. Further validation of its diagnostic performance in clinical cohorts will be necessary in future work.

Applications in respiratory pathogen detection

In recent years, microfluidic SERS-based detection of SARS-CoV-2 has progressed rapidly. Lin et al. developed a ratiometric microfluidic chip targeting viral RNA (57). This system includes complementary base pairing for selective recognition and internal standard molecules for signal normalization. When combined with a portable Raman spectrometer, a detection limit as low as 7.6×10−14 M can be achieved, with a dynamic range spanning six orders of magnitude, suggesting strong potential for point-of-care applications.

Vedelago et al. addressed multiplex detection requirements (58) by developing a microarray platform based on gold-silver hollow nanobox barcodes. This approach simultaneously detects spike and NP through electrohydrodynamically induced nanomixing. Notably, this platform was reported to be capable of distinguishing between the original SARS-CoV-2 strain and variants such as Delta and Omicron. The platform exhibited an LOD of 50 pg/mL for the receptor-binding domain (RBD) protein. The practical limit of detection for intact virions was 20 virus particles per µL, whereas the theoretical LOD was as low as 3 virus particles per µL. Specificity tests revealed no significant cross-reactivity with other viruses, such as Zika virus and MERS-CoV. A total of 28 nasopharyngeal swab samples were included for clinical validation, comprising 24 positive samples from cases involving the Delta variant, Omicron variant, and wild-type strain, and 4 negative samples. Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated good agreement between the SERS results and RT-qPCR data. The intra-assay and inter-assay RSDs were 10.8% and 14.0%, respectively. Notably, Nb105 and Nb21 produced stronger signals in response to the Delta variant, whereas Nb34 preferentially recognized the Omicron variant. These findings highlight the potential of this SERS platform for viral variant identification. Huang et al. employed V-shaped resonant cavity arrays to capture viral particles via periodic nanostructures (59). Spectral data were analyzed via supervised deep learning algorithms, enabling detection within 20 minutes. Under optimized conditions, the platform achieved a LOD of 3.5×10−4 PFU/mL (approximately 35–350 copies/mL) for SARS-CoV-2 virions. Its detection sensitivity for inactivated vaccines reached a maximum dilution factor of 1010. When integrated with the ResNet deep learning algorithm, the platform yielded a sensitivity of 91.4%, a specificity of 95.0%, and an overall accuracy of 94.1% when tested on 235 clinical throat swab samples, including 35 positive cases and 200 healthy controls. The corresponding AUC was 0.938. Furthermore, this platform was applied for the first time to the real-time monitoring of 58 environmental samples collected from vaccination sites, achieving an accuracy of 84.5%. Tikhonova et al. (60) attempted to detect the influenza A virus using a composite track-etched membrane to construct a 3D spiky surface. By integrating broad-spectrum DNA aptamer recognition, nasal swab samples were analyzed within 10 minutes, achieving detection limits in the range of 120–2,000 VP/mL, which were comparable to those obtained by RT-qPCR.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of microfluidic SERS platforms can be observed across multiple dimensions. Process integration allows for the continuous execution of mixing, enrichment, and detection steps, thereby obviating the need for manual intervention. The consumption of samples and reagents is minimized to microliter levels, leading to cost reduction. In addition, the use of closed systems effectively decreases the risk of contamination while platform portability facilitates on-site deployment. Potential application scenarios include emergency departments, border quarantine, and environmental monitoring. For instance, Song et al. successfully detected SARS-CoV-2 contamination on packaging surfaces (61), and the reliability of the method was validated through blinded testing.

However, several critical limitations remain. Chip design typically involves the integration of multiple functional modules, requiring coordinated optimization of fluid control, substrate immobilization, and optical signal acquisition. Any deficiency in these components may adversely affect overall system performance. High manufacturing costs also represent a significant barrier, as large-scale fabrication of precise nanostructures and functionalized surfaces remains technically challenging. Furthermore, batch-to-batch consistency is difficult to ensure. At present, most microfluidic SERS platforms remain at the prototype validation stage. Although some systems have been tested with the use of real samples and have demonstrated feasibility, a considerable gap between prototype development and the implementation of a practical product remains.

Microfluidic and portable SERS platforms represent a critical transition of SERS technology from a high-sensitivity laboratory tool to a rapid clinical diagnostic system. With the continued advancement of research on highly reproducible substrates, low-cost portable Raman devices, automated sample-processing modules, and artificial intelligence-based data analysis, these platforms are expected to play an increasingly important role in the rapid diagnosis of respiratory viruses, epidemiological surveillance, and response to public health emergencies. Clinical validation and real-sample performance of SERS for respiratory virus detection

Methodological performance does not necessarily translate into clinical effectiveness. The practical value of SERS platforms can only be fully assessed through successful validation in real respiratory samples. In recent years, research efforts have gradually shifted from model systems toward clinical specimens; however, the field remains at an exploratory stage. Types and characteristics of real samples

Respiratory virus detection typically relies on multiple types of clinical specimens, which differ significantly in composition and physicochemical properties. These differences directly influence SERS detection performance.

Nasopharyngeal swabs are among the most commonly used sample types. They generally contain relatively high viral loads and are therefore suitable for early-stage screening. However, such samples also contain abundant mucus and epithelial cell debris, which may cover the nanostructured substrate surface and hinder the adsorption of target molecules, thereby reducing signal stability. Several studies have attempted to validate SERS performance in nasopharyngeal swab samples. For influenza A virus detection, Tikhonova et al. developed a spike-like track-etched membrane SERS sensor (60), which, after immobilization of DNA aptamers, achieved a detection limit of 120–2,000 VP/mL in nasopharyngeal swabs. Liu et al. developed a multiplex magnetic SERS lateral flow immunoassay (62), which enabled simultaneous detection of H1N1, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV without sample pretreatment. In addition, Wang et al. combined CRISPR-Cas13a with SERS-based lateral flow assays (49) to quantitatively detect H1N1 RNA in throat swab samples, reporting complete agreement with RT-qPCR results in small-scale validation studies and providing a new approach for highly specific POCT. However, mucin-induced matrix interference remains a significant challenge, requiring either sample dilution or magnetic bead enrichment steps.

Oropharyngeal swabs are easier to collect and are better tolerated by patients; however, they typically contain lower viral loads than do nasopharyngeal samples. At present, SERS studies based on real oropharyngeal swab samples remain limited, with some investigations restricted to simulated sample validation. Gu et al. developed a “sandwich-type” sensor based on spherical mixed aptamer-gold nanoparticle constructs (63) and used spike protein-specific DNA aptamers as recognition elements. Testing with simulated oropharyngeal sample systems supported the potential of this system. However, most SERS studies using this sample type are limited to simulated samples, with only one small-scale clinical validation reported to date.

Saliva can be noninvasively collected and is suitable for repeated sampling. However, it contains an abundance of proteins, enzymes, and microorganisms, which generate strong background interference signals. Moreover, variability in viscosity and composition further increases detection uncertainty. To address these challenges, various SERS strategies have been proposed. Zhang et al. fabricated a dense and uniform gold nanoparticle film using an oil-water-oil three-phase interfacial self-assembly method for SERS-based immunosensing (64). Additionally, Berus et al. used a label-free SERS approach, along with partial least squares discriminant analysis and support vector machine classification (65), to achieve direct saliva analysis, enabling differentiation between SARS-CoV-2-positive and -negative samples within 15 minutes. Label-free SERS performance in saliva is significantly lower due to overwhelming background signals.

Sputum samples typically contain relatively high concentrations of pathogens; however, their high viscosity presents challenges for sample processing. Without adequate pretreatment, sputum may contaminate the substrate surface, thereby compromising signal quality. Driskell et al. used silver nanorod arrays to detect influenza viruses in clinical sputum samples (66), achieving favorable results in individual samples. Jia et al. developed wheat germ agglutinin-modified magnetic SERS nanotags (Fe3O4@Au-WGA) (67), which, when combined with dual-channel SERS lateral flow assays, enabled simultaneous detection of influenza A virus and Streptococcus pneumoniae in sputum and throat swab samples. This platform achieved 100% diagnostic accuracy and outperformed conventional colloidal gold-based test strips. However, the label-free approaches remain largely untested.

Exhaled breath is a sample type that may allow for noninvasive collection. However, the concentration of target molecules in breath is extremely low, imposing stringent requirements for detection sensitivity. To address this challenge, Grieve et al. developed a prototype for a handheld breath analyzer (68). The device incorporates a chip functionalized with three SERS probe molecules. Upon exhalation, viral biomarkers interact with the sensor surface, and signals are subsequently read by a portable Raman spectrometer. The system yielded a false-negative rate of 3.8% and a false-positive rate of 0.1%, with screening completed within 5 minutes, indicating strong potential for large-scale rapid screening. However, breath-based detection often relies on indirect biomarkers or host-response-related molecular signals, and the distinction between such signals and direct viral detection requires further clarification.

The fundamental mechanism underlying matrix interference is competitive adsorption: non-target biomolecules (mucins, albumin, amylase, and cellular debris) have a higher affinity for noble metal surfaces than do most viral components, leading to hotspot occlusion and reduced adsorption of target molecules. This effect is particularly pronounced in label-free SERS, where detection relies entirely on the direct adsorption of viral particles to the substrate surface. For label-based strategies, matrix components can also block antibody-antigen binding sites, further compromising detection performance.

Overall, these sample types share common characteristics, including complex matrices, diverse components, and substantial variability in viral load. This suggests that SERS-based detection must not only achieve high sensitivity but also possess strong resistance to background interference. Different sample types often require tailored pretreatment strategies, which increases operational complexity and poses challenges to platform universality.

Matrix effect mitigation strategies

Several approaches have been developed to address matrix interference in clinical samples.

Magnetic bead enrichment: uses antibody-functionalized magnetic beads to selectively capture target viruses from complex matrices, thereby separating them from background components prior to SERS detection (33,67). This approach has been shown to improve sensitivity by 10- to 100-fold in saliva and sputum samples.

Antifouling surface modification: coating SERS substrates with polyethylene glycol (PEG) or zwitterionic polymers reduces non-specific adsorption of matrix proteins while preserving target binding capacity.

Microfluidic integrated pretreatment: integrates on-chip lysis, filtration, and enrichment steps to automatically purify samples before detection, thereby eliminating manual processing and reducing variability (57,59).

Ratiometric detection: uses internal standard Raman reporters to normalize signal variations caused by matrix effects, thereby improving quantitative accuracy in complex samples.

Performance comparison with established clinical diagnostic methods

To evaluate the practical application potential of SERS, its performance has frequently been compared with existing clinical diagnostic methods, particularly RT-qPCR and antigen-based assays.

RT-qPCR is widely regarded as the gold standard for respiratory virus detection due to its high sensitivity and specificity. However, this method relies on nucleic acid amplification, resulting in relatively long detection times and stringent requirements for laboratory conditions. In contrast, antigen-based assays are characterized by operational simplicity and rapid turnaround but have insufficient sensitivity, especially in samples with low viral loads.

In several studies, SERS has demonstrated lower limits of detection, in some cases approaching or even matching those of RT-qPCR, particularly in label-based detection and amplification-coupled strategies. For example, the multiplex magnetic SERS lateral flow immunoassay developed by Liu et al. (62) achieved detection limits of 8 pg/mL for both SARS-CoV-2 and RSV, exhibiting significantly higher sensitivity than conventional colloidal gold-based lateral flow test strips.

In addition, SERS offers advantages in detection speed. Certain systems have been reported to complete detection within relatively short timeframes. A magnetic SERS biosensor developed by Li et al. (69) enabled on-site detection of SARS-CoV-2 within 15 minutes. Similarly, a single-particle SERS immunoassay proposed by Mohammadi et al. (70) achieved saliva-based detection within 30 minutes via a handheld Raman spectrometer, demonstrating suitability for rapid field screening.

However, substantial variability exists across different studies on this subject. For one, SERS strategies exhibit significant performance differences. For another, variability in sample type, substrate structure, and experimental condition have contributed to inconsistencies in the reported results.

Regarding sensitivity and specificity, some studies have reported high concordance with clinical standards in real samples. Hennigan et al. used a silver nanorod array-based SERS to detect Mycoplasma pneumoniae (71), achieving a classification accuracy exceeding 97% in clinical throat swab samples. Moreover, Shen et al. were able to achieve simultaneous detection of Streptococcus pneumoniae and influenza virus on a single test line and further validated their method using 31 clinical throat swab samples (35). They achieved a 172- to 312-fold improvement in sensitivity compared with conventional colloidal gold assays.

However, these findings are predominantly derived from small-scale studies, and a lack of standardized evaluation criteria limits cross-study comparability. Therefore, although SERS can achieve favorable performance under certain conditions, its overall performance remains inconsistent, and direct benchmarking against established clinical methods remains challenging.

Major challenges

Despite the high analytical sensitivity and promising specificity demonstrated by SERS in respiratory virus detection, multiple challenges must be addressed before successful clinical translation can be achieved. These challenges are primarily associated with platform stability, data consistency, and clinical validation, and a unified standardization framework has yet to be established.

Insufficient reproducibility

SERS signals are highly dependent on localized hotspots, and even minor variations in measurement position can lead to significant signal fluctuations. In addition, batch-to-batch variability in substrate properties, including nanoparticle size, morphology, and aggregation behavior (72), is an obstacle to achieving consistent results across experiments that have the same protocol. The inherent complexity of respiratory samples further exacerbates this variability (73). Without substantial improvements in reproducibility, SERS-based detection is unlikely to meet the reliability requirements of clinical diagnostics.

Challenges in quantitative analysis

In principle, SERS signal intensity is positively correlated with the concentration of target molecules. However, in practice, this relationship is often unstable due to the influence of multiple factors, including laser power, focal position, integration time, and local temperature variations (74). Moreover, in complex respiratory samples, proteins, salts, and mucus components may compete with target molecules for adsorption sites on the substrate surface (75). As a major glycoprotein component of saliva and nasopharyngeal swab samples, mucin may further impair detection performance by forming a physically adsorbed barrier layer on the SERS substrate, blocking access to plasmonic hotspots, causing spectral overlap with viral proteins, and altering substrate wettability (76). Collectively, these effects undermine signal uniformity, reproducibility, and quantitative accuracy. To achieve stable and reproducible quantification, more robust methodological frameworks are required, including the development of internal standard calibration strategies, substrate homogenization techniques, and standardized data normalization approaches.

Lack of standardization and comparability

No unified standards have been established in the SERS field regarding experimental procedures and evaluation criteria. Variations in sample preparation, substrate fabrication, instrument parameter settings, and data-processing workflows lead to substantial discrepancies across studies, making direct comparison difficult. Zeng et al. systematically analyzed the challenges associated with the clinical translation of SERS in multiplex biomolecular analysis (77), highlighting a significant gap between proof-of-concept studies and real-world clinical applications. Factors such as sample pretreatment, the morphology of noble metal substrates, and laser wavelength and power settings can all significantly influence spectral responses (78). In addition, inconsistencies in baseline correction, denoising, and peak-fitting methods may introduce further variability (45). The absence of standardization not only hinders the cross-validation of results but also presents a barrier to regulatory approval and broader clinical adoption.

At present, most SERS-related studies remain at the stage of small-scale laboratory investigations, with only limited numbers of clinical samples being evaluated. A considerable proportion of studies are based on simulated samples or a small number of real specimens, which differs substantially from actual clinical settings. For example, Abdullah et al. achieved a sensitivity of 10−9 M in detecting the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein in saliva (79); however, artificially spiked samples, rather than specimens from infected individuals, were used. Similarly, the SERS immunosensor developed by Zhang et al. achieved a detection limit of 6.07 fg/mL for spike protein in untreated saliva (64), but systematic evaluation in clinical cohorts has not yet been conducted. In studies on RSV strain differentiation, Shanmukh et al. successfully distinguished different strains (80), although the sample size was limited and primarily based on cultured viruses, which may not adequately reflect variations across real populations or disease stages.

One of the most prominent limitations is the extremely small sample size used in most published studies, with the majority including fewer than 100 clinical specimens. Only three studies included more than 50 samples, and none have reached the sample size typically required for regulatory approval of in vitro diagnostic devices, which generally exceeds 1,000 specimens. Such limited sample sizes reduce the statistical power to detect rare false-positive or false-negative results, particularly in low-prevalence settings, where even a small number of classification errors can markedly affect positive and negative predictive values.

Another major limitation is that most available studies are retrospective and single-center in design. All studies listed in Table 6 were conducted at a single institution, and most relied on stored frozen samples with known outcomes rather than prospective enrollment of consecutive patients. This introduces potential selection bias, because stored samples may not accurately represent the full spectrum of disease severity, viral loads, and sample quality encountered in routine clinical practice. In addition, multicenter and prospective evaluations are largely lacking. The multiplex magnetic SERS lateral flow immunoassay proposed by Liu et al. (62) was proven capable of triple-virus detection in throat swab samples; however, the validation was performed through the use of samples with known outcomes and without a prospective study design. Furthermore, spectral characteristics of early-stage infections, asymptomatic carriers, and convalescent individuals have not yet been systematically characterized. This limitation restricts the comprehensive assessment of sensitivity, specificity, and clinical utility. The absence of multicenter prospective studies remains a critical gap, as such studies are essential for demonstrating generalizability across different patient populations and healthcare settings.

Concordance with the clinical gold standard, RT-qPCR, also varies substantially across studies, ranging from 87.1% to 100%. Lower concordance has been consistently observed in label-free SERS studies (59,65), primarily owing to background interference from complex sample matrices. Notably, no study has systematically evaluated performance in specimens with low viral loads, such as those containing fewer than 1,000 copies/mL, despite the fact that such samples are common in early-stage infections and asymptomatic carriers. This represents a major translational gap, because early identification of presymptomatic individuals is critical for controlling the spread of respiratory viruses.

In addition, cohort representation remains highly limited. Most studies have focused exclusively on symptomatic adults aged 18–65 years, with little or no information available for pediatric populations, elderly individuals, immunocompromised patients, or convalescent individuals. These populations may exhibit atypical clinical manifestations, distinct viral load kinetics, and altered sample matrix compositions, all of which could significantly affect SERS detection performance. Additionally, only Vedelago et al. (58) attempted to distinguish between viral variants, despite the importance of this capability for epidemiological surveillance during outbreaks.

Finally, the lack of standardized evaluation criteria further hinders meaningful clinical assessment. Different studies have used inconsistent definitions of true positive and true negative results, variable RT-qPCR cycle threshold (Ct) cutoffs ranging from 35 to 40, and nonuniform reporting of diagnostic performance metrics. As a result, direct cross-study comparison is difficult, and the accumulation of robust clinical evidence is impeded. For example, some studies report only analytical sensitivity based on spiked samples, whereas others report clinical sensitivity using patient specimens, potentially leading to misleading comparisons of platform performance.

Taken together, these limitations indicate that SERS technology remains at an early stage of clinical validation. Although proof-of-concept studies have demonstrated its feasibility and considerable diagnostic potential, rigorous large-scale, multicenter, and prospective evaluations are still required before SERS can be established as a routine clinical diagnostic tool.

Cost and instrument accessibility

High-performance Raman spectrometers are typically expensive, and their maintenance and operational costs are substantial, limiting deployment in primary healthcare settings. Although portable devices have fewer barriers to usage, they are still inferior to benchtop instruments in terms of spectral resolution, signal-to-noise ratio, and stability. These limitations are particularly pronounced in the analysis of complex clinical samples, where measurement variability may be amplified. In contrast, RT-qPCR and antigen-based assays already have established, mature industrial ecosystems. Ali et al. reviewed the application of handheld Raman devices in POCT for respiratory viruses (81), noting that despite progress in miniaturization, only a limited number of SERS-based products have received clinical approval. Tripathy et al. further highlighted inconsistencies in the use of the term “POCT” (82), pointing out that some of the related studies relied on large stationary spectrometers, which violate the principles of near-patient testing. Moreover, the transition from laboratory-based systems to field-deployable platforms remains constrained by challenges in instrument integration and user-friendliness (83). Therefore, for SERS to transition from laboratory platforms to clinical point-of-care applications, improvements are needed not only in analytical performance but also in instrument integration, automation, operational simplicity, and user-oriented design.

Future perspectives

SERS has demonstrated considerable promise in respiratory virus detection; however, its development is in a transition between proof-of-concept validation and clinical feasibility assessment. To achieve this shift, a change in research focus from isolated performance optimization to the systematic improvement of platform stability, workflow design, and clinical adaptability is necessary.

Development of highly reproducible and standardized substrates

Substrate consistency is a prerequisite for reliable SERS detection. Future efforts should be focused on ensuring batch-level reproducibility rather than on single-instance high performance. Emphasis should be placed on the development of standardized fabrication approaches, including controlled synthesis, self-assembly, and template-assisted methods. In addition, elements of unified evaluation frameworks should be established, such as the use of standard molecules as internal references to validate enhancement factors, thereby enabling cross-laboratory comparability.

Clinically oriented sample pretreatment and integrated platforms

Real respiratory samples contain complex background components that can significantly interfere with signal detection. Among these, mucin represents a major source of interference because it can increase sample viscosity, hinder analyte access to plasmonic hotspots, and compromise detection performance. To address mucin-related interference, several targeted strategies have been developed. For example, Farsaeivahid et al. (84) employed nylon-brush filtration as a pretreatment method, which reduced saliva viscosity by 80% and improved the detection limit for the S protein by fivefold. In another study, Zhang et al. (85) developed an evanescent-field-enhanced SERS substrate that enabled an ultralow detection limit of 1.6×10−16 M in untreated saliva without antibody functionalization, effectively circumventing the physical barrier effect imposed by mucin. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating sample-specific pretreatment and interference-resistant sensing strategies into clinically applicable SERS platforms. Future platforms should integrate sample pretreatment, target enrichment, and detection processes within microfluidic or chip-based systems, thereby reducing manual variability and improving analytical efficiency. However, currently available prototype systems are often limited by structural complexity and high cost. Therefore, platform development should focus on simplifying system design while maintaining stable performance.

Artificial intelligence-driven spectral interpretation

The complex spectral outputs generated by label-free SERS often require advanced computational methods for efficient interpretation. At present, techniques such as principal component analysis, support vector machines, and deep learning have been applied to spectral classification and pathogen identification, demonstrating considerable potential for improving detection efficiency and extracting high-dimensional features. However, their practical application in viral detection remains constrained by significant limitations and potential risks. First, model performance is highly dependent on the scale, quality, and representativeness of the training data; however, existing studies are often limited by small sample sizes, imbalanced class distributions, and insufficient coverage of viral subtypes, which can lead to overfitting and reduced generalizability in real clinical settings. Second, SERS spectra are highly susceptible to variations in substrate fabrication, fluctuations in detection conditions, differences in sample pretreatment, and instrument-dependent effects, all of which can introduce substantial batch effects and cross-platform shifts, thereby compromising the stability of a single model across datasets acquired in different laboratories or at different time points. Furthermore, although deep learning-based methods offer strong feature extraction capabilities, their “black-box” nature limits result interpretability and makes it more difficult to trace the sources of misclassification and assess associated risks in clinical practice. In viral detection, false-positive and false-negative results may directly affect patient triage, isolation decisions, and subsequent treatment; therefore, model stability and tolerance to error must be rigorously evaluated. In addition, viral mutation, co-infection, and samples with low viral loads may also expose models to feature drift and recognition failure.

Therefore, future research should focus on the construction of large-scale, multisource datasets and the implementation of cross-platform validation to enhance the robustness and transferability of artificial intelligence models. This will enable reliable multiplex pathogen identification in practical applications. Furthermore, the interpretability of algorithmic outputs and the stability of models across different clinical scenarios should be further validated to facilitate their acceptance in real-world medical diagnostics.

Multiplex detection and integrated diagnostics

Clinical symptoms alone are often insufficient to distinguish among the different types of respiratory viral infections. Therefore, the development of SERS platforms capable of simultaneous identification of multiple pathogens is of considerable clinical significance. The design of Raman reporter molecules with distinct spectral fingerprints can aid in achieving parallel multitarget detection. However, challenges such as spectral cross-interference and inconsistent sensitivity between different targets remain to be addressed. Further optimization of probe design and signal deconvolution strategies is required to enhance multiplex detection performance.

Development of portable POCT devices

To enable the application of SERS in field settings and primary healthcare, the development of devices that balance analytical performance and portability is essential. Current portable Raman spectrometers still lag behind laboratory-based systems in terms of spectral resolution and signal-to-noise ratio. Improvements should be centered on optimizing optical pathways, enhancing signal-processing algorithms, and simplifying user interfaces. These advancements can be expected to improve the reliability of portable devices when applied to complex biological samples, thereby meeting the requirements of POCT.

Large-scale clinical validation and regulatory standards

The lack of robust clinical evidence remains a major barrier to the translation of SERS to clinical practice. Large-scale, prospective, multicenter clinical trials that examine a diversity of populations, disease stages, and geographic regions are needed to comprehensively evaluate diagnostic performance. Additionally, standardized protocols related to sample processing, substrate evaluation, instrument parameter settings, and data analysis workflows should be established. The development of regulatory-compliant quality control systems is essential to ensuring that SERS-based technologies meet clinical approval requirements.

Overall, the research focus of SERS in respiratory virus detection is shifting from technical feasibility to practical applicability. Only through systematic advances in substrate reproducibility, platform integration, algorithm robustness, and clinical validation can SERS be successfully translated from a laboratory-based analytical tool into a clinically applicable diagnostic modality.

Targeted strategies to overcome current translational barriers

Although the future directions outlined above are promising, their significance lies not merely in technological advancement per se, but also in their capacity to address the specific barriers that currently hinder the clinical translation of SERS. In this context, the development of highly reproducible substrates, integrated sample-processing platforms, artificial intelligence-assisted spectral analysis, and portable point-of-care devices should not be viewed as isolated innovations, but as targeted strategies for addressing the major limitations outlined in the preceding section.

The issue of insufficient reproducibility can be most effectively addressed through advances in substrate engineering and fabrication standardization. Since spectral variation often originates from the uneven distribution of electromagnetic hotspots and batch-to-batch substrate heterogeneity, the development of controllable and standardized substrates is essential for reducing signal fluctuation at the source. Approaches such as template-assisted fabrication, lithographic patterning, and self-assembly-based nanostructure production may improve structural uniformity and batch-to-batch consistency. In parallel, the incorporation of internal standards and reference molecules into substrate design may further facilitate signal calibration across measurements, thereby improving repeatability and allowing more reliable comparison across experiments and laboratories.

The challenges associated with quantitative analysis may be alleviated by integrating sample pretreatment, target enrichment, and signal acquisition into closed, standardized microfluidic or chip-based systems. In conventional workflows, quantitative instability often arises from manual handling, matrix interference, and inconsistent analyte adsorption on substrate surfaces. Integrated platforms can reduce operator-induced variability, minimize sample loss, and improve control over key reaction conditions, such as mixing efficiency, incubation time, and analyte distribution. When combined with internal standard strategies and automated data normalization, such systems may markedly enhance the reproducibility and robustness of quantitative SERS measurements in complex respiratory specimens.

The lack of standardization and comparability across studies may be addressed through the establishment of unified technical protocols and data-driven analytical frameworks. Standardized criteria for substrate evaluation, harmonized sample-processing workflows, consistent instrument parameter settings, and transparent data-preprocessing pipelines are all necessary for improving interstudy comparability. At the same time, artificial intelligence-based spectral interpretation may contribute to this goal by enabling more objective and automated feature extraction, provided that models are trained on large-scale, multisource datasets and externally validated across platforms. Importantly, artificial intelligence should not be regarded solely as a classification tool, but also as a means of identifying hidden sources of variability, optimizing preprocessing strategies, and improving the consistency of spectral interpretation under heterogeneous experimental conditions.

Furthermore, the problem of insufficient clinical validation requires not only larger sample sizes, but also a shift in technological development toward clinically adaptable systems. Future SERS platforms should be designed with real-world clinical workflows in mind, including compatibility with minimally processed specimens, resilience to biological variability, and suitability for prospective multicenter evaluation. More standardized and automated platforms would facilitate validation in large cohorts by reducing methodological inconsistency between sites. In addition, artificial intelligence models trained on diverse clinical datasets may also help improve diagnostic robustness across different patient populations, disease stages, and viral loads, thereby supporting broader clinical applicability.

Finally, the limitations related to cost and instrument accessibility may be mitigated through the development of portable, user-oriented POCT devices. Although current portable Raman systems remain inferior to benchtop instruments in several analytical respects, progress in miniaturized optics, embedded signal-processing algorithms, and integrated cartridge-based assay designs may gradually reduce this gap. Importantly, portability alone is insufficient for practical deployment; the true value of POCT-oriented SERS systems lies in their capacity to integrate simplified operation, rapid turnaround, reduced training requirements, and acceptable analytical performance within a clinically usable format. Therefore, future device development should aim not only at instrument miniaturization, but also at improving automation, workflow integration, and cost-effectiveness to meet the demands of decentralized healthcare settings.

Overall, these emerging technological directions are meaningful only if they are explicitly aligned with the major bottlenecks hindering the translation of SERS into clinical practice. Reproducible substrates can improve measurement stability, integrated platforms can enhance quantitative reliability, artificial intelligence can enhance analytical consistency and model robustness, and portable POCT systems can expand accessibility in clinical practice. Therefore, future progress should emphasize problem-oriented technological integration rather than isolated optimization, so that each innovation contributes directly to overcoming the specific limitations currently restricting the routine clinical use of SERS in respiratory virus detection.

Conclusions

SERS technology, characterized by high sensitivity, molecular fingerprint recognition capability, and multiplex detection, has demonstrated unique advantages in the analysis of viral proteins, nucleic acids, and complex clinical samples. It is particularly well-suited for emerging pathogen identification and multiplex pathogen discrimination. However, limitations such as insufficient signal reproducibility, poor substrate consistency, lack of standardized methodologies, and limited-scale clinical validation continue to hinder its clinical translation.

SERS is more likely to be incorporated into existing respiratory virus diagnostic frameworks as a complementary rather than a replacement technology. For one, during the early stages of emerging infectious disease outbreaks, its rapid detection capability makes it a suitable tool for the frontline screening and the preliminary identification of suspected cases, thereby providing valuable time for public health interventions. For another, in cases where RT-qPCR results are inconclusive, SERS may serve as an auxiliary validation method by providing multidimensional biomarker information, thus improving overall diagnostic accuracy and reliability. Furthermore, for infections caused by multiple respiratory pathogens with highly similar clinical manifestations, the multiplex detection capability of SERS offers the potential to evolve into a platform capable of single-test, multipathogen differentiation, thereby supporting precision diagnostics.

However, the transition from laboratory research to clinical application requires enhancements in detection stability through significant improvements in substrate engineering, sample processing, and platform integration. In addition, extensive validation with real clinical samples and multicenter trials is necessary, along with the establishment of unified and implementable quality control and regulatory standards. It can be anticipated that with the progressive resolution of existing technical challenges and the clarification of its clinical role, SERS will evolve from being a research-oriented tool and become an important complementary technology for respiratory virus detection, occupying a distinct but necessary niche in clinical practice and public health.
