Influence of CeO₂ nanoparticle addition on engine performance, combustion, and emissions of ethiopian podocarpus falcatus biodiesel
Authors: Biru Birhanu, Devendra Deshmukh, Tigabu Haileleul Yemane, Kumlachew Yeneneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The identification of region-specific, non-edible biodiesel feedstocks is critical for expanding sustainable fuel use without competing with food resources. This study reports the first comprehensive evaluation of engine performance, combustion behavior, and emissions of Ethiopian Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel enhanced with low-dose cerium oxide (CeO₂) nanoparticles in a compression ignition engine. Biodiesel–diesel blends containing 10–30% biodiesel was tested with and without 80 ppm CeO₂. Increasing biodiesel content alone reduced brake thermal efficiency from 54.7% for diesel to 49.2% for B30 and decreased brake power from 4.44 to 3.86 kW at full load. The addition of CeO₂ effectively mitigated these penalties, increasing brake thermal efficiency by up to 11.7% and restoring brake power by 3–10% across all blends, while brake specific fuel consumption decreased by 54–56% relative to untreated fuels under identical conditions. Combustion analysis showed higher peak cylinder pressure up to 71.2 bar, enhanced premixed heat-release rate reaching 130.2 J per °CA, advanced combustion phasing by 2–3 °CA, shortened ignition delay by about 2 °CA, and reduced combustion duration by 3–4 °CA. Emission measurements indicated substantial reductions in carbon monoxide by 7.5–16.7%, unburned hydrocarbons by up to 70.2%, and smoke opacity by 9–10%, accompanied by a moderate increase in NOx emissions of approximately 7% at full load due to higher combustion temperatures. Overall, the results demonstrate that low-dose CeO₂ nanoparticles can effectively offset biodiesel-induced performance losses while enhancing combustion and emission characteristics, highlighting Podocarpus falcatus as a promising and sustainable biodiesel feedstock for high-efficiency compression ignition engine operation without hardware modification.

Introduction

Compression ignition (CI) engines continue to play a central role in transportation, agricultural mechanization, and decentralized power generation because of their high thermal efficiency, durability, and reliability across diverse operating conditions1. However, their extensive use contributes substantially to nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), carbon monoxide (CO), unburned hydrocarbons (HC), and particulate matter (PM), posing significant environmental and public-health concerns. These challenges are especially acute in developing regions where aging engine fleets remain predominant and advanced emission-control technologies are limited. As global emission standards tighten, fuel-based mitigation strategies that reduce pollutants without major modifications to existing compression-ignition engines have become increasingly important1,2.

Biodiesel represents one of the most practical and technically mature alternatives to petroleum diesel. Owing to its inherent oxygen content, it typically promotes more complete combustion and reduces CO and HC emissions. Its biodegradability, low toxicity, and favorable safety characteristics further reinforce its environmental advantages3 .Nonetheless, the long-term scalability of biodiesel is constrained by feedstock costs, availability, and competition with food resources issues that have historically hindered edible-oil-based biodiesel programs4. Consequently, research attention has shifted toward non-edible, underutilized, and region-specific oil sources that can be cultivated on marginal land without affecting food security. Extensive studies have examined the performance and emission characteristics of compression-ignition engines fueled with biodiesel from various feedstocks5. These investigations consistently show that biodiesel properties viscosity, cetane number, oxygen content, and fatty-acid composition strongly influence combustion phasing, efficiency, and pollutant formation. Most biodiesel blends exhibit decreased CO, HC, and particulate emissions due to enhanced oxidation, while NOₓ trends often vary with molecular saturation and in-cylinder temperature behavior6,7. Recent studies have shown that non-edible biodiesel blends significantly influence cylinder pressure, heat release rate, and ignition delay relative to diesel fuel8.

Parallel research has explored metallic and metal-oxide nanoparticles including aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), cerium oxide (CeO₂), titanium dioxide (TiO₂), and copper oxide (CuO) as combustion enhancers to alleviate biodiesel-related performance limitations6. For instance, Tuan et al.9 investigated CeO₂ nanoparticles in biodiesel-diesel blends and reported that 30-50 ppm additions significantly improved engine performance while reducing CO, HC, and smoke emissions. Krishnaiah et al.10 found that 50 ppm CuO nanoparticles enhanced mahua biodiesel–diesel blends, improving thermal efficiency, reducing fuel consumption, and lowering CO, HC, and smoke emissions, with only a small increase in NOₓ. Zeleke et al.11 showed that titanium dioxide and ethanol reduced fuel consumption and emissions in castor biodiesel blends. Dessie et al.12 reported that combined Al₂O₃ and CeO₂ nanoparticles in diesel–cottonseed biodiesel reduced CO, CO₂, and HC while increasing exhaust oxygen, indicating more complete combustion. Similar studies by Kaushik et al.13 found that Al₂O₃ nanoparticles in diesel–neem biodiesel improved performance and reduced emissions.Mahendran et al.14, Tamrat et al.15, and Akram et al.16 demonstrated that CeO₂ nanoparticles enhanced brake thermal efficiency, lowered fuel consumption, and reduced emissions across different biodiesel feedstocks. Akansu et al.17 ,Gad et al.18 ,Anish et al.19, Santosh et al.20, and Das et al.21 further confirmed that metallic and metal-oxide nanoparticles improve combustion, engine performance, and emission characteristics in various biodiesel blends.

Cerium oxide nanoparticles have long been investigated as combustion enhancers in biodiesel–diesel blends because of their catalytic oxidation behavior, oxygen storage and release capability, and high thermal stability. Across a wide range of studies, these properties are associated with improved oxidation kinetics, enhanced in-cylinder heat transfer, and suppression of carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons, and soot. As a result, CeO₂ has become one of the most frequently examined metal oxide nano-additives in biodiesel research, particularly for improving combustion efficiency and moderating emission formation. Building on these fundamental attributes, experimental investigations and comprehensive reviews have shown that nano-additives can partially offset the inherent performance penalties of biodiesel–diesel blends. Reported outcomes typically include small gains in brake thermal efficiency, moderate reductions in brake specific fuel consumption, and incremental shifts in combustion phasing. Emission improvements are often evident for CO, HC, and smoke, although they are frequently accompanied by trade-offs such as increased NOₓ formation. Importantly, these benefits are highly sensitive to operating conditions, nanoparticle dosage, and fuel properties, indicating that the effectiveness of CeO₂ is not universal but context dependent3,4,21.

As the literature has expanded, attention has turned to non-edible biodiesel feedstocks, where low-dose nano-additives have been shown to restore engine performance while maintaining acceptable emission balances when fuel stability is properly controlled. Nevertheless, most of these studies emphasize generalized performance and emission trends rather than detailed combustion-resolved behavior20–22. Moreover, the overwhelming focus remains on conventional biodiesel sources, leaving limited understanding of how feedstock-specific molecular composition and physicochemical characteristics influence nano-additive interactions during combustion.

Within this context, Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel represents a distinct and largely unexplored fuel. Derived from a high-oil, non-edible species widely distributed in the Ethiopian highlands, it possesses a unique chemical profile that may interact differently with CeO₂ nanoparticles. This gap in knowledge is particularly relevant given Ethiopia’s dependence on imported diesel and the availability of indigenous oil-bearing plants that grow on non-arable land while contributing to soil stabilization and watershed protection. Despite its potential for region-specific and sustainable biofuel development, the combustion behavior of Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel, especially under CeO₂-assisted conditions, has not been reported.

Against this background, the present study is positioned to address both feedstock-level and performance-level gaps by providing the first engine-based evaluation of CeO₂-enhanced Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel. Using a single-cylinder compression ignition engine, the work systematically examines the influence of 80 ppm CeO₂ nanoparticles on engine performance, combustion characteristics including cylinder pressure, heat release rate, ignition delay, and combustion phasing, and regulated emissions. Through this chronological progression from established nano-additive principles to unexplored feedstock application, the study offers a mechanistic and regionally relevant assessment of CeO₂ effectiveness in biodiesel-fueled engines.

Finally, it is important to clarify the distinct roles of CeO₂ considered in this framework. In biodiesel production, CeO₂ participates as a heterogeneous catalyst that facilitates transesterification through surface redox activity. In contrast, when introduced into the fuel at low concentration, it functions as a combustion additive, promoting oxidation reactions, enhancing oxygen transfer, and supporting micro-explosion phenomena. These mechanisms are independent and operate at different stages, underscoring the need to evaluate CeO₂ specifically under engine combustion conditions, as undertaken in this study.

Materials and methods

Feedstock collection and oil extraction

Podocarpus falcatus seeds were collected from the Sidama Region, Ethiopia. The seeds were manually cleaned to remove adhering dust and foreign materials and subsequently air-dried at ambient conditions for one week to reduce the moisture content to approximately 8 wt%. To achieve uniform dehydration while avoiding thermal degradation of oil constituents, the seeds were further oven-dried at 60 °C for 24 h prior to oil extraction. Oil was extracted from Podocarpus seeds using a hydraulic press. Using whole seeds, including the husk, the oil yield was 0.42 v/w (42%), corresponding to 420 mL/kg or approximately 17.6 L from 42 kg of dry seeds. When only the dehulled kernels were used, the yield increased to 0.51 v/w (51%), corresponding to 510 mL/kg or approximately 21.4 L from the same seed mass. The increase in yield demonstrates that husk removal significantly improves oil recovery, as the husk contributes minimal oil. These values are consistent with reported literature ranges for Podocarpus kernel extraction, with maximum yields approaching 58% under optimal conditions. Reporting both whole-seed and kernel-only yields provides a comprehensive assessment of oil extraction efficiency and practical applicability for hydraulic pressing methods23,24.

Chemicals and catalysts used

Calcium oxide (CaO, purity 99.9%) and cerium oxide (CeO₂) nanoparticles with an average particle size of 30–50 nm were procured from Ultra Nanotech, India. Analytical-grade methanol (≥99.8%) was obtained from a local supplier and used without further purification. CeO₂ was employed in two distinct roles in this study. First, as part of the heterogeneous CaO-CeO₂ catalyst, it facilitated the transesterification of Podocarpus falcatus oil, improving biodiesel yield and catalyst stability. Second, at a low concentration of 80 ppm, CeO₂ was added directly to biodiesel–diesel blends as a combustion additive, enhancing oxidation, reducing brake-specific fuel consumption, and improving overall engine performance. These two functions are separate: the first affects biodiesel production, while the second improves fuel combustion characteristics6,16.

Catalyst characterization

The structural and morphological characteristics of the synthesized catalysts, including pure CaO, pure CeO₂, and CaO-CeO₂ composites with 70/30 and 80/20 wt% ratios, were investigated using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). XRD measurements were performed in a 2θ configuration using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) in continuous scan mode, with a counting time of 0.4 s per step, a step size of 0.02°, and a scanning speed of 3° min⁻1 over a 2θ range of 10°–80°. Preparation of biodiesel and blends

Biodiesel was synthesized from Podocarpus falcatus seed oil via transesterification using a heterogeneous CaO-CeO₂ composite catalyst. Catalysts with compositions of 20 wt% CeO₂-80 wt% CaO and 30 wt% CeO₂-70 wt% CaO were employed during biodiesel production. The reaction was carried out in a 1 L batch reactor, in which preheated oil was reacted with methanol at a molar ratio of 12:1 in the presence of 2 wt% catalyst. The process was conducted at a controlled temperature of 65 °C for 1.52 h under continuous mechanical stirring at 600 rpm.After completion, the reaction mixture was allowed to settle, yielding biodiesel and glycerol phases. The biodiesel layer was subsequently purified by warm distilled-water washing, dried over anhydrous Na₂SO₄, filtered (Whatman No. 541), and stored at ambient temperature for further characterization. From the purified biodiesel, fuel blends containing 10%, 20%, and 30% biodiesel (B10, B20, and B30) were prepared by volumetrically mixing the required biodiesel fraction with commercial diesel until homogeneity was achieved. To improve physicochemical and combustion characteristics, CeO₂ nanoparticles were incorporated into each blend at a dosage of 80 ppm. The nanoparticles were accurately weighed and dispersed using ultrasonication (110 W, 35 kHz, 30 min), a method widely recognized for deagglomerating particle clusters and restoring nanoscale dispersion. The resulting nano-enhanced blends (B10+80 ppm, B20+80 ppm, and B30+80 ppm) exhibited good stability, confirmed through periodic visual inspection of sealed samples stored under controlled conditions, with no observable sedimentation or clustering throughout the test period.

The dispersion stability of CeO₂-treated fuel blends was initially evaluated through visual observation under static storage conditions, where no visible sedimentation or phase separation was detected over the test duration. Although no quantitative stability metric was employed in the present work, previous studies have shown that low-dose CeO₂ nanoparticles with ultrafine particle size and appropriate mixing protocols exhibit adequate short-term stability in hydrocarbon and biodiesel fuels. Based on these literature findings and the absence of visible settling during the experimental period, the prepared nano-fuel blends were considered sufficiently stable for engine testing.

Experimental setup and engine testing procedure

The experimental investigations were conducted on a single-cylinder, four-stroke, water-cooled, CT110 direct-injection compression ignition engine coupled to an eddy-current dynamometer. The engine was operated at a constant speed of 2500 rpm, while the load was varied from 20% to full load to evaluate performance, combustion, and emission characteristics under different operating conditions. The detailed specifications of the engine and measuring instruments are provided in Table 3. Fuel consumption was measured using a calibrated burette and electronic timer, while brake torque and brake power were obtained directly from the dynamometer system. Intake air flow was monitored using a standard air box fitted with an orifice meter21. Cooling water flow rates and exhaust gas temperatures were continuously monitored to ensure stable operating conditions throughout the experiments. In-cylinder pressure data were acquired using a piezoelectric pressure transducer mounted flush with the cylinder head and synchronized with a crank angle encoder having a resolution of 1°CA. The pressure signals were recorded over 100 consecutive engine cycles and ensemble-averaged to minimize cyclic variability. Apparent heat release rate, ignition delay, combustion duration, and CA50 were calculated from the averaged pressure–crank angle data using a first-law-based combustion analysis22. Exhaust emissions, including carbon monoxide (CO), unburned hydrocarbons (HC), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), and carbon dioxide (CO₂), were measured using a calibrated Kane Auto plus five exhaust gas analyzer, while smoke opacity was determined using an AVL smoke meter. Prior to each test, the analyzers were zeroed and spanned according to the manufacturer’s specifications to ensure measurement accuracy. All tests were initially performed using neat diesel fuel to establish baseline data. Subsequently, biodiesel blends (B10, B20, and B30), both with and without 80 ppm CeO₂ nanoparticle addition, were evaluated under identical operating conditions. The CeO₂ concentration of 80 ppm was selected based on previous experimental evidence identifying the 75-100 ppm range as optimal for diesel-biodiesel compression ignition engine. Lower concentrations (<50 ppm) generally yield insufficient catalytic activity, whereas higher concentrations may cause nanoparticle agglomeration, compromised fuel atomization, and potential NOₓ escalation due to excess oxygen availability. Thus, 80 ppm represents a balanced and scientifically justified dosage for achieving performance gains without inducing undesirable emission penalties13,26.Before each fuel change, the fuel line was flushed, and the engine was allowed to stabilize to ensure repeatability and eliminate fuel cross-contamination. Each experimental point was repeated three times, and the average values were reported to ensure data reliability. Brake power (BP)

Brake power (BP) increased steadily with engine load for all fuels, with neat diesel (B0) consistently producing the highest output (Fig.7c) for pure blends. The variation of brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) with engine load is presented for pure blends (Fig.7e) and for blends with 80 ppm CeO₂ nanoparticles (Fig.7f). Overall, the addition of CeO₂ leads to a consistent reduction in BSFC, highlighting its potential as an effective combustion catalyst for biodiesel–diesel blends13,35.

Comparative study of podocarpus falcatus biodiesel with other biodiesel feedstocks

While many biodiesel feedstocks (Jatropha, Pongamia, Soybean, WCO, Palm) show trade-offs reducing CO, HC, and smoke but slightly compromising performance or increasing BSFC Podocarpus biodiesel with CeO₂ nanoparticles restores engine performance almost to diesel levels while simultaneously reducing CO, UHC, and smoke significantly. CeO₂ addition provides a synergistic advantage, compensating for the typical performance penalties of biodiesel blends observed in other feedstocks. Podocarpus blends also demonstrate better BTE recovery, lower BSFC, and moderated NOx increase compared to most other feedstocks, making it more balanced across both performance and emissions14,45,47.

In the comparative assessment, qualitative descriptors such as high, moderate, or balanced performance are applied based on the combined behavior of brake thermal efficiency, brake specific fuel consumption, emission reduction levels, and combustion stability relative to baseline fuels. These terms are used to represent overall performance balance rather than superiority in any single parameter.

Previous studies on cerium oxide nanoparticles as combustion enhancers in biodiesel–diesel blends generally report modest benefits that are highly dependent on feedstock type and operating conditions as shown in Table 6. Typical findings include brake thermal efficiency gains of about 2–6%, brake specific fuel consumption reductions of 5–15%, peak cylinder pressure increases below 5%, and reductions in unburned hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide that rarely exceed 40–50%, often with limited or inconsistent changes in combustion phasing5,8,9. Against this established background, the present results show markedly stronger effects when low-dose CeO₂ is combined with Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel. At full load, brake thermal efficiency increases by up to 11.7%, brake power is recovered by 3–10% across blends, and brake specific fuel consumption decreases by more than 54% compared with untreated fuels. Uncertainty results

To assess the reliability of the experimental results, the uncertainties associated with the measured and derived parameters were quantified. For engine performance, the uncertainties in brake power, brake thermal efficiency, and brake-specific fuel consumption were ±1.2%, ±2.4%, and ±2.0%, respectively30. These values are significantly lower than the variations observed among different biodiesel blends and CeO₂ additive conditions. For combustion characteristics, the uncertainties in peak cylinder pressure and heat release rate were estimated to be ±1.9% and ±2.2%, respectively. The changes in combustion behavior with increasing biodiesel content and nanoparticle addition exceeded these uncertainty limits. Regarding emissions, the maximum uncertainties were ±3.3% for CO, ±3.0% for HC, ±2.8% for NOₓ, and ±2.5% for smoke opacity17. The observed reductions in CO, HC, and smoke emissions, as well as the variations in NOₓ emissions, were substantially greater than the corresponding uncertainty margins. Overall, since the observed differences in performance, combustion, and emission characteristics consistently exceed the associated uncertainties, the experimental trends and comparative analyses presented in this study are considered reliable and statistically meaningful17,30,38.

While individual measurement uncertainties arise from pressure transducers, crank angle resolution, and fuel flow measurements, their influence on derived combustion parameters such as CA50, premixed heat release fraction, and combustion duration is primarily associated with cycle-to-cycle pressure variation and numerical differentiation during heat release analysis. Because identical instrumentation, data acquisition settings, and analysis procedures were applied to both treated and untreated fuel blends, the relative comparison of combustion parameters remains robust despite absolute uncertainty propagation. Consequently, the observed shifts in combustion phasing and heat release characteristics can be attributed with confidence to CeO₂ addition rather than experimental variability.

Conclusions and future perspective

The experimental investigation confirms that Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel blends (B10–B30) possess physicochemical properties compatible with compression ignition engines, exhibiting systematic trends in viscosity, density, cetane number, and flash point that support stable engine operation. FTIR analysis verified successful conversion of PF oil to methyl ester, while catalyst characterization showed that the CaO-CeO₂ heterogeneous catalyst, particularly with higher CaO loading, exhibits favorable crystallinity and surface characteristics for enhanced catalytic activity. Engine tests revealed that increasing biodiesel fraction led to moderate reductions in brake power and brake thermal efficiency and slightly higher brake-specific fuel consumption due to lower heating value and higher viscosity. The addition of 80 ppm CeO₂ nanoparticles effectively mitigated these penalties, improving thermal efficiency, restoring brake power, and reducing fuel consumption, particularly at higher loads. Combustion analysis indicated that biodiesel blends showed lower peak cylinder pressure and slightly delayed combustion phasing; however, CeO₂ addition advanced ignition, increased peak pressure and heat release rate, shortened combustion duration, and shifted CA50 closer to top dead center. Emission results demonstrated significant reductions in CO, unburned hydrocarbons, and smoke opacity, further enhanced by CeO₂, while NOₓ increased moderately but remained within manageable limits.

Overall, CeO₂-enriched Podocarpus falcatus biodiesel blends present a viable and sustainable alternative fuel for compression ignition engines without major hardware modifications. The uncertainty analysis confirms that the observed trends exceed experimental error limits, validating result reliability. Future research should focus on spray characterization and mixture formation, long-term engine durability and deposit formation, and assessment of potential environmental and health impacts of nanoparticle use. Integration of optimized nano-additive dosing with appropriate after-treatment strategies offers a practical pathway toward safe and sustainable deployment.
