Effects of dietary supplementation with pumpkin powder on egg quality and antioxidant function in laying hens
Authors: Mengxian Chen, Zheng Xu, Junxing Pan, Shiting Ju, Yu Ding, Xinyue Zhang, Yang Song, Hainan Lan, Suo Li, Min Wu, Xin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractβ-carotene, a carotenoid with strong antioxidant and immunity-enhancing properties, is a safe and widely available precursor of vitamin A in nature. Pumpkin powder, as a familiar agricultural product, is rich in vitamins, amino acids, and minerals, which also contain β-carotene. Currently, there remain few published reports on the effects of pumpkin powder on antioxidant function, reproductive performance, and egg quality in laying hens, representing a significant gap in existing research. 270 laying hens were divided into three groups; the CON group was fed a basal diet, the BCA group was fed the basal diet supplemented with 120 mg/kg β-carotene, and the PPD group was fed a diet containing pumpkin powder to provide an equivalent dose of β-carotene. On 35 days, the β-carotene content in the egg yolk was significantly increased in the BCA and PPD groups compared to the CON group (p<0.01). On 49 days, the UFA, vitamin A, and β-carotene content in the BCA and PPD groups were significantly higher than in the CON group (p<0.01). In the BCA and PPD groups, levels of SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and T-AOC markedly increased in yolk and serum. The relative expression of Keap1 and Nrf2 levels in the BCA and PPD groups was remarkably higher compared to the CON group (p<0.01). The relative expression levels of the BMP15, and FOXL2 genes in the BCA and PPD groups of laying hens were significantly higher than in the CON group (p<0.05). The relative abundance of the genera Megamonas and Eubacterium in the fecal microbiota was significantly higher in the PPD group than in the CON group (p < 0.05). In summary, dietary supplementation with pumpkin powder as a source of β-carotene in laying hens effectively enhanced egg production and quality, improved systemic antioxidant status and gut health, and reduced ovarian inflammation.Keywords: Antioxidant function, Egg quality, Gut microbiota, Laying hens, Pumpkin powder

Introduction

β-carotene, a carotenoid with strong antioxidant and immunity-enhancing properties, is a safe and widely available precursor of vitamin A in nature. Vitamin A, a fat-soluble vitamin essential for animals, cannot be synthesized by the body and encompasses a group of retinol derivatives, including retinol, retinaldehyde, and retinoic acid. Beyond its roles in vision, immune function, epithelial tissue maintenance, bone development, and reproduction, vitamin A also regulates the expression of genes and signaling pathways associated with lipid metabolism in animals (Barbour et al., 2020; Kuraz Abebe et al., 2024). In recent years, studies have demonstrated that β-carotene, as a feed additive, can enhance the reproductive function of livestock and poultry breeders. As the most active precursor of vitamin A, β-carotene is converted into vitamin A through digestion and absorption in the animal body. This conversion plays a crucial role in stabilizing follicular development and early embryonic development by regulating hormones such as follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, estrogen, and progesterone (Ma et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2019). In addition, β-carotene supplementation is effective in boosting immunity. It protects the body from pathogenic bacteria by transmitting inflammatory signals to immune cells, activating Th1 and Th2-like cytokines through the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway or the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway. This process induces specific immune cells, such as lymphocytes and macrophages, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects (Song et al., 2024). It demonstrated that β-carotene intervention mitigates immune injury by increasing the organ weight of immune-injured chickens, repairing the intestinal mucosal barrier, reducing apoptosis and mRNA expression of oxidative stress-related genes, and lowering the levels of pro-inflammatory factors in the blood (Hui et al., 2020). Based on previous laboratory research, adding β-carotene to the diet of laying hens at 120 mg/kg can effectively improve their growth performance and enhance immunity. However, due to the high market price of β-carotene, its use in production would significantly increase feed costs. Therefore, to better utilize the role of β-carotene in promoting reproductive performance and immunity, it is necessary to explore β-carotene-rich plants or vegetables as alternatives to pure β-carotene in the diet.

Pumpkin is a trailing herb with very strong vitality; it is very adaptable to the environment, it is widely grown in China and has been cultivated for more than 500 years, it has always been considered a widely cultivated, low-cost vegetable, and most of it can be used directly. Pumpkin contains a large number of vitamins, including VB, VE, VC, and carotene. The carotenoid content of pumpkin is equivalent to the VC content of tomato and higher than that of cucumber, and some carotenoids have strong antioxidant properties, which can enhance the body's immunity (Mounes et al., 2024; Santos et al., 2022). Pumpkin is relatively rich in macronutrients. The potassium content is much higher than other common vegetables, such as squash and cabbage, and some elements such as manganese and zinc are also higher than other melon vegetables (Lestari et al., 2019; Šamec et al., 2022). Pumpkin powder, as a familiar agricultural product, is rich in vitamins, amino acids, and minerals, which also contain β-carotene. The use of pumpkin flour as a source of β-carotene is low cost and more compatible with the requirements of poultry production.

In recent years, carotenoids have been widely studied for their multifunctional applications in the development of functional foods. β-carotene, one of the major carotenoids in pumpkin, has attracted significant attention (Ninčević Grassino et al., 2023). Pumpkin exhibits various health benefits, including antidiabetic, antihypertensive, antitumor, immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, antihypercholesterolemic, intestinal antiparasitic, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties. Additionally, pumpkin products have been shown to possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and immune-enhancing effects (Rolnik and Olas, 2020; Salehi et al., 2019). In a study by Song et al. (2013) antioxidants in the serum of piglets in the treated group were significantly increased, and the expression of inflammatory factors in the liver was inhibited, demonstrating a mitigating effect on inflammation. Abdelnour et al. (2023) found that supplementing pumpkin seed oil into the basal diet effectively enhanced the antioxidant capacity and immune system of rabbits. Furthermore, the addition of pumpkin powder to the diet reduced total cholesterol and triglyceride levels in rabbit serum, increased SOD and GSH-Px activities in mouse serum, and decreased MDA levels (Hu et al., 2024; Ti et al., 2022). The above results suggest that pumpkin and its derivatives have the potential to improve antioxidant and immune function in various animal species, including poultry and swine.

Currently, there remain few published reports on the effects of pumpkin powder on antioxidant function, reproductive performance, and egg quality of laying hens, representing a significant gap in existing research. Therefore, this study holds great value in addressing this research gap by investigating whether pumpkin powder can enhance the antioxidant function in laying hens, improve reproduction performance, and elevate egg quality, thereby providing valuable insights for practical production.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. This animal experimental protocol was implemented under the supervision of the Chinese Guidelines for Animal Welfare and Experimental Protocol and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee, Jilin Agriculture University (NO. 2017050001). The stocking densities for each housing system were established in accordance with standard industry and regulatory guidelines. Specifically, the conventional cage system maintained a density of approximately 9 birds/m², consistent with common commercial practices for intensive quail production. These free-range densities were designed to comply with, or exceed, established organic and free-range poultry welfare standards, including USDA Organic regulations and EU Council Regulation 889/2008.

Experiment preparation

Food-grade pumpkin powder and carotene powder were purchased from Henan Feishi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, and Shanxi Mixianer Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China, respectively. The β-carotene content of the two powders was tested by Pony Testing Group, China. Briefly, the samples were saponified to release carotenoids into their free form, extracted with petroleum ether and dichloromethane to a fixed volume, and then separated by reversed-phase chromatography and quantified using the external standard method.

Animal management

Two hundred and seventy healthy laying hens (Jinghong NO. 1) were selected and randomly divided into three treatment groups of 90 hens each (with three replicates of 30 hens per group). There was no significant difference in the initial body weights of the hens among the treatment groups. The treatment groups were designed as follows: (i) Control group (CON): Laying hens were fed a basal diet. (ii) β-carotene treatment group (BCA): Laying hens were fed a basal diet supplemented with 120 mg/kg β-carotene. (iii) Pumpkin powder group (PPD): Laying hens were fed a pumpkin powder diet containing an equivalent amount of β-carotene. The composition and nutrition levels of the basal diets are shown in Table 1. The daily feed intake and egg production of laying hens in each group were recorded to calculate the daily feed intake and egg production. Additionally, the egg quality parameters (n = 5 for each group, including egg weight, yolk weight, egg white weight, yolk score, eggshell weight, eggshell thickness, egg white height, and Haugh units) were measured weekly for each treatment group. The experiment lasted for 49 days. On day 28 of the experiment, blood was collected from hens via venipuncture, and fecal samples were obtained for subsequent analysis (n = 10). At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected from the laying hens, and the weights of the liver, ovary, and intestines were recorded. The MA2 protein height analyzer was purchased from Beijing Heng Odd Instrument Co., Ltd., China. The Roche colorimetric fan was purchased from Nanjing Mingao Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd., China. The electronic scale with thousandth place precision was purchased from Mettler-Toledo International Inc.

Growth performance and organ index data collection

Feed intake (ADFI) and egg production (ADE) were recorded daily for each group of laying hens. The organ indices were calculated using the formula: (organ weight / body weight of the hen) × 100%.

Quantification of lipid metabolism and antioxidant compounds in egg yolk and serum

Blood was collected (n = 10 for each group) and left at 4°C for 30 min before serum was obtained by centrifugation (3500 g, 15 min). The assay kits for Malondialdehyde (MDA), Catalase (CAT), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Total Antioxidant Capacity (T-AOC), and Glutathione Peroxidase (GSH-Px) were purchased from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute Co., Ltd., China. The procedures for all the aforementioned assay kits were performed in strict adherence to the manufacturer's guidelines. For the determination of reducing power and DPPH content in egg yolk, the physiological saline extraction method (Klompong et al., 2008) and the ethanol extraction method (Zhang et al., 2011) were employed, respectively. The detection of β-carotene, vitamin A, and unsaturated fatty acids (UFA) in eggs was conducted by the listed company Huace Testing, China. As follows: For vitamin A, the sample was extracted, saponified (if necessary), and purified, followed by separation using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with detection by an ultraviolet (UV) detector and quantification via the external standard method. For β-carotene, the sample was extracted using organic solvents and purified, then separated by fluorescence high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with detection by an ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) detector and quantification using the external standard method. For polyunsaturated fatty acids, the sample was extracted and methylated to form fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs), which were then separated by gas chromatography (GC) with detection by a flame ionization detector (FID) and quantification via the external standard method.

Histological observation of the intestine and liver

The ileum and liver (n = 10 for each group) of laying hens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 5 µm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Soleberg Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Photomicrographs of ileal sections were captured using an Olympus light microscope, and villus height and crypt depth were measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, CA, U.S.A.). Intestinal goblet cells were observed using the PAS staining method, and the PAS reagent was purchased from Leagene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China.

RNA extraction and real-time fluorescence quantitative analysis

RNA was extracted from the ileum and liver (n = 10 for each group). The RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and reverse-transcribed to cDNA. (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Then, qPCR analyses were carried out on a CFX RealiTime PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). For intestinal analysis, GAPDH was set as the housekeeping gene, and MUC2, ZO1, Occludin, TLR-4, IFN-γ, and Foxp3 were the target genes. For heptical analysis, β-actin was set as the housekeeping gene, and Keap1, Nrf2, HO1, and NQO1 were the target genes. For ovarian analysis, β-actin was set as the housekeeping gene, and BMP15, FOXL2, FSHR, and GDF9 were the target genes. The relative expression levels of the target genes in the ileum and liver were calculated using 2−△△Ct, with three replicates per chicken. The primers were designed and synthesized by Shenggong Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, and the sequences are shown as Table 2.

Immunohistochemical examination of the intestine and liver

The intestines and livers (n = 10 for each group) of laying hens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 3 µm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Soleberg Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Photomicrographs of livers sections were captured using an Olympus light microscope. The liver and ileum sections on slides were treated with xylene, different concentration gradients of alcohol, and washed in PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed using to room temperature, the slides were incubated with the peroxidase blocking solution (Fuzhou Meixin Biotechnology Development, Fujian, China) at 37°C in the goat serum for 20 min. Hepatic and ileal slides were incubated overnight at 4°C with the mouse anti-chicken proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA, 1:100, Monoclonal, Southern Biotech, Cat. NO. 8330-01, U.S.A.) antibody and immunoglobulin A (IgA, 1:1000, Monoclonal, Abcam, Cat. NO. ab157691, U.K.)antibody, respectively. The slides treated with PBS instead of the primary antibody served as the negative control. After being exposed to biotinylated secondary antibody goat anti-mouse IgG for 20 min at 37°C, slices were incubated with streptavidin for 10 min at 37°C. Under dark conditions, the slices were immersed in diaminobenzidine hydrochloride to develop the immunoreaction. This reaction was quenched by immersion in distilled water after the brown staining developed. Slices were counter-stained with H&E, followed by hydrochloric acid alcohol color separation. They were finally washed with tap water, dehydrated in ethanol, and cleared using xylene. Sections on the slide were viewed under the light microscope, and any IgG positive areas in the five different microscope fields for each tissue were measured with Image J and the average was calculated.

Western blot analysis

First, about 50 mg of laying hen liver tissue samples were weighed, and 1 ml of lysis buffer of radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, ST505, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in a ratio of 100:1 was added for total protein extraction. Then, the total protein concentration was determined using BCA protein concentration (P0009, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein samples (15 µg per lane) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) membrane. The transferred PVDF membrane was incubated overnight with the primary antibody at 4 °C. Primary antibodies and dilutions were as follows: β-actin (1:3000, GB11001-100) from Servicebio (Wuhan, China); NF-κB (1:3000, #8242) and MAPK (1:3000, #8690) from Cell Signaling Technology (Beijing, China). After washing with TBST, the membrane was placed in the corresponding secondary antibody and incubated at room temperature for 1 h, including goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:5000, A0208) from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). After washing with TBST, the Millipore High-Sig ECL Western Blotting Substrate was used as a chemiluminescence substrate. Immune-reactive protein bands were captured with a gel imager system (Bio-Rad, U.S.A.).

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing

Total microbial DNA was extracted from fecal contents. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.), and integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified via PCR with barcoded fusion primers: 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Each 25 µL PCR reaction contained 5 µL of Q5 High-Fidelity GC Buffer (5×). Amplicons were purified using Agencourt AMPure Beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, U.S.A.), quantified with the PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, U.S.A.), and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, U.S.A.) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. Raw sequences were demultiplexed, and primers were trimmed using the demux plugin. Quality control, including error correction and chimera removal, was performed with the DADA2 plugin. Non-singleton amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were aligned with MAFFT, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree2. Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was carried out with the feature-classifier plugin against the Greengenes OTU reference database.

Molecular docking

β-carotene files and the PDB format of proteins were downloaded from the website of PubChem and the PDB database. The crystal structure of the kelch domain of keap1 (PDB ID: 1U6D) pdb was searched, and it was converted into a pdbqt. The two-dimensional (2D) structural formula of β-carotene was converted to a three-dimensional (3D) structure formula using ChemBio3Dltura 14.0 software. PyMOL 2.5 was used to perform the removal of water molecules. AutoDock Vina was used for hydrogenation and charging and calculation of the binding affinity. Taking the target structure as the grid center, the center coordinates and box size parameters were adjusted to ensure that the gris box completely covered the results presented in PyMOL 2.5.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed for significance using SPSS, and mean ± standard error was plotted using Prism software. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and P-values less than 0.01 were considered highly statistically significant. * In the figure, lower case letters in the table indicate a p-value less than 0.05, and ** in the figure and uppercase letters indicate a p-value less than 0.01.

Effects of pumpkin powder on egg quality

As shown in Fig. 1, egg weight, yolk weight, protein weight, Haugh unit, and egg yolk score were presented. Additionally, the protein weight in the BCA group was significantly higher than that in the CON group on days 14 and 28 (p < 0.05), and the PPD group showed a significant increase in protein weight compared to the CON group on day 42 (p < 0.05). The Haugh unit in the BCA group was significantly higher than that in the CON group on days 7, 14, and 42 (p < 0.05). On day 49, both the BCA and PPD groups exhibited significantly higher Haugh units compared to the CON group (p < 0.05). The yolk color scores in the BCA and PPD groups were significantly higher than those in the CON group on days 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 (p < 0.01). Effects of pumpkin powder on ileum in laying hens

As shown in Fig. 4A and 4B, figures of ileal goblet cells stained in the three groups revealed that the number of goblet cells in the BCA group was significantly higher compared to the CON group (p < 0.05). Discussion

β-carotene, a carotenoid with antioxidant and immunity-enhancing properties, significantly increases production costs when used in feed. Since pumpkin is known to contain β-carotene, this study aimed to investigate the effects of replacing β-carotene with pumpkin powder on egg quality, antioxidant function, and reproductive performance in laying hens. We monitored feed intake and egg production throughout the entire trial period. Surprisingly, from day 21 onward, both the BCA and PPD groups exhibited significantly higher ADFI and AE compared to the CON group, and this trend persisted until the end of the experiment. Furthermore, the BCA group showed superior performance relative to the PPD group. Previous studies have indicated that appropriate intake of β-carotene promotes growth and development in newly hatched chicks, enhancing the growth rates of immune organs, increasing body weight, and improving tibia length (Riley et al., 2021). This observation is consistent with our primary conclusion, despite the absence of body weight measurements in the laying hens used in this study. Due to variations in egg production, we conducted further examinations of the ovaries in laying hens to evaluate their reproductive performance and antioxidant capacity. Interestingly, the number of secondary follicles in ovarian sections from the BCA and PPD groups was significantly higher than in the CON group. Beyond this fundamental finding, we further analyzed ovarian genes associated with it and found that relative expression levels of BMP15, FOXL2, and GDF9 were significantly elevated in the BCA and PPD groups compared to the CON group. The findings of Lopez-Flores et al. (2020) indicated that dietary supplementation with 50 mg of β-carotene per hen significantly increased luteinizing hormone (LH) secretion and improved ovulation rate. Comparative staining images of ovarian tissue from Miao et al. (2023) revealed that the lowest follicular atresia rate was observed when β-carotene supplementation exceeded 60 mg/kg. By promoting ovulation, β-carotene consequently enhances egg production in laying hens. This finding is consistent with our experimental results. Through reviewing extensive literature, we found that laying hens are subjected to various stressors (especially oxidative stress) throughout their laying cycle, which can lead to inflammation and a decline in egg production (Abbas et al., 2020; Broxmeyer et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021). Therefore, ELISA was used to measure antioxidant levels in ovarian tissue proteins. Results showed that CAT content, a key antioxidant marker, was significantly higher in the BCA and PPD groups than in the CON group. In addition, POD content was elevated, although this increase was not statistically significant. Furthermore, we further examined the expression of proteins involved in classical inflammatory pathways in ovarian tissue. The results showed that MAPK and NF-κB protein levels were significantly lower in the BCA and PPD groups than in the CON group. Yu et al. (2019) found that ROS induced oxidative stress in ovarian development, severely impairing oocyte maturation. Supplementation of β-carotene reduced ROS formation, mitigated cytopathological damage, and improved ovarian function. Li et al. (2021) found that β-carotene can prevent intestinal inflammation caused by weaning in piglets. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies. These findings further suggest that dietary supplementation with pumpkin powder may effectively reduce ovarian inflammation by increasing antioxidant levels in laying hens.

In light of these findings, promote further investigation into the mechanism by which dietary β-carotene supplementation enhances antioxidant levels in ovarian proteins. In light of the significant differences in liver index among the three groups and considering that β-carotene is primarily stored in the liver after intake (Raghuvanshi et al., 2015). So, we further investigated the liver of laying hens. Following intestinal absorption and circulatory transport, β-carotene enters the bloodstream. Accordingly, we quantified circulating β-carotene levels and found that they were significantly increased in the BCA and PPD groups relative to the CON group, confirming our initial hypothesis. Subsequently, we performed ELISA assays on liver tissue proteins and found that CAT and POD levels in the BCA and PPD groups exhibited significant changes relative to the CON group. Furthermore, the relative expression of antioxidant genes Keap1, Nrf2, and NQO1 also showed significant alterations. Sun et al. (2022) reported that lycopene supplementation in rats effectively increased Keap1 and Nrf2 protein expression. Additionally, lycopene binds to the Keap1 protein, thereby effectively mitigating oxidative stress. Given the structure similarities between β-carotene and lycopene as members of the carotenoid family. In addition, the Kelch domain of Keap1 binds to the Neh2 domain of Nrf2. Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1 into the nucleus and then binds to AREs to transcriptionally activate phase II enzymes and antioxidative stress genes. Concurrently, serum antioxidant levels in laying hens from the BCA and PPD groups indicated that pumpkin powder supplementation effectively enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity. Therefore, we believe that dietary supplementation with pumpkin powder enhances systemic antioxidant capacity by regulating Keap1 protein expression in the liver.

In our team’s previous research, we explored the phenomenon of maternal effects and elucidated the mechanism of maternal enhancement (Gong et al., 2023). Therefore, we further investigated whether enhancing maternal antioxidant capacity leads to improved antioxidant capacity in offspring. Due to the fact that we use laying hens rather than breeding hens, we conducted tests on the antioxidant content in the eggs. The results indicated that the antioxidant content in eggs from both treatment groups was significantly higher than that in the CON group. These findings provide preliminary support for our hypothesis, which will be further validated in subsequent experiments. As a fat-soluble compound, β-carotene is known to be primarily absorbed in the small intestine (Cervantes-Paz et al., 2017; Rodriguez-Concepcion et al., 2018). Based on this, conducted further observations on intestinal tissues. Observation of ileal tissue sections significantly increased villus height in both the BCA and PPD groups, with the number of goblet cells also significantly elevated in the BCA group. Gong et al. (2020) indicated that dietary supplementation with 60 mg/kg β-carotene in laying hens significantly increased the intestinal length and jejunal villus height of their offspring chicks. Our results are similar. Reports indicated that β-carotene supplementation can effectively enhance gut health and immune function. We performed fluorescent staining on the ileum and observed a significant increase in sIgA+ cell counts in both treatment groups. Moreover, the ileal relative expression level of barrier genes was significantly increase in the BCA group compared to the CON group, but this increased did not reach statistical significance in the PPD group. Nishida et al. (2014) found that adding β-carotene increased IgA concentrations in the jejunum and ileum of mice. Honarbakhsh et al. (2022) demonstrated that β-carotene supplementation in vitamin A deficient mice improved intestinal barrier integrity and significantly upregulated the relative expression of Muc2. These findings are similar to us. Furthermore, the fecal microbiota analysis further revealed a reduced Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio upon beta-carotene supplementation. Consistently, an elevated F/B ratio is widely recognized as a microbial signature associated with pro-inflammatory states in various pathological conditions (An et al., 2023; Magne et al., 2020). In the PPD group, we observed a significant increase in the abundance of Megamonas and Eubacterium. This finding is noteworthy as both genera are reported in the literature to be key producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Mukherjee et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023). SCFAs in the gut are established as key regulators of multiple physiological functions, including the maintenance of electrolyte balance, protection of the intestinal mucosa, promotion of nutrient absorption, suppression of intestinal inflammation, and modulation of immune responses (Akhtar et al., 2022; Ziętek et al., 2021). Building on this evidence, we conclude that pumpkin powder supplementation enhances systemic antioxidant capacity and promotes intestinal health. Although the results of this trial were obtained in Jinghong No. 1 laying hens, the effects of pumpkin powder on egg production, antioxidant status, intestinal health, and ovarian inflammation are likely to apply to other laying hen breeds as well. This inference is supported by breeds (e.g., Hy-Line Brown), as well as the conservation of vitamin A metabolism and immune function across poultry.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with pumpkin powder as a source of β-carotene in laying hens effectively enhanced egg production and quality, improved systemic antioxidant status and gut health, and reduced ovarian inflammation. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, multi-omics integrated analyses will be conducted in subsequent studies.
