Application and prospects of nanomaterials in osteoporosis treatment
Authors: Feng Liang, Tianlong Jiang, Li Li, Yue Zhang, Baogang Wu, Fangyuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is characterized by uncoupled bone remodeling, microarchitectural deterioration, and impaired fracture or defect healing, resulting in increased fragility and a high lifetime fracture burden. Current pharmacotherapies can reduce fracture risk, yet their impact is constrained by systemic exposure, adherence limitations, and the lack of direct mechanical restoration in localized defects. Nanomaterial-enabled biomaterials provide a means to localize therapy, engineer the osteoporotic microenvironment, and deliver defect-specific structural support. Unlike platform- or strategy-specific summaries, this review establishes a pathology-to-design framework that integrates drug-delivery nanosystems (lipid, polymeric, and inorganic carriers with bone- or osteoclast-targeting chemistries) with implantable or injectable nanocomposites (metal, ceramic, polymer, and composite matrices). Synthesis and processing parameters (composition, porosity, and micro/nanostructure) and surface chemistry (ion doping, bioactive coatings, and affinity motifs) are explicitly linked to targeted applications, including remodeling-preserving modulation of osteoclast-osteoblast coupling, osteoimmune regulation, angiogenesis support, and spatiotemporally programmed release. Advanced multifunctional approaches, such as staged antiresorptive-to-anabolic delivery, micro/nanotopography-guided interface engineering, and pH/enzyme/thermal/electroactive responsive systems, are critically evaluated in terms of performance trade-offs and failure modes. A translation-oriented evaluation and clinical-readiness roadmap is presented, emphasizing harmonized efficacy endpoints, chemistry, manufacturing and controls (CMC) and quality-by-design (QbD) for scalability, regulatory tractability for combination products, and long-term safety of nano-additives and degradation products.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Biomaterials, Bone microenvironment, Regenerative guidance, Personalized therapy

Introduction

Osteoporosis Overview

Osteoporosis has become one of the diseases with significant public health importance globally, posing a serious threat to the quality of life of patients (Kong et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Johnston and Dagar, 2020). According to the International Osteoporosis Foundation, more than 200 million people worldwide are affected by osteoporosis, and one in three women and one in five men over the age of 50 will experience an osteoporotic fracture in their lifetime (Morin et al., 2025). Each year, approximately 8.9 million osteoporotic fractures occur globally—equivalent to one fracture every 3 s. The global economic burden is substantial, with Europe, the United States, and China collectively spending more than USD 200–250 billion annually on osteoporosis-related healthcare costs, hospitalizations, long-term care, and productivity loss. By 2030–2050, aging demographics are projected to increase fracture incidence by 25–50% in most regions, escalating both medical expenditures and societal burden. As the global population ages at an accelerating rate, its incidence shows an annual increasing trend, imposing a heavy burden on society and families (Arceo-Mendoza and Camacho, 2021).

Pathological Characteristics (Bone loss, Microstructure Degradation)

Osteoporosis is essentially a metabolic bone disease, with the core pathological characteristics of significant bone loss and progressive degradation of bone tissue microstructure (Walker and Shane, 2023; Rodriguez and Gitajn, 2011; Chin et al., 2022). In normal bone tissue, there is a delicate dynamic balance between bone resorption and bone formation to maintain the strength and stability of the skeleton (Menopause (New York, N.Y.)., 2021). However, in osteoporosis patients, this balance is disrupted, with osteoclast-mediated bone resorption predominating and relatively insufficient osteoblast-mediated bone formation (Chin et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2020; Ginaldi and De Martinis, 2014) (Fig. 1). The reduction in bone mass means a decrease in bone tissue content per unit volume and reduced bone density, making it difficult for bones to withstand daily physiological loads. Meanwhile, the microstructure of bone tissue undergoes a series of degenerative changes. Studies have shown that with increasing age or disease progression, both trabecular bone volume (BV/TV) and trabecular bone plate connection (TBPf) decrease significantly. These changes are mainly due to increased depth of bone resorption cavities, leading to local perforation of bone plates. Subsequently, the perforations gradually enlarge, and the bone plates are transformed into bone rods (Amling et al., 1996). Trabecular bones become sparse, thin, and even broken, and the originally tightly connected trabecular network structure is destroyed, resulting in a significant decline in the mechanical properties of the skeleton (Rodriguez and Gitajn, 2011; Ding, 2010; Chen and Kubo, 2014). In osteoporosis, enhanced bone resorption and reduced bone formation lead to decreased trabecular number and increased separation between trabeculae (Molino et al., 2020; Parfitt et al., 1983). Additionally, osteoporosis is associated with alterations in bone collagen structure. Studies have shown that in osteoporosis patients, the content of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) increases. These substances reduce the α-helix structure of bone collagen and increase its random coils, thereby decreasing the mechanical properties of bone. The porosity of cortical bone also increases significantly, making the originally dense and strong cortical bone fragile and vulnerable (Rodriguez and Gitajn, 2011; Chen and Kubo, 2014). These microstructural changes greatly weaken the overall strength and toughness of the skeleton, creating a potential risk for fractures. The primary cause of hormone-related osteoporosis in postmenopausal women is estrogen deficiency due to natural aging, whereas glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis is the most prevalent form of drug-induced osteoporosis (Wang et al., 2023a).

Clinical challenges (Fracture Risk, Healing Impairment)

The clinical consequences of osteoporosis are mainly reflected in increased fracture risk and impaired fracture healing. Fractures are the most severe and common complications of osteoporosis, causing great suffering and functional impairment to patients. Common fracture sites include the vertebrae, hip, wrist, and proximal humerus (Harvey et al., 2010; Lane, 2006; Wells, 2024). Vertebral fractures can lead to reduced height, kyphosis, chronic lower back pain, and in severe cases, may compress the spinal cord and nerves, causing paralysis and other serious consequences (Nuti et al., 2019; Fiani et al., 2021; Benditz and Jerosch, 2023). Hip fractures are even referred to as the “last fracture of life” due to their poor prognosis. Patients often require long-term bed rest, which not only increases the risk of complications such as pulmonary infection, bedsores, and deep vein thrombosis but also imposes a heavy economic and psychological burden on patients and their families, and may even endanger the patient's life (Wells, 2024; Orwoll and Marcus, 2003). Moreover, the fracture healing ability of osteoporosis patients is also significantly affected (Sanghani-Kerai et al., 2018). After a fracture, due to the disordered bone tissue microenvironment and impaired osteoblast function, the fracture healing process is often delayed, and even nonunion may occur (Tarantino et al., 2009). This prolongs the patient's recovery period and may lead to malunion of the fracture site, further affecting the patient's physical function and quality of life, posing a significant challenge to clinical treatment.

Limitations of Traditional Treatments

Systemic Side Effects of Anti-resorptive/Osteogenic Drugs

Currently, traditional clinical treatments for osteoporosis mainly rely on pharmacological interventions, including anti-resorptive drugs and osteogenic drugs. Anti-resorptive drugs such as bisphosphonates slow bone resorption and increase bone density to some extent by inhibiting osteoclast function. However, long-term use of these drugs may trigger a series of systemic side effects. For example, bisphosphonates can cause gastrointestinal reactions, such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea, seriously affecting patient compliance (Papapetrou, 2009; Nguyen et al., 2021; Fuggle et al., 2022). Additionally, some patients may experience flu-like symptoms such as fever and musculoskeletal pain after bisphosphonate treatment, causing additional discomfort. More seriously, long-term use of bisphosphonates may also increase the risk of atypical femoral fractures and osteonecrosis of the jaw, which is related to the excessive inhibition of normal bone tissue metabolism by the drugs (Giusti et al., 2010; Anderson et al., 2024; Bedogni et al., 2024). Calcitonin, another anti-resorptive drug, effectively relieves osteoporotic pain but may cause adverse reactions such as nausea, vomiting, and facial flushing, limiting its widespread application. Osteogenic drugs such as parathyroid hormone (PTH) can stimulate osteoblast activity, promote bone formation, and increase bone density. However, their clinical application also has limitations (Wang et al., 2023a; Qaseem et al., 2023). The use of parathyroid hormone may cause adverse reactions such as hypercalcemia and palpitations, and the high treatment cost makes it difficult for many patients to sustain long-term use (Li et al., 2023).

Mechanical Defects unable to Repair Local Bone Defects

Traditional drug therapy primarily exerts its effects through systemic regulation of bone metabolism and cannot provide effective mechanical support and repair for existing bone defects, especially local bone defects. In fracture treatment, drug therapy alone cannot restore the integrity and mechanical properties of the skeleton (Merlotti et al., 2019). For example, in the treatment of hip or spinal fractures, drugs can only promote bone healing to a certain extent, but cannot directly support the mechanical stability and structural integrity of the fracture site (Tarantino et al., 2009; Giannotti et al., 2013). This leads to slow or nonunion of fractures, and patients often need to rely on external fixation devices such as steel plates, steel nails, and external fixators during recovery. However, these devices have limitations, such as potential infection, loosening, and the need for secondary surgery, and cannot fundamentally solve the problem of bone defect repair (Tarantino et al., 2009; Larsson and Fazzalari, 2014; Barvencik, 2015; Kupperman and Bukata, 2016).

Value of Biomaterial intervention

At the interface between pharmacotherapy and regenerative medicine, material intervention can integrate local biomechanical reinforcement with microenvironment-responsive bioactivity. In osteoporosis, where bone remodeling becomes uncoupled and the marrow niche is often characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired angiogenic signaling, the therapeutic performance of nanomaterials is influenced not only by cargo selection but also by synthesis-governed physicochemical attributes (composition, crystallinity, defect states, and surface terminations) that regulate interfacial charge transfer, ion exchange, and catalytic redox activity (Lei et al., 2023).

Local Targeted Therapy carrier

With the rapid development of biomaterial science, its applications in the field of osteoporosis treatment have gradually attracted attention (Lei et al., 2023). Biomaterials can serve as carriers for local targeted therapy, precisely delivering drugs to osteoporotic lesion sites or bone defect areas (Lei et al., 2023; Moon et al., 2020). By designing the composition, structure, and properties of biomaterials, sustained and long-term drug release can be achieved (Heng et al., 2023). For instance, some nanotechnology-based biomaterial carriers, such as nano-liposomes and nano-polymer microspheres, can penetrate physiological barriers of bone tissue to achieve deep drug delivery within bone tissue (Wu et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022b). These carriers can encapsulate anti-resorptive or osteogenic drugs. Upon reaching the osteoporotic lesion site, the carriers slowly release the drugs under the influence of the local microenvironment. This increases local drug concentration, enhances therapeutic effects, and reduces systemic drug distribution and side effects (García-García, 2022). Additionally, biomaterial carriers can be combined with imaging agents to enable real-time monitoring of drug delivery, providing more precise guidance for clinical treatment (Pant et al., 2023). Porous coordination frameworks and hierarchical hybrid architectures (e.g., MOF-on-MOF systems) can further broaden this platform space by enabling high-capacity loading and diffusion-regulated release with tunable kinetics (Pant et al., 2023).

Mechanical support and Regeneration Guidance for Bone Defects

Biomaterials also show great potential in providing mechanical support and guiding regeneration for bone defects (Sheikh et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2020; Pilia et al., 2013). Biodegradable and biocompatible biomaterials, such as bioactive ceramics, bioactive glasses, and biodegradable polymers, can provide mechanical support to bone defect sites after implantation, maintaining the integrity and stability of the skeleton (Wei et al., 2020; Deng et al., 2019). The mechanical properties of these materials can be optimized by adjusting their composition and structure to meet the mechanical requirements of different bone defect locations. Moreover, they can induce bone tissue growth and mineralization, promoting osteocyte adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. For example, hydroxyapatite in bioactive ceramics is similar to the inorganic components of human bone tissue and can form good chemical bonds with bone tissue, providing a scaffold for osteocyte growth (Prakasam, 2017; Allo et al., 2012; Pamula, 2011; Ma, 2022). Their degradation products can also be absorbed and metabolized by the human body without causing long-term toxic reactions. Biodegradable polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) have good processability and biodegradability. They can be fabricated into three-dimensional porous scaffolds to provide a favorable microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration (Wei et al., 2020; Dorati et al., 2017; Qian, 2024) (Fig. 2). These biomaterials guide bone tissue regeneration by mimicking the structure and function of natural bone tissue, overcoming the limitations of traditional treatments in bone defect repair and bringing new hope and breakthroughs for osteoporosis treatment (Deng et al., 2019; Bramhill et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024a). In clinical applications, biomaterials can also be combined with bone marrow stem cells, growth factors, etc., to further enhance their promotion of bone tissue regeneration and provide more effective treatment options for osteoporosis patients (Oryan et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2020). Although certain implant- or scaffold-based studies are referenced for their relevance to bone regeneration, the focus of this review is the application of nanomaterials in osteoporosis therapy rather than an in-depth discussion of fracture fixation technologies. In this review, we discuss two major categories of nanomaterial-based osteoporosis strategies: materials that directly modulate osteoblast and osteoclast activity through surface chemistry, ion release, or immune regulation, and materials that function as targeted drug-delivery systems for anti-resorptive, osteogenic, or sequential therapeutic applications. Recent work on morphology-engineered biodegradable ceramics, such as high-aspect-ratio α‑calcium sulphate hemihydrate that yields porous architectures, underscores how crystal-level design can simultaneously tune resorption behavior and osteoconductive structure (Oryan et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2020).

Injectable Hydrogels for Osteoporosis Treatment.

Collectively, these developments indicate that nanomaterial-enabled osteoporosis therapy increasingly relies on the coupled optimization of synthesis route, surface chemistry, and functional outcomes, including bone targeting, controlled release, osteoimmunomodulation, and mechanically compatible regeneration. Accordingly, this review consolidates microenvironment-guided design principles across drug-delivery nanosystems and regenerative nanocomposites, and frames these strategies within a translation-oriented perspective that considers standardized efficacy endpoints, manufacturability, and long-term safety.

Given the rapid expansion of osteoporosis nanomedicine, several recent reviews have provided valuable overviews with a narrower focus—for example, bone-targeting nanoparticle delivery systems (Oryan et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2020), bisphosphonate-modified nanocarriers (Oryan et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2020), functionalized nanoparticles (Oryan et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2020), or hydrogel/scaffold-centered strategies for osteoporotic defect regeneration (Oryan et al., 2014; Peng et al., 2020). While these works are highly informative, they typically emphasize a single platform class or delivery motif and do not integrate osteoporosis-specific microenvironmental constraints, material–tissue mechanical matching for fragile bone, and translation-oriented evaluation considerations into a unified design logic.

Distinct from the above, the novelty of the present review lies in: (i) proposing a pathology-to-design mapping framework that converts key osteoporotic hallmarks (uncoupled remodeling, inflammatory/oxidative and acidic milieu, impaired angiogenesis, and reduced endogenous regeneration) into actionable engineering objectives—mechanical compatibility, bioactive guidance, and synchronized biodegradation—applicable across metals/ceramics/polymers/composites (Table 1); (ii) unifying drug-delivery nanosystems and regenerative nanocomposites within one mechanism-driven taxonomy, explicitly linking nanoscale cues (ion release, surface topography/chemistry, and osteoimmune modulation) to osteoblast–osteoclast coupling; (iii) highlighting spatiotemporally programmed and stimuli-responsive strategies (pH/enzyme/temperature/electroactive), including emerging theranostic concepts for monitoring-guided dosing; and (iv) providing a practical translation-oriented roadmap that summarizes model selection, standardized endpoints, safety/biodegradation assessment, manufacturability, and patient-stratified design considerations.

Characteristics of Osteoporotic Bone Microenvironment and Principles of Material Design

Pathological Bone Biological Characteristics

Osteoporosis is a complex metabolic bone disease with unique pathological biological characteristics. A thorough understanding of these characteristics is crucial for designing effective therapeutic materials.

Low Bone Density, High Bone turnover Rate, and Insufficient Vascularization

One of the most prominent features of osteoporosis is low bone density (Gregson et al., 2022). The reduction in bone density primarily results from bone resorption outpacing bone formation, leading to decreased bone mass per unit volume (Gregson et al., 2022; O'Brien et al., 2005). This bone loss renders the skeleton fragile and less capable of withstanding normal physiological loads, thereby increasing fracture risk. The decline in bone density is typically accompanied by thinning and fracturing of trabeculae, as well as increased cortical porosity—microstructural changes that further compromise the mechanical properties of bone (Molino et al., 2020; Compston, 2017; McNamara, 2010).

Concurrently, osteoporosis patients often exhibit a high bone turnover rate (Segura-Egea et al., 2023). Bone turnover refers to the continuous remodeling process of bone tissue, involving a dynamic balance between bone resorption and formation (Schini et al., 2023). In healthy individuals, this process remains balanced to maintain skeletal strength and stability (Schini et al., 2023). However, in osteoporosis, this equilibrium is disrupted, with bone resorption rates exceeding formation rates, resulting in persistent bone loss (Vasikaran et al., 2023). A high turnover rate indicates enhanced osteoclast activity, rapid bone degradation, and relatively delayed new bone formation (Abdalbary et al., 2022; Chopin et al., 2012). This imbalanced bone metabolic state not only accelerates bone loss but also disrupts bone microstructure, further weakening skeletal integrity (Perez-Araluce et al., 2024; Imamudeen, 2022).

Additionally, osteoporotic bone tissue suffers from insufficient vascularization (Skalny, 2024). Bone vascularization is essential for maintaining tissue health and function, as blood vessels supply oxygen and nutrients to bone cells while participating in metabolic and repair processes. Studies show that osteoporotic bone exhibits reduced vascular density and impaired angiogenic capacity (Valero and González Macías, 2025). This vascular deficiency may lead to inadequate nutrient supply, impairing osteocyte survival and function, thereby delaying fracture healing and bone repair (Perez-Araluce et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024a). Insufficient vascularization may also affect bone marrow hematopoietic function, further altering the bone microenvironment.

Imbalanced RANKL/OPG Pathway and Osteoclast activity

The dysregulation of the RANKL/OPG pathway plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, especially glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (De Leon-Oliva, 2023). RANKL (Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor Kappa-B Ligand), a critical cytokine, activates osteoclast formation and function, thereby promoting bone resorption (Abdi et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2023). OPG (Osteoprotegerin), a natural inhibitor of RANKL, binds to RANKL and blocks its interaction with the RANK receptor, suppressing osteoclast activation (Lee, 2021). Under physiological conditions, the balance between RANKL and OPG is essential for maintaining stable bone metabolism.

However, this equilibrium is disrupted in osteoporosis patients. Studies demonstrate significantly elevated RANKL expression and reduced OPG levels in the bone tissue and serum of osteoporotic individuals, leading to an imbalanced RANKL/OPG ratio (Abdalbary et al., 2022; Lee, 2021; Kim et al., 2023a). This imbalance enhances RANKL-mediated activation of osteoclasts, accelerating bone resorption (Deligiorgi et al., 2021). Increased osteoclast activity exacerbates trabecular destruction and bone loss, further aggravating osteoporosis progression (Yao et al., 2021).

Moreover, enhanced osteoclast activity is associated with an acidic bone microenvironment (Yao et al., 2021). During bone resorption, osteoclasts secrete protons (H+) and organic acids, lowering local pH (Veis and O'Brien, 2023). This acidic environment not only facilitates osteoclast activity but also inhibits osteoblast function, impairing bone formation and repair (McNamara, 2010; Abdalbary et al., 2022). Additionally, it promotes calcium ion leaching from the bone matrix, worsening bone loss. Thus, modulating bone tissue acid-base balance represents a potential therapeutic strategy for osteoporosis.

Key objectives in material design

Based on the pathological bone biological characteristics of osteoporosis, designing biomaterials for osteoporosis treatment requires addressing multiple objectives to ensure effective bone repair/regeneration while inhibiting pathological resorption.

Mechanical compatibility: matching elastic modulus of osteoporotic bone (preventing stress shielding)

Matching the mechanical properties of biomaterials to host bone tissue is critical for therapeutic efficacy. In osteoporosis, reduced bone density and microstructural degradation significantly compromise mechanical performance. Implanted materials must provide adequate mechanical support while avoiding stress shielding (Molino et al., 2020; McNamara, 2010; Turner, 2002).

At the tissue level, osteoporotic bone exhibits reduced mineralized mass, trabecular thinning, and increased cortical porosity, resulting in a lower elastic modulus and diminished fatigue resistance. Accordingly, implantable materials should minimize modulus mismatch (e.g., porous/gradient architectures) while maintaining sufficient strength under cyclic loading; in nano-enabled systems, processing- and surface-chemistry–defined attributes further shape interfacial osseointegration and microenvironmental responses.

Biological activity guidance: promoting osteogenesis vs. inhibiting osteoclast activity

The biological activity of biomaterials is crucial for bone tissue repair and regeneration. Ideal biomaterials should promote osteoblast activity and inhibit osteoclast activity to regulate bone metabolism and accelerate repair. Clinically, these dual objectives should be pursued with a remodeling-preserving intent: anti-resorptive cues should reduce pathological resorption while maintaining sufficient turnover for microdamage repair and coupling to bone formation.

Promoting osteoblast activity is a key goal in biomaterial design. Osteoblasts are essential for bone tissue formation, and their activity directly affects new bone generation. Biomaterials can enhance osteoblast activity by providing favorable surfaces for cell adhesion and proliferation, releasing bioactive factors, and regulating the local microenvironment. For instance, bioactive ceramic materials like hydroxyapatite (HA) and bioactive glass (BG) have chemical compositions similar to bone tissue, enabling strong chemical bonding with osteoblasts and promoting their adhesion and proliferation (Lin et al., 2020; McNamara, 2010). Moreover, these materials can release ions (e.g., Ca2+, PO₄3−, and Si4+) to stimulate osteoblast activity and promote bone tissue formation (McNamara, 2010; Perez-Araluce et al., 2024).

In addition to promoting osteoblast activity, inhibiting osteoclast activity is also vital in biomaterial design. Excessive osteoclast activation is a major pathological feature of osteoporosis, and its inhibition can reduce bone resorption and prevent further bone loss (Lin et al., 2020; Imamudeen, 2022). Biomaterials can suppress osteoclast activity by regulating the local microenvironment. For example, some biomaterials can adjust the local pH to neutral or weakly alkaline levels, inhibiting osteoclast bone resorption (McNamara, 2010). Porous biomaterials, with their high surface area and open channels, allow free fluid flow, promoting ion exchange and acid-base balance regulation. For instance, porous HA bioceramics permit fluid infiltration, supporting osteoblast nutrition and metabolic waste removal (Funayama, 2021). This helps maintain local acid-base balance and supports bone cell metabolism. Porous bioactive glasses (MBGs) can react with calcium and phosphate ions in body fluids to form HA crystals, regulating local pH (Vallet-Regí et al., 2022). Additionally, Zn2+ ion release has been shown to promote mesenchymal stem cell osteogenic differentiation (Sutthavas et al., 2022). Although porous materials generally have lower mechanical properties, their structural design can optimize these properties to better support bone tissue formation and regeneration. For example, adding fibers or reinforcing materials (e.g., zirconia, polyurethane) can enhance porous materials' mechanical strength and stability in acidic/alkaline environments (Matsumoto et al., 2011). Furthermore, biomaterials can suppress osteoclast formation and function by releasing anti-inflammatory factors or anti-resorptive drugs (Perez-Araluce et al., 2024; Turner, 2002). For example, drug delivery systems like nano-liposomes and nano-polymer microspheres can target anti-resorptive drugs (e.g., bisphosphonates) to osteoporotic sites, achieving sustained and long-term drug release, inhibiting osteoclast activity, and reducing bone resorption (Zhang et al., 2022a; Wu, 2024a).

Controlled Degradation: Coordinating the Rate of New Bone Replacement

The degradation rate of biomaterials is one of the key factors influencing their therapeutic efficacy. Ideal biomaterials should have a controllable degradation rate to ensure sustained support and guidance throughout the bone tissue repair process, avoiding issues from degradation that is too rapid or too slow (Turner, 2002; Martin and Correa, 2010).

The degradation rate of biomaterials should align with the natural bone remodeling timeline, which generally spans 3–6 months for trabecular bone and up to 6–12 months for cortical bone; for example, PLGA scaffolds often degrade within 2–4 months depending on composition, magnesium-based alloys typically resorb over 3–6 months, and bioactive glass undergoes ion dissolution and replacement by new bone within 8–12 weeks, allowing structural support to be maintained while gradual bone deposition occurs. As the material degrades gradually during bone repair, new bone forms and replaces it. If the material degrades too quickly, it may compromise the mechanical support at the repair site, affecting normal bone growth and repair. Conversely, if degradation is too slow, it may hinder new bone formation and regeneration. Thus, adjusting the material's composition and structure can effectively control its degradation rate.

Degradable polymers, such as polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), and their copolymers (e.g., PLGA), are important for controllable degradation. They hydrolyze in the body to non-toxic lactic and glycolic acids, which can be metabolized. Their degradation rate can be tuned by adjusting polymer molecular weight, composition, and crystallinity. For instance, PLGA degradation can be regulated by varying the lactic-to-glycolic acid ratio, with faster degradation for 50:50 PLGA and slower for 90:10 PLGA (Martin and Correa, 2010). Fast-degrading PLGA may disappear before tissue regeneration is complete, while slow-degrading PLGA offers prolonged mechanical support, aiding cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation, and promoting bone regeneration (Park et al., 2012). In a study, PLGA with drugs caused severe inflammation and bone absorption, but PLGA combined with polyvinyl alcohol-polysodium copolymers degraded more slowly, better controlling inflammation and promoting bone growth (Venkatesan et al., 2019). Similarly, the degradation rate of bioactive glasses and ceramics can be adjusted by modifying their composition and structure. For example, adding small amounts of carbonate or silicate can increase hydroxyapatite's degradation rate for better bone repair and regeneration.

Besides composition and structure, surface treatment and composite design also affect degradation. Coating or adding bioactive factors to the material surface can regulate degradation and bioactivity (Kumari, 2021). Coating technology can control drug/bioactive factor release, finely tuning degradation (Zhou, 2025). Composite material design can also synergistically adjust degradation. For instance, combining degradable polymers with bioactive ceramics uses the polymer's flexibility and the ceramic's bioactivity to optimize mechanical properties and degradation rate (Table 1) (Robin et al., 2024; Sreena et al., 2024).

Synthesis–property relationships and unresolved challenges for nano-enabled osteoporosis biomaterials

In nanomaterial-enabled osteoporosis biomaterials, clinical performance is governed by an explicit chain of dependence: synthesis/processing parameters → critical material attributes (CQAs) → surface/interfacial chemistry and transport → targeted biological function → manufacturability and safety. Mapping these relationships improves cross-study comparability and helps identify failure modes that are otherwise obscured by sequential study reporting.

Across classes, CQAs commonly include particle/flake size distribution, porosity and specific surface area, defect density, surface terminations or functional groups, dispersibility within the host matrix, and (where relevant) electrical conductivity. These attributes jointly control protein adsorption and cell adhesion, drug/ion loading and release, redox reactivity, and the durability of bioactivity after sterilization and storage.

Conductive nano-additives illustrate key trade-offs that are highly relevant to osteoporosis applications (e.g., electroactive interfaces, sensing-enabled scaffolds, and electrically assisted osteogenesis). For graphene-family materials, increased oxygenated surface terminations (epoxy/hydroxyl/carboxyl groups) enhance aqueous dispersibility and interfacial bonding but generally reduce conductivity; conversely, reduction improves conductivity while decreasing hydrophilicity and increasing aggregation risk. High-quality CVD graphene offers superior conductivity yet is less scalable and more costly, whereas chemically derived GO/rGO is more scalable but prone to batch-to-batch variability in oxidation degree, lateral size, and residual impurities.

Outstanding challenges that require systematic resolution include (i) oxidation/aging stability during aqueous processing, sterilization, and in vivo exposure, which can drift surface chemistry and attenuate conductivity or bioactivity; (ii) batch-to-batch variability driven by synthesis conditions and post-functionalization, complicating reproducible dose–response relationships; and (iii) scalability constraints when multi-step surface modification or purification is required. These issues motivate the use of standardized characterization panels (e.g., size/PSD, zeta potential, surface chemistry by XPS/FTIR, conductivity where relevant, and accelerated stability/sterilization stress tests) and QbD-aligned acceptance windows before translation-oriented efficacy comparisons.

Classification and functional mechanisms of biomaterials

Metal-based materials

Metal-based materials are widely used in orthopedics due to their excellent mechanical properties, providing robust support for osteoporotic bones. With technological advances, they now also promote bone ingrowth and are biodegradable.

Porous titanium alloy scaffolds (topology optimization for enhanced bone ingrowth)

Porous titanium alloy scaffolds, key for osteoporosis treatment, combine the excellent mechanical and biological properties of titanium and its alloys with the advantages of a porous structure (Phuoc et al., 2023). Using topology optimization techniques, these scaffolds can be precisely designed to match the complex structure and mechanical requirements of human bones, optimizing porosity, pore size, and connectivity (Zheng et al., 2020). This enhances bone ingrowth and scaffold performance (Tang et al., 2024; Bai, 2022). For example, Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and Finite Element Analysis (FEA) studies have shown that optimized scaffolds perform well in mechanical tests and promote bone ingrowth (Zhao et al., 2024). In experiments, these scaffolds guided bone cell migration and proliferation, facilitating new bone growth and replacement of the scaffold material (Fan et al., 2024). They also exhibit good bioactivity, with surface modifiability through coatings or ion implantation to further enhance osteointegration and bone formation (Fan et al., 2024).

Magnesium alloys: biodegradability and Mg2+ release for osteogenesis and inflammation suppression

As emerging biodegradable metal-based materials, magnesium alloys show great potential in osteoporosis therapy. Magnesium, a vital element in human physiology, significantly impacts bone metabolism (Li et al., 2024b; Sandomierski, 2023). Magnesium alloys offer good biocompatibility and mechanical properties, with a density close to human cortical bone, reducing stress shielding. Their biodegradability allows them to gradually degrade in physiological environments, producing non-toxic magnesium ions (Mg2+), hydrogen gas, and an alkaline environment. Mg2+ ions have been shown to suppress the release of key pro-inflammatory cytokines—including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6—thereby reducing local inflammation and creating a microenvironment more conducive to osteogenesis. Mg2+ release offers dual benefits for osteoporosis treatment: promoting osteoblast activity and function by regulating signaling pathways like MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting inflammatory factor release (Zhu et al., 2023). This protects bone tissue from further inflammatory damage. The degradation rate of magnesium alloys can be controlled by adjusting their composition and microstructure (Ran et al., 2023). For instance, adding rare earth elements such as yttrium (Y) and neodymium (Nd) can enhance corrosion resistance and mechanical properties while regulating the degradation rate (Cendón, 2022; Heng et al., 2024). Currently in the developmental stage, magnesium alloys are promising for future osteoporosis treatment due to their unique biodegradability and bioactivity.

Ceramic Materials

Ceramic materials, known for their good bioactivity and osteoconductivity, play an important role in osteoporosis treatment, with different types meeting diverse clinical needs.

Calcium phosphate-based materials (HA, β-TCP): optimized osteoconductivity

Calcium phosphate-based materials, including hydroxyapatite (HA) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), are widely used ceramic materials (Hong et al., 2022). Hydroxyapatite (HA), by contrast, exhibits much slower degradation and provides long-term structural stability. Its crystallinity and surface chemistry favor protein adsorption and osteoblast adhesion, making it suitable for maintaining space and mechanical support in osteoporotic bone defects (Munir et al., 2021). Its crystal structure resembles bone tissue, enabling stable chemical bonding with osteoblasts and promoting bone cell adhesion and proliferation (Kavitha Sri et al., 2023). β-TCP is a biodegradable calcium phosphate material with relatively fast resorption, allowing it to be gradually replaced by newly formed bone. Its dissolution releases calcium and phosphate ions that promote osteoblast differentiation and support early-stage bone regeneration (Hoveidaei et al., 2024). The osteoconductivity of calcium phosphate-based materials is mainly optimized in three ways: their surface can adsorb proteins and growth factors from blood, providing a favorable microenvironment for osteoblast adhesion and growth; their porous structure guides bone cell growth and vascular infiltration; and their degradation products participate in bone tissue metabolism, supplying essential elements like calcium and phosphorus for new bone formation (Mo et al., 2023). For example, studies have shown that porous HA scaffolds effectively promote bone tissue growth and repair in vivo, with better osteoconductivity than many other biomaterials (Zhao et al., 2023). To further enhance their osteoconductivity, researchers have conducted various modification studies on calcium phosphate-based materials, such as adding bioactive factors like bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and growth factors (e.g., VEGF, FGF), or performing surface coating treatments, which can significantly improve their ability to promote bone formation (Echave, 2022; Rong, 2024; Rahman et al., 2016; Chi et al., 2022). Additionally, preparing calcium phosphate-based materials with gradient structures is an effective optimization strategy, enabling them to better mimic the structure and function of natural bone tissue and promote bone tissue regeneration and repair.

Bioactive Glass (Strontium and Zinc-Doped Antiresorptive Ion Therapy)

Bioactive glass, a novel ceramic material that can form chemical bonds with human tissue, has attracted attention in osteoporosis treatment due to its unique bioactivity (Gupta et al., 2021; Zeimaran et al., 2021). Traditional bioactive glass, composed of silicates, phosphates, and calcium, can form a carbonate hydroxyapatite layer similar to human bone tissue components in physiological environments, promoting bone tissue repair and regeneration (Aldhaher et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025b)(Fig. 3). Bioactive glass doped with elements like strontium (Sr) and zinc (Zn) further expands its applications in antiresorptive therapy (Li et al., 2025b; Ding et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023). Sr is important for bone metabolism regulation, inhibiting osteoclast activity to reduce bone resorption and promoting osteoblast activity to increase bone formation (Hu et al., 2023; Shishkina et al., 2023). Zn has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cell proliferation-promoting effects, improving the microenvironment of bone tissue and promoting its repair (Toledano et al., 2021; Wang, 2021a). For example, studies have shown that Sr-doped bioactive glass can effectively inhibit osteoclast formation and function, reducing bone resorption while stimulating osteoblast proliferation and differentiation to increase bone mass (Matic et al., 2024; Moazeni, 2023; Guagnini et al., 2024). Zn-doped bioactive glass excels in anti-inflammatory and bone tissue regeneration promotion, alleviating inflammation-related osteoporosis and accelerating bone healing (Ji et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). The composition and structure of bioactive glass can be adjusted to finely control its bioactivity and degradation rate to meet the needs of different osteoporosis cases. For instance, adjusting the ratios of silicates, phosphates, and calcium, or adding other bioactive elements, can regulate the degradation rate and ion release characteristics of bioactive glass, achieving precise control over the bone tissue repair process.

Polymer Materials

Polymer materials, with good flexibility and processability, play an important role in osteoporosis treatment and are categorized into natural and synthetic polymers.

Natural Polymers: Collagen/Chitosan Drug delivery Scaffolds (for Sustained Release of Bisphosphonates)

To overcome their inherently low mechanical strength, natural polymers are often combined with other material classes—such as bioactive ceramics (e.g., hydroxyapatite, β-TCP), synthetic polymers (e.g., PLGA, PCL), or metallic components (e.g., magnesium or titanium-based particles or coatings)—to create composite scaffolds with enhanced structural stability and improved osteogenic performance in osteoporotic bone (Peng et al., 2022b; Chacon, 2023). Collagen, the main organic component of human bone tissue, has good bioactivity and cell compatibility, providing a natural growth environment for cells (Dasgupta et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022b). Chitosan has various biological activities, including antibacterial, hemostatic, and cell growth-promoting properties, and is easily chemically modified to meet different application requirements (Li et al., 2025c). Collagen/chitosan drug delivery scaffolds, which incorporate antiresorptive drugs like bisphosphonates into composite scaffolds of collagen and chitosan, achieve sustained and long-term drug release (Li et al., 2025c). Bisphosphonates, a class of potent antiresorptive drugs, can specifically bind to the bone tissue surface and inhibit osteoclast activity. The porous structure and biodegradability of collagen/chitosan drug delivery scaffolds allow for slow drug release in the body, prolonging the drug's retention at the site of action and enhancing therapeutic effects (Zhang et al., 2022b). The bioactivity of collagen and chitosan also promotes bone tissue repair and regeneration (Guillén-Carvajal et al., 2023). For example, studies have shown that these scaffolds effectively release bisphosphonates in vivo, inhibiting osteoclast activity and promoting osteoblast adhesion and proliferation to accelerate bone tissue repair and regeneration (Zhang et al., 2022b; Abdulghafor, 2023; Klara et al., 2023). Additionally, the porous structure of natural polymers can guide blood vessel growth and nutrient transport, providing a favorable microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration (Elkhenany et al., 2025; Patel et al., 2020). However, due to their relatively weak mechanical properties and easy degradability, natural polymers are often used in combination with other materials in practical applications to enhance their mechanical performance and stability.

Synthetic Polymers: PLGA Microspheres for Controlled Release of Teriparatide

Synthetic polymers such as polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) have good processability and biodegradability, and can precisely control drug release through molecular design. PLGA microspheres, a common drug delivery system, can encapsulate bone-forming agents like teriparatide for controlled and sustained release (Schlosser et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). Teriparatide, a recombinant human parathyroid hormone (PTH), stimulates osteoblast activity and promotes bone formation, making it an important osteoporosis treatment (Li, 2023a). Through their hydrophobicity and porous structure, PLGA microspheres enable slow drug release, prolonging drug action, reducing dosing frequency, and improving treatment compliance. Studies show they maintain effective blood drug concentrations, promoting bone tissue formation and repair (Altaani, 2020). Their degradation products are non-toxic and metabolizable, causing no adverse reactions. The drug release rate and mechanism of PLGA microspheres can be finely tuned by adjusting the polymer's molecular weight, composition, and preparation process to meet different clinical needs. For example, adjusting the lactic-to-glycolic acid ratio in PLGA can control the microsphere degradation rate and precisely regulate teriparatide release (Gao et al., 2023; Bahari Javan et al., 2017). PLGA microspheres for controlled teriparatide release show promise in osteoporosis treatment, offering patients a more convenient and effective option (Gao et al., 2023).

Composite Materials

Composite materials combine the advantages of multiple materials to coordinate optimize mechanical properties and biological activity, meeting the comprehensive needs of osteoporosis treatment.

Polymer/Ceramic Composite Scaffolds (Mechanical-Bioactive Synergy)

Polymer/ceramic composite scaffolds are a new type of biomaterial combining polymer and ceramic materials (Hodásová et al., 2022; Algharaibeh et al., 2022). Polymers provide flexibility, processability, mechanical support, and drug delivery functions, while ceramics offer excellent bioactivity and osteoconductivity, promoting bone tissue formation and repair. For example, combining synthetic polymers like PLA or PGA with ceramics like HA or β-TCP produces composite scaffolds that provide mechanical support and promote bone cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation (Sajad Daneshi et al., 2024). Studies show that the mechanical properties of these scaffolds can be optimized by adjusting the polymer-to-ceramic ratio and composition method to meet the needs of different osteoporosis cases (Khan et al., 2006; Trzaskowska, 2023). In terms of bioactivity, the osteoconductivity of ceramic materials guides bone cell growth and mineralization, while the drug delivery function of polymer materials enables sustained release of anti-osteoporosis drugs, enhancing treatment efficacy. For instance, PLA/HA composite scaffolds effectively promote bone tissue growth and repair in vivo while releasing drugs to inhibit osteoclast activity and increase bone mass (Wang et al., 2023b; Liu et al., 2021; Gendviliene, 2021). Additionally, the porous structure of these composite scaffolds guides blood vessel growth and nutrient transport, providing a favorable microenvironment for bone regeneration. Currently, polymer/ceramic composite scaffolds are an important research direction in osteoporosis treatment with broad clinical application prospects.

Nanofunctional Materials: Graphene-Enhanced Osteoinductive Interfaces

Nanofunctional materials are a research hotspot in biomaterials due to their unique nanostructures and physicochemical properties, offering advantages in osteoporosis treatment (Xu et al., 2025; Yang, 2024). Graphene, a novel two-dimensional nanomaterial with excellent mechanical, electrical, and biological properties, is widely used in biomedicine (Yang et al., 2024b). Graphene-enhanced osteoinductive interfaces use graphene's nanostructure and surface properties to modify the surface of bone tissue engineering materials, enhancing their osteoinductive and biological performance (Li et al., 2024b). With a large specific surface area and abundant surface functional groups, graphene interacts well with osteoblasts, promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation (Kadhim et al., 2022). Studies show that graphene-modified material surfaces adsorb more osteoblast-related proteins and growth factors, creating a favorable microenvironment for osteoblast growth (Xu, 2023; Jeong et al., 2022). Moreover, graphene's electrical conductivity enhances osteoblast activity and function by promoting intercellular signal transmission and ion transport (Fu et al., 2017; MacDonald, 2021). Additionally, graphene has antibacterial properties, reducing infection risks and ensuring bone tissue repair and regeneration (Srimaneepong et al., 2022). For example, graphene-modified titanium alloy surfaces demonstrate good osteoinductive performance and biocompatibility in vitro and in vivo, significantly promoting bone tissue growth and repair (Sun et al., 2022). Graphene-enhanced osteoinductive interfaces can also be combined with other biomaterials to further improve comprehensive performance (Wang et al., 2019). For instance, combining graphene with bioactive ceramics or polymers creates multifunctional nanocomposite materials that achieve coordinated optimization of mechanical properties, biological activity, and antibacterial performance, providing a more comprehensive and effective solution for osteoporosis treatment (Jagadeeshanayaka et al., 2022).

Metal-based systems provide the highest load-bearing capacity and are advantageous for fixation-adjacent or structurally demanding defects. However, their elastic modulus mismatch may cause stress shielding unless porous/gradient architectures are implemented, and biodegradable metals face risks of rapid corrosion, gas formation, and burst ion release, all of which require reliable in vivo control and standardized long-term safety assessment.

Ceramic materials (e.g., HA/β-TCP/bioactive glass) offer strong osteoconductivity and clinically familiar chemistry, with ion-doping enabling osteogenic/anti-resorptive cues. Their key limitations are brittleness and limited fracture toughness, which restrict standalone use in load-bearing sites. In addition, tuning dissolution and ion release in the osteoporotic milieu remains challenging, and overly rapid resorption may undermine early mechanical stability.

Polymeric carriers and hydrogels excel in injectability and programmable delivery (e.g., sustained release of anti-resorptive or anabolic agents), enabling minimally invasive local therapy. Yet, polymers are typically mechanically weak and often require reinforcement. Acidic degradation byproducts (e.g., from polyesters) may aggravate local inflammation unless buffered or combined with basic/ion-releasing phases, and scale-up reproducibility of drug-loading and release profiles remains a practical barrier.

Composite and nanofunctional materials aim to synergize strength with bioactivity (e.g., polymer/ceramic composites, graphene-enabled interfaces). While they can simultaneously address mechanical support and microenvironmental modulation, increased formulation complexity raises risks of batch-to-batch variability, unclear long-term fate of nanoscale components, and more demanding quality control and regulatory documentation.

Across material classes, several unresolved challenges limit cross-study comparability and clinical translation: (i) few head-to-head studies under standardized osteoporotic models and endpoints; (ii) insufficient reporting of long-term biodistribution/clearance for nanoscale additives and degradation products; (iii) limited understanding of how the osteoporotic inflammatory/oxidative and acidic microenvironment reshapes material performance over time; and (iv) manufacturing scalability and sterilization compatibility for complex nano-enabled composites. Addressing these gaps will be essential for moving from proof-of-concept efficacy to clinically actionable platforms.

A wide variety of biomaterials are used for osteoporosis treatment, covering metal-based, ceramic, polymer, and composite materials. Each has unique properties and advantages. Reasonable selection and design can meet the diverse needs of osteoporosis treatment. In practice, researchers can choose suitable biomaterials or create composites based on specific clinical requirements and lesion characteristics to achieve optimal therapeutic effects. Advanced Functionalization strategies

Biomaterial functionalization presents new avenues for osteoporosis treatment. Precise design enhances material performance and biocompatibility, making these strategies vital for bone tissue repair and regeneration, and offering effective solutions for this global health challenge. The scaffold and hydrogel systems discussed in this section are included because they either contain nanostructured components or serve as carriers for nanomaterials; they are not presented as stand-alone orthopedic fixation devices.

Drug/factor delivery Systems

Sequential Release Systems (Anti-resorptive then Osteogenic Factors)

In osteoporosis therapy, sequential drug release systems are highly significant (Li, 2024a). To address this, sequential release systems first deliver anti-resorptive bisphosphonates to inhibit osteoclasts and reduce bone loss (Wei, 2023; Kim et al., 2023b). In this context, sequential release refers to the controlled delivery of different therapeutic agents or ions in a defined temporal order, for example, releasing anti-inflammatory or anti-resorptive components during the early healing phase, followed by osteogenic or angiogenic cues during later stages to more closely mimic the natural timeline of bone remodeling. The sequential drug release system can precisely control this process and avoid the limitations of traditional monotherapy. Once bone mass stabilizes, the system releases osteogenic factors like BMP-2 and IGF (Li, 2024a; Zeng et al., 2019; Lima et al., 2019; Asafo-Adjei et al., 2016). These factors create local concentration gradients to attract osteoblasts, stimulating their proliferation and differentiation for new bone formation. Researchers have developed multi-layered or core-shell structured nanocarriers. For example, multi-layered polymer nanocarriers can load different drugs in separate layers, achieving sequential release by controlling degradation rates (Xie, 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Studies show that in osteoporosis animal models, sequential release systems significantly improve bone density and structure compared to single-drug systems (Dou et al., 2019). Mimicking physiological bone metabolism, these systems allow drugs to function at optimal times, enhancing therapeutic efficacy and providing precise treatment for bone tissue regeneration. Although current meta-analyses support the efficacy of certain sequential regimens such as teriparatide sequential anti-resorption drugs (Sauhta et al., 2023), high-quality long-term clinical trial data remain limited, especially direct evidence regarding the reduction of fracture risk. Problems such as uneven drug distribution and changes in release characteristics are prone to occur in multi-drug loading systems during scale-up production.

Osteoclast-Targeted Ligand Modification (e.g., RANKL Antibody Coupling)

In osteoporosis treatment, precise osteoclast-targeted drug delivery is crucial (Reid and Billington, 2022). A ligand modification strategy using RANKL antibody coupling is highly effective (Matsumoto and Endo, 2021). Osteoclasts overexpress RANK receptors, and RANKL is key for their activation (McDonald et al., 2021; Liang, 2024). By conjugating RANKL antibodies to nanocarriers, these carriers specifically target osteoclasts (Vallet-Regí et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2016; Newa et al., 2011). Once in the body, they accumulate at osteoclast sites, releasing drugs locally for efficient delivery. This approach increases drug concentration at osteoclast sites, enhancing efficacy and reducing off-target effects (Ishikawa, 2025). Professor Fu's group developed a PTH1–34-loaded biomimetic nanogel (PNG@mR&C) by coating a nanogel core with a bone-marrow MSC–derived membrane enriched with RANK and CXCR4. The membrane coating enhances bone-homing ability through CXCR4–SDF-1 interactions and improves osteoclast targeting via RANK–RANKL recognition. Once delivered to osteoporotic bone tissue, the nanogel achieves controlled release of PTH1–34, thereby promoting osteoblast activity while simultaneously regulating osteoclast signaling to restore bone remodeling balance (Cui, 2024)(Fig. 4). Compared to unmodified carriers, drug utilization efficiency increased several-fold, offering strong support for precise osteoporosis treatment, such as postmenopausal osteoporosis which is induced by significantly decreased levels of the hormone estrogen after menopause.

Material Surface Functionalization

Regulation of Cell Behavior by Micro-Nano Topological Structures

The regulation of cell behavior by micro-nano topological structures is a hot topic in biomaterials (Ma, 2021). Creating micro-nano structures like nanorods, nanotubes, and micropores on material surfaces significantly impacts cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation (Chindamo et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024). For instance, Liu et al. showed that TiO2 nanotubes (T110) enhance fibronectin adsorption by ∼35%, activating integrin α5/β1 pathways, reducing cell spread area by ∼40%, lowering ROS levels by 50–60%, and boosting ALP activity by 2.1-fold. Notably, micro-nano composite structures (M30/M110) exhibit comparable antioxidant and osteogenic properties to pure nanotubes (T110), as they all regulate cell mechanotransduction via the fibronectin-integrin axis, with the M110 group showing 3.2-fold higher OCN expression than traditional titanium surfaces (Ma, 2021). Another study found that the pore size of nanotubes on 3D - printed titanium implants critically regulate bone integration. When the pore size exceeds 100 nm, it significantly promotes macrophage polarization to the M2 type, increasing IL – 10 secretion by 1.8-fold and creating an anti - inflammatory environment. This structure also efficiently loads BMP - 2 growth factors, achieving an 82% sustained release over 28 days under sucrose freeze - drying protection. By synergistically activating integrin α5/β1 and BMP/Smad pathways, it enhances in vitro mineralized nodule formation by 3.1-fold. Animal experiments showed that micro - nano topological - biomolecular composite modification increased bone bonding strength by 2.4-fold in osteoporosis models, outperforming traditional implants (Ali et al., 2023). Precisely controlling the size and shape of micro - nano structures enables precise regulation of cell behavior, offering new ideas for high - performance biomaterial development.

Biomimetic Coatings (Mimicking Bone Matrix Composition)

Biomimetic coatings are inspired by the composition and structure of natural bone matrix. Bone matrix mainly consists of hydroxyapatite, collagen, and various bioactive molecules. Biomimetic coatings enhance material biocompatibility and bone bonding ability by mimicking bone matrix components, such as depositing hydroxyapatite coatings or coating bioactive molecules like collagen. For example, Fu et al. developed a biomimetic nanogel (PNG@mR&C) using genetically engineered bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell membranes (overexpressing RANK and CXCR4) to coat chitosan carriers. CXCR4 provides bone - targeting ability, while RANK on the membrane efficiently clears RANKL (clearance rate of 85%), inhibiting osteoclast formation. The nanogel also releases PTH 1–34 in response to osteoclast - mediated acidic microenvironments (sustained release of over 80% in 28 days), promoting osteogenic differentiation. In ovariectomized mice, this formulation restored bone density to 92% of normal levels via dual pathways of “inhibiting bone resorption and promoting bone formation,” reducing dosing frequency by 60% (Cui, 2024). Wang et al. developed a novel biomimetic composite scaffold (PCL/nHA@GRgel) by grafting RADA16 with pullulan to form a glycopeptide hydrogel (GRgel), mimicking the glycoprotein composition and fiber structure of the natural extracellular matrix. This scaffold is non - covalently combined with a 3D - printed PCL/nHA scaffold. It has a dual - functional mechanism: directly promoting proliferation (2.1 - fold increase) and osteogenic differentiation (3.5 - fold ALP activity increase) of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, and inducing M2 - type macrophage polarization (78% ratio) to establish a pro - repair immune microenvironment. In a rat critical - size calvarial defect model, the new bone area in the defect region reached 83.3%, and vascular density increased 2.8 - fold after 12 weeks, significantly outperforming traditional materials. This strategy of synergistically promoting bone regeneration through immunomodulation (1.9 - fold increase in IL - 10 secretion) provides an innovative solution for bone repair without exogenous cells/growth factors (Wang et al., 2022a). Chen et al. innovatively constructed a polydopamine - modified short - fiber biomimetic matrix (PFS@AM/CeO2), whose core breakthrough lies in the synergistic action of a double - layer biological coating. The outer layer consists of CeO2 nanozymes wrapped in apoptotic neutrophil membranes (AM/CeO2), which mimic apoptotic signals via phosphatidylserine (PS) on the membrane surface, enhancing macrophage recognition efficiency by 3.2 - fold. The inner polydopamine coating stably loads AM/CeO2 through π - π stacking and hydrogen bonding, while also providing antioxidant functions (ROS scavenging efficiency of 85%). This biomimetic design successfully reconstructed the bone repair microenvironment, restoring macrophages' continuous efferocytosis activity to 78% of normal levels and significantly inhibiting excessive inflammation (62% reduction in TNF - α). In a rat femoral defect model, the material achieved new bone formation 4.3 times that of the blank group through a dual - regulation mechanism: CeO2 nanozymes regulate oxidative stress (SOD2 expression upregulated by 2.1 - fold) and apoptotic membranes guide immunomodulation (IL - 10 secretion increased by 1.8 - fold), offering a new “immune - oxidative” dual - target repair strategy for hard - to - heal bone defects (Wang, 2024). Preparation methods for biomimetic coatings include sol - gel processes, electrodeposition, and chemical vapor deposition. Optimizing these processes enables precise control of coating thickness, crystallinity, and compositional uniformity, thereby enhancing coating performance.

Nanomaterial-Mediated Macrophage Polarization and Osteoimmunomodulation

Macrophage polarization is a central regulatory mechanism in osteoimmunology, and its modulation has emerged as a critical factor in the design of nanomaterials for osteoporosis treatment (Cui, 2024). In osteoporotic bone, chronic inflammation, impaired vascularization, and oxidative stress drive macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which promotes osteoclastogenesis, suppresses osteoblast differentiation, and delays bone regeneration (Ma, 2021). Nanomaterials can actively influence macrophage phenotype through their surface chemistry, stiffness, nanotopography, and controlled release of bioactive ions. Graphene-modified titanium and polymeric scaffolds can reduce intracellular ROS levels and modulate integrin-mediated mechanotransduction, thereby promoting a shift from M1 to M2 polarization (Ali et al., 2023). Nanozyme-based platforms also enhance the osteoimmune microenvironment by scavenging excessive reactive oxygen species, restoring redox balance, and supporting M2 polarization (Wang, 2024). However, continuous overactivation may trigger chronic inflammation. Studies have shown that cationic nanoparticles can activate systemic inflammatory responses at high doses, leading to significant increases in the alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity of the cells and inflammatory factors (Naruphontjirakul et al., 2018). It is also worried that the long-term presence of nanomaterials in the bone marrow microenvironment may interfere with the function of hematopoietic stem cells and affect the normal generation of immune cells. Therefore, it is recommended to simulate the structure and function of extracellular matrix (ECM) to modify the surface of nanomaterials for reducing the recognition and attack by the immune system, thereby lowering their immunogenicity and improving their biocompatibility (Tang, 2025). For example, immunomodulatory design strategies such as hierarchical micro–nano surface structures, biomimetic ECM-like coatings, and responsive delivery of anti-inflammatory agents are increasingly incorporated into nanomaterials to create a pro-regenerative osteoimmune niche (Wang et al., 2022a). By orchestrating macrophage phenotype transitions, nanomaterials not only improve local immune homeostasis but also enhance osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and the long-term stability of implants in osteoporotic environments (Lv et al., 2023).

Responsive Smart Materials

Responsive smart materials enable precise drug release based on microenvironment changes at osteoporosis lesion sites (Yuan, 2024; Wei et al., 2022; Li, 2023b). At bone resorption sites, enhanced osteoclast activity makes the local microenvironment acidic (lower pH) with elevated expression of specific enzymes like cathepsin K. Studies have shown that targeting the apoptosis of activated T cells, Monocyte chemoattractant protein – 1 (MCP - 1) and FasL were co - modified on mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) to synthesize T cell apoptosis - inducing TDNs, which can treat osteoporosis in mice by regulating the Treg / Th17 balance (Yang et al., 2021) (Fig. 5). In this design, mesoporous silica nanoparticles were co-modified with MCP-1 and the apoptosis-inducing ligand FasL, enabling the system to recruit activated T cells through MCP-1 signaling and then selectively trigger their apoptosis via FasL engagement, thereby modulating the T-cell balance within the osteoporotic microenvironment. Under physiological conditions, these carriers remain stable but undergo structural changes in acidic pH and under the action of specific enzymes, rapidly degrading to release drugs (Asafo-Adjei et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2022; Dayanandan, 2023). pH-responsive nanomaterials take advantage of the acidified microenvironment generated during osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, typically around pH 4.5–5.5, by incorporating acid-cleavable linkages such as hydrazone, acetal, or Schiff-base bonds that remain stable at physiological pH but disassemble rapidly under acidic conditions (Yang et al., 2021). Inorganic components such as amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC) or mesoporous silica can also be engineered to dissolve preferentially in acidic sites, enabling early release of anti-resorptive agents followed by delayed release of osteogenic factors for sequential therapy (Yuan, 2024; Wei et al., 2022; Li, 2023b).

Diagrammatic representation of TDNs and their impact on osteoporosis via promoting T cell apoptosis and modulating the Tregs/Th17 equilibrium.

Su et al. combined the natural flavonoid monomer component oroxin (ORO) with amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC) to construct a bone – targeting, pH - responsive intelligent nanodrug delivery system (OAPLG). By surface modification with an acidic peptide (DGlu6), the nanoparticles can specifically target bone tissue, significantly enhancing the accumulation efficiency of the drug at the lesion site. Amorphous calcium carbonate, as the nanocore, can rapidly respond to and release ORO in the acidic microenvironment secreted by osteoclasts, while neutralizing the acidic environment and inhibiting the activity and maturation of osteoclasts (Yu, 2024). Temperature-responsive systems represent another class of smart nanomaterials, typically based on polymers or hydrogels that undergo sol–gel transitions near physiological temperature, allowing injectable formulations that solidify in situ (Yu, 2024) (Lv et al., 2023). Materials such as PNIPAM, PLGA–PEG–PLGA triblock copolymers, or MgFe-LDH-reinforced hydrogels can be engineered to provide an initial burst of angiogenic factors followed by sustained osteogenic release, thereby matching the sequential requirements of bone repair.

Weng et al. developed a thermosensitive hydrogel (CSP-LB) that remains a free-flowing solution at room temperature but rapidly transforms into a semi-solid gel once exposed to body temperature, allowing it to be injected easily and then solidify in situ to provide localized, sustained release of PDGF-BB and BMP-2. Animal experiments showed it increased new bone volume by 3.1-fold and mineralization density by 2.5-fold compared to controls, highlighting the advantages of responsive materials in precise bone regeneration (Lv et al., 2023). Enzyme-responsive platforms further enhance specificity by incorporating peptide linkers cleaved by cathepsin K, MMPs, or alkaline phosphatase—enzymes that are overexpressed in osteoporotic bone [218, 219]. These systems allow early osteoclast-targeted release when cathepsin K activity is high, followed by osteoblast-directed release as remodeling progresses, thus reconstructing a physiological sequence of bone resorption and formation.

Huang et al. developed a smart responsive bone repair scaffold based on carboxylated carbon nanotubes (CNT) (Huang et al., 2023). Integrating 0.5% w/v CNT into a carboxymethyl chitosan hydrogel created a 3D porous structure with mechanical enhancement (2.1-fold increase in elastic modulus) and electrochemical responsiveness. Carboxylation modification not only significantly reduces cytotoxicity, but also stably binds growth factors through covalent bonds for excellent bone regeneration effects. In vivo, the degradation of CNTs was more effective for p,f-CNT membranes, likely mediated by oxidation-induced myeloperoxidase (MPO) in the inflammatory conditions of neutrophils and macrophages. This shows the biodegradability feature of functionalized CNTs (Mata et al., 2015). This smart design integrating electroactive monitoring with osteogenic functions offers a new paradigm for precise control of bone regeneration. However, CNT scaffolds have shown great potential in preclinical studies, but their clinical transformation still needs to overcome multiple obstacles. The chiral separation of single-walled CNT relies on expensive density gradient centrifugation technology, which is difficult to industrialize. Long-term accumulation of nanomaterials may induce mitochondrial membrane potential collapse and ROS outbreak, and traditional toxicological methods are difficult to evaluate its size-dependent effect (Hsieh et al., 2014). Together, pH-, temperature-, and enzyme-responsive designs provide spatiotemporally controlled drug delivery tailored to the pathological characteristics of osteoporotic bone, making them promising tools for sequential anti-resorptive and osteogenic therapy.

Sequential release systems are attractive when remodeling needs temporal coordination (early anti-resorptive/anti-inflammatory control followed by osteogenic/angiogenic stimulation). Their main limitation is formulation complexity, as multi-compartment designs must maintain predictable kinetics under variable osteoporotic turnover and local pH.

Ligand- or membrane-mediated targeting increases local drug exposure and may reduce systemic toxicity, but targeting specificity can be compromised by heterogeneous cell states, competitive binding in vivo, and potential immunogenicity of complex surface coatings.

Surface topography and biomimetic coatings are particularly suitable for implants where long-term interface stability is critical; however, the durability of coatings under mechanical loading and the reproducibility of micro–nano features after sterilization remain key challenges.

Responsive smart materials (pH/enzyme/temperature/electroactive) provide on-demand release and may better match spatial heterogeneity of resorption sites, yet they require rigorous validation to avoid premature activation, incomplete triggering, or off-target release in non-skeletal tissues.

Overall, strategy selection should be indication-driven: load-bearing defects may prioritize interface engineering and mechanically robust scaffolds; diffuse metabolic osteoporosis may benefit more from targeted carriers and long-acting release; and complex defects with inflammatory/oxidative stress may require combined osteoimmunomodulatory and osteogenic cues. Standardized head-to-head comparisons and release-kinetics reporting will be critical to establish best-in-class approaches.

Clinical translation and evaluation systems

Animal model validation

Animal model validation is crucial for pre - clinical assessment of biomaterials' efficacy and safety in osteoporosis treatment, offering key evidence for subsequent development and clinical translation.

Osteoporosis animal model selection (OVX rats, aged primates)

The ovariectomized (OVX) rat model, created by surgically removing the ovaries to mimic postmenopausal estrogen decline, is widely used in osteoporosis research. It exhibits pathological features like reduced bone density and microstructure damage, similar to human osteoporosis. With advantages like low cost, simplicity, and short experimental duration, it's suitable for initial biomaterial efficacy screening (Lelovas et al., 2008; Yoon, 2012; Comelekoglu et al., 2007). Numerous studies have used OVX rats to evaluate various biomaterials, finding some can significantly increase bone density and improve bone structure (Yoon, 2012; Comelekoglu et al., 2007; Mathavan et al., 2015; Chai, 2024; Zheng et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024b; Tian et al., 2023). However, in OVX rat experiments, certain biomaterials may significantly increase bone density within 4 to 8 weeks, but in humans, this effect may take 6 to 12 months to manifest, and the peak effect may be much lower than expected due to the higher bone turnover rates of rats. Moreover, ERα expression is dominant in rat bone tissue, while ERβ expression is more abundant in human bone tissue. This leads to the fact that some estrogen analogues or regulators that are effective in OVX rats may not produce the same biological effects in humans (Chen et al., 2023b). It is important to note that most nanomaterial-based osteoporosis studies rely on OVX female models, and comparative data regarding sex- or age-related differences—such as responses in older male or non-OVX aged models—remain limited, highlighting a need for future research in this area.

Multimodal evaluation (μCT bone morphometry, biomechanics)

Micro - computed tomography (μCT) is key for assessing osteoporosis animal models and biomaterial efficacy (Haffer et al., 2023). It provides high - resolution 3D bone tissue images, enabling quantitative analysis of microstructural changes through parameters like bone volume fraction and trabecular thickness. In animal experiments, μCT allows non - invasive scanning of the same animal at different time points to monitor osteoporosis progression and bone tissue repair (Jang, 2024).

Biomechanical testing, measuring maximum load, elastic modulus, and fracture load, is essential for evaluating bone tissue mechanical properties (Schleifenbaum et al., 2021). By applying mechanical loads to animal bone samples and measuring deformation and failure, it quantifies bone strength and toughness, which is important for predicting biomaterials' clinical mechanical support. For example, biomechanical tests on OVX rat femurs showed some biomaterials significantly increased maximum load and elastic modulus, indicating enhanced bone tissue mechanical properties and supporting clinical application (Mackert, 2023; Stephens et al., 2021).

Long-term in vivo biosafety, osteointegration, and biodegradation of representative nanomaterials

Long-term animal studies are essential for evaluating the translational potential of nanomaterials in osteoporosis, as osteoporotic bone exhibits impaired healing, reduced vascularization, and altered metabolic turnover. Evidence from ovariectomized (OVX) rodent models, aged rabbits, and large-animal studies provides important insights into three critical aspects: systemic toxicity, osteointegration, and biodegradation kinetics. Multiple long-term studies (12–36 weeks) demonstrate that well-designed nanomaterials—including magnesium alloys, bioactive glass nanoparticles, and polymeric carriers such as PLGA or chitosan composites—exhibit excellent systemic biocompatibility in osteoporotic animals (Zhang et al., 2022c; Witte, 2005). Serial monitoring of hepatic and renal biochemical parameters, inflammatory cytokine profiles, and major-organ histology consistently shows no evidence of hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or chronic inflammation. In biodegradable systems such as Mg-based implants or PLGA microspheres, ion release and polymer byproducts remain within physiological tolerance, and no accumulation in soft tissues has been observed (Witte, 2005).

Long-term μCT and histological evaluations highlight robust osteointegration of nanomaterials under osteoporotic conditions. Porous titanium scaffolds fabricated by additive manufacturing, Sr/Zn-doped bioactive glass, and graphene-functionalized interfaces support progressive bone–implant contact and stable fixation over 12–24 weeks (Wang et al., 2022b). μCT analyses reveal sustained increases in bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness, and mineral deposition at the implant interface. Push-out and pull-out biomechanical tests also demonstrate enhanced interfacial strength, indicating that nanoscale topographies and ion-regulated microenvironments can overcome the impaired osteogenic capacity characteristic of osteoporotic bone. Biodegradable systems exhibit predictable degradation profiles that correlate with new bone formation. Magnesium alloys gradually corrode in vivo over 3–6 months, releasing Mg2+ ions that stimulate osteogenesis while maintaining adequate structural integrity during early healing. PLGA nanocarriers show composition-dependent degradation patterns, with 50:50 formulations degrading more rapidly and 75:25 compositions providing prolonged support and controlled drug release. Bioactive glass nanoparticles dissolve steadily, forming hydroxyapatite layers that promote mineral deposition and facilitate replacement by host bone over 12–20 weeks (Will et al., 2012; El-Rashidy et al., 2017). These results highlight the need to synchronize material degradation with the slower bone turnover rate observed in osteoporotic conditions. Overall, long-term studies support the biosafety and functional integration of diverse nanomaterials in osteoporotic models. However, differences in animal species, implantation sites, and follow-up duration still limit cross-study comparability. Future research should standardize long-term evaluation protocols to facilitate eventual clinical translation.

Clinical Research advances

Injectable Bone Cement (PMMA/CPC) in Vertebroplasty

Bone cement, mainly consisting of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) and calcium phosphate cement (CPC), is widely used in vertebroplasty. PMMA has fast curing and high strength, quickly stabilizing fractured vertebrae (González et al., 2023). However, it has biocompatibility issues and doesn't form a biological bond with bone tissue. CPC has good bioactivity and osteoconductivity, as it can degrade and be replaced by new bone tissue. New injectable bone cement composites, such as CPC with bioactive factors or drugs, are being developed to improve therapeutic outcomes (Belaid et al., 2022).

Drug - eluting implants in fracture internal fixation clinical trials

Drug - eluting implants show promise in fracture internal fixation. Based on traditional internal fixation devices (plates, nails, intramedullary nails), they incorporate drug delivery systems to load anti - osteoporosis drugs or antibiotics. They provide slow drug release at the fracture site for local treatment (Yang, 2023). Studies indicate drug – eluting plates can continuously release bisphosphonates at the fracture site, inhibiting osteoclasts, reducing bone resorption, promoting fracture healing, and lowering re - fracture risk (Aggarwal et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). Drug - eluting intramedullary nails are also gradually being applied in clinical trials, offering comprehensive treatment for patients with fractures and osteoporosis. Although several biomaterial-based strategies such as bone cements and drug-eluting implants have progressed into early clinical use, it is important to note that nanomaterial-specific osteoporosis therapies have not yet entered substantial FDA or EMA-registered clinical trials, and significant translational challenges—such as long-term safety evaluation, biodegradation control, and compliance with regulatory standards for implantable biomaterials—remain to be addressed.

Personalized treatment

3D – printed customized scaffolds (matching patient - specific bone defects)

3D printing technology offers a powerful tool for personalized osteoporosis treatment (Lei et al., 2023). Using patient imaging data (CT or MRI), a 3D model of the bone defect can be accurately constructed. Customized scaffolds perfectly matching the defect can then be fabricated via 3D printing. These scaffolds provide precise mechanical support while promoting bone tissue regeneration and repair (Wang, 2021b; Wu et al., 2024b). 3D printing also allows customization of material composition, porosity, and pore size based on specific patient needs.

Precision drug release based on bone metabolism biomarkers

Bone metabolism biomarkers are biological molecules reflecting bone metabolism status, including bone formation and resorption markers (Mohammadi et al., 2024; Brown et al., 2022). Detecting these biomarkers in patients allows real - time monitoring of bone metabolism, enabling precise drug release. Some smart drug delivery systems can automatically adjust drug release rates based on changes in bone metabolism biomarker concentrations (Mohamad et al., 2021; Luo, 2024; Singh et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2021). This precise drug release system based on bone metabolism biomarkers achieves personalized and precise drug therapy, improves treatment efficacy, and reduces drug side effects.

Barriers to clinical translation and practical de-risking strategies

Despite rapid progress in nano-enabled delivery systems and regenerative nanocomposites, their clinical adoption in osteoporosis has been slower than the pace of preclinical innovation. In most cases, the limiting factors are not the biological rationale per se, but cross-cutting translation bottlenecks spanning (i) manufacturing scalability and CMC maturity, (ii) regulatory classification and evidentiary expectations, and (iii) residual uncertainty regarding toxicity and long-term safety of nanoscale components and their degradation products.

Scalability remains a central barrier, particularly for multi-component nanosystems that rely on laboratory-scale synthesis, multi-step surface functionalization, or poorly controlled self-assembly. Batch-to-batch variability in particle size distribution, drug loading, surface chemistry, and release profiles can translate into clinically meaningful differences in biodistribution and efficacy. Moreover, clinical-grade manufacturing must accommodate sterile processing, endotoxin control, solvent/impurity limits, stability during storage and transport (e.g., aggregation, hydrolysis, ligand desorption), and robust in-process analytics for critical quality attributes.

Regulatory hurdles are amplified by the frequent classification of these products as drug-device combination systems (e.g., drug-eluting implants, bioactive cements, and scaffold-carrier hybrids). Such products must satisfy both device requirements (mechanical integrity, wear/fatigue behavior, extractables/leachables, and sterilization validation) and drug requirements (dose uniformity, release specifications, and pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics where applicable). Complexity-driven uncertainty – for example, biomimetic membranes, multiple ligands, or nano-additives embedded within degradable matrices - increases the burden of physicochemical characterization, comparability packages after process changes, and risk-based justification of the proposed mechanism and control strategy.

Long-term safety remains a decisive challenge. For systemically administered nanocarriers, translation requires evidence beyond short-term cytotoxicity, including chronic biodistribution, clearance kinetics, immunotoxicity, and potential accumulation in the mononuclear phagocyte system. The accumulation of nanoparticles in the body is mainly driven by the enhanced penetration and retention effect (EPR), which enables nanoparticles to penetrate the “gaps” in the tumor blood vessel walls and accumulate in tumor tissues (Jeon et al., 2022). Nanoparticles can activate or inhibit the immune system to achieve chronic toxic effects, influencing macrophage polarization and T cell function. Accordingly, extended-duration studies with clinically relevant dosing, large-animal validation when feasible, and standardized safety panels (organ histology, hematology/biochemistry, and fate tracking of nano-components) are essential to establish acceptable margins for chronic use.

To enhance clinical readiness, a pragmatic de-risking workflow can be adopted: (1) start with an indication-driven design that matches the platform to the clinical use-case (local defect repair vs. systemic disease modification); (2) prioritize design simplification by minimizing non-essential components and selecting materials with precedented clinical use when possible; (3) define platform-specific critical quality attributes and acceptance windows early (size, charge, drug content, release kinetics, mechanical properties, and degradation behavior); and (4) align preclinical efficacy with a translation-grade safety and manufacturability package, including sterilization compatibility and stability testing. This integrated approach can help transform promising nanomaterial concepts into reproducible, regulatorily tractable, and clinically implementable osteoporosis therapies.

Clinical balancing of osteoclast inhibition and osteoblast stimulation

Because osteoclast-mediated resorption and osteoblast-mediated formation are biologically coupled, the clinical goal is not maximal suppression of remodeling, but restoration of a safe and productive turnover state that reduces fracture risk while preserving microdamage repair and material replacement.

Excessive or prolonged suppression of bone remodeling can impair the renewal of aged bone, promote accumulation of microcracks, delay fracture healing, and increase the risk of rare but clinically important complications (e.g., atypical femoral fractures and osteonecrosis of the jaw) that are linked to overly low turnover under potent anti-resorptive exposure.

From a practical standpoint, balancing dual strategies is best guided by indication and baseline turnover phenotype. In high-turnover states, an early phase of targeted anti-resorptive control can rapidly reduce excessive resorption and local acidity, whereas patients with very high fracture risk and low formation may benefit from an anabolic-first approach followed by anti-resorptive maintenance to preserve gains. For local defect repair, transient peri-defect osteoclast attenuation may be desirable, but complete or long-lasting osteoclast ablation should be avoided because osteoclast activity also provides coupling signals needed for subsequent formation and scaffold replacement.

Accordingly, clinical translation of nano-enabled platforms should explicitly consider therapeutic windows and monitoring strategies (e.g., bone turnover markers such as serum CTX and PINP, imaging-based structural readouts, and when available histomorphometric indices) to ensure that resorption is moderated rather than eliminated. Time-bounded or feedback-responsive regimens that permit remodeling recovery are conceptually aligned with current risk-mitigation practices for long-term anti-resorptive therapy.

These considerations have direct design implications for nanomaterials: platforms that enable site-selective delivery to resorption lacunae, staged release (anti-resorptive then osteogenic/angiogenic cues), and dose-limited exposure can support the intended balance. Conversely, highly persistent formulations with uncertain clearance, or designs that deliver strong anti-resorptive payloads continuously without off-switch mechanisms, may increase the likelihood of over-suppression and should be evaluated with extended-duration safety and turnover endpoints. Conclusion

Osteoporosis remains a prevalent, multifactorial skeletal disorder in which sustained fracture prevention and local defect repair are constrained by systemic exposure, limited site selectivity, and incomplete restoration of coupled remodeling. The nano-enabled strategies surveyed herein—spanning bone-targeted drug-delivery systems and implantable/regenerative nanocomposites—can increase local drug concentration, impose temporal control over resorption-versus-formation cues, and engineer osteoimmunomodulatory microenvironments. However, the transition from preclinical efficacy to routine clinical use is primarily limited by translational bottlenecks, including scalable GMP manufacturing with tight control of critical quality attributes, regulatory clarity for drug–device combination products, and convincing evidence of long-term safety (clearance, wear/debris, ion release, and chronic immunotoxicity). Clinically, nano-enabled interventions should aim for tunable, time-bounded anti-resorptive effects that preserve physiological remodeling while augmenting osteogenesis, ideally guided by turnover markers and fracture-risk stratification. In particular, progress toward clinical implementation will depend on scalable GMP manufacturing with well-defined critical quality attributes, a clear regulatory strategy for drug-device combination products, and rigorous long-term safety assessment of nanoparticle fate and degradation products.

Looking ahead, near-term clinical translation is most plausibly driven by platforms that combine strong biological rationale with clinical precedent and CMC/regulatory tractability. In this context, drug-eluting implants and injectable bone cements based on established matrices (e.g., titanium, PMMA/CPC, and calcium phosphate) paired with clinically validated anti-resorptive or anabolic agents; ion-doped calcium phosphate and bioactive glass systems (e.g., Sr/Zn/Mg) delivering well-characterized inorganic cues; and biodegradable polymer depots (e.g., PLGA microspheres and thermogelling hydrogels) enabling local, dose-limited delivery of approved agents (e.g., bisphosphonates and PTH analogues) represent the most immediately actionable candidates.

To accelerate translation while avoiding over-suppression of remodeling, future work should prioritize standardized, head-to-head evaluation under osteoporotic models with harmonized endpoints (μCT, biomechanics, and turnover markers) and, where feasible, in vitro–in vivo correlations for release/degradation with extended follow-up to capture remodeling recovery; remodeling-preserving dual modulation via defined dose windows and temporal sequencing—favoring staged, time-bounded, or feedback-responsive regimens guided by turnover phenotype and fracture-risk stratification; and CMC/QbD-driven manufacturability, including early definition of critical quality attributes (size distribution, surface chemistry, drug content, release kinetics, mechanical performance, and degradation behavior), scalable processes, sterilization compatibility, and shelf-life stability. Long-term safety and regulatory de-risking should be treated as co-primary objectives, including chronic biodistribution/clearance studies of nano-additives, assessment of particulate shedding/wear and cumulative ion exposure, and early alignment with combination-product requirements. More complex multifunctional approaches (e.g., multi-ligand biomimetic coatings, fully responsive theranostic platforms, and gene/nucleic-acid nanostructures) remain scientifically compelling but will likely require longer development cycles due to higher characterization burdens, CMC complexity, and residual uncertainty in chronic safety and regulatory tractability.
