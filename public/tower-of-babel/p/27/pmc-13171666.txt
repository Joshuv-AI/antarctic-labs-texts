Nicotinamide N-Methyltransferase Inhibition Mitigates Cerulein-Induced Pancreatic Fibrosis via Galectin-3-Mediated Regulation of Stellate Cell Activation and Macrophage M2 Polarization in Mice
Authors: Niansheng Ren, Ruixi Li, Qiang Tao, Shengjie Hong, Jicai Wang, Jiandong Zha, Shikai Wang, Guangquan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is a chronic disease characterized by pancreatic fibrosis driven by pancreatic stellate cell activation and M2 polarization of macrophages. Nicotinamide n-methyltransferase (NNMT) is a methylase critically involved in fibrosis. Galectin-3 (LGALS3), a member of the galectin family, drives M2 macrophage polarization. This study explores the roles of NNMT and LGALS3 in CP. CP was induced in C57BL/6 mice via repeated intraperitoneal injections of 50 µg/kg cerulein. NNMT was upregulated in pancreatic tissues of CP mice, especially in mouse pancreatic stellate cells (mPSCs). Functional inhibition of NNMT in mPSCs, via lentiviral short-hairpin RNA-mediated knockdown or treatment with NNMT inhibitor, suppressed mPSC activation, proliferation, and migration. Moreover, conditioned medium from mPSCs with NNMT inhibition reduced M2 polarization of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). In vivo, intraperitoneal administration of 20 mg/kg NNMT inhibitor for 28 days alleviated pancreatic collagen deposition and macrophage M2 polarization, resulting in reduced pancreatic fibrosis. RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis identified LGALS3 as a potential downstream target of NNMT. NNMT inhibition downregulated LGALS3 expression in mPSCs, and chromatin immunoprecipitation - quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR) confirmed that this suppression was associated with increased trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me3) enrichment at the LGALS3 promoter. Lentivirus-mediated LGALS3 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of NNMT knockdown on BMDM M2 polarization. In conclusion, inhibition of NNMT alleviates mPSC activation and suppresses mPSC-induced macrophage M2 polarization via H3K27me3-mediated repression of LGALS3 transcription, with the ultimate effect of mitigating cerulein-induced pancreatic fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02491-w.

Introduction

Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is a progressive chronic inflammatory disorder characterized by persistent parenchymal inflammation, irreversible structural damage, and interstitial fibrosis of the pancreas [1, 2]. Pancreatic fibrosis, often accompanied by exocrine and endocrine dysfunction, serves as a key driver of debilitating clinical manifestations, including diabetes mellitus, intractable abdominal pain, and an elevated risk of pancreatic cancer [3]. Epidemiologically, CP is linked to multiple risk factors, with smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, and genetic predispositions being the most well-recognized [4, 5]. Over the past few decades, advances in research and medical technology have transformed the therapeutic paradigm for CP, shifting from a predominantly surgical resection-centered approach to a multidisciplinary strategy integrating pharmacological interventions and endoscopic procedures [6, 7]. For instance, antioxidant therapy has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating symptoms in approximately 50% of CP patients [2]. Additionally, emerging evidence has highlighted the potential therapeutic value of probiotics in CP management [8, 9]. Recent preclinical studies [10, 11] have explored gene therapy as a promising curative route, such as pancreas-directed AAV-mediated delivery of functional genes that target key pathogenic pathways in CP.

Pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) are recognized as the primary drivers of fibrogenesis in CP [12]. Within the persistent inflammatory microenvironment of the pancreas, PSCs undergo activation and engage in crosstalk with other cell types, particularly macrophages, thereby promoting the progression of pancreatic fibrosis [13]. Consequently, targeting PSC activation-related signaling pathways and macrophage-mediated inflammatory and fibrotic processes has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for CP [14]. Nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT), a methyltransferase enzyme, catalyzes the methylation of nicotinamide to facilitate its systemic elimination [15]. NNMT overexpression results in excessive nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide consumption, contributing to the development of hepatic steatosis and liver fibrosis [16]. NNMT also modulates the biological processes underlying renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis [17]. Knockdown of NNMT markedly suppressed the lipofibroblast-myofibroblast phenotypic transition and the expression of extracellular matrix proteins [18]. Beyond fibrosis, NNMT plays a role in immune regulation. Gallbladder cancer cells with high NNMT expression induce M2-type macrophage polarization through the secretion of interleukin-6 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor [19]. NNMT promotes the polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype, thereby facilitating the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cells [20]. However, despite its established roles in fibrosis and immune modulation in other tissues, the functional significance of NNMT in CP remains largely unexplored.

Galectin 3 (LGALS3) is a member of the galectin family. LGALS3 exhibits diverse functions and participates in cell growth, differentiation, and immune regulation [21–23]. In the pancreas, LGALS3 expression patterns differ markedly between normal and diseased states. In normal human pancreatic tissue, LGALS3 is abundantly expressed in the cytoplasmic and nuclear regions of approximately 50% of cells [24, 25]. In contrast, in tissues from CP, LGALS3 shows increased and more uniform expression in cells [24, 25]. Functionally, LGALS3 activates PSCs to secrete inflammatory cytokines [26]. Knockout of LGALS3 impedes the progression of pancreatic cancer and augments the effectiveness of immunotherapy [27]. In addition, LGALS3 modulates immune responses by promoting M2 macrophage polarization [28, 29], a process critical to fibro-inflammatory cascades in CP. Epigenetically, the LGALS3 promoter is subject to trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me3) modification [30], a repressive histone mark known to regulate gene transcription. Notably, recent evidence indicates that NNMT can upregulate downstream gene transcription by inhibiting H3K27me3 modification [31]. This raises intriguing questions: whether NNMT directly regulates LGALS3 transcription, and more broadly, what roles NNMT and LGALS3 collectively play in M2 macrophage polarization induced by PSCs in the context of CP.

This study aims to elucidate the mechanistic role of NNMT in the pathogenesis of CP, with a specific focus on two key pathways: NNMT-mediated activation of PSCs and the subsequent promotion of pancreatic fibrosis, as well as its involvement in M2 macrophage polarization. Additionally, this study investigates whether LGALS3 acts as a downstream effector through which NNMT modulates CP progression. Collectively, these findings seek to establish a theoretical framework for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for CP.

Materials and Methods

Animal

All animal experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No.2025-054-01). All animals were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Chinese Medicine Technology Co., LTD. A total of 108 female C57BL/6 mice were used in this study. Six- to eight-week-old mice (18 ± 1 g) were employed for animal experiments and the isolation of pancreatic stellate cells, while four-week-old mice (13 ± 1 g) were utilized for the isolation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). A murine model of cerulein-induced CP was established as previously described [13, 32]. Briefly, mice received intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (C860403, Macklin, Shanghai, China) at a dose of 50 µg/kg, administered six times daily at 1-hour intervals. This protocol was performed three days per week for four consecutive weeks.

For in vivo inhibition of NNMT, C57BL/6 mice were given intraperitoneal injections of 20 mg/kg NNMTi (5-Amino-1-methylquinolin-1-ium iodide, A931862, Macklin) [31] once daily for 4 weeks, starting 24 h prior to the first cerulein injection [32]. Three days after the final cerulein injection, mice were euthanized, and pancreatic tissues were harvested.

Cell Isolation and Identification

Mouse pancreatic stellate cells (mPSCs) were isolated using the outgrowth explant technique [33]. Pancreatic tissues from sham or CP mice were dissected into 0.5–1 mm³ fragments with ophthalmic scissors. These tissue pieces were placed in cell culture plates and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (G4611, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (11011 − 8611, Tianhang) and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine (BS179, Biosharp, Hefei, Anhui, China). The tissue blocks were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator (HF-90, Lishen, Shanghai, China), and the medium was replaced after 18 h. After 24 h, the tissues were transferred to new culture plates. MPSCs migrated out from the tissue blocks approximately three days later. Activated mPSCs were identified via alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) immunofluorescence staining.

Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of normal 4-week-old female C57BL/6 mice [34]. Bone marrow cells were then induced to differentiate into BMDMs in DMEM medium (G4511, Servicebio) containing 10 ng/mL murine M-CSF (HY-P7085, MCE, SAN Jose, CA, USA) for 5–7 days. Macrophages were identified by flow cytometry for CD11b and F4/80 expression.

Lentivirus Infection

Lentiviral vectors were constructed by Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd, including those carrying the coding sequence for short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting NNMT (pLVX-shRNA1, BR004) and those harboring the full-length LGALS3 coding sequence for overexpression (pLVX-IRES-puro, BR025). MPSCs were infected with the lentiviruses, and 72 h post-infection, the cells were harvested for detection.

At 72 h post-infection, the culture medium of mPSCs from CP mice was replaced with fresh medium. The conditional medium was then collected after an additional 48-hour re-culture period. BMDMs were harvested 24 h after treatment with the mPSC-derived conditional medium.

Cell Treatment with NNMT Inhibitor (NNMTi)

MPSCs isolated from CP mice were incubated with 80 µM NNMTi (A931862, Macklin) for 72 h [31, 35], after which the cells were harvested for detection. Separately, CP mouse-derived mPSCs were exposed to 80 µM NNMTi for 72 h. Following this treatment, the culture medium was replaced, and the conditioned medium was collected after an additional 48-hour culturing period. BMDMs were harvested 24 h after being treated with the conditioned medium derived from mPSCs of CP mice.

Flow Cytometry

For F4/80 or CD11b and CD206 double staining, the procedure began with preparing a single-cell suspension from pancreatic tissues. 5 µL of F4/80 antibody (E-AB-F0995D, Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China) was added to the cell suspension, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Following cell membrane permeabilization, 5 µL of CD11b (E-AB-F1081C, Elabscience) or CD206 antibody (E-AB-F1135E, Elabscience) was added, and the sample was incubated in the dark for another 30 min. After incubation, the cells were resuspended in staining buffer and analyzed using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

For CD206 single staining in BMDMs, 5 µL of CD206 antibody was added to the BMDM single-cell suspension. The mixture was gently mixed and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The BMDMs were then washed with staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry (NovoCyte, Agilent).

EdU Staining

For EdU staining, cells were incubated with 10 µM EdU staining solution (E-CK-A370, Elabscience) for 90 min (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After harvesting, cells were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and the cells were resuspended in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by another centrifugation (300×g, 5 min). Cells were then fixed in 1 mL PBS with 4% paraformaldehyde (room temperature, dark, 15 min), centrifuged (300×g, 5 min), and washed twice with 1% BSA-PBS (300×g, 5 min each). Subsequently, cells were permeabilized with 0.5 mL 1% saponin-PBS (room temperature, 20 min), centrifuged (300×g, 5 min), and incubated with prepared Click reaction mixture (room temperature, dark, 30 min). After centrifugation (300×g, 5 min), cells were washed with 1% saponin-PBS (300×g, 5 min), resuspended in an appropriate volume of 1% BSA-PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence

Mouse pancreatic tissues were prepared into 5 μm paraffin sections. MPSC slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (80096618, Sinopharm, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. After removing the paraformaldehyde, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (ST795, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 30 min. All tissue and cell samples were blocked with 1% BSA (A602440-0050, Sangon, Shanghai, China), followed by incubation in a humidified chamber at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies were then added dropwise to fully cover the sections and incubated at room temperature for 90 min. After immunostaining, cell nuclei in the tissue sections were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (D106471-5 mg, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) for 5 min. Histological Staining

Mouse pancreatic tissue blocks were paraffin-embedded and sectioned into 5-µm sections. Pancreatic tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated via successive immersion in xylene (1330-20-7, Aladdin) and gradient ethanol solutions (10009218, Sinopharm). Subsequently, the sections were stained with hematoxylin (H8070, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 min and eosin (A600190, Sangon) for 3 min. Following eosin staining, the sections were dehydrated in gradient ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a drop of neutral resin. Based on previous reports [36], the three key pathological features of pancreatic tissue were observed and scored according to the HE staining results: infiltration of inflammatory cells, atrophy of acini, and fibrosis.

For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, mouse pancreatic tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval following deparaffinization. To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, tissue sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (10011218, Sinopharm) at room temperature for 15 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked by treating the sections with 1% BSA solution, followed by incubation at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with F4/80 primary antibody (1:200, 28463-1-AP, Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight, then with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:200, #31460, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 60 min. Finally, these slices were treated with hematoxylin for 3 min to stain the nuclei.

Transwell Assay

The transwell chambers (14342, LABSELECT, Hefei, Anhui, China) were placed in a 24-well plate. The lower chambers were filled with 800 µL of culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and 200 µL of mPSC suspension (6 × 10³ cells per well) was added to the upper chambers. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. After incubation, the transwell chambers were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet staining solution (0528, Amresco, Solon, OH, USA) for 5 min. Finally, the cells on the lower surface of the microporous membrane were counted manually under an inverted microscope (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) at 200× magnification.

Immunoblots

Pancreatic tissue and cell samples were lysed in RIPA buffer containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, P0100, Solarbio). Proteins were subjected to separation via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Following the transfer procedure, the PVDF membranes were submerged in a blocking buffer (SW3010, Solarbio) and incubated for 1 h to block non-specific binding sites. The PVDF membranes were incubated sequentially with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:3000, SE134, Solarbio) or goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:3000, SE131, Solarbio) at room temperature for 1 h. The enhanced chemiluminescence solution (PE0010, Solarbio) was subsequently applied to the PVDF membrane to visualize the target proteins. The primary antibodies are as follow: NNMT antibody (1:3000, 15123-1-AP, Proteintech), α-SMA antibody (1:20000, 67735-1-Ig, Proteintech), H3K27me3 antibody (1:500, DF6941, Affinity, Liyang, Jiangsu, China), Histone H3 antibody (1:1000, AF0863, Affinity), LGALS3 antibody (1:2000, A11198, Abclonal), Arg1 antibody (1:1000, A1847, Abclonal), MRC1 antibody (1:5000, A21014, Abclonal), GAPDH antibody (1:10000, AF7021, Affinity).

Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tissue and cell samples using the TRIpure total RNA extraction reagent (RP1001, BioTeke, Beijing, China). Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA by the All-in-One First-Strand SuperMix (MD80101, Magen, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). Real-time PCR was performed on an ExicyclerTM 96 fluorescence quantifier (BIONEER, Daejeon, South Korea) using 2×Fast Taq plus PCR Master Mix (BL1014, Biosharp) and SYBR Green (SR4110, Solarbio). The primers were synthesized by General Biology, Inc., and the primer sequences were as follows: LGALS3 forward (F): TGTTGCCTTCCACTTTA, LGALS3 reverse (R): GAATGGTTTGCCACTCT; NNMT F: CCCCACCATCTATCAGC, NNMT R: TTCCTTCTTCAGCCATTT; transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1) F: GCGGTGCTCGCTTTGTA, TGFB1 R: CACTGCTTCCCGAATGTC; arginase 1 (Arg1) F: TATCTGCCAAAGACATCG, Arg1 R: ATCACCTTGCCAATCCC; mannose receptor C-type 1 (MRC1) F: TCCGTCACCCTGTATGCC, MRC1 R: TGCTCCACAATCCCGAAC; GAPDH F: AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG, GAPDH R: TGTAGACCATGTAGTTGAGGTCA.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation - Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP-qPCR was performed to determine the enrichment level of H3K27me3 on the LGALS3 promoter in mPSCs. Cross-linked cell lysates were subjected to sonication and immunoprecipitation following the manufacturer’s instructions of the ChIP Assay Kit (P2078, Beyotime). The antibody against H3K27me3 (1:50, DF6941, Affinity) was used as the primary antibody for ChIP. Purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR using 2×Fast Taq Plus PCR Master Mix (BL1014, Biosharp). The ChIP primer sequences were as follows: F: GCTGCTTTGGACCTAATA and R: AAATCAAGAGGACAGGGT.

RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

Following a 72-hour treatment of mPSCs derived from CP mice with either NNMTi or DMSO, total RNA was extracted from the cells. Library preparation and sequencing were carried out by Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd (China). The reference genome was indexed, and paired-end clean reads were aligned to GRCm39 using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). HTSeq (version 2.0.2) was applied to quantify the number of reads mapped to each gene. Differential expression analysis between the two cell groups was performed using DESeq2 software (version 1.24.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened based on the criteria of |log2FoldChange|>1 and p value < 0.05. ClusterProfiler (version 4.6.2) software was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

Protein-coding genes related to macrophage polarization were retrieved and downloaded from the GeneCard database [37]. The online tool Venny 2.1 [38] was used to identify the overlapping factors between macrophage-related GO terms (GO:1905517, macrophage migration; GO:0048246, macrophage chemotaxis) and genes obtained from GeneCards using the keyword “Macrophage polarization, protein coding gene”. The enrichment of H3K27me3 modification on the promoters of these intersecting genes was queried in the Cistrome Data Browser (http://cistrome.org/db/#/) under the entry ID: 57,002-H3K27me3-ChIP-seq.

Detection of Lipase and Amylase Activities

A Lipase Assay Kit (A054-2-1) and an α-Amylase Assay Kit (C016-1-1) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute Co., Ltd. Serum samples and assay reagents were added to 96-well plates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For lipase activity detection, samples were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 1 min, and absorbance at 580 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Biotek, USA). For α-amylase activity detection, absorbance at 660 nm was measured in a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shanghai Youke Company). Lipase and α-amylase activities were finally calculated using the manufacturer’s provided formulas.

ELISA

To remove cellular sediment, supernatants from pancreatic stellate cells were centrifuged at 300 ×g for 10 min. Standard curves were then prepared in strict accordance with the Mouse LGALS3 ELISA Kit manufacturer’s operating instructions (EM1054, Fine Test, Wuhan, Hubei, China). The concentration of the LGALS3 was calculated via the standard curve, where the standard concentration was plotted on the ordinate and the calibrated OD value on the abscissa.

Cell Viability Analysis

Cells were treated with varying concentrations of NNMTi and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After a 72-hour incubation period, 10 µL of CCK‑8 reagent (Solarbio, CA1210) was added to each well. The cells were then returned to the incubator for an additional 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA).

Data analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Prior to statistical analysis, data were first subjected to normality test and homogeneity of variance test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-test was applied. For two groups with unequal variances, the unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction was used to analyze differences. Differences among multiple groups were assessed by Ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NNMT is Pregulated in the Pancreatic Tissues of Mice with Cerulein-Induced CP

The cerulein-induced CP mouse model was established via intraperitoneal injection (Fig. 1A). Compared with sham mice, CP mice exhibited significantly elevated serum α-amylase and lipase activities (Fig. 1B-C). CP mice showed higher pathological scores, with hemorrhage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and acinar cell atrophy in pancreatic tissues (Fig. 1D). The pancreatic tissues of CP mice exhibited marked fibrous deposition (Fig. 1E). IHC analysis further demonstrated an increase in F4/80-positive cells within the pancreatic tissues of CP mice (Fig. 1F), indicative of macrophage infiltration. The percentage of F4/80 and CD206 double-positive cells among F4/80-positive cells significantly increased, suggesting that cerulein treatment promoted M2 polarization of pancreatic macrophages (Fig. 1G-H). Notably, NNMT expression was upregulated in pancreatic tissues of CP mice (Fig. 1I-J), particularly in α-SMA-positive cells (Fig. 1K), a marker of PSC activation. In contrast, NNMT expression was lower in desmin-positive cells, representing resting PSCs. These results indicate that NNMT expression is upregulated in activated mPSCs of CP mice.

NNMT Knockdown Inhibits the Activation, Proliferation, and Migration of mPSCs from CP Mice

The effect of NNMT on mPSCs was explored through the isolation of mPSCs from sham or CP mice and the interference of NNMT expression (Fig. 2A, S1A). Lentiviral infection with NNMT-targeting plasmids effectively reduced NNMT mRNA and protein levels in mPSCs (Fig. 2B). In line with their activation status, mPSCs from sham mice showed robust desmin expression, whereas those from CP mice exhibited high α-SMA expression (Fig.S1B). NNMT knockdown suppressed the activation of mPSCs (Fig. 2C). CP-derived mPSCs displayed elevated Collagen I expression (Fig. S1C), which was reversed by NNMT knockdown (Fig. 2D), indicating reduced collagen deposition. Furthermore, the decreased proportion of EdU-positive cells revealed that NNMT downregulation inhibited mPSC proliferation (Fig. 2E, S1D). Consistently, NNMT knockdown impaired the migratory capacity of CP-derived mPSCs across the membrane (Fig. 2F, S1E). Collectively, these findings indicate that NNMT knockdown suppresses the activation, proliferation, and migratory capacity of mPSCs derived from CP mice.

NNMTi Suppresses the Activation, Proliferation, and Migration of mPSCs

Exposure of CP-derived mPSCs to NNMTi led to the inhibition of NNMT activity (Fig. 3A). 80 µM NNMTi was selected for mPSC treatment (Fig. 3B). In line with the findings from NNMT knockdown experiments, NNMTi suppressed the activation of mPSCs (Fig. 3C) and decreased the deposition of Collagen I (Fig. 3D). Additionally, inhibition of NNMT activity resulted in decreased proliferation (Fig. 3E) and migration (Fig. 3F) of mPSC. These results suggest that inhibition of NNMT activity restricts the activation, proliferation and migration of mPSCs derived from CP mice.

Inhibition of NNMT in CP mPSCs Limits M2 Polarization of Mouse Macrophages

BMDMs were cultured in the conditioned medium collected from mPSCs isolated from sham-operated or CP mice (Fig. 4A, S2A). BMDM identity was confirmed via flow cytometric analysis of CD11b and F4/80 expression, with the percentage of CD11b and F4/80 double-positive cells exceeding 90% (Fig.S2B). Conditioned medium from CP-derived mPSCs significantly increased the proportion of CD206-positive BMDMs (Fig.S2C), along with upregulated mRNA levels of Arg1, MRC1, and TGFB1—key markers of macrophage M2 polarization (Fig.S2D). Lentivirus-mediated NNMT knockdown in mPSCs reduced CD206-positive BMDMs (Fig. 4B) and concomitantly downregulated mRNA levels of these M2 markers (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that NNMT knockdown in CP mouse-derived mPSCs inhibits M2 polarization of BMDMs. To further validate this effect, BMDMs were cultured in the conditioned medium of CP-derived mPSCs treated with NNMTi (Fig. 4D). Consistent with the knockdown experiment, NNMTi reduced the number of CD206-positive cells in BMDMs (Fig. 4E) and the mRNA levels of Arg1, MRC1, and TGFB1 in BMDMs (Fig. 4F). These findings imply that the inhibition of NNMT in mPSCs from CP mice restrains M2 polarization of BMDMs.

NNMTi Alleviates Pancreatic Fibrosis in Mice with CP

Intraperitoneal injection of NNMTi suppressed the protein activity of NNMT in CP mice (Fig. 5A). The protein level of α-SMA was decreased in mice treated with NNMTi, indicating inhibited mPSC activation (Fig. 5B). Concomitantly, NNMTi mitigated cerulein-induced pancreatic acinar cell injury in mice (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, NNMTi attenuated pancreatic fibrosis (Fig. 5D) and reduced the Collagen I-positive area (Fig. 5E) in CP mice. These results suggest that inhibition of NNMT reduces pancreatic fibrosis caused by mPSC activation in CP mice.

NNMT Inhibition Promotes H3K27me3-Mediated Repression of LGALS3 Transcription

The effect of NNMT inhibition on the transcriptional profile of mPSCs was observed by mRNA sequencing. Figure 7A illustrates the PCA results of NNMTi-treated cells and their control cells. DEGs were screened using the criteria of |log2FoldChange| > 1 and p-value < 0.05 (Fig. 7B). The differentially downregulated genes were enriched in pathways associated with macrophages, fibrosis, and the extracellular matrix, among others (Fig. 7C). GSEA results indicated that upon NNMT inhibition, biological processes including collagen biosynthesis, macrophage activation, extracellular matrix regulation, and the inflammatory response were suppressed in CP mPSCs (Fig. 7D). A cross-referencing analysis of the top two significantly enriched macrophage-related GO terms from DEGs with macrophage polarization-associated genes from GeneCards identified seven core genes: CCL3, CSF1R, MIF, CMKLR1, TREM1, C5AR1, and LGALS3 (Fig. 7E). The expression of these seven genes was downregulated in CP mPSCs with NNMTi (Fig. 7F). By comparing H3K27me3-ChIP-seq data of C57BL/6 mice bone marrow cells from the CistromeDB database (ID: 57002), H3K27me3 modification was found to be enriched on the promoters of CMKLR1 and LGALS3. CMKLR1 has been primarily implicated in macrophage M1 polarization [39]. Here, LGALS3 was selected as a downstream factor of NNMT for further investigation (Fig. 7G). LGALS3 expression was reduced in both lentivirus-mediated NNMT knockdown and NNMTi-induced inhibition of NNMT activity (Fig. 7H-I). Inhibition of NNMT elevated H3K27me3 levels in mPSCs (Fig. 7J), with a specific enrichment at the promoter region of LGALS3 (Fig. 7K). These findings suggest that NNMT inhibition reduces LGALS3 expression through elevated H3K27me3 levels at promoter of LGALS3.

Discussion

PSCs and macrophages are key cellular players in the complex pathological cascade of CP, where their dynamic interplay critically influences disease progression [13, 40]. Targeting this intricate cellular crosstalk is therefore emerging as a pivotal strategy for developing effective CP therapies. In the present study, we observed that NNMT expression is significantly upregulated in pancreatic tissues of cerulein-induced CP mice. Functional experiments demonstrated that NNMT inhibition effectively attenuated PSC activation, migration, and collagen fibril synthesis in CP mice. Concurrently, NNMT suppression impaired macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, our data revealed that NNMT inhibition enhances H3K27me3 modification at the LGALS3 promoter, thereby suppressing LGALS3 transcription. This epigenetic regulation directly blocks M2 polarization of BMDMs induced by CP-derived PSCs, disrupting the pro-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory signaling cascades within the pancreatic microenvironment (Fig. 9).

Under physiological conditions, PSCs remain in a quiescent state. In response to pancreatic injury or exposure to multiple stimuli, PSCs become activated and transform into myofibroblast-like cells, with the concurrent expression of fibrotic markers, notably α-SMA and type I collagen. In our experimental design, CP in mice was induced via cerulein administration. Consistent with the findings reported by Chen et al. [41], cerulein administration induced the activation of mPSCs and fibrosis of pancreatic tissue in mice. NNMT participates in the fibrotic mechanisms underlying various pathological conditions. Enhanced NNMT expression within the kidneys dysregulates nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and methionine metabolism, which in turn accelerates the process of renal fibrosis [42]. Liver fibrosis developed in transgenic mice engineered to overexpress NNMT [43]. The expression of NNMT was upregulated in pancreatic tissues of CP mice [44]. This result aligned with our findings. Notably, we observed an upregulation of NNMT in activated mPSCs, highlighting a potentially novel aspect of the molecular mechanisms underlying PSC activation. The activation, proliferation, and migration of PSCs represent critical determinants in the pathogenesis of CP [45]. Our data revealed that by inhibiting NNMT, the activated state of pancreatic stellate cells is mitigated, leading to a reduction in collagen deposition and fibrotic process within the pancreas. During CP disorders, oxidative stress [46] and the stimulation of various inflammatory factors [47] appear to enhance the expression of NNMT.

During the progression of CP, macrophages commonly acquire an M2-polarized phenotype and interact with pancreatic stellate cells, which in turn drives the fibrotic process of the pancreas [48]. Our study demonstrated the presence of macrophage infiltration and an increase in M2-polarized macrophages within the pancreatic tissues of CP mice. As reported by Yang et al., there was a notable correlation between the expression of NNMT and the immune score of M2 macrophages within bladder cancer tissues [49]. The conditioned medium derived from macrophages was able to enhance the expression of both NNMT mRNA and protein in endometrial stromal cells [50]. Our research showed that the knockdown of NNMT in CP-mPSCs exerted an inhibitory effect on the M2 polarization of macrophages. During the polarization of M2 macrophages, the expression levels of Arg1, MRC1, and TGFB1 are markedly upregulated [51]. These molecules are intricately associated with the tissue-repair functions of M2 macrophages [51]. NNMT inhibition in CP-mPSCs decreased the mRNA levels of Arg1, MRC1, and TGFB1 in BMDMs. Research has indicated that NNMT facilitates the differentiation of M2 macrophages through the reduction of trimethylhistone H3 modifications at the promoter regions of pivotal factors governing M2-type polarization [19]. Furthermore, it is possible that NNMT contributes to the indirect promotion of M2 macrophage differentiation via its effects on glutamine metabolism and fatty acid utilization [35, 52, 53].

NNMT exerts its regulatory effects on target genes by modulating histone methylation status, a process closely intertwined with its ability to regulate the levels of methyl donors [54]. Excessive expression of NNMT results in the exhaustive depletion of the methyl donor SAM, which in turn results in decreased methylation of histone residues H3K9 and H3K27 [31]. Through transcriptome analysis, we identified LGALS3, a downstream target of NNMT with H3K37me3 modification at the promoter and associated with M2 polarization of macrophages. LGALS3 modulates gene expression in M2 macrophages through its interaction with ITGB1, which subsequently triggers the activation of downstream signaling cascades [55]. During the progression of pancreatic cancer, LGALS3 facilitates the differentiation of M2-polarized macrophages through the enhancement of angiogenesis and the intensification of immunosuppressive mechanisms [56]. Additionally, LGALS3 enhanced the proliferation of hepatic stellate cells, which in turn promoted the development of liver fibrosis [57]. LGALS3 further modulates fibroblast growth via its specific engagement with surface-exposed glycoconjugates on fibroblasts [58]. This study demonstrated that NNMT inhibition increased the H3K27me3 level on the LGALS3 promoter. Overexpressing LGALS3 in CP-mPSCs reduces the ability of NNMT knockdown to inhibit the polarization of M2 macrophages. These findings indicate that NNMT affects the M2 polarization of mouse macrophages through LGALS3. Notably, LGALS3 is likely one of the pivotal downstream molecules by which NNMT drives the M2-type polarization of macrophages in CP. Therapeutically targeting the NNMT/LGALS3 axis has a strong potential to become a significant approach for reducing fibrosis in the context of CP.

The limitations of this study mainly include the following aspects. Firstly, the research was conducted exclusively in a cerulein-induced mouse model of CP, with no human clinical samples involved for validation. Cerulein, a cholecystokinin analog, induces pancreatic injury via repeated acute chemical stimulation and subsequent secondary fibrosis, which cannot fully recapitulate the multifactorial and progressive pathological processes of human CP—primarily driven by chronic etiologies such as long-term alcohol consumption, smoking, biliary tract diseases and genetic susceptibility [59]. Moreover, human CP exhibits distinct individual heterogeneity in pathological features, molecular expression profiles and inflammatory microenvironment across different etiologies and disease stages, which cannot be reflected in the uniform cerulein-induced mouse model. Thus, more cautious validation and further experiments are warranted to translate the therapeutic potential of targeting the NNMT/LGALS3 axis into clinical applications for CP patients. Future investigations should prioritize the evaluation of NNMT and LGALS3 expression patterns, as well as the intervention effects of NNMT inhibitors, in alternative CP models, such as alcohol-induced or high-fat diet-induced mouse models. Such cross-model validation will be critical to strengthen the translational significance of the NNMT/LGALS3 axis as a potential therapeutic target for human CP. Besides, the current study lacks PSC-specific NNMT intervention experiments in vivo and there may be the potential non-specific effects of systemic NNMTi administration. Future studies should construct PSC-specific NNMT knockout mouse models or develop PSC-targeting NNMTi delivery systems, specifically verify the regulatory effect of PSC-derived NNMT on pancreatic fibrosis in vivo, and elucidate the cell-specific mechanism of NNMTi therapeutic effect.

Conclusion

In conclusion, inhibition of NNMT increases H3K27me3 enrichment at the LGALS3 promoter, thereby suppressing LGALS3 expression of mPSCs. This regulatory axis alleviates mPSC activation and mPSC-induced M2 macrophage polarization, ultimately mitigating pancreatic fibrosis in CP.
