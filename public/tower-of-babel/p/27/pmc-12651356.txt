Late-onset dementia with leukoencephalopathy and a COL4A2 gene variant, causal link or fortuitous association: a case report
Authors: Antoine Garnier-Crussard, Alexandre Bani-Sadr, Isabelle Quadrio, Maïté Formaglio, Virginie Desestret, Elisabeth Tournier-Lasserve, Thibault Coste
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Vascular cognitive impairment is one of the most common causes of dementia after Alzheimer’s disease, with several subtypes such as strategic infarcts or cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) for example [1]. Cerebral SVD encompasses a heterogeneous group of pathological processes affecting the small arteries, arterioles, venules, and capillaries of the brain, leading to cognitive impairment, dementia, and/or stroke [[2], [3], [4]]. While age and hypertension-related cerebral SVD as well as cerebral amyloid angiopathy are common forms, other mechanisms such as inherited SVD (e.g., Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy CADASIL), inflammatory and immunologically mediated SVD (e.g., Wegener’s granulomatosis or Churg-Strauss syndrome) may also be involved [3]. Several genes may be associated with vascular changes (including white matter hyperintensities), and memory clinic physicians should consider genetic analyses in the diagnostic workup of vascular leukoencephalopathies. They should also interpret cautiously the causality between clinico-radiological phenotypes and molecular pathogenic variants, specifically when dealing with variants with a possible incomplete penetrance. This case represents an example of late-onset dementia with leukoencephalopathy and a COL4A2 gene variant, with the discussion of the link between this gene variant and the clinical phenotype.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 74-year-old Caucasian woman referred to our memory clinic due to progressive cognitive decline. Born in Spain and residing in France, she had a medical history of inflammatory arthritis (seronegative rheumatoid arthritis or HLA-B27 associated peripheral arthropathy) managed with methotrexate and stable remission for decades. She had no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or hypercholesterolemia, nor any ophthalmological diseases or migraine. While there was no known family history of dementia or stroke, her mother experienced sudden death at 50, and her father at 67.

She reported memory and language complaint evolving over a few months, along with behavioral changes noticed by her family, including irritability and verbal aggression. Sadness without anhedonia was also present. No signs of rapid-eye movement, sleep behavior disorders, nightmares, fluctuations, or visual hallucinations were noted. Her mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score was 22/30. She had mild orthostatic hypotension but no parkinsonism. Physical examination was unremarkable. Treatment with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (sertraline) was initiated, and she underwent neuropsychological assessment, blood tests, and brain MRI. Three months later, neuropsychological evaluation showed significant cognitive decline affecting memory, executive, and instrumental functions, with an MMSE score of 15/30. Blood tests revealed no abnormalities indicating inflammation, kidney or hepatic failure, folate and vitamin B12 deficiency, or thyroid dysfunction and syphilis serology was negative.

Brain MRI (Fig. 1) revealed widespread confluent hyperintensities on T2-Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery images in the periventricular and deep white matter. The subcortical white matter in the anterior temporal lobes and the orbitofrontal subcortical white matter were involved, resulting in a pattern suggesting CADASIL. Bilateral cortico-subcortical anterior frontal sequelae of post-traumatic appearance were noted, characterized by substance loss and hemosiderin deposits, as well as two left temporopolar microbleeds. No other microbleeds were identified within the T2-FLAIR hyperintense white matter lesions nor elsewhere. Ventricular dilatation was observed without any obstruction to the flow of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A lumbar puncture was performed and CSF analysis showed normal level of neopterin (2.3 nMol/L, normal < 5 nMol/L) and negative detection of 14–3–3 proteins, with no onconeuronal antibodies detected. CSF Alzheimer’s biomarkers were not suggestive of Alzheimer’s disease. Indeed, total tau (309 ng/L, normal < 400 ng/L) and 181-phosphorylated tau (54 ng/L, normal < 60 ng/L) were in the normal range, but she had low amyloid Aβ1–42 at 206 ng/L (normal > 550 ng/L), and low amyloid Aβ1–40 at 3199 ng/L (normal > 4540 ng/L), with a Aβ1–42/Aβ1–40 ratio normal at 0.064 (not suggestive of Alzheimer’s pathology). α-synuclein seeding assay in the CSF using real-time quaking-induced conversion was negative [5]. CSF neurofilament light chain (NfL) was increased (2296 ng/L, normal < 750 ng/L) indicative of axonal damage.

The association in this patient of a diffuse leukoencephalopathy whose pattern is consistent with a vascular leukoencephalopathy (Fig. 1), without significant vascular risk factors or microbleeds suggestive of a cerebral amyloid angiopathy, led us to suspect a hereditary vascular leukoencephalopathy. Molecular analyses of known SVD genes (NOTCH3, HTRA1, LAMB1, COL4A1, COL4A2, TREX1, APP) were conducted. Molecular analysis identified a heterozygous variant in the exon 16 of COL4A2 (NM_001846.4: c.940G>C; p.Gly314Arg). This variant showed concordant deleterious predictions across all in silico tools (Supplementary Table S1). The affected residue is located within the triple-helical domain, a mutational hotspot characteristic of COL4A1/2-related small vessel disease. In addition, its extremely low frequency in population databases (rs757680081; gnomAD v4.1: 0.001 %) further supports a pathogenic role. According to the ACMG/AMP criteria, the p.(Gly314Arg) variant was therefore classified as likely pathogenic, although its causality in the clinical symptoms of this patient cannot be established at this point. The diagnosis was communicated to the patient and her family, who were proposed specialized genetic counselling.

Subsequent clinical deterioration occurred in the next few months with a fall resulting in a femoral neck fracture, progressive gait worsening and epileptic seizures managed with lamotrigine, and a rapid progression of dementia with an MMSE score of 3/30 one year later (MMSE score underestimated due to the appearance of language difficulties).

Discussion and conclusion

Here, we present the case of a 74-year-old Caucasian woman referred to our memory clinic for a cognitive complaint and a rapidly evolving significant cognitive decline affecting memory, executive, and instrumental functions. She had no history of cerebral infarcts or intracerebral hemorrhage. However, the neuroimaging phenotype, notably the extensive and confluent white matter hyperintensities in both hemispheres and bilateral anterior temporal white matter, occurring in the absence of classical vascular risk factors, led us to suspect a genetic vascular leukoencephalopathy.

Molecular screening identified a likely pathogenic variant in COL4A2 whose nature and location (missense variant affecting a triple helix glycine residue) are typical of those observed in patients with a COL4A1/COL4A2 vascular leukoencephalopathy. The MRI phenotype is consistent with the phenotype observed in COL4A1/COL4A2 variant carriers. However, considering the atypical clinical presentation and the incomplete penetrance of COL4A1/COL4A2 mutations we cannot establish the causality of this variant in patient’s symptoms; this variant might indeed have been identified serendipitously.

COL4A1 and COL4A2 encode the proα1(IV) and proα2(IV) chains, respectively, that combine to form type IV collagen. This collagen is a crucial component of basement membranes in various tissues, including vascular endothelia [6]. Most pathogenic variants are missense variants involving highly conserved glycine residues of the triple-helical domain which impact the heterotrimer formation. COL4A1/COL4A2 pathogenic variants can cause a range of cerebrovascular, renal, ophthalmological, cardiac, and muscular disorders. The clinical presentation widely varies between and within families ranging from asymptomatic carriers to severely disabled individuals including at young ages [7]. The presence of a COL4A1/COL4A2 pathogenic variant does not consistently lead to clinical symptoms, as evidenced by Ferguson et al. who showed that, in general population, only 4 % of COL4A2 carriers displayed a detectable phenotype, underscoring incomplete penetrance [8]. This incomplete penetrance suggests that additional modifying factors, either environmental or genetics, are likely involved and remain to be identified. MRI findings are also highly variable and include, in addition to a vascular leukoencephalopathy, intracerebral hemorrhages, lacunar infarcts, porencephaly, cerebral calcifications, cerebellar atrophy, hydrocephalus, intracranial aneurysms and/or focal cortical dysplasia [6]. Whittaker et al. performed in 2022 a systematic review for publications describing COL4A1/COL4A2, TREX1, HTRA1, ADA2, or CTSA pathogenic variant carriers [9]. Authors summarized clinical and radiological cerebral phenotypes of 41 individuals with COL4A2 mutations. Cognitive abnormalities were found in 11 of these COL4A2 mutated patients (11/41, 27 %). Strokes were reported in 22 % of them, intracerebral hemorrhages and porencephaly were described respectively in 68 % and 76 % of these patients with stroke [9].

Lack of neuropathological confirmation and clinico-pathological correlations is a limitation. Although biomarkers did not suggest the presence of Alzheimer’s pathology, other differential diagnoses remain possible, including other neurodegenerative diseases or rare causes of rapidly progressive dementia. However, the patient had no clinical criteria suggestive of a differential diagnosis and the rapid evolution may be attributed to geriatric complications (femoral neck fracture, delirium), the probable head trauma sequelae (discovered with brain MRI) and onset of epileptic seizures. Although low levels of Aβ1–40 and Aβ1–42 in CSF and the left temporopolar microbleeds may suggest the presence of cerebral amyloid angiopathy [10], the Boston Criteria 2.0 for cerebral amyloid angiopathy were not clearly met, as the microbleeds appeared to be of post-traumatic origin [11]. In older adults, we should be very cautious about making a diagnosis in the absence of neuropathological evidence, as co-pathologies are extremely frequent [12].

Although the late disease onset in this patient is compatible with a COL4A2-related disorder given the well-known phenotypic variability of these mutations, the rapid clinical progression is atypical and has not been previously reported. Genetic influences on disease expression generally decline with age as environmental factors accumulate [13], which may explain why COL4A2 variants are rarely identified in late-onset aggressive cases. However, it is possible that the COL4A2 variant contributed to an underlying small-vessel pathology and acted mainly as a modifying factor in another, still unidentified, cause of dementia.

In conclusion, this case highlights the occurrence of a late-onset and rapidly progressive dementia without any history of overt stroke, in association with a COL4A2 likely pathogenic variant. This phenotype has not been reported so far in patients carrying the same type of COL4A2 variant. Based on the very atypical neurological phenotype of this patient and the well-known incomplete penetrance of typical COL4A2 variants, the imputability of this variant to this patient's clinical phenotype cannot be proven, as this association could either be causative or fortuitous or a modifier of another underlying cognitive defect condition [7,9,14]. Therefore, the identification of a pathogenic COL4A2 variant must be interpreted cautiously, and the diagnostic approach to rapidly progressive dementia must be rigorously followed to avoid missing a differential diagnosis [15].
