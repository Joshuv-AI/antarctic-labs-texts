Deep learning-powered multi-parametric ultrasound for classifying metastatic versus reactive axillary lymph nodes
Authors: Manali Saini, Tanin Adl Parvar, Claire Graham, Nicholas B Larson, Mostafa Fatemi, Azra Alizad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo propose a multi-parametric ultrasound imaging-based deep learning method for accurately classifying metastatic and non-metastatic axillary lymph nodes in breast cancer patients.MethodsThe proposed method integrates the conventional ultrasound B-mode imaging with shear wave elastography and color Doppler images of 174 patients to train a transfer learning-based network comprising pretrained MobileNetv2 with a custom shallow head consisting of a convolutional neural network with mixed pooling, weighted sum mixed pooling and squeeze-and-excite attention mechanisms for the first time in the context of ALN classification.ResultsThe proposed method was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation, achieving a mean classification accuracy of 0.91, specificity of 0.91, sensitivity of 0.93, F1 score of 0.93, area under the precision-recall curve of 0.94, and a cross-validated AUC (cvAUC) of 0.92. A network ablation study confirmed the robustness of the model, with relatively narrow 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cvAUC. Comparative analysis showed that the proposed network (Acc: 0.91) outperformed state-of-the-art deep learning models (Acc: 0.67–0.88) for ALN classification and exhibited narrower CIs, highlighting its relative stability. Additionally, results demonstrated that multi-parametric imaging significantly enhanced classification performance, reducing the 95% CI width by nearly half compared to uni-parametric data, further supporting the method’s robustness and reliability.ConclusionThe integration of multi-parametric ultrasound imaging with deep learning network can remarkably improve the classification of metastatic and non-metastatic ALNs in breast cancer patients.Graphical abstract Keywords: Ultrasound, Breast cancer, Axillary lymph node, Multi-parametric imaging, Deep learning

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women in 2024, and the second leading cause of cancer-related death after lung cancer [1]. Axillary lymph nodes (ALNs) are the most common sites of breast cancer metastasis [2]. Accurate assessment of ALN involvement is crucial for determining the stage of disease, treatment planning, and overall prognosis [2–4]. Invasive approaches including core needle biopsy (CNB), sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB), and ALN dissection are gold standards to rule out ALN metastasis, however, there are possible long-term complications and infections [5]. Therefore, developing non-invasive imaging methods to more accurately assess the status of ALN and improve diagnostic accuracy is of great importance [6].

Ultrasound (US) is the primary imaging tool for identifying ALNs due to its accessibility and non-invasive characteristics, however, it is operator-dependent [7]. Its sensitivity varies from 35 to 80%, and specificity ranges from 73 to 90% [8] with area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.6–0.7 [9]. To enhance the diagnostic efficacy of traditional B-mode US for ALN, supplemental imaging modalities such as Color Doppler (CD) US and shear wave elastography (SWE) have been investigated [10]. While CD improves the diagnostic performance of US by allowing clinicians to assess blood flow velocity and the degree of vascularity within and around the ALN [11], SWE provides supplementary information about ALN stiffness to differentiate between metastatic and non-metastatic/reactive ALN [12, 13].

The increasing demand for efficient and objective cancer diagnosis has led to the development of AI-powered ultrasound, which is progressively approaching routine use in clinical applications [14]. Zhang et al. presented bimodal ultrasound features extracted from B-mode and SWE images of ALN for evaluating metastasis using machine learning classifier [15]. Recently, deep learning (DL) is increasingly used for these tasks, since it is capable of automatically learning predictive features from imaging data [16]. DL has shown success in predicting ALN metastasis in breast cancer patients using conventional B-mode ultrasound [17–24]. While DL-powered single-modality imaging has been used, its efficacy in increasing the diagnostic accuracy is limited. Where multi-parametric US imaging has been increasingly used over the past decades, its integration with deep learning can further boost the classification performance [14].

To the best of our knowledge, the potential of this integrative approach has yet to be established. Therefore, we propose a deep learning network trained using multi-parametric US data including B-mode, SWE, and CD images to predict ALN metastasis in primary breast cancer.

Materials and Methods

Ethics approval

This prospective study was conducted in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and under the guidelines and regulations of an institutional review board (IRB)-approved protocol (IRB#13-006035 and IRB# 19-003028). Each patient provided prior written and IRB-approved informed consent, along with permission to publish the study on the data.

Study population

The study population included breast cancer patients aged above 18 years with suspicious axillary lymph nodes identified in clinical ultrasound and recommended for Fine needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB). Patients who were under breast cancer treatment were excluded from the study. Patients were labeled reactive/non-metastatic or metastatic based on the cyto- logical results of FNAB. Final diagnoses for all metastatic and non-metastatic lymph nodes were based on the standard clinical findings and FNAB results. The overall study cohort included 174 female patients with a mean (standard deviation (SD)) age of 56.84(13.45) years. The number of metastatic and non-metastatic ALNs were 80/174 and 94/174 respectively.

Image acquisition

For each patient, two B-mode and six SWE images were acquired in each orientation (longitudinal and transverse) using GE LOGIQ E9, (GE Healthcare, Wauwatosa, WI), equipped with a 9L-D linear array transducer with frequency range of 2–8 MHz. Patients were instructed to minimize any body movement and stop breathing during each SWE acquisition for approximately 3 s, to curtail the pre-compression during scanning. The standard experimental protocol of SWE study in our research lab involves acquiring two B-mode and six SWE images in each orientation. This allows the selection of at least two or three acquisitions with minimal artifacts, based on visual assessment by experienced radiologists, as data acquisition with the GE LOGIQ E9 is sensitive to motion. The CD images were obtained from the clinical GE LOGIQ E9 machine. For each patient, up to two CD images were acquired in each orientation. To maintain the uniformity and data consistency with respect to the CD images, we selected an equal number of B-mode and SWE images for training the proposed model.

Proposed network

The proposed deep learning network was based on transfer learning, which consisted of a pretrained MobileNetV2 (a light-weight network) and a custom shallow network, which is a two-dimensional convolutional neural network with mixed pooling and squeeze-and-excitation (SE) mechanisms for classification of metastatic and reactive axillary lymph nodes using multi-parametric ultrasound, as shown in Fig. 2. Transfer learning enables the reuse of representations learned from large-scale datasets to enhance model performance on domain-specific tasks with limited training data [25].

Proposed transfer-learning-based-network architecture for multi-parametric ultrasound-based classification of metastatic and reactive ALNs

The multi-parametric US images were initially fed to the pretrained MobileNetV2 architecture which extracted the high-level features from these images based on the learned weights from the ImageNet dataset. The corresponding learned features were fed to a custom shallow network fine-tuned on our dataset for multi-parametric US-based feature extraction and classification. It comprised majorly two blocks of two-dimensional convolutional (2Dconv) and mixed pooling (MP) layers, followed by 2Dconv layers, a weighted sum mixed pooling (WSMP) layer, squeeze-and-excite-attention block (SE), flatten and a fully connected output layer with sigmoid activation. The weighted operation enhanced the relevant features extracted by the max-pooling and average-pooling layers while suppressing less relevant ones, thereby contributing to static feature enhancement [26]. This regulated their outputs, which helped in retaining the effective amount of local and global information about the ALNs in the resulting feature map F1WSMP. This output was further enriched by using a channel-wise recalibration mechanism implemented in the squeeze-and-excite block. This block was originally proposed in [27], and explained as follows. The SE block initially uses global average pooling to take the spatial global average of each output feature map or channel (squeeze phase). The resulting channel descriptor is passed through two dense layers which produce the channel weights in the range (0,1) (excitation phase). Finally, these channel weights are multiplied with the original output feature maps/channels, which results in weighted feature maps, and can be regarded as a self-attention function on channels. Therefore, in the context of this work, firstly, was squeezed into a single scalar value which was obtained as the average of the overall feature matrix by using the global average pooling (GAvg ()) operation. Depending upon the preceding layer, such 128 values were obtained, and the corresponding vector was presented to a dense layer with Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) in the excite phase. The resulting output was again presented to a dense layer with sigmoid activation which helped to extract the learned weights for each of the 128 feature maps in the preceding block. Finally, each was multiplied with its corresponding weight obtained from the excite phase to further enhance the relevant features in the maps with this channel-wise recalibration mechanism. All feature maps were flattened and finally fed to a fully connected/dense layer with sigmoid activation for classifying the flattened vector as metastatic or non-metastatic.

In this study, we utilized the TensorFlow Keras 2.16.1 deep learning framework in Python 3.10 Spyder environment, to build and train the proposed network. To ensure sufficient diversity in the training data, we applied dynamic data augmentation exclusively during training. This approach generates randomly transformed versions of the input in each epoch, improving model robustness [28]. We included medically appropriate transformations such as light rotation (± 5°), slight cropping (< 5%), and slight zoom (≤ 5%), to preserve the structures. Although the ratio of augmented to original data was 1:1 due to the use of dynamic augmentation, it introduced greater diversity by presenting different transformed images to the model in each epoch. We adopted a five-fold cross-validation of the proposed network to avoid over-fitting. In each fold, the data was split into 70–10-20% for training–validating-testing the proposed network, without overlapping in the test set across the folds. The hyper-parameters used for training are described in the next section. The proposed network was trained using hyperparameters selected through automated tuning with Keras Tuner using Bayesian optimization. These included a dropout rate of 0.5 after the flatten layer to reduce overfitting, batch sizes ranging from 2 to 16, 50 to 100 training epochs (adjusted by early stopping to prevent overfitting), and a squeeze-and-excite ratio between 2 and 8. Adaptive moment estimation (ADAM) algorithm was used for optimizing the gradient descent for adjusting the model loss with a learning rate of 0.0001. The binary cross-entropy loss function was used for assessing the performance of classification during training [29, 30].

Results

The trained network was used to classify the test set consisting of 20% of the data in each fold and the performance was evaluated in terms of Accuracy (Acc), sensitivity/recall (Sen), specificity (Spe), F-1 score, area under receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve (AUC), and area under the precision-recall curve (AUCp) for each fold [30].

Performance assessment for multi-parametric data

For performance assessment, we estimated the means (STDs: standard deviations) of the classification metrics obtained in five folds, i.e., the cross validated metrics and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (C.I.) using the bootstrapping method [31]. The proposed network, trained using multi-parametric images, achieved a mean test accuracy of 0.91 (0.04, 95% C.I.: 0.85–0.94), Sen of 0.93 (0.06, 95% C.I.: 0.83–0.96), Spe of 0.91 (0.03, 95% C.I.: 0.81–0.94), and F-1 score of 0.93 (0.03, 95% C.I.: 0.88–0.95), AUCp of 0.94 (0.04, 95% C.I.: 0.90–0.97). We estimated the cross validated AUC (cvAUC) to be 0.92 (95% C.I.: 0.88–0.94).

Network ablation

To analyze the contribution of different layers of the proposed network towards classification of ALNs, we performed an ablation study of the network. For this purpose, we first removed the mixed pooling layers and replaced them with the max-pooling layers as in a traditional convolutional neural network model and then trained the network for classification. Secondly, we removed the squeeze-and-excite attention block while retaining the mixed-pooling layers in the network and trained it for classification. Thirdly, we added one more convolutional layer before the SE block in our proposed network. Finally, we replaced the classification layer (i.e., Dense layer with sigmoid activation) with some machine learning models like support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), and random forest (RF) classifiers [32]. We also replaced the learning algorithm optimizer from Adam to AdamW. Table 1 shows the corresponding classification metrics for each ablation study.

Performance comparison with state-of-the-art methods

We performed a comparative analysis of the proposed network with respect to some of the commonly used deep learning architectures for ALN classification. We implemented and trained the deep learning networks [17–23] on the multi-parametric data and evaluated them on the test set. These included: GoogleNet Xception [17], DenseNet-121 [20], ResNet-50 [22], Inception V3, Inception-ResNet V2, and ResNet-101 [21], all using the same experimental settings and hyper-parameters of our proposed network. Figure 3 presents the standard mean classification metrics and cvAUCs obtained by these networks and the proposed network for ALN classification. The mean Acc values achieved by these networks are in the range of 0.67–0.88 vs 0.91 achieved by the proposed network. Similarly, the values of Sen, Spe, F − 1 and cvAUC for existing networks were in the ranges of 0.65–0.82, 0.69–0.91, 0.68–0.87 and 0.72–0.90 respectively, vs 0.93, 0.91, 0. 93 and 0.92 for the proposed network respectively. Further, we estimated the 95% C.Is. of cvAUCs for these networks, as shown in the form of interval bars in Fig. 4. We observed wider C.Is. (almost twice) for the existing networks as compared to the proposed network, as also indicated by the length of the error bars.

Performance assessment for uni-parametric data

In this study, we demonstrated the efficacy of using multi-parametric ultrasound for classification of metastatic and reactive ALNs by analyzing the network’s performance with respect to training and testing it using uni-parametric data. Table 1 presents the classification metrics of the proposed network trained using different modes of ultrasound images. For the uni-parametric cases, a test accuracy of 0.84 (± 0.11) was achieved using only B-mode images, compared to 0.87 (± 0.05) using only SWE, and 0.81 (± 0.14) using only CD. However, the trends were different for the Sen and Spe values, specifically in Spe of 0.80 (0.05) for SWE vs. 0.82 (0.15) and 0.86 (0.15) for B-mode and CD respectively. Despite this, the F − 1, cvAUC and AUCp values were 0.90 (0.04) vs 0.83 (0.11) vs 0.83 (0.15), 0.91 vs 0.84 vs 0.90, and 0.91 (0.04) vs 0.80 (0.10) vs 0.82 (0.04) respectively for SWE vs B-mode vs CD respectively. Further, the 95% C.Is. for cvAUC were considerably wide for these three modalities. Finally, it was noticeable that all metrics were considerably higher, specifically Sp of 0.91, F − 1 of 0.93, cvAUC and AUCp of 0.92 and 0.94 (0.04) respectively with almost half times the width of 95% C.I. for cvAUC, for the multi-parametric data, B + CD + SWE.

Discussion

This study proposed multi-parametric US imaging (B-mode, SWE, CD)-based deep learning network for classification of metastatic and reactive ALNs in breast cancer patients. This study introduced the use of learned weights from the pretrained MobileNetV2, and fine tuning them on our multi-parametric dataset using a shallow network including mixed-pooling and squeeze-and-excite mechanisms for the first time in the context of ALN classification. The evaluation metrics demonstrated the capability of the proposed method to precisely identify reactive and metastatic ALNs while achieving a minimal trade-off between the falsely detected metastatic and non-metastatic ALNs. The results showed a notable decrease in all metrics after replacing mixed-pooling layers with standard max-pooling layers and removing the SE block. This indicated that emphasizing the global features along with the local features of images could improve the discrimination between ALNs, along with adeptness of weighing the features, which could retain the decisive features for better discrimination. Further decrease was observed in all metrics after replacing the final layer with machine learning-based classifiers, along with considerably wide confidence intervals of cvAUC. This indicated the capability of the proposed architecture in effectively classifying ALNs.

The existing deep learning networks for prediction of ALN metastasis used uni- parametric data including mostly B-mode imaging [17–24]. However, for comparative analysis, we used these networks by implementing and training them multi-parametric data. The evaluation results demonstrated that among these existing networks, GoogleNet Xception [17] outperformed in terms of all classification metrics. This could be attributed to the use of more efficient layers, i.e., depth-wise separable convolutional layers used by Xception [17]. The performance of Inception-ResNet V2 [21] was slightly better than the Inception- v3 [21], while the DenseNet-121 [20] was comparable to Inception-v3. Resnet-101 [21] and ResNet-50 [22] performed reasonably lower than all these networks. Finally, the results indicated that the proposed network architecture was superior in terms of classification performance with respect to these networks. The 95% confidence intervals of cvAUC were considerably narrow for our method, further indicating its robustness and effective discrimination capability. Although GoogleNet Xception stood comparably well in performance with respect to our network, it was limited in terms of detecting the true metastatic cases indicated by its comparatively low sensitivity and the wider confidence interval of cvAUC.

The conventional US B-mode imaging exhibited markedly low values of classification metrics, while CD showed slight improvement in all metrics. SWE considerably improved the discrimination performance, although it resulted in slightly low Spe. Integrating SWE with B-mode as well as CD resulted in a remarkable improvement in the overall classification performance. These results further indicated that SWE provided enhanced features to the proposed network for identifying the metastatic ALNs. Finally, the analysis demonstrated that the combination of SWE with CD and B-mode effectively improved the overall performance among all parameters, with a narrower C.I. of cvAUC, indicating stability and robustness. This signified the capability of multi-parametric US imaging to further enhance the learning of the proposed network.

In a nutshell, this study demonstrates that multi-parametric imaging with proposed deep learning network can effectively boost the classification performance for metastatic and non-metastatic ALNs. One of the major limitations of this study was the dataset size. However, we utilized transfer learning along with fivefold cross validation and dynamic data augmentation to mitigate overfitting and benefit from knowledge acquired on large-scale datasets. As a part of future study, we plan to acquire additional images for increasing the number of examples during training to further improve performance. Once trained on large dataset, we may incorporate the trained network for predicting ALN metastasis in real-time.

Conclusion

This study proposes multi-parametric US based deep learning network for the classification of metastatic and non-metastatic ALNs in patients with breast cancer. The integration of conventional B-mode US with shear wave elastography and color Doppler demonstrates a considerable enhancement in the performance of the proposed network. Further comparison study with state-of-the-art networks demonstrates the superiority of the proposed network for ALN classification.
