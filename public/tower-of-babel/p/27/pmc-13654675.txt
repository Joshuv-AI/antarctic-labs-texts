Hemodynamically neutral intubation: management of a physiologically difficult airway
Authors: Stefan M Kornstaedt, Aishwarya Palaniappan, Maximilian Weinhofer, Stephan Steger, Scott D Weingart
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Airway management in patients with a physiologically difficult airway carries a high risk of peri-intubation hemodynamic collapse, particularly in the setting of massive pulmonary embolism. Preservation of negative pressure ventilation may mitigate the risk of cardiovascular collapse. We report a case in which awake intubation with preservation of spontaneous negative pressure ventilation was deliberately employed as a hemodynamically neutral intubation (HNI) strategy and use this case to further delineate and contextualize this physiology-based approach.

The concept of HNI was informally introduced by Weingart [1] in 2018 in an educational podcast and has not yet been formally described in the peer-reviewed literature. HNI refers to a conceptual intubation strategy in the physiologically difficult airway that integrates the following: (1) awake intubation facilitated by pdissociative sedation (e.g., ketamine), topical anesthesia, or a combination of both; (2) active hemodynamic stabilization and augmentation; and (3) a ventilatory strategy that closely mimics the patient’s spontaneous negative pressure breathing both before and after intubation. The following case illustrates the practical application of this physiology-driven concept in a patient with massive pulmonary embolism and right ventricular (RV) failure.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 75-year-old woman was brought to the emergency department by emergency medical services because of progressive generalized weakness and dizziness. On arrival, vital signs revealed tachypnea (22/min), tachycardia (114/min), borderline hypotension (103/71 mmHg), and an oxygen saturation of 89% on room air. She was transferred to the high-acuity area of the emergency department.

The patient reported several days of fever and cough and had been largely bedridden for the preceding 2 days. She complained of dyspnea but denied syncope or chest pain. Her medical history included a resection of right-sided renal oncocytoma in 2021, depression, and chronic arterial hypertension. Physical examination was otherwise unremarkable. Body weight was 90 kg and height was 170 cm.

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed markedly elevated D-dimer levels (6,916 µg/L), elevated high-sensitivity troponin I (147.6 ng/L), acute kidney injury (creatinine, 1.6 mg/dL), and elevated C-reactive protein (18.2 mg/dL). Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation (pH, 7.385; PaO2, 96.1 mmHg; PaCO2, 26.6 mmHg; bicarbonate, 18.3 mmol/L; base excess, –8.5 mmol/L) and hyperlactatemia (3.9 mmol/L) on 4 L/min oxygen via nasal cannula. Polymerase chain reaction testing was positive for influenza A.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography demonstrated bilateral large central pulmonary emboli with multiple segmental thrombi (Fig. 1). The patient received 5,000 IU of unfractionated heparin. Shortly thereafter, she deteriorated hemodynamically, developing profound hypotension (65/47 mmHg) with clinical signs of peripheral hypoperfusion (mottling score 3). Bedside echocardiography revealed RV dysfunction with an RV to left ventricle ratio of >1.0, a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) of 10 mm, a positive McConnell sign, septal flattening (D-sign) and a tricuspid regurgitation velocity of 3.1 m/sec, consistent with RV strain caused by acute pulmonary hypertension.

The patient was treated with supplemental oxygen via a non-rebreather mask (15 L/min) and inhaled milrinone (5 mg). Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring and central venous access were established. Despite vasopressor support with high-dose norepinephrine (approximately 1.3 µg/kg/min), vasopressin (0.03 IU/min), and inotropic-dose epinephrine (0.04 µg/kg/min), the patient progressed to a peri-arrest state. A single bolus of epinephrine (500 µg intravenous [IV]) was required to maintain circulation with a mean arterial pressure ≥65 mmHg. Rescue thrombolysis with alteplase (50 mg over 15 minutes) resulted in no meaningful hemodynamic improvement. After consultation with interventional cardiology, emergent mechanical pulmonary thrombectomy was planned.

Because of increasing agitation, orthopnea, and inability to tolerate the supine position, airway control was required to facilitate intervention. Given the presence of obstructive shock with RV failure and high catecholamine dependence, conversion to positive pressure ventilation was considered highly likely to precipitate cardiovascular collapse. Therefore, conventional rapid sequence or delayed sequence intubation was deliberately avoided, and an HNI strategy, employing awake intubation with preservation of spontaneous negative pressure ventilation, was chosen.

Airway management was supported by meticulous preparation of all required equipment (Fig. 2). The patient was placed on high-flow nasal oxygen at 60 L/min with a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 1.0. Intravenous glycopyrrolate (500 µg) was administered as an antisialagogue. Airway topicalization was achieved using nebulized lidocaine (100 mg) and additional topical oropharyngeal lidocaine (100 mg). Low-dose intravenous esketamine 50 mg (equivalent to approximately 100 mg of racemic or standard ketamine) and midazolam 1 mg were administered to achieve a dissociative state while preserving spontaneous ventilation.

Equipment prepared for a hemodynamically neutral airway management strategy, including high-flow nasal oxygenation, topical airway anesthesia, video laryngoscopy with a preshaped stylet, and vasoactive agents and sedatives, used to preserve spontaneous ventilation and minimize peri-intubation hemodynamic instability.

Hyperangulated video laryngoscopy provided a full view of the glottis (Cormack-Lehane grade I). Topical anesthesia of the vocal cords and trachea was achieved using a mucosal atomization device (MADgic, Teleflex), delivering 120 mg of lidocaine under direct visualization. A 7.5-mm endotracheal tube was advanced through the opened vocal cords during spontaneous breathing with first-pass success, and correct tube placement was confirmed by continuous waveform capnography.

Throughout the intubation procedure, no clinically relevant changes in blood pressure or vasopressor requirements were observed. Norepinephrine was maintained at approximately 1.3 µg/kg/min without escalation, and no additional rescue vasopressor boluses or adjustments to vasopressin or epinephrine were required. The patient was initially ventilated in pressure support ventilation (PSV) mode with both positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and PS set at 0 cm H2O, thereby preserving spontaneous negative pressure ventilation.

Following administration of fentanyl (two 100-µg boluses) and propofol (approximately 2.5 mg/kg/hr) as a post-intubation sedation strategy, the patient developed transient apnea requiring brief back-up positive pressure ventilation (continuous mandatory ventilation; tidal volume, 500 mL; respiratory rate, 10/min; PEEP, 0 cm H2O). This was immediately associated with a marked decrease in mean arterial pressure, necessitating repeated epinephrine boluses (100 µg IV each). Upon return of spontaneous, albeit insufficient, respiratory effort, the patient was transitioned to PSV (FiO2, 1.0; PEEP, 0 cm H2O; PS, 2–4 cm H2O) to provide minimal positive pressure assistance without adding PEEP. This limited support restored adequate ventilation without recurrent hemodynamic compromise.

The patient was transferred to the cardiac catheterization laboratory under ventilatory support with ongoing analgosedation and hemodynamic support. Mechanical pulmonary thrombectomy resulted in immediate hemodynamic improvement. Vasopressin and epinephrine were weaned over the following hours, and norepinephrine was discontinued after 2 days. The patient was successfully extubated approximately 18 hours after intubation in the emergency department, discharged from the intensive care unit after 5 days, and subsequently transferred to a rehabilitation facility.

DISCUSSION

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the concept of the physiologically difficult airway in emergency and critical care medicine [2,3]. This term, first coined by Weingart [4], describes airway difficulty stemming from rapid desaturation and the potential for hemodynamic collapse during airway management. Observational studies of endotracheal intubation in critically ill patients have consistently demonstrated high rates of serious peri-intubation complications, including severe hypoxemia, arterial hypotension, and cardiac arrest [5–8]. Importantly, these adverse events are frequently unrelated to anatomical airway difficulty and instead reflect preexisting physiological instability at the time of intubation [6–8].

The physiologically difficult airway is characterized by any of hypoxemia, hypotension, severe metabolic acidosis, and acute RV failure [2,3], driving the paradigm shift toward “resuscitate before intubate” [9]. Contemporary recommendations emphasize pre-intubation optimization, including maximal preoxygenation, early vasopressor support, careful selection and dose reduction of induction agents, high-dose neuromuscular blockade, and strategies to maximize first-pass success such as video laryngoscopy and use of airway adjuncts [10–12]. Each additional intubation attempt is associated with a stepwise increase in complications, underscoring the importance of first-pass success [5].

Complications during intubation arise from three interrelated mechanisms: vasodilatory and negative inotropic effects of induction agents, loss of endogenous sympathetic tone, and the transition from spontaneous negative pressure to positive pressure ventilation [8,9,13]. Among these, the latter mechanism may have particularly profound hemodynamic consequences in patients with RV failure [8,13–15]. Spontaneous inspiration augments venous return and RV preload, whereas positive intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return, increases pulmonary vascular resistance, and acutely raises RV afterload [14,15]. In patients with limited cardiopulmonary reserve, these effects may rapidly precipitate circulatory collapse [13].

While vasodilation and loss of sympathetic tone can be mitigated by judicious drug selection and dose adjustment [2,3,12], initiation of positive pressure mechanical ventilation is often unavoidable following intubation [14,15]. In this context, awake intubation emerges as a physiologically advantageous alternative. Traditionally reserved for anatomically difficult airways, it enables tracheal intubation while preserving spontaneous ventilation and avoiding deep sedation and paralysis [16,17]. Based on these pathophysiological considerations, awake intubation may confer hemodynamic advantages compared with conventional rapid sequence intubation in carefully selected patients with physiologically difficult airways.

Recent reports have extended awake intubation to patients with physiologically difficult airways, suggesting a potential reduction in peri-intubation hemodynamic complications. In a cohort study comparing awake intubation using dexmedetomidine sedation and topical anesthesia with conventional rapid sequence intubation in critically ill patients, peri-intubation complications occurred significantly less frequently in the awake intubation group (2.2% vs. 39%), with only a single episode of hypotension reported [18]. These findings suggest that minimizing exposure to cardiovascular depressive induction agents while preserving spontaneous ventilation and avoiding hypoxemia may enhance procedural safety in critically ill patients.

In the described case, awake intubation was undertaken with deliberate preservation of spontaneous negative pressure ventilation during and immediately after airway management. Within the conceptual framework of “hemodynamically neutral intubation,” we hypothesize that preservation of spontaneous negative pressure ventilation—by maintaining negative intrathoracic pressure and thereby augmenting venous return—may attenuate the preload reduction and RV afterload increase associated with positive pressure ventilation. In acute RV failure, even moderate elevations in intrathoracic pressure can critically impair venous return and exacerbate RV afterload, potentially precipitating circulatory collapse.

Prior data indicate that endotracheal tube–related airway resistance does not substantially increase the work of breathing in intubated spontaneously breathing patients [19], supporting the feasibility of maintaining spontaneous negative pressure ventilation immediately after intubation. This strategy, however, requires strict vigilance. Continuous monitoring of respiratory mechanics and gas exchange is mandatory, and a controlled apnea ventilation strategy must be immediately available. Should spontaneous negative pressure ventilation become insufficient, cautiously titrated pressure support may be introduced to prevent respiratory decompensation while minimizing additional intrathoracic pressure.

The physiological relevance of this approach became evident during a brief episode of apnea requiring positive pressure ventilation, which was immediately associated with marked hypotension necessitating rescue push-dose epinephrine. Controlled mandatory ventilation, despite ventilator settings theoretically optimized to minimize intrathoracic pressure [20], resulted in immediate hemodynamic compromise. This observation reinforces the central importance of preserved spontaneous negative pressure ventilation in patients with acute RV failure.

In our case, a combination of low-dose esketamine and midazolam was used to facilitate awake intubation, achieving a dissociative state while preserving spontaneous ventilation and avoiding oversedation. This regimen was selected for its rapid onset, ease of titration, and favorable hemodynamic profile, representing a pragmatic alternative to other commonly described sedation strategies [16–18]. The dose required for dissociative anesthesia is generally 0.5–1.0 mg/kg for ketamine and 0.25–0.5 mg/kg IV for esketamine. From a pragmatic clinical perspective, however, it may be preferable to administer small incremental boluses (e.g., 25 mg ketamine or 12.5 mg esketamine) and repeat them until a dissociative state is achieved. This titrated approach may help avoid oversedation and potentially harmful cardiodepressive effects, particularly in catecholamine-depleted patients.

In our case, fentanyl and propofol were chosen as agents for post-intubation sedation. In retrospect, a ketamine (0.5 mg/kg/hr) or esketamine drip (0.25 mg/kg/hr) would have allowed dissociation without loss of spontaneous respiratory efforts. Additionally, it would have provided sufficient analgesia to dispense with opioid administration. Esketamine or ketamine may facilitate procedural tolerance while preserving the hemodynamic objectives of hemodynamically neutral intubation.

Based on these pathophysiological considerations, the present case suggests that established principles of awake intubation may be extended by deliberately integrating post-intubation preservation of spontaneous negative pressure ventilation to prioritize hemodynamic stability in the physiologically difficult airway. The term “hemodynamically neutral intubation” is used descriptively to denote this combined strategy, linking awake intubation with structured hemodynamic stabilization and a ventilatory approach that minimizes intrathoracic pressure increases. At present, “hemodynamically neutral intubation” remains a physiology-driven didactic concept rather than an intervention supported by robust outcome data. Pending further evidence, this approach may represent a viable airway strategy in carefully selected patients with a physiologically difficult airway, including those with massive pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, severe valvular stenosis, or pericardial tamponade, in whom even transient increases in intrathoracic pressure may precipitate cardiovascular collapse.

Capsule Summary

What is already known

Intubation of critically ill patients with right ventricular failure or massive pulmonary embolism is associated with a high risk of hemodynamic deterioration and cardiovascular collapse, particularly related to sedative agents and positive pressure ventilation.

What is new in the current study

Awake intubation with preservation of spontaneous negative pressure ventilation, termed hemodynamically neutral intubation, may represent a physiology-driven airway strategy and offer a potential alternative to conventional rapid sequence intubation in carefully selected patients with severe physiologic instability.
