Mucosal immune responses in the lung during respiratory infection: The organization and regulation of iBALT structure
Authors: Xintong Gao, Cuihua Liu, Shuo Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe local immune defenses of respiratory system play a crucial role in safeguarding against pathogens and eliminating infected cells. In this article, we review the current knowledge regarding the establishment and regulation of local immune responses within the lungs, with a particular focus on the formation of inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT), which has demonstrated a capacity to mount protective immune responses against several pathogens, including influenza virus, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV)/SARS-CoV-2, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). Furthermore, we explore the development and regulation of long-term immune memory within the lungs, which may be facilitated by iBALT. This review aims to provide a summary of potential targets within iBALT for pathogen defense and vaccine design.

Introduction

The respiratory tract filters through thousands of liters of air every day, making the lungs vulnerable to airborne pathogens. This daily exposure to exogenous antigens often leads to infections and cancerous changes in the lungs. The recent emerging and resurgent acute infectious pathogens, such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) and influenza virus, have caused acute respiratory infections that resulted in global pandemics [1,2]. Additionally, chronic infections from long-existing pathogens, like Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), have claimed millions of lives annually [3]. The immune response plays a critical role in eradicating pathogen-infected and transformed cells. Although systemic immunity has been found to control respiratory infections, the pulmonary immune system provides immediate and unique local protection. Bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT) is not persistent in humans and mice. Instead, inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT), an inducible tertiary lymphatic structure in the lungs, plays a crucial role in controlling lung infections. Understanding how to induce and maintain iBALT structure would provide valuable insights for developing effective therapies and vaccines for pulmonary infectious diseases.

THE FORMATION and REGULATION of iBALT

iBALT, which is embedded in the pulmonary walls, is a type of tertiary lymphoid organs (TLOs) that forms in the lungs following infection [4]. This structure closely resembles conventional secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs), such as Peyer's patches in the small intestine [5]. Typically, iBALT consists of B-cell follicles surrounded by T cells [6]. It can be triggered by various airway stimuli, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, allergens, and particulates. Once inflammation has resolved and reached a homeostatic state, iBALT promotes the clearance of respiratory pathogens [7]. The formation of iBALT serves as an immune response against infectious pathogens or antigens. Once formed, iBALT, like many TLOs, collects antigens and recruits antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to prime B and T cell responses. The cellular composition of iBALT is similar to that of SLOs, consisting of germinal center (GC) B cells, follicular helper T cells, and follicular dendritic cells (FDCs). It can be speculated that crucial developmental processes occur following antigen stimulation, including stromal cell activation and differentiation, GC formation, activation of specific T and B cells, and differentiation of plasma cells [8,9].

The Initial Stage of iBALT Formation

The development of iBALT begins with the activation of stromal cells and the recruitment of lymphocytes. Upon exposure to pulmonary stimuli, stromal cells differentiate into three types of cells: follicular dendritic cells, high endothelial venules (HEVs) expressing CD31+PNAd+, and lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) expressing thymus cell antigen (Thy)1+ FDCs. These cells are located at the center of iBALT, and are responsible for recruiting B cells and forming B cell follicles [10] (Figure 1). HEVs are found near B cell follicles and they facilitate the exchange of immune cells and antigens between lymphoid organs and the circulatory system [10]. In SLOs, HEVs are conventionally derived from blood endothelial cells via signaling through the lymphotoxin-β receptor (LTβR) and nuclear factor kappa-B (NFκB) pathways. Lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells play a crucial role in the development of SLOs such as lymph nodes and Peyer's patches by promoting the upregulation of adhesion molecules and lymphocyte-homing chemokines through its interaction with stromal cells. However, the specific role of LTi in the formation of TLOs such as iBALT is still not fully understood. Although iBALT formation has been observed in mice lungs lacking LTi (RORγ-deficient and Id2-deficient mice) [12], it has been suggested that a subset of type 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) expressing neuropilin 1 (NRP-1), lymphotoxin α (LTα), lymphotoxin β (LTβ), and retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor-γ t (RORγt), which induce adhesion molecules on mesenchymal stromal cells similar to LTi, may participate in TLO induction in the lungs of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [13]. Some researchers propose that the function of LTi in TLO formation may be carried out by other similar cells, such as natural killer T cells and CD3+CD4+ cells [14,15]. However, it remains unclear whether this mechanism also applies to iBALT formation.

The Development and Maintenance of iBALT

The cytokines and chemokines in the local inflammatory microenvironment are required for the development of iBALT. LECs, which are located around iBALT, secrete chemokines C–C chemokine ligand (CCL)19 and CCL21 to recruit T cells and maintain their survival [16] (Figure 1). LECs also play a role in recruiting migratory dendritic cells and facilitating the penetration of migratory dendritic cells into lymphoid organs [17]. iBALT accommodates members of the adaptive immune system and is a structure for priming T cells by dendritic cells [9]. Specially, tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells take iBALT as niches for maturation [18]. iBALT contains various types of B cells, including naïve B cells, plasma B cells, GC B cells, and isotype-switched memory B cells [8]. There are two types of B cell follicles in iBALT, each secreting chemokines to attract B cells. In one type of follicles, FDC expresses C-X-C motif chemokine (CXCL)13 to recruit C-X-C motif chemokine receptor (CXCR)5+ B cells, while the other B cell follicle takes CXCL12+PDPN+ fibroblast-like stromal cells to attract CXCR4+ B cells [12,19] (Figure 1). CCL21 and CCL19 secreted by LECs interact with C–C chemokine receptor (CCR)7 on dendritic cells (DCs) and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells to help with iBALT organization [8,16].

The maintenance of iBALT is supported by the homeostasis between stromal cell and immune cell interaction, which heavily relies on lymphotoxin (LT)-mediated signaling through LTβR. Following the initial stages of iBALT formation, activated lymphocytes express LT and begin to accumulate in iBALT. LT is expressed by various cell types, including lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and LTi cells, whereas LTβR is found on stromal cells such as endothelial and fibroblast cells. LT is comprised of two subsets, LT-α and LT-β, with LT-β signaling determining the differentiation of HEVs, FDCs, and PDPN+CXCL12+ stromal cells [20]. On the other hand, LT-α promotes the development of TLOs and new lymphatic vessels [21]. LTβR blockade prevents the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures in smoking-related mice models [22]. Mice lacking LTα exhibit T cell and B cell accumulation in lung tissue, but the T and B cell areas are not conventionally separated, and FDCs do not exist [12], highlighting the requirement of LTα for iBALT organization. CD11c+ DCs are a source of lymphotoxin-α1β2 and they are crucial for iBALT maintenance. Depletion of CD11c+ DCs disrupts LTβR signaling, leading to iBALT deconstruction and GC reaction [23]. Additionally, LT is crucial in supporting stromal cell maturation in iBALT. Following influenza virus infection, which can prime iBALT formation, FAPα+ LTβR+ mesenchymal cell precursors are found in the lung tissue of mice, where LTα3+ LTi cells are recruited [24].

The Roles of iBALT during Infections

iBALT serves as a lymphoid follicle-like structure that plays a crucial role in coordinating local T and B cells. The impact of iBALT can be protective or pathogenic, depending on the specific pathogenic conditions [25]. In the case of common infections such as viral and Mtb infections, iBALT has been found to exhibit protective characteristics by eliciting immune responses following primary or secondary infections with viruses, such as influenza A virus (IAV). It can also limit pathogen dissemination within the lymphoid follicle, as observed during Mtb infection [26]. However, in conditions such as allergy/asthma, COPD, and pulmonary arterial hypertension, iBALT is typically associated with exacerbating lung disease rather than resolving chronic inflammation [25].

IAV Infection

The iBALT structure is commonly observed during influenza virus infection. CD4+T cells called T resident helper (TRH) cells are recognized in the lung iBALT along with CD8+ T cells following influenza infection [27]. TRH cells develop with the help of B cells, and iBALT serves as a homeostatic niche for TRH cells. Abrogation of the Bcl6 gene in mice redistributes TRH cells outside of the iBALT [27]. The formation of iBALT during IAV infection depends on interleukin (IL)-1R signaling. In IL-1β-deficient mice, the levels of CXCL13 (which attracts B cells) decrease, whereas the implementation of IL-1α in mice lungs promotes iBALT formation and GC B cell response [28]. DCs also play a crucial role in iBALT formation after IAV infection by expressing LT-β and various chemokines that help recruit lymphocytes and facilitate iBALT formation. Depletion of DCs leads to disintegration of iBALT and GC reactions [23].

The presence of iBALT is beneficial for protection against IAV infection. iBALT serves as a niche for B cell maturation and selection, facilitating humoral responses. GC-like B cells, expressing canonical GC B cell marker genes such as Bcl6, Fas, Aidca, Gcsam, and S1pr2, exist in iBALT and drive the maturation of the humoral immune response [29]. Mice lacking peripheral lymphoid organs except for iBALT exhibit not only higher survival rates but also better resistance against IAV invasion under high-dose virus challenge [30]. The improved protection of iBALT against IAV in mice lung is partly explained by the T helper (Th)1 response [[30], [31], [32]]. In mice with pre-formed iBALT (induced by IAV), the morbidity is lower than the mice without pre-formed iBALT. Alveolar macrophages (AMs) and DCs are apparently increased in the lung when challenged with IAV, exhibiting a protective effect [32]. In mice with no SLOs (LT−/− mice), noncanonical iBALT structures (like the one in lipopolysaccharide-stimulated LT−/− mice lung appearing without distinct T-B border and FDCs) [12] are found in the lung following influenza virus infection, and influenza-specific antibodies and memory CD8+T cells are maintained in LT−/− mice, suggesting long-term protection against infection by iBALT [31].

SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2 Infection

The research on the coronavirus-induced iBALT is still limited. It remains unclear if SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2 can directly induce iBALT formation, and further investigation is needed to explore this possibility. Protective effects of iBALT against a mouse-adapted SARS-coronavirus have been observed when mice are pre-immunized by a protein cage nanoparticle (PCN) [33]. The immunomodulatory effects elicited by the PCN treatment are largely mediated by the induction of iBALT in immunodeficient mice that lack conventional secondary lymphoid organs, suggesting that SARS-CoV and related vaccines may induce the formation of iBALT.

For SARS-CoV-2 infection, recent research by our group showed evidence that SARS-CoV-2 can induce the formation of iBALT structure at the very early stage of infection in hACE2-transgenic mice. B1 cells contributed to the formation and function of iBALT at early stage of SARS-CoV-2 infection and might be beneficial for the early protection of SARS-CoV-2 infection (unpublished data). In rhesus macaques infected with SARS-CoV-2, there is a significant increase in CXCL13 in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid（BALF） and serum [34]. This may account for the gathering of antibody-secreting B cells in coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) patients [35], as CXCL13 can recruit B cells by binding to CXCR5 expressed on the surface of B cells. Therefore, this evidence supports the formation of iBALT during SARS-CoV-2 infection. For clinical research, although the formation of iBALT during SARS-CoV-2 infection has not been investigated too much, there are similarities between the molecule expression patterns in SARS-CoV-2 infection and crucial molecules required for iBALT formation, such as CXCL13, CCL19, CCL21, and CCR7. Research has shown that COVID-19 patients with moderate symptoms have a higher level of CXCL13 in their sera than severe patients, while survivors have higher levels of CXCL13 than fatal cases [36]. Additionally, CCL19 in the plasma of COVID-19 patients has shown an upward trend compared to healthy controls [37], which phenomenon is also observed in human ACE2-transgenic mouse models accepting SARS-CoV-2 infection [38]. Proteomic analysis of spleen and BALF from fatal COVID-19 cases has revealed a decreased level of CCL19 and CD8+ T cells [39], suggesting that death cases lack CCL19 for recruiting CD8+ T cells and fail to combat virus infection.

Recent studies on deceased COVID-19 patients demonstrated a deficiency in GC structure and Tfh cells in the lungs, whereas focal organizing pneumonia patients who underwent surgery had GC and Tfh cells in their lungs, suggesting that GC and Tfh in the lung may be beneficial for individual survival and contribute to protective humoral immune responses [40]. Although the local immune responses in the lungs of SARS-CoV-2-infected cases are not yet fully understood, existing evidence indicates the formation of iBALT in SARS-CoV-2 patients as well. The immune balance of iBALT may also be associated with the disease outcome of COVID-19.

Thus, during respiratory virus infections, especially IAV infection, iBALT shows the protectivity in many aspects by supporting GC responses and local immune cell differentiation, maintaining antibody response, accelerating antigen clearance, and providing a better control of morbidity. During respiratory virus infection, iBALT also synergizes with local lymph nodes. In mice with pre-existed iBALT, the systemic immunity in distal locations like tracheobronchial lymph nodes and spleen could be affected, which is characterized by a faster contraction of antiviral CD4+ T cell responses compared to mice without iBALT [32]. GC B cells in iBALT enable to drive the maturation of humoral responses in mediastinal lymph nodes [29]. Thus, iBALT provides a more controlled and augmented immune response to virus infection and is integrated with systemic immunity in lymph nodes via vasculature and lymphatic connections.

Mtb Infection

During Mtb infection, iBALT structures typically form around granulomas. Granulomas consist of Mtb-infected macrophages at their center, surrounded by follicle-like structures containing activated APCs, B cells, and T cells [41]. This structure formation helps control the spread of bacteria. The cytokines CCL19, CCL21, CXCL12, CXCL13, and their receptors CCR7, CXCR4, and CXCR5, which are required for iBALT formation [19,42,43], are also involved in Mtb infection. In addition, cytokines IL-23, IL-17, and IL-22 produced at an early stage of Mtb infection play a crucial role in triggering the secretion of homeostatic cytokines, thereby facilitating iBALT formation [[44], [45], [46]].

iBALT provides protective immunity against Mtb infection, possibly through the secretion of interferon (IFN)-γ by Th1 cells, which enhances macrophage activation, enabling them to engulf bacteria [47]. Research indicates that several proteins of Mtb inhibit the formation and function of granulomas, leading to immune evasion. One of these proteins is mycobacteria membrane protein large 7 (MmpL7), which is involved in iBALT formation. Infected animals with Mtb lacking MmpL7 (Mtb Δmmpl7) showed an increase in iBALT-containing granulomas [48]. Additionally, another mutant Mtb lacking SigH, a sigma factor involved in Mtb stress response [49], contributes to the profound occurrence of iBALT [50].

The CXCL13-CXCR5 axis is consistently shown to play a critical role in iBALT formation during Mtb infection [7,51]. Furthermore, recent research has revealed that innate lymphoid cell subgroup ILC3s contribute to iBALT formation by secreting IL-17 and IL-22 in a CXCR5-CXCL13-dependent manner [52]. ILC3s have a protective effect on the pulmonary immune response against Mtb infection. A study has demonstrated that ILC3s accumulate in the lungs of Mtb-infected mice concurrent with an elevated accumulation of AMs. A lack of ILC3s results in decreased early AMs accumulation, impaired iBALT organization, and compromised Mtb resistance [53]. Therefore, it is possible that ILC3s not only recruit AMs but also organize the iBALT structure to eliminate Mtb.

iBALT-associated Immune Memory

The immune system employs various mechanisms to rapidly respond to respiratory pathogens upon secondary exposure. In addition to killing infected cells and pathogens during the first exposure, T and B cells, as well as innate immune cells, establish mechanisms to quickly respond to subsequent infections. TRM cells and tissue-resident memory B (BRM) cells located in the iBALT structure following lung infection and play a vital role in this rapid local secondary response [52]. Upon re-exposure to pathogens, TRM and BRM cells in the iBALT are activated first, followed by the recruitment of peripheral lymphocytes. Evidence suggests that the constant presence of memory CD4+ or CD8+ T cells is correlated with iBALT [54,55], while Thy1+IL-7+ lymphoid endothelial cells provide a survival niche for memory CD4+ T cells [56,57]. Although these findings are primarily based on allergic inflammation in the lung, they provide insightful clues for understanding the relationship between iBALT formation and mucosal immune memory under other infectious conditions.

In COVID-19 patients, memory phenotypes are frequently found in SARS-CoV-2 infected lungs. SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells are maintained as a memory phenotype in various tissues, such as the bone marrow, lung, spleen, and lung-associated lymph nodes, and tissue-resident subtype (CD69+ CD103+) are predominantly found in the lung and lung-associated lymph nodes, while SARS-CoV-2 specific follicular helper T cells and GC structure are found in the lungs [38]. Furthermore, CD4+ TRM accumulated mainly in the lungs and lung-associated lymph nodes of COVID-19 patients [63]. CD8+ T cells in the BALF of moderate patients exhibit higher levels of tissue-resident markers than severe patients [64], and these CD8+ T cells have a prolonged activation profile lasting for at least 5–6 weeks after hospital discharge [65]. Interestingly, they also pre-exist in the tonsils of unexposed individuals [66]. Although all this evidence indicates a memory phenotype of T cells, it is unclear whether TRMs can attenuate COVID-19 severity in humans and whether they reside in iBALT.

Tissue-Resident Memory B

A recent study has shown that memory B cells in the lungs of humans have a distinct molecular profile when compared to those found in lung-draining lymph nodes and peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Lung-resident memory B (BRM) cells express higher levels of residence-associated markers, such as CXCR3, CD69, and CCR6, which help retain BRMs in the lungs and facilitate their differentiation into plasma cells, thereby enabling a rapid humoral response against viral infections. Moreover, in murine models, influenza-specific BRMs have been found to stably reside in pulmonary iBALT and exhibit a resident phenotype [67] (Figure 2). In human childhood, lung B cells express higher levels of CD69 and CD95 than those in lymph nodes, which indicates a more tissue-adapted and activated state of BRM in induced BALT. The study also observed that induced BALT appeared in the first year of life and the number peaked during 2–3 years old, declining at 4–5 years old. In SARS-CoV-2 infected patients, memory B cells are detected in the lungs, spleen, bone marrow, and various lymph nodes and persist for up to 6 months after infection [63]. Tissue resident-like GC B cells are found in SARS-CoV-2 infected patients’ lungs from a broad age range from 10 to 74, indicating an age-preserved GC B response to pathogens [38]. The frequency of CD69+ BRM cells in total B cells in the lungs and lung-associated lymph nodes is significantly higher (>50–75%) than in the bone marrow and spleen (0–10%). In the lungs, SARS-CoV-2–specific CD69+ memory B cells constitute 50%–80% of all SARS-CoV-2–specific memory B cells [38]. These findings suggest that tissue-resident B cells make up the majority of SARS-CoV-2-specific memory B cells in the lungs and they may contribute to the formation of iBALT, promoting long-term mucosal immunity (Figure 2).

Vaccine-Induced long-Term Memory: iBALT formation

Vaccines have long been utilized as a preventive measure against infections by generating immune memory in lymphoid organs or peripheral tissues such as the lung and intestinal mucosa. iBALT, which serves as a crucial lymphoid organ for storing memory lymphocytes in the lung, has been observed in various contexts including IAV or Mtb infections and allergic inflammation. However, it remains unclear whether vaccination against these pathogens could induce iBALT formation. Here we explore the potential for iBALT formation following vaccination against IAV, SARS-CoV, SARS-CoV-2, and Mtb.

IAV Vaccine

While it is unclear whether IAV vaccines can induce the formation of iBALT, some IAV vaccines have been shown to induce the occurrence of memory T cells with tissue-resident phenotypes in the lungs. Lung-resident CD8+ T cells, for example, have been identified as crucial mediators of IAV infection [60]. Recent studies have explored novel vaccine strategies, such as using M2 epitopes on virus-like particles, to enhance lung-resident nucleoprotein-specific memory CD8+ T cell responses, which express tissue-resident markers CD69 and CD103 and provide heterosubtypic immunity [68,69]. Live-attenuated influenza virus (LAIV, FluMist) has been shown to induce long-term CD8+ and CD4+ lung tissue-resident memory T cells when administered intranasally [61]. Additionally, a vaccine using RNA phage Qβ-derived virus-like particles as an antigen has shown promise in inducing GC responses and follicular T helper cell development [70], indicating the potential for iBALT induction by IAV vaccines.

SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2 Vaccine

iBALT is considered to be a crucial defense mechanism against a range of pathogens, including SARS-CoV. Researchers have developed different strategies to induce iBALT formation, such as pre-treating SARS-CoV-challenged mice with a PCN, which results in obvious iBALT formation and provides beneficial effects with decreased lung damage and accelerated production of viral-specific antibodies [33]. Mucosal vaccines have been suggested to be more efficient than systemic vaccines, and novel mucosal vaccines are needed for SARS-CoV-2 infections [71]. CXCL13, an important molecule that attracts B cells to iBALT, has been suggested as an immunostimulant to initiate a better local immune response to prevent SARS-CoV-2 replication. A recombinant vaccine designed with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein formulated with a delta-inulin adjuvant can generate an iBALT structure in mice lungs when administered intratracheally and induce robust CD4+ or CD8+ TRMs in the lung, which are absent when administered intramuscularly and the lung-local neutralizing antibody can last for 6 months [72]. Studies have shown that a modified vaccinia virus Ankara (MAV) vaccine with stabilized membrane spike protein (MAV/S) can strongly induce iBALT formation in mice lungs. This kind of vaccine can provide better protection against SARS-CoV-2 challenge with strong CD8+ T cell response and neutralizing antibody response [73]. Reports showed that MAV vaccine provides host with 6-month durability of active antibody response. For example, an intranasal vaccination with Venezuelan equine encephalitis replicons launches protection against SARS-CoV with support of memory CD4+ T cells in airway [63]. Another vaccine using recombinant vaccinia virus encoding SARS-CoV spike glycoprotein can trigger virus-specific tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells in the lungs, which produce IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, granzyme B, and perforin to reduce viral load [74]. Studies that investigated the SARS-CoV-specific memory CD8+ T cells have shown that they can persist for at least 6 years in patients who had recovered from SARS. Another report showed a type of next-generation vaccines using adenoviral vectors and full-length S protein of prototype SARS-CoV-2 which is intranasally vaccinated could induce unique clusters of CD8+ TRM in the lung of mice which express markers: CD44, CD69, CD103, and CD49a. Memory T cells residing in iBALT based on allergic inflammation express the same resident markers: CD44, CD69, and CD103 [60], indicating the induction of TRM in iBALT [75]. Additionally, intranasal drops of RBD vaccine of prototype SARS-CoV-2 with polyethyleneimine (PEI) adjuvant can induce long-term memory in mice challenged with multiple variant strains of SARS-CoV-2, including Omicron. One year after the prime immunization, RBD-specific immunoglobulin (Ig)G titers in serum and RBD-specific IgA titers in BALF still exist at high level. Memory CD8+ T cells express higher resident markers, including CD69 and CD103 in the lung after RBD+PEI vaccination. Also, CD4+ T cells, which help establish CD8+ TRM, exhibit higher expression of CD69 and/or CD103 in the lung [76]. It is possible that TRMs can be induced in the lung by the vaccine, but further investigation is needed to confirm whether they are resident in iBALT.

Mtb Vaccine

The available vaccines against Mtb, such as Bacillus Calmette Guerin (BCG), can establish adaptive long-term memory and trained immunity against the pathogen. Memory T and B cells, once established, rapidly and robustly initiate immune activity against Mtb [83]. However, to boost the efficacy of BCG, different vaccine strategies have been explored, including inactivated or viable whole-cell vaccine and recombinant subunit vaccine with adjuvants.

An experiment of a cytomegalovirus (CMV)-vectored BCG vaccination in rhesus macaques showed reduced Mtb infection (compared to non-vaccination controls) with induction of tissue-resident Mtb-specific CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cell responses, about one year after the first vaccination. This finding suggests that vaccine-mediated control of Mtb is possible [77]. However, BCG has shown poorer and more variable protection against TB in adolescents and adults compared to children [84], highlighting the need for a new vaccine that targets both children and adults.

One such vaccine is VPM1002, a modified viable vaccine that is more efficient and safer than BCG. VPM1002 improves the immunogenicity of BCG by exchanging the urease C encoding gene with the listeriolysin-encoding gene of Listeria monocytogenes. This exchange promotes the release of Mtb antigens into the cytoplasmic matrix and initiates autophagy and apoptosis. VPM1002, once named rBCG, is reported to expand central memory CD4+T cells with a CXCR5+CCR7+phenotype in mice lymph nodes (LNs), and CD4+ memory T cells are thought to be initiated in LNs and subsequently migrate to lung [79]. Based on rBCG vaccination, antigen-specific T cells are recruited to mice lungs, and profound Th17 response after Mtb infection is evoked [78] (Table 1).

Several vaccines have been developed to protect against Mtb by inducing immune response and establishing immune memory. For instance, an intranasal vaccine named Ag85B-rHPIV2 (Ag85B-expressing human parainfluenza type 2 virus) can induce iBALT formation supported by Th17 cells and CD11b+CD11c+ DCs in mice lungs. This kind of vaccine-induced iBALT is highly organized by B220+ B-cell follicles with CD3+T cells and CD11c+ DCs around it and contains PNA+ B cells, suggesting the initiation of GC reaction. This iBALT is in charge of producing antibody and antigen-specific T cells, contributing to resolve inflammation in the lung since the inflammatory neutrophils and eosinophils decreased significantly in 2 weeks after the last immunization [80]. This vaccine was demonstrated to initiate an anit-Mtb immune response, and the vaccinated mice showed significantly reduced CFU of Mtb in lungs and spleens [81]. Another vaccine, the MtbΔsigH vaccine, which is made by Mtb mutant in SigH, induces profound iBALT formation and alleviates granuloma pathology, resulting in a strong immune response of central memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [50]. Additionally, a recent study showed that a vaccine consisting of a fusion protein CysVac2 and the delta-inulin adjuvant Advax™ can induce the formation of iBALT when administered intratracheally in mice. Infected lungs contained TRM-like cells expressing CD44 and CD69 and low level of CD103 and killer cell lectin-like receptor G1 (KLRG1) that may reside in iBALT and provide TRM with a survival niche [82,85].

Concluding Remarks and Perspectives

The respiratory tract serves as the first line of defense against respiratory pathogens, relying mainly on mucosal immunity. Respiratory pathogens such as influenza virus, SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV2, and Mtb have a significant impact on the respiratory system and can be life-threatening, resulting in high morbidity and mortality. This review focuses on the formation and regulation of iBALT and immune memory, specifically in the lung following infections. We mainly focus on the examples from influenza virus, SARS-CoV/SARS-CoV-2, and Mtb infections to illustrate the unique and characteristic mucosal immune response that occurs locally, particularly in iBALT. The organization of iBALT structure is tightly associated with the disease outcome. However, there are still some argumentations about the protective or pathological roles of iBALT, and how long iBALT could stably sustain after inflammation is settled. What's the effectiveness of protection? Moreover, methods of inducing and controlling iBALT formation remain a big challenge which is beneficial for getting a better therapeutic intervention or vaccine development.

In the future, understanding the formation and regulation of iBALT structure would provide valuable insights for developing effective therapies for pulmonary infectious diseases. Additionally, vaccines are identified as an effective strategy for treating or preventing these infections. A significant challenge in future vaccine design will be to induce life-long memory, which involves enhancing the accumulation of memory immune cells not only circulating in the lymphatic system but also present in peripheral organs like iBALT in the lung. Thus, as the initial entrance of pathogens, the lung tissue is an excellent target for inducing life-long memory through vaccination.
