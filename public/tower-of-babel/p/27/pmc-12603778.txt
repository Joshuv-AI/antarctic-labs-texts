Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation‐driven modulation of unbiased functional connectivity in the supracallosal anterior cingulate cortex causally ameliorates information processing speed in amnestic mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Jiu Chen, Lijuan Gao, Zigang Che, Shanshan Chen, Chen Xue, Huimin Wu, Wenzhang Qi, Yanjiao Huang, Jia Fan, Yeming Zhong, Caiyun Zou, Qiang Li, Xiaoqin Liu, Bing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAmnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) exhibits biased functional connectivity (FC) abnormalities impairing neural plasticity modulation. This study aimed to identify unbiased FC deficits using a brain‐wide association study (BWAS) and investigate repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)‐driven plasticity restoration.METHODSBWAS identified unbiased FC‐altered voxels (robustness‐validated). Region‐of‐interest (ROI)‐wise FC analysis localized disrupted circuits, which were modulated through precuneus‐targeted rTMS. Effective connectivity (EC) tested whether the precuneus exerted a causal influence on these disrupted circuits. Correlation analyses linked FC plasticity to cognitive and clinical outcomes.RESULTSEleven brain regions with 31 altered unbiased FC circuits were robustly identified, centered on the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). rTMS causally restored FC in the right supracallosal ACC and postcentral gyrus, correlating with improved information processing speed (IPS). Remarkably, 80.77% (21/26) of aMCI responded clinically to rTMS.DISCUSSIONThis study first maps unbiased FC lesions in aMCI, confirming rTMS‐mediated ACC plasticity causally enhances IPS. These findings inform network‐targeted therapies to delay Alzheimer's disease (AD) progression.Highlights This is the first study to robustly map unbiased FC lesions in aMCI patients using a BWAS.rTMS causally restored FC in right supracallosal ACC in aMCI patients.FC and EC recovery demonstrated causal links to improvements in IPS and MoCA scores.Remarkably, 80.77% (21/26) of aMCI patients responded clinically to rTMS modulation.

BACKGROUND

Amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) is widely recognized as a prodromal form of Alzheimer's disease (AD), 1 , 2 yet its pathophysiology and targeted therapies remain challenging. Resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)‐based functional connectivity (FC) analyses 3 help clarify aMCI's neuropathology mechanisms and link it to neural plasticity and cognitive deficits. 4 , 5 However, most studies focus on biased FC alterations via region‐of‐interest (ROI)‐based analyses, 6 which can introduce biases, hampering accurate modulation of neural plasticity to improve cognitive deficits in aMCI.

Brain‐wide association studies (BWASs), inspired by genome‐wide association studies, identify unbiased FC alterations without a priori ROI selection. 7 This approach has been extensively used to identify unbiased lesion localization in psychiatric disorders. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Therefore, this method is expected to find unbiased and consistent aMCI‐related neuroimaging circuits.

In this study, we employed the BWAS approach to identify unbiased altered FC circuits in aMCI within the first cohort. Subsequently, in an independent cohort, these identified circuits were experimentally modulated with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS). Furthermore, spectral dynamic causal modeling (spDCM) was applied to map effective connectivity (EC), 12 , 13 , 14 which identifies the causal effects of precuneus‐targeted rTMS on these circuits and establishes causal relationships between FC alterations and cognitive recovery in aMCI. Drawing on rTMS network regulation evidence, 4 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 we hypothesize that if an unbiased pathological FC circuit contributes to cognitive decline, rTMS‐driven modulation of this circuit may induce neural plasticity, thereby ameliorating both clinical manifestations and cognitive impairments.

METHODS

Participants

Data were obtained from NBH‐ADsnp2. Inclusion/exclusion criteria, ethical approval (Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University), and informed consent is detailed in Supporting Information methods S.1.

Initially, a total of 140 subjects were enrolled in this study, comprising 76 healthy controls (CN) and 64 aMCI. During fMRI scanning, two CN and three aMCI were excluded due to excessive head motion (> 3.0 mm/°), which would affect imaging data quality. Following this screening, the final valid cohort included 135 subjects: 74 CN (31 male, 43 female; age 63.28 ± 6.46 years; Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA] scores 25.25 ± 2.35) and 61 aMCI (20 male, 41 female; age 64.38 ± 7.81 years; MoCA scores 22.51 ± 3.20). A subset of 26 aMCI (eight male, 18 female; age 67.23 ± 7.00 years; MoCA scores 24.65 ± 2.17) received a 4‐week course of rTMS, along with follow‐up fMRI scans and cognitive assessments.

Neuropsychological assessments

Information processing speed (IPS) via Digit Symbol Substitution Test and Stroop Test B – Episodic Memory (EM) was assessed via the Auditory Verbal Learning Test–Immediate Recall (AVLT–IM), AVLT–5‐min Delayed Recall, AVLT–20‐min Delayed Recall, Logical Memory Test–20‐min Delayed Recall, and the Rey Complex Figure Test 20‐min delayed recall, with details in prior studies 15 , 19 , 20 , 21 and in Supporting Information methods S.2 and S.3.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: A systematic review of PubMed‐indexed literature revealed that individuals with aMCI exhibited biased FC abnormalities, impairing their capacity for accurate and effective neural plasticity modulation. Notably, unbiased FC deficits in aMCI remain underexplored, alongside uncertain causality between symptom‐imaging associations and disease trajectory. Interpretation: This study is the first to robustly reveal the unbiased lesion localization of brain‐wide FC and causally confirms that rTMS‐driven modulation of unbiased FC in the supracallosal ACC causally improves IPS in aMCI. Our findings elucidate the biological basis of brain network dysfunction in aMCI, paving the way for targeted interventions to alleviate cognitive symptoms and potentially delay AD progression. Future directions: The manuscript proposes a neuroimaging‐based regulatory framework for rTMS that requires further validation across larger and more diverse datasets. The 4‐week rTMS intervention demonstrated short‐term efficacy, while long‐term effects have yet to be investigated. Future trials should incorporate prolonged follow‐up periods. While we have verified how stimulating the precuneus leads to ACC modulation at the levels of FC and EC, future studies need to confirm this regulatory mechanism from the perspective of basic research. Further investigation is needed to determine whether network‐targeted rTMS, as a disease‐modifying intervention, can establish delayed AD progression through neuroplasticity‐driven cognitive restoration.

MRI data acquisition, structural MRI, and fMRI data preprocessing

Image acquisition parameters are provided in Supporting Information methods S.4.

The image processing procedures were as previously described 22 and provided in Supporting Information methods S.5 and S.6. Briefly, the image processing procedures included slice‐timing and head‐motion corrections (subjects with excessive head motion [> 3.0 mm/°] were excluded), 23 , 24 functional‐structural co‐registration, nuisance covariate removal (Friston 24‐parameter regression; scans losing ≥ 50% volumes were discarded), spatial normalization (4 × 4× 4 mm3), spatial smoothing (6 mm full‐width half‐maximum [FWHM]), and temporal filtering (0.01 to 0.1 Hz).

For EC analysis, the image processing procedures included slice‐timing and head‐motion corrections, functional‐structural co‐registration, spatial normalization (3 × 3 × 3 mm3), spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM), temporal filtering (0.01 to 0.1 Hz), and detrending.

Unbiased lesion localization of brain‐wide FC related to aMCI using voxel‐wise association study (BWAS)

Each resting‐state fMRI image comprises 23,178 voxels. Pairwise voxel time series were analyzed using Pearson cross‐correlation and subjected to Fisher's z‐transformation to assess FC for each subject. To identify FC differences between aMCI and CN, two‐sample t tests were performed on z‐transformed correlation coefficients from all pairwise voxel combinations ([23,178 × 23,178]/2), adjusting for age, education, framewise displacement, and total intracranial volume (TIV).

Calculation of measure of association (MA)

MA quantifies voxel‐brain disorder association as MA(i) = Nα (Nα = voxel links with family‐wise error [FWE]‐corrected p < 3.33 × 10−2), where a larger MA means greater aMCI FC alterations. Significant voxels were identified using MA > 40 and cluster size ≥ 20. Although MA highlighted voxels with differential FC, it did not specify the affected brain regions. To interpret results, these voxels were mapped to networks showing altered FC links between aMCI and CN using the AAL3 atlas (Table S1 and SI methods S.7). 25

Robustness analysis of BWAS

To assess the robustness of significant brain regions discovered in prior analyses utilizing the entire dataset, we performed a time‐domain half‐split reliability analysis by splitting fMRI time series for each participant into two halves, 11 recomputing MA separately, defining ROIs in one half, and cross‐validating FC changes in the other, controlling for confounders.

Altered neural circuits identified using ROI‐wise FC analysis with BWAS‐guided anatomical localization

MA analysis revealed voxel‐aMCI associations but lacked anatomical specificity. We addressed this via ROI‐based FC analysis, defining ROIs as clusters with ≥20 MA‐significant voxels. Average BOLD time series were extracted from these ROIs, and FC between them was analyzed using Pearson correlation. False discovery rate (FDR) correction (p < 0.05) ensured significance, enabling both quantitative association strength measurement and localization of aMCI‐affected brain regions.

rTMS modulation of altered FC in aMCI

rTMS was administered at 10 Hz over the precuneus using a Magstim Rapid2 device with a 70‐mm figure‐8 coil positioned at the Pz site (10 to 20 EEG system). 26 Each daily session consisted of trains of 1000 pulses delivered at 100% motor threshold intensity. Stimulation consisted of 4‐s ON periods with 56‐s intervals across 25 sessions (∼25 min/day). Detailed protocols are described in Supporting Information methods S.8.

Characterizing how rTMS‐driven modulation causally affected the identified circuits using BWAS

First, we performed a FC analysis to characterize how rTMS‐driven modulation affected the identified circuits using BWAS. Time‐series data were extracted from the precuneus‐targeted stimulation site and the corresponding brain regions within these circuits. Pearson correlation coefficients were then calculated between the extracted time course and all voxels within the circuits.

Second, based on the FC changes observed in brain regions modulated by precuneus‐targeted rTMS, we further conducted spDCM to map EC between the precuneus and these regions. 12 , 13 , 14 This approach aimed to validate that precuneus‐targeted rTMS exerted a causal influence on the altered brain areas, yielding insights into mechanisms and therapeutic potential. A parametric empirical Bayes framework was used to identify the changes in EC before and after 4 weeks of rTMS modulation. Detailed protocols for spDCM are described in Supporting Information methods S.9.

Relationships between changes in FC, EC, and changes in EM and IPS in aMCI after rTMS modulation

To empirically validate the causal relationships between rTMS‐induced FC changes, EC changes, EM, and IPS improvements in aMCI, we performed paired t tests to compare pre‐ and post‐rTMS FC and assessed individual correlations between FC and EC alterations and clinical changes. This approach enabled us to dissect rTMS causal effects on FC and EC dynamics and their association with EM, IPS, and MoCA changes in aMCI.

Clinical efficacy evaluation of aMCI

To illustrate the clinical efficacy of aMCI before and after 4 weeks of rTMS modulation, we investigated the changes in EM and clinical diagnosis status for aMCI, and calculated the number of cases in which aMCI improved to non‐amnestic mild cognitive impairment (naMCI), subjective cognitive decline (SCD), and CN, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Two‐sample t tests were used to compare demographics, neuropsychological measures, and head rotation between groups (chi‐squared for gender). Z‐transformed composite scores reduced variability (Supporting Information methods S.2 and Table S2 for details). Paired t tests (plus 10,000‐resampling bootstrap) were used to analyze pre‐post rTMS changes in aMCI (neuropsychological measures, FC, EC). Hedge's g quantified effect sizes (g values with 95% confidence intervals [CIs]; see Supporting Information methods S.10); Bonferroni‐corrected multiple comparisons were also performed (p < 0.05 significant).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to identify unbiased FC alterations in aMCI using a BWAS approach. It establishes a symptom‐pathophysiology association by manipulating unbiased FC circuit changes using the rTMS modulation and assessing their impact on clinical symptoms and cognitive recovery.

Our results identified 31 unbiased FC alterations in aMCI, mainly in the frontoparietal regions and ACC (bilateral pregenual/supracallosal ACC as key hubs with disrupted FC). Robustness analyses confirmed consistent significant regions across both halves of the fMRI BOLD halves. Prior studies highlighted the anatomical and functional subdivision of ACC subregions, particularly in receiving inputs from the orbitofrontal cortex. 27 The subgenual ACC integrates reward/punishment signals, emotion regulation, and action‐outcome linking, 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 whereas the supracallosal ACC processes non‐reward and aversive stimuli. 34 Our results suggest that disconnection between these ACC hubs and the orbitofrontal cortex underlies aMCI‐related cognitive deficits, particularly in valence‐based information processing.

Critically, extensive evidence supports direct and indirect connections between the orbitofrontal cortex and the hippocampal memory system via the pregenual/supracallosal ACC and the lateral orbitofrontal cortex. 32 , 35 Integrating these observations with our findings implies that ACC‐mediated network disruptions – specifically in reward/emotion valuation – may contribute to EM deficits in aMCI. The observed subregion‐specific damage in ACC areas and their disconnection from frontoparietal networks likely drive these pathologies.

Our most compelling finding revealed that 4‐week precuneus‐targeted rTMS causally reversed unbiased FC alterations and improved EM and IPS in aMCI, verified through both functional and EC analyses. Notably, FC plasticity in the right supracallosal ACC (a key hub) emerged as a critical driver of IPS enhancement, aligning with prior theories, 36 and linking network remodeling to functional recovery (IPS predicts EM).

We propose that the mechanism by which rTMS targeting the precuneus induces modulation of the ACCsup might involve the following possibilities: (1) as a default mode network (DMN) hub, the precuneus connects to ACC via the superior longitudinal fasciculus. 37 , 38 By enhancing precuneus excitability, rTMS may reinforce its role as a “network orchestrator,” 26 normalizing aMCI's aberrant DMN and improving IPS/MoCA. (2) As a “rich club” node, the precuneus, when stimulated, may indirectly affect ACCsup via network connections. 39 , 40 (3) Under top‐down control and salience mediation, precuneus activation sends bottom‐up signals to the ACCsup (a salience network hub), regulating DMN/ECN interactions and enhancing cognitive flexibility. 38 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 (4) Finally, per global neuronal workspace theory, rTMS strengthens precuneus–ACCsup long‐range connections, enabling large‐scale neuronal synchronization. 45 , 46 However, our experiment cannot fully distinguish rTMS‐specific effects from non‐specific factors, which needs future study.

Critically, IPS served as a key moderator in the relationship between EM, FC reorganization, and the enhancement of EC within the left inferior parietal lobe–right occipital network – a novel finding that refines theoretical frameworks for targeted cognitive rehabilitation in aMCI. 47 This mechanism maps onto our core clinical outcome: significant post‐intervention improvements in EM.

The clinical impact is further underscored by an 80.77% (21/26) response rate, with 11 patients transitioned to non‐amnestic MCI, six improved to SCD, and four reverted to CN, while only five showed no change. Therefore, these findings not only validate network‐targeted rTMS as a disease‐modifying intervention but also establish proof of concept for delaying AD progression through neuroplasticity‐driven cognitive restoration.

Limitations

(1) Four‐week rTMS shows short‐term efficacy, but long‐term effects (especially on cognitive decline) are unstudied. Future trials will extend follow‐up periods beyond 4 weeks. (2) Pre–post design lacked a sham‐rTMS control (risk of confounders). To mitigate these limitations, we implemented complementary analytical strategies: When evaluating neuromodulatory effects, we combined FC and EC analyses to disentangle direct rTMS‐induced causal influences from correlative patterns. And the literature supports rTMS efficacy. 4 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Future studies will reequire sham groups. (3) Sample size, though relatively large for rTMS research, is limited; replication in larger cohorts is necessary. Finally, single‐center data require multicenter trials to validate generalizability.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study is the first to map unbiased brain‐wide FC alterations in aMCI and to demonstrate that rTMS‐driven modulation of supracallosal ACC connectivity causally enhances IPS. By elucidating the biological basis of network dysfunction in aMCI, our work paves the way for network‐targeted interventions aimed at delaying AD progression.
