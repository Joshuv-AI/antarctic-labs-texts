Eukaryotic and bacterial gut communities vary along a lifestyle-associated urbanization gradient: comparative analysis of Germany and Madagascar
Authors: Johanna Saalfrank, Malte Christoph Rühlemann, Philipp Rausch, Jana Christina Hey, Rivo Andry Rakotoarivelo, Tahinamandranto Rasamoelina, Raphael Rakotozandrindrainy, Njari Randriamampionona, Norbert Georg Schwarz, Ravo Razafindrakoto, Daniela Fusco, Andre Franke, Corinna Bang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntestinal eukaryotes, often neglected in gut microbiome studies, play crucial roles in human health and cause life-threatening diseases affecting millions worldwide. This neglect has also been driven by the underrepresentation of samples from regions with a high prevalence of (parasitic) intestinal eukaryotes. As a result, the overall understanding of how intestinal eukaryotes vary among broad lifestyle and socioeconomic differences, remains limited. Addressing this gap is increasingly urgent given the global rise of urbanization and industrialization and their profound effects on lifestyle, pathogen exposure, and environmental factors. We characterized the diversity and composition of eukaryotic and bacterial microbiota in 1387 fecal samples from Madagascar (Andina, Ankazomborona, Tsiroanomandidy) and Germany (Kiel), spanning a composite gradient of urbanization-associated lifestyles. Using a parallel approach of 18S V4–V5 rRNA and 16S V3–V4 rRNA amplicon gene sequencing, we identified distinct regional patterns in eukaryotic and bacterial community composition. Malagasy cohorts showed higher prevalence of helminths (e.g. Schistosoma, Necator) and protozoa (e.g. Entamoeba, Dientamoeba). Notably, the diversity of particularly food-associated fungi increased along the composite urbanization-associated lifestyle gradient, peaking in samples from Germany. Bacterial 16S amplicon sequencing confirmed and extended known geographical differences, showing a dominance of Bacteroides in Germany versus Prevotella and Firmicutes in Madagascar. This work highlights the importance of integrating eukaryotic and prokaryotic data, as well as considering different lifestyle-associated factors in microbiome research. We further highlight the need for deeper investigation into the role of dietary and environmental fungi in the human gut ecosystem.

Introduction

The human gastrointestinal (GI) tract is colonized by a diverse community of microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, viruses, and eukaryotes. 1 While the bacterial part of the microbiota has been characterized extensively, intestinal eukaryotes remain largely underexplored. 2 , 3 This is mainly due to difficult implementation of high-throughput sequencing methods for eukaryotic taxa, a lack of well-curated eukaryotic databases, 4 and the underrepresentation of samples from regions with a high prevalence of intestinal (parasitic) eukaryotes. 4 Consequently, fundamental questions regarding how intestinal eukaryotic communities differ across populations living in diverse environments and socioeconomic contexts remain largely unresolved.

However, throughout evolution, intestinal eukaryotes, including protozoa, fungi, and helminths, have coevolved and coexisted with their human hosts, 5 forming a dynamic ecological network, encompassing parasitic, opportunistic, commensal, and mutualistic interactions. 6 In fact, many intestinal eukaryotes are well-known pathogens that are associated with a substantial global burden of diseases. Soil-transmitted helminths affect an estimated 24% of the global population. 7 In addition, pathogenic fungal infections pose severe health risks, affecting over 300 million people worldwide, thereby causing severe infections and high morbidity in immunocompromised individuals. 8 At the same time, epidemiological and experimental evidence indicate that several intestinal eukaryotes might be beneficial for human health and their importance may differ locally. 9 Chronic helminth infections e.g. can exert protective effects against allergies. 10 In addition, several protozoa, including Blastocystis and Dientamoeba, historically classified as pathogens in their entirety, are now in part recognized as ubiquitously prevalent in healthy individuals 11 , 12 and have been shown to play beneficial roles in gut homeostasis and immune system modulation. 13 These contrasting findings challenge the strict binary classification of intestinal eukaryotes as either pathogenic or commensal and highlight the need for a more nuanced characterization of the eukaryotic part of the intestinal microbiome. 3 Particularly with respect to their impact on human health, the role and impact of eukaryotes within the human microbiota might vary across different global regions, thereby also reflecting differences in environmental exposures, diet, sanitation, and healthcare. Thus, these variations might be influenced and shaped by different lifestyles that are associated with urbanization gradients. Urbanization is a process by which populations concentrate in towns and cities, a phenomenon that has intensified in the last 200 y, 14 mainly driven by industrialization and accompanied by socioeconomic development and interrelated changes in lifestyle. These processes are known to affect human health in contrasting directions: less infectious diseases through improvement of medical care and sanitation on the one hand, and rising exposure to environmental pollution as well as an increase of lifestyle-related chronic diseases on the other hand. 15 Particularly, the latter has been attributed to changes in the gut microbial ecosystem driven by e.g. dietary shifts. 16

However, since these changes are so far mainly described for bacterial taxa only, 2 , 15 , 17 the knowledge on how intestinal eukaryotic communities change between humans living in differently urbanized and industrialized environments with associated differences in lifestyle and infrastructure remains poorly understood. 3 To address this particular research question, we analyzed a unique cohort that brings together 1387 fecal samples from Madagascar and Germany, which were analyzed using the same 18S and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing workflow. As urbanization and industrialization are closely linked and cannot be disentangled, Germany represents a densely urbanized and industrialized setting, whereas the different Malagasy sites reflect varying degrees of urbanization within a less industrialized national context. Madagascar is one of the few countries that is still considered predominantly rural, 18 though it is currently undergoing rapid growth of urban centers, in which 60% of the urban population live in unplanned settlements with limited sanitation. 16 Accordingly, we interpret observed microbial patterns along a composite lifestyle gradient rather than attributing effects to urbanization or industrialization only.

By systematically characterizing diversity, composition, and distribution of fecal eukaryotes, and by complementing these data with eukaryote-bacteria co-abundance patterns, we provide novel insights into how intestinal microbiota composition differs across contrasting global environments that vary in terms of socioeconomic development and associated lifestyle changes.

Materials and methods

Specimen collection in Madagascar was conducted as part of the cross-sectional SCHISDIMA study. 19 1042 fecal samples were collected without preservation medium between March 2020 and December 2020 at three Primary Health Care Centers (PHCCs) in Madagascar: Andina (n = 499), Tsiroanomandidy (n = 324), and Ankazomborona (n = 219). Stool was collected at home, stored under cool conditions and delivered within 24 h to the PHCC, where they were frozen (−20 °C). At the Centre d'Infectiologie Charles Meriéux, Antananarivo, samples were stored (−80 °C), before shipment on dry ice to the Institute for Clinical Molecular Biology in Kiel, Germany. Ethical approval was granted by the National Ethics Committee of Madagascar (ref. no. N°23-MSANP/CERBM, 05/03/2018) and the Ethics Committee of the Hamburg State Medical Chamber, Germany (ref. no. PV7019-4419-BO-ff, 29/10/2019). Sample collection was conducted in accordance with the Nagoya Protocol (protocol 015 aaI4/MEDD/sG/DGGE/DAPRNE/ANc-APA-03/2023-MEDD/DGGE/DAPRNEIAPA, 12/11/2023). Additional 375 age- and sex-matched samples from Germany were selected from two independent cohorts recruited at the University Hospital Schleswig Holstein, Campus Kiel, where the study was approved by the local Ethics Committee at Kiel University (AZ A103/14 and D441/16). German stool samples were likewise collected at home without preservation medium and sent within 24–48 h to the Institute for Clinical Molecular Biology in Kiel, where they were immediately frozen. 20 , 21 The “degree of urbanization” for each location was classified using the DEGURBA index (Table 1), a standardized classification system based on population density and settlement structures that facilitates comparability. 22 However, this index primarily describes geographic settlement density and does not capture socioeconomic or lifestyle-related differences among the study cohorts.

DNA was extracted from all fecal samples using the same standardized protocol as previously described. 23 Due to logistical constraints, Malagasy and German samples were processed in separate batches; however, identical reagents, protocols, and sequencing platforms were used for all samples, and subsequent bioinformatic processing and analysis were performed jointly to minimize potential batch effects. Although no extraction controls were included for the Malagasy samples thereby representing a limitation, extraction reagents from the same batch of chemicals were used in parallel projects, in which extraction controls were incorporated. These controls showed no evidence of contamination in 16S rRNA gene sequencing, indicating no systematic reagent contamination during that period (Supplementary Table S1).

For subsequent sequencing of the 18S V4–V5 region, the primer set 616*F (5′-TTAAARVGYTCGTAGTYG-3′) and 1132R (5′-CCGTCAATTHCTTYAART-3′) 24 was used. The reaction volume consisted of 5 μL 5× Phusion HF buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 0.3 μL Phusion™ HotStart II DNA polymerase, 0.5 μL 10 mM dNTP-Mix, 1 μL 10 mM primer mix (Metabion International AG, Planegg, Germany), 22.2 μL RNA-free water, and 2 μL of the respective DNA. PCR was conducted as follows: 98 °C for 30 s, subsequent 35 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 40 s followed by 72 °C for 10 min. Genomic DNA from Caenorhabditis elegans served as positive control in each batch, and RNA-free water as negative control. The taxonomic composition of negative and positive controls, as well as representative samples before and after prevalence-based decontamination and taxonomic filtering, are shown in the supplementary material (Supplementary Figure SF1).

PCR products were verified using the QIAxcel Advanced and purified using the Monarch® PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (5 μg) (New England Biolabs Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol for dsDNA < 2 kb. Cleaned DNA was eluted in 30 μL. Barcoding was performed using Nextera® XT Index Kit v2 (Illumina Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). Adapter sequences i7 and i5 were ligated to the samples according to the manufacturer's protocol. 16S rRNA gene amplification and barcoding of V3–V4 region was performed as described earlier. 25

PCR products were normalized using SequalPrep™ Normalization Plate Kit (Thermo Fisher), pooled to equimolar concentrations, and sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq™ platform with v3 chemistry and 2×300 bp read length (MiSeqFGx; Illumina Inc.). Demultiplexing was based on zero mismatches in the barcode sequences; the raw sequencing data was stored in FASTQ files. Amplicon primer sequences were subsequently removed using cutadapt (v4.6). All analysis steps were performed in R (v4.2). Data processing followed the DADA2 workflow 26 (v1.34.0), resulting in amplicon sequence variant (ASV) abundance tables. In detail, paired-end FASTQ files were quality-filtered using “filterAndTrim” with truncation lengths of 250 bp (forward) and 210 bp (reverse). To remove low-quality leading bases, additional 5 bp were trimmed from the 5′-end of both reads (trimLeft = c(5,5)). Filtering thresholds were set to maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,2), and truncQ = 5; PhiX reads were removed (rm.phix = TRUE). Error models were trained separately for forward and reverse reads using 100 million bases (leanErrors, nbases = 1e8). Denoising was performed using DADA with default parameters. For 16S gene sequencing data, forward and reverse reads were merged using overlap-based merging (mergePairs), whereas for 18S gene sequencing data reads were concatenated using mergePairs with justConcatenate = TRUE, since the generated amplicon (~516 bp) exceeded the overlap achievable after quality trimming of the 2× 300 bp reads. 26 Sequencing tables were generated with makeSequenceTable, and chimeric sequences were removed using removeBimeraDenovo with consensus method. Sequencing quality was assessed by tracking 18S amplicon read counts across processing steps (Supplementary Table S1). The median number of input reads per sample was 26,405 (IQR 16,453–41,872), with 77.38% retaining after filtering, and 99.60% retaining after denoising/concatenation. Following chimera removal, the median number of non-chimeric reads per sample was 9361 (IQR 5603–14,928).

Eukaryotic ASVs were annotated to the PR2 reference database (v5.0.0), and prevalence-based decontamination was applied using R:decontam (default settings). Bacterial ASVs were annotated using the Naive Bayesian Classifier in DADA2 and the Ribosomal Database Project taxonomy (v16), with species-level classification where possible.

Across 1395 samples, eukaryotic 18S rRNA amplicon sequencing reads generated a median of 9403 reads/sample (IQR: 5647.5–14,442.5; range: 0–104,866), resulting in 16,764,884 reads assigned to 89,858 ASVs. Of these, 5,284,226 reads (31.52% of total reads) remained unclassified at the genus level, consistent with limited reference coverage for intestinal eukaryotes reported earlier. 4 Prior to eukaryotic analysis, ASVs assigned to Bacteria, Archaea, plants, aquatic organisms, and dietary eukaryotic sequences (e.g. Gallus, Capra) were excluded (Supplementary Table S1). Fungal taxa associated with food production, fermentation, processing, or storage were retained. After excluding samples with missing metadata and those with zero ASVs, 1387 samples were retained for downstream eukaryotic analyses. Rarefaction was not applied; instead, diversity analyses were performed on the non-rarefied ASV tables, whereas all other downstream analyses were performed on genus-level aggregated data transformed to relative abundances with missing taxa reassigned (“Family_Unassigned”/“Unassigned”).

Across 1393 samples, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing reads generated a median of 21,700 reads/sample (IQR: 14,791–31,170; range: 0–212,636), resulting in 40,650,468 total reads assigned to 159,840 ASVs. Of these, 70,057 ASVs corresponding to 5,722,554 reads (14.08%) remained unclassified at genus level. Only sequences assigned to Bacteria were included in the downstream analysis of prokaryotic ASVs, excluding photosynthetic phyla.

Following independent quality filtering steps, the 16S dataset was reduced to 1286 samples after rarefaction (10,000 reads). Of these, 1280 samples overlapped with the eukaryotic dataset and were included in the final analyses. The final bacterial dataset comprised 81,799 ASVs from 1280 participants (mean age 40.72 y, 50.31% female; see Supplementary Table S2 for participant flow).

α-Diversity on ASVs was calculated using the Shannon diversity index (natural logarithm) applying R:vegan. Associations between microbial diversity and location or sex were assessed using pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for nonparametric data with Bonferroni correction. To account for potential confounding, multivariable linear regression models were fitted including log10-transformed sequencing depth, age, sex, location, and cohort as covariates. In these models, Shannon diversity was used as the outcome, and regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were reported. To further interpret group differences while accounting for covariates, estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were calculated using R:emmeans with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction for multiple testing. Because cohort and location were partially confounded, marginal means were estimated from models including location, age, sex, and sequencing depth, without cohort, to obtain estimable location-level contrasts. To assess whether observed patterns related to urbanization were driven by cross-country differences, sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the dataset to Malagasy sites only and repeating the same multivariable modelling framework.

β-Diversity was visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity via metaMDS in R:vegan. Differences in community composition were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, 999 permutations) via R:adonis2, to evaluate the effect of location, sex, and age on both datasets, and the presence of eukaryotic genera on the bacterial community composition. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was tested using R:betadisper (999 permutations) on the 18S dataset to assess whether significant PERMANOVA results could be influenced by within-group dispersion.

Relative abundance comparisons included taxa with ≥100 total reads and genera with a minimum overall relative abundance of 0.01% (eukaryotes) or 0.5% (prokaryotes). Taxa were agglomerated at genus level and missing genus annotations were replaced by the corresponding family name, where available. Sample-level abundances were normalized by dividing sample-level abundances by the overall mean abundance of that genus across all samples, allowing comparison of relative genus enrichment between locations. Mean scaled abundance per genus was then calculated for each location, log10-transformed, and visualized using R:pheatmap. Differences in the scaled genus abundances among locations were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, and p-values were adjusted via BH (p adj < 0.05 considered significant). For genera showing significant overall differences, Dunn's test as a post-hoc test (FDR adjusted) was performed for pairwise comparison between locations using R:FSA.

For core microbiota analysis, eukaryotic as well as bacterial core taxa were defined as genera present in at least 50% of samples within a certain location. The shared core microbiota was defined as the intersection of location-specific cores across all locations.

As an orthogonal validation of the sequencing-based Saccharomyces signals, S. cerevisiae was quantified by species-specific qPCR in a randomly selected age-and sex-matched subset of samples from Kiel, Germany (n = 50) and Ankazomborona, Madagascar (n = 48). Reactions were performed using SYBR Green chemistry with serial dilutions of S. cerevisiae DNA (1ng-1fg) as standards. Absolute concentrations were compared between locations using Wilcoxon rank-sum testing.

Community-level associations between bacterial and eukaryotic assemblages were assessed using Procrustes and Mantel tests based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices on filtered 18S and 16S datasets (n = 1280). Pairwise associations between eukaryotic and bacterial genera were derived via pairwise correlation of clr-transformed relative abundances, via Kendall-, Spearman-, Pearson- and distance correlation 27 (based on 5000 permutations) using R:base, R:compositions, R:stats, and R:energy (1.7.1–2).

Taxa included in the bi-partite networks included genera from independently filtered fungal/eukaryotic as well as bacterial datasets. Therefore, taxonomic count data were transformed to relative abundances within each sample. Missing genus annotations in 18S datasets were replaced with corresponding family-level annotations where available, and taxa was subsequently agglomerated at genus level. Genus-level taxa were then filtered by prevalence thresholds (>0.001 relative abundance in ≥5% of samples for 18S and in ≥20% for 16S). For network construction, only genera present in these prevalence-filtered 16S and 18S datasets (fungal and protist/helminths taxa) were retained. Bipartite networks were generated from significant associations using distance correlation (p permuted ≤ 0.005) via R:igraph and R:ggraph. Edge weights were defined by the magnitude of distance correlation coefficient, capturing linear and non-linear relationships. The potential direction of the pairwise relationships was inferred from corresponding signed Spearman rank correlations coefficient.

Elevated eukaryotic diversity in Germany correlates with higher fungal diversity

Eukaryotic richness and evenness varied significantly across locations, as measured by the Shannon diversity index (H) (Figure 1A). Observed median α-diversity was highest in the semi-dense peri-urban site Ankazomborona (H = 3.98; IQR: 3.45–4.31; range: 0.00–5.16), followed by dense urban Kiel (H = 3.88; IQR: 3.57–4.18; range: 0.00–5.13), semi-dense urban Tsiroanomandidy (H = 3.69; IQR: 3.23–4.06; range: 0.00–4.87) and dispersed rural area Andina (H = 3.46; IQR: 2.90–3.87; range: 0.00–4.69). Differences were significant among most locations (p adj < 0.001), except between Kiel and Ankazomborona (p adj = 0.50).

Samples from Kiel showed the highest median fungal diversity (H = 3.03; IQR: 1.75—3.54; range: 0.00–4.12), significantly higher than all Malagasy locations (Andina-Kiel: p adj < 2 × 10−16; Ankazomborona-Kiel: p adj < 2 × 10−16; Tsiroanomandidy-Kiel: p adj = 6.9 × 10−11). Fungal diversity was second highest in Tsiroanomandidy (H = 2.03; IQR: 0.68–3.04; range: 0.00–4.48) and differed modestly from that in Andina (H = 1.54; IQR: 0.55–2.77; range: 0.00–4.43; p adj = 0.02). Ankazomborona displayed the lowest fungal diversity (H = 0.69; IQR: 0.00–2.34; range: 0.00–3.95) (Andina-Ankazomborona: p adj = 2.5 × 10−4, Ankazomborona-Tsiroanomandidy: p adj = 7.5 × 10−8).

In contrast, helminth and protozoa α-diversity displayed an inverse pattern: Ankazomborona's diversity (H = 3.28; IQR: 2.21–3.82; range: 0.00–4.63) was significantly highest (Ankazomborona-Kiel: p adj < 2 × 10−16; Ankazomborona-Andina: p adj = 5.5 × 10−13, Ankazomborona-Tsiroanomandidy: p adj = 1.0 × 10−8), followed by Tsiroanomandidy (H = 2.70; IQR: 0.00–3.36; range: 0.00–4.76) and Andina (H = 2.59; IQR: 0.00–3.26; range: 0.00–4.32), which significantly differed from Kiel, where the helminth/protozoan diversity (H = 1.57; IQR: 0.00–2.65; range: 0.00–4.16) was the lowest (Kiel-Andina: p adj = 4.6 × 10−08; Kiel-Tsiroanomandidy: p adj = 2.5 × 10−7).

Adjustment for age, sex, sequencing depth, and cohort attenuated some pairwise differences. In multivariable models, eukaryotic α-diversity was significantly lower in Ankazomborona compared to Andina (β = −0.24, 95%CI: −0.33 to −0.16, p < 0.001), while no significant difference was observed between Andina and Kiel (β = 0.04, 95%CI: −0.03 to 0.11, p = 0.288) and Tsiroanomandidy (β = −0.06, 95%CI: −0.13 to 0.01, p = 0.11). Sequencing depth was a strong positive predictor of diversity (p < 2 × 10−16), whereas sex and age had no significant effect.

Sensitivity analyses restricted to Malagasy locations confirmed these observed patterns, with only Ankazomborona differing significantly from Andina (Supplementary Table S3). Within Kiel, no significant differences in overall diversity were observed between the two studies cohorts, although fungal and helminth/protozoan diversity showed cohort-specific variation. Adjusted marginal means and pairwise contrasts are provided in Supplementary Table S4. These estimates were derived from models without cohort to allow estimation of location-level contrasts.

Regarding fungal α-diversity, multivariable models showed higher diversity in Tsiroanomandidy compared to Andina (β = 0.09, 95%CI: 0.01 to 0.18, p = 0.026), with no significant differences when comparing Andina to Ankazomborona and Kiel. In contrast, helminth/protozoan diversity was significantly lower in Kiel (β = −0.30, 95%CI: −0.40 to −0.20, p < 0.001), and in Tsiroanomandidy (β = −0.15, 95%CI: 0.24 to −0.05, p = 0.002) compared to Andina, while Ankazomborona showed a slight reduction relative to Andina (β = −0.13, 95%CI: −0.24 to −0.02, p = 0.02). These findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses restricted to Malagasy locations.

Location accounted for the largest proportion of variation in eukaryotic community composition (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.028, p = 0.001), followed by age (R2 = 0.001, p = 0.006) and sex (R2 = 0.001, p = 0.054). Tests for homogeneity of dispersion indicated statistically significant but small differences between locations (PERMDISP, p = 0.001; mean distance to centroid: 0.644–0.679), suggesting that differences in within-group dispersion may partly contribute to the observed PERMANOVA results. NMDS ordination showed an excellent fit (stress = 0.047), indicating subtle but detectable differences in community composition across the different locations.

Highest prevalence of food-associated fungal reads in Germany

When comparing the abundance of taxa across locations, food-associated fungi (fungal taxa associated with food production, fermentation, processing or storage as defined earlier 28 , 29 ) were most prevalent in samples from Kiel (92.23%) and least prevalent in Ankazomborona (65.12%). Saccharomyces and Candida showed prevalence patterns that were ordered similarly to the location classification by degree of urbanization as indicated in Table 1: Candida was highly prevalent across all sites but showed its highest prevalence in Kiel (74.26%). Saccharomyces was abundant in 43.16% of Kiel samples, but only in 8.28% in Andina. Consistent with these sequencing-based observations, species-specific qPCR confirmed significantly higher Saccharomyces cerevisiae DNA concentration in Kiel (median 0.0359 pg/sample) compared to Ankazomborona (median 0.0069 pg/sample, p = 7.66 × 10−5, Supplementary SF2). Other food-associated genera, including Galactomyces, Pichia, and Debaryomyces were also detected more frequently in samples from Kiel (Figure 1, Table 2).

Schistosoma was the most prevalent intestinal helminth, found in 13.04% of samples from Andina, in 13.02% from Ankazomborona, in 14.24% from Tsiroanomandidy, but was only detected in a minority of 16 samples from Kiel (4.29%).

Protozoa were highly prevalent across all sites, with the highest detection rate in Ankazomborona (86.05%) and the lowest in Kiel (70.24%). Blastocystis was the most frequently detected protozoan genus, followed by Entamoeba and Dientamoeba (Figure 1, Table 2). Blastocystis was the only genus consistently detected across all four locations, as revealed by core microbiota analysis (Supplementary Table S8) with prevalences ranging from 62.47% in Kiel to 83.26% in Ankazomborona. In contrast, other genera showed location-specific core patterns, with Candida forming part of the core microbiota in Kiel (74.26%) and Tsiroanomandidy (56.01%), whereas Entamoeba was restricted to Ankazomborona, where it was detected in 59.07% of the samples.

In contrast, Pichia was significantly more abundant in Kiel and Tsiroanomandidy compared to Andina, with additional differences between Tsiroanomandidy and Ankazomborona, while Aspergillus was more abundant in Tsiroanomandidy. Malassezia, Torulaspora, and Cryptococcus showed higher abundance in Andina and Tsiroanomandidy relative to Kiel.

Malagasy samples generally displayed significantly higher abundances of protozoan and helminth genera. However, we also observed strong geographic variation of these abundance patterns within Madagascar. Blastocystis, Entamoeba and Pentatrichomonas were most abundant in Ankazomborona, while Dientamoeba was most abundant in Andina. Helminths, like Necator were most abundant in Ankazomborona, and Bothriocephalus in Andina, whereas Schistosoma was generally elevated across all Malagasy samples relative to Kiel.

Bacterial composition differs between locations and covaries with the eukaryotic community

Following independent quality filtering and rarefaction, the final bacterial dataset comprised 81,799 ASVs from 1280 participants (mean age 40.72 y, 50.31% female, Supplementary Table S2).

Bacterial α-diversity (Shannon diversity index) varied across locations. The highest median community diversity was observed in Ankazomborona (H = 4.45; IQR: 4.23–4.67; range: 2.64–5.12), followed by Kiel (H = 3.98, IQR: 3.59–4.31; range: 0.54–5.55), Tsiroanomandidy (H = 3.95; IQR: 3.56–4.27; range: 0.96–4.90), and Andina (H = 3.91; IQR: 3.56–4.23; range: 1.99–5.21). Ankazomborona differed significantly from all other locations (p < 2 × 10−16), while no significant differences were observed among the remaining sites (Figure 3A).

β-diversity analyses revealed that location explained the largest proportion of variation within the bacterial community composition (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.125, p = 0.001; Figure 3B), consistent with patterns observed in the eukaryotic communities. Sex (R2 = 0.0029, p = 0.001) and age (R2 = 0.0022, p = 0.001) were also significantly associated with community composition but explained only a smaller proportion of the observed variation. When assessing associations between the abundance of eukaryotic taxa and prokaryotic community composition, Blastocystis showed the strongest association to bacterial community composition (R2 = 0.00396, p = 0.001). Other taxa with significant but smaller correlations included Dientamoeba (R2 = 0.00246, p = 0.001), Entamoeba (R2 = 0.00256, p = 0.001), Pichia (R2 = 0.00124, p = 0.004), Malassezia (R2 = 0.00121, p = 0.007), Hanseniaspora (R2 = 0.00099, p = 0.027), and Aspergillus (R2 = 0.00101, p = 0.036).

We identified 37 bacterial genera with significantly different abundances between locations (Figure 3C, Supplementary Table S7). Alistipes, Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Barnesiella, and Dialister were significantly enriched in Kiel compared to multiple Malagasy locations. Alloprevotella, Prevotella, Oscillibacter, Roseburia, Succinivibrio and Treponema were significantly more abundant in Ankazomborona. Clostridium_sensu_stricto, Blautia, Catenibacterium, Collinsella, Holdemanella, Intestinibacter, Lactobacillus, Olsenella, Romboutsia, Streptococcus, Terrisporobacter, and Turcibacter were predominantly enriched in samples from Andina relative to other locations, although patterns were not uniform across all pairwise comparisons. Klebsiella was significantly enriched in Tsiroanomandidy. In contrast to eukaryotic communities, bacteria exhibited a larger conserved core community across all four locations. In total, 27 bacterial genera were shared, including e.g. Bacteroides, Blautia, Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Ruminococcus, and Dialister, which showed particularly high prevalences, frequently exceeding 90% across locations. In addition to this shared core community, each location harbored location-specific bacterial core genera. (Supplementary Table S8).

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic associations are strongly regional

Combining the two datasets and analysing co-abundances revealed strong associations between protozoa and bacteria, spanning various phyla, with Firmicutes dominating the bacterial side of these potential interactions. We examined consistent correlation patterns across our dataset and identified differences in these potential cross-kingdom interactions across the locations, ordered according to the defined composite urbanization gradient (Table 1, Figure 4).

Across all locations, the protozoa Blastocystis and Entamoeba emerged as key players in microbial networks, but the structure and complexity of these associations varied across locations.

In Andina, networks appeared denser and involved Blastocystis and Entamoeba with numerous potential positive and negative co-abundances with bacterial genera including Ruminococcus, Oscillibacter, Blautia, and Clostridium. In Ankazomborona Blastocystis acted as a central node, showing more potential negative associations, particularly with members of Firmicutes. In Tsiroanomandidy, networks remained highly connected with Blastocystis, Entamoeba, and fungal taxa (Candida) contributing to cross-kingdom co-abundances. In contrast, networks in Kiel involved fewer taxa: Co-abundances were primarily centred around Blastocystis and several food-associated fungal genera such as Candida, Debaryomyces, Saccharomycetales, and Pichia. Notably, fungal-bacterial co-abundances were more prominent in Tsiroanomandidy and Kiel compared to Andina and Ankazomborona. Consistent co-abundance patterns across locations highlighted a core set of interactions, particularly involving Blastocystis and different Firmicutes members. Co-abundances with Entamoeba were mainly observed in Malagasy locations, suggesting a potential link to rural and peri-urban environmental or lifestyle factors.

Discussion

Our findings reveal that geographic context and urbanization-associated lifestyles are significantly associated with differences in the composition of intestinal eukaryotes in humans. By characterizing helminth, protozoan, and fungal communities across diverse environments, we observed distinct microbial patterns with potential implications for global health, emerging infectious diseases, and ongoing urbanization-associated lifestyle changes. Notably, we identified: (1) higher abundance of food-associated fungi in more densely urbanized environments; (2) distinct regional profiles within Madagascar that transcend urban-rural classifications, and (3) shifts in bacteria-eukaryote co-abundance linked to lifestyle changes.

Abundance of food-associated fungi is associated with more densely urbanized locations

A striking finding is the increasing prevalence and abundance of food-associated fungi 28 , 29 in samples from more urbanized and industrialized environments. This trend peaks in samples from Germany, representing the most densely urbanized and industrialized setting in our study, where intestinal fungal prevalence reached 93.03%. This pattern may have critical implications for globally emerging infectious diseases regarding e.g. rising antifungal resistances. 30 Notably, Candida emerged as a location-specific core genus in urban Kiel, present in 74.26% of samples, compared to 46.79% in rural Andina. Often a harmless commensal, but due to its rising pathogenicity and antifungal resistance in susceptible hosts, Candida is listed as a top priority on the WHO's Fungal Priority Pathogens List (FPPL). 8 However, mechanisms driving its commensal-to-pathogen transition remain poorly understood. 31 Its elevated prevalence coincides with urban dietary shifts towards simple carbohydrates, 32 potentially facilitating fungal overgrowth, 33 but may also be driven by dietary yeast products present in highly processed foods and cheeses. Candida abundance, as well as the consumption of highly processed foods, among other factors, have been linked to the emergence of various inflammatory diseases, including inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD). 34 , 35

Notably, we also observed a higher abundance of Saccharomyces and further unassigned Saccharomycetales in German samples compared to those from Madagascar, which was confirmed by qPCR demonstrating 5.2-fold higher concentrations of S. cerevisiae in samples from Kiel compared to Ankazomborona. This discrepancy likely reflects lifestyle-related differences in diet: Germany's high average consumption of grain-based baked goods 36 contrasts sharply with the rice-based diet in Madagascar. 37 This nutrient-rich fungus potentially influences intestinal immune responses, as seen in elevated levels of antibodies against S. cerevisiae (ASCA) in different inflammatory diseases. 28 Moreover, the prevalence of the widely distributed fungus Debaryomyces also increases with the urbanization-associated lifestyle gradient. Its high resistance to saline environments and its wide distribution among various food items 38 emphasizes the close connection between fungal patterns and dietary/urbanization patterns.

Taken together, as diet seems to be the primary driver of changes in the fungal microbiota, 39 our findings might reflect a dietary transition in more densely urban populations, characterized by greater food variety, enhanced caloric density, and extensive industrial processing, which fundamentally alters both intestinal fungal and bacterial communities. 17 , 39 However, these findings still require confirmation by future studies that include contextual metadata, particularly regarding dietary intake. Moreover, it remains unclear whether the identified fungi are transient or actively colonizing the GI tract. 39 However, fungal dysbiosis is increasingly reported as associated with “urban-associated diseases” like IBD. 34 Furthermore, the increased abundance of potential antigens which trigger overshooting immune reactions against foods are not necessarily driven by active community members. Thus, shifts in food-associated fungi and their high abundance in intestinal communities (active or not) may serve as early biomarkers of dietary changes and emerging disease risk in urbanizing populations.

Distinct regional eukaryotic profiles within Madagascar challenge simple urban-rural classifications

Our study reveals complex regional patterns within Madagascar, which cannot be explained by the degree of urbanization alone and underlines the complexity of associated lifestyle changes. This includes variations in intestinal fungi, helminths, and protozoa. Compared to Germany, where fungal profiles were strongly influenced by diet-associated taxa (according to classifications defined earlier- 28 , 29 ), fungal communities in Madagascar suggest alternative ecological or host-associated drivers. Malassezia, the second most prevalent gut fungus and a common skin colonizer, 40 showed regional differences with highest levels in rural Andina (30.03%) and urban Tsiroanomandidy (29.43%) but reduced presence in peri-urban Ankazomborona (8.84%). Malassezia has been associated with compromised gut and skin barrier function and neurodegeneration 41 and thus warrants further attention. Cryptococcus, an environmental 42 and FPPL-listed pathogen, 9 was significantly increased in rural Andina (14.28%). This pattern is concerning due to its role in fatal meningoencephalitis representing 19% of HIV-related deaths, particularly in resource-limited settings, 43 further underlining the potential importance of these elevated Cryptococcus abundance patterns.

Various other fungal pathogens show local abundance patterns with potential implications for the local public health infrastructure. The zoonotic yeast 44 Lodderomyces was more abundant in Tsiroanomandidy. Its fecal presence suggests intestinal colonization, which might serve as a reservoir for translocation across the gut barrier and subsequent bloodstream infections. 44

Regional variation in protozoan and helminth abundances

Helminth and protozoan prevalence showed additional significant regional variations across Madagascar. The overall high Schistosoma prevalence (13.02%–14.24%) aligns with the national schistosomiasis burden, 19 causing liver cirrhosis, bladder cancer, and neurological complications. 19 Other helminths displayed localized patterns. Necator, a parasitic hookworm associated with anemia and GI symptoms, was enriched in Ankazomborona, while Bothriocephalus was additionally detected in Andina, likely reflecting dietary habits and poor sanitation.

Protozoan distributions varied significantly between regions, mirroring the heterogeneous patterns seen in helminth abundances. Blastocystis, the most common protozoa across all regions (including Germany), was most abundant in peri-urban Ankazomborona. Recently, Blastocystis has increasingly been recognized as a commensal and linked to healthier diets and favorable cardiometabolic outcomes. 11 Entamoeba showed its highest prevalence also in Ankazomborona. While Entamoeba histolytica invades the colon wall, causes amoebiasis and affects 500 million people annually, 45 other non-pathogenic Entamoeba species contribute to the ecological balance through nutrient cycling in the gut. 46 Dientamoeba, an understudied trichomonad causing GI symptoms or remaining asymptomatic, was most frequent in Andina. 10 Ankazomborona's high helminths/protozoa abundances, as seen in the fecal-orally transmitted Endolimax and Entamoeba, could be related to poor sanitation infrastructure, as seen in coastal Madagascar. 47

While most intestinal protozoa exhibit regional clustering linked to local environmental and infrastructural conditions, Blastocystis uniquely emerged as the only eukaryotic core genus shared across all study regions. Our results highlight its broad ecological adaptability across diverse urbanization-associated lifestyles and support its proposed role as a commensal.

In our dataset, higher helminths abundance co-occurred with higher gut bacterial diversity, consistent with prior reports. 12 Notably, regions with high helminth/protozoa prevalence are dominated by Prevotella and Firmicutes. ß-diversity analysis revealed that the bacterial composition differed significantly with eukaryote abundances, supporting the hypothesis of active helminths/protozoa-bacteria interactions and emphasizing the importance of considering helminthic load when studying microbiome variations across populations.

Interestingly, highland communities (rural Andina and urban Tsiroanomandidy) appear more similar in their microbial profiles than expected based on their DEGURBA urbanization status, suggesting that environmental factors such as climatic and therefore also cultural and dietary conditions exert stronger influences on gut microbiome composition than urban-rural classification alone. Socio-anthropological structures in non-industrialized cities may differ fundamentally from those in industrialized settings, introducing previously unrecognized determinants of microbial and epidemiological variation. As data from low- and middle-income countries become more available, our insights emphasize the need to move microbiome research beyond simplistic urban-rural comparisons and highlight the necessity of re-evaluation WEIRD-based 48 urban-rural frameworks.

Bacteria-eukaryote interaction patterns vary across urbanization-associated lifestyles

The contrast between bacterial and eukaryotic core patterns underscores fundamental differences in community stability. While bacteria displayed a large and conserved core across all locations, eukaryotes were characterized by a minimal shared core, restricted to only Blastocystis, with additional taxa showing strong location specificity, including Candida in the two urban locations (Kiel and Tsiroanomandidy) and Entamoeba in Ankazomborona. These results suggest that gut eukaryotes might be more sensitive to geographic and lifestyle-related factors, whereas bacterial core members appear more resilient.

Co-abundance analyses revealed that urbanization-associated lifestyle changes are accompanied by shifts in multi-kingdom interactions. However, Blastocystis emerged as a key node across all locations, supporting its role as common intestinal eukaryote in humans. 11 , 49 In Madagascar, protozoa, particularly fecal-orally transmitted Entamoeba, emerged as central nodes in the prokaryote-eukaryote networks, showing extensive co-abundances with several Firmicutes genera, including SCFA-producing taxa. As these protozoa feed on bacteria, 50 their presence may be consistent with predation-mediated effects on bacterial diversity. 49

These potential positive associations were most prominent in Malagasy locations. In contrast, in Kiel, SCFA-producing bacteria remained connected to Blastocystis but were involved in additional fungal-associated co-abundances. This pattern might have significant health implications 51 as SCFA-producers are associated with anti-inflammatory effects, while their depletion has been linked to increased intestinal permeability and opportunistic fungal overgrowth. 31 Treponema, commonly associated with rural populations, 52 is almost absent in the German cohort and in Tsiroanomandidy, and might reflect a lifestyle-driven loss as has been described earlier. 20

Networks in settings with urban-associated lifestyles showed a shift towards fungal-bacterial interactions, with Candida, Galactomyces, Debaryomyces and Saccharomyces/Saccharomycetales becoming dominant components of the eukaryotic microbiome in Tsiroanomandidy. In general, the observed shift from protozoa- to more fungi-centred networks and communities along urbanization-associated lifestyle gradients might suggest a reorganization of gut ecosystem dynamics. However, the observed correlations do not imply causality but instead might reflect shared ecological niches or indirect interactions. The loss of protozoan diversity and the rise of potentially detrimental fungal associations might further contribute to the increased prevalence of inflammatory diseases in densely urban, industrialized populations and warrants direct investigation.

Limitations of the study

As stated before, due to the selection of study sites, urbanization, industrialization, and overall lifestyles are strongly correlated within our study design. Thus, it is not possible to disentangle their individual effects on eukaryotic and prokaryotic gut microbes. Germany reflects a generally highly urbanized and industrialized context, while the Malagasy sites represent less industrialized settings with varying degrees of urbanization. Consequently, we interpret our findings along a composite urbanization-associated lifestyle gradient (defined according to the DEGURBA classification based on geographic density) rather than attributing effects to urbanization or industrialization alone. Future studies incorporating multiple urban and rural locations across various countries with different levels of industrialization will be required to disentangle these factors more explicitly.

All sample donors were population-based controls without acute illness at time of sampling. However, individual-level metadata (e.g. dietary intake, recent antimicrobial, antiparasitic, or antifungal treatments) were not systematically collected. Additionally, potential seasonal effects and temporal differences between cohorts, including Malagasy sample collection in 2020 and earlier recruitment of one German cohort, might have influenced the observed differences. Although DNA extraction and sequencing were performed using identical protocols and all participants followed comparable home-based stool collection procedures, differences in transport duration and storage conditions between the cohorts and additional broader contextual factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic, cannot be excluded. Consequently, our interpretations are based on population-level, urbanization-associated patterns rather than on individual-level causal inferences. In addition, extraction blanks were not available for the Malagasy samples, which is a relevant limitation when analysing microbiota samples. However, the inclusion of sequencing controls, prevalence-based decontamination, and orthogonal qPCR validation suggests that the main fungal patterns reported here are unlikely to be explained by reagent contamination.

A further limitation is the inclusion of only one highly industrialized and urbanized site (Kiel, Germany), which limits the generalisability of conclusions regarding eukaryotic microbiota in these populations and warrants future studies including more global sites. Moreover, a substantial proportion of 18S rRNA gene sequences remained unclassified, reflecting current limitations of eukaryotic reference databases. While α- and β-diversity analyses based on ASV abundances are expected to be robust, taxon-level interpretations, particularly for eukaryotes, should be made with caution.

Complexity of gut microbial shifts and implications for infectious disease surveillance in global health

Overall, our study underscores two critical needs for advancing microbiome research and global health surveillance: First, integrating eukaryotic taxa into microbiome studies to understand cross-kingdom interactions driving health and disease transitions. As global urbanization intensifies and associated lifestyle changes reshape gut ecosystems, 17 understanding these multi-dimensional interactions becomes critical for predicting and preventing emerging infectious or inflammatory diseases in our rapidly changing world.

Second, our work emphasizes that infectious disease surveillance must account for geographic patterns beyond “simple” urban-rural classifications. The regional clustering of specific eukaryotic (and bacterial) taxa—from Cryptococcus in rural Andina to food-associated fungi in Kiel—reveals complex epidemiological landscapes and interaction patterns between the host, environment, and microbiome. Many identified fungi across all locations appear on WHO's FPPL, 8 yet their ecological patterns remain poorly understood 3 , 6 and may lack consideration of regional disparities. 9
