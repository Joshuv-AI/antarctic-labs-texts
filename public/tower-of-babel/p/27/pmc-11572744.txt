The prevalence of neuropsychiatric symptoms and correlation with MRI findings in CADASIL patients
Authors: Li Bai, HaoTian Yan, Yu Guo, Yong Shan, Qing Peng, Haiqiang Jin, Yunchuang Sun, Fan Li, Wei Sun, Wei Zhang, Zihao Zhang, Zhaoxia Wang, Yun Yuan, Chen Ling
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the prevalence, timing, and functional impact of neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) and to assess whether these neuropsychiatric symptoms are associated with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of the patients.MethodsOur study included a total of 78 patients with CADASIL. To assess neuropsychiatric symptoms, we evaluated the caregivers using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI). Patients were considered to have an irritability, depression, apathy, aggression, or anxiety disorder if they scored ≥1 in the NPI. Subsequently, we conducted a more detailed assessment of irritability, depression, apathy, aggression, and anxiety. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to analyze the relationships between neuropsychiatric symptoms and clinical/MRI features in the patients.ResultsOverall, 57.69% of patients with CADASIL experienced neuropsychiatric symptoms. Among these symptoms, irritability was the most prevalent (52.56%), followed by depression (19.23%), apathy (17.95%), aggression (7.69%), and anxiety (6.41%). The mean age of onset for irritability was the youngest, followed by anxiety, apathy, aggression, and depression. Among patients with both stroke/TIA and neuropsychiatric symptoms, 31.03% reported experiencing neuropsychiatric symptoms prior to stroke/TIA. Furthermore, both irritability and apathy had a negative impact on the patients' daily functioning. Additionally, there was a correlation between the presence of neuropsychiatric symptoms and the patients' MRI lesion burden.InterpretationOur study has discovered that neuropsychiatric symptoms are highly prevalent in patients with CADASIL and may occur before cerebrovascular events, suggesting that neuropsychiatric symptoms of CADASIL deserve more attention and earlier exploration.

Introduction

Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is the most common hereditary cause of stroke. 1 It results from mutations in the NOTCH3 gene located on chromosome 19q12. 2 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of CADASIL include white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), lacune of presumed vascular origin (lacunes), and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs). 3 Clinically, CADASIL is characterized by migraine, recurrent stroke, cognitive impairment, and neuropsychiatric disorders. 4 Among these symptoms, neuropsychiatric symptoms notably impact patients' quality of life. Despite this, there has been insufficient comprehensive research on neuropsychiatric symptoms in CADASIL. 5 A detailed understanding of the timing and impact of various neuropsychiatric symptoms in CADASIL will aid in the development of more targeted therapies.

Current researches on neuropsychiatric abnormalities in patients with CADASIL have primarily focused on depression and apathy. Several studies indicate that depression is prevalent among CADASIL patients, affecting approximately 24%–28.6% of patients. Depression significantly impacts the quality of life for these patients and increases the burden on caregivers. 6 , 7 , 8 Additionally, research has confirmed that apathy is a common neuropsychiatric abnormality in CADASIL patients and has a distinct impact on their overall quality of life. 9 However, other aspects of neuropsychiatric abnormalities, such as irritability and aggressive behavior, remain underexplored in CADASIL research.

In this study, we analyzed neuropsychiatric symptoms, including irritability, depression, apathy, aggression, and anxiety, in 78 patients with CADASIL. We also examined the relationship between these neuropsychiatric symptoms and the clinical/MRI characteristics of the patients.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee at Peking University First Hospital and was performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects in this study. From June 2016 to June 2023, all patients with CADASIL who visited our department and met the criteria described below as well as who agreed to participate in this study were enrolled. The inclusion criteria for patients with CADASIL were as follows: (1) confirmed genetic or pathological diagnosis of CADASIL; (2) no history of acute ischemic/hemorrhagic cerebrovascular events in recent 3 months; and (3) >18 years old. A total of 84 patients with CADASIL were recruited in our study.

Clinical and neuropsychiatric measures

The following demographic and clinical data were collected for each patient on the same scales as our previous study 10 including age, gender, history of stroke (both ischemic and hemorrhagic), or transient ischemic attack (TIA). The degrees of dependence of all patients were determined by the modified Rankin scale (mRS). mRS score ≥ 2 was defined as abnormal mRS. 10 Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) was performed for global cognitive efficiency. Patients with abnormal MMSE were defined as those whose MMSE scores were lower than the lower quartile of the age‐ and educational level‐matched healthy controls. 11 Based on previous study, cognitive dysfunction may affect the outcome of neuropsychiatric measures, so we excluded patients with MMSE ≤ 20 (six patients) based on previous studies. 12 Seventy‐eight patients with CADASIL were included in the final study (40 males and 38 females).

We next performed a detailed neuropsychiatric evaluation of these 78 patients. We initially evaluated the caregivers familiar with the patient's behavior using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) at the first visit. 13 The NPI comprises 12 screening questions that evaluate various neuropsychiatric symptoms. These symptoms include delusions, hallucinations, agitation, dysphoria, anxiety, apathy, irritability, euphoria, disinhibition, aberrant motor behavior, nighttime behavior disturbances, and appetite and eating abnormalities. If a patient scores ≥ 1 in any of these sections on the NPI, 9 it suggests the presence of potential neuropsychiatric symptoms. After conducting the initial screening, we identified five primary aspects of neuropsychiatric symptoms in our patients: irritability, depression, apathy, aggression, and anxiety.

Subsequently, we conducted a more detailed assessment of irritability, depression, apathy, aggression, and anxiety using specific scales for each symptom. Irritability was assessed using a scale designed by Burns et al., 14 and the caregivers were questioned. Patients were considered irritable if they were rated by the caregiver as anything other than “not at all irritable” (2 points and above). Apathy was evaluated by the Apathy Scale, 15 and a cutoff score of 14 points was used to determine whether a patient had apathy, higher scores indicate more severe apathy. 16 The Yudofsky Aggression Scale 17 was used to assess aggressive behavior. Patient was considered aggressive if the caregiver's response to the question “Does he/she have outbursts of aggression or temper?” was “yes.” Self‐Rating Depression Scale (SDS) 18 and Self‐Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) 19 were used to evaluate depression and anxiety. The raw score (sum of the scores from all 20 items) was multiplied by 1.25, 20 and the standard score is the integer part. Based on the standard score, depression/anxiety was assessed using a cutoff score of 50, and patients with scores of 50 and above are considered depressed/anxious.

The age of onset of different neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with CADASIL was estimated based on responses from the patients and their caregivers. The age of onset of the patient's first stroke (both ischemic and hemorrhagic) or TIA was obtained from the responses of the patient and his or her caregiver, as well as from the patient's medical records.

Quantification of MRI lesion burden

Among all the patients, 63 patients (31 males and 32 females) underwent a 7T brain MRI examination. Parameters of brain imaging protocol included the following: 3D T1‐magnetization prepared gradient echo images (TR = 2200 ms, TE = 3.29 ms, voxel = 0.70 × 0.70 × 0.70 mm), fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery images (TR = 14000 ms, TE = 94 ms, voxel = 0.43 × 0.57 × 3.00 mm), and susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI)‐gradient echo image (TR = 18 ms, TE = 12 ms, voxel = 0.30 × 0.30 × 1.20 mm). The MRI lesion burden of patients with CADASIL were defined according to the STandards for ReportIng Vascular changes on nEuroimaging, 21 including white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), lacune of presumed vascular origin (lacunes), and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs). At bilateral frontal/parietal/occipital/temporal white matter, basal ganglia and thalamus, the number of lacunes was counted manually. WMHs were scored using age‐related white matter changes (ARWMC). 22 The number of CMBs was calculated using the Microbleed Anatomical Rating Scale (MARS). 23 The total small vessel disease (SVD) scores were calculated as previously described. 24

MRI images processing and analysis

Subsequently, we examined the quality of MRI images obtained from patients. Ten patients were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete images, three patients were excluded due to poor image quality caused by head motion. Finally, 50 patients underwent image processing. The Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12; C. Gaser, Structural Brain Mapping Group, Jena University Hospital, Jena, Germany, http://www.neuro.uni‐jena.de/cat/) is implemented in SPM12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping, Institute of Neurology, London, UK) and provides a fully automated method to estimate cortex thickness by a projection‐based thickness (PBT) method. 25 All T1‐weighted high‐resolution images are corrected for bias‐field inhomogeneities, automatically segmented into gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid, 26 and spatially normalized using the Diffeomorphic Image Registration Algorithm (DARTEL), 25 implemented in the SPM12. In the PBT approach used by CAT12, distance information from the white matter boundary is used to estimate the thickness of the gray matter. For each gray matter voxel, the distance to the nearest voxel at the white matter boundary is calculated, and its successors are identified. The successor of a voxel is defined as the neighboring voxel in the gray matter that is about one voxel from the white matter boundary. Accordingly, voxels with no successors are considered as local maxima and are assumed to be located at the cerebrospinal fluid boundary. Thus, the distance of voxels with no successors from the nearest voxel at the white matter boundary is considered the thickness of the cortex in that voxel. 27 All scans passed a visual check for artifacts and the automated CAT12 quality check protocol. In this morphometric brain study, we used the Desikan–Killiany atlas 28 as the region of interest (ROI) segmentation.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL). Normally distributed data were compared using unpaired t‐test (t). Non‐normally distributed data and small sample data were compared using Mann–Whitney U‐test (z). The chi‐squared (χ 2) test was used to compare the ratios. The association of abnormal mRS and presence of neuropsychiatric symptoms was analyzed using multivariate binary logistic regression (forward LR), covariates included age, gender, and MMSE. The association of presence of neuropsychiatric symptoms and MRI features was first analyzed using unpaired t‐test (t)/Mann–Whitney U‐test (z), and the Bonferroni correction was used to adjust multiple comparisons (MRI lesion burden: statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05/6, six ROIs; cortical thickness: p < 0.05/10, ten ROIs), If the p value of univariate analysis was statistically significant, multivariate binary logistic regression (forward LR) was further performed to adjust age, gender, and MMSE. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Neuropsychiatric symptoms were not rare in patients with CADASIL

Clinical and MRI features of patients with CADASIL are displayed in Tables 1 and SI. Overall, 57.69% (45/78) of patients with CADASIL experienced neuropsychiatric symptoms. In our group of patients, the most prevalent symptom was irritability, affecting 52.56% (41/78) of the patients, followed by depression (19.23%, 15/78) and apathy (17.95%, 14/78). Additionally, 7.69% (6/78) of the patients exhibited aggression, while 6.41% (5/78) had anxiety (Table 1). There was no significant difference in the proportion of male and female patients presenting with these neuropsychiatric symptoms (Table 2).

Timing of stroke/TIA and neuropsychiatric symptoms in patient with CADASIL

In our cohort, the main reason for most patients seeking medical treatment for the first time was stroke/TIA (80%, 40/50) and neuropsychiatric symptoms were often overlooked. Therefore, we examined the relationship between the time of onset of five neuropsychiatric symptoms and the age of first stroke/TIA in patients with CADASIL. A total of 40 patients had a history of stroke/TIA, of whom 11 patients had no neuropsychiatric symptoms, 26 patients had irritability, 10 patients had depression, 9 patients had apathy, 4 patients had aggression, and 4 patients had anxiety. Among patients with both stroke/TIA and neuropsychiatric symptoms, 31.03% (9/29) of patients reported neuropsychiatric symptoms prior to stroke/TIA, whereas 68.97% (20/29) of patients exhibited neuropsychiatric symptoms after stroke/TIA. Irritability was present before stroke/TIA in 34% (9/26) of patients. Depression/apathy/aggression/anxiety, on the other hand, tended to develop after stroke/TIA, with onset after stroke/TIA in 80% (8/10), 77.78% (7/9), 100% (4/4), and 75% (3/4), respectively. Neuropsychiatric symptoms were also seen in patients without a history of stroke/TIA, the most common being irritability (39.47%, 15/38), followed by depression (13.16%, 5/38) and apathy (13.16%, 5/38), and the least seen being aggression and anxiety in 5.26% (2/38) and 2.26% (1/38), respectively.

Neuropsychiatric symptoms are associated with increased functional dependence in patients with CADASIL

In order to investigate the impact of neuropsychiatric symptoms on daily life functioning, we grouped the patients according to their mRS scores (mRS score ≥ 2 and mRS score < 2) and found that the proportion of patients presenting with irritability, depression, and apathy was associated with abnormal mRS scores (mRS score ≥ 2) (Fig. 2A). After further adjustment for age, gender, and MMSE, the presence of irritability and apathy still associated with abnormal mRS scores in patients (irritability: odds ratio [OR] = 6.052, 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 1.603–22.854, p = 0.008; apathy: OR = 5.803, 95% CI: 1.492–22.568, p = 0.011) (Table 3).

Neuropsychiatric symptoms are associated with MRI features in patients with CADASIL

We first grouped the patients according to their SVD scores and found that the proportion of each neuropsychiatric symptom, except irritability, increased with increasing SVD scores (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, patients with depression and apathy had higher SVD scores compared to those without depression (z = −4.478, p < 0.001) and apathy (z = −2.447, p = 0.014). After adjusting for age, gender, and MMSE, increased SVD score was still associated with increased risk of developing depression (OR = 4.306, 95% CI: 1.911–9.702, p < 0.001) and apathy (OR = 1.864, 95% CI: 1.102–3.152, p = 0.020). However, there were no significant differences in SVD scores among patients with or without irritability, aggression, and anxiety (Table SII–SVI).

We also analyzed differences in MRI lesion burden in different brain regions in patients with/without neuropsychiatric symptoms, and the results are shown in Table SII–SVI. We further performed multivariate binary logistic regressions for statistically significant differences in results from univariate analyses to adjust for age, gender, and MMSE. Increased number of lacunes in the frontal region and thalamus associated with increased risk of developing irritability in patients (frontal region: OR = 1.162, 95% CI: 1.027–1.314, p = 0.017; thalamus: OR = 2.352, 95% CI: 1.118–4.944, p = 0.024). Higher ARWMC scores in the frontal region/parietal region/thalamus, increased number of lacunes in the frontal region/basal ganglia, and increased number of CMBs in the frontal/parietal region associated with increased risk of developing depression in patients (ARWMC scores: frontal region: OR = 1.984, 95% CI: 1.306–3.013, p = 0.001; parietal region: OR = 1.743, 95% CI: 1.197–2.537, p = 0.004; thalamus: OR = 1.648, 95% CI: 1.032–2.633, p = 0.037) (number of lacunes: frontal region: OR = 1.254, 95% CI: 1.091–1.440, p = 0.001; basal ganglia: OR = 1.396, 95% CI: 1.007–1.936, p = 0.045) (number of CMBs: frontal region: OR = 3.657, 95% CI: 1.395–9.589, p = 0.008; parietal region: OR = 5.829, 95% CI: 1.200–28.309, p = 0.029). In addition, higher ARWMC scores and increased number of lacunes in the frontal region associated with increased risk of developing apathy in patients (ARWMC scores in frontal region: OR = 1.795, 95% CI: 1.175–2.742, p = 0.007; number of lacunes in frontal region: OR = 1.183, 95% CI: 1.049–1.335, p = 0.006). And increased number of lacunes in the thalamus associated with increased risk of developing anxiety in patients (OR = 2.533, 95% CI: 1.285–4.996, p = 0.007).

Finally, we studied the differences in cortical thickness in various brain regions between patients with and without neuropsychiatric symptoms and found no significant differences (Table SVII–SXI).

Discussion

Our study found that neuropsychiatric symptoms were prevalent in patients with CADASIL. Among these neuropsychiatric symptoms, irritability was the most common symptom, followed by depression, apathy, aggression, and anxiety. We also observed that irritability had the youngest mean age of onset, followed by anxiety, apathy, aggression, and depression. Although most patients initially sought medical attention for stroke/TIA, we discovered that neuropsychiatric symptoms can appear before the onset of stroke/TIA in some patients. Furthermore, neuropsychiatric symptoms were present even in patients who had not yet developed stroke/TIA. Additionally, irritability and apathy significantly impair daily functioning. These findings indicate that neuropsychiatric symptoms in CADASIL are often overlooked, emphasizing the need for heightened awareness and early intervention for these symptoms in CADASIL patients.

In this study, we conducted a more comprehensive examination of neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with CADASIL. We found that irritability was the most common neuropsychiatric symptom in our patients, a finding not previously reported in the studies. Our analysis also revealed that depression and apathy were common neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with CADASIL, which aligns with the findings of previous studies. 9 , 29 In addition, we reported the rates of aggression and anxiety in CADASIL, albeit at low rates, the two neuropsychiatric symptoms also seen in patients with CADASIL.

Previous studies have shown a high prevalence of apathy in 41% of CADASIL patients 9 and depression in 28.6% of CADASIL patients. 29 In our cohort, the prevalence of apathy was lower than previous studies, which may be attributed to the inclusion of a different group of patients. Irritability, aggression, and anxiety have been less extensively studied in CADASIL. Nevertheless, several of these neuropsychiatric symptoms have been documented in stroke and vascular dementia. For instance, studies have shown that approximately 33% of post‐stroke patients experience irritability. 30 Aggression has been reported in 40% of the patients with vascular dementia 31 and 18.5% of patients with stroke. 32 Additionally, the prevalence of post‐stroke anxiety has been reported to be 16%. 33 These studies indicate that irritability, aggression and anxiety are also common and frequently overlooked neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with cerebrovascular disease. It is evident that these neuropsychiatric symptoms require increased attention in patients with hereditary cerebrovascular diseases such as CADASIL.

In our study, neuropsychiatric symptoms predominantly occurred mostly after stroke/TIA events, suggesting that these symptoms may be related to cerebrovascular events. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have examined the association between neuropsychiatric symptoms and cerebrovascular events in CADASIL. 34 Additionally, our data show that neuropsychiatric symptoms, particularly apathy and depression, correlate with SVD scores. This correlation supports the notion that neuropsychiatric symptoms are linked to cerebrovascular damage. However, we also found that some patients can present with neuropsychiatric symptoms, especially irritability, before the onset of stroke/TIA. The early onset of these neuropsychiatric symptoms may be associated with subtle WMHs and lacunes in patients with CADASIL. Furthermore, we observed that neuropsychiatric symptoms adversely affect their function of daily life and increase the burden on caregivers, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and management of neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with CADASIL.

In our study, we found that depression in patients with CADASIL was associated with increased MRI lesion burden, including WMHs, lacunes, and CMBs. This burden was particularly pronounced in the frontal lobe, parietal lobe, basal ganglia, and thalamus. Several studies have indicated that the frontal and parietal lobes are involved in the fronto‐subcortical pathways regulating depression, 35 , 36 while the basal ganglia and thalamus play a crucial role in the limbic‐cortical‐striatal‐thalamic circuits. 37 Thus, MRI lesion burden affecting these structures and regulatory pathways may contribute to depression in CADASIL patients. Additionally, we found that WMHs and lacunes in the frontal lobes were associated with the presence of apathy in these patients. Previous research also suggests that apathy development is linked to neuroimaging changes in the frontal lobe, possibly due to disruption of the frontal cortex‐basal ganglia circuits. 38

Our study also found that CADASIL patients with irritability had a higher number of lacunes in the frontal lobes and thalamus, and CADASIL patients with anxiety had more lacunes in the thalamus. Previous studies have suggested that irritability may arise from failures in the frontal executive control mechanisms, 39 suggesting that the frontal lobes and thalamus play a central role in the development of irritability.

Lacunes in the frontal lobes and thalamus of patients with CADASIL may contribute to irritability by disrupting the fronto‐subcortical pathways. Concurrently, previous studies have linked the limbic cortical‐striato‐thalamocortical circuits to the development of anxiety, with the thalamus serving as a key relay station. 40 Thus, thalamic infarctions in CADASIL patients may exacerbate anxiety by affecting these anxiety circuits. Although evidence suggests that lesion burden in specific brain regions are associated with certain neuropsychiatric symptoms, further research is needed to confirm these associations. In contrast, we did not find a correlation between cortical thickness and neuropsychiatric symptoms, which is consistent with previous findings. 41 These results suggest that the occurrence of some neuropsychiatric symptoms may be more closely related to deep brain lesions such as lacunes and white matter lesions, rather than cortical thickness.

The study has several limitations. Firstly, due to the rarity of CADASIL, our study size was small, and the conclusions drawn require validation through additional research. Secondly, as a cross‐sectional study, it is subject to limitations such as recall bias and difficulties in establishing causality. Thirdly, irritability and anxiety can occur in healthy individuals, and since our study did not include a healthy control group, some instances of irritability and anxiety in our patients may not be attributable to CADASIL itself. Thus, the prevalence of these symptoms in CADASIL may have been overestimated. Finally, because apathy, depression, and aggressive behavior are rare in the healthy population and are more likely to appear in the later stages of CADASIL, and as our cohort included relatively few late‐stage patients, we may have underestimated the prevalence of apathy, depression, and aggressive behavior.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study found that neuropsychiatric symptoms are prevalent in patients with CADASIL and may precede cerebrovascular events. Neuropsychiatric symptoms can adversely affect the function of daily life of patients with CADASIL. There was also a correlation between neuropsychiatric symptoms and the patients' MRI lesion burden. Our study suggests that neuropsychiatric symptoms of CADASIL deserve more attention and earlier exploration.
