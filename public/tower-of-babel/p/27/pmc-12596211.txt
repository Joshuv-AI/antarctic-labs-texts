Simultaneous carotid ultrasound and transcranial Doppler to detect cholesterol crystal emboli from unstable carotid plaque to the brain☆
Authors: Fadi Shamoun, Dorothy R Pathak, Nicholas Campbell, Akanksha Mehla, Mounzer Yassin-Kassab, Jordan Knepper, George S Abela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetection of unstable carotid plaques at risk for cholesterol crystal (CC) emboli to the brain causing strokes can be challenging. Although plaques with lumen stenosis of >70 % have been shown to pose a risk for embolism, detection prior to this stage of plaque progression (<70 % stenosis) may identify early instability. One such approach would be to obtain simultaneous carotid ultrasound (CUS) and transcranial Doppler (TCD) that can detect CCs discharged from plaque as a signal of instability. Application of contact pressure as usually applied by the interrogating ultrasound probe over a carotid artery during CUS that can trigger high intensity transient signals (HITS) on TCD could provide insight into plaque instability. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated 23 patients with moderate stenosis (∼50 %) by CUS/TCD to check for instability. Of the patients insonated, 4/21 had evidence of HITS on TCD during CUS and 3 had neurological symptoms. The presence of HITS was observed only for emboli from heterogenous/complex plaques (4/14) 28.6 % (95 % CI: 0.084–0.581), p ≤ 0.05. Moreover, to confirm the role of CCs on HITS, we injected 1 or 5 CCs into the femoral circulation of a rabbit model and demonstrated that even a single crystal could be detected by a HIT on TCD. Detection of CCs by HITS with TCD was highly sensitive for number of injected crystals (1/1, 5/5) and specific (0/0 for whole blood) indicating 100 % accuracy. We propose that this simple and readily feasible approach could be useful to detect unstable carotid plaques at an early stage.

Introduction

Detection of unstable carotid plaques that are at an increased risk for rupture releasing emboli to the brain and causing either strokes or transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) can be clinically challenging especially in association with intermediate lesions that are ∼50 % stenosis. Most studies have focused on the degree of carotid arterial stenosis as the measure to assess the risk for stroke associated with an embolic event [1]. The North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial Collaborators (NASCET) criteria of internal carotid artery lumen stenosis of ≥70 % has usually been used as a standard in decision making for either carotid endarterectomy or stenting to prevent a stroke especially if associated with neurologic symptoms [[2], [3], [4]]. However, carotid arterial lumen stenosis of 50 % may also be considered for intervention or medical therapy if associated with symptoms [5]. Carotid ultrasound (CUS) and more specifically, computer tomography angiography (CTA) have been the main methods for obtaining measurements as guide for intervention in patients with symptoms and ipsilateral stenosis but these measurements do not necessarily provide an assessment of plaque instability. Although carotid stenosis greater 70 % with a large lipid core is the current guide for intervention, however a better understanding of instability for lesser stenotic lesions could be useful in guiding therapy for those cases. [6].

Transcranial Doppler (TCD) has been used to interrogate the middle cerebral artery (MCA) to detect micro-emboli from the carotid arteries recorded as pings or high intensity transient signals (HITS) to identify a patient at increased risk for stroke [7,8]. However, guidelines do not specify use of TCD as a marker for unstable carotid plaques [9]. In this study we propose to combine simultaneous use of CUS and TCD to determine the state of plaque instability based on detection of cholesterol crystal emboli. The hypothesis is that when applying the standard pressure with the probe on the carotid artery during CUS while monitoring the MCA with TCD can detect release of particulates from the plaque that could reflect their instability. This hypothesis is rooted in the standard teaching that manual pressure on carotid arteries with atherosclerotic plaque is associated with an increased risk for embolization and stroke [10,11]. In this report we evaluated the simultaneous use of CUS and TCD in patients with moderate stenosis and verified the technique by detection of cholesterol crystal emboli in an in vivo model.

Methods

The CUS/TCD technique in human patients

Patient selection included those who were > 18 years old with internal carotid artery (ICA) lumen stenosis >20 % and < 70 % by ultrasound as candidates for the study. These included patients with or without neurologic symptoms consistent with transient ischemic attack (TIA). However, patients with neurologic symptoms within 30 days prior to the study were excluded. The TCD method is performed by placing the 2 MHz probe (VIASYS NeuroCare, Sonara, Madison, WI) over the temporal bone window aimed anteriorly at the bifurcation of the middle cerebral and anterior cerebral arteries at an approximate depth of 65 mm allowing insonation of both arteries. Simultaneously, a standard carotid ultrasound is performed by placing a vascular 9.0 L MHz probe (GE, Vivid 7, Milwaukee, WI) over the ipsilateral carotid artery while the patient's head is tilted in the opposite direction (Fig. 1). The CUS study is performed in the standard fashion by insonating the common, external, internal carotid arteries and bulb by a certified technologist. Peak flow velocities are measured with a Doppler ultrasound beam at an incidence angle below 60° with the axis of the artery and the range of percent stenosis recorded. Co-localization between CUS and TCD is recorded on the software of the TCD machine. Significant embolic signals were then recorded while insonating specific carotid sites. The detection of any micro-emboli by TCD are dependent on the TCD emboli detection software (Sonara Application Software, Madison, WI) with sensitivity and specificity of 90 % respectively per manufacturer's specifications. All micro-embolic HITS detected by the software were verified by an experienced vascular board certified reader. Verification of detecting HITS was confirmed by injecting cholesterol crystals (CCs) in a rabbit model described below. Prior to simultaneous measurements, TCD was performed for 15 min before applying the CUS probe to the neck in order to confirm that there was no spontaneous embolization. The morphology of the plaque was described as smooth or heterogeneous and complex with surface irregularity, with or without calcification and/or hemorrhage. This project was approved by Michigan State University Institutional Review Board: #09–050.

Detection of cholesterol crystals by doppler in a rabbit model

To demonstrate the feasibility of detecting CCs by using an 8 MHz probe and the TCD machine, we monitored for pings as HITS immediately after injection of CCs into the femoral artery of a normal NZW rabbit. This was performed under general anesthesia using Ketamine (50 mg/kg IM) and xylazine (20 mg/kg IM) followed by a right femoral cut-down to introduce a 4F end hole catheter advanced retrogradely into the abdominal aorta above the aortoiliac bifurcation. The probe was placed over the left femoral artery to detect the crystals acoustically and by Doppler recordings of HITS on the TCD machine. Whole blood (10 ml) from the rabbit was withdrawn and heparinized in a tube and 3 ml reinjected via the catheter while insonating the left femoral artery. This served as a negative control for Doppler signals. For a positive control, 3 ml of agitated saline was injected. This was then followed by injection of CCs (n = 1 or n = 5) mixed with 3 ml of heparinized blood and monitored for HITS. After completion of the study the rabbit was euthanized using euthanasia solution (buthanasol 2 ml IV). Procedures were performed according to Michigan State University's Animal Care and Use Committee approved protocol following NIH guidelines. IACUC approval #08/08–126.

Results

Human Studies (Table 1): Of 23 patients (15 males) recruited, 21 were insonated. Two patients could not be insonated due to hyperostosis of the temporal bone. By CUS, the mean stenosis range in the right ICA was 32–51 % and 27–46 % in the left ICA. HITS were detected by TCD in 4/21 patients who had heterogenous/complex plaque with 3 having neurologic symptoms (Fig. 2). An example of the presence and appearance of CCs in atherosclerotic plaques are shown from patients who had carotid endarterectomy (Fig. 3). The presence of HITS was observed only for CC emboli from heterogenous/complex plaques (4/14) 28.6 % (95 % CI: 0.084–0.581), p ≤ 0.05. These data also demonstrate that CCs can be detected based on the presence of neurologic symptoms. We observed HITS in (3/9) 33 % (95 % CI: 0.022–0.358), p ≤ 0.05 of such cases. If both symptoms and heterogeneous/complex plaque were present, HITS were detected in 100 % (3/3) of these patients (Table 1). All HITS (100 %) were associated with the presence of heterogenous/complex plaques from patients with <70 % stenosis. Of the nine patients who had symptoms, six did not have HITS during CUS/TCD. Furthermore, no patient experienced any neurologic event as a consequence of the CUS/TCD procedure.

Discussion

Embolization of CCs from carotid plaques was initially demonstrated by Hollenhorst in the early 1960's when he injected CCs in the carotid arteries of primates and demonstrated their presence in the retinal arteries by ophalmoscopy [13]. Similar findings have been made with CC emboli in the brain of a rat model that subsequently developed a ministrokes and a reduction in cognitive function [14]. Also, comparable observations have been made in human patients with TIAs and strokes [15]. In a recent study of patients presenting with strokes simultaneous TCD and CUS was able to detect an association with unstable plaques as demonstrated by CT angiography [16]. However, our study suggests that in a prospective evaluation of patients with moderate stenosis who had positive HITS during simultaneous CUS/TCD, they were more frequently present in patients who had previous neurologic symptoms. Keeping in mind that not all embolic events to the brain are associated with neurologic symptoms but may subsequently have positive signals for stroke on brain MRI. Further studies using brain MRI would be needed to confirm a significant association between a positive TCD during CUS as a risk for neurological events. Similar to our observations, a recent study using angioscopy demonstrated that CCs can be seen oozing from atherosclerotic plaques in the aorta of live patients when an angioscope was mildly engaging an atherosclerotic plaque [17]. Moreover, they also demonstrated that CCs continue to emerge from ruptured plaques for several weeks following the initial event [18].

Many patients present emergently with symptoms suggestive of TIA that are not well defined as an embolic event and often require a subsequent brain MRI to confirm the diagnosis. Hence a technique to identify such patients rapidly so they can be started on long term medical therapy may be helpful. Specifically, aspirin and statins which are usually used clinically to treat patients with TIAs are known to dissolve cholesterol crystals, hence their effects are likely beyond anti platelet action of aspirin and anti-inflammatory role for statins [19,20]. The direct effect of dissolving CCs is especially relevant to statins since lowering of LDL takes several weeks yet the effect on dissolving CCs is immediate. Use of statins in acute myocardial infarction and before percutaneous procedures have shown similar short as well as long term benefits [21,22].

Detection of CCs by TCD is highly sensitive and specific based on our study in the rabbit model. In humans with carotid plaques ∼50 % stenosis and having both symptoms and heterogenous plaque, analysis of HITS detected with CUS/TCD showed high sensitivity, 100 % (3/3), of such patients. For patients with heterogenous plaque detection of CC's was 29 % (4/14) and for those with neurological symptoms was 33 % (3/9). CUS/TCD was highly specific, and did not detect any HIT in the absence of either heterogenous/complex plaque or neurologic symptoms alone.

Previous studies have demonstrated that plaques can become unstable when cholesterol undergoes crystallization forming sharp tipped crystals that expand the core, puncture the fibrous cap causing rupture (Fig. 1, Fig. 3) [23,24]. Specifically, the greater CCs content in carotid plaques was found to be significantly associated with greater plaque size and association with neurological symptoms (Fig. 5) [25,26]. Moreover, presence of CCs in plaques was associated with hemorrhage from traumatized vasa vasorum which is also known to be a risk for plaque growth and rupture [24,[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Furthermore, CCs circulating in the arteries can injure the vascular intima promoting vasoconstriction and deposition of CCs in the brain tissue triggering inflammation [30] with consequent interruption of neurologic functions and reduced cognition [14,31]. Similar findings have been proposed for humans with chronic CCs emboli to the brain from carotid arteries and aortic arch [31,32].

While only 4 of 14 patients with heterogenous/complex plaques had a positive TCD/CUS study, all had moderate stenosis (∼50 %). This is a clinically significant cohort, as these patients often fall into an equivocal area of management where surgical or endovascular intervention is debated. The detection of cholesterol crystal emboli in this group suggests that CUS/TCD may serve as a critical adjunct in risk stratification and timely referral for carotid intervention, even in the absence of >70 % stenosis.

The limitations of our study include a small number of cases. However, the technique as demonstrated by the in vivo in the rabbit model demonstrated that even a single crystal emboli discharged into the circulation can be detected by a clear and audible ping or HIT suggesting a highly accurate and sensitive method.

In conclusion, this pilot study and preliminary data describe a method for potentially identifying unstable internal carotid plaques with moderate stenosis by using simultaneous CUS/TCD detecting the release CCs into the cerebral circulation as a risk for TIAs or stroke.
