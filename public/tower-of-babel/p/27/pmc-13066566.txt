Impact of unmet expectations in clinical practice on nursing student competencies in a multi-center study
Authors: Asefu Awoke Degefa, Worku Animaw Temesgen, Mastewal Ayehu Sinshaw, Lidiya Abrham Degu, Eskindir Kassa Assen, Betelhem Mekonnen Alem, Yetimwork Gebrie Demewoz, Henok Biresaw Netsere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical practice is essential for competency acquisition in nursing education. However, unmet expectations can negatively affect nursing students’ clinical competency. Factors related to unmet expectations among nursing students remain unclear within the Ethiopian context. To understand the factors related to unmet expectations in clinical practice, it is crucial to investigate these issues. This study aimed to explore the factors related to unmet expectations of nursing students in clinical practice and its impact on competency. A qualitative descriptive study was conducted at Bahir Dar University and the University of Gondar, Ethiopia, from November 1 to December 31, 2024. The study participants were undergraduate nursing students, preceptors, head nurses, hospital administrators, and lecturers. Data were collected through five focus group discussions (each with six purposively selected students) and four key informant interviews, both using semi-structured guides. All interviews and discussions were recorded. The recorded data were transcribed, translated, and analyzed thematically with the help of ATLAS.ti 9. Finally, themes were identified and presented with narration. 34 participants (18 male and 16 female), 30 nursing students, and 4 key informants were involved from two public universities having teaching hospitals. Three main themes were identified: insufficient clinical support and mentorship, gaps between nursing education and practical application, and communication gaps along with a lack of initial orientation. This study concluded that unmet expectations in a clinical learning environment affect the student’s learning experiences. Factors contributing to these unmet expectations included inadequate supervision and guidance, discrepancies between theory and practice, communication gaps, a theory focused clinical practice, lack of initial orientation, and unsupportive clinical settings. All these factors hinder the development of nursing students’ competency. These findings imply the need for stronger collaboration between clinical instructors and nurses, offering incentives and training for clinical staff, improving resource allocation, creating less crowded clinical setting and the establishing of structured orientation and effective communication systems. Introduction

Clinical practice is the essential component of nursing education1. The purpose of the clinical practice training is to enable students to translate what they have learned in theoretical education into practice, to identify and solve problems, improve their skills, and facilitate the development of competences2. Clinical practice in the clinical environment contributes to shaping the professional development of nursing students3. Nursing students have expectations for their educational experiences and the support they will receive from the clinical learning environment when they enter to these clinical settings4. All students have expectations regarding what they will learn during their clinical experience5. Expectations might be met or unmet6. Met expectations are defined as a match between what a student expected to experience before starting a clinical attachment compared to what he or she experienced6. On the other hand, unmet expectation is defined as a nursing student’s expectation regarding their clinical courses or experiences that are not fulfilled or realized as they had hoped5,6.

One of the strategic directions outlined in the Global Strategic Directions for Nursing and Midwifery focuses on education, emphasizing educating enough midwives and nurses with competencies to meet population health needs7. To achieve this strategic plan, the expectation of nursing students should be met. When students’ expectations are met, it can have a positive impact on their clinical learning outcomes and lead to being competent in nursing practice8,9. However, numerous studies worldwide reveal that their expectations might not always be met in a clinical learning environment10. For instance, studies conducted in Ireland highlighted that an unsupportive learning environment combined with unwelcoming clinical staff, clinical instructors’ lack of expertise, and a lack of sense of belongingness among students were some of the major issues that negatively affect students’ learning in the clinical learning environment11. This insufficient support from clinical staff can result in unmet expectations and negative experiences for learners, and this lack of support can lead to disappointment and demotivation and may even contribute to learner attrition8.

Similarly, a study in Sri Lanka found that 85% of nursing students were dissatisfied with the curriculum due to a lack of updates on current medical issues, 17% felt theoretical content was not helpful in practice integration, and 36% suggested updating standard procedures to match clinical reality12. This disconnect between curriculum and clinical settings can affect the students’ ability to integrate knowledge into practice and hinder skill development.

Another study done in Iran revealed that, in the clinical environment, nursing students may encounter resistance from staff when attempting to adhere to evidence-based practices; for instance, when it comes to hand washing, some individuals may dismiss the recommended guidelines and urge students to hurry up, suggesting to them the prescribed methods are only theoretical and not applicable in real-life situations, and these challenges make the students undermine the importance of evidence-based care and undermine their expectations of applying evidence-based care in real-life situations13.

Another descriptive qualitative study conducted in Iran found that most participants were concerned about the lack of adequate clinical supervision, which led to insufficient follow-up and missed learning opportunities14. If adequate learning opportunities are not provided, students may graduate without meeting the professional standards and competencies required for the nursing field15. The lack of opportunities to develop essential skills could also adversely affect nursing students’ learning and the quality of care they provide to patients16. A qualitative descriptive study in Italy showed that one of the reasons the students to drop out from the nursing program is unmet expectation in the clinical setting17.

In Southeastern Nigeria, a study has identified a lack of tutorial/supervisory support and guidance by lecturers, clinical supervisors, and preceptors, and a lack of equipment for practice encountered by student nurses that hinders their clinical skills acquisition in the surveyed educational programs16. These issues result in nursing students perceiving their clinical learning as misaligned with their expectations, which can negatively affect their competence. A qualitative study conducted in Addis Ababa found that nursing students faced challenges such as inadequate follow-up, guidance, insufficient presence of instructors in clinical settings, and subjective evaluations in the clinical learning environment, which can lead to limited learning opportunities18.

All these unmet expectations during their clinical attachments can discourage nursing students and contribute to attrition from the profession8. They also impact their learning experiences, professional growth, and overall satisfaction19. This can further lead to frustration, disillusionment, and dissatisfaction with the nursing profession, as well as perceptions of incompetence among professional nurses, which may ultimately have an impact on their motivation and academic performance8,20.

Some studies highlight the expectations of nursing students in nursing education worldwide4,8,21,22. Most reviewed studies have focused on general student expectations in clinical practice without going into detail on the factors contributing to unmet expectations. Information is needed on factors related to unmet expectations in clinical practice in order to gain a deeper understanding of these issues. Therefore, this study aims to explore the factors related to unmet expectations of nursing students in clinical practice and its impact on competency at Bahir Dar University and University of Gondar.

Materials and methods

Study design

A qualitative descriptive study design was conducted23.

Study setting, participant, and period

This study was conducted at the College of Medicine and Health Sciences of Bahir Dar University and University of Gondar, Ethiopia. The study was conducted from November 1 to December 31, 2024. The study participants were undergraduate nursing students who had at least one month of clinical exposure. Various nursing programs that is, comprehensive nursing, pediatrics, emergency and critical care nursing (ECCN), and psychiatry, starting from the second year, were included. The study also included preceptors, head nurses, hospital administrators, and lecturers from the two universities.

Inclusion Criteria

All undergraduate nursing students with at least one month of clinical experience at two universities were included.

Preceptors

Nurses working at the two teaching hospitals who mentor, monitor, teach, and provide feedback to undergraduate nursing students during clinical practice, with at least two years of experience in the preceptorship role, were included.

Head nurses

Head nurses working at the two teaching hospitals who were responsible for directly supervising clinical learning environments and supervising nursing students, with at least two years of experience in a head nurse role, were included.

Hospital administrators

Administrators with at least one year of experience in an administrator role were included.

Lecturers

Nursing faculty members from Bahir Dar University or the University of Gondar with at least 2 years of teaching experience in undergraduate nursing programs were included.

Sampling, and sample size

The maximum variation purposive sampling method was used to include students with different characteristics (gender and academic year). This method allowed us the collection of diverse insights and ensured the findings reflected multiple perspectives.

Two class representative students of different genders from each academic year and from each nursing specialty (ECCN, pediatrics, comprehensive nursing, and psychiatry) were purposely recruited for the study. One or two class-representative students from each specialty within one academic year were selected for the focused group discussions (FGDs). Six students with all specialties were grouped according to their academic year. Thus, each focus group comprised six students from different nursing specialties but from the same academic year. Students with less than one month of clinical experience were not included because of their limited clinical experience. Five FGDs were conducted with each group having six participants (total 30 students). Since no new codes or themes emerged in the consecutive focus groups, the sample size was considered sufficient. Regarding the selection of key informants, the maximum variation purposive sampling method was used based on their professional roles, positions, gender, and years of experience within the healthcare and educational settings. Four key informants were interviewed; One preceptors, one head nurse, and one hospital administrator and one lecturer. Both the discussions and interviews were carried out until data saturation was reached.

Data collection tool

Data were collected from students using semi-structured discussion guides and from key informants through semi-structured interview guides, both adapted from different literature24–26. The research team revised the guides and pretested them in one group consisting of six students and one key informant, who was a nursing lecturer, to assess their clarity and cultural appropriateness and to identify any problems with the interview and discussion guide. A pretest discussion and interview results were excluded in the later analysis. Instead, they were used to refine the semi-structured interview and discussion guides. Separate guides were developed for students and for key informants. The guides for both key informants and focus group discussions were initially prepared in English. However, all the interviews and discussions were conducted in the local language.

Data collection procedure

Before the discussions, participants were informed about the study’s purpose, and their permission was obtained. The discussions were conducted in a quiet classroom. The principal investigator, who is an MSc candidate with formal training in qualitative research methods and analysis, facilitated the FGD by using a semi-structured guide.

A BSc nurse took notes throughout the discussion. The discussion was recorded with a digital audio recorder. The duration of each FGD was 70 min on average. Written consent was obtained from key informants before conducting the interviews. Then, they were informed about the study’s purpose. The interviews were taking place in their office. Questions were asked based on the interview guide, and subsequently, probing questions were asked for further information. The interviews were recorded with a digital audio recorder. A principal investigator, an MSc candidate with formal training in qualitative research methods and analysis, conducted the interview, while a BSC nurse took notes. Each interview lasted 63 min on average.

Data management and analysis

Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently for two months. All interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded with participant consent. Then the audio data was transcribed verbatim into textual format. The principal investigator read the transcriptions several times and listened to the audio recordings multiple times. The transcripts and audio data were cross-checked, and corrections were made for transcription errors. The data were then translated into English by a language expert. The principal investigator read the translated document several times to get a general sense of the content. All audio recordings, transcripts, and translated data were transferred to a password-protected computer and securely stored. The translated data were imported into ATLAS.ti 9 software. The data analysis was conducted using thematic analysis. Initial codes were generated for segments of the data. The codes were then organized into categories. Similar categories were grouped into themes, and finally, the study findings were presented with narrative descriptions and quotes.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki’s principles and approved by Bahir Dar University’s institutional review board with protocol number 930/2024. Prior to data collection, the title of the study, the purpose of the study, the procedure and duration, risks and benefits, and the right to refuse or withdraw at any time were explained. Additionally participants were informed that the FGDs and interviews would be audio-recorded. Participants were assured that all information they provided would remain confidential and the data would be used only for the purpose of the study. Then, written informed consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to participate in the interviews and discussions, as well as for audio recording. To ensure anonymity, paricipants were assigned pseudonyms and no personal identifiers were recorded. All audio recordings, transcripts, and other information were stored securely in a locked storage and on password-protected personal computers. The information was used only for research purposes and not shared with unauthorized individuals.

Unmet expectations and its impacts on student competencies

A total of 503 codes were initially identified. After merging duplicates or similar codes, these were reduced to 120. Codes were allocated into categories according to similarities. Finally, three themes were identified: insufficient clinical support and mentorship, gaps between nursing education and practical application, and communication gaps along with a lack of initial orientation.

Theme 1: Insufficient clinical support and mentorship

Nursing students expect to learn and develop their skills when they enter the clinical area. However, insufficient clinical support and mentorship contribute to the gaps between their expectations and experiences. This theme has two subthemes: inadequate supervision and guidance and an unconducive clinical learning environment.

Subtheme 1: Inadequate supervision and guidance

As key informants reported, instructors visit the hospital primarily to take attendance and do not spend time with the students. This may lead to missed educational opportunities. As a result, students might not develop their skills and knowledge during their clinical attachments.

Participants described:

“They (instructors) are usually there just to take attendance. They don’t stay with the students for the entire day, which means students might miss out on learning opportunities.” (Key informant, 03).

Students reported that clinical nurses do not provide detailed guidance and offer instructions on how to perform tasks.

As mentioned in the FGD:

“Clinical staff don’t provide any guidance, such as telling us, “Do it like this, do it like that…” (P3, FGD4).

Key informants stated that the reason for lack of supervision and guidance is that some clinical nurses find it tedious to provide continuous supervision. Nurses simply instruct the students to do tasks without guidance and supervision. This may result in errors and missed learning opportunities.

The key informants stated:

Some clinical staff find it tedious to supervise students consistently. They simply instruct students to administer medication to a specific patient without proper guidance. They may say things like, “Give this medication to patient bed number X.” They may then just sit back. (Key informant, 04).

Participants reported that hospital nurses are not compensated/receive incentives for teaching students in the clinical area. This leads to a lack of motivation to support students, and failure to supervise students by hospital nurses. Additionally, the absence of nursing educators in the hospital prevented students from receiving feedback or support regarding their clinical performance. As a result, students may not gain adequate support from nursing educators and nurses. Key informants cited a lack of incentives as the primary reason for not supervising and supporting students by the clinical nurses.

A key informant stated:

Clinical staff have raised concerns, expressing that they have questions and need incentives. They seek motivation to teach students, demonstrate procedures, and show practical applications. This is a common concern among many clinical staff members. So, the lack of incentives for the clinical staff is the primary reason for not supervising the students in the clinical area. (Key informant, 02)

Similarly, a fourth-year male nursing student from pediatrics department raised:

During our second-year attachment in the Psychiatry Department, the staff expressed that.

they weren’t obligated to teach us without compensation since the teachers were being paid to do so. As a result, cooperation from the hospital staff is limited. This has led to a situation where we don’t even get the chance to practice under supervision. (P1, FGD4)

Students struggle to meet learning expectations without supervision and guidance. Lack of guidance and correction during clinical practice leads to discomfort for students. An instructor’s presence in the clinical area is important for increasing confidence when students perform procedures.

A fourth-year female nursing student from the ECCN department reported:

I did not achieve my expectations. The reason is that I don’t feel comfortable practicing without someone guiding us and telling us when we’re wrong. If a teacher is with me while I perform a procedure, I feel much more comfortable. (P4, FGD4)

This lack of guidance and supervision also leads to fear of performing procedures independently. This results in a lack of confidence in performing clinical tasks, as mentioned by key informants.

This can be understood from the expression of the key informants:

“Students often fear performing procedures independently because instructors do not supervise and guide them.” (Key Informant, 01).

Students feel that a lack of supervision from experienced staff increases their fear of making mistakes, causing them to miss opportunities to perform tasks. They also lack the confidence to carry out procedures without guidance.

A fourth-year female nursing student from the ECCN department stated:

“If something happens to the patient when we perform tasks on our own, the risk may fall on us. We may not do it because of that fear. (P5, FGD4)

A third-year male student from ECCN department also stated:

“We worried that we might harm a patient if we made a mistake. This lack of guidance and support has greatly impacted our confidence.” (P6, FGD5).

Another third-year male student from comprehensive nursing department said:

“If we don’t practice under supervision, we can’t practice with confidence alone. I don’t believe we are practicing competently.” (P3, FGD1).

Students stated that insufficient support and supervision not only affected their confidence but also restricted them from acquiring skills. Without supervision and support, they worry about making mistakes and the consequences that might occur to the patient. This fear of making mistakes leads to reluctance to perform procedures. Eventually, this may prevent them from enhancing the necessary skills.

A third-year female nursing student from the ECCN department described:

“If I wanted to practice, I didn’t have any assistance. And I worried about what might happen if I made a mistake and potentially harmed a patient. This concern made me hesitate to perform procedures.” (P6, FGD1).

Students reported that they were not given any guidance or direction when they went to the clinical attachment, which reduced their opportunities to practice and learn.

A second-year female nursing student from the comprehensive nursing department said:

There was no one to guide us after we arrived, and there was no direction on what to do. Due to this, we didn’t have many opportunities to learn and practice. Because health work is risky, we did not feel confident practicing there. (P6, FGD3)

Key informants described that the lack of guidance and supervision not only prevents them from practicing but also reduces their competency.

Participants shared the following:

“Another factor is the lack of clinical or academic staff who consistently demonstrate procedures and provide hands-on guidance. This absence of support can hinder the development of practical skills and competence.” (Key informant, 04).

Key informants said that inadequate supervision also leads to violations of sterility during procedures.

One of the Key Informants explained:

“… If the clinical staff are not always available to supervise them, students may fail to maintain sterility in wound care, leading to infections in patients who are normally recovered.” (Key Informant, 03).

Key informants strongly believe that nursing students must be supervised in clinical settings to prevent medication errors. One informant stressed that students should never practice unsupervised.

The key informant described:

“Medication errors might occur primarily due to the student’s lack of knowledge and understanding. From my perspective, students should never be left alone in the hospital and should not be allowed to practice unsupervised.” (Key informant, 02).

Participants suggested that today’s nursing students are the future healthcare providers who will serve our families. Healthcare professionals and instructors should not compete with each other to supervise or teach students. Instead, they should work collaboratively to provide the best education. In their opinion, more emphasis must be given to teaching and supervising students. They have a concern that if current students do not receive proper education and supervision, students may not be able to meet the healthcare needs of the community in the future.

The key informant described this suggestion as follows:

These students are the future healthcare providers who will serve our sisters, brothers, mothers, and fathers. We should not compete with instructors to supervise, demonstrate, and teach them. If we do not teach and guide them properly today, it will be challenging for these students to care for our sisters, brothers, mothers, and fathers in the future. It is difficult to say that the current professionals will be able to serve everyone if these students do not learn and reach for the community tomorrow. In my opinion, we must focus on teaching and supervising them effectively. (Key informant, 03).

The participants confirmed that there is a lack of guidance and supervision due to limited incentives for hospital nurses, which leads to reduced confidence, fear of mistakes, restricted skill acquisition, and diminished competence and increases the risk of procedural violations and medication errors. They recommended that the instructor and nurses work together to provide regular supervision and guidance to address these issues.

Subtheme 2: Unconducive clinical environment

Students have shared their view that a clinical learning environment is affected by challenges that arise from the clinical environment. These are overcrowding and limited resources.

As expressed by participants, not only nursing students but also other medical students and professionals were available in the same clinical environments. This high number of students contributed to a crowded environment and made it difficult for students to have comfort.

A second-year female nursing student from the comprehensive nursing department described:

“There are others from us up to the 4th year. It’s not only nurses; there are others, including medical students. It is very crowded. It’s hard to say that it’s even a little bit comfortable.” (P6, FGD3).

Students reported that one of the challenges they face is the high number of students sent to the hospital for their clinical attachments. This increased number of students can lead to difficulty practicing in a clinical setting.

A fourth-year female nursing student from the ECCN department reported:

“Because of the increased number of students, many students are sent to the hospital together for our attachments, and this is one of the challenges we face…” (P5, FGD4).

As described by key informants, the clinical environment is saturated with too many students or healthcare professionals. This overcrowding made it difficult to use medical resources efficiently. Key informants described as follows:

The institution was facing a number of challenges due to overcrowding, including the improper use of medical equipment. You can’t provide sufficient resources for them. For example, I am currently the head nurse of this ward. I offered disposable gloves, vital sign equipment, alcohol, and many other supplies. However, what I can offer is not intended for all staff and students, but specifically for the nurses who deliver direct patient care. If I provide a resource and every student uses it, then it creates another resource problem. It is a resource wastage. (Key informant, 02)

As participants reported, several students repeatedly asked the same patient for medical history. Each student needs to collect a medical history from patients to prepare for presentations. However, this results in the patient becoming bored and exhausted as a result of several students interacting with the same patient.

One of the participants reported:

A student focused on taking a history from a patient. As long as it is part of their education, the student must be prepared for their presentation. So, the student must ask a patient to take a history because they have to present something to their instructor. So, one student would ask a patient, and then another student would come and ask the same patient again. There were also instances where patients said, “I’m just tired and don’t want to be asked any more questions. (Key informant, 02)

Similarly, another fourth-year male student from the comprehensive nursing department reported: “The more students there are, the more exhausted the patients become.” (P2, FGD4).

Key informant also said:

… Many students come to this hospital. When so many students arrive, they all want to perform their tasks, starting with history taking. More than five students may ask one patient at a time. This can cause the patient to feel restless and bored. (Key informant, 04)

Participants stated that the presence of many students might cause the patient to get bored. This may affect the patient’s willingness to provide a medical history. This may result in missed learning opportunities.

Participant from the ECCN department reported:

When we interact with patients, they appear bored. As I mentioned, many students come from private colleges, and a lot of students from this university practice with patients. I think the patients get bored and may not be willing to cooperate, especially when we take their history. (P4, FGD4)

As students described, when patients appear bored, it creates a challenging environment for students. Students feel uncomfortable when they see that patients are not interested in sharing their medical histories. As a result, the students choose to avoid interacting with the patients. This may result in missed learning opportunities for students.

A fourth-year male nursing student from the comprehensive nursing department described:

Some patients are good enough to allow us to ask for their medical history, but others look bored. We feel uncomfortable when we see their faces, and we end up leaving them without asking them because we don’t have anything to do for them other than potentially exhausting them further. (P3, FGD4)

As students stated, when numerous students are assigned to practice on the same patient, they struggle to perform tasks and each student has fewer opportunities. As a result, they may not be able to learn effectively.

A fourth-year male comprehensive nursing student described:

… Three or four students practicing on one patient is not suitable for performing tasks like history taking or any other procedures. You can’t repeat what someone else has done. For example, if another student takes a history from the patient, you should not take the history again. (P2, FGD4)

Students also mentioned the limited availability of resources in the clinical learning environment. Vital sign equipment was not provided when the student went to the hospital. This prevented them from performing physical examinations effectively, and they could not distinguish between pathological and physiological sounds beyond theoretical knowledge. Additionally, this absence of vital sign equipment made the students copy objective data from the patient chart and take only subjective data from the patient. As a result, they may not have the opportunity to engage in other practical aspects of patient assessment. Therefore, they may not be able to practice adequately.

A fourth-year male student from the comprehensive nursing department elaborated on this as follows:

Performing a physical examination requires materials. For example, we do not have stethoscopes provided, even for groups in the department. This lack of resources prevents us from practicing in the hospital. For instance, during every physical examination, there are sounds we need to identify, such as cardiovascular murmurs and respiratory wheezing. But what I know about murmurs is just theoretical; I don’t know what a murmur sounds like. What we have experienced in the hospital is subjective history-taking, but we have not had the opportunity to practice anything else. This makes it difficult to gain the necessary experience. We go to the hospital for clinical attachment without materials, like thermometers, so it is impossible to say that we have practiced by copying objective data from the chart. (P3, FGD4)

As key informants described, there is often a shortage of gloves in the hospital. Some students brought gloves from their department, but they quickly ran out because of their frequent use. When they asked the clinical nurse to receive gloves, the clinical nurse didn’t give gloves to students to save the limited supply. As a result, students became afraid to perform tasks without using gloves. This fear made them reluctant to perform clinical tasks. This eventually reduces their practical experience and learning opportunities.

Key informant stated that:

There’s often a shortage of gloves here in the hospital. Sometimes they bring their gloves, but they might finish it soon. Students waste a lot of gloves. Most of the time, they use gloves just to touch something and they discard it, they put them on again, and sometimes we don’t give them. They fear practicing without gloves and stop practicing in the clinical area. (Key informant, 02)

As mentioned by key informants, medical equipment is often broken when students use it. Since the equipment is broken and cannot be replaced, the clinical nurses chose not to give this equipment to students. This makes them unable to practice and master the skills and prevents them from gaining practical experience.

A key informant said:

Equipment like blood pressure cuffs, pulse oximeters, and thermometers might be broken and there is nobody that can replace that again for us, so we don’t give them this equipment which hinders students’ ability to take vital signs properly and prevents them from knowing what they need to know. since they don’t get that material to take vital signs, they record it from the chart without taking it. ( key informant, 01)

The participants explained that the clinical setting was not ideal for learning due to overcrowding and shortages of resources (inadequate gloves and medical equipment).It restricts students’ opportunities to practice, reduces confidence, and limits skill acquisition. Patients may become exhausted and unwilling to participate due to repeated interactions with multiple students, which further diminishes learning opportunities.The lack of adequate resources makes them rely on copying from the patient chart rather than practicing skills, prevents them from performing procedures effectively, makes them unable to practice and master the skills, and ultimately reduces competency development.

Theme 2: Gaps between nursing education and practical application

Under this theme, two subthemes were identified.

Subtheme 1: Theory focus clinical practice

Students described their hospital experience as mostly theoretical. Despite studying theory in the classroom and the hospital, they lack practical experience. They believed that only learning theory in both settings was insufficient for their competency development.

This point was raised by a fourth-year nursing student from the ECCN department as follows:

Our experience in the hospital is mostly theory. Most of it is theory. So, I don’t think we’ve had enough practical experience. We learn theory in the classroom and again in the hospital setting. I believe learning these two theories in the classroom and hospital setting does not significantly contribute to developing our competency. (P6, FGD4)

As students reported, theoretical assignments such as writing diaries, histories, and seminars have been concentrated during their clinical attachment. They were concerned that their clinical education was only theory with insufficient practical experience. Their instructor evaluated them based on these written tasks. This may lead them to concentrate on just writing documents rather than performing practical tasks and prevent them from acquiring skills.

A third-year female nursing student from the pediatrics department elaborated in detail:

We focused on writing histories, diaries, and seminars. Our instructor asked us to document these, but we lack practical experience. We always focused on the score. Since our instructor evaluated us based on it, we just wrote; we focused on writing histories, diaries, and seminars. We concentrated on theoretical tasks rather than practical applications. It feels theory-oriented: just history, diaries, and seminars… (P2, FGD1).

The key informant also supported this:

The instructor told the students things like, “Choose a case for tomorrow, prepare it, and you will present it at the bedside.” Then the students are focused on these assignments and are concerned about how to answer the instructor’s questions. However, the instructor doesn’t tell the students to interact with the clinical staff, doesn’t tell them to understand from the clinical staff when they perform procedures, or to learn from the clinical staff. They frequently focus on theory rather than practice. (Key informant, 02)

Students were worried about when they would acquire clinical skills if their time in the hospital was dedicated to theoretical activities. This could result in graduating before having adequate practical skills.

A fourth-year female nursing student from the comprehensive nursing department mentioned:

“If our clinical attachment focuses only on theory, when do I practice my skill then? “(P4, FGD2).

Clinical learning for students is primarily theoretical, with little practical training, which leads them to an excessive focus on theoretical tasks rather than practical skill acquisition. This hinders students from developing adequate practical skills and competencies before graduation.

Subtheme 2: Discrepancy Between the theory learned in the class and the practice learned in the hospital setting

Students stated that their expectation at the beginning of their hospital attachment was to understand every theoretical concept they had been taught. However, they found that practice in the hospital did not always match the theoretical knowledge they acquired in the class. This mismatch may make it difficult for them to apply their theoretical knowledge.

A third-year female nursing student from pediatrics department described:

When I first went to my hospital attachment, I had a plan to achieve a clear understanding of all the concepts I had learned in theory, whether in fundamental, medical-surgical, or psychiatric contexts. I had a specific objective in mind. However, I found that the procedures performed in the hospital did not always align with what I had learned theoretically. (P2, FGD1)

Similarly, a third-year female nursing student from the comprehensive department stated that:

What we learned in theory often seems unrelated to practice. And sometimes it even appears to contradict each other. There are moments when you might question, “Is it really necessary to learn to do this?” What we learn in theory follows international standards, but the reality of practice might be different.” (P4, FGD1).

The key informant cited limited resources as the primary factor contributing to the theory-practice gap in nursing education.

The key informant described:

“Students often say, “What we learned in theory and what is done in the hospital are practically different. Why?” This is associated with the issue related to resources.” (Key informant, 04).

As key informants described, students were taught procedures in detail during their theoretical training. However, students found that procedures in the clinical setting differed from what they were taught. This discrepancy can make them confused.

A key informant said:

“Students were taught the entire step-by-step procedure when they learned theory. For example, there are many materials to perform catheterization, but when it comes to practice, it’s different. Sometimes, this difference confuses them.” (Key informant, 03).

Students also shared their experience of confusion due to conflicting instructions between their classroom training and hospital practice. Teachers instructed them to perform tasks in a specific manner in class. However, at the hospital, they received another instruction from the clinicians. As a result, they may struggle to identify and take the correct instruction.

A third-year nursing student from the comprehensive department stated:

When I attended class, our teachers instructed us to do things a certain way, but when I came to the hospital for practice, the healthcare professionals said, ‘What is this? It’s your ignorance that made you act like this.’ These teachings contradict each other. When situations like this arise, it confuses me and brings me to the point of asking, ‘Should I trust what I’m studying in class, or should I trust what I’m learning in the hospital?” (P3, FGD5).

Similarly, in another FGD a third-year nursing student stated their experience:

When I was assigned to the clinical attachment, the nurse instructed me to take a patient’s vital signs while the nurse was administering medication. I greeted the patient by asking, “How are you? good morning, are you feeling better?” At that moment, the nurse responded, “This is not a place to learn theory. You will not come here to learn theory, are you going to hang out while talking like that?” I questioned myself, asking, “What does this mean? Is what we are learning false education?”, Is the education we are receiving false? I wondered, So, what should I continue with? (P1, FGD1)

Similarly, another participant from a fourth-year nursing student in the ECCN department reported:

“It’s confusing whether we should follow the procedure we learned during demonstrations or the one practiced in the hospital. “(P4, FGD4).

Students said that their theoretical education teaches them the proper and standard procedures. However, the practical application in the hospital often varies from this education. In practice, tasks were performed in non-standard practices rather than strictly following the standards. This makes the students adapt and perform the practical methods commonly used in the hospital setting.

This is understood by the participants from fourth-year students in the ECCN department:

In theory, we learn the correct and standard way of doing things. But when it comes to practice in the hospital, like drug calculations or administering IV fluids and setting the drop rate, it’s often done through habit rather than following the exact steps from theory. Even if you don’t see students doing things strictly by theory when they reach the fourth year, they tend to adapt to the practical methods used in the hospital.” (P5, FGD4).

Students encountered differences between hospital practice and classroom theory, primarily due to non-standardized hospital procedures and limited resources. This discrepancy makes it difficult for students to apply what they have learned in the classroom effectively, leading to misunderstandings and a tendency to adopt common hospital practices over standard practices.

Theme 3: Lack of orientation and communication in the clinical learning environment

Two subthemes were identified under this theme:

Subtheme 1: Lack of initial orientation

The interviewee described several issues related to students’ failure to adhere to professional ethics, such as taking photos, gossiping, and disturbing patients. These violations are due to inadequate orientation about the policies when they enter the hospital environment.

One of the key informants reported:

Most of them abide by the policies, but some do not respect the policies. Some take photos with their phones in front of patients, gossiping, laughing, and disturbing people. This indicates that students do not maintain professional ethics. Outside of that… they come to the hospital on time and wander from ward to ward after their instructor has left. Then, when it’s time to leave, they come back. (Key informant, 03)

After probing why they violated all those policies, the informant described that when students arrive at the hospital, they do not receive adequate explanations from healthcare providers about the institution’s policies and procedures.

He said:

“When they arrive at the hospital, there’s often no healthcare provider who explains these aspects. Consequently, the students frequently lack knowledge about the policies and procedures.” (Key informant, 02).

Another key informant has also reported that non-compliance with institutional policies, such as punctuality, dress code, and professional behavior, is an issue among some students. This non-compliance with institutional policies is due to inadequate orientation.

He reported:

“If students received adequate orientation on the importance of professional appearance, they were more likely to follow these guidelines/ policies effectively.’’ (Key Informant, 01).

Nursing students experienced confusion during their initial clinical attachment at the hospital due to a lack of proper orientation. They did not receive the necessary guidance to familiarize themselves with the environment.

The participants from the pediatrics department stated:

When we first went to the hospital, we didn’t know anything. We had learned the theory, but we did not know what to do. The instructors left us there. The instructors left us without familiarizing us with the environment at that time. At that time, the place where we were, we didn’t know where to go. We didn’t even know where the entry and exit of the hospital were. There were occasions when we went to the bathroom thinking it was the room (laugh…) because we didn’t know. (P1, FGD1)

Another student reported that instructors failed to familiarize the students with the hospital environment, even on the first day of their attachment.

A third-year student from the comprehensive nursing department reported:

It is also a problem for the instructors. They didn’t let us know the environment, even on the first day. We didn’t even know the hospital environment. For example, I had never been to a hospital before, I had never seen. When we first arrived, we didn’t know the environment at all. (P4, FGD1)

Nursing students’ experience of inadequate orientation upon entering the hospital environment leads to poor adherence to professional ethics and institutional policies and confusion during their initial clinical attachment and hinders their ability to familiarize themselves with the hospital setting. This failure to adhere to professional ethics and institutional policies negatively affects their attitudes and professional development.

Subtheme 2: Communication gaps

Most of the interviewed nurses expressed concerns about the lack of communication regarding the presence of students in the hospital. There is often no prior notice about students coming to the hospital, which leads the staff unaware of their presence. This leads to a lack of information about students’ identities and the purposes of their arrival.

The key informant described:

“When students are assigned to the hospital, we often do not know who they are: whether they are students, nursing students, or professionals from other fields.” (Key informant, 01).

The key informant reported that many instructors bring students to the hospital without communicating with the hospital staff beforehand. This lack of prior communication leads to challenges in supporting the students.

The key informants stated:

“Many instructors bring students without communicating with us. As a result, we are unable to support the students effectively.” (Key informant, 01).

The key informant reported that when students first arrive at the hospital, instructors do not introduce the students to the hospital staff. This lack of communication means that staff are unaware of the students’ purpose or status, whether they are there for clinical attachment or another reason. As a result, there is no one available to guide the students on what to do, leading to failure to meet the expected tasks.

He explained:

However, when the students first come, the instructors don’t tell us about the students or introduce the students to us. Since it is not known whether the students come to the hospital for attachment or not, there is no one to tell them what to do. So, they are not doing what is expected of them. (Key informant, 01)

A second-year nursing student also explained:

“Our instructors didn’t introduce us, but they brought us to the place, and they left.” (P6, FGD3).

Again, this is elaborated by a third-year student from the pediatrics department students as:

The other reason is related to the supervisors or instructors. Even on the first day, they just let us in and left without introducing us to the ward nurses. I remember the first day we entered, we didn’t know anyone, not even the ward coordinator, and even the staff said, “Who are you? Who are they?”, we didn’t know anyone. (P1, FGD1)

The key informant reported that a common issue is that teachers bring students to the hospital but then leave without ensuring proper introduction to the hospital staff. This results in students having minimal interaction with the staff. Additionally, there is no instructor present to facilitate the handover of the students. Consequently, staff are unable to explain important aspects of hospital approaches, rules, regulations, and policies.

He reported:

Often, teachers bring them to the hospital. There is a common bad practice: they bring the students and leave them without us being able to see them. The students have little interaction with us. Unless we ask specifically about their department or the year, they’re in, we don’t get much information, and there is no teacher available to hand them over to us or to explain anything about the students. As a result, there’s no way to explain things like, “This is how it is, this is how our approaches are, this is how our rules and regulations are, and this is how our policies are so far. (Key informant, 02)

The same key informant reported that patients and attendants often recognize students and refuse to share medical history. This issue arises when students have not effectively communicated or interacted with the hospital staff. This problem of patient irritation may have been avoided if students interacted with nurses.

He reported:

The patients and their attendants know who the students are, and they might say, “Just don’t ask me, don’t ask me again. You are a student, right?” This happened because the students didn’t communicate with us. If the students had interacted with us when they first arrived, this problem would not have occurred. Therefore, a lack of communication becomes a hindrance to their practice and education. (Key informant, 02)

Students reported that when they enter the hospital for clinical attachments, nurses are often unaware of the specific purpose or objectives of the attachment. The staff perceives all students, regardless of their year, as similar. The students believe that effective communication between instructors and nurses would provide more appropriate guidance based on their academic year.

One of the participants from pediatrics department stated:

When we enter, no matter what the student enters, even if they made us to practice, they do not know what the purpose or the objective of the attachment is. Therefore, 2nd-year and 3rd-year students appear similar for clinical staff, but it should not be like that. I believe the instructors should at least communicate with the nurses who work there. I believe it would be better if this happened. (P1, FGD4)

The key informant reported a desire for instructors to accompany students to the hospital and provide a formal handover to the clinical staff. The key informant suggested that if instructors communicated the students’ academic year, length of stay in the ward, content covered in class, and the tasks they are expected to perform, they would receive better support from the nurses.

The key informant described:

What we want is, the instructor to come with the students and hand them over to us. If the instructor said, “These are third-year nursing students who will be with you for two weeks, one week in wing A and one week in wing B. They will do what you do; they learned everything in the class.” We would better support them. (Key informant, 02)

The key informant reported that if the instructors provided prior notice and formally handed over the students to the clinical staff, the clinical staff could introduce them to the patients. This approach would enable students to work more effectively with the staff. However, the key informant noted that this is not the case.

The key informant described:

If the instructors inform us in advance and hand the students over, we can introduce them to the patients. If we do this, they will be able to work effectively with us the next day. But unfortunately, there is no such thing. (Key informant, 01)

The absence of prior communication and formal introduction of nursing students in the hospital leads to a lack of support from clinical staff, resulting in students being unguided and unable to meet expected clinical tasks. This lack of interaction between students and nurses prevents the effective explanation of hospital policies and protocols and potentially causes the patients to refuse to provide medical history. Key informants recommend that instructors introduce clinical staff regarding students’ academic year, duration of clinical practice, and expected tasks, which facilitates support and integration, thereby enhancing students’ clinical learning experiences.

All these factors (inadequate supervision and guidance, the discrepancy between theory and practice, communication gaps, theory-focused clinical practice, lack of initial orientation, and an unsupportive clinical environment) contributed to nursing students’ unmet expectations. These factors have an impact on the competency of nursing students.

Discussion

The main aim of this study was to explore the factors related to unmet expectations of nursing students in clinical practice and its impact on competency. This study revealed that students often have unmet expectations during their clinical attachment.

Insufficient clinical support and mentorship was one of the identified themes. According to the participant, inadequate supervision and guidance during clinical attachments is one of the causes of unmet expectations for nursing students. Supervision is not consistently provided, leading to missed learning opportunities. There is also insufficient guidance. This lack of adequate guidance and supervision affects the effectiveness of practical learning. Similarly, a study conducted in Addis Ababa found that nursing students in the clinical area faced challenges due to insufficient follow-up and guidance18. However, it is contrast with a finding from a study conducted in Malawi that revealed that students received adequate support and guidance from the trained preceptors in the clinical setting28. This difference may be due to the variations in incentives provided. Students expressed concerns about the lack of instructor presence in clinical settings, which they felt diminished their learning opportunities18. A study conducted in Norway concluded that supervision was thought to be crucial during training in the simulation center and in clinical placement, as well as planning of the training29. Therefore, this study also suggests continuous student supervision and guidance to enhance learning opportunities and learning experiences for effective practical training and to meet students’ expectations. This can be achieved by strengthening the role of the preceptors through the provision of incentives.

In addition to inadequate supervision and guidance, students have unmet expectations due to inadequate resources and overcrowding in clinical settings. The lack of essential equipment, such as stethoscopes and thermometers, hinders their ability to perform physical examinations. This limited resource restricts their ability to gain practical experience and makes them depend on theoretical knowledge without practical application. According to Bandura’s social cognitive learning theory, nursing students learn by observing experienced healthcare professionals, and modeling their behavior30,31. Inadequate resource limits their opportunity to observe procedures when nurses perform. As a result, students may not be able to observe best practices, which is essential for gaining competence in clinical skills. Without sufficient observation opportunities, students may struggle to integrate theoretical knowledge into practice, an absence of effective learning, and eventually limit their clinical competence. Similarly, A study conducted in Iran found that inadequate training and resources can leave learners feeling that their expectations are unmet; this includes a lack of sufficient equipment, inadequate training spaces, and insufficient faculty members, all of which can negatively impact learners’ satisfaction and overall experience32. Similarly, a study done in Ghana found that one of the challenges students face in the clinical environment is a lack of equipment, and this lack prevented them from practicing what they had been taught in the classroom33. A study in Botswana also concluded that the unavailability of resources has a negative effect on the learning and teaching experience of students34. It is further argued that the lack of facilities and the unavailability of necessary equipment create a challenging and unpleasant educational environment for students35. Therefore, this study suggests that educational institutions and healthcare facilities should allocate adequate resources and essential equipment to ensure a positive learning experience.

This study reveals that the presence of an excessive number of students also creates competition for limited opportunities to engage in clinical tasks, affecting the opportunity to learn. The combined impact of inadequate resources and overcrowding results in a challenging learning environment. Students struggle to gain practical skills and experience. This is supported by related literature, which emphasizes that students perform better in environments where they feel respected and included as part of the team36. Similarly, a study conducted in South Africa found that participants reported negative experiences as a result of overcrowding. They noted that professional nurses faced challenges managing numerous students from different disciplines, which led to competition among students for clinical procedures. This overcrowding in clinical areas reduced learning opportunities and contributed to students’ overall negative experiences37. A study done in the USA revealed that a better understanding of attributes in the clinical learning environment and collaboration between nursing education programs, and healthcare agencies creates meaningful clinical experiences and enhances student preparation for the professional nurse role38. This study found that overcrowding is one of the contributing factors to the unmet expectations of the students. Thus, this study suggests that nursing schools should distribute students to health institutions where each student can participate in tasks and meet their expectations during clinical attachments. It would enhance their learning experiences and build their confidence in performing nursing skills.

The findings under the theme “gaps between nursing education and practical application” reveal that the other factor that contributes to unmet expectations of nursing students during their clinical attachments is the discrepancy between the theory learned in college and the practices observed in the clinical environment. Students often find that the procedures and guidelines taught during classroom demonstrations do not align with the methods used in hospital settings. This discrepancy results in the students feeling confused and uncertain about whether to adhere to their academic training or to adapt to the practices they see in the hospital. This discrepancy between theory and practice makes it more difficult to apply principles that have been taught and leads to frustration and unmet expectations. Students enter the clinical area expecting to see a direct application of their theoretical knowledge. However, they often encounter inconsistencies that undermine their confidence. This study aligns with other research conducted in Namibia, where the procedures taught in the classroom often differed from the hospital practice, leading to confusion39. The primary factor contributing to this theory-practice gap in this study is a limitation of resources. A study conducted in Ghana recommended allocating additional resources to enhance student learning during clinical placements, including providing adequate equipment, supplies, and preceptors40. In line with the studies, therefore addressing this issue is required to reduce the gap between theory and practice by ensuring that clinical environments are adequately resourced. Additionally, the study identifies that tasks in the clinical area are frequently performed based on non-standard practices rather than strictly adhering to standard methods, contributing to the theory-practice gap. When students observe these non-standard practices, their understanding and application of evidence-based procedures taught during their theoretical training become compromised. This inconsistency can lead to confusion. However, when clinical practices are standardized, students observe and practice procedures that align with the theoretical knowledge and help them develop competence40. Therefore, this study also suggests that the educational institutions and health institutions shall collaborate to standardize clinical practice. This can be achieved by providing ongoing education and training for nurses, developing clear guidelines for commonly performed procedures in the clinical learning environments, and allocating adequate resources for equipment and materials. These measures help to improve the learning experience of the student.

Beyond the discrepancy between theory and practice, students also expressed that their training in the hospital did not meet their expectations, since it prioritized theoretical tasks, and this did not significantly improve their competency. Instructors prioritized documentation and theoretical assignments over practical learning, which concerned students when they would have the opportunity to develop clinical skills. Students emphasized the need for more practical experience to complement their theoretical knowledge and develop clinical competency. Similarly, in a study in Turkey, 17.0% of the students reported that instructors emphasize theory more than practical experience41. Another study in Colombia indicated that instructors place excessive emphasis on theory over practical application42. This study found an excessive focus on theory in both the classroom and clinical settings, without preparing them in skills. However, studies reveal that students value clinical skills and practical experience over theoretical knowledge for their nursing profession43. Thus, this study suggests that instructors shall consider a balance between theoretical instruction and practical learning during clinical attachments. That means instructors shall incorporate skills during their clinical attachment to meet their expectations.

The findings under theme three indicated that there is a lack of prior communication between instructors and hospital staff during clinical attachments. This lack of communication between instructors and hospital staff leads to confusion about students’ identities and purposes upon arrival. Instructors often bring students to the hospital without proper introduction, resulting in staff having minimal interaction with students. This lack of communication and formal handover leads to students being left unsupervised, and inconsistent support by clinical staff. A study conducted in Dire Dawa, Ethiopia supported this, revealing that a lack of collaboration between nursing schools and clinical environments constrains effective communication and affects clinical teaching and learning44. Research has shown that collaboration between nursing faculty and staff nurses is crucial for enhancing the learning environment for students and improving student learning45. As suggested by other studies, this study also recommends enhancing communication between educational institutions and clinical settings and fostering collaboration between nursing instructors and clinical staff to meet students’ needs and expectations. This study also recommends fostering a strong relationship and clear communication between instructors and clinical staff. Such communication can enhance receiving support from the clinical staff and meeting their expectations.

In addition to lack of communication, students described that the lack of proper orientation contributes to unmet expectations during clinical attachments, leading to confusion, non-compliance with professional ethics, and overall dissatisfaction. Upon arriving at the hospital, students usually did not receive the necessary guidance regarding institutional policies. This inadequate orientation results in ethical violations, such as inappropriate behavior towards patients, and confusion about basic hospital procedures. Students reported feeling lost and confused during their initial clinical attachment due to a lack of familiarization with the hospital environment. The absence of adequate orientation may lead them negative clinical experiences. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Namibia, where some participants reported that the lack of orientation caused them to get lost in the wards and struggle to locate certain equipment46. A study conducted in Singapore highlighted the significance of providing orientation, noting that since nursing students are considered novice learners, they should be given an orientation program to ease their fears and enhance effective apprenticeship-based learning47. Therefore, this study also suggests that educational institutions and healthcare facilities should implement orientation programs to familiarize students with hospital policies and the physical environment. This would ensure that students’ needs are fulfilled and their overall satisfaction with their clinical experiences is improved.

Conclusion and recommendation

Unmet expectations in a clinical learning environment affect the student’s learning experiences. Factors contributing to these unmet expectations included inadequate supervision and guidance, discrepancies between theory and practice, communication gaps, a theory focused clinical environment, lack of initial orientation, and unsupportive clinical settings. These factors collectively hinder the development of competency for nursing students. The findings show the need for actions to meet nursing students’ expectations, enhance clinical competency, and improve the quality of education. It is essential to collaborate between clinical instructors and nurses to continuously supervise, guide, and provide constructive feedback to the students. Provide incentives for nurses who participate in student mentorship (preceptors). Collaborate with nursing institutions and hospitals to develop standardized protocols that align with classroom theory and provide training for hospital nurses. A clinical setting with necessary materials, equipment, and a less crowded environment shall be available to enable students to practice effectively. Clear communication between nursing schools and clinical areas shall be established. Nursing students should be formally introduced to the clinical staff, the nursing director, and other personnel. Orientation programs should be implemented to familiarize students with hospital policies at educational institutions and healthcare facilities. Furthermore, future research in a wider population may provide more useful information for nursing education.

Limitations

Since this is a qualitative study, the findings cannot be generalized to all populations. Potential researcher bias in data collection and interpretation may shape the study’s finding, and participant selection bias may have occurred. Additionally, the study was conducted with a small sample even though the data saturation was achieved. This limited sample may have constrained the diversity of perspectives.
