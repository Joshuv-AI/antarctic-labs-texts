Complication rates in thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations with and without neurological injury a Latin American multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Guillermo Ricciardi, Ignacio Cirillo, Rodrigo Pons Belmonte, Juan Pablo Cabrera, Alfredo Guiroy, Charles Carazzo, Ratko Yurac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThoracolumbar fracture-dislocations from high-energy trauma are severe injuries requiring surgical stabilization. The risks faced by neurologically intact patients remain unclear ObjectivesTo compare early complication rates in surgically treated patients with thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations according to neurological status. Methods: This retrospective multicenter cohort study included patients from 24 institutions in 9 Latin American countries who underwent surgery for thoracolumbar fracture-dislocation between 2015 and 2023. Eligible patients were aged ≥16, of any gender, with high-energy trauma requiring surgery. The primary outcome was early postoperative complications from admission to discharge or death, with patients categorized by neurological injury at admission ResultsA total of 262 patients were analyzed, primarily young men (n=212; 80.6%) with a mean age of 36.8 years (SD=13.7). The thoracic and thoracolumbar regions were most affected. Only 18% had no neurological damage, and over half with deficits had complete spinal cord injuries. A total of 268 complications were identified (median=1; range 0–9). Postoperative complications occurred in 28.6% of patients without neurological injury and 74.3% with neurological injury (p<0.001). Neurological injury was strongly associated with complications (OR=7.4; 95% CI=3.6–15.4; p<0.001), and clinical instability also had a significant association (OR=3.2; 95% CI=1.6–6.4; p=0.001) ConclusionsPatients with neurological damage face a markedly higher risk of early postoperative complications after thoracolumbar fracture-dislocation surgery. Results may have limited generalizability due to non-probabilistic institutional sampling.

CONCEPTOS CLAVE

Qué se sabe sobre el tema.

Thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations due to high-energy trauma are severe injuries characterized by mechanical instability and frequent neurological damage, accounting for approximately 10% of traumatic vertebral injuries. Surgical reduction and stabilization are widely recommended, typically performed using long posterior instrumentation with decompression. However, these injuries are often observed in polytraumatized patients, where associated injuries influence therapeutic decisions and surgical timing. While early decompression (<24 hours) is advised for patients with neurological deficits, the management of cases without initial neurological impairment remains uncertain, necessitating individualized evaluation.

Qué aporta este trabajo.

This study provides a multicenter analysis comparing early complication rates in surgically treated thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations based on the presence of neurological injury. By analyzing 262 patients from 24 institutions across Latin America, the study reveals that patients with neurological damage have a 7.2-fold higher risk of early complications than those without. This finding underscores the importance of assessing neurologically intact patients separately, as they may constitute a distinct clinical subgroup regarding the safety of early surgery.

Divulgación

Thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations are serious spinal injuries caused by high-energy trauma, such as car accidents or falls from height. These injuries often lead to significant instability in the spine and, in many cases, neurological damage, including partial or complete paralysis. Surgery is the standard treatment to stabilize the spine, but the timing and approach depend on the patient’s condition. While early surgery is recommended for those with neurological impairment, it is less clear how to manage patients who appear neurologically intact. This study analyzed data from multiple hospitals and found that patients with neurological damage are much more likely to experience early complications after surgery. These findings highlight the need for careful evaluation of all patients with these injuries, as even those without initial neurological deficits may still face significant risks.

Introduction

Thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations caused by high-energy trauma are severe injuries. 1 , 2 They account for 10% of traumatic vertebral injuries and often require surgical stabilization due to mechanical instability and frequent neurological damage 1-6 .

Thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations are usually diagnosed in the context of severely polytraumatized patients with associated injuries that determine therapeutic decisions and the timing of surgery 2 , 4 . Surgical delay can be caused by several factors, including those related to the mechanism of trauma, the presence of non-vertebral injuries that put the patient's life at risk, and the physiological impact, including the risk of a second injury associated with surgery 7-9 . On the other hand, the healthcare system is influenced by variables that affect the timing of surgery 10 . Guiroy et al. reported that in Latin America, surgical treatment for unstable thoracolumbar fractures was delayed beyond 72 hours in more than half of the cases, and exceeded one week in approximately one quarter of the sample 10 .

Decompression and reduction within 24 hours after trauma is recommended for patients with neurological deficits, especially those with incomplete deficit syndrome 11-15 . However, there is uncertainty regarding the rare cases of thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations where no neurological impairment is initially apparent. Fracture-dislocations of the thoracolumbar spine without neurological deficits make up less than 30% of all cases and have mostly been reported as isolated case reports or brief series in the literature 5 . In such cases, delaying surgery and evaluation based on the patient's clinical condition and setting appears necessary 5 , 16 . Surgical treatment often involves extensive procedures with multi-level instrumentation, risking significant morbidity, especially in patients with multiple traumatic injuries. This presents a dilemma: without neurological deficits, the timing must balance early stabilization benefits with the risks of major surgery.

We aim to compare early complication rates among surgically treated patients with thoracolumbar fracture-dislocation according to the presence of neurological damage.

Methods

This retrospective multicenter cohort study included patients who underwent surgical treatment for thoracolumbar fracture-dislocation at 24 institutions in 9 Latin American countries between 2015 and 2023. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The institutional review board approved the study protocol and waived the requirement for informed consent due to its retrospective design (29857420.0.1001.5342).

Institution Selection

An open call was made to recruit centers for the AO Spine Latin America spinal database. Eligible centers managed more than 20 vertebral trauma cases annually and included various settings such as public, private, and occupational health. Patient recruitment was retrospective during the study period, with data collected from medical records to document demographic, clinical, and surgical information.

Patient Selection

We included patients aged ≥ 16 years who suffered high-energy trauma that resulted in thoracolumbar vertebral fracture-dislocation, requiring surgical intervention. Patients with incomplete data, revision surgeries, or penetrating vertebral trauma were excluded. Thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations were defined according to the AOSpine Thoracolumbar Spine Injury Classification System. In this system, type C injuries are defined as those with displacement or translation of one vertebral body relative to another in any direction: anterior, posterior, lateral, or vertical 3 .

Clinical evaluation

The primary outcome measure was early in-hospital complications, documented between admission and hospital discharge or death. The number and type of complications were noted and classified into distinct subgroups for descriptive purposes, with a focus on early major clinical, neurological, and surgical complications 14 . Surgical complications were categorized according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. 14 .

Patients were grouped according to neurological injury to distinguish a rare subgroup of neurologically intact patients with thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations. This group was compared with patients who had any type of neurological injury to determine whether neurologically intact patients represent a different subgroup regarding the safety of early surgery. Neurological injury at admission was graded using the ASIA impairment scale 15 .

We also considered demographic and clinical data, type of trauma, injured segment, spinal fracture region, clinical instability on admission, delay between trauma and spinal surgery, number of instrumented vertebrae, and length of hospital stay.

Clinical instability on admission was defined as documented clinical conditions (such as severe thoracic or head trauma) or hemodynamic compromise (like hypovolemic shock) resulting from trauma-related injuries, which could justify the postponement of spinal surgery. We categorized the timing of surgery as occurring either within 24 hours or after 24 hours post-trauma. This cut-off was established based on current literature 19-21 .

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, applying the chi-square method or Fisher's test. To compare continuous variables, we used Student's t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. The description of numerical variables depended on their distribution, and their dispersion measures included mean, median, SD, and range. A binary logistic regression model was applied to investigate the relationship between predictive variables and complications. Considering variables that were statistically and/or clinically significant in the bivariate analysis, for multivariate comparison. Variable selection in the regression model followed a stepwise forward (conditional) method.

All statistical analyses were conducted using complete case analysis. Complete data were required for key exposure and outcome variables. For covariates, a missing data rate of up to 10% was deemed acceptable without the need for imputation. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

A total of 294 patients who suffered type C fractures were reviewed, of whom 32 were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in a final sample of 262 (Figure 1). Most were young men (n = 212; 80.6%) and the mean age was 36.8 years. Motor vehicle accidents were the main cause of injury in over half of the patients (n = 149; 56.7%), followed by falls from height (n = 93; 35.7%). The thoracic and thoracolumbar regions were the most affected (approximately 40% each). However, the lower lumbar spine accounted for less than one-tenth of the total injuries. Only 18% of the patients had no neurological damage (Figure 2); of those who did, over half had a complete spinal cord injury. We identified 268 complications among 173 (65.8%) patients (median = 1; range = 0-9). The most frequent clinical complications were urinary infections (n = 25; 9%) and pneumonia (n = 19; 7%). Regarding surgical complications, wound dehiscence occurred in 19 patients (7%), surgical infection in 15 (6%), and postoperative neurological deterioration in 10 (4%). Neurological deterioration was documented in 2 of the 46 patients who had no previous neurological damage. The early mortality rate was 4% (10 patients). Among patients treated surgically for spinal fracture-dislocations, we observed significant differences in complication rates based on the presence or absence of neurological damage at admission: those with neurological injury experienced more complications than those without. The distribution of postoperative complications varied significantly according to the severity of spinal cord injury, as classified by the ASIA Impairment Scale (p<0.001). Patients with complete injuries (Grade A) had the highest complication rate, with 121 out of 149 patients (69.9%) experiencing one or more complications, compared to 28 (31.1%) without complications. In bivariate analysis, other notable differences between groups included sex, the number of instrumented vertebrae, and length of hospital stay (Tables 3 and 4). The selection of variables for the multivariable model was guided not only by statistical significance in the bivariate analyses but also by clinical judgment and biological plausibility. In the multivariable logistic regression, the following variables were initially considered: age, gender, surgical delay, type of surgery, number of instrumented vertebrae, length of hospital stay, neurological status, number of comorbidities, injury mechanism, anatomical region, and clinical instability. Neurological injury was the strongest predictor of complications, increasing the odds more than sevenfold (OR=7.4; 95%CI=3.6-15.4; p<0.001). Clinical instability also showed an independent association, tripling the risk of complications (OR=3.2; 95%CI=1.6-6.4; p=0.001). Interestingly, the lumbar location appeared as a protective factor (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.99; p = 0.045), although the effect was modest and its confidence interval was close to unity. These findings indicate that neurological status and clinical instability are the primary determinants of postoperative complications, while regional differences may reflect anatomical or surgical factors that merit further investigation (Table 5).

Discussion

Type C thoracolumbar injuries can lead to significant morbidity among young adults. Often caused by high-energy trauma, these injuries may be accompanied by concurrent vertebral and non-vertebral injuries, resulting in substantial physiological and clinical consequences 16 , 17 . Recently, through logistic regression analysis, an observational retrospective study of records in the Japan Trauma Data Bank (2004-2019) identified 5 factors associated with type C thoracolumbar fractures (versus non-type C injuries): younger age, male sex, complete paralysis, bradycardia, and hypotension(16).

Severe spinal instability in type C thoracolumbar injuries has been associated with a higher likelihood of neurological damage, and the degree of preoperative spinal translation is a strong predictor of both the occurrence of complete spinal cord injury and the potential for postoperative neurological recovery(18). The extent of neurological impairment also influences surgical timing, although decision-making factors may vary by region(10–11). Recent evidence, including a meta-analysis by Daher et al. of 10 studies comprising 584 patients, suggests that minimally invasive approaches in thoracolumbar trauma may offer advantages such as reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stays, decreased early postoperative pain, and improved correction of regional kyphosis(22). Nevertheless, type C fractures frequently continue to be managed with extensive instrumentation and open surgical techniques, unlike thoracolumbar fractures without significant translation 23 , 24 . In this context, surgical timing should be considered not only according to neurological status but also in relation to the patient’s overall clinical condition in the setting of high-energy trauma. Consistent with these considerations, our series demonstrated that clinical instability was independently associated with a threefold increase in the risk of complications, while neurological impairment remained the strongest predictor. Both factors reached statistical significance in the multivariate model, highlighting their relevance in risk stratification for postoperative outcomes.

The specific subtype of neurologically intact patients with type C injuries has been described in the literature through case reports and small case series 25-32 . According to our knowledge, the most extensive case series was described by Kanna et al., including 36 patients with thoracolumbar fracture-dislocations with minimal (ASIA Impairment scale Grade D; n=16) or no spinal cord injury (Grade E; n=20)(5). They described critical steps to ensure safe surgical spine reduction without postoperative neurological worsening. Nevertheless, the descriptive nature of this case series could not measure the impact of neurological damage on the early clinical safety of surgically treating these mechanically unstable injuries. Moreover, these studies are limited by retrospective design, institutional selection bias, inter-center heterogeneity, and variability in management criteria which may compromise consistency and generalizability.

As previously stated, this is why thoracolumbar type C injuries with and without neurological damage should be compared to assess the relationship between neurological injury and early complication rates. In our study, after logistic regression analysis, the patients with neurological damage showed a significantly higher susceptibility to early complications. These patients exhibit a 7.4-fold increased risk of such complications compared to those without neurological damage. It is also noteworthy that postoperative neurological deterioration occurred in 2 (4.3%) patients who showed no evidence of neurological damage upon admission. These findings are consistent with well-established clinical concepts in the literature regarding the impact of neurological injury and the associated risks related to systemic, hemodynamic, and immunological consequences of spinal cord injury, as well as prolonged bed rest(19).

Our study has several limitations, primarily due to its retrospective nature and the critical confounding variables that could not be consistently measured, which further restricts causal inference. Additionally, our multicenter cohort was based on a non-probabilistic sample of institutions, introducing potential selection bias and limiting the representativeness of our findings for Latin America. There was uncontrolled heterogeneity among the centers, including differences in protocols, clinical practices, and patient populations, which may have influenced the outcomes. Moreover, institutions may also vary according to the availability of human and financial resources, as well as the type of institutional funding (public or private), which could bias the results for complication rates or length of hospital stay among thoracolumbar trauma patients. Taken together, these methodological limitations constrain the generalizability of our conclusions; however, the magnitude of the observed outcomes still provides valuable insight into thoracolumbar trauma management in the region. These findings emphasize the need for personalized decision-making in thoracolumbar trauma care, considering institutional differences and patient-specific factors. They may assist in developing decision algorithms for surgical timing, resource allocation, and risk stratification, although such tools require external validation before widespread implementation. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to address these limitations, better identify confounders, and validate findings across diverse populations.

Conclusions

In our cohort, patients with neurological damage showed 7.4 times higher odds to experience complications than those without it. Neurologically intact patients appear to be a challenging and clinically distinct group of patients and, according to our results, may benefit from individualized assessment to determine the safety of early surgical reduction and fixation. These findings require validation in prospective studies before definitive clinical recommendations.
