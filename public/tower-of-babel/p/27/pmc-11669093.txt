Inhibitor Development for α-Synuclein Fibril’s Disordered Region to Alleviate Parkinson’s Disease Pathology
Authors: Shenqing Zhang, Huaijiang Xiang, Youqi Tao, Juan Li, Shuyi Zeng, Qianhui Xu, Haonan Xiao, Shiran Lv, Caiwei Song, Yan Cheng, Martin Li, Zeyun Zhu, Shengnan Zhang, Bo Sun, Dan Li, ShengQi Xiang, Li Tan, Cong Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The amyloid fibrils of α-synuclein (α-syn) are crucial in the pathology of Parkinson’s disease (PD), with the intrinsically disordered region (IDR) of its C-terminal playing a key role in interacting with receptors like LAG3 and RAGE, facilitating pathological neuronal spread and inflammation. In this study, we identified Givinostat (GS) as an effective inhibitor that disrupts the interaction of α-syn fibrils with receptors such as LAG3 and RAGE through high-throughput screening. By exploring the structure–activity relationship and optimizing GS, we developed several lead compounds, including GSD-16-24. Utilizing solution-state and solid-state NMR, along with cryo-EM techniques, we demonstrated that GSD-16-24 binds directly to the C-terminal IDR of α-syn monomer and fibril, preventing the fibril from binding to the receptors. Furthermore, GSD-16-24 significantly inhibits the association of α-syn fibrils with membrane receptors, thereby reducing neuronal propagation and pro-inflammatory effects of α-syn fibrils. Our findings introduce a novel approach to mitigate the pathological effects of α-syn fibrils by targeting their IDR with small molecules, offering potential leads for the development of clinical drugs to treat PD.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) pathology is distinguished by the amyloid aggregation of α-synuclein (α-syn), the main component of both Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites.1−6 Compelling evidence indicates that α-syn fibrils, as opposed to their monomeric forms, are implicated in a range of pathological processes such as neuron-to-neuron transmission,7−9 the initiation of neuroinflammation,10 and the disruption of protein homeostasis.11,12 Crucially, various membrane receptors located on neurons and microglia have been identified as mediators in the myriad of pathological events attributed to α-syn fibrils.8,10,13−15 Conceivably, developing interventions to obstruct the interactions between α-syn fibrils and these receptors holds significant potential for therapeutic advancements aimed at curtailing α-syn-fibril-related pathologies in disease contexts.

α-Syn is characterized as an intrinsically disordered protein, composed of a N-terminal region, a central NAC region, and a C-terminal region.3,16 Upon fibrillation, the central NAC region alongside portions of the N-terminal region (which vary across different α-syn fibril polymorphs) transition into a β-sheet conformation, culminating in a cross-β spine that forms the core structure of the fibril.5,17−23 Conversely, the entire C-terminal region maintains its intrinsically disordered conformation, draping over the fibril core’s surface to create a dynamic, flexible coat present across different fibril polymorphs identified so far.20,22−25 Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) studies have shown that this C-terminal IDR retains a comparable flexible conformation in both the monomeric and fibrillar states.26 Significantly, research has highlighted the C-terminal IDR’s critical role in facilitating the binding of α-syn fibrils to various membrane receptors, such as lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG3) and amyloid precursor-like protein 1 (APLP1), key in cellular uptake for neuronal spread,8,9,14 and the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) on microglia, which triggers cytokine release in neuroinflammation.10,27 Therefore, small molecules that target the C-terminal IDR could be vital contenders for inhibiting α-syn fibril pathology. However, the inherent disorder of the C-terminal IDR poses significant challenges in identifying potent compounds that can effectively target this region.28−32

Given the pivotal role of α-syn aggregation in both the initiation and progression of PD pathologies, the development of chemical compounds such as small molecule inhibitors and PET tracers targeting α-syn fibrils has become a critical research focus for therapeutic intervention and early diagnosis.33−35 Numerous efforts in developing inhibitors have primarily targeted the prevention of monomeric α-syn transforming into mature cytotoxic fibrils, such as Curcumin36,37 and Fasudil,38,39 or the disassembly of mature fibrils, such as EGCG40,41 and CLR01.42,43 However, compounds that effectively inhibit the cell-to-cell transmission of α-syn fibrils remain scarce. Here, we propose focusing on the C-terminal IDR of α-syn fibrils, which also serves as an interaction site for cell membrane surface receptors, to identify inhibitors that can curtail the pathological activities of α-syn fibrils.

In our work, we established an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based high-throughput screening (HTS) assay to pinpoint small molecules that can prevent receptor binding of α-syn fibrils. Through this approach, we discovered Givinostat (GS) as a primary candidate. Subsequent investigations into the structure–activity relationships (SAR) yielded several optimized GS derivatives (GSDs), such as GSD-16-24, with enhanced inhibitory effects. Further mechanistic studies utilizing solution-state and solid-state NMR, alongside cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), elucidated the direct interaction between GSD-16-24 and the C-terminal IDR of α-syn, in both its monomeric and fibrillar forms. Subsequent cell-based assays confirmed that GSD-16-24 effectively blocked the binding of α-syn fibrils to primary cortical neurons and BV2 cells, thereby inhibiting their subsequent neuronal spread and inflammatory stimulation. These discoveries underscore the promising strategy of targeting the IDR region of α-syn fibrils for the development of inhibitory compounds with therapeutic potential in PD.

Results

Discovery of GS to Disrupt Receptor Binding of α-Syn Fibril by HTS

To pinpoint small molecules capable of inhibiting the binding of α-syn fibrils to their receptors, we developed a HTS protocol utilizing a LAG3-based ELISA (Figure 1a). This assay began with the purification of the LAG3 D1 domain, previously established as a key region for α-syn fibrils’ interaction,9 which was then coated onto the ELISA plates. Small molecules from our chemical library were introduced alongside α-syn preformed fibrils (PFFs) and incubated altogether. The effectiveness of a compound in this assay was determined by its ability to compete with LAG3 D1 in binding to α-syn PFFs, which could be quantitatively measured through a colorimetric reaction based on α-syn-specific antibodies. This HTS assay allowed us to sift through over a thousand small molecules with reported bioactivity, leading to the identification of Givinostat (GS), an HDAC inhibitor approved by FDA,44 as a standout compound that virtually eliminated the binding of α-syn PFFs to LAG3 D1 (Figure 1b, c).

To validate this finding, we titrated GS for its inhibitory potency on the interaction between α-syn fibrils and LAG3 D1, and determined a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 8.990 μM (Figure 1d). Furthermore, considering the shared binding regions of α-syn fibrils on the C-terminal IDR for another receptor, RAGE,10 we also explored GS’s ability to interfere with the α-syn-fibril-RAGE interaction. We purified the V domain of RAGE (vRAGE), which is known to mediate the binding between α-syn fibrils and RAGE, and used it to substitute for LAG3 D1 coated on the plate. Impressively, GS also significantly inhibited the α-syn fibrils’ binding to vRAGE, with a slightly more potent IC50 value of 5.433 μM compared to that of LAG3 D1 (Figure 1e). To further confirm the binding target of GS, we determined the binding affinity of GS to α-syn PFFs, LAG3 D1, and vRAGE by using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), revealing that GS bind to α-syn-PFF, but not the two receptors or α-syn-monomer, with a dissociation constant (KD) of 11.58 μM (Figure 1f–h, Figure S1).

Rational Design of GS Derivatives with Enhanced Inhibitory Activity

Following the discovery of GS as a potential inhibitor of α-syn fibril binding to LAG3 D1 and vRAGE, we embarked on a structure–activity relationship (SAR) study aiming to optimize GS for stronger and more specific inhibitory efficiency against these interactions. GS is known for its robust inhibition of class I/II histone deacetylase (HDAC) and is undergoing phase I/II clinical trials for the treatment of solid/hematological cancers.45,46 Its structure predominantly consists of two components:1 a zinc-binding warhead, characterized by hydroxamic acid groups, and2 a selectivity-enhancing group (Figure 1c). Our goal was to mitigate the toxic side effects associated with hydroxamic acid groups, while also enhancing the selectivity and effectiveness of GS against the binding of α-syn fibrils to the two receptors. This led us to systematically dissect and optimize GS’s synthetic route, refining each structural element from part E to part A (Figure 2a). The efficacy of these modified GS derivatives was then assessed using both LAG3 D1-based and vRAGE-based assays (Figure 2b–d, Figure S2).

Initially, we experimented with replacing the hydroxamic acid groups in part E with alternative functional groups, which resulted in a loss of activity, underscoring the critical role of hydroxamic acid groups. Modifications such as methylation of these groups led to reduced activity. Adjustments were then made to the positioning of the hydroxamic acid groups, with analogues in the meta-position displaying similar activity levels but reduced toxicity to neuronal cells. The introduction of electron-donating/withdrawing groups and other typical substitutions on part D did not significantly alter the activity. Further modifications on part C revealed that replacing the carbamate group with a carbamido linker maintained equivalent activity and offered greater chemical stability. Importantly, subsequent structural refinements on parts B and A led to the identification of several highly effective inhibitors of α-syn fibrils. In the optimization, several key molecules were distinctly marked in the screening outcomes (Figure 2b, c). Specifically, GSD-1-10 and GSD-15-100 were eliminated due to their insolubility in aqueous solutions, which led to false positive results. Subsequent validations were performed on these optimized GSD candidates through titrations to quantify their inhibitory activities. The data indicated that GSD-15-82, GSD-16-24, and GSD-7-33 each showed an increase in activity exceeding 5-fold in both the LAG3 and RAGE assays (Figure 2d).

Before proceeding to the subsequent NMR-based assays, the candidates were assessed for their solubility in polar solvents. It was found that GSD-15-82 and GSD-7-33 lacked the necessary solubility for NMR analysis (Table S1). In contrast, GSD-16-24 were identified as the most optimal GS derivatives, exhibiting both high activity and solubility (Figure 2e).

GS and GSD-16-24 Interact with the C-Terminal IDR of α-Syn Monomer

To elucidate the mechanisms through which GS and most effective GSDs, particularly GSD-16-24, impede the α-syn-receptor interaction, we embarked on solution-state NMR spectroscopy studies. These involved the titration of 15N-labeled α-syn monomers with GS and GSD-16-24. Analysis through two-dimensional (2D) 1H–15N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) spectra exposed significant reductions in signal intensity (I/I0 < 0.7) within residues located in the C-terminal IDR of α-syn upon addition of GS (Figure 3a, c, e). In contrast, titration with GSD-16-24 induced changes in signal intensity that were similar in nature yet markedly more pronounced, along with pronounced CSDs (>0.015 ppm) at the C-terminal IDR of α-syn, indicating that GSD-16-24 binds to α-syn in a manner akin to GS but with significantly greater affinity (Figure 3b, d, f, Figure S3). This observation aligns with the outcomes of our binding competition assays, leading us to concentrate on GSD-16-24 in subsequent analyses.

Notably, the specific residues involved in binding by GSD-16-24, including acidic residues (D115, D119, D121, E123, E126, E130, and E137) and hydrophobic residues (L113, V118, A124, Y125, Y136, and A140), show a high degree of overlap with those identified as crucial for interaction with both LAG3 D1 and vRAGE (Figure 3g). To gain a deeper understanding of the interactions between GSD-16-24 and the C-terminal IDR of α-syn, we conducted molecular dynamic simulations based on the full-length α-syn monomer, as calculated using AlphaFold 2,39,47,48 and assessed the potential hydrophobic contacts, salt bridges, aromatic π-stacking interactions, and hydrogen bonds between the compound and each residue of α-syn in predicted binding modes (Figures S4 and S5). The simulation results indicate that GSD-16-24 has a higher affinity for residues 100–140. The most prominent interactions were hydrogen bonds between GSD-16-24 and the residues E123, Y125, E126, M127, E130, and E139, with M127 forming hydrogen bonds through the nitrogen on its amide group. Hydrophobic contacts, such as aromatic π-stacking and van der Waals interactions, were predicted for V118, P120, A124, Y125, M127, Y136, and A140. These predictions align well with the results from NMR experiments, corroborating that GSD-16-24 interacts with the C-terminal IDR of the α-syn monomer. Considering that the C-terminal IDR retains a highly similar disordered conformation in both monomeric and fibrillar states of α-syn,26 it is reasonable to predict that GSD-16-24 primarily targets this corresponding C-terminal IDR segment in the fibrillar form of α-syn.

GSD-16-24 Predominately Binds to the C-Terminal IDR of α-Syn Fibril

We next sought to elucidate the interaction between GSD-16-24 and α-syn fibrils by employing the insensitive nuclei enhanced by polarization transfer (INEPT)-based ssNMR and cryo-EM techniques. Initially, the binding affinity of GSD-16-24 to α-syn-PFF was measured using ITC, revealing a KD of 3.48 μM (Figure 4a). No significant interaction signals were detected between GSD-16-24 andα-syn monomer, α-syn1–100 PFF (deletion of the C-terminal 101–140 aa), LAG3 D1, or vRAGE (Figure 4b, Figure S6).

Given that residues 40–100 of α-syn undergo substantial conformational transition from an IDR in the monomer to a β-sheet structure forming the rigid core in the fibril,17,18,20,22 while the N-terminal and C-terminal IDR maintain consistent configurations in both monomeric and fibrillar states,26 we investigated the potential binding of GSD-16-24 to the rigid core of α-syn fibril via cryo-EM. GSD-16-24 was incubated with mature α-syn fibrils at a 50:1 molar ratio for 1 h before cryo-EM data collection. Cryo-EM structural analysis revealed no discernible additional electron density adjacent to the fibril core in the complex (Figure 4c, Table S2), excluding direct interactions between GSD-16-24 and the core of α-syn fibrils. In addition, we confirmed that 2% DMSO alone which was used as solvent of GSD-16-24 has no effect on the morphology and cryo-EM structure of α-syn fibril (Figure S7, Table S2).

Subsequently, we prepared a 13C and 15N-double labeled α-syn fibril sample for INEPT-based ssNMR analysis. After incubating GSD-16-24 with α-syn fibrils at varying molar ratios and centrifuging to collect the pellets, we acquired 2D HSQC spectra of GSD-16-24-bound α-syn fibrils and compared them to those of unbound fibrils. As expected, only residues from the flexible regions outside the rigid core were detected in the spectra (Figure 4d, e), with their chemical shifts closely aligning with those in the monomeric form, indicating similar conformations in both states.

Remarkably, increasing concentrations of GSD-16-24 led to a significant reduction in signal intensity from most residues, although CSDs remained minor (Figure 4e, Figure S8). Detailed analysis of intensity changes highlighted a marked decrease in signal intensity for the C-terminal IDR residues, especially E126 and M127, which saw a reduction to about 20% at a 1:1 molar ratio of α-syn to GSD-16-24. At a 1:5 ratio, the intensity for these C-terminal IDR residues dropped dramatically to roughly 30% compared to the apo condition, with specific residues such as E114, N122, E126, and M127 showing a decrease to less than 20%. Escalating the GSD-16-24 concentration further to a 1:10 molar ratio saw the intensity for the C-terminal IDR residues plummet to about 15% of the apo condition, with residues E114, N122, E126, M127, S129, E130, E131, G132, E139, and A140 experiencing a significant decline to less than 10%. The N-terminal region also displayed a notable intensity reduction, albeit to a lesser extent than the C-terminal region. Together with solution-state NMR HSQC titration results, these findings suggest that GSD-16-24 predominantly targets the C-terminal IDR of α-syn fibrils, leading to substantial signal intensity reduction, likely due to chemical exchange at the interface upon compound binding. Such interactions could indirectly influence the chemical exchange rate of residues within the N-terminal IDR.

GSD-16-24 Prevents α-Syn-Fibril-Induced Neuronal Propagation and Inflammation

Given that GSD-16-24 directly interacts with the C-terminal IDR of α-syn fibrils, blocking their association with LAG3 and RAGE–key receptors in mediating neuroinflammation and propagation of α-syn fibrils,9,10 we further explored its ability to mitigate neuroinflammatory responses and halt neuronal spread induced by α-syn fibrils. To evaluate the impact of α-syn fibrils on inducing inflammation, we utilized microglia-like BV2 cell lines known to release cytokines upon activation through the interaction between α-syn fibrils and the RAGE receptor.10 We conducted two previously established assays: a cell surface binding assay, and an inflammatory cytokine secretion assay10 (Figure 5a, d).

In the cell surface binding assay, we introduced GSD-16-24 and α-syn PFFs into the culture medium and maintained the conditions for 2 h at 25 °C to minimize BV2 cellular internalization of the PFFs. Subsequent staining of the fixed BV2 cells with α-syn and DAPI indicators demonstrated that GSD-16-24 dose-dependently inhibited the attachment of α-syn PFFs to the BV2 cell membrane (Figure 5b, c). In the inflammatory cytokine secretion assay, after treating BV2 cells with GSD-16-24 and α-syn PFFs for 7 h at 37 °C, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analyses of mRNA expressions for TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 indicated a significant, dose-dependent reduction in the levels of these inflammatory cytokines elicited by exogenous α-syn PFFs in cell cotreated with GSD-16-24 (Figure 5e–g). These findings confirm GSD-16-24’s effectiveness in preventing α-syn fibril binding to BV2 membranes and the subsequent induction of inflammation.

Meanwhile, to assess GSD-16-24’s ability to the neuronal propagation of α-syn fibrils, we conducted a neuron surface binding assay followed by a well-established neuronal propagation assay9,49 (Figure 6a, d). In the binding assay, GSD-16-24 and α-syn PFFs were again added to the culture medium under conditions designed to minimize PFF uptake by neurons. Staining of fixed neurons with antibodies against α-syn and MAP2, a mature neuron marker as a reference signal for α-syn, revealed a dose-dependent reduction in the cell surface-associated α-syn PFF signal. The results demonstrated that GSD-16-24 reduced the binding of α-syn PFF to neuron membranes. (Figure 6b, c). In the neuron propagation assay, extending the treatment with GSD-16-24 and α-syn PFFs over 14 days at 37 °C and staining for MAP2 and pS129 α-syn, a marker for pathological α-syn aggregates. The results showed a dose-dependent reduction in the intracellular α-syn-pS129 signal, demonstrating that GSD-16-24 significantly curtailed the formation of endogenous α-syn aggregates in primary cortical neurons triggered by exogenous PFFs in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6e, f). Owing to extended incubation periods, the highest concentration of GSD-16-24 demonstrated cytotoxicity, precluding the conduct of statistical data analysis.

Collectively, our results underscore GSD-16-24’s potent inhibitory action against the binding of α-syn fibrils to multiple receptors, effectively dampening both neuroinflammation and neuronal propagation induced by α-syn fibrils.

Discussion

Pathological α-syn amyloid fibrils have become crucial targets for therapeutic strategies in PD.50−52 Traditionally, the development of α-syn inhibitors has concentrated on preventing the formation of pathological fibrils and breaking down existing ones.53 For example, the HTS assay identified Anle138b, which has progressed to clinical research stages, as it primarily inhibits α-syn fibrillation.54,55 Similarly, EGCG, a natural polyphenolic compound, inhibits α-syn fibrillation by directly interacting with the intact α-syn monomer and converting large mature fibrils into smaller amorphous protein aggregates.40,41 Fasudil, a synthetically repositioned drug, prevents the pathological transition to fibrils by binding to tyrosine within the α-syn monomer’s C-terminal IDR.38,39 However, our work is distinct from previous studies as we focused on developing a targeted HTS assay to identify small molecules that competitively inhibit the interaction between receptors and α-syn fibrils. We screened a library of known bioactive compounds and discovered that GS effectively disrupted the binding of α-syn PFFs to LAG3 D1 (Figure 1b, c). Interestingly, although Fasudil targets the C-terminal IDR of the α-syn monomer, it did not emerge as a positive hit in our fibril-receptor assay (Figure 1b, c), suggesting that Fasudil’s binding site within the C-terminal IDR is different from the receptor binding site.

Following an intensive SAR study on GS, we developed over 100 GSDs, several of which exhibited 5- to 16-fold increased inhibitory activity. Attempts to modify the hydroxamic acid group of GS to reduce its activity against HDACs proved invalid, as this group is also crucial for affinity to α-syn. This indicates that the activity of GSDs relies on their interactions with zinc atoms chelated by acidic residues in the α-syn C-terminal IDR, similar to GS’s interaction with zinc atoms in the catalytic site of HDACs. Both ssNMR results and predicted binding modes suggest that the hydroxamic acid group directly interacts with acidic residues of the α-syn C-terminal IDR, such as E126 and E130. However, switching the hydroxamic acid group from the para- to the meta-position and modifying other functional groups significantly reduced the cytotoxicity of GSDs. Based on potency, solubility, and cytotoxicity, GSD-16-24 was considered the most suitable lead for further investigation.

Results from NMR and cryo-EM further revealed that GSD-16-24 bind directly to the C-terminal IDR of α-syn, coinciding with the receptor binding sites. This robust binding mechanism presents a feature not possessed by previous inhibitors and provides valuable references for future drug development targeting IDRs.39 Furthermore, GSD-16-24 obstructs the association between α-syn fibrils and membrane receptors in both microglia-like cells and neurons, substantially reducing neuronal propagation and inflammatory responses associated with α-syn fibrils. In addition, we found that GSD-16-24 inhibits the aggregation of α-syn monomers both in the presence and absence of α-syn seeds (Figure S9), implying the potential of GSD-16-24 to both inhibit α-syn fibrillation and prevent the pathology of preformed α-syn fibril. Nonetheless, further study is needed to explore the molecular mechanism underlying the inhibition of GSD-16-24 in α-syn fibrillation.

Despite these promising findings, our study has limitations. To advance GSD-16-24 as a viable preclinical drug candidate, further optimization is needed to enhance its efficacy and mitigate toxic effects associated with prolonged high-dose administration. Additionally, receptor-mediated pathways may not be the exclusive mechanisms for α-syn fibril internalization; other routes, including endocytosis post-HSPG recruitment,56,57 vesicular transport,58,59 and intercellular transfer through tunneling nanotubes,60,61 may require specific compounds to interfere with. Moreover, the inhibitors reported in this study need to be assessed and optimized, if necessary, for their central nervous system (CNS)-penetrating properties before applications in animal models.

In summary, our findings introduce a new series of chemical modalities with proof-of-concept compounds such as GSD-16-24, demonstrating the potential of targeting the C-terminal IDR of α-syn fibrils for therapeutic intervention. This study offers valuable perspectives on the development of targeted inhibitors for IDRs, paving the way for novel therapeutic strategies in PD.

Method

Commercial Chemical Compounds

Givinostat (GS, CAS No. 732302–99–7) was purchased from MedChemExpress. Fasudil (CAS No. 103745-39-7) was purchased from TargetMol. The compounds were dissolved in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare 100 mM stock solutions.

Chemical Synthesis of Chemical Compounds

GS derivatives (GSDs) were synthesized following the synthetic routes described in the Supplementary Note. The compounds were dissolved in anhydrous DMSO to prepare 50 mM or 100 mM stock solutions.

Preparation of Recombinant Proteins

The construction methods for the pET-22b-α-syn, pET-22b-FLAG-α-syn, pET-22b-α-syn1–100, pET-28a-L3D1, and pET-28a-vRAGE vectors were the same as previously described.9,10 In brief, human α-syn full-length, FLAG-α-syn, α-syn1–100 (residues 1–100 of human α-syn), L3D1 (residues 23–167, excluding residues 74–92, of mouse LAG3), and vRAGE (residues 23–125 of human RAGE) were inserted into the corresponding vectors. Plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Cells were grown to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of around 0.6–0.8 at 37 °C, and then induced with 1 mM isopropyl-1-thio-D-galactopyranoside (IPTG) for 12 h at 25 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in buffer.

For α-syn proteins (full-length α-syn, FLAG-α-syn, and α-syn1–100) purification, the collected cells were lysed in a buffer containing 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride using a high-pressure cell crusher. The lysate was boiled for 10 min and centrifuged at 25,000g and 4 °C for 30 min. To remove nucleic acids, 20 mg/mL of streptomycin was added to the supernatant and stirred for 30 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, the pH of the supernatant was adjusted to 3.5, centrifuged, and then dialyzed to 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) at 4 °C overnight.

The protein samples were loaded onto an anion exchange column (cation exchange column for α-syn1–100) and eluted with a gradient of NaCl. Fractions containing the target proteins were pooled and concentrated using 3 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) centrifugal filters. The samples were centrifuged at 4,000g for 10–20 min at 4 °C, and the flow-through was discarded. This step was repeated until the desired concentration was achieved. The concentrated protein was then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a Hiload 16/600 Superdex 75 pg column pre-equilibrated with a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) and 150 mM KCl. The purity of the target protein samples was verified by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The purified proteins were concentrated, filtered, aliquoted, flash-frozen, and stored at −80 °C. For 15N- or 15N/13C-labeled α-syn protein, cells were grown in M9 minimal medium with 15NH4Cl (1 g/L) in the absence or presence of 13C6-glucose (4 g/L), respectively. The purification was performed as described above.

For receptors purification, the collected cells were lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 500 mM NaCl using a high-pressure cell crusher. The lysate was centrifuged at 25,000g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed with buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 0.5% Triton X-100, followed by an additional wash with buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 1 M NaCl. The pellets were then dissolved in buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, and 6 M guanidine hydrochloride.

The protein solution was loaded onto a Ni-NTA column and eluted with the buffer containing 50 mM Na2HPO4–NaH2PO4 (pH 4.0), 50 mM NaCl, and 6 M guanidine hydrochloride. The protein fraction was further concentrated and refolded using a three-step dialysis process at 4 °C. Then, the dialysate was filtered and concentrated for a further purification by using a Hiload 16/600 Superdex 75 pg column pre-equilibrated with a buffer containing 50 mM Na2HPO4–NaH2PO4 (pH 6.5) and 50 mM NaCl. Fractions containing target protein were concentrated, filtered, aliquoted, flash-frozen, and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation of α-Syn Fibrils

α-Syn monomers were diluted to 400 μM in fibril growth buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, and 0.02% NaN3. The protein sample was incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 900 rpm in a ThermoMixer C for 1 week. Then, the α-syn fibril seeds were obtained by sonicating a 500 μL sample on ice for 30 s (1 s on, 1 s off) at 20% power using a ϕ2 probe on a JY92-IIN sonicator (Xinyi Sonication Equipment Company, Ningbo, China) and added to the unlabeled or 15N/13C-labeled α-syn monomer. The mixture was further incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 900 rpm for 5 days to obtain mature fibrils for ssNMR and cryo-EM studies.

For HTS, ITC, and cellular assay, the unlabeled fibril samples were centrifuged at 20,000g for 1 h at 25 °C. The quantity of fibrils in the pellets was calculated as the amount of total monomer minus the amount of protein in the supernatant, as measured by bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA assay). The pellet was then washed twice and resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 200 μM. α-Syn preformed fibrils (PFFs) were prepared by sonicating the resuspended fibrils on ice for 50 s (1 s on, 1 s off, 20% amplitude by a sonicator). The PFFs for cellular assays required sterile handling and were sterilized by UV irradiation before use.

ELISA-Based High-Throughput Screening

High-binding 384-well ELISA plates were coated with 100 μL of L3D1 or vRAGE at a concentration of 3 μg/mL in PBS at 4 °C overnight. After blocking with 5% milk in PBS and 0.05% Tween-20, a compound library with drugs at a final concentration of 20 μM (0.2% (v/v) DMSO) was added to each well using an Echo 525 Acoustic Liquid Handler (Beckman Coulter) and mixed with 100 nM of sonicated FLAG-α-syn PFFs in PBS. The adsorbed FLAG-α-syn PFFs were then quantified using a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-FLAG antibody (abcam, ab49763), followed by coloration with the TMB substrate reagent. If a compound inhibits the interaction between α-syn PFF and receptors, the unbound PFFs are washed out in the subsequent steps, resulting in attenuated coloration of the corresponding well. For the structure–activity relationship (SAR) study, GS and GSDs were screened using a similar ELISA assay conducted in high-binding 96-well plates.

Hit compounds were further validated using an ELISA assay conducted in high-binding 96-well plates. Multiple concentrations of the compounds (0.00256–200 μM, 5× serial dilution) were tested to inhibit the binding of α-syn PFFs to receptors. ELISA absorbance values at 450 nm (A450) were plotted against the concentration of hit compounds, and the inhibitory dose–response curves were fitted by nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism to acquire IC50 values.

Binding Affinity Measurement by ITC

The binding properties of GS and GSD-16-24 to α-syn, L3D1, and vRAGE were measured using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) with a MicroCal ITC200 calorimeter (Malvern) at 25 °C. The proteins and compounds were prepared to the desired concentrations and volumes in PBS buffer (proteins at 50 μM in 200 μL, compounds at 500 μM in 40 μL) before loading. Titrations involved 20 injections of 2 μL of ligand into 200 μL of protein solution, with a stirring speed of 1,000 rpm. As the titration progressed, the stoichiometric ratios of the compound to protein ranged from 0:1 to 2:1. Dissociation constants and thermodynamic parameters were determined using the embedded Origin7 software package (Microcal).

Solubility Measurement

10 mg of each compound were suspended in 100 μL of pure water. After initial mixing by brief vortexing, the samples were equilibrated in a shaking incubator for 24 h at room temperature (130 rpm). The suspensions were subsequently filtered through 0.22 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane filters. The solubility was calculated based on the peak area of the filtrate at a UV absorbance of 254 nm in liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), with reference to the standard calibration curve prepared from a 50 mM DMSO stock.

Solution NMR Spectroscopy

All NMR experiments were performed at 298 K in a buffer containing 50 mM Na2HPO4–NaH2PO4 (pH 7.0), 50 mM NaCl, and 10% (v/v) D2O. Titration experiments were collected on a Bruker 900 MHz spectrometer. The backbone assignment of α-syn monomer in solution was accomplished according to our previous publications,26,62 referencing the assignment from BMRB ID 16300 and further confirmed by the collected 3D HNCACB and CBCACONH spectra. 20 μM 15N-α-syn monomer was titrated with GS and GSD-16-24 at the indicated concentrations, respectively. The chemical shift deviations (CSD) were calculated using the empirical equation ΔCSD = [ΔHN2 + 0.0289(ΔN2)]1/2, where ΔHN and ΔN represent the CSDs of amide proton (HN) and nitrogen (N), respectively. The dashed lines indicate the residues with CSD > 0.003 ppm and CSD > 0.015 ppm, which are chosen based on the commonly employed approach that involves using the standard deviation of CSDs across all residues.63,64 All NMR data were analyzed with NMRPipe65 and Sparky.66

Scoring criteria: For data of α-Syn:L3D1 = 1:4 and α-Syn:GS or GSD-16-24 = 1:20, CSD > 0.01 is scored as 1, CSD > 0.02 is scored as 2, CSD > 0.03 is scored as 3, and CSD > 0.04 is scored as 4. For data of α-Syn:vRAGE = 1:2, CSD > 0.005 is scored as 1, CSD > 0.01 is scored as 2, CSD > 0.015 is scored as 3, and CSD > 0.02 is scored as 4. Data of L3D1 or vRAGE from previous studies.9,10

Molecular Dynamic Simulation

Molecular docking of the ligand was performed using Schrödinger2021 with full-length α-syn, calculated by AlphaFold-2.67,68 Ligands were designed and synthesized by the Tan Lab, with 3D structures imported from ChemBio3D into Maestro 12.8. Ligands were prepared using the LigPrep Wizard with default parameters, predicting ionization and conformation states at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 using Epik and the OPLS4 force field. Chiralities were determined by their original 3D structural data. Protein structures were downloaded from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database and prepared by adding hydrogens and optimizing at pH 7.0 ± 2.0. Receptor grids were generated with Ala30, Ala90, and Met127 as centroids for the N-terminal IDR, fibril core, and C-terminal IDR, respectively, using 36 Å docking boxes. Docking employed extra precision scoring functions and flexible ligand mode, outputting five poses per ligand.

For molecular dynamics simulations, systems were built with GPU/Desmond in the SPC model, using an orthorhombic box at 300 K and 150 mM NaCl buffer, with a 20 Å buffer from the protein–ligand complex. Overlapping solvent molecules were removed, resulting in systems of ∼209,000 atoms. Systems were relaxed using a five-step process in the NPT ensemble. Simulations were conducted in the NPT ensemble with the OPLS4 force field, using a 100 ps time step and 100 ns total simulation time. Hydrogen bonds were identified with a 2.8 Å distance cutoff. Charge contacts were defined within 5 Å, hydrophobic contacts within 2.5 Å, and halogen bonds within 3.5 Å.

Cryo-EM Data Collection

3 μM α-syn fibrils were incubated with 150 μM compounds in a final DMSO concentration of 1.5% at 25 °C for 1 h. Another sample of 3 μM α-syn fibrils was incubated with DMSO alone at a final concentration of 2% at 25 °C for 1 h. Additionally, an apo α-syn fibril sample was prepared without any DMSO. Four μL of each sample was then applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grid (Quantifoil R2/1, 300 mesh). The grids were then blotted for 0.5 s with filter paper using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI) at 16 °C and 100% humidity before being plunge-frozen into liquid ethane.

Micrographs were acquired using a Krios G4 cryo transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV with a BioContinuum K3 direct detector (Gatan) in super-resolution mode. A GIF Quantum energy filter (Gatan) with a slit width of 20 eV was used to remove inelastically scattered electrons. Automated data collection was performed using EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, 40 dose-fractioned frame videos per micrograph were recorded at a calibrated magnification of ×105,000, resulting in a pixel size of 0.83 Å pixel–1. The total dose was approximately 55 e– Å–2 for a total exposure time of 2.0 s. Defocus values ranged from −1.0 μm to −2.0 μm.

Image Preprocessing and Helical Reconstruction

MotionCorr2 for motion correction implement was carried out to correct beam-induced motion of movie frames with dose-weighting.69 Then, CTFFIND-4.1.8 was used to estimate the contrast transfer function of the motion-corrected images.70 Next, fibrils were manually picked by using the manual picking method of RELION version 3.1.71

Helical reconstruction included particle extraction, two-dimensional (2D) classification, 3D classification, 3D autorefinement and postprocessing. Micrographs were extracted into segments with a box-size of 360 pixels. Several iterations of 2D classification were then applied to the extracted segments, with the in-plane angular sampling rate decreasing from 10° to 0.5° and a T = 2 regularization parameter. A cylindrical map model generated by relion_helix_toolbox program was used as the initial 3D reference for the subsequent 3D classification (K = 3). When the local search of symmetry for helical twist and rise was carried out after the separation of β-strands, the clearest class was selected for 3D autorefinement. Finally, the overall resolution was reported according to the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143 criteria.

Atomic Model Building and Refinement

Three-layer models of α-syn fibrils were based on the structure of α-syn fibril (PDB accession no. 6A6B) and manually adjusted in WinCoot v.0.8.9.2,72 followed by refinement against the corresponding map by the real-space refinement program in PHENIX v.1.15.73 Figures of atomic models were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX.74,75

Solid-State NMR Spectroscopy

The 15N/13C-labeled α-syn fibril was first spun down by ultracentrifuge (Beckman) at 150, 000 g for 120 min. The gel-like pellet was then transferred to a 1.3 mm rotor using a pipet tip and a benchtop centrifuge. The pipet tip was temporarily trimmed to ensure it matched the 1.3 mm rotor. For the samples binding GSD-16-24, GSD-16-24 was first dissolved in DMSO, and then GSD-16-24 solutions were mixed with preformed fibrils with deserved molar ratios before spun down.

The 1H-detected ssNMR experiments of 15N/13C-labeled α-syn fibril samples were conducted at 60 kHz MAS on a wide bore 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 1.3 mm HXY MAS probe (Bruker Biospin). 2D HSQC were implemented as their liquid-state counter partners with the MISSISSIPPI water suppression modules. All NMR data were processed using NMRpipe65 and analyzed with Sparky.66 The assignment of the flexible terminal regions of α-syn fibrils was achieved in accordance with our previous studies,26 with reference to the assignment data from BMRB ID 51559. The error of I/I0 was calculated according to Gauss’ error propagation, in the same way as in the previous publication.76

Cell Culture

BV2 cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, 11995065) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 10099-141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P.S.) in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator at 37 °C.

For primary neurons, the 24-well plates were precoated with poly-l-lysine (0.2 mg/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C and then washed three times with water. Primary neuronal cultures were prepared from Embryonic day (E) 16 to E18 Sprague–Dawley rat (Shanghai SIPPR BK Laboratory Animals Ltd.) embryos. Neurobasal medium supplemented with B-27, GlutaMAX, P.S. (all from Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) was used for the cultures. All animal experiments were performed according to the protocols approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Interdisciplinary Research Center on Biology and Chemistry (IRCBC), Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS).

Cell Surface Binding Assay

Following the previously established cell surface binding assay,9,10 BV2 cells or primary cortical neurons were treated with PBS, 200 nM α-syn monomer, 200 nM α-syn PFFs, and 200 nM α-syn PFFs premixed with compounds for 2 h at 25 °C. The cells were then collected, washed, and fixed for immunostaining (IF). Primary antibodies used were anti-α-syn (Abcam, ab138501) and anti-MAP2 (Abcam, ab5392). Secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat antichicken (ThermoFisher Scientific, A11039) and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat antirabbit (ThermoFisher Scientific, A11036). Fluorescent images were scanned using a SP8 confocal microscope. The fluorescent gray values of α-syn and MAP2 or DAPI signals were measured using ImageJ.

Inflammatory Cytokine Secretion Assay

Following the previously established assay,10 BV2 cells were treated with PBS, 100 ng/mL LPS, 5 μM α-syn monomers, 5 μM α-syn PFFs, and 5 μM α-syn PFFs premixed with compounds for 7 h at 37 °C. The cells were then collected, and RNA extraction was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions (ZYMO, R1055). Next, reverse transcription was carried out using a kit (Vazyme, R333-01), and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bimake, B21202) with the Step One Plus PCR System (ThermoFisher). The PCR conditions for each cycle were as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 1 min, for a total of 40 cycles. The expression levels of β-actin, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were examined. The fold change of gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the reference gene for normalization. Data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism.

Neuronal Propagation Assay

Primary cortical neurons were treated with PBS, 200 nM α-syn monomer, 200 nM α-syn PFFs, and 200 nM α-syn PFFs premixed with compounds for 14 days at 37 °C. The neurons were then collected and fixed for immunostaining (IF). Primary antibodies used were anti-α-syn-pS129 (Abcam, ab51253) and anti-MAP2 (Abcam, ab5392). The secondary antibodies used were the same as those in the “Cell surface binding assay”. Fluorescent images were scanned using a SP8 confocal microscope. The fluorescent gray values of α-syn-pS129 and MAP2 signals were measured using ImageJ.

ThT Fluorescence Assay

A ThT fluorescence assay was conducted with 50 μM α-syn monomer incubated with varying concentrations of GSD-16-24, in the absence or presence of seeds. The reaction buffer comprised 50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM KCl, pH 7.5, 0.02% NaN3, and 10 μM ThT. Reactions were performed in a black 384-well optical bottom plate (Thermo Scientific, 142761), with orbital shaking at 37 °C and 700 rpm. ThT fluorescence signals were monitored using a BMG FLUOstar Omega plate reader, with excitation at 440 nm and emission at 485 nm. Data were analyzed, processed, and graphed using GraphPad Prism.
