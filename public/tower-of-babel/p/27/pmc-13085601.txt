Genome-wide identification of maize G protein genes and regulatory roles of the ZmGG1 subfamily in saline-alkali stress response
Authors: Li-quan Sun, Chun-xiao Zhang, Jing Dong, Wei Huang, Feng-xue Jin, Cheng-yang Li, Wen-qian Zhou, Wei-lin Wu, Xiao-hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaline-alkaline stress is a major constraint in maize (Zea mays L.) production. Heterotrimeric G proteins play pivotal roles in plant stress responses. However, their functional mechanisms in maize under saline-alkaline stress remain poorly understood. This study aimed to characterize the maize G protein gene family and elucidate its regulatory mechanisms in response to saline-alkaline stress. Ten G protein genes (belonging to the Gα, Gβ, and Gγ subunit families) were identified from the maize genome using bioinformatics approaches. Their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, promoter cis-acting elements, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were systematically analyzed. Gene expression patterns in the saline-alkaline tolerant inbred line Zheng58 and the sensitive line Chang7-2 were investigated using qRT-PCR, alongside the determination of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging enzyme activities. The Gγ subfamily members (ZmGG1, ZmGG3, and ZmGG5) exhibited differential expression patterns. PPI network analysis revealed extensive interactions between G proteins and signaling components such as phospholipase C and MAP kinases. Under stress conditions, Chang7-2 displayed significant fluctuations in ROS-scavenging enzyme activity and accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA). Therefore, this study elucidated the mechanism by which maize G proteins coordinate saline-alkaline stress responses through subfamily-specific expression regulation and intricate signaling networks, thereby providing potential candidate target genes for breeding stress-tolerant maize.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08509-7.Keywords: Maize, G protein gene family, ZmGG1 subfamily, Salt-alkali stress, Expression analysis, Phylogenetics

Introduction

Soil salinization-alkalization constitutes a major abiotic stress that puts serious constraints on plant growth, development, and productivity [1]. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), over 1 billion hectares of land worldwide are salt-affected [2], with combined salinity-alkalinity stress predominating in such areas [3]. This complex stress imposes dual pressures of ion toxicity and high-pH conditions on plants, consequently causing developmental retardation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [4, 5], membrane system damage, chloroplast structural disintegration, and reduced photosynthetic efficiency [6]. Ultimately, these effects trigger tissue senescence and plant death [7]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of plant salt-alkali tolerance [8] can enhance stress resistance and facilitate the sustainable utilization of saline-alkaline land resources. This has strategic significance in ensuring agricultural sustainability and ecological security.

Heterotrimeric G proteins serve as central signaling hubs in eukaryotes by mediating responses to extracellular stimuli in both animals and plants [9, 10]. The canonical G protein complex comprises Gα, Gβ, and Gγ subunits. Notably, plants have evolved structurally distinct, extra-large Gα subunits (XLGs) with approximately double the molecular weight of canonical Gα [11]. Although plant canonical Gα proteins (e.g. GPA1) share low sequence identity with mammalian homologues, and their tertiary structures are highly conserved [12]. Striking diversity exists between the two kingdoms with respect to the Gα subunit alone. Mammals possess more than 20 Gα isoforms, whereas Arabidopsis, rice, and other plants typically encode only one canonical Gα (GPA1/RGA1) and three XLGs (XLG1–3) [13–17]. Furthermore, XLGs contain a C-terminal Gα-like domain and an N-terminal extension harboring nuclear localization signals and cysteine-rich domains [18–20]. They retain GTPase activity [21] and interact with Gβ in the nucleus to facilitate intercellular signaling [22].

Gβ subunits are evolutionarily conserved in plants (e.g. a single copy in Arabidopsis and maize). The 36 kDa Arabidopsis Gβ contains coiled-coil domains and WD40 repeats [23], constitutively dimerizes with Gγ [24, 25], and localizes to plasma/endomembrane systems [18] or the nucleus to modulate transcription factors [26, 27]. In Arabidopsis, AGB1 regulates sodium ion flux in roots and its translocation from roots to shoots. Notably, loss of AGB1 function significantly reduces root-to-shoot sodium translocation, thereby mitigating sodium toxicity and improving plant growth under salt stress [28–30]. These findings provide direct evidence for the role of the Gβ subunit in maintaining ionic homeostasis in plants. Moreover, studies in crop species further support the conservation of this mechanism. For instance, specific G-protein subunits in soybean have been demonstrated to modulate ion transport efficiency within the root-shoot system, effectively reducing excessive sodium accumulation while enhancing potassium retention [28, 31]. This functional consistency with the AGB1 subunit in Arabidopsis suggests the presence of homologous or analogous regulatory mechanisms in crops such as soybean. Collectively, these findings imply that G-protein signaling, particularly through the Gβ subunit, may also play a critical role in mediating Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis and salt stress responses in maize. Gγ subunits are classified into three types based on their C-terminal features: Type I (with isoprenylation sites), Type II (only N-terminal coiled-coil domain), and Type III (atypical Gγ with cysteine-rich domains and CaaX motifs) [32]. Atypical Gγ critically regulates key agronomic traits, including plant architecture, panicle development, and nitrogen consumption efficiency [27]. Genome-wide association studies have identified the sorghum alkaline-tolerance gene AT1 (maize orthologue ZmGG1), whose knockout enhances alkali tolerance in monocots (e.g. rice GS3) [33]. Similarly, silencing DPE1 in rice alters the tiller angle and improves sheath blight resistance [34–36]. All types of Gγ form dimers with Gβ and subsequently assemble into functional heterotrimers with Gα/XLGs [37, 38]. Under abiotic stress conditions such as salinity, activation of G-proteins has been shown to enhance signaling through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, thereby inducing specific defense responses. These include the regulation of stress-related gene expression, which collectively enhances the plant’s overall resistance [39, 40]. This evidence underscores the role of G-proteins as upstream signaling hubs that coordinate complex stress response networks, in part through the activation of key downstream pathways such as the MAPK cascades.

As a globally prevalent cereal crop, maize cultivation faces severe threats from salinity-alkalinity stress. Although G proteins have been implicated in the architecture establishment, stress responses, and yield formation of maize [41–43], their molecular mechanisms in grain development and stress adaptation remain elusive. Consequently, the present study systematically identified ten G protein-encoding genes from the maize reference genome (RefGen_V4). Subsequently, integrated bioinformatics approaches were used to characterize their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal locations, gene structures, conserved motifs, cis-regulatory elements, synteny relationships, and protein-protein interaction networks, with emphasis on their expression dynamics under salt-alkali stress. Based on these results, this study provides novel insights into the functional mechanisms of maize G proteins in abiotic stress responses (particularly salinity-alkalinity). It reveals their role as signaling hubs that integrate ABA pathways, ROS homeostasis, and downstream effectors (e.g. phospholipase C, MAP kinases) in order to effectively coordinate ion homeostasis and antioxidant defenses. Therefore, this study establishes a theoretical foundation for the stress-resilient breeding of maize and other agriculturally relevant plants.

Results

Identification and characterization of ten G protein family members in the maize genome

To identify G-protein genes in maize, we conducted a systematic screening using the amino acid sequences of eight Arabidopsis G-protein subunits—comprising one Gα, one Gβ, three XLGs, and three Gγ subunits—as reference queries. An integrated approach combining local BLASTp searches and PFAM domain analysis against the maize protein database identified 10 non-redundant G-protein homologs in maize. Applying the same strategy, we further identified G-protein family members in other representative plant species (Table S1). The numbers of identified genes per species were as follows: Arabidopsis (8), rice (11), sorghum (10), foxtail millet (8), barley (4), Triticum urartu (5), Triticum turgidum (13), Aegilops tauschii (5), Brachypodium distachyon (6), Medicago truncatula (7), and common wheat (14) (Table 1).

Identification and characterization of ten G protein family members in the maize genome

Ten G protein family members were identified in the maize genome through a combined approach of local BLASTp and Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiling, using Arabidopsis G protein sequences as queries (Table 2). The α-subunit family comprised one canonical Gα subunit (ZmGA1) and three XLG proteins (ZmXLG1, ZmXLG2, and ZmXLG3). Additionally, the β-subunit family contained only one member, whereas the γ-subunit family included five members, among which ZmGG1 is a key gene conferring saline-alkali tolerance. The encoded protein sequences exhibited significant variation in length, ranging from 143 (ZmGG2) to 878 (ZmXLG1) amino acids. Corresponding molecular weights varied considerably from 16.51 to 97.59 kDa and demonstrated a strong positive correlation with the amino acid number (R² > 0.99). The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) ranged from 4.98 to 10.46, suggesting that Gα and Gβ are likely acidic proteins functioning in weakly acidic cellular environments, whereas most Gγ proteins operate in weakly alkaline environments. Instability index (II) values also varied considerably (34.77 to 71.56), thus indicating differences in protein stability under physiological conditions. ZmGB1 was the sole member with a II below 40, which classified it as stable (II < 40), whereas all others were unstable. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) scores ranged from − 1.436 to 0.031, which was reflective of the variability in hydrophobicity. Except for ZmGG1 and ZmGG5, which had positive GRAVY scores, all members were hydrophilic. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that ZmGA1 and ZmGB1 are localized to the cytoplasm, while Gγ subunits are targeted to the nucleus or chloroplast. These predictions are closely aligned with the experimentally verified localization patterns of their orthologs in model plants such as Arabidopsis and rice, underscoring the evolutionary conservation of G-protein signaling components in plants. The distinct compartmentalization of different subfamilies further suggests functional diversification within the signaling pathway [44–46].

Molecular characterization of G protein gene families in maize

Chromosomal localization of maize G protein genes

Chromosomal localization analysis was performed for the ten identified genes to elucidate the genomic distribution of maize G protein family members. The results revealed a non-random distribution across six chromosomes (1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 7) (Fig. 1). Chromosome 1 harbored the highest density of family members (ZmGA1, ZmGB1, ZmXLG1, ZmGG1, and ZmGG2), thus forming a distinct gene cluster. Although co-localized on the same chromosome, these genes were physically distant from each other, and no evidence of tandem duplication events was detected. This suggests that family expansion likely occurred through independent evolutionary events rather than gene duplication. Each of the remaining chromosomes contained a single family member, indicating potential functional diversification at their respective loci.

Phylogenetic Analysis

This study investigated the functional and evolutionary relationships of maize G proteins by constructing a phylogenetic tree using sequences from monocots (maize, rice, sorghum) and a dicot (Arabidopsis thaliana) (Fig. 2). Maize G proteins were classified into three subfamilies (Gα, Gβ, and Gγ) and clustered robustly with their monocot orthologues, which was consistent with the established plant evolutionary history. Specifically, ZmGA1 grouped with SbGA1, OsGA1, and AtGA1. This observation directly supported the closer phylogenetic affinity of maize to monocots, and was congruent with the canonical monocot-dicot divergence framework. Similarly, ZmGB1 clustered with SbGB1, OsGB1, and AtGB1, thereby indicating high conservation of the Gβ subunit across these species. The Gγ subfamily diverged into three types (I, II, and III), wherein types II and III formed plant-specific clades. This suggested a potential functional divergence among the Gγ subtypes.

Analysis of conserved motifs, domains, gene structures, and predicted 3D structures of maize G protein family members

To gain deeper insights into the functional and structural characteristics of the maize G protein family, the conserved motifs, functional domains, and gene structures were analyzed for its ten members. Ten conserved motifs were initially identified using Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation (MEME) (Fig. 3a). Motif composition differed significantly between subfamilies, which reflected functional divergence during evolution and the underlying protein functional diversity. Furthermore, members within the same subfamily exhibited similar motif patterns, indicating evolutionary conservation.

Analysis of (a) conserved motifs, (b) domains, (c) gene structures, and (d) three-dimensional structure prediction of maize G protein family members

Conserved domain analysis revealed five distinct functional domains across the ten members (Fig. 3b). Except for ZmGG1, ZmGG4, and ZmGG5 (which demonstrated no significant domain hits), each member possessed a single domain. Within the Gα subfamily, only ZmXLG1 lacked the G-alpha binding domain. The Gβ members contained only a WD40 domain. Contrastingly, the Gγ subfamily displayed the most domain diversity: ZmGG3 harbored a NosR superfamily domain, ZmGG2 contained a G-gamma domain, whereas ZmGG1, ZmGG4, and ZmGG5 exhibited no significant matches.

Moreover, gene structure analysis indicated that the Gα subfamily contained significantly more exons (ranging from 8 in ZmXLG2/ZmXLG3 to 14 in ZmGA1) compared to other subfamilies (Fig. 3c). In contrast, the Gβ member possessed only 3 exons. Within the Gγ subfamily, only ZmGG2 had 4 exons, whereas the others contained 5 exons. Exon/intron numbers varied markedly between subfamilies but were highly similar within subfamilies. This suggested that biological functions are conserved among members of the same subfamily.

The predicted 3D structures revealed overall structural divergence among the ten proteins, but with significant similarity within the subfamilies (Fig. 3d). In particular, Gα members shared a conserved C-terminus and featured an N-terminal helical domain with a P-loop motif, whereas the XLG subfamily was characterized by a cysteine-rich sequence. The Gβ protein contained seven WD40 repeats at its C-terminus, forming a β-propeller structure. Gγ proteins exhibited a coiled-coil motif at the N-terminus, which facilitated dimerization with Gβ. Notably, Type I Gγ proteins possessed a palmitoylation site and a CaaX prenylation motif (CXXL), whereas Type II Gγ proteins lacked additional C-terminal structural elements. In contrast, Type III Gγ proteins contained a transmembrane helix and a cysteine-rich region, which contributed to their increased length.

Analysis of Cis-Regulatory Elements (CREs) in promoter regions of maize G protein genes

CREs within promoter regions play a crucial role in the regulation of gene expression in plants. By interacting with regulatory proteins such as transcription factors, CREs precisely orchestrate the spatiotemporal expression patterns of genes in response to diverse internal and external signals. Consequently, they influence plant growth, development, and adaptation to environmental stress. To gain deeper insights into the functional characteristics of the maize G protein gene family and their potential regulatory mechanisms in various physiological processes, the present study analyzed the 2200 bp upstream regions of the start codon (ATG) for enriched CREs (Fig. 4b). with the relative positions and distribution features of key elements further illustrated (Fig. 4a).

Analysis of cis-acting regulatory elements in the promoter regions of the maize G protein family. a Relative Position and Distribution Characteristics of CREs. b Enrichment analysis of CREs

Our analysis revealed a significant enrichment of CREs associated with abiotic and biotic stress responses. These included MYB-binding sites involved in cellular stress and immune responses, MYC-binding sites promoting DNA damage repair, heat shock protein-related elements (STRE), anaerobic induction elements (ARE), and elements responsive to drought and high salinity (DRE core). A few members also harbored defense and stress-responsive elements (TC-rich repeats), wound-responsive elements (WUN-motif), drought-responsive elements (MBS), wound response elements (W box), and low-temperature-responsive elements (LTR). This indicates the broad involvement of maize G protein genes in the regulation of stress adaptation. Abundant elements were further identified within the hormone-responsive category, including abscisic acid (ABA)-responsive elements (ABRE), methyl jasmonate-responsive elements (CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif), and an oxidative stress-responsive element (as-1).This indicated the importance of this gene family in the modulation of hormone signaling. With regard to growth- and development-related CREs, significant enrichment was observed for meristem expression regulatory elements (CAT-box, A-box) and diverse light-responsive elements (e.g. AE-box, ATCT-motif, Box 4, G-box, I-box, MRE, Sp1, TCT-motif, and GA-motif).

Global Distribution and Clustering Characteristics of CREs Analysis of CREs revealed a non-uniform distribution across promoter regions, with distinct clustering patterns observed in specific genomic intervals. As illustrated in Fig. 4a, dense clusters of CREs were identified within particular regions of several gene promoters. For instance, ZmGA1 and ZmGG3 exhibited pronounced enrichment in the 50–200 bp and 400–600 bp intervals, whereas ZmGG1 showed clustering within the 100–400 bp and 600–1000 bp regions. These CRE-enriched segments may represent regulatory “hotspots,” potentially functioning as enhancers or composite regulatory modules that act cooperatively. Notably, key stress-responsive elements, such as DRE and ABRE, were preferentially localized within the proximal promoter region (within 500 bp upstream of the transcription start site). This spatial preference aligns with their functional roles in mediating rapid responses to environmental stimuli.

Furthermore, striking differences were observed in the number and types of CREs among the different G protein subunits. ZmGA1 contained the highest number of CREs within the α subfamily, wherein heat shock-related elements (STRE) represented the highest proportion. This suggests that ZmGA1 expression is likely influenced by multiple regulatory factors and exhibits high sensitivity to stressors such as heat, drought, and salinity. In contrast, ZmXLG1, another α subfamily member, possessed the fewest CREs, potentially reflecting a more conserved regulatory mechanism acquired during evolution. ZmXLG2 contained the highest number of ABA-responsive elements (ABRE), which indicated that its expression may be strongly regulated by ABA signaling. Additionally, ZmGB1 featured a relatively high abundance of MYB, MYC, and STRE. This pointed to complex and diverse regulation with likely important functions in plant growth, stress responses, and hormone signaling.

The number of CREs also varied among the five Gγ subfamily members, but all of them commonly contained elements such as ARE, MYB, MYC, A-BOX, and G-BOX. This highlights the high regulatory complexity and functional diversity of this subfamily, particularly with respect to growth, stress adaptation, and hormonal responses. Notably, ZmGG2 was the sole γ member lacking STRE elements, which suggested that it may not participate in heat stress responses or fulfil any distinct role therein. Despite the absence of STRE, ZmGG2 might respond to other stresses through alternative elements, such as ARE, MYB, or MYC. Furthermore, ZmGG4 was the only γ member devoid of ABRE elements, implying that it may not be involved in ABA signaling, but instead participates in other independent physiological processes or signaling pathways. During subfamily evolution, most genes retained ABRE for ABA responsiveness, whereas ZmGG4 potentially acquired novel functions or adaptations through the loss of this element. Consequently, such functional divergence may enable this gene to better adapt to specific environmental conditions or meet particular physiological demands.

Syntenic analysis of maize G proteins

Intra- and inter-species synteny analyses were performed to investigate the evolutionary characteristics of the maize G protein family. Results revealed collinear relationships only between “ZmXLG2-ZmXLG3” and “ZmGG3-ZmGG5” (Fig. 5a). This indicates a complex evolutionary history that potentially involves gene rearrangement and chromosomal repatterning. These two gene pairs represent ideal models for investigating functional divergence following gene duplication within the family. For the Gα subfamily, the ZmXLG2–ZmXLG3 pair originated from a single duplication event but subsequently underwent divergent evolutionary trajectories, likely leading to neofunctionalization or subfunctionalization. While they retain similar core structures, differences may have arisen in their regulatory elements or specific protein interaction interfaces, enabling adaptation to distinct signaling pathways or response to specific upstream signals. In the Gγ subfamily, the syntenic relationship between ZmGG3 and ZmGG5 reveals a potential origin of functional diversity within this group. Gene duplication provided the raw genetic material for functional innovation, and ZmGG3 and ZmGG5 appear to have diverged from a common ancestral gene, each evolving distinct temporal expression patterns in response to saline-alkaline stress.

Collinearity of the maize G protein family. a Intraspecific collinearity analysis of maize. b Collinearity analysis of maize G proteins in comparison with those from Arabidopsis thaliana, rice, and sorghum. c Ka/Ks ratios of duplicated pairs of G-protein in maize, The dashed line represents Ka/Ks equal to 1

Additionally, inter-species analysis demonstrated higher conservation of syntenic blocks between maize and monocots (sorghum/rice) compared to the dicot Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 5b). Twelve orthologous gene pairs were identified in sorghum and 9 in rice, but only 2 in Arabidopsis. Therefore, these results corroborated the existence of closer evolutionary relationships among monocots and aligned with the established phylogenetic position of maize.

To elucidate the evolutionary paths of the identified syntenic gene pairs, we calculated the nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates, along with their ratio (Ka/Ks), for each pair (Table S2). The analysis revealed that all maize G-protein homologous gene pairs exhibited Ka/Ks ratios significantly less than 1 (Fig. 5c), indicating strong purifying selection acting on this gene family to maintain conserved protein structures and core functions. This trend was particularly pronounced among core signaling components. Within the Gα subfamily, the maize syntenic pair ZmXLG2–ZmXLG3 showed an exceptionally low Ka/Ks ratio (0.14) coupled with a relatively high Ks value (1.27). This suggests that despite an early duplication event, their functions have remained strictly constrained, potentially through subfunctionalization, to preserve the essential role of XLG proteins in signal transduction. In the Gγ subfamily, the ZmGG3–ZmGG5 pair displayed a comparatively higher Ka/Ks ratio (0.74), yet still below 1. This implies that while under purifying selection, these genes have retained a degree of sequence plasticity, which may provide the evolutionary groundwork for functional divergence. In interspecific comparisons, orthologous gene pairs between maize and sorghum or rice consistently showed Ka/Ks ratios below 0.5, further supporting the high functional conservation of the G-protein signaling pathway in Poaceae. However, certain Gγ ortholog pairs (e.g., SbGG5–ZmGG3 and OsGG4–ZmGG3) exhibited relatively higher Ka values, with Ka/Ks ratios ranging between 0.36 and 0.46, ranking among the highest within this subfamily. This indicates relatively relaxed evolutionary constraints, possibly related to their role as signaling modulators, granting them greater sequence adaptability to meet species-specific environmental or developmental needs. Furthermore, for some maize-Arabidopsis ortholog pairs (e.g., AtGG2–ZmGG2 and AtGG3–ZmGG3), Ks values were not calculable (NaN), likely due to synonymous site saturation resulting from the vast divergence time between monocots and dicots. Their notably high Ka values nonetheless reflect significant sequence divergence over this extended evolutionary period. Collectively, the synteny and evolutionary pressure analyses demonstrate that the maize G-protein gene family is predominantly under strong purifying selection to preserve core signaling functions. Concurrently, the Gγ subfamily exhibits relatively greater evolutionary flexibility, which may provide the molecular basis for fine-tuning the signaling pathway and optimizing its function as maize adapts to specific environmental stresses such as salinity and alkalinity.

GO (Gene Ontology) analysis of G protein genes

GO enrichment analysis of the ten maize G protein family members revealed significant functional specialization in transmembrane signaling (Fig. 6). At the cellular level, the gene set exhibited pronounced enrichment in plasma membrane-associated terms. The heterotrimeric G-protein complex (all ten genes) was the most enriched, followed by the external side of the plasma membrane, plasma membrane external component, and membrane protein complex. Notably, the top nine significantly enriched terms were all membrane-associated, which strongly validated their core role in transmembrane signaling. Regarding molecular function, all genes were enriched in signal transducer activity, whereas five genes specifically possessed GTPase activity, which was consistent with their function as molecular switches. With regard to biological processes, the gene set was primarily enriched in G protein-mediated signaling networks, with core pathways including the G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway (ten genes) and cell surface receptor signaling pathway (eight genes). Crucially, seven genes exhibited significant enrichment for response to abscisic acid (ABA), thereby demonstrating a dual regulatory role in integrating phytohormone signaling and environmental responses.

Analysis of the Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network of G proteins in zea mays

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was predicted and constructed to gain deeper insights into the potential regulatory mechanisms of maize G protein family members (Fig. 7). Analysis revealed that these family members occupy central hub positions within the network and exhibit high connectivity, which aligns with their pivotal functional roles in key signaling pathways and regulatory networks. Based on PPI network analysis, we identified 50 protein partners that demonstrated significant interactions with maize G protein family members (Table S3). The potential biological roles of these interacting proteins were elucidated via functional annotation using the STRING database. They were enriched primarily in the following major functional categories:

Predicted PPI network of G proteins with other proteins in maize. Each node represents a protein, whereas the edges indicate the interactions between them

Signal Transduction Components: This category contains multiple phosphoinositide phospholipase C (PLC) members, which act as key effectors downstream of G protein signaling pathways involved in second messenger generation. It also includes G protein coupled receptor 1 (GPCR1), a classical upstream signal receiver and transmembrane transducer, as well as leucine-rich repeat receptor-like protein kinases (LRR-RLKs, e.g., FEA2). Furthermore, the identification of multiple big MAP kinases (BMK) and protein kinase superfamily proteins suggests an association between the G protein network and key signaling cascades, such as the MAPK pathway.

Enzymes Related to Post-Translational Modification (PTM): This group primarily comprises multiple glycylpeptide N-tetradecanoyltransferases (N-myristoyltransferases), which catalyze myristoylation, a modification critical for the membrane localization and function of specific G protein subunits, and multiple ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolases (deubiquitinating enzymes, DUBs). Therefore, it implies the potential regulation of G protein signaling via the ubiquitination pathway.

Metabolism-Related Enzymes: The identification of multiple β-amylases suggests the plausible involvement of G proteins in carbohydrate metabolism or energy sensing processes.

The construction and analysis of this PPI network not only confirmed the status of maize G protein family members as core nodes within the signaling network, but also facilitated the systematic elucidation of their potential interaction partners and functional associations. Consequently, this provides crucial molecular clues for a deeper understanding of G protein-mediated signal transduction, regulation by post-translational modifications, and their cross-talk with other biological processes, such as metabolism and cytoskeleton organization.

Tissue-wide expression of maize G proteins under varying stress conditions, including salt-alkali stress

RNA-seq data downloaded from MaizeGDB were analyzed to investigate the expression of maize G protein genes across different tissues. The results indicated that maize G protein genes exhibit tissue-specific expression, with significant variation across developmental stages (Fig. 8a). Several G-protein genes showed tissue-preferential expression patterns, with ZmXLG1, ZmGG1, ZmGG4, and ZmGG5 demonstrating low transcript abundance in roots, while ZmXLG1, ZmXLG3, ZmGG1, and ZmGG5 exhibited reduced expression in mature leaves.

Tissue-wide gene expression analysis of maize G proteins under various stress conditions, including salt-alkali stress, in the roots of resistant and susceptible genotypes. a Heatmap of maize G protein expression in different tissues; b Heatmap of maize G protein expression in response to cold, heat, salt, and UV stress in seedlings of inbred lines B73; c Transcriptomic heatmap of maize G protein expression in the roots under salt-alkali stress

All maize G protein genes were highly expressed in the meristem, internode, and ear tissues, except for ZmGG5, which exhibited lower expression during silking. Notably, the expression levels were generally low in mature pollen.

Furthermore, gene expression under various abiotic stress conditions was examined using RNA-seq data from the inbred B73 line that had been subjected to UV, salt, heat, and cold stress (Fig. 8b). The expression patterns of maize G protein genes differed significantly under these stresses. ZmXLG1 and ZmXLG3 consistently demonstrated low expression across all stresses, which potentially reflected evolutionary conservation or adaptive strategies. Most of the other G protein genes displayed diverse expression patterns under different abiotic stress conditions. Even under the same stress treatment, some genes exhibited opposing expression trends. For instance, ZmGG1 and ZmGG5 had low expression under salt stress, whereas ZmGG2, a member of the same subfamily, exhibited high expression. Notably, ZmGB1 was highly expressed under all stress conditions.

To further investigate short-term responses to saline-alkaline stress, we analyzed transcript levels in the roots—the primary site for sensing and responding to such stress—of sensitive (Chang7-2) and tolerant (Zheng58) genotypes at 5 and 24 h post-treatment (Fig. 8c). The results demonstrate distinct expression patterns and significant differential regulation of G-protein family genes under both salt and alkaline stress conditions, indicating their crucial roles in mediating maize responses to saline-alkaline stress. Tolerance and sensitive materials appear to employ different regulatory mechanisms to mediate stress adaptation. Notably, ZmGG1, previously characterized as a negative regulator, was highly expressed in stress-sensitive materials. This elevated expression likely contributes to reduced stress adaptability by suppressing positive signaling pathways. Conversely, the heightened expression of ZmGB1 and ZmGG4 in tolerant materials suggests their potential function as positive regulators that enhance stress adaptation. Other genes, including ZmGA1, ZmXLG1, ZmXLG3, and ZmGG3, showed elevated expression in sensitive materials. This pattern may reflect aberrant activation of signaling pathways under stress conditions, ultimately leading to reduced stress tolerance.

This study initially identified ten candidate G protein genes in the maize genome through systematic bioinformatic analysis. These genes were classified into three subfamilies (α, β, and γ) based on their sequence similarity and conserved domains. Subsequent investigations focused specifically on the γ-subfamily gene ZmGG1 (ZmGS3). Previous studies indicate that ZmGG1 acts as a negative regulator of maize growth and development, making it a primary focus of this research.

To identify the genes that potentially share functional similarities with ZmGG1, we performed a detailed analysis of their conserved sequences and functional domains. Using ZmGG1 as a reference, secondary screening of the maize genome was conducted. Targeted screening was aimed at identifying other G protein genes that exhibited high homology or potential functional associations with ZmGG1. This approach successfully identified ZmGG3 and ZmGG5. Notably, both genes were already included in the initial candidate list and belonged to the γ-subfamily. This result confirmed the sequence and structural similarity between ZmGG3, ZmGG5, and ZmGG1 and suggested potential functional relatedness. Therefore, although ZmGG3 and ZmGG5 were identified in the primary screening, secondary screening using ZmGG1 as a reference further confirmed their significance within the γ-subfamily and provided a clearer direction for subsequent functional validation.

Subsequently, the expression patterns of the ZmGG1 subfamily (ZmGG1, ZmGG3, and ZmGG5) under salt and alkaline stress were evaluated using qRT-PCR in the roots and leaves of the tolerant (Zheng58) and sensitive (Chang7-2) genotypes (Table S4). The results revealed a distinct and sustained downregulation of ZmGG1 expression under both stress conditions (Fig. 9). Furthermore, this downregulation was particularly pronounced in roots under alkaline stress, closely aligning with the saline-alkaline tolerance characteristics of the two genotypes and further supporting the role of ZmGG1 as a negative regulator of the stress response. In contrast, ZmGG3 expression displayed a dynamic pattern of initial upregulation, followed by downregulation under saline-alkaline stress. Differences in ZmGG3 expression between Zheng58 and Chang7-2 were more significant in the roots under salt stress, thereby indicating a higher sensitivity of this gene to salt stress in the root tissues. Moreover, ZmGG5 also exhibited a general trend of sustained downregulation under saline-alkaline stress. However, atypical transient upregulation followed by downregulation was observed for ZmGG5 in Zheng58 roots under salt stress. Despite this fluctuation, the overall expression pattern of ZmGG5 resembled that of ZmGG1 and corresponded to the tolerance phenotypes. Overall, the ZmGG1 subfamily genes exhibited a general downregulation trend under saline-alkaline stress, with a more pronounced decrease in the roots compared to the leaves. ZmGG1 and ZmGG5 demonstrated similar sustained downregulation under prolonged stress exposure. Conversely, ZmGG3 exhibited an initial upregulation, followed by down-regulation under specific stress conditions. ZmGG1 has previously been established as a negative regulator under alkaline stress, wherein its downregulation in roots and leaves has a clear biological basis. However, similar negative regulatory roles for ZmGG5 and ZmGG3 under saline-alkaline stress remain unconfirmed. These findings suggest that the ZmGG1 subfamily may play distinct regulatory roles during growth, development, and environmental stress responses in plants.

Analysis of fresh weight, dry weight, and Na⁺/K⁺ ratio in maize seedlings under saline-alkaline stress

Maintaining ionic homeostasis represents a crucial physiological mechanism determining maize tolerance to saline-alkaline stress, with Na⁺/K⁺ balance playing a pivotal role in cellular osmoregulation, enzymatic activity, and membrane potential stability. To systematically compare physiological responses between tolerant (Zheng58) and sensitive (Chang7-2) genotypes under stress conditions, and to link these findings with previous G-protein regulatory network analyses, we dynamically monitored changes in Na⁺/K⁺ ratios in leaves and roots (Fig. 15a, Table S5), along with shoot fresh and dry weights (Fig. 15b, Table S6) at various time points.

Determination of (a) Na+/K +content and (b) dry/fresh weight of Zheng58 and Chang7-2 at different time points. All measurements were performed with three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates

The results identified disrupted Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis as a primary physiological defect in the sensitive material. During early stress exposure (5 h), all materials showed rapidly elevated Na⁺/K⁺ ratios, indicating universal disruption of ionic homeostasis. However, Zheng58 demonstrated remarkable capacity for ionic rebalancing as stress prolonged. By day 7 of salt stress, Zheng58 maintained leaf Na⁺/K⁺ ratios at 0.45–0.48, while Chang7-2 reached 5.0–5.1 representing a more than 10-fold difference. Under alkaline stress, Zheng58 leaf ratios remained at approximately 0.56–0.59, whereas Chang7-2 ratios surged to around 10.5. Similar trends were observed in root tissues, indicating Zheng58 possesses more efficient Na⁺ exclusion, vacuolar compartmentalization, and selective K⁺ uptake/retention capabilities throughout the root-shoot system.

Consistent with ionic homeostasis patterns, biomass accumulation showed significant genotypic variation. By day 7 of saline-alkaline stress, Zheng58 maintained shoot fresh weight at approximately 80% and 75% of control levels under salt and alkaline conditions, respectively, while Chang7-2 declined to approximately 55% and 45%. Dry weight data further confirmed Zheng58’s superior capacity to maintain biomass accumulation and carbon assimilate partitioning, suggesting more effective resource allocation between growth and stress defense.

Further analysis revealed close coupling between Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis, biomass maintenance, and expression of core G-protein components. In tolerant Zheng58, the Gβ subunit ZmGB1 showed constitutively high root expression, potentially serving as a central hub for heterotrimeric G-proteins that activates phospholipase C (PLC) to generate IP₃ second messengers. This pathway may regulate intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling and enhance SOS pathway activity, thereby promoting Na⁺ exclusion. Simultaneously, the Gβγ dimer may enhance K⁺ uptake and retention through regulation of K⁺ channels (e.g., AKT1), collectively maintaining lower Na⁺/K⁺ ratios.

Conversely, in sensitive Chang7-2, the Gγ subunit ZmGG1 exhibited higher basal expression with delayed downregulation during early stress. Functioning as a negative regulator, ZmGG1 may inhibit aquaporin phosphorylation or interfere with ion transporter activities (e.g., SOS, HKT), impeding both Na⁺ exclusion and K⁺ uptake. This leads to substantial Na⁺ accumulation and K⁺ loss, ultimately causing uncontrolled elevation of Na⁺/K⁺ ratios. This ionic imbalance further exacerbates oxidative stress, consistent with our previous observations: Chang7-2 showed rapidly collapsing SOD, POD, and CAT activities following dramatic fluctuations, accompanied by surging MDA content and root vitality failure. These findings indicate that G-protein dysregulation triggers a vicious cycle between ionic homeostasis collapse and oxidative damage, collectively contributing to the sensitive phenotype and sharp biomass reduction.

Discussion

Saline-alkaline stress limits maize yield by disrupting the ionic and redox balance. In this study, we identified 10 maize G protein genes, characterized their genomic features, and analyzed their stress-responsive expression. The observed patterns, particularly the dynamic responses of ZmGG3 and ZmGB1, suggest their roles in ROS regulation and stress tolerance, providing a foundation for discussing their functional divergence and evolutionary significance.

Functional divergence and evolutionary significance of the maize G protein gene family

Our study provides a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis of the maize G-protein gene family, revealing significant functional divergence during evolution. This diversification aligns with the multifaceted roles of G-proteins in plant growth, development, signal transduction, and stress responses. Through systematic comparison with other major crops and model plants, we can more clearly delineate the conserved characteristics and species-specific features of the maize G-protein family.

Analysis of gene number and composition across plant species reveals a pattern of “core conservation” and “regulatory variation.” This and previous studies indicate that the canonical Gα, Gβ, and XLG subunits, serving as the “core signaling unit,” are highly conserved in copy number (1, 1, and 3, respectively) in species such as Arabidopsis, sorghum, and maize [2, 5, 8], underscoring the non-redundant nature of their core signaling functions [47]. In contrast, the Gγ subfamily, which acts as functional modulators, exhibits notable species-specific expansion. For instance, while Arabidopsis possesses 3 Gγ members, rice, maize, and sorghum harbor 5 [7]. This expansion in Poaceae species, particularly maize, likely reflects an adaptive response to more complex environments and the regulation of diverse agronomic traits (e.g., plant architecture, inflorescence development, stress response), illustrating the principle of “functional demand driving gene family evolution” [48, 49].

Synteny and evolutionary selection pressure (Ka/Ks) analyses provide key evolutionary dynamics evidence for the observed functional divergence. We found that all maize G-protein homologous gene pairs are under strong purifying selection (Ka/Ks < < 1), confirming the extreme conservation of their core signaling functions. However, the intensity of selection varied significantly among subfamilies. For example, the Gα subfamily members ZmXLG2 and ZmXLG3 exhibited an exceptionally low Ka/Ks value (0.14) coupled with a high Ks value, indicating that despite an early duplication event, their protein sequences remain highly constrained. This suggests that any amino acid change could potentially impair their crucial GTPase activity or protein interaction interfaces, consistent with their central role in signal transduction. In stark contrast, the Gγ subfamily members ZmGG3 and ZmGG5 displayed the highest relative Ka/Ks ratio (0.74) within the family, implying relatively relaxed evolutionary constraints. This sequence “plasticity” provides evolutionary space for functional innovation within the Gγ subunits, allowing for fine-tuning of signal output through subtle sequence variations to adapt to specific environmental stresses. This finding offers an evolutionary explanation for the differential expression patterns observed in the Gγ subfamily under saline-alkaline stress (Fig. 9) and its role as a key regulatory node for complex traits.

Maize G-protein genes also exhibit unique features in their genomic distribution and evolutionary modes. Our analysis revealed that family members are dispersed across chromosomes (e.g., a gene cluster on chromosome 1), with no evidence of tandem duplication events. This differs from situations in species like rice, where some G-protein genes have undergone tandem duplication. This suggests that the expansion of the maize G-protein family likely originated primarily from whole-genome or dispersed duplication events, with subsequent retention of duplicates via subfunctionalization or neofunctionalization, thereby increasing the complexity of its signaling network.

Comparative analysis of promoter cis-regulatory elements uncovered the regulatory basis for functional implementation. Consistent with studies in Arabidopsis and rice [23, 45], we found significant enrichment of ABA-responsive elements (ABRE) and drought-responsive elements (DRE core) in the promoters of several maize G-protein genes (e.g., ZmGA1, ZmXLG2, ZmGB1). This cross-species conservation strongly supports an ancient and core function for G-proteins in ABA-mediated stress responses. Concurrently, we identified species-specific regulatory strategies [50]. For instance, maize Gγ genes (e.g., ZmGG3) showed higher enrichment for light-responsive elements in their promoters, potentially related to the more refined light signaling requirements of maize as a C4 plant. Notably, ZmGG4 was the only Gγ member lacking ABREs, suggesting it may execute its unique function through a novel, ABA-independent pathway.

The typological diversification of the Gγ subfamily (Types I, II, III) is particularly characteristic in monocots [7]. Among these, ZmGG1, a Type III member, encodes a protein lacking a canonical Gγ domain but possessing a cysteine-rich region. This structural feature is compatible with its function as the ortholog of sorghum AT1, which has been experimentally validated as a negative regulator under alkaline stress [2, 4, 7, 8]. In contrast, Type I member ZmGG2 possesses a C-terminal CaaX prenylation motif, predicting potential membrane anchoring via lipid modification—a functional mode similar to its rice ortholog DEP1, which regulates panicle architecture through membrane localization [50]. This structure-based diversity provides the molecular foundation for functional specificity within the Gγ subfamily.

In summary, through systematic comparison with multiple plant species, we demonstrate that while the maize G-protein family inherits conserved core signaling functions, its Gγ subfamily has undergone specific expansion and diversification. This is coupled with corresponding regulatory elements and structural variations, potentially constructing a more complex and specialized signaling regulatory network. This enhanced complexity likely enables maize to meet its intricate growth and developmental demands and adapt to saline-alkaline stress environments.

PPI network and biological implications of G proteins in maize

PPI network analysis revealed that heterotrimeric G proteins in maize form intricate interaction networks with other key proteins [51–53]. These networks are likely to play crucial roles in intracellular signal transduction and stress responses. G proteins mediate the transduction of extracellular signals primarily through interactions with G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) [54]. Furthermore, interactions with diverse transcription factors and signaling proteins suggest that G proteins regulate both plant growth/development and stress responses [23]. Our PPI network analysis identified several potential G protein interactors, which may hold significant biological importance in the signaling pathways governed by G proteins [55]. Under salt stress conditions, maize G proteins interact with POD and CAT to regulate redox homeostasis [56, 57], while also forming a modular network with core components of the abscisic acid (ABA) signalling pathway—such as PP2Cs and SnRK2s—to coordinate stomatal movement and ion homeostasis [58]. This complex interactome positions G proteins as central regulatory nodes responsible for integrating plant growth and stress responses.

Differential expression and functional coordination of G Protein genes in maize under saline-alkaline stress

As conserved signaling hubs in plants, heterotrimeric G-proteins play a pivotal role in maize responses to saline-alkaline stress. By integrating transcriptome data with qRT-PCR validation, this study systematically deciphered the expression dynamics of maize G-protein subfamily members under stress and their functional specialization in tolerance regulation.

The canonical Gα subunit ZmGA1 exhibited relatively stable expression under stress, though a slight upregulation was observed in the roots of the sensitive line Chang7-2, suggesting its potential role as a GTPase switch during early stress response. In contrast, the three XLG members (ZmXLG1, ZmXLG2, ZmXLG3) were generally suppressed under multiple stress conditions, with more pronounced downregulation in the sensitive genotype. Given the nuclear localization and transcriptional regulatory potential of XLGs, their repressed expression may impair nucleus-cytoplasmic signaling coordination, compromising the activation of downstream stress-responsive genes and thereby exacerbating ionic imbalance and oxidative damage.

The sole Gβ subunit ZmGB1 showed constitutively high expression under salt, alkali, cold, and heat stresses, with significantly higher transcript levels in the roots of the tolerant line Zheng58 compared to the sensitive line. Its stable high expression likely helps maintain the integrity of the Gβγ dimer, ensuring effective activation of downstream effectors such as phospholipase C and MAPK cascades, thereby playing a central role in ionic homeostasis, ROS scavenging, and stomatal movement. The superior expression of ZmGB1 correlated with higher root activity and lower MDA accumulation in Zheng58 under stress, indicating its potential as a positive regulator of saline-alkaline tolerance.

The Gγ subfamily members displayed distinct functional differentiation and temporal specificity: the reported negative regulator ZmGG1 was consistently downregulated in root tissues, potentially enhancing water and ion homeostasis by releasing inhibition of aquaporins or ion transporters; ZmGG3 exhibited a biphasic expression pattern of “rapid induction–timely attenuation,” where early upregulation may activate MAPK cascades or Ca²⁺ signaling to initiate antioxidant defense [40, 42], while subsequent decline prevents signal over-amplification [59, 60]; ZmGG5 showed an overall decreasing trend (Fig. 9) but transient upregulation in Zheng58 roots under salt stress, suggesting a fine-tuning role in specific genetic backgrounds and stress phases; ZmGG2 and ZmGG4 displayed minimal fluctuations but notable tissue specificity, implying functions more specialized in developmental regulation or light signal integration.

An integrated model of G-protein-mediated dynamic ROS and ionic homeostasis under saline-alkaline stress

Based on integrated analysis of G-protein expression patterns, ROS metabolism, ionic homeostasis, and biomass data, we developed a G-protein-mediated comprehensive model of maize response to saline-alkaline stress. This model elucidates the molecular physiological mechanism through which G-proteins, as core signaling hubs, coordinately regulate ionic homeostasis restoration and reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism via specific expression combinations of their subunits, ultimately determining the comprehensive trait of biomass accumulation.

In the tolerant genotype Zheng58, the constitutive high expression of ZmGB1 combined with rapid downregulation of ZmGG1 forms an advantageous signaling unit. ZmGB1 serves as a stable signaling foundation, activating downstream effectors including phospholipase C (PLC) and MAP kinases. This signaling configuration enhances both SOS pathway-mediated Na⁺ exclusion and K⁺ channel-mediated K⁺ retention, thereby maintaining lower Na⁺/K⁺ ratios (Fig. 15a). Simultaneously, this signaling combination ensures stable enhancement of ROS-scavenging enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT), avoiding the “over-activation and rapid collapse” dynamics observed in the sensitive line (Figs. 10, 11 and 12), thus effectively restraining the accumulation of membrane lipid peroxidation product MDA (Fig. 13). This precise regulation at both ionic and redox levels sustains root system activity (Fig. 14), enabling Zheng58 to allocate more resources to growth maintenance, ultimately manifesting as significantly higher shoot fresh and dry weights under stress (Fig. 15b).

Conversely, in the sensitive genotype Chang7-2, insufficient ZmGB1 expression combined with delayed downregulation of ZmGG1 leads to dysregulation of the G-protein signaling network, failing to effectively coordinate downstream physiological responses. The direct consequences include collapse of ionic homeostasis (uncontrolled elevation of Na⁺/K⁺ ratio) and failure of the ROS scavenging system (surge in MDA). Oxidative damage and ionic toxicity form a vicious cycle, ultimately resulting in root function failure and significantly reduced biomass.

This study thereby demonstrates that the maize G-protein family, through specific combinations of member expression patterns, constitutes a key regulatory module determining saline-alkaline stress adaptation. The efficacy of this module is directly reflected in the plant’s capacity to coordinate two fundamental biological processes: “stress defense” and “growth development.” These findings provide a novel theoretical framework and candidate targets for strategies aiming to synchronously improve multiple stress tolerance traits in crops through manipulation of G-protein genes (such as enhancing ZmGB1 expression or precisely inhibiting ZmGG1 function).

Future research perspectives

Based on systematic bioinformatic analysis, expression profiling, and correlation with physiological indices, this study provides preliminary insights into the role of maize G-protein family members—particularly Gγ subunits such as ZmGG1, ZmGG3, and ZmGG5, as well as the Gβ subunit ZmGB1—in coordinately regulating ion homeostasis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) balance under saline-alkaline stress. However, these conclusions are primarily based on correlative evidence, and the underlying molecular mechanisms, signaling pathway connections, and protein interaction networks require further validation through multi-level experimental approaches. To this end, we propose an integrated research framework for systematically deciphering G-protein functions and facilitating their use in breeding.

We will employ CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to generate single and multiplex mutants of ZmGG1, ZmGG3, ZmGG5, and ZmGB1. These mutants will be systematically phenotyped under controlled saline-alkaline stress conditions, assessing key physiological parameters including ion homeostasis (Na⁺/K⁺ ratio), antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT), membrane lipid peroxidation (MDA content), root activity, and biomass accumulation. Concurrently, overexpression constructs for putative positive regulators such as ZmGB1 and ZmGG3 will be introduced into both tolerant and sensitive genetic backgrounds to test their sufficiency in enhancing stress tolerance. The use of tissue-specific or stress-inducible promoters will be explored to optimize expression patterns.

Protein-protein interactions, such as those between the Gβγ dimer and phospholipase C, MAP kinases, or ion channels (e.g., HKT, AKT1), will be validated using in vivo and in vitro techniques including yeast two-hybrid (Y2H), bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP). Furthermore, interactions between G-proteins and ABA signaling components (e.g., PP2Cs, SnRK2s) will be investigated to uncover their potential role in hormone signal integration. Changes in second messenger levels (e.g., IP₃, Ca²⁺) and the phosphorylation status of downstream MAPK cascades will be measured in G-protein mutants or overexpression lines to determine whether G-proteins regulate ROS metabolism via the MAPK pathway.

Subcellular localization dynamics under stress will be analyzed by constructing GFP-tagged fusion proteins (e.g., GFP-ZmGG1, GFP-ZmGB1) and expressing them transiently in maize protoplasts or in stable transgenic lines. Confocal microscopy will be used to investigate potential stress-induced nucleocytoplasmic shuttling or membrane relocalization, particularly exploring whether Gγ subunits participate in nuclear transcriptional regulation.

The transcriptional activation properties of key cis-acting elements (e.g., ABRE, DRE, MYB) within the promoters of ZmGG1 and ZmGB1 under saline-alkaline stress will be assessed using promoter-reporter gene fusion systems (e.g., GUS/LUC). Transcription factors binding to these elements will be identified via yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) screening or DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP-seq), systematically constructing the upstream regulatory network of G-protein genes.

Comparative transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses will be conducted between G-protein mutants and wild-type plants to comprehensively reveal the global molecular network regulated by G-proteins. This will be integrated with high-throughput phenotyping to dynamically monitor growth, photosynthetic parameters, and water use efficiency under stress, establishing a complete association chain from “genotype → expression pattern → physiological response → final phenotype”.

Field trials in artificially simulated saline-alkaline pools and natural saline-alkaline soils will evaluate the stress resistance and yield performance of key gene-edited or overexpression lines under real-world complex conditions. Superior allelic variations of genes such as ZmGB1 and ZmGG1 will be identified, and functional molecular markers will be developed to assist selection in maize molecular breeding for saline-alkaline tolerance.

Through the systematic implementation of this multi-level experimental framework, we aim to comprehensively elucidate the molecular mechanisms of maize G-proteins in saline-alkaline stress responses across multiple dimensions—gene function, protein interaction, signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, and field application. This work will provide a solid theoretical foundation and technical pathway for the targeted regulation of G-proteins and stress-resilient crop breeding.

Conclusion

This study identified ten G protein genes (Gα, Gβ, and Gγ subunits) in the maize genome and elucidated the regulatory role of the ZmGG1 subfamily (ZmGG1, ZmGG3, and ZmGG5) in saline-alkaline stress responses. Under stress conditions, ZmGG1 and ZmGG5 demonstrated sustained downregulation, whereas ZmGG3 exhibited transient upregulation, followed by a decline. The sensitive line Chang7-2 displayed drastic fluctuations in ROS-scavenging enzyme (SOD, POD, CAT) activities, significant MDA accumulation, and impaired root vigor. In contrast, the tolerant line Zheng58 maintained ROS homeostasis through constitutively high ZmGB1 expression and suppression of ZmGG1. PPI analysis revealed that G proteins coordinate stress adaptation by interacting with phospholipase C (PLC), MAP kinases, and ABA pathway components, thereby integrating antioxidant defense and ion homeostasis. Consequently, these findings establish a G protein-centered regulatory network for saline-alkaline tolerance and propose ZmGB1 (overexpression), ZmGG1 (knockout), and ZmGG3 (promoter optimization) as potential targets for molecular breeding of stress-resistant plants.

Materials and methods

Identification and comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of the maize G protein gene family

Genomic data, including coding sequences, protein sequences, and annotation files for maize (Zea mays), rice (Oryza sativa), and sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), were retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database. Corresponding data for Arabidopsis thaliana were obtained from the TAIR10 database.

For the systematic identification of G-protein family members in maize, a dual screening strategy was employed. First, a local BLASTP search against the maize proteome was performed using TBtools(50), with the amino acid sequences of eight canonical Arabidopsis G-protein subunits as queries. These included one Gα, three XLGs, one Gβ, and three Gγ subunits. To maximize coverage and minimize omissions, a complementary hidden Markov model (HMM)-based screening was conducted. The HMM profiles for the Gα (PF00503), Gβ (PF00400), and Gγ (PF00631) domains were downloaded from the Pfam database and used to query the maize proteome via the `hmmsearch` command in the HMMER software suite [61].The results from both approaches were merged, and duplicate entries were removed. All candidate sequences were subsequently validated using the NCBI Conserved Domains Database (CDD), Pfam, and SMART online tools to confirm the presence of key functional motifs: the GTP-binding P-loop in Gα subunits, the WD40 repeat domains in Gβ subunits, and the N-terminal coiled-coil structures in Gγ subunits that mediate interaction with Gβ. Sequences exhibiting low overall similarity but retaining these characteristic structural domains were retained for subsequent analysis. The final set of validated, non-redundant genes was confirmed as bona fide maize G-protein family members and were renamed according to the nomenclature of their Arabidopsis homologs.

The molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and instability index (II) of all the identified proteins were predicted using the ExPASy ProtParam tool [62]. Subcellular localization was predicted using WoLF PSORT [63] and CELLO v.2.5 [64]. Furthermore, the presence and cleavage sites of the signal peptides were predicted using SignalP 5.0 [65]. The physical positions of genes on chromosomes were determined using the “Gene Location Visualise from GFF” function in TBtools, based on the maize GFF3 annotation file from Ensembl Plants. Subsequently, the resulting map was refined using Adobe Illustrator. To investigate evolutionary relationships, G protein subunit sequences were collected from maize, Arabidopsis, rice, and sorghum. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MEGA 7 [66], and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbour-Joining (NJ) method [67] (settings: Poisson correction model, pairwise deletion, Bootstrap = 1000 replicates). The final tree was visualized and optimized using the iTOL online platform.

Gene structure (intron-exon organization) was analyzed using TBtools based on the GFF3 file. Furthermore, conserved motifs were identified using the MEME online tool (v5.5.3), with the parameters set to the maximum expected number of motifs and a width range of 6–50 amino acids. Gene structures and motif distributions were integrated and visualized using TBtools. Additionally, promoter sequences (2000 bp upstream of the start codon) for each gene were extracted using TBtools and submitted to the PlantCARE database for cis-acting regulatory element (CRE) prediction. The distribution features of CREs were also visualized using TBtools charting functions.

The ten identified maize G protein genes were submitted to the Gene Ontology (GO) database for functional annotation covering biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. Subsequently, the annotation results were subjected to enrichment analysis and visualized using the Majorbio cloud platform. Syntenic relationships within the maize genome (syntenic blocks) and homologous synteny relationships (collinear gene pairs) between maize and Arabidopsis, rice, or sorghum were analyzed using the MCScanX algorithm implemented in TBtools, with BLAST E-value ≤ 1e-5. The results were visualized using TBtools. To assess the evolutionary constraints acting on the G-protein gene family, the nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates, along with their ratio (Ka/Ks), were calculated for the protein-coding sequences using the Simple Ka/Ks Calculator. The resulting data were processed and graphically represented using Microsoft Excel 2021. Moreover, the protein secondary structure composition (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, or random coil) was predicted using the SOPMA online server. Three-dimensional structures were modelled via homology modelling using the Swiss-Model online platform based on homologous templates. Finally, the protein sequences of maize G proteins were submitted to the STRING database (v12.0) to construct a PPI network (minimum required interaction confidence score ≥ 0.400). The network data were imported into Cytoscape software (v3.10.1) for visualization and editing, wherein nodes represent proteins and edge thickness reflects the interaction confidence score.

Transcriptome data analysis and stress treatments

To investigate the tissue-specific expression patterns of maize G protein genes and their responses to abiotic stresses, transcriptome data covering the complete growth cycle of maize tissues along with data from ultraviolet, salt, heat, and cold stress treatments were retrieved from the Maize GDB database. Each dataset contained three independent biological replicates. Thereafter, the expression data were normalized and visualized using R software (v4.3.1). The elite maize inbred lines Zheng58 and Chang7-2 [68], provided by the Maize Research Institute of Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences, were used. Plump and uniformly sized seeds were surface-sterilized with 0.1% HgCl₂ for 10 min and subsequently sown in a maize seedling cultivation system. The growth conditions were as follows: 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, day/night temperatures of 25 ± 2 °C / 20 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 60%, photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 50–60 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, with regular irrigation using 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution (pH adjusted to 7.0 prior to use) (Table S7). When seedlings reached the three-leaf stage, they were subjected to stress treatments. Salt stress was applied by irrigation with a 200 mmol·L⁻¹ NaCl solution (prepared by dissolving 11.7 g of NaCl in half-strength Hoagland solution, final volume 1 L, pH ≈ 6.8). Alkaline stress was induced using a 100 mmol·L⁻¹ Na₂CO₃ solution (prepared by dissolving 10.6 g of Na₂CO₃ in half-strength Hoagland solution, final volume 1 L, pH ≈ 11.4). The detailed composition of the Hoagland nutrient solution is provided in the Supplementary Table. Subsequently, the leaf and root tissues (25 mg each) were collected at 0 h (control) and 3 days post-treatment. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C, resulting in the following sample sets: salt-stressed leaves, salt-stressed roots, alkali-stressed leaves, and alkali-stressed roots. Three independent biological replicates were established for each treatment.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the leaves and roots of both Zheng58 and Chang7-2 using the EZ-10 Spin Column Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; Cat# B618583), All treatments were performed with three independent biological replicates. Subsequently, first-strand cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the MightyScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Master Mix (with gDNA digester) (Sangon Biotech; Cat# B639252), which served as the template for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The maize Ubiquitin (UBI) gene was used as the internal reference control. qRT-PCR primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software with the following parameters: primer length 17–24 bp, GC content 40–60%, amplicon size 260–280 bp, and annealing temperature 55–58 °C. Primers with the highest scores were selected and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S8). qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using 2×SGFast qPCR Master Mix (Low Rox) (Sangon Biotech; Cat# B639272). The 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of 2×SGFast qPCR Master Mix (Low Rox), 0.4 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µmol·L⁻¹), 1 µL of cDNA template, and 8.2 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 3 s, and combined annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s (fluorescence signal acquisition at this step). The relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

Determination of physiological and biochemical parameters

Leaf and root tissues of maize seedlings were collected at ten time points (0, 5, and 12 h, and days 1 to 7) following salt stress (200 mmol·L⁻¹ NaCl) and alkaline stress (25 mmol·L⁻¹ Na₂CO₃) treatments. The collected tissues were rinsed with deionized water, surface-dried with filter paper, promptly fragmented, and stored for subsequent analysis. All measurements were performed with three independent biological replicates. For biomass determination, intact plants were rinsed with distilled water, blotted dry, and their fresh weight (FW) was recorded. Samples were then deactivated at 105 °C for 20 min and oven-dried at 80 °C until a constant dry weight (DW) was achieved. The dried samples were ground into a fine powder, and 0.5 g aliquots were accurately weighed for mineral analysis. Tissue digestion was carried out using a microwave digestion system (MARS 6) with nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide of guaranteed reagent grade. Sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) contents were quantified by atomic absorption spectrometry (ZEEnit 700) following the Chinese National Standard method GB/T 5009.91–2017, and the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio was calculated. For enzymatic assays, 0.2 g of fresh tissue was homogenized in pre-chilled extraction buffer using an ice bath. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD, using the WST-8 method), catalase (CAT, microplate method), and peroxidase (POD, guaiacol colorimetric method), as well as the malondialdehyde (MDA, thiobarbituric acid method) content, were determined using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Geruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Root activity was assessed by the triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) reduction method using 0.03 g of fresh root tissue. The relative percentage change for each parameter was calculated as [(Value in treated sample – Value in 0-hour control) / Value in 0-hour control] × 100%. Data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2021, and statistical analysis and graphing were performed with SigmaPlot 14.0. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.
