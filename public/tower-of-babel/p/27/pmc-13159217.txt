Prevalence of maternal psychological disorders after immediate postpartum hemorrhage: a repeated nested cross-sectional survey in a cohort study
Authors: Marine Pranal, Anne Legrand, Ingrid de Chazeron, Denis Gallot Pr, Pierre Michel Llorca Pr, Françoise Vendittelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostpartum hemorrhage (PPH) remains a major cause of maternal morbidity worldwide. Few published studies have examined its psychological impact, and the scientific evidence is inadequate. Our principal objective was to assess whether the prevalence of depression at 2 months postpartum was greater among women with immediate PPH (≥ 500 mL) than among women without PPH. The secondary objectives were to describe at 6 and 12 months of the postpartum period the prevalence of depression, the mean depression score and the prevalence and mean scores for anxiety and PTSD at 2, 6 and 12 months postpartum among women with immediate PPH (≥ 500 mL) compared to women without PPH, and according to the type of delivery (cesarean or vaginal delivery).MethodsOur single-center cohort included women who gave birth at ≥ 22 weeks of gestation from April 27, 2017, through April 2, 2019. Self-administered questionnaires at M2, M6, and M12 have been used to assess the prevalence of depression (EPDS score ≥ 11), anxiety (GAD-7 score > 7) and PTSD (IES-R score ≥ 30). The results are expressed as percentages, least square means, and adjusted mean scores, for all deliveries and by type of birth (cesarean or not). The women with and without PPH were compared at each study time point (M2, M6, and M12).ResultsWe included 1,298 participants (PPH: n = 528; No-PPH: n = 770). The prevalence rate of depression was higher in the PPH group than in the No-PPH group among all the deliveries at M2 (24.4 vs. 18.2%; p = 0.03) It was greater at M12, in the case of a vaginal birth, in the No-PPH group than in the PPH group: 15.9% vs. 6.2% (p = 0.03). The adjusted mean score for depression was higher only at M6, for the vaginal births, among the women with no-PPH than among the women with PPH (6.9 vs. 5.5; p = 0.02). The anxiety prevalence and adjusted mean scores did not differ between the PPH and No-PPH groups for all deliveries and regardless of the type of birth at M2, M6 or M12. The prevalence of PTSD was higher at M2 in the PPH group compared to the No-PPH group (12.8% vs. 7.6%, p = 0.02). Among the cesarean births, women with PPH had a greater prevalence of PTSD than women without PPH at two months (15.4% vs. 4.6%; p = 0.02). The adjusted mean scores for PTSD were higher only at M2 and M12 among the cesarean births for women with PPH than for those without PPH: 11.0 vs. 6.5 (p = 0.005) and 10.6 vs. 5.8 (p = 0.02), respectively.ConclusionsPerinatal professionals must bear that these three postpartum psychological outcomes showed high levels of positive screening at all time points.Trial registrationThe study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03120208, 27 April 2017.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09020-5.

Introduction

Women sometimes develop psychological disorders after giving birth, and perinatal mental health is receiving increasing international attention [1–3]. The prevalence of postpartum depression is estimated to be between 10% and 20% [2, 4]. The prevalence of postpartum anxiety ranges from 8.5% to 18% [5, 6]. Studies measuring postpartum posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have reported a prevalence ranging from 0.3% [5] to 4.0% [7], but the prevalence was higher among women with notable medical or obstetric histories (18.5%) [7]. A history of psychiatric or psychological disorders before pregnancy is a risk factor for both postpartum depression and PTSD [8, 9]. Studies have shown that the risks of postpartum depression, anxiety and PTSD are greater among women with obstetric complications than among those with uneventful pregnancies [7, 10]. Cesarean and operative vaginal deliveries also increase the risk of depression and PTSD [8, 11]. Conversely, the risk of PTSD is lower for women with physiologic births, women who feel supported during the birth process and those who have an empowered birth [12]. The rate of PPH, defined as estimated blood loss greater than 500 ml in the hours following childbirth, was 11.6% in France in 2021 [13]. Suicide is the leading cause of maternal death at one year postpartum (16.5%), in France [13]. The management of severe PPH can require medical or surgical procedures [14]. Postpartum anemia after PPH can cause fatigue, infections, and cardiovascular diseases; it can also compromise the mother‒child bond [15–17]. All of these potential consequences can lead to increased rates of psychological disorders in these women [18, 19]. No relationship between PPH and subsequent PTSD was formally established before the publication of our protocol, contrary to other known obstetric factors (cesarean, etc.) [8, 11, 20]. Six published studies have examined the psychological impact of immediate PPH. Neither Ricbourg et al. nor Eckerdal et al. reported any difference in the prevalence of depression or PTSD between the two groups [21, 22]. These studies presented methodological problems, including the use of non-validated questionnaires [23, 24], retrospective designs [23], large losses to follow-up [23, 25], information bias (interviews at different intervals after delivery) [23], recall bias [23], small sample sizes [21, 23], and failure to adjust for prenatal risk factors known to affect postpartum depression, PTSD, or anxiety [23, 24, 26]. They also focused only on severe PPH [8, 21, 23, 24, 26]. Most studies have assessed postpartum psychological disorders only for short periods (≤ 4 months) [21–24, 26], although these assessments are recommended to be performed for up to one year [27]. Our research hypothesis is that women with PPH have a higher rate of postpartum psychiatric disorders than non-exposed women do and that this occurs until one year after birth.

The study’s principal objective was to assess whether the prevalence of depression at 2 months postpartum was greater among women with immediate PPH (≥ 500 mL) than among women without PPH. The secondary objectives were to describe at 6 and 12 months of the postpartum period the prevalence of depression, the mean depression score and the prevalence and mean scores for anxiety and PTSD at 2, 6 and 12 months postpartum among women with immediate PPH (≥ 500 mL) compared to women without PPH, and according to the type of delivery (cesarean or vaginal delivery).

Methods

Study design

The PSYCHE study was a single-center repeated nested cross-sectional study within a prospective cohort of women who gave birth at a level 3 maternity unit.

Participants and sampling

The women eligible for this study were those who gave birth at or after 22 weeks of gestation. Women were affiliated with a French health insurance fund and consented to participate. Women younger than 18 years and those who did not speak French were excluded.

Outcomes

Our principal outcome was the prevalence of depression, measured by the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), with a discrimination threshold set at 11 and above according to Guedeney N et al., and Adouard F et al. [28, 29]. Our secondary outcomes were the mean EPDS score [28] and the prevalence and mean scores of the other two studied psychological disorders. We considered generalized anxiety disorders, measured by the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) with a discrimination threshold set at 7 [30], and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTDS), measured by the Revised Impact of Event Scale (IES-R) with a discrimination threshold set at 30 and above [31].

Data collection

Inclusion took place in the immediate postpartum period from April 27, 2017, through April 2, 2019. The volume of bleeding was quantified for vaginal births with graduated collection bags and for cesarean deliveries with a suction canister and by weighing the degree of blood loss. PPH prevention and management applied the French clinical practice guidelines [14].

The exposed group included women who had primary PPH (≥ 500 mL in the 24 h after delivery whatever the type of delivery), and the unexposed group included those without PPH. For each woman with PPH, we planned to recruit 2 women without it: those who gave birth immediately before and after the delivery of the woman with PPH [17]. If eather or both of the patients before and after delivery had a PPH or declined to be included, the women who gave birth just before or after those eligible, were offered to participate in the study.

The computerized medical files allowed us to collect social, demographic, medical, and obstetric data for the women and their children. To optimize exhaustiveness, this database was crossed with those of the hospital’s blood bank, radiology department, and local hospital patient discharge data summaries.

Self-administered questionnaires specific to the study were completed by the women immediately postpartum (on paper) and at 2, 6, and 12 months (online) These questionnaires asked about medication use and consultations with psychologists and/or psychiatrists.

They also included the three questionnaires described above, which were validated in French (Additional file 1). Non-respondents were e-mailed a reminder within one week with a reminder phone call after another two weeks.

To assess these women’s preexisting depressive and anxious characteristics, version 5 of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) [32] was completed at inclusion, M6, and M12. The Spielberger’s State-Trait Anxiety Inventory form Y-B (STAI form Y-B, threshold ≥ 48) [33] was used only at inclusion, and the STAI form Y-A (threshold ≥ 46) [33] was also completed at inclusion, M2, M6, and M12 (Additional file 1).

On the basis of the study by Thompson et al., who reported depression in 10% of unexposed and 16% of exposed women at 4 months postpartum [26], and including 2 women in the group without PPH, we required a sample of 1542 women—514 exposed and 1028 unexposed—for a power of 90%, with a bilateral alpha equal to 5%.

Data analysis

The social, demographic, medical, and perinatal data of both groups (exposed and unexposed) were described. Categorical variables were compared via chi-square (χ2) tests (or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate), and continuous variables were compared via Student’s t test (or the Mann‒Whitney test). Prevalence rates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for depression, anxiety, and PTSD were calculated at 2, 6, and 12 months to compare the PPH group with the no-PPH group for all deliveries and according to the type of delivery (cesarean or vaginal delivery).

The scores of each questionnaire were log transformed to obtain a normal distribution. The results are presented first as geometric means and compared between the groups at each study time point with Student’s t test (or the Mann‒Whitney test). A multivariate analysis with a manual backward stepwise generalized linear model was performed to study the associations between exposure and the scores obtained for each questionnaire. The adjusted geometric means were compared between the groups. The model included covariates known from the literature to be confounding factors (maternal age, body mass index, type of delivery) and clinically relevant prognostic factors (basis psychological context before the delivery). Because of an interaction in our multivariate model between cesareans and psychological disorders, the model was then stratified by type of birth (cesarean or not).

Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed with SAS software version 9, 2002–2010 (SAS Institute Inc.). The complete PSYCHE protocol has been published [17].

Ethical considerations

The French regional ethics review committee (Southeast VI) approved the study in February 2017 (IRB AU1243). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on 2017-04-27, with the unique identifying number: NCT03120208. Investigators provided eligible women a written information sheet and obtained their written consent before their discharge. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Results

During the study period, our maternity department had 7,274 births, including 887 with a PPH. The study ultimately included 1,298 participants; no delayed PPHs or suicides were observed. Among the women with a PPH, 819 were eligible, and 64.4% (n = 528) agreed to participate (528 women) (Additional file 2). At M2, M6, and M12, the participation rates were 61.3%, 45.3%, and 36.5%, respectively, in the exposed group (PPH) and 63.5%, 42.1%, and 36.9%, respectively, in the non-exposed group (No-PPH) (Additional file 2).

Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4 and Additional file 3 report the descriptive socioeconomic, medical, and obstetric and characteristics. Several variables differed between the groups of women with and without PPH. The women in the PPH group came more frequently from North Africa or Sub-Saharan Africa (p = 0.005) and had a lower rate of tertiary education than did those in the No-PPH group (Table 1). The women in the PPH group also had more histories of preterm birth (p = 0.0001) or a cesarean (p < 0.0001) and infertility than did the No-PPH women (Table 2). Compared with those in the No-PPH group, women in the PPH group had a singleton pregnancy (p < 0.0001), a spontaneous pregnancy (p = 0.0007) or a gestational age ≥ 37 weeks of gestation at delivery (Table 3) less frequently. Compared with that in the other groups, the onset of labor in the PPH group was more common due to induction or cesarean section. We noted more instrumental deliveries and cesareans in the PPH group than in the No-PPH group (p < 0.0001) (Table 4).

The mean blood loss volume was 922.0 ± 520 mL in the PPH group and 182.3 ± 110.4 mL in the No-PPH group (p < 0.0001). A second-line surgical procedure (defined as a B-Lynch suture, Cho suture, hypogastric arterial ligation, other vessel ligation, cervical suture, hysterectomy, uterine wound closure repair, or other surgery) and/or vascular arterial embolization were performed for 0.6% (n = 3) of the women with PPH. The group with PPH also had a 6.8% rate of packed red blood cell transfusions. A secondary surgical intervention (return to the delivery sector of a woman already transferred to postpartum care for reopening of the abdominal wall, scar revision, hematoma drainage, etc.) was performed for 13 women (2.5%) in the PPH group.

For antenatal psychological characteristics, the percentages of positive MINI scores for depression at inclusion, M6 and M12, between the PPH group and the No-PPH group were 12.6% vs. 16.3% (p = 0.07), 19.3% vs. 18.6% (p = 0.85), and 16.5% vs. 20.6% (p = 0.26), respectively (Table 4). At inclusion, the percentage of patients who were positive for anxiety according to the STAI Y-B was 41.9% in the PPH group and 48.2% in the No-PPH group (p = 0.04). The percentage of anxiety measured by the STAI Y-A was greater in the PPH group at inclusion, at M2 and M6, but not at M12: 18.1% vs. 10.3% (p = 0.01), 20.1% vs. 13.3% (p = 0.01), 16.9% vs. 14.5% (p = 0.45), and 11.8% vs. 14.5% (p = 0.40), respectively (Table 4). The percentages of women receiving psychological and/or psychiatric support in the groups with and without PPH at M2, M6, and M12 were, respectively, 6.4% vs. 2.9% (p = 0.01), 5.8% vs. 3.9% (p = 0.29), and 7.1% vs. 3.8% (p = 0.11) (Additional file 3).

The children’s descriptive characteristics saw more neonatal transfers in the PPH group than in the No-PPH group (p < 0.0001) but with no difference concerning the mean length of hospital stay (p = 0.31) (Table 4). We did not find a difference at 2, 6 and 12 months for the hospitalization of a child since returning home and the rate of breastfeeding (Additional file 4).

At M2, M6 and M12, the comparison of respondents and non-respondents, respectively, among women with PPH, and among women without PPH stressed that women were less often from France, had less a high education level, worked less during pregnancy and smoked more frequently among the non-respondents than among the respondents (results not shown). For the same comparisons at the same endpoints the neonatal data did not statistically differ (results not shown).

The prevalence rate of depression was higher in the PPH group than in the No-PPH group among all the deliveries at M2 (24.4 vs. 18.2%; p = 0.03) (Table 5). However, no statistically significant difference was observed for this prevalence of depression at 6 and 12 months. Elsewhere, no statistically significant difference was observed for the prevalence of depression at two and six months between the women with and without a PPH, regardless of the type of birth (Table 5). We noted that among the women with a vaginal birth, at 12 months, women with no-PPH had a greater prevalence of depression than women with a PPH did (15.9% vs. 6.2%; p = 0.03) (Table 5). The comparison of the least square depression means at the three study assessment points for the two types of delivery did not reveal a statistically significant difference between women with and without PPH (p > 0.05) (Table 5). After considering maternal age, body mass index (BMI), the GAD-7, the PTSD, and the MINI scores, the adjusted mean depression score was only higher at M6 among the women with a vaginal birth in the No-PPH group than in the PPH group (6.9 vs. 5.5; p = 0.02) (Table 5). The study’s posteriori power for the prevalence of depression at two months between the PPH group and the No-PPH group for all deliveries was 75%.

The prevalence and adjusted mean scores of anxiety in the two groups at two, six, and twelve months did not differ significantly between the women with PPH and the women without PPH for all deliveries and regardless of the type of birth (p > 0.05) (Table 5).

The prevalence of PTSD was higher at M2 in the PPH group compared to the No-PPH group (12.8% vs. 7.6%, p = 0.02) (Table 5). This last prevalence did not differ at M6 and M12. The prevalence of PTSD was significantly different only at M2 among the women with a cesarean section and those in the PPH group compared with the group with no-PPH (15.4% vs. 4.6%; p = 0.02). The least square PTSD means were significantly greater in the PPH group than in the No-PPH group, but only among women with a cesarean at M2 and at M12: 9.5 vs. 5.8 (p = 0.009) and 8.4 vs. 5.1 (p = 0.03), respectively (Table 5). After the GAD-7 and EPDS scores were considered, at M2 and M12, women with PPH had higher adjusted mean PTSD scores than women with no-PPH did in the case of cesarean delivery, the scores were 11.0 and 6.5 (p = 0.05) and 10.6 and 5.8 (p = 0.02), respectively (Table 5).

Discussion

We have stressed the high prevalence of the different disorders studied, regardless of exposure to PPH or not, and regardless of the type of birth, as late as one year after childbirth. The prevalence rate of depression was increased only at M2 in the PPH group compared to No-PPH group (24.4% vs. 18.2%, p = 0,03) (Table 5). The highest prevalence of postpartum depression was noted for women with PPH and a cesarean at M2 (25.9%). However, the only statistically significant difference between the 2 groups was noted at M12, where the prevalence was greater in the No-PPH group than in the PPH group but only for vaginal deliveries (15.9% vs. 6.2%; p = 0.03). The adjusted mean score for postpartum depression was only higher at six months for the No-PPH group in the case of a vaginal birth (6.9 vs. 5.5; p = 0.02). The prevalence of general anxiety disorders were not significantly different between the PPH group and No-PPH group at M2, M6 and M12 among all deliveries (Table 5). The prevalence and adjusted mean scores of general anxiety disorders were not significantly different between the two groups, regardless of the type of birth, at each postpartum assessment period. The PTSD was higher at M2 in the PPH group vs. the No-PPH group (12.8 vs. 7.6; p = 0.02) (Table 5) but not at M6 and M12. Compared with women with no-PPH, those with PPH had a greater prevalence of PTSD at two months after cesarean delivery (15.4% vs. 4.6%; p = 0.02). The adjusted mean PTSD scores at M2 and M12 were higher in the PPH group than in the No-PPH group, but only among the cesareans: 11.0 vs. 6.5 (p = 0.005) and 10.6 vs. 5.8 (p = 0.02), respectively.

Among women with PPH, the prevalence of postpartum depression (EPDS ≥ 11) at two months was 24.4% vs. 18.2% for the No-PPH group. These prevalence rates are close to those reported by Ricbourg et al. (15% at one month and 23.5% at three) [21] and higher than those reported by Eckerdal et al. (13.8% at six weeks) [22] and Thompson et al. (11% at two months and 13% at four) [26]. The different thresholds used for the EPDS may explain these differences. While a threshold ≥ 11 is recommended for the French version, the other studies used higher thresholds: Thompson et al. and Eckerdal et al. ≥12 [22, 26]. The definition of PPH also varied between the studies [21–23, 26, 34]. A recent French study did not find an association between severe PPH (blood loss > 1000 mL) and a depression at 2 months after a singleton vaginal delivery or cesarean at or near term [EPDS score ≥ 13] [35, 36]. For women without PPH or cesarean deliveries, long-term psychological consequences, such as developing depression at one year after birth, seem to be present. This could be linked not only to the exposure studied (PPH) but also to other potential confounding factors or clinical prognostic factors that might affect this prevalence (unplanned pregnancy, the women’s investment in the pregnancy, etc.).

Two months after delivery, the prevalence of GAD for anxiety (GAD-7 > 7) was 15.9% in the PPH group vs. 11.7% in the No-PPH group for all deliveries. This last prevalence was 16.2% after cesareans and 15.6% after vaginal births among women with PPH vs. 10.8% among those without PPH after cesarean birth and 11.8% after vaginal births. As no study has used the GAD-7 to evaluate this anxiety disorder among women with a PPH, comparison with the literature was difficult. A meta-analysis by Goodman et al. revealed an overall mean GAD prevalence of 3.59% (95% CI: 1.85–6.66) in a population of women during the postpartum period [6]. This prevalence was lower than our results. Diaz Ogallar et al. reported that 47.3% of women postpartum experienced anxiety (from mild to severe anxiety) [37]. They used the GAD-7, with a score ≥ 5 indicating the presence of anxiety, and they included women who had given birth less than 18 months prior and without subgroups for type of delivery or PPH.

At M2, the prevalence of PTSD (IES-R ≥ 30) among women with PPH was 12.8% for the PPH group vs 7.6% for the No-PPH group at 2 months. This prevalence was higher among the PPH group vs the No-PPH group, in case of a cesarean (15.4% vs 4.6%, p = 0.02) compared with 45% at one month and 23.5% at three months reported by Ricbourg et al. [21], 5% at two months reported by Thompson et al. [26], and 7.0% reported by van Steijn et al. [38] approximately 4–6 weeks postpartum. These differences in prevalence may be explained again by the choice of questionnaires used. Thompson et al. used the 17-item PTSD Checklist (PCL) [26]. Ricbourg et al. used the same scale and the same endpoint as we did: IES-R ≥ 30 [21]. The definitions of PPH differed between the studies [21, 26, 38], and the number of individuals was sometimes low [21]. It is possible that the PSTD rate is higher in patients with severe PPH or maternal morbidity [severe preeclampsia/eclampsia, Hemolysis Elevated Liver enzymes and Low Platelets (HELLP) syndrome, or admission to intensive care] than in those with non-severe PPH [39]. In a recent French study, a clinically significant PPH (defined by a positive answer by the attending surgeon to the question “was there a PPH?”) was associated with a PTSD after a cesarean (defined as IES-R Score Above the 95th Percentile at 2 Months after the Delivery) for the adjusted model 1: aOR 1.61 (95%CI: 1.04–2.46)( p = 0.03) and model 2: aOR 1.54 (95%CI: 1.01–2.39)(p = 0 .04) [40]. Among the vaginal deliveries at 2 months, postpartum hemorrhage of 1000 mL or more was also associated with a higher risk of PTSD: aOR 2.0 (95 CI: 1.0-4.2) [41].

As expected, our two groups differed in terms of known risk factors for PPH, specifically, the type of birth. We therefore adjusted the analyses for clinically pertinent prognostic factors (maternal age and body mass index) and stratified the analysis according to the type of delivery.

At M2 and M12, we found that the adjusted mean PTSD score was greater for women with PPH than for those without PPH, but only in the case of a cesarean section: 11.0% vs. 6.5% (p = 0.005) and 10.6% vs. 5.8% (p = 0.02), respectively. A meta-analysis revealed that, compared with women without a cesarean section, women with a cesarean section apparently have a higher rate of PTSD (10.7% pooled prevalence; 95% CI: 4.0–20.2) [42]. Our results suggested that PPH could increase the PTSD profile criteria in cases of cesarean section at M2, M6 and M12. The temporal proximity of the traumatic event, here the PPH, appears to increase the prevalence of PTSD at two months and thus suggests a response to the acute event for women with cesarean delivery.

Our work focused on two current major public health issues. Suicide is the leading cause of maternal death at one year postpartum (16.5%), in France [13]. Few publications have considered the psychological impact of immediate PPH, and as discussed in the introduction, all of them have methodological problems [21–24, 26]. Most of these studies are limited to postpartum depression [21–23].

Specific details about episodes of PTSD after a PPH are largely unknown, and the heterogeneity of the published studies has prevented meta-analysis [39]. The strengths of our study include its prospective design, non-exposed group, full year of follow-up after birth, and large sample size (n = 1298, including 528 with PPH). Our participation rate was higher than that of other studies at two months (61.3% for the PPH group and 63.5% for the No-PPH group) [21, 22]. The participation rate in our non-exposed group was higher than that in the French national survey (n = 7133 women): 63.5% vs. 58.7% [43].

Our study’s first limitation was its single-center design, which could have induced selection bias. Nonetheless, our hospital is the only level 3 maternity department in the Auvergne region and receives most pregnant women at known risk of PPH. Health care during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period is free of charge in France. The second limitation was the difficulty of recruiting unexposed women (770 recruited vs. 1028 expected). For this reason, the protocol planned to recruit two unexposed women for every woman with PPH. However, when 514 exposed women were recruited, we had to stop the study due to the method used to recruit non-exposed women. The third limitation of our study was an attrition bias (we had more women with a low socio-economic status among the non-respondents than respondents in the PPH group and in the control group at M2, M6 and M12) in a context of an important loss to follow-up over time in the two groups, which could result in overestimating the prevalence of psychological disorders in each group. Indeed, women who feel well might find themselves increasingly less interested in the study as the months passed. This was probably not the case for women with postpartum psychological distress, as the co-parent was informed of the study and the investigators’ availability if needed. The fourth limitation was the use of self-administered questionnaires rather than a psychiatric interview to diagnose psychological disorders. The questionnaires have been validated in French, and it would not have been feasible to have mental health providers interview the women. Most likely, women would have not well accepted this medical research appointment, in one part, and, in other parts, his medical intervention is very expensive. However, the questionnaires selected for our study are commonly used by health-care providers as screening tools for psychological disorders. The fifth limitation was that we did not perform a stratified analysis on whether the severity of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) induced more anxiety and PTSD. Our protocol did not include this secondary analysis, and we do not have the power for this sub-analysis (24,37). The last limitation of our study was that, for ethical reasons, we offered a free appointment with a psychologist or a psychiatrist for women identified at high risk of psychological disorders during the study; this might have reduced the prevalence of the studied psychological disorders at M6 and M12.

Conclusion

The high levels of positive screens found among women with PPH or without PPH at all-time points for postpartum depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress warrant systematic screening by perinatal providers during the extended postpartum period, regardless of PPH status. Indeed, systematic reviews provide evidence that psychosocial and psychological interventions reduce the incidence of postnatal depression. Interventions that are individually-based and initiated in the post-partum may be beneficial [44]. Further studies, such as multicenter or qualitative studies, are needed to determine the critical parts of the care process for each parturient and to assess what types of additional structured support might be appropriate and effective. Elsewhere, further studies should assess the impact of a national screening program using validated instruments conducted by perinatal health-care providers.
