Cold atmospheric plasma mediates antibiofilm activity and sensitization to further plasma and antibiotic challenges against Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Elena Capuzzo, Guillem Mas Fiol, Nadège Cayet, Hugo Varet, Javier Pizarro-Cerda, Nadira Frescaline, Antoine Rousseau, Olivier Dussurget
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Staphylococcus aureus biofilms pose significant challenges in clinical settings due to their growing resistance to conventional antibiotics. While cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) is known for its bactericidal effects, its impact on biofilm architecture and physiology, and subsequent antimicrobial susceptibility remains poorly understood. This study investigates the effects of CAP treatment on S. aureus biofilms. We demonstrate that CAP not only reduces biofilm but also disrupts biofilm architecture and alters bacterial cell morphology. Transcriptomic analysis reveals distinct gene expression profiles following treatments with CAP and hydrogen peroxide, one of its active components, highlighting unique stress responses. We further show that CAP treatment of S. aureus grown on agar plates sensitizes bacteria to additional CAP exposure and induces genomic alterations involved in cell envelope integrity, defence mechanisms and DNA replication, recombination, and repair. Importantly, CAP treatment enhances bacterial susceptibility to the topical antibiotic for wound care mupirocin. These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying CAP-mediated biofilm control and offer a promising strategy to potentiate existing antimicrobial therapies.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is an opportunistic bacterial pathogen capable of producing a wide arsenal of virulence factors that facilitates immune evasion and infection (Cheung et al. 2021, Di Bella et al. 2025). It is the leading bacterial cause of death worldwide after Mycobacterium tuberculosis, being associated with more than one million deaths every year (Ikuta et al. 2022). Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) strains account for around 10% of these deaths (Murray et al. 2022), with MRSA-related mortality having increased by 90% over the past 30 years (Naghavi et al. 2024). Beyond antibiotic resistance, the ability of S. aureus to form biofilms further complicates eradication. Biofilms are structured bacterial communities embedded in a self-produced extracellular matrix that shields bacteria from host immune defences and impedes the penetration of antimicrobials. Within biofilms, bacterial cells adopt a metabolically dormant state that renders them even less susceptible to antibiotics (Koo et al. 2017). As a consequence, biofilm-associated S. aureus infections are notoriously difficult to eradicate and often persist despite aggressive antimicrobial therapy (Ciofu et al. 2022).

Clinically, S. aureus causes a wide spectrum of diseases, ranging from minor superficial ailments to life-threatening invasive infections. It is a primary cause of skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs), which represent one of the most common infectious disease burdens (Ikuta et al. 2022). Importantly, S. aureus is also a leading cause of mortality in severe burn victims (Yali et al. 2014, Maslova et al. 2021), where wound colonization rapidly progresses to invasive infection. In these contexts, biofilm formation on wound surfaces promotes bacterial persistence and complicates healing (Percival et al. 2015).

The wide array of virulence factors, the capacity for adaptation that confers tolerance to antibiotics, and the ability to form biofilms make S. aureus infections increasingly difficult to eradicate with conventional therapies. Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) represents an innovative tool that has emerged to fight infection and antibiotic resistance (Scholtz et al. 2021, Das et al. 2022, Oliver et al. 2024). CAP is an ionized gas at near-room temperature that generates a mixture of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), through interactions between the gas plasma and ambient air. These include short-lived species (e.g. hydroxyl radical and superoxide anion radical) and long-lived species, including hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), nitrite (NO2-) and nitrate (NO3-), which differ in stability and reactivity (Takamatsu et al. 2014). Together with the chemical components, physical components such as UV light and electromagnetic fields are present in CAP. Although the ultraviolet contribution to antimicrobial activity is limited, it may enhance the reactivity of other molecules (Privat-Maldonado et al. 2016, Estifaee et al. 2019). In contrast, electric fields and charged particles appear to contribute to membrane damage in bacterial cells (Dezest et al. 2017, Estifaee et al. 2019). These characteristics highlight CAP multifactorial antimicrobial activity. CAP has been shown to exert broad-spectrum bactericidal effects, including against multidrug-resistant strains (Han et al. 2016, Mai-Prochnow et al. 2016, Connor et al. 2017, Brun et al. 2018). In addition to its antimicrobial effects, CAP is capable of promoting wound healing through stimulation of tissue and immune modulation (Choi et al. 2017, Arndt et al. 2018, Strohal et al. 2022). Evidence from randomized controlled clinical trials indicates that patients with burns or chronic wounds who received CAP treatment required fewer doses of analgesics and antibiotics and experienced an overall improvement in quality of life (Abu Rached et al. 2023, Rodríguez-Ferreyra et al. 2025). Along this line, we recently demonstrated that CAP accelerated wound healing in a burn mouse model infected with S. aureus, significantly reducing bacterial burden and promoting tissue repair (Blaise et al. 2024).

Given the clinical importance of S. aureus biofilms in wound infections, understanding how CAP impacts S. aureus at the molecular and structural level may contribute to the development of new strategies for the management of biofilm-associated infections, including burn wound infections, where innovative treatments are urgently needed. In this study, our objective was to investigate the capacity of CAP to control S. aureus and its biofilms, to decipher its mechanisms of action and to explore its synergy with antibiotics.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and growth conditions

Staphylococcus aureus USA300, a virulent MRSA (strain ATCC BAA-1717), was used in this study. Bacteria were cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Difco) supplemented with 1% (w/v) glucose (TSBG) at pH 7.3, with shaking at 180 rpm, at 37°C under aerobic conditions. Overnight cultures were used for downstream experiments, including CAP antibiofilm assay in 24-well plates and on agar plates.

Plasma jet device

CAP treatment was performed using a helium-based plasma jet device. Helium (He) gas was supplied at a flow rate of 500 standard cubic centimetres per minute (sccm), regulated by a mass flow controller. When a 10 kHz AC voltage of 8 kV was applied, a 200 mW CAP was ignited. The distance between the plasma jet nozzle and the target was set to 10 mm. The plasma propagated beyond the nozzle through a helium-air mixture toward the target. Biofilms were exposed to either CAP or helium (control) for 2 min or 10 min.

Hydrogen peroxide quantification

H₂O₂ generated during plasma treatment was quantified using Pierce Quantitative Peroxide Assay Kit™ (ThermoFisher Scientific). Briefly, 50 µl of phosphate-buffered saline 1x (PBS, Gibco) used in the CAP antibiofilm assay was treated with CAP for 2 min, collected immediately after exposure and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. H₂O₂ concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated with known concentrations of H₂O₂.

Biofilm eradication and viability assay

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.05 in fresh TSBG. Aliquots of 500 µl were dispensed into a 24-well sterile polystyrene plate (TPP, Deutscher) and incubated statically at 37°C for 24 h to allow biofilm formation. After incubation, planktonic cells were removed, and the wells were washed three times with 500 μl of PBS 1x. Before CAP exposure, 50 μl of PBS 1x was left in each well to prevent biofilm desiccation during treatment. Biofilms were then exposed either to helium or CAP for 2 min or 10 min or treated with 700 μM of H2O2 (Sigma Aldrich). The distance between the CAP nozzle and the bottom of the plate was kept at 10 mm.

After treatment, biofilms were gently washed once with 500 µl of PBS 1x and resuspended in 500 µl of PBS 1x by vigorous pipetting. Serial dilutions were plated on Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, Difco) agar to determine colony-forming units (CFU). For 2-min and H2O2 treatments (including the helium control), CFU counts were obtained from five technical replicates for each of five biological replicates. For 10-min treatments, CFU counts were obtained from three technical replicates for each of three biological replicates. To generate a single representative value for each biological replicate, the CFU counts from its respective technical replicates were averaged. These averaged CFU values then served as the independent data points for subsequent statistical analysis.

Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the five biological replicate values for 2-min treatments and H2O2 conditions, and the three biological replicate values for 10-min treatments. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparison test to compare CFU counts across all experimental conditions. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.

Confocal microscopy

Biofilms were prepared and treated as described in the biofilm eradication and viability assay. After He or CAP treatment, biofilms were stained with 500 µl of Filmtracer™ LIVE/DEAD™ Biofilm Viability Kit (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific). After 30 min, samples were rinsed with PBS to remove the excess stain. Biofilms were imaged with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica). Images were analyzed with ImarisViewer 10.2.0. Biofilm viability was assessed using ImageJ (Fiji version 2.16.0). Four replicates per condition were performed, acquiring three regions of interest (ROI) per sample. For both helium and CAP-treated samples, imaging was performed at the centre of the wells, where CAP is targeted. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparison test; a P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.

Scanning electron microscopy

Biofilms were prepared and treated as described in the biofilm viability assay. After He, CAP or H2O2 treatment, biofilms were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma) in 1× PHEM buffer overnight at 4°C. Samples were washed three times in 1× PHEM buffer and post-fixed for 1 h in the dark in 1% OsO4 (Electron Microscopy Sciences) in water. After three washes in water, samples were post-stained with 1% uranyl acetate in 25% ethanol. Samples were gradually dehydrated through an ethanol series (50%, 75%, 95%, 3 × 100%) and put on CPD 300 (critical point dryer with CO2) from Leica. Before observation by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a JEOL IT700HR and SED detector, samples were metalized with 10 nm AU/Pd using an ACE 600 from Leica. Three replicates per condition were performed.

RNA extraction

Biofilms for RNA extraction were formed and treated as described above for the eradication assay. After treatment, biofilms were resuspended in ice-cold PBS and pelleted by refrigerated centrifugation. Pellets from identical conditions were pooled and resuspended in RNA later™ solution (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific) to stabilize RNA. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen) with modifications for efficient cell lysis. Pellets were resuspended in RLT buffer and transferred to Lysing Matrix B tubes (MP Biomedical), then processed in a Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin technologies) for 3 × 30 s cycles, with tubes cooled on ice between cycles. Following the mechanical disruption, the protocol of Qiagen was followed. Residual DNA was removed using Ambion™ TURBO DNA-free kit (Invitrogen, Thermofisher). DNA removal was confirmed by PCR and Qubit quantification. RNA quality and integrity were assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer using the RNA 6000 Nano kit (Agilent), and RNA integrity number (RIN) was determined. Biological triplicate samples for each strain were then diluted to equal RNA concentrations.

RNA sequencing

Libraries were prepared from 200 ng of total RNA using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus kit (Illumina) with custom probes targeting S. aureus, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were amplified with 12 PCR cycles. And unbound adapters and primers were removed by two successive purifications using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) at a 0.8:1 bead-to-sample ratio. The resulting libraries contained fragments ranging from 200 to 1000 bp, with a peak around 450 bp, as determined using a 5300 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were then pooled, diluted to 0.75 nM, and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 system (Illumina) using a P3 50-cycle kit to generate 67-base single-end reads (4–5 million reads per sample).

RNA-seq analysis

The RNA-seq analysis was performed with Sequana 0.18.0 (Cokelaer et al. 2017). We used the RNA-seq pipeline 0.20.0 (https://github.com/sequana/sequana_rnaseq) built on top of Snakemake 7.32.4 (Köster and Rahmann 2012). Briefly, reads were trimmed from adapters using Fastp (Chen et al. 2018), then mapped to the S. aureus genome assembly CP020619.1 from NCBI using bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). FeatureCounts (Liao et al. 2014) was used to produce the count matrix, assigning reads to features using the corresponding annotation with strand-specificity information. Quality control statistics were summarized using MultiQC 1.17 (Ewels et al. 2016). Expression data were analyzed using R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team 2013), and the Bioconductor package DESeq2 version 1.42.1 (Love et al. 2014). The normalization and dispersion estimations were performed with DESeq2 using the default parameters and statistical tests for differential expression were performed applying the independent filtering algorithm. A generalized linear model including the replicate effect as blocking factor was set in to test for the differential expression between He, CAP, and H2O2. For each pairwise comparison, log2(Fold-Changes) where shrunk using the ashr (Stephens 2016) method and raw P-values were adjusted for multiple testing according to the Benjamini and Hochberg (BH) procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Genes with an adjusted P-value lower than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

CAP assay on agar plates and assessment of CAP-surviving bacteria

Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 1:100 in sterile Ultrapure™ distilled water. 5 ml of culture were spread on BHI plate (parental strain). After 30 s, any liquid not absorbed by the agar was carefully removed, and the plates were incubated for 30 min at 37°C to let them dry. Plates were treated with either He or CAP for 2 min or 10 min, followed by incubation at 37°C for 24 h. The next day, zones of growth inhibition were measured, plates were imaged using a Scan 500 (Interscience), and inhibition areas were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ). From plates treated for 10 min, three colonies which survived CAP treatment were randomly selected from the inhibition zone and sub-cultured for further analysis; these were designated first-passage strains (FP1, FP2, FP3). Each FP strain was independently grown overnight, and 5 ml of cultures were spread on fresh BHI agar plates and exposed again to CAP under the same conditions (2 or 10-min exposure). After 24 h at 37°C, three colonies per FP strain which survived CAP treatment were randomly selected from the inhibition zone, resulting in nine second-passage strains (SP1.a–c, SP2.a–c, SP3.a–c). These nine second-passage clones were isolated solely for genomic sequencing and characterization and were not subjected to further CAP exposure after isolation. Each condition was tested in three independent biological replicates (separate overnight cultures and plates prepared on different days), each with three technical replicates (individual plates per condition).

Statistical analysis of growth inhibition zones

The growth inhibition zone was measured from three independent biological replicates (separate overnight cultures and plates prepared on different days), each with three technical replicates (individual plates per condition). To generate a single representative value for each biological replicate, the inhibition zone of growth from these technical replicates was averaged. These values served as the independent data points for subsequent statistical analysis. Results are presented as the mean ± standard error. Statistical analysis was performed using Welch’s t-test. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.

DNA sequencing and genotypic analysis of CAP-surviving bacteria

DNA was extracted from the parental strain, all three first-passage strains, and all nine second-passage strains. For each strain, 2 ml of the overnight culture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Pellets were resuspended in RLT buffer and transferred to Lysing Matrix B tubes (MP Biomedical), then processed in a Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin technologies) for 3 × 30 s cycles, with tubes cooled on ice between cycles. Following the mechanical disruption, DNA was extracted using the Purelink™ Genomic DNA MiniKit (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were identified using Snippy v4.6.0 (Seeman T., https://github.com/tseemann/snippy) with S. aureus genome assembly CP020619.1 from NCBI, as the reference genome. Functional annotation of protein-coding genes in the B7H15 genome was performed using eggnog-mapper v2.1.2 based on Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories. Data analysis and visualization were performed in R (v4.4.2) within RStudio. Enrichment of COG functions among SNV-affected genes was assessed using Fisher’s exact test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR), with an adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

DNA sequencing and genotypic analysis He-treated bacteria

For SNV analysis of helium-treated bacteria, overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 1:106 in sterile Ultrapure™ distilled water. Five millilitres of bacterial suspensions were spread on BHI plate (parental strain_He). From plates treated for 10 min with helium, three colonies from the area where He hits the plate were randomly selected and sub-cultured for further analysis; these were designated first-passage strains (FP1_He, FP2_He, FP3_He). Each FP strain was independently grown overnight, and 5 ml cultures were spread on fresh BHI agar plates and exposed again to He for 10-min. After 24 h at 37°C, three colonies per FP strain from the area hit by He were randomly selected, resulting in nine second-passage strains (SP1.a–c_He, SP2.a–c_He, SP3.a–c_He). These nine second-passage clones were isolated for genomic sequencing and characterization and were not subjected to further He exposure after isolation. DNA sequencing and genotypic analysis were performed according to the method described above in the section DNA sequencing and genotypic analysis of CAP-surviving bacteria.

Synergy between CAP and mupirocin

Plates were prepared and treated as described above. After exposure to CAP or He alone, a 200 µg mupirocin disk (Sigma–Aldrich PHR2806) was placed on the surface of each agar plate. Plates were then incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The following day, zones of growth inhibition were measured, plates were photographed using a Scan 500 and inhibition areas were quantified using Fiji (ImageJ). The experiment was performed in four independent biological replicates (separate overnight cultures and freshly prepared plates on different days), each including three technical replicates (individual plates per condition). To generate a single representative value for each biological replicate, the growth inhibition zone from these technical replicates was averaged. These values then served as the independent data points for subsequent statistical analysis. Results are presented as the mean ± standard error. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA to compare inhibition zones between treatment conditions. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.

Results

CAP reduces S. aureus biofilm formed in vitro

To evaluate the antibiofilm activity of CAP, MRSA biofilms were treated for either 2 min or 10 min with He (control) or with CAP (Fig. 1A). Since reactive oxygen species (ROS) are considered the main active components of CAP against bacteria, we also compared 2-min CAP activity with that of H₂O₂. After 2 min of CAP treatment, the generated H₂O₂ concentration in PBS was around 700 μM (Table S1). CAP treatment significantly reduced biofilm, as evidenced by the lower numbers of bacteria compared to those of helium control (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1). H₂O₂ treatment led to a modest reduction of biofilm, which did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1B). The 10-min CAP treatment produced ∼1 Log10 reduction, exceeding the effects of both the 2-min CAP and H₂O₂ treatments. As expected, H₂O₂ and 2-min CAP treatments showed comparable antibiofilm activity (Fig. S1). The 10-min treatment likely allowed accumulation of longer-lived reactive species (Han et al. 2016), which may have caused more extensive cellular damage and thus greater biofilm reduction.

The antibacterial activity of CAP was confirmed by fluorescence labelling and confocal microscopy (Fig. 1C–E). Both 2-min (Fig. 1C, D, right panel, and video 1) and 10-min (Fig. 1C, E, right panel, and video 3) CAP treatments induced greater cell death compared to He treatment for 2-min (Fig. 1C, D, left panel, and video 2), and 10-min (Fig. 1C, E, left panel, and video 4).

CAP alters biofilm architecture and S. aureus morphology

To better understand how CAP and H₂O₂ treatments impact biofilm structure, we next examined their architecture by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Biofilm treated for 2 min with CAP displayed a distinctive “fishing net-like pattern” (Fig. 2B, E). This pattern was not visible in helium-treated biofilm, which showed regular S. aureus clusters (Fig. 2A, D). In contrast, H₂O₂-treated biofilm was composed of heterogeneous clusters without any organizational pattern (Fig. 2C, F). Additionally, bacterial density was higher in helium-treated biofilm than in CAP or H₂O₂-treated biofilms (Fig. 2A–F).

CAP alters S. aureus biofilm structure and bacterial morphology. Biofilms were treated for 2 min with He (A, D, G, J), CAP (B, E, H, K), or 700 µM H₂O₂ (C, F, I, L) and imaged by SEM. The red arrow indicates the ring-shaped structure of the CAP treated biofilm (K), the green arrow indicates the perforation of the envelope, and the blue arrow indicates fissures on the cell wall of H₂O₂-treated biofilm (L). Imaging was performed using 3 biological replicates per condition.

At higher magnifications, more dead bacteria and cellular debris were visible in biofilms treated with CAP or H₂O₂ (Fig. 2H, I), compared to biofilm treated with helium, which showed typical spherical cells isolated or in clusters and rare debris (Fig. 2G). Notably, CAP treatment resulted in a greater abundance of dead bacteria than H₂O₂ treatment (Fig. 2H, I). In addition, the morphology of dead cells differed between the two treatments. In H₂O₂-treated biofilms, cells exhibited perforations in the envelope (Fig. 2L), whereas in CAP-treated biofilms, cells displayed a characteristic ring-shaped structure (Fig. 2K). Furthermore, fissures were observed on the surface of H₂O₂-treated cells, a feature absent in both helium- and CAP-treated cells (Fig. 2L). The extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) also exhibited a distinct aspect in biofilms treated with CAP compared with biofilms treated with H2O2, further contributing to the observed architectural differences (Fig. S2).

These structural findings are in line with the reduction in bacterial survival upon CAP and H₂O₂ treatments determined by quantitative assays (Fig. S1). The peculiar “fishing net” pattern and the morphology of dead cells observed after CAP treatment highlight that CAP induces structural alterations in the biofilm and bacterial envelope that differ from those caused by H₂O₂. This is also supported by the absence of fissures on CAP-treated cells and by the different aspect of the EPS, which could indicate a mode of action distinct from oxidative damage caused by H₂O₂.

CAP and H₂O₂ treatments of S. aureus biofilms induce different transcriptional responses

We next asked whether differences between CAP-treated and H₂O₂-treated biofilms observed at the microscopic level were also reflected at the transcriptional level. RNA-seq was performed on total RNA extracted from S. aureus biofilms treated for 2 min with helium, CAP or 700 µM H₂O₂. 16 genes were differentially expressed between CAP- and He-treated biofilms (Fig. 3A). CAP treatment led to higher levels of 11 transcripts and lower levels of 5 transcripts compared to He treatment. In contrast, comparison between H₂O₂- and He-treated biofilms identified 701 differentially expressed genes (Fig. S3, Table S2), including, as expected, the catalase (Avican et al. 2021).

To gain insight into the biological functions of these genes, we used the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification system (Table S3). This system categorizes proteins from complete genomes into functional groups based on their evolutionary relationships, thereby allowing inference of gene function from conserved orthologs. According to this classification, 12 of the differentially expressed genes were assigned to the function unknown category, while the remaining genes encoded a putative acetyl esterase/lipase and the glutamine synthetase repressor (GlnR), both belong to the transcription category, the lipoteichoic acid synthase (LtaS) classified in cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis, and N-acetylneuraminate lyase (NanA) associated with both amino acid transport and metabolism and cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis.

Strikingly, the expression level of these genes exhibited an opposite pattern between CAP– and H₂O₂–treated biofilms: genes with higher expression levels under CAP treatment showed lower expression under H₂O₂, and the reverse was also observed, except for transcripts of putative acetyl esterase/lipase and lipoteichoic acid synthase (Fig. 3B). Indeed, the majority of genes more expressed upon CAP vs He treatment was less expressed upon H₂O₂ vs He treatment and vice versa (Fig. 3C). The different transcriptional responses of biofilms treated with CAP or H₂O₂ was further confirmed by the direct comparison of H₂O₂ vs CAP, in which 946 genes were differently expressed, 479 were up-regulated and 467 were down-regulated (Fig. S3).

The limited transcriptional response to 2-min CAP treatment, despite significant bacterial killing, suggests rapid damage that might prevent an immediate and widespread response to the stress induced by CAP. The contrasting gene expression profiles of biofilms treated with CAP or H₂O₂ reveal that mechanisms of CAP antibiofilm activity extend beyond the generation of oxidative stress.

CAP treatment sensitizes S. aureus to further CAP exposure

Given the antibacterial effect of CAP and the high adaptive capacity of bacteria, we next explored whether bacteria that survived CAP exposure could develop resistance to subsequent CAP treatments. Bacteria were spread onto agar plates and treated with either helium or CAP for 2 or 10 min (Fig. 4A). CAP treatment produced a clear zone of growth inhibition, whose size increased with treatment duration (Fig. 4B). In contrast, helium treatment did not affect bacterial growth (Fig. 4B). After the 10-min CAP treatment, a few surviving clones were detected within the treated area (Fig. 4B). These clones were re-exposed to helium or CAP for 2 or 10 min (Fig. 4C). As in the initial assay, helium treatment did not affect growth, while CAP inhibited bacterial growth, suggesting that bacteria did not develop resistance. Once again, a few clones survived the 10-min treatment with CAP (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, comparison of the inhibition zones between the first and second CAP exposures revealed that the halos after re-exposure were larger following 10-min treatment, indicating that the population stemming from surviving clones had become more sensitive to CAP (Fig. 4D).

CAP induces diverse genomic alterations affecting cell envelope and defense mechanisms-related genes

To investigate why a second 10-min treatment sensitized bacteria to CAP, their DNA was extracted and whole-genome sequencing was performed to identify potential CAP-induced genetic changes. A total of 310 single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were identified across 90 genes. Some genes contained multiple SNVs, and 13 SNVs were in intergenic regions. The main variant types were synonymous (105 SNVs) and missense (86 SNVs) mutations (Fig. 4E). A consistent mutation pattern could not be identified across all clones: most SNVs identified in first-passage strains were absent in second-passage strains, and vice versa. Only 19 genes carried SNVs in more than one clone (Fig. S4), suggesting that CAP treatment led to a broad-ranging non-specific mutational pattern. For comparison, genomic analysis of bacteria treated with helium alone revealed a total of 18 SNVs across only 8 genes (Fig. S5A-B). Unlike the broad-ranging pattern observed with CAP, these helium-induced SNVs were largely conserved between first-treatment and second-treatment strains (Fig. S5B). This difference in both number and pattern of variants underscores that extensive and non-specific mutation are specifically induced by CAP treatment.

To explore functional impacts of these genetic changes, genes carrying SNVs were assigned to COG categories, and their distribution was compared to that of the entire S. aureus genome (Fig. 4F). Notably, the cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis, defence mechanisms and replication, recombination, and repair categories were significantly enriched, indicating that changes in bacterial structures and pathways known to be critical for bacterial survival in response to stress were overrepresented in S. aureus after CAP treatment. Interestingly, SNVs in replication, recombination, and repair genes were mainly found in first-passage clones. On the other hand, SNVs in cell wall- and defence-related genes were more frequent in second-passage clones (Table S4). In contrast, no significant enrichment in any COG category was observed for genes carrying SNVs in helium-treated bacteria (Fig. S5C, Table S5).

These results suggest that CAP causes distinct genetic changes in multiple types of genes, including those related to the cell envelope, especially lipoproteins. Such mutations may alter cell envelope composition and/or integrity, potentially influencing bacterial response to subsequent exposure to CAP.

Pre-treatment with CAP increases the susceptibility of S. aureus to mupirocin

Given that CAP sensitizes S. aureus to CAP re-exposure, we next asked whether CAP could sensitize S. aureus to exposure to conventional antibiotics. Mupirocin was selected as it is a topical antibiotic widely clinically used to treat S. aureus wound infections. Staphylococcus aureus grown on BHI agar plates was first exposed to helium or CAP for 2 or 10 min, with or without 200 µg of mupirocin (Fig. 5A). Combination of a 10-min CAP pre-treatment, with the mupirocin led to an inhibition zone that was significantly larger than that upon treatment with helium + mupirocin, mupirocin alone, or CAP alone (Fig. 5C, E). A similar but nonsignificant trend was observed after the 2-min CAP treatment (Fig. 5B, D). These results support the hypothesis that CAP induces cellular damage that may facilitate antibiotic penetration and/or potentiate the effect of mupirocin.

Discussion

Our findings show that CAP exerts a multifaceted impact on S. aureus, exhibiting antibiofilm properties and immediate bactericidal activity that persists beyond initial exposure. While CAP and H₂O₂ reduced biofilm survival to similar levels, their impacts on biofilm architecture and cell morphology were clearly distinct, suggesting that CAP acts through mechanisms that cannot be explained by oxidative mechanisms alone. Here, we showed that CAP reduces biofilm formed by MRSA USA300 strain in a treatment time-dependent manner (Fig. 1, S1). Similarly, Guo et al., reported a different efficacy of CAP treatment, depending on the treatment time (Guo et al. 2021). However, the exact mode of action of CAP remains unclear, given that it delivers a complex mixture of short and long-lived RONS, together with physical effects such as UV radiation, charged particles, and electric fields. To begin dissecting this complexity, we compared CAP with H₂O₂ as a representative long-lived ROS. H₂O₂ is known to contribute to the bactericidal activity of CAP; indeed, studies on Escherichia coli have shown that the addition of catalase immediately after CAP exposure restores bacterial survival, highlighting the role of H₂O₂ among CAP-generated species (Joshi et al. 2011, Nicol et al. 2020). Additionally, reactive oxygen species have been shown to play an important role in removal of polymers by non-thermal atmospheric plasma jets, which could have a similar activity on biofilms (Fricke et al. 2011). Although both treatments reduced S. aureus survival (Fig. S1), SEM imaging revealed clear differences in biofilm disruption and bacterial cell morphology (Fig. 2). Interestingly, biofilm structural alterations induced by CAP resembled the “fishing-net” biofilm pattern reported when S. aureus biofilms grown on PEDOT-C60 were exposed to visible light for 1 h (Reynoso et al. 2021). This similarity raises the possibility that light, physical components or other ROS produced by CAP, contribute significantly to its antibiofilm activity, in addition to H₂O₂. Mechanistically, computational investigations of ROS interactions with bacterial cell wall components have shown that OH, H, and O₃ act differently compared to H₂O₂. Specifically, OH, H, and O₃ are more efficient at breaking C–O, C–C, and C–N bonds in peptidoglycan, whereas H₂O₂ is less effective and unable to break C–N bonds (Yusupov et al. 2013). This differential chemical activity provides a plausible explanation for the distinct morphologies observed in bacterial cells following CAP versus H₂O₂ treatment and highlights the multifactorial nature of CAP-induced stress.

Despite CAP having clear bactericidal activity (Fig. 1), transcriptomic analysis revealed only a mild response of S. aureus to CAP, with a mere 16 genes differentially expressed compared to helium (Fig. 3). One could hypothesize that the severe structural damage observed by SEM (Fig. 2) prevents bacteria from mounting a broad adaptive response. Interestingly, in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, significant transcriptional changes become apparent 4 h after CAP exposure and increase further at 6 h post-treatment (Maybin et al. 2023). Therefore, surviving S. aureus subpopulations presumably require time to adapt and exhibit a wider transcriptional response. Of note, CAP has been shown to cause protein damage besides its effect at its transcriptional level (Guo et al. 2021).

Notably, one of the most affected genes was ltaS, encoding lipoteichoic acid (LTA) synthase. This enzyme is responsible for the synthesis of LTA, essential cell wall polymers and its repression is expected to reduce LTA production, leading to abnormal cell morphology, impaired cell division, and growth defects (Gründling and Schneewind 2007, Richter et al. 2013, Chee Wezen et al. 2022). In parallel, upregulation of glnR, encoding glutamine synthetase (GS) repressor, points to reduced GS activity, limiting glutamine availability. Beyond altering nitrogen metabolism, this would also impair peptidoglycan amidation, weakening the cell wall structure and reducing methicillin resistance, as well as biofilm (Gustafson et al. 1994, Travis et al. 2022). Downregulation of N-acetylneuraminate lyase NanA, further suggests that CAP-exposed bacteria downregulated sialic acid utilization and associated carbon fluxes (Olson et al. 2013). Together, these transcriptional changes indicate a metabolic slowdown combined with weakened cell envelope integrity, two features that would compromise the capacity of surviving bacteria to recover and defend against stress.

At single cells levels, whole-genome sequencing of clones surviving CAP-treatment revealed diverse SNVs across multiple functional categories without a consistent mutational signature (Fig. 4). This pleiotropic mutational profile suggests that CAP exerts broad, non-specific stresses, lowering the likelihood of resistance development. In stark contrast, bacteria treated with helium alone exhibited a much lower number of SNVs, and these variants were largely conserved across passages (Fig. S5). Interestingly, re-exposure experiments revealed that a 10-min CAP treatment increased bacterial susceptibility, whereas a 2-min treatment did not induce any measurable genomic or CAP susceptibility changes. The more pronounced effects of longer exposure to CAP likely reflect the cumulative action of both short- and long-lived RONS together with physical factors such as UV and electric fields, resulting in more extensive damage. In contrast, a 2-min treatment with CAP may only produce sublethal effects in a small subset of cells, insufficient to generate detectable genomic or CAP susceptibility shifts. This treatment time-dependent effect was consistent with CFU counts, which showed greater reduction after prolonged CAP exposure. Importantly, the absence of resistance development was also reported by Lunder et al., who showed that after 10 generations of sequential exposure of MRSA to CAP, the inhibition zone remained unchanged (Lunder et al. 2024) and by Brun et al., who exposed S. aureus to CAP for seven consecutive days without detecting any increase in CFU (Brun et al. 2018).

Whole-genome sequencing also showed that CAP is associated with various SNVs across several functional categories. Specifically, functional analysis revealed a notable enrichment of mutations in genes related to the cell envelope, defence mechanisms, and replication/recombination/repair. This characteristic was absent in helium-treated clones, whose genes with SNVs did not show any significant enrichment in specific COG categories. In CAP-treated clones, several SNVs occurred in tandem lipoprotein genes, which are critical for membrane integrity, and alterations in the membrane could also affect nutrient transport and signal transduction (Nguyen et al. 2020). Some of these tandem lipoprotein genes are located in the νSAα island, and they are involved in the invasion process of non-phagocytic cells (Nguyen et al. 2015). Additionally, mutations were identified in genes involved in aggregation and biofilm formation, including fibronectin-binding proteins and clumping factor B. These proteins are important for surface adhesion, intracellular interaction and biofilm matrix stability (Foster et al. 2014, McCourt et al. 2014). Mutations in these genes may reduce the structural integrity and cohesiveness of cell communities grown on agar, facilitating penetration of ROS and antibiotics. Thus, CAP not only exerts direct bactericidal effects but could also induce subtle alterations that potentially weaken bacterial fitness, rendering cells more vulnerable to subsequent stresses. As variants/alterations in cell wall integrity, metabolism and DNA stability emerge after CAP treatment rather than variants on a single pathway, CAP generates multifaceted pressure against which bacteria are far less likely to develop resistance (Nyerges et al. 2020)

In addition, S. aureus treated with CAP displayed increased susceptibility to mupirocin. Since mupirocin inhibits isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (Parenti et al. 1987), the enhanced antibiotic activity upon CAP exposure suggests that plasma-induced damages compromise bacterial homeostasis in a way that amplifies the effects of protein synthesis inhibition alone. Potential alterations in membrane lipoproteins and cell envelope integrity may play a role in this increased sensitivity, as a weakened envelope can reduce the cell’s ability to buffer the stress of antibiotic exposure and to inhibit antibiotic penetration.

Taken together, our results indicate that CAP has multiple impacts on S. aureus. It disrupts biofilm cell structures, possibly impacting metabolism. It alters single cells, leaving surviving bacteria more vulnerable. Unlike antibiotics, CAP does not seem to drive resistance but instead increases bacterial susceptibility to both repeated CAP exposure and conventional antibiotics.

Nevertheless, several limitations of our study should be considered. All the experiments were conducted on a single S. aureus strain, USA300, and in vitro, which do not represent the diversity of clinical isolates or the complexity of in vivo biofilm environments. CAP is a complex combination of RONS, UV radiation, and electromagnetic fields. Our study did not dissect the contribution of each component to CAP antibacterial effects. Moreover, RNA-seq was performed shortly after CAP treatment, missing delayed transcriptional responses or adaptive mechanisms that could appear hours later and give additional insights into CAP mechanisms of action. Further experiments will be necessary to confirm the consequences of the changes in gene transcription. Finally, CAP pre-treatment on single cells enhanced bacterial susceptibility to mupirocin, suggesting that CAP could act in combination with antibiotics. While this proof-of-principle is promising, future studies should explore additional antibiotic classes in vitro and in vivo to establish the clinical relevance of such combinations.

In conclusion, CAP represents a promising complementary approach to conventional antimicrobials. CAP targets S. aureus through pleiotropic mechanisms that circumvent traditional resistance pathways. CAP may provide a valuable strategy to combat persistent biofilm infections in the era of rising antimicrobial resistance.
