Intra-Calcium Modification
Authors: Calvin Leung, Alan Ka Chun Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: atherectomy, calcified nodule, calcium management, intravascular ultrasound

Calcified nodules (CNs) present a major therapeutic challenge in contemporary percutaneous coronary intervention, with increased risk of adverse outcomes.1 Their rigid physical properties can lead to eccentric and suboptimal stent expansion.1 Recent intravascular imaging studies have further revealed CN subtypes with distinct morphological, histopathological, and biological characteristics.2 Eruptive CNs, characterized by a disrupted fibrous cap, are more deformable and thus allow for better stent expansion compared with noneruptive nodules with an intact, thick fibrous cap.2,3 However, eruptive CNs are also more biologically active and are prone to in-stent restenosis and target vessel failure, primarily owing to regrowth of the nodule through the stent struts.1, 2, 3, 4

The optimal treatment for CN remains unclear. Effective calcium modification—achieving both debulking and calcium fractures to enhance compliance—is believed to be crucial for improved stent deployment and long-term outcomes. However, current devices have notable limitations. Rotational atherectomy may be less effective in cases of poor guidewire bias, whereas orbital atherectomy, with its circumferential shaving and bidirectional ablation, is theoretically more advantageous. Nevertheless, the overall debulking effect of atherectomy devices on CN is often modest, and observational data have not demonstrated a reduction in target vessel revascularization after rotational atherectomy of CN.5 Intravascular lithotripsy may modify CN and the surrounding calcium sheets, potentially improving stent expansion, but its ability to modify large nodules may be limited without a debulking effect.6

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Sudo et al7 demonstrate the application of the ARCADIA (aggressive wire recanalization in calcified atheroma and dilation) technique for achieving “intra-calcium modification” of CN during percutaneous coronary intervention. Originally described in patients with peripheral vascular disease,8 the ARCADIA technique has now been adapted for coronary interventions. This approach uses a high-penetration, directional guidewire (eg, Conquest Pro 12 Sharpened Tip, Asahi Intecc) supported by a microcatheter to puncture the protruding CN and re-enter the distal vessel lumen (Figures 1A and 1B).

A high-penetration, directional guidewire (eg, Conquest Pro 12 Sharpened Tip, Asahi Intecc), supported by a microcatheter, is used to puncture the protruding calcified nodule and re-enter the distal vessel lumen. Angiographic and IVUS-guided 3D wiring techniques are employed to precisely control guidewire manipulation. (C and D) Cross-sectional illustrations of (C) extra-calcium and (D) intra-calcium wire positions. The advantages of intra-calcium wire positioning may include: 1) direct wire contact with calcium; 2) reduction in effective calcium thickness; 3) closer proximity to deep calcium; 4) calcium positioned concentrically relative to the wire; and 5) redistribution of calcium during balloon dilatation, potentially reducing stent eccentricity. 3D = three-dimensional; IVUS = intravascular ultrasound.

To enhance safety, angiographic 3D wiring and IVUS-guided 3D wiring techniques—commonly used in contemporary chronic total occlusion interventions—were adopted to precisely control guidewire manipulation.9,10 After small-balloon dilatation, intravascular ultrasound was advanced through the calcified area to confirm the guidewire's intra-calcium position and ensure optimal wire bias (ie, passing closer to the center of the nodule rather than eccentrically). This positioning enables the calcium nodule to be modified from the “inside-out” using atherectomy, intravascular lithotripsy, or balloon-based strategies.

However, there are several considerations and aspects that warrant further evaluation. This technique requires specialized guidewires and advanced technical expertise, which may limit its immediate accessibility to all operators. Additionally, further studies are needed to gain a clearer understanding of its safety profile. The potential risks of perforation or dissection highlight the need for careful procedural techniques to minimize complications. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether intra-calcium atherectomy may increase the likelihood of the no-reflow phenomenon, warranting additional investigation to address this aspect.

An important consideration is the potential variability in treatment responses between eruptive and noneruptive CN, which remains poorly understood. Further investigation is needed to determine whether “intra-calcium modification” can reduce “biologically active” calcium regrowth in eruptive nodules and improve long-term outcomes. Additionally, the criteria for optimal patient selection and the role of this technique—whether as an upfront or a bailout strategy in the contemporary coronary calcium treatment pathway—have yet to be defined.

Overall, while the ARCADIA technique offers a promising and innovative approach to modifying calcium nodules, further data and research are crucial to address these key questions and demonstrate its safety and long-term efficacy.
