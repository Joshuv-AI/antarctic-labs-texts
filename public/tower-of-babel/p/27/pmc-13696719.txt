Shared genetic architecture of reading and attention disorders using genomic structural equation modeling
Authors: Shiying Wang, Joan Bosson-Heenan, Andrew Grotzinger, Jan C. Frijters, Jeffrey R. Gruen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reading disorder (RD), characterized by difficulties in reading, is the most common learning disability and frequently co-occurs with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), suggesting shared genetic underpinnings. To investigate their genetic overlap, we leveraged summary statistics from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of reading assessment scores and ADHD diagnosis using genomic structural equation modeling. We modeled a common genetic factor capturing shared genetic influences between reading-related traits and ADHD. GWAS of this common factor revealed a pleiotropic locus on chromosome 12 (rs7969091, ββ = −0.035, pp = 3.86 × 10−8). Transcriptome-wide structural equation modeling analysis identified associations with tissue-specific expression of RHEBL1 and LMBR1L within the same locus as GWAS hit. Genetic correlation analyses showed significant correlations of the common factor with six psychiatric disorders (rg = −0.47 to 0.24) and six behavioral traits (rg = 0.31 to 0.74). Our findings provide novel insights into the shared genetic underpinnings of RD and ADHD.

Introduction

Reading disorder (RD), or dyslexia, is the most common learning disability and is characterized by difficulties in reading despite educational opportunities and normal intelligence. Assessment typically involves a comprehensive battery of intelligence quotient (IQ) and reading- and language-related skills, including reading fluency, comprehension, phonological awareness, decoding, and spelling abilities. RD diagnosis is based on performance well below the level expected based on intelligence1. Prevalence in the general population ranges from 4% to 17%2,3. RD has significant and enduring adverse effects on educational achievement, economic potential, and health outcomes4. Early diagnosis and intervention are critical for mitigating these negative effects and improving outcomes.

RD often co-occurs with other developmental and learning disorders. Studies have consistently found that RD and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) co-occur at greater-than-chance levels, with 25–40% of children with ADHD meeting criteria for RD and 15–40% of those with reading difficulties meeting criteria for ADHD5–8. ADHD is characterized by a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, which can interfere with academic achievement, social functioning, and overall development9.

Both RD and ADHD have a strong genetic basis. Family and twin studies show heritability ranges of 40%–80% for RD10,11 and 60%–90% for ADHD12,13. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified multiple genetic variants associated with RD14–16 and ADHD17,18, respectively. Moreover, there is consistent evidence for a genetic overlap between RD and ADHD, which may underlie their frequent co-occurrence. The genetic correlation between RD and ADHD has been estimated at approximately 53%16. Chromosomal regions 16p, 17q, and possibly 10q have also been identified to have pleiotropic effects on RD and ADHD19,20. While these findings support genetic sharing, the shared genetic architecture underlying RD and ADHD remains incompletely understood, partly because prior studies have frequently been predicated on dichotomized phenotypes and partly because they used bivariate analyses.

Maximizing power for joint analyses of RD and ADHD entails a common trade-off between phenotypic assessments and sample size. When the sample size is fixed, quantitative assessments can capture more phenotypic variation and increase statistical power compared to arbitrary dichotomization21,22. However, dichotomous case–control GWASs often achieve substantially larger sample sizes, which can enhance overall statistical power. Quantitative assessments based on standardized tests are available for multiple reading-related traits, whereas large-scale GWASs of quantitative attention measures remain limited. Because of this trade-off, we leveraged quantitative assessments for reading-related traits and the largest available dichotomous case–control GWAS for ADHD. Likewise, in GWAS settings, multivariate analyses have greater power by leveraging correlations among a greater number of phenotypes to detect pleiotropic genetic effects23. When used together, quantitative assessments and multivariate analyses enhance the power to detect associations and identify novel genetic variants. To further elucidate the genetic architecture of RD and ADHD, multivariate approaches that can incorporate a variety of data types are needed to identify and characterize the full range of shared genetic factors.

Genomic structural equation modeling (SEM)24 is a multivariate approach that has been used to analyze the joint genetic architecture of complex traits predicated on both dichotomous and quantitative assessments within the same model. It uses the principles of SEM to model the genetic covariance derived from linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC)25 and provides a flexible framework that can accommodate GWAS summary statistics from samples of varying and potentially unknown degrees of participant sample overlap. Several statistical methods have been developed to investigate shared genetic architecture across traits. Approaches such as LAVA26 and SUPERGNOVA27 estimate local genetic correlations across genomic regions, capturing heterogeneity in genetic sharing across the genome. In contrast, Genomic SEM models genome-wide genetic covariance among multiple traits and enables estimation of latent genetic factors. Genomic SEM also allows for the incorporation of individual genetic effects into the model, facilitating the identification of genetic variants influencing general dimensions of cross-trait liability and increasing discovery power. Genomic SEM has been successfully applied to study the genetic architecture of neuroticism traits24, ADHD28, autism spectrum disorder29, and major psychiatric disorders30. By enabling more comprehensive modeling of genetic relationships across traits, Genomic SEM offers a valuable framework for advancing our understanding of the genetic basis of complex traits.

In this study, we utilized publicly available GWAS summary statistics to investigate the genetic architecture of reading and ADHD using Genomic SEM. The genetic correlation between RD and ADHD suggests shared genetic influences. Therefore, we first modeled a common factor to capture the shared genetic influences between quantitative reading assessments and ADHD diagnosis. Then, through a GWAS predicated on the common factor, we examined the individual effects of genetic variants on the common factor to identify loci contributing to the common genetic liability, followed by fine-mapping to detect credible sets of variants. Additionally, we incorporated tissue-specific gene expression into the model to detect gene effects across different biological tissues. We also assessed the genetic correlation between the common factor and a range of complex traits, as well as enrichment in genomic annotations across species. Through these approaches, the goal was to improve our understanding of the genetic architecture of reading and ADHD beyond what had been previously published.

Results

Heritability and genetic correlation of reading-related traits and ADHD

In this study, we used summary statistics from large-scale GWASs of reading assessment scores and ADHD diagnosis. Specifically, summary statistics of European ancestry for reading-related traits were extracted from the GenLang Consortium15. These traits included word reading (n = 27,180), nonword reading (n = 16,746), spelling (n = 17,278), nonword repetition (n = 12,828), and phoneme awareness (n = 12,411). GWAS summary statistics of European ancestry for ADHD (38,691 cases and 186,843 controls) were obtained from the most recent Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC) GWAS17. A summary of each dataset used in this study, with sample characteristics, is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Heritability estimates based on LDSC for the six traits are as follows: 0.20 (SE = 0.02) for word reading, 0.24 (SE = 0.04) for phoneme awareness, 0.26 (SE = 0.03) for nonword reading, 0.10 (SE = 0.03) for nonword repetition, 0.24 (SE = 0.03) for spelling, and 0.41 (SE = 0.02) for ADHD.

As a sensitivity analysis to evaluate whether the common factor model was specific to ADHD rather than reflecting broader psychiatric liability, we fitted a parallel model in which ADHD was replaced with anxiety disorders using summary statistics from the PGC GWAS31. In the model including reading and anxiety disorders (Supplemental Note 2), anxiety disorders did not load significantly, and model fit was poor (SRMR > 0.1), indicating that the common factor model is specific to reading and ADHD.

Fine-mapping of the common factor locus

Fine-mapping was conducted at the locus centered on the lead SNP rs7969091 using the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) model32. Variants within ±100 kb of the top SNP were selected, resulting in 194 SNPs for analysis. The association signal was resolved into a single 95% credible set containing 22 variants (Table 2). All of them had suggestive p-values (pp < \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times {10}^{-5})$$\end{document}1×10−5) in the GWAS predicated on Cg. The credible set achieved 95% cumulative posterior probability, indicating a high likelihood that it contains the causal variants. The strongest candidates were rs7969091 (posterior inclusion probability (PIP) = 0.128), rs10875917 (PIP = 0.124), and rs10459221 (PIP = 0.114), all in strong linkage disequilibrium (r² = 1.0) and located upstream and intergenic regions surrounding RHEBL1 (MIM: 618956). Although no single variant reached a PIP > 0.5, the posterior probability was distributed among multiple highly correlated SNPs, reflecting the dense LD structure in the region. Transcriptome-wide structural equation modeling on Cg

Genetic correlation between Cg and complex traits

To further characterize Cg, we examined the genetic correlation of Cg with seven psychiatric disorders, nine behavioral traits, and 71 metrics of brain morphology. Results for brain morphology are presented in Supplemental Table 4, as none of these associations were significant at a Bonferroni-corrected threshold for 87 traits (p < 5.74 × 10−4). Results for psychiatric disorders and behavioral traits are presented in Fig. 4. Cg had modest but significant genetic correlations with six psychiatric disorders (rg ranges from −0.47 to 0.24). The strongest genetic correlation was with post-traumatic stress disorder (rg = −0.47, SE = 0.07). There were significant modest-to-high genetic correlations with six behavioral traits (rg ranges from 0.31 to 0.74). Since reading ability is genetically correlated with general cognitive ability (g), part of these correlations with behavioral traits may reflect shared genetic influences related to g. To evaluate this, we incorporated a measure of intelligence as a proxy for g into Genomic SEM. Cg remained significantly associated with educational attainment, executive function, Trail Making Test-B, matrix pattern recognition, and verbal numerical reasoning after controlling for intelligence, whereas the association with symbol digit substitution was no longer significant (details are provided in Supplementary Note 5).

Biological annotations and gene-level analyses

To explore the biological functions of identified genetic variants, 29 suggestive SNPs from the GWAS predicated on Cg, including all 22 SNPs within the credible set, were annotated using ANNOVAR34. The functions of SNPs and mapped genes are summarized in Supplemental Table 3. Regarding the physical positions of SNPs, 10 SNPs are located in 5 protein-coding genes (RHEBL1, DDN, MIM: 610588; KMT2D, MIM: 602113; PRKAG1, MIM: 602742; and DHH, MIM: 605423), and the remaining are in noncoding RNA genes or intergenic regions. Among the three SNPs within exons, one is a nonsynonymous variant in DDN with a Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score of 9.235, suggesting that it is likely benign35.

The top SNP rs7969091 is located upstream of gene RHEBL1 and lies in a region marked by H3K27Ac in the UCSC Genome Browser, indicating its potential role in active regulatory elements such as enhancers or promoters. Consistent with its potential regulatory role, SNP rs7969091 is an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) for multiple genes across a range of tissues as identified in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project database (Supplemental Table 5). Of note, rs7969091 is a significant eQTL for RHEBL1 in several brain regions, including the cerebellar hemisphere, putamen, cortex, nucleus accumbens, caudate, amygdala, hypothalamus, anterior cingulate cortex, and substantia nigra, as well as in the pituitary and thyroid glands.

For the five mapped protein-coding genes and LMBR1L identified in T-SEM, lookups for previously reported gene-trait associations were performed in the NHGRI-EBI GWAS catalog36. These genes have been associated with a wide range of complex traits, including brain structural traits (e.g., left–right brain asymmetry and whole-brain restricted directional diffusion), cognitive performance (e.g., intelligence and math ability), bipolar disorder, reaction time, educational attainment, and anthropometric traits. The detailed results are summarized in Supplemental Table 6.

Gene expression levels of the six genes in human brain tissues were examined using the GTEx and BrainSpan databases (Fig. 5). DDN shows high and regional-specific expression in brain tissues, with higher expression in the amygdala, frontal cortex, hippocampus, anterior cingulate cortex, and basal ganglia (Fig. 5a). KMT2D and LMBR1L have higher expression in the cerebellum compared to other brain tissues. Stratified genomic SEM analysis on Cg

To investigate the enrichment of the common factor Cg across functional genomic annotations, we performed a stratified Genomic SEM analysis using 50 binary annotations from the BaselineLD Version 2.2 model37. Only primate-conserved regions (conserved_primate_phastcons46way) reached Bonferroni-corrected significance (enrichment ratio = 14.62, SE = 3.92, p = 2.5 × 10−4), indicating that SNPs in genomic regions conserved across primate species contribute disproportionately to the shared genetic architecture captured by Cg. Results for all 50 annotations are reported in Supplementary Table 7.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the shared genetic architecture of reading-related traits and ADHD using Genomic SEM. By leveraging summary statistics from large-scale GWASs, we constructed a common factor, Cg, representing latent genomic covariance shared across four reading-related traits (word reading, phoneme awareness, nonword repetition, and spelling) and ADHD diagnosis. We further examined the genetic effects of individual SNPs on Cg through GWAS and identified one genome-wide significant locus, 12q13.12. Fine-mapping of this locus resolved the association signal into a 95% credible set containing 22 SNPs, highlighting variants most likely to be causal. In the T-SEM analysis, we detected associations between Cg and tissue-specific gene expression of two genes located within the same locus as our GWAS hit. We also assessed the genetic correlation between Cg and a range of complex traits and found significant correlations with several psychiatric disorders and behavioral traits. Stratified genomic SEM analysis showed that Cg was significantly enriched only in primate-conserved regions. Our results provide novel insights into the genetic underpinnings of reading and ADHD, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic factors that contribute to these traits.

The common factor model demonstrated significant loadings for reading-related traits and ADHD, indicating substantial shared genetic influences. The fit indices confirmed that this model adequately captures the shared genetic architecture between traits. The negative loading of ADHD on Cg suggests that genetic variants contributing to ADHD have opposing effects on reading-related traits, which is consistent with findings from the genetic correlation analysis. Of note, the common factor primarily captures shared genetic variance related to reading traits, as reflected by their high loadings, whereas ADHD shows a moderate loading and high residual variance. This indicates that the common factor captures only the portion of ADHD genetic variation that is shared with reading-related traits, while the remaining ADHD genetic variation is trait-specific. Although previous Genomic SEM analyses on reading-related traits, IQ, and educational attainment have suggested the presence of multiple latent factors within reading-related traits15, model comparison analyses in our study showed that a two-factor model was nearly identical to the common factor model (Supplemental Note 1). We retained the common factor model for a more direct and interpretable representation of the shared genetic covariance across reading-related traits and ADHD. A model including anxiety disorders was not a good fit, reinforcing that Cg is specific to reading and ADHD rather than broader psychiatric disorders. These findings highlight the potential utility of Genomic SEM in disentangling complex genetic relationships across neurodevelopmental traits.

Our results are also consistent with recent work examining the shared genetic architecture of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders. In particular, Ciulkinyte et al.38 applied Genomic SEM across multiple neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders and identified a latent factor representing “attention and learning difficulties,” on which both ADHD and dyslexia loaded positively. This factor was distinct from other psychiatric and neurodevelopmental factors, highlighting a shared genetic component between dyslexia and ADHD. Although the phenotypic definitions differ between studies, the identification of a latent factor involving both dyslexia and ADHD is conceptually consistent with the common factor in our model.

Our GWAS predicated on Cg identified a genome-wide significant locus on chromosome 12q13.12. This locus has not been previously associated with reading or ADHD in univariate GWASs of individual traits, including a large study of dyslexia16, suggesting a genetic locus contributing to shared liability for reading-related traits and ADHD. However, in a recent multivariate analysis of dyslexia and ADHD, Ciulkinyte et al.38 identified a lead SNP in the 12q13.12 region that overlaps with the Cg locus but that reached only suggestive significance. The convergence of association signals in this region across studies using different phenotypic definitions and analytical methods provides independent evidence that chromosome 12q13.12 contributes to the shared genetic architecture of reading-related traits and ADHD. It also provides another example of how the multivariate framework of Genomic SEM can successfully aggregate genetic covariance across correlated traits, uncovering loci that are otherwise undetectable in univariate analyses. The use of quantitative reading-related traits further enhances this power by capturing continuous variation in reading ability across multiple domains. Together, these features highlight the advantage of Genomic SEM for detecting pleiotropic effects and characterizing the shared genetic architecture underlying neurodevelopmental and behavioral traits30.

In this study, one locus and several genes were suggested to underlie the genetic overlap between reading and ADHD (Fig. 6). Within the genome-wide significant locus 12q13.12, 29 suggestive SNPs and 22 credible-set SNPs were mapped to five protein-coding genes (RHEBL1, DDN, KMT2D, PRKAG1, and DHH). Fine-mapping also indicated that the top SNPs with the highest PIPs are located upstream and intergenic regions surrounding RHEBL1. T-SEM analysis across all available genes and 13 brain tissues identified significant associations between Cg and tissue-specific expression of RHEBL1 and LMBR1L after Bonferroni correction. Notably, both genes are located within the same 12q13.12 locus identified in the GWAS of Cg. Among these genes, we highlighted RHEBL1 and LMBR1L, which were implicated across GWAS, fine-mapping, and T-SEM. RHEBL1 (Ras Homolog Enriched in Brain Like 1) encodes a small GTPase protein that is involved in cell growth, proliferation, and signaling pathways, particularly in the mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) pathway39,40. This pathway plays a crucial role in neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and brain function. The mTOR pathway has been linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism, ADHD, and intellectual disabilities41. Dysregulation in this pathway may disrupt the frontal-striatal and frontal-parietal circuits underlying executive functions. Meta-analytic evidence has shown that individuals with ADHD exhibit significant impairments in executive functions, particularly in response inhibition, working memory, and planning42. Alterations in mTOR signaling can also lead to structural and functional changes in the hippocampus and cortex43 that could potentially impair language processing abilities. RHEBL1 has been associated with reaction time44, bipolar disorder45, intelligence46, and educational attainment47. Genetic variants within RHEBL1 have also been reported to have different allele frequencies between cases of bipolar disorder and ADHD48.

LMBR1L (Limb Development Membrane Protein 1 Like) gene encodes a protein involved in receptor-mediated endocytosis and signal transduction. LMBR1L has been associated with left-right brain asymmetry49 and whole-brain restricted directional diffusion50. A multi-omics study of brain tissue transcription and DNA methylation suggests that LMBR1L is involved in the pathogenesis of ADHD51. Other genes (DDN, KMT2D, PRKAG1, and DHH) may also play a role in neuronal function and neurodevelopmental pathways that influence both reading and attention (detailed in Supplemental Note 6).

Furthermore, T-SEM analysis suggests important brain regions that may be involved in the biological mechanisms underlying reading and ADHD. For example, cortical regions, including the temporal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, and inferior frontal gyrus, are related to word decoding, phonological processing, and reading comprehension52,53. Brain cortex, particularly the prefrontal cortex, plays a significant role in ADHD. Studies have shown that structural and functional differences in this brain region contribute to greater difficulties with attention, planning, and behavioral regulation in individuals with ADHD compared to controls54,55. Additionally, the cerebellum has been implicated in both reading ability and ADHD, as cerebellar dysfunction has been linked to language processing and reading fluency56,57, as well as executive function deficits such as impulsivity and inattention in ADHD58. These brain regions, which collectively influence attention regulation and reading ability, may contribute to the neurobiological basis of both traits, helping explain their genetic overlap.

Our findings support the Generalist Genes Hypothesis, which posits that a broad set of genes contributes to a range of cognitive traits rather than specific domains11. The common factor, Cg, identified through Genomic SEM reflects this pleiotropy, consistent with prior multivariate twin and GWAS studies59. The results also extend the Simple View of Reading60 by implicating attentional and executive processes in decoding-related traits, suggesting a need to integrate constructs such as cognitive control and working memory into future reading models. The Multiple Deficit Model61 is likewise supported, given the cross-trait loadings and biological evidence pointing to multifactorial origins of both reading disorder and ADHD.

Cg showed no significant genetic correlations with structural brain morphology. In contrast, Cg was significantly correlated with several psychiatric disorders and behavioral traits. Specifically, Cg showed moderate negative genetic correlations with schizophrenia, anxiety disorder, major depressive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and autism spectrum disorder, suggesting shared genetic influences with broader mental health conditions. These correlations were largely driven by ADHD rather than reading-related traits. ADHD showed strong and statistically significant genetic correlations with these psychiatric disorders, whereas individual reading-related traits generally showed weak and non-significant correlations (Supplemental Table 8). The opposite sign of Cg relative to ADHD is consistent with the negative loading of ADHD on the common factor. This pattern aligns with previous findings showing substantial genetic overlap between ADHD and other psychiatric disorders17.

Cg showed a moderate and significant positive genetic correlation with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Notably, OCD did not exhibit significant genetic correlations with any of the individual traits included in the model but was significantly correlated with Cg (Supplemental Table 8). One possible explanation is that the increased sample size in the common factor analysis enhanced the power to detect shared genetic influences compared to the individual traits. This finding suggests that shared genetic influences with OCD are detectable only when these traits are modeled jointly.

Cg also showed strong positive genetic correlations with educational attainment, executive functions, and several cognitive abilities. Analyses controlling for general cognitive ability in the Genomic SEM framework (Supplemental Note 5) indicate that Cg has genetic overlap with general cognitive ability, but it also captures a component of genetic variation that is not fully explained by general cognitive ability.

To place these findings in the context of previous studies, we compared the genetic correlation patterns observed for Cg with those reported in the dyslexia GWAS16, the GenLang GWAS of quantitative reading traits15, the ADHD GWAS17, and a recent multivariate analysis of dyslexia and ADHD38 (Supplementary Table 9). A consistent pattern of sign reversal is observed between ability-direction studies (Cg and reading ability) and deficit-direction studies (dyslexia, ADHD, and ADHD + dyslexia latent factor), reflecting that these studies index the same underlying genetic dimension from opposite directions. Overall, the genetic correlation profile of Cg is broadly consistent with prior findings while reflecting contributions from both reading-related traits and ADHD.

There are multiple limitations that should be taken into consideration in interpreting the results. First, the study was based on summary statistics from GWAS of European ancestry, limiting the generalizability of findings to other populations. Future studies should aim to replicate these findings in diverse ancestral groups. Second, while our Genomic SEM model accounted for sample overlap, potential biases related to heterogeneity in multi-cohort GWAS datasets cannot be completely ruled out. Additional studies incorporating more refined phenotypic measures may help clarify the biological mechanisms linking reading ability and ADHD. Third, although sex differences have been well documented in both reading62 and ADHD63,64, the current study was unable to examine sex-specific genetic architecture due to the lack of sex-stratified GWAS summary statistics. Future research with access to sex-stratified data will be important for exploring potential differences in the genetic architecture of these traits between males and females. Fourth, we did not include the large dyslexia GWAS from Doust et al.16 in our model, which, despite relying on self-reported diagnosis, provides substantially greater statistical power than currently available quantitative reading GWAS and could strengthen detection of shared genetic signals. At the same time, the quantitative reading traits used in our model were obtained from standardized performance-based assessments and capture continuous variation across multiple reading domains. Future work integrating both approaches will be important for fully characterizing the shared genetic architecture. Fifth, there is an imbalance in the number of phenotypic measures in the current model, with multiple reading-related traits and a single ADHD measure. Future studies incorporating more ADHD-related phenotypes, such as inattention and hyperactivity measurements, may further refine the shared genetic architecture. Finally, functional validation of the identified genetic variants and genes is needed to better understand their roles in neurodevelopment.

Building upon prior findings of genetic overlap between reading ability and ADHD, this study provides new multivariate genomic evidence characterizing their shared genetic influences and demonstrates the power of multivariate approaches in uncovering genetic loci of cross-trait relevance. The identification of a locus on chromosome 12, which is functionally implicated by gene expression data and prior associations with neurodevelopmental phenotypes, offers a promising entry point into understanding pleiotropic risk for reading and attention-related disorders. These results validate theoretical models such as the Generalist Genes Hypothesis and highlight the conceptual utility of Genomic SEM for parsing complex, multidimensional genetic architectures. As the field moves toward more integrated models of cognitive neurogenetics, multivariate genomic tools will be critical for refining our understanding of comorbidity, pleiotropy, and neurodevelopment.

Methods

GWAS dataset

Summary statistics of European ancestry for reading-related traits were extracted from the GenLang Consortium, which conducted the largest GWAS on a range of reading assessments, including word reading (n = 27,180), nonword reading (n = 16,746), spelling (n = 17,278), nonword repetition (n = 12,828), and phoneme awareness (n = 12,411)15. GWAS summary statistics of European ancestry for ADHD (38,691 cases and 186,843 controls) were obtained from the most recent PGC GWAS17, where ADHD diagnoses were based on clinical evaluations by psychiatrists according to ICD-10 or DSM-IV criteria.

Genomic SEM

Genomic SEM was implemented using the R package GenomicSEM. Initially, we performed a standard set of quality control filters on all GWAS summary statistics using the munge function within Genomic SEM. This process included filtering to HapMap3 SNPs and excluding SNPs with a MAF < 0.01 and an imputation score (INFO) < 0.9. These “munged” summary statistics were then used as input to the multivariable version of ldsc implemented in Genomic SEM, which estimates genetic covariance and sampling covariance matrices across the traits included in the analysis. For the binary ADHD diagnosis, these estimates were converted to the more interpretable liability scale using the population prevalence of ADHD and the effective GWAS sample size65. LD weights used for estimating the regression model in LDSC were derived from the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European LD scores, excluding the major histocompatibility complex region due to its complex LD structure, which can introduce bias into the estimates.

The genetic covariance matrix was transformed into a genetic correlation matrix. Notably, the genetic correlations between nonword reading and word reading, as well as between nonword reading and spelling, exceeded 1.0. This can occur because the LDSC estimator is unbounded and may produce estimates larger than 1.0 due to sampling variation. These genetic correlation estimates suggest that the current effective sample size of nonword reading or the variation within the sample may not be sufficient to accurately estimate the genetic correlation with other traits. As a result, we excluded nonword reading from the subsequent Genomic SEM analyses.

The estimated covariance matrices were subsequently used as input to models specified within Genomic SEM. CFI and SRMR are indexes of approximate model fit, with CFI values > 0.95 considered good fit and >0.90 considered acceptable fit66, and values of SRMR < 0.05 considered good fit and <0.10 considered acceptable fit67. To evaluate the latent structure among the traits, we first conducted exploratory factor analysis to examine a two-factor structure. Based on these results, we further evaluated a confirmatory two-factor model in Genomic SEM and compared it with the common factor model. Since the two models showed nearly identical fit and parameter estimates, and the common factor model provides a more direct and interpretable representation of the shared genetic covariance across reading-related traits and ADHD, we retained the common factor model for the main analyses (details are provided in the Supplemental Note 1).

To show that our model is specific to reading and ADHD, we also fitted a series of models examining the genetic architecture among reading traits and anxiety disorders using summary statistics from the PGC GWAS (detailed in Supplemental Note 2)31. To evaluate whether including four reading traits and one ADHD trait might bias the model toward reading, we conducted a leave-one-reading-trait-out sensitivity analysis. Specifically, we re-estimated the Genomic SEM common factor model four times, each time excluding one reading phenotype (word reading, spelling, phoneme awareness, or nonword repetition) while retaining ADHD and the remaining reading traits (detailed in Supplemental Note 3).

GWAS on the common factor

To estimate the effect of individual SNPs on Cg, the common factor for reading and ADHD, we incorporated individual SNP effects into both the genetic covariance matrix and the sampling covariance matrix. We then applied the sumstats function, followed by the commonfactorGWAS function, to compute summary statistics representing SNP effects on Cg. After quality control, a total of 6,421,479 SNPs were analyzed. The threshold for genome-wide significance was set at the field standard of p < 5 × 10−8.

Fine-mapping of the common factor locus

We performed statistical fine-mapping using the SuSiE model32 for the common factor locus identified by GWAS. SuSiE applies a Bayesian regression framework to identify multiple causal variants within a locus while accounting for LD structure. For this analysis, we focused on the genome-wide significant locus containing the lead SNP and all variants within ±100 kb. The LD matrix was computed using European ancestry reference genotypes from the 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3). We set the maximum number of causal variants per locus to 10 and used default prior parameters. SuSiE estimated PIPs for each variant and identified credible sets of variants that jointly captured 95% of the posterior probability of containing a causal variant.

T-SEM

We used T-SEM to identify tissue-specific gene expression associated with Cg33. Univariate transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) uncover the relationship between gene expression and a single trait through summary-based transcriptomic imputation. T-SEM, as a multivariate extension of TWAS, enables the examination of gene expression patterns across genetically correlated traits.

First, we used FUSION68 to perform univariate TWAS for word reading, spelling, nonword repetition, phoneme awareness, and ADHD based on summary statistics and publicly available functional weights from 13 brain tissue types curated in the GTEx v869. These univariate FUSION statistics were then used as input to the read_fusion function in Genomic SEM. Finally, the userGWAS function was used to estimate the effect of gene expression on Cg. Significant gene expression associations were identified at a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of p < 9.76 × 10−7 (0.05/51,223 gene expression estimates).

Genetic correlation with complex traits

To further characterize Cg, we examined the genetic correlation with a range of complex traits. These traits included seven psychiatric disorders, nine behavioral traits, and 71 metrics of brain morphology. Genetic correlations were calculated using LDSC25, based on the summary statistics for Cg and publicly available GWAS summary statistics from PGC and UK Biobank70 for complex traits (details provided in Supplemental Table 4). All analyses were based on HapMap3 SNPs only, and precalculated LD scores from the European 1000 Genomes reference cohort. Significant genetic correlations were identified at a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of p < 5.74 × 10−4 (0.05/87).

Post-hoc analyses

To explore the biological functions of identified genetic variants and genes, SNPs associated with Cg from the GWAS were annotated using ANNOVAR34. Regulatory features were examined using the UCSC Genome Browser71. The expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) were assessed in the GTEx69. Gene expression levels in tissues for protein-coding genes identified in the GWAS and T-SEM were examined using the GTEx69 and BrainSpan72 databases. Previously reported gene-trait associations for these genes were retrieved from the GWAS Catalog36.

Stratified genomic SEM

We performed a stratified genomic SEM analysis to investigate the enrichment of the common factor Cg across functional genomic annotations. We used annotations from the most recent 1000 Genomes Phase 3 BaselineLD Version 2.237, which includes a total of 97 annotations, including coding regions, untranslated regions, promoters, and flanking windows. Continuous and flanking window annotations were excluded from enrichment estimation, resulting in a total of 50 binary annotations. Enrichment ratio was calculated as the proportion of the common factor’s genetic variance attributable to each annotation divided by the proportion of SNPs in that annotation, compared to the whole genome. Statistical significance was assessed using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.001 (0.05/50).
