Path analysis reveals cross-country differences between Czech and Chinese university students in effect of internet and smartphone addiction, mental health, and personality traits on academic achievement in the post-pandemic era
Authors: Jana Kvintova, Jan Sebastian Novotny, Hongyang Liu, Lucie Vachova, Jiri Kantor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite extensive evidence on the impact of various mental health issues including smartphone/internet addiction, and personality traits on academic achievement, little is known about the complex interactions between multiple of these factors simultaneously, as well as cross-country differences in these nuanced relationships. In particular, understanding the role of the mentioned addictions has become increasingly important in recent years in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. The aim of this cross-country study was to investigate, using path analysis, the complex relationships between mental health determinants (depression, anxiety, stress, resilience, and smartphone/internet addiction) and academic achievement in 1785 Czech and Chinese university students using an online battery of psychological tests. The results confirmed the previously described effect of multiple factors (anxiety, stress, resilience, smartphone/internet addiction, personality traits, and sex, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness) on academic achievement, overlapping in most cases for both groups of students. At the same time, however, different country-dependent patterns of interactions emerged. For the Czech students, the variables formed a complex network of interacting factors, whereas for the Chinese students, the effect of each cluster of factors was separate for individual domains of academic achievement. These cross-country differences have implications particularly for planning and targeting the most effective interventions to promote and develop academic achievement.Keywords: Smartphone addiction, Internet addiction, Mental health, Personality traits, Academic achievement, Cross-country differences

Introduction

In recent years, the COVID-19 pandemic has led to an increase in various problems in young adults and university students, including depression, anxiety, and increased stress [1–3]. Related anti-pandemic measures, which have severely curtailed most people's direct social activities and skyrocketed the need to move online and use computers, tablets and smartphones almost constantly, have also led to an increase in problematic use of (up to addiction to) smartphones and the Internet [4–6]. These and other factors have had a negative impact on the academic performance and outcomes of university students [7–11]. At the same time, it is most likely that these relationships are complex, heterogeneous, and culturally contingent, and encompass a range of contributing factors, including internet and smartphone addiction, mental health issues (depression, stress, anxiety), resilience, and personality traits, which were the focus of this study.

Review of current evidence on this study topic

Internet addiction may be defined as an inability to control excessive and impulsive use of online features, even as it causes problems to one’s social, familial, working, and/or academic life [12]. Its manifestations include the constant extension of time spent online, disruption or impairment of work and activities due to spending time online, and various behavioural and cognitive-emotional symptoms when one is offline (feelings of nervousness/depression, persistent thoughts about being online again, avoiding unpleasant thoughts about life by indulging in soothing fantasies about the Internet). Internet abuse and addiction have been associated with mental health difficulties such as depression [13, 14], anxiety [14, 15], stress [14, 16], as well as social difficulties [17, 18], and/or impaired academic performance and procrastination [13, 15, 16, 19, 20].

The I-PACE (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) model offers a detailed framework for understanding the development of internet addiction. According to this model, internet addiction arises from interactions between personal predispositions (such as personality traits and genetic factors), affective and cognitive responses to situational triggers, and executive functioning. This model highlights that the interplay between these components can lead to both the onset and maintenance of addictive behaviours [21]. For example, individuals with high levels of Neuroticism might use the Internet as an escape from negative emotions, while those with low Conscientiousness may struggle with self-regulation, leading to excessive use. The I-PACE model also emphasizes the role of internal and external triggers in facilitating craving and addictive behaviours. Internal triggers, such as negative moods or stress, and external triggers, including advertisements or notifications, can induce craving and lead to impulsive internet use [22].

Similarly, excessive use of smartphones can have several negative impacts. Inappropriate use of mobile phones may hinder face-to-face communication [23], reduce interpersonal intimacy [24], act as a distraction for students and be a cause of academic procrastination or lower academic performance [25, 26]. Moreover, excessive smartphone use can result in physical and psychological problems or the development of smartphone addiction. Smartphone addiction was first described by Young as a type of technology addiction [27]. It is considered an addictive behaviour (as it encompasses some of the general characteristics of addictive behaviour), characterized by compulsive overuse of mobile devices [28]. It is manifested in significant behavioural disruptions (missing planned work, concentration problems, constant checking of the phone, longer than planned smartphone use), emotional and cognitive changes (restlessness when not holding the phone, constant thinking about the smartphone, inability to give up the phone despite the inconvenience it causes) and physical difficulties (wrist and back pain) [27, 29]. Smartphone addiction can have a variety of negative consequences. Excessive/addictive smartphone use has been linked to a higher risk of anxiety and depression [30, 31], increased perceived stress [32, 33], worsened social skill and relationships together with withdrawal [18, 34, 35], as well as higher academic procrastination and worse learning and academic performance [25, 26, 36–38]. Although the two types of addictions are relatively closely intertwined (the smartphone is one of the essential tools for consuming the internet content) and have similar symptoms and negative effects, it is worth noting that their characteristics are nevertheless different and distinctive, and the presence of one addiction does not imply the presence of the other.

As is evident, there is a clear relationship between smartphone and internet addiction and various mental health issues such as depression, anxiety and stress. These difficulties have also been proven to be closely linked to academic achievement. Depression [39, 40], (social) anxiety [41–43] and stress [44] have been proven to have a negative impact on academic performance, recently further exacerbated by the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic [1, 45]. These effects are mediated through mechanisms such as increased feelings of hopelessness [46] and loss of interest, motivation and energy [8], emotion-driven impairment of concentration, memory, and other cognitive functions [47, 48], decreased self-efficacy [49–51], or increased ruminations and negative thoughts [52]. On the other hand, however, some studies indicated that extensive use of technologies such as smartphones and the Internet might also have the positive impact on academic performance. For example, online communication and discussion through various messaging apps had positive effects on academic collaboration, engagement in discussion activities, better learning outcomes [53–55], improved social skills and better intimacy and interpersonal relationships [54], as well as reduced depression, and ensured higher functioning and quality of life [56].

Finally, several studies have shown that most personality traits may play a significant role in relation to smartphone/internet addiction, mental health, and academic achievement. For example, high Neuroticism and low Openness to experience, Conscientiousness, Extraversion and Agreeableness have been associated with internet addiction [57–59], Extraversion, Neuroticism, and Openness to experience were related to smartphone addiction [60], and Openness to experience, Neuroticism, Conscientiousness and Agreeableness were related in various combinations to academic performance [61–63].

Rationale and aim of the study

When considering the relationships between different factors and academic achievement, however, other aspects such as the socio-cultural environment must also be considered. The presented study also includes a comparison of Czech and Chinese students. These two countries represent relatively different socio-cultural environments, with specific characteristics typical of European or Asian countries.

Cultural Context: China has a community-oriented culture, where collective welfare and harmony are highly valued [64]. This societal structure often leads to a greater reliance on social networks, both physical and digital, for maintaining professional and personal relationships. Chinese culture is characterized by high expectations for academic achievement and success. It has a highly centralized and exam-oriented education system with high levels of competition that can contribute to greater levels of stress, anxiety and depression [65]. In China, the emphasis on academic excellence and high-pressure environments fosters a culture of constant connectivity, which is reinforced by the widespread adoption of digital learning platforms and social media [66]. China has a highly digitized society and reports some of the highest scores of problematic smartphone use [6]. On the other hand, the Czech Republic represents a more individualistic European culture with a more decentralized education system that places greater emphasis on personal autonomy and individual rights. In addition, Czech students experience an educational culture that encourages autonomy and places less emphasis on centralized, high-stakes examinations, potentially allowing for greater individual variability in responses to technological influences [67].

Technological Integration: This difference in cultural orientation can significantly influence technology usage. In the Czech Republic, people have a more moderate or cautious approach to technology adoption, with technology being used primarily for individual pursuits and self-expression [68]. In addition, the difference in freedom of access to internet content and censorship may also play a role [69]. Such cultural nuances can profoundly affect social behaviour, stress management, and consequently, academic performance in both countries. The intense use of social media in China for work and educational purposes creates a unique form of stress that Czech students do not have to face [70]. Chinese students often operate under immense pressure from both society and family to excel academically and secure a prosperous future [71], which bolsters the extensive use of technology to meet these expectations.

Differences in learning environment: In China, there is also the expectation to always be connected and promptly responsive to work-related messages and academic notifications, even outside of formal working or studying hours [72, 73]. This contrasts with the Czech context, where there is a much more relaxed approach to selecting and pursuing a career path and generally less pressure to be perpetually available online for professional or educational reasons. The 'working' bond to the device (like a smartphone) is not so strictly demanded. Chinese students thus face additional stress due to these expectations, potentially affecting their academic performance differently than their Czech counterparts [74].

The above findings (mostly examined in rather narrowly constrained combinations of variables) clearly indicate that the relationships between individual variables are likely to be rather complex, affected by interactions between them as well as by other factors such as culture. However, studies involving multivariable models and/or cultural context are still relatively few. Thus, an understanding of the interrelationships between multiple factors must be inferred rather indirectly. Moreover, there is only a very limited amount of research in some geographic/demographic areas, e.g., in the Czech Republic. One prevalence study was carried out in undergraduate students, focused on internet addiction [75], and one intervention study found a positive effect of combined chamber REST interventions on problematic smartphone usage and mental health of female students [76]. Furthermore, the results from Czech and Chinese university students were compared by a pilot intercultural study, suggesting that Czech students manifested greater rumination and perceived stress, and Chinese students showed greater mobile phone addiction [52].

Aims of the study

Several factors have been identified by previous studies as potential risk factors or influential determinants of academic achievement. However, knowledge of the complex culture-specific relationships between these factors and academic achievement is still scarce. To fill this gap, this cross-country study aimed to examine the complex relationships between smartphone and internet addiction, mental health issues, and personality traits, and their impact on academic achievement among university students in the Czech Republic and China in the post-pandemic era. These relationships were analysed by comparing a series of structural models (path analyses) to reveal the best representation of the interactions between all variables for both groups of students.

The hypotheses for this study are grounded in the I-PACE (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) model, which provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how personal predispositions, affective and cognitive responses, and executive functions interact to contribute to internet addiction [21]. Methods

Study design and sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Faculty of Education, Palacky University Olomouc (Czech Republic) and the Faculty of Educational Sciences, Chongqing Normal University in Sichuan (China).

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

University student studying full-time teaching study programs (that passed the subject educational competence) at the Faculty of Education,

University student studying full-time teaching study programs (that passed the subject educational competence) at the Faculty of Education,

Minimum age of 18 years, and.

Minimum age of 18 years, and.

Czech/Chinese nationality.

Czech/Chinese nationality.

Sex, socioeconomic status, language, and academic results were not considered as inclusion criteria. Students with nationalities other than Czech/Chinese, part-time students, and/or students of study programs not leading to qualification were excluded as ineligible for the study.

The target sample of respondents eligible towards the inclusion criteria at Palacky University Olomouc totaled more than 2000 students, and at Chongqing Normal University in Sichuan, approximately 1800 students. As the study design involved exploratory testing and comparison of multiple complex structural models without a predefined tested/validated model setting, we defined the approximate required sample size based on two calculations (required significance 0.05, power = 0.8). First, an a-priori simple regression power analysis was performed to determine the minimum sample size required to test the stated assumptions about the relationships between the variables (size and significance of the regression coefficients). The minimum sample size was 67 respondents. Second, we used the online A-priori Sample Size Calculator for Structural Equation Models [78] with parameters corresponding to the use of all variables. The minimum sample size to detect effect was 947, and minimum sample size for model structure was 288. Based on these parameters, the target number of respondents was set at 50% of eligible students (i.e., 1000 in the Czech Republic and 900 in China).

Participants were contacted via official university emails (Czech Republic) and via the WeChat social app (China). Data were collected from February to April 2022 and from February to May 2023 using an online questionnaire (Google Forms in Czech Republic and Wenjuanxing in China) in two identical forms in the Czech and Chinese language. No responses were excluded.

The final sample consisted of 1075 Czech (mean age ± SD: 24.07 ± 6.7, 913 (84.9%) females) and 710 Chinese (mean age ± SD: 21.0 ± 2.3, 388 (54.6%) females) students. The estimated response rate in Czech students was approximately 50%, whereas in Chinese students it was approximately 40%, falling slightly below, but still acceptably, the expected sample size based on the power analysis. Czech students were most frequently in the second (N = 524, 48.7%) and first (N = 326, 30.3%) year of study, while Chinese students were most frequently in the second (N = 407, 57.3%) and third (N = 191, 26.9%) year of study. The sex distribution in the two groups was different (with a predominance of women in the Czech group), yet these sex ratios corresponded quite well to the actual sex distribution of students in the Czech Republic and China [79, 80].

Measurements

Mobile phone addiction (abbr. MPA), defined as compulsive overuse of the mobile devices, was measured using the short version of the Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS-SV, [28]). This scale, developed to measure addictive symptoms, consists of 10 items (each with a 6-point scale from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’), with higher scores indicating a higher level of smartphone addiction. The reliability in this study was ω = 0.90. Past research has shown good internal consistency reliability in studies using this scale with adolescent groups in Italy (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79) and adolescents in Brazil (α = 0.81; ω = 0.78) [81, 82].

Internet addiction (abbr. IAD), defined as excessive and impulsive use of online features, even as it causes problems to one’s social, family, working, and/or academic life, was assessed using a six-item version of the Internet Addiction Test (IAT [83],). This short unidimensional scale measures symptoms of internet addiction using 5-point Likert scale (from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’) with score ranging from 0 to 30, and a higher score representing stronger addiction. This instrument has previously demonstrated good psychometric properties [84, 85] and demonstrated excellent reliability (ω = 0.86 and α = 0.96) in the present study.

Mental health issues were measured using the 4-item version of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4) [86]. This short tool is designed to screen for the presence of depression (abbr. DPR) and anxiety (abbr. ANX). Each question is scored on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), providing two scores (with a range of 0–6 points). Higher scores indicate greater presence of symptoms. A score of 3 or more indicates the presence of significant anxiety/depressive symptoms. Despite its shortness, it has shown good psychometric properties. Reliability in this study was ωdepr = 0.77, ωanx = 0.85.

Perceived stress (abbr. PSS) was measured using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS [87],). The scale is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = never to 5 = always. A higher score indicates increased perceived stress. Studies have shown good reliability in different cultural settings. According to previous studies, the PSS is an easy-to-use questionnaire with established acceptable psychometric properties [88, 89]. A higher the score indicates increased perceived stress. The reliability in this study was ω = 0.81.

Resilience (abbr. RES), defined as the personal qualities that enable one to thrive in the face of adversity, was assessed by the short version of the Connor-Davidson test (CD-RISC) [90]. It is a 2-item (5-point scale from ‘not at all’ to ‘almost always’) unidimensional tool for the quick assessment of resilience level that shows similar psychometric properties as longer versions. Reliability in this study was ω = 0.80.

Personality traits were assessed using the Ten Item Personality Measure (TIPI) [91]. This short, 10-item questionnaire assesses five personality traits based on the Big Five: Extraversion (item 1,6; abbr. PEX), emotional stability (Neuroticism, item 4,9; abbr. PES), Agreeableness (item 2,7; abbr. PAG), Conscientiousness (item 3,8; abbr. PCO), and Openness to experience (item 5,10; abbr. POP). Each consist of a pair of descriptors that were scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Each of the five dimensions has two corresponding items: the former is a forward score, and the latter is a reverse score. Despite being short, the questionnaire has proven its usefulness and validity when compared to longer scales [91, 92], although some psychometric indicators suffer from some limitations due to the TIPI design [93, 94].

Academic achievement was assessed using an Academic Achievement Questionnaire (AAQ) [95]. This new 9-item scale measures overall academic achievement and its three dimensions: study performance (6-point scale from A to F; abbr. AASP), coping with study demands (abbr. AASD), and social adaptation (both 6-point scale from ‘not at all’ to ‘without problem’; abbr. AASA). Reliability (McDonald’s omega) proved to be good (ωtotal = 0.77, ωAASP = 0.76, ωAASD = 0.79, ωAASA = 0.59).

Statistical analysis

The reliability of the data was examined using McDonald’s omega. A preliminary presence of relationships between variables was verified by a Spearman correlation analysis. The models of the effects and interactions of the variables were then analysed with structural equation modelling (path analysis) using maximum likelihood estimation. Analyses were performed separately for samples from both countries to capture cross-country differences.

Several goodness-of-fit indicators were used to assess the quality of the models: Chi-square statistics, Comparative Fit Index [96], Tucker-Lewis Index [97], Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) [98], Akaike information criterion (AIC) [99], and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [100]. Another parameter of interest was the explained variance of individual intermediate and dependent variables, expressed by adjusted R-squared. In evaluating the models, the measure of explained variance ranked highest, followed by the individual indicators (in order of RMSEA, CLI/TLI, AIC/BIC).

Individual models were gradually built by adding individual variables in different patterns of interactions (paths) in the analysis following several rules, such as academic achievement dimensions could only appear as dependent and intermediate variables (performance dimension only as a dependent variable equals an outcome), personality traits and gender could only be independent variables. Variables were included in the model in the order: addictions- > mental health- > personality- > gender, while the significant impact of the variables already present in the model was continuously verified. To limit the number of models examined, only paths supported by significant preliminary correlations between variables were tested for mental health and personality trait variables. The final model included all variables and paths showing a significant effect, while exhibiting the best goodness-of-fit.

All statistical analyses were performed as two-tailed and all the results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data analysis and visualizations were performed in RStudio (v.2022.07.2 with R environment v.4.2.1). The path analysis was performed using the Lavaan package (v.0.6–15).

Results

To check whether it is feasible to analyse complex relationships between the variables, it was first tested whether there are significant simple relationships between the variables. A preliminary correlation analysis revealed several significant relationships for both groups of students (Fig. 1). The overall pattern of answers (positive vs. negative relationships) was similar for Czech and Chinese students. At the same time, more significant relationships were observed for Czech students compared to Chinese ones. This difference was most evident for internet addiction and its relationship with other variables (mainly personality traits and academic achievement). Collectively, these findings substantiated the subsequent analysis of complex relationships between variables using path analysis.

The performance component of academic achievement was directly associated with gender (females performed better) and coping with academic demands, indicating that academic performance is influenced indirectly by various factors affecting students' ability to manage study-related tasks. These interactions highlight the multifaceted nature of academic success, where personal, psychological, and behavioural factors interplay.

In terms of the effect size of the individual predictors (the amount of explained variance of the outcome variables, Table 1A), it was observed that the model explained most of the variance of Internet addiction (52.4%) for Czech students through five variables: Extraversion, Mobile phone addiction, sex, Stress, and Conscientiousness. Within academic achievement, the observed predictors explained 32.1% of the variance in coping with study demands, and 36.1% of the variance in social adaptation. For academic performance alone, the model (observed variables and their relationships) explained only 12.7% of its variance.

In contrast, for Chinese students, a much more separated effect of individual variables on academic achievement was observed, as its individual components were influenced by different predictors (Fig. 2B, Table 2B). Social adaptation was negatively related to anxiety and positively related to coping with academic demands, Extraversion, and Agreeableness, and, surprisingly, Internet addiction. Coping with study demands was independently negatively affected by stress and positively affected by Conscientiousness. Finally, academic performance was positively affected by gender (better results for female gender), mobile phone addiction, and most of all by coping with study demands. In addition, Internet addiction increased with greater anxiety and mobile phone addiction.

Differences with Czech students were also observed in the amount of explained variance of outcome variables (Table 1B). Anxiety and mobile phone addiction explained only 15.5% of internet addiction (compared to 52.4% for Czech students). Similarly, the predictors explained only 9.9% of coping with academic demands (as opposed to 32.1%). Within social adaptation, similar predictors and the same amount of explained variance were observed (36.5%). In contrast, academic performance was explained much more by the observed predictors in the Chinese model (25.5% of variance as opposed to 12.7%).

Discussion

The aim of this cross-country study was to examine the complex interactions through which internet/smartphone addiction, mental health issues, and personality traits affect the academic achievement of university students in the Czech Republic and China. The results revealed different patterns of interactions for the two groups of students with slightly different mix of variables at play. The findings confirmed the initial assumption that internet and smartphone addiction would be closely related. This relationship is to be expected as in recent years, with the increase in performance and capabilities of smartphones, these have become the primary tool through which the younger population communicates and consumes Internet content.

Surprisingly, the expected negative relationship of these addictions with academic achievement was found only for Czech students, while the relation was positive for Chinese students. There are several possible explanations for this difference. Firstly, previous studies have shown the significant role of smartphones and the Internet in Chinese students' social and academic life. Chinese population deeply integrated digital platforms, such as WeChat, QQ, and various online educational forums, blurring the lines between online and offline interactions. A student with high engagement on these platforms might be better adapted socially and academically, as these platforms serve multifunctional purposes, from social communication to academic collaboration [101, 102]. Similarly, Bing et al. showed that intensive use of messaging apps in an academic setting can have a positive effect on students' learning outcome, as it promotes students' engagement and collaboration and thus better academic performance [53]. Moreover, internet addiction is associated with social adaptation, i.e., the domain of academic achievement, which contains a strong element of communication. Second, this deep integration of technology into education may outweigh the negative consequences of the presence of the addiction itself. In other words, although the cognitive-emotional-behavioural effects of smartphone/internet addiction are negative (including their effects on mental health [13, 20, 26, 30–38], as suggested, for example, by the strong link between internet addiction and anxiety in Chinese students), the direct effects of excessive use of these technologies on learning outcomes are positive. Thirdly and finally, the unprecedented challenges brought by the COVID-19 pandemic that led to a massive shift towards online education must be considered. Study by Chen et al. showed that, contrary to conventional concerns about technology addiction's negative impacts, such addiction may indeed have beneficial ramifications in specific academic spheres [103]. Their work revealed that heightened engagement with technology, often deemed as 'addiction', can correlate with improved social adaptation and better study performance among Chinese students.

On the other hand, in the Czech Republic, smartphones, the Internet, and other similar technologies are not such an integral part of studies and school-related content is not the primary focus of the use of these technologies. The excessive use of these technologies at the level of abuse and addiction thus does not bring any positive effect for the learning of these students and, on the contrary, the negative symptoms of smartphone/internet addiction, together with the impaired mental health and the difficulties and limitations that this addiction brings, have negative impacts on the learning outcomes and academic achievement of these students. In this respect, the findings for Czech students are consistent with a number of previous studies [15, 20, 25, 26, 36, 38, 104]. However, it should be noted that some studies of Chinese students have also shown no or negative effects of smartphone/internet addiction [20, 105–107]. This highlights that the dynamics of this relationship in populations with high levels of technology integration into the learning process/life (including the balancing of positive and negative impacts) require further research. The initial assumption was thus fully confirmed for Czech students, while only partially (in the sense of the existence of a relationship between addictions and academic achievement) for Chinese students.

The results confirmed the expected negative relationship between mental health issues (specifically stress and anxiety) and academic achievement in both groups of students. This is consistent with previous studies [39–44, 108–110] and indicates the negative impact that disturbed cognitive-emotional settings have on academic performance. A positive relationship between anxiety and Internet addiction was also observed in Chinese students, which, in the context of the complex relationships in the model, suggests that internet addiction (which further positively affects academic coping) is closely associated with higher experienced anxiety. Thus, the effect of mental health issues can be considered negative overall. On the other hand, the predicted positive effect was confirmed for resilience, but only for Czech students. This relationship is consistent with previous studies that have shown that the ability to cope with challenging and adverse situations without significant disruption to normal functioning has a significant protective effect and is beneficial for academic achievement [77, 111–113]. For Chinese students, a competitive and demanding environment (with high expectations and pressures on academic performance) seems to creates a specific pressure to perform sufficiently, independent of the ability to cope with potential adversities, i.e., resilience, which then plays no significant role in student performance here.

The results also confirmed the role of personality traits on Internet addiction and academic achievement. As we predicted, greater Extraversion and Agreeableness had a positive effect on coping with social adaptation in academic achievement. These prosocial personality traits appear to promote interaction with others and involvement in the classroom collective, as well as increase the ability to obtain help and support from others in dealing with study-related demands [31, 61–63, 114]. Similarly, higher Conscientiousness was positively related to better academic performance, which is consistent with previous studies [115–118]. Students with higher Conscientiousness are generally self-disciplined, detail-oriented, thoughtful, careful, and organized, which is a key prerequisite for academic success [119, 120]. Intriguingly, in the Czech group, introverts and less conscientious students were more prone to internet addiction. Arguably, introverts may be highly attracted to features of the Internet that improve their social capabilities where they struggle offline [121]. Also poorer social connectedness (more typical for introverts) is associated with a greater risk of developing compulsive Internet use habits [122]. The reason why this was only observed in Czech students is probably the fact that Chinese students are forced to use technology to a much greater extent, regardless of their personality traits, in order to achieve success. Contrary to the initial assumptions, an effect of Openness to Experience and Emotional Stability was not observed. That may have been driven by greater variability in these personality traits (in the context of models of complex relationships designed in SEM analysis), or (masking of) the effects of these traits through other factors and personality characteristics. Regarding the role of sex, females scored better in academic performance in both samples, which is a typical trend [123–125]. Males were more at risk of technology addiction than females in the Czech sample, which is congruent with the survey findings before COVID-19 pandemics [75].

In terms of a general comparison of the patterns in the models of the two groups of students, a number of similarities in the factors included in the models was observed. At the same time, however, it was possible to reveal differences in the relationships between the variables. While the variables in Czech students form a relatively complex network among each other, the effect of most of the variables on academic achievement in Chinese students was compartmentalized into separate groups. The less interconnectedness of the variables for Chinese students likely stems from differences in the pressure to perform and to be collective compared to the more 'individualistic' and laissez-faire approach in the Czech Republic, which affect the extent to which the effect of some factors may be manifested or masked by pressure and influence from the external environment. The above findings and the comparison of regression coefficients between the two groups of students also confirmed the hypothesis that smartphone/internet addiction and Conscientiousness would have a stronger effect on academic achievement among Czech students.

Moreover, differences in the degree of explained variance of individual intermediate and outcome variables were observed, which indicates the extent to which the available data explain underlying processes and relationships. The models explained most of the internet addiction of Czech students (52.4%), but only 15.5% in the case of Chinese students. Possibly, the Chinese sample may be affected by other influential factors that were not researched in this study: mainly the overall orientation of Chinese society on communication via social medias [101, 102]. Prior research has often emphasized cultural nuances in how individuals engage with technology [126]. In the context of our findings, the high variance (52.4%) in explaining internet addiction among Czech students suggests that factors prevalent in their socio-cultural or educational environment might be closely tied to their digital habits. This might include the social norms surrounding technology use, the role of technology in daily life, or even curriculum structures that necessitate or discourage extended online interactions. In contrast, the lower variance (15.5%) for Chinese students might indicate that their internet habits are influenced by a broader array of factors, not all strictly tied to their immediate cultural or academic environment [12, 127]. The competitive educational environment, epitomized by rigorous examinations and high-stakes tests, can further push students towards online platforms. These platforms offer supplementary lessons, resources, and sometimes, a break from the rigors of academic life. However, this engagement, when driven by pressures rather than intrinsic motivation, may not translate directly into what is traditionally labelled as 'internet addiction' [128]. Furthermore, Chinese students often operate under immense pressure from both society and family to excel academically and secure a prosperous future [71, 129]. This external expectation can sometimes lead to coping mechanisms that may manifest as increased online activities, be it seeking academic resources, peer interactions, or simply avenues for relaxation and escape [130–132].

Similarly, we observed differences in the ability of the models to explain the variance of individual domains of academic achievement. While the model explained better the coping with study demands for Czech students (32.1% vs. 9.9% of explained variance in the Czech and Chinese samples, respectively), study performance itself was better explained by the observed variables for Chinese students (25.5% vs. 12.7% in the Chinese and Czech samples, respectively). These results are consistent with the interpretations described above. The rigorous and competitive educational landscape in China that prioritizes high academic achievements and exam scores [133, 134] forces students to manage all demands associated with studying at any cost, and the effects of the observed mental health- and personality-related factors do not have as much room to enter into this part of study life. At the same time, it is likely that coping with study-related demands is affected by other influential factors that were not researched in this study (such as self-esteem, self-efficacy, time management and self-organization skills, ability to prioritize school work, motivation, family/social/material support, and others). On the contrary, in the Czech Republic with an educational system that fosters independent thinking, problem-solving, and adaptability to various academic challenges [135, 136] and does not place such an emphasis on unconditional excellence and mastery of all demands, the possible effect of mental health- and personality-related factors is already manifested at the level of coping with study demands, which are then subsequently reflected in students' study performance. Thus, the effect of the observed factors on study performance is indirect.

This study has several practical implications. The results may be used to detect the population of students that need external support to achieve better academic performance. In the Czech (European) context, male students who are introverts, perceive a high level of stress, and show a low level of resilience or Conscientiousness seems to be at higher risk of academic underperformance. Also, smartphone/internet addiction has negative direct impact on non-performance components of academic achievement, whereas performance components are indirectly impacted by coping with study demands. This suggest that Czech male students with such characteristics might require more comprehensive interventions and supports to mitigate the effects of internet addiction and to bolster their academic achievements. The pronounced effect of sex in this context might be rooted in cultural, societal, or possibly biological differences that influence coping strategies or susceptibility to addiction. This warrants further exploration.

In the Chinese sample, the risk of low academic achievement is higher for males with high stress, anxiety, and a low level of Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness. Considering the Chinese cultural context, it is possible that societal expectations and pressures play a role in these observed gender differences. The Chinese educational system is notoriously competitive, and male students might experience unique stressors tied to societal expectations regarding their academic and eventual professional success [137, 138]. Smartphone/internet addiction was found to be negatively correlated to academic achievement; however, this finding may have only local relevance and more research is needed for its explanation.

The different patterns of relationships and interconnectedness of individual factors affecting academic achievement in the Czech and Chinese samples also suggest that interventions targeting individual factors may have different effectiveness in different cultural contexts. Indeed, the segregated effect of the factors for Chinese students means that interventions targeting one of the factors will have a narrower impact on academic achievement. For Czech students, the same intervention may be more effective/richer in impact due to greater interconnectedness between the variables.

Also, several recommendations for future research might be outlined. First, a more representative sample is needed to explore the relationship between smartphone/internet addiction and academic achievement in Chinese students. Future research could also explain dynamics behind the positive effect of smartphone/internet addiction on academic achievement. Second, this study found basic cross-country differences between both samples. More cross-country comparative studies or evidence synthesis comparing results from different countries is needed to identify the impact of demographic and cultural conditions on academic achievement in university students.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study sample was limited to a non-representative set of students from one university in each country. This may reduce the generalizability of the results, especially in the context of Chinese students (in the Czech Republic, the sample/findings can be considered broadly representative of the entire potential population, given the small demo-socio-geographic differences of students at individual universities in the Czech Republic), as well as simplifying to some extent the overall understanding of the data in terms of cross-country comparisons. Cultural variations within each country (e.g., between urban and rural populations) may lead to different patterns of technology use, mental health, and academic performance. Therefore, caution must be exercised when generalizing the results, as additional studies including multiple universities would provide a more comprehensive understanding of these relationships across broader populations.

Second, although the simulated structural models and their comparisons provide information about which combination of relationships and effects best fits the data, this is still a cross-sectional study, therefore it is not possible to evaluate the actual causality between variables. Longitudinal/experimental studies in the future should verify the relationships and interactions suggested by our models.

Third, the sex ratio was not equal and balanced in both groups of students, which may have slightly affected the results (especially within the model for Czech students). However, we consider the effect of this bias to be very small given the robustness of the structural models, the still acceptable number of male students, and the more complex structural models with multiple variables. Moreover, the proportions of males and females in the two groups of students are reasonably well representative of the actual proportions of students in the respective fields of study in the Czech Republic and China. Similarly, the two groups of students differed slightly in average age and most frequent grade. Although the structural models used are based in some way on intra-individual relationships between a complex group of variables, previous bivariate studies have shown that there may be differences in the strength of relationships between variables across grades at this age period. Thus, the observed difference between the two groups of students may have had some effect on the results. However, this effect, if present, could be described as negligible. Nonetheless, future studies could test the validity of our findings when controlling for/adjusting for student age and grade.

Fourth, this study conceptualized Internet addiction in terms of the presence of disruption of normal functioning due to Internet use in general terms. However, this approach overlooks to some extent the more nuanced nature of content-specific Internet use-related addictions. Moreover, empirical studies provide inconsistent criteria to define internet addiction, impacting the generalizability and comparability of findings across different contexts. Future research should consider these subtypes and the broader spectrum of internet-use behaviours to offer a more nuanced understanding of internet addiction's impact on mental health and academic performance [139]. The current understanding of internet addiction would benefit from distinguishing between various forms of problematic internet use, such as excessive gaming, social media use, or compulsive online shopping [140]. These subtypes can have different psychological and behavioural profiles, which require distinct prevention and intervention strategies. By examining the broader spectrum of internet-use behaviours, researchers can better understand the diverse impacts of internet addiction on individuals' lives. This approach can enhance the development of targeted treatments and support systems that address the specific needs of those struggling with different forms of internet-related issues.

Finally, the data were collected using self-assessment instruments, which may have affected the objectivity of the responses due to the subjective nature of individual respondents' answers. However, this approach is a common practice in psychological research and the risk of subjective bias was reduced using validated instruments, which have repeatedly proven their effectiveness in objectively measuring the phenomena under study. In any case, confirmation of our findings in future studies implementing other forms of data collection and/or experimental design would be beneficial.

Conclusion

This study is one of the few examining the complex relationships between mental health- and personality-related variables and academic achievement in a cross-country design. Path analysis yielded several significant findings. First, academic achievement was affected by both mental health issues (anxiety, stress, smartphone/internet addiction) and personality traits (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness), conforming to previous studies. Many of these effects overlapped in both samples, with some being specific to one or the other group of students. Second, the culturally/socially imposed pressure to excel among Chinese students seems to result in improved academic achievement despite/due to the excessive overuse of smartphones/internet (driven by the desire to meet these expectations). Thirdly and finally, the effect of the individual determinants analysed is culture-/country-dependent. While the Czech students showed a relatively complex net of interactions between the variables, the effect of these variables on academic achievement for Chinese students was fragmented into distinct clusters. These findings have implications for future research and practice, namely, they identify target variables for interventions to support and improve academic achievement of university students and highlight the need to consider cultural context when planning such interventions.
