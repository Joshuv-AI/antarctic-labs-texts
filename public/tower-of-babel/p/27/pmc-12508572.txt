Rare case of extralymphatic dirofilariasis presenting as a facial subcutaneous nodule in non-endemic region of Spain
Authors: Asuncion Maria Gonzálvez-Gasch, Guzman Lopardo Villarino, Enrique Bernal Morell, José Moltó Marhuenda, Raquel Martínez Goñi, Marta Rolo Téllez, Maria Carmen Martín-Higuera, Rosa Maria Cañon Rodríguez, Juan Enrique Costell, Marta Valles Bergada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFilarial infestation is highly prevalent in tropical countries, lymphangitis and chronic lymphedema being the most common complaints in these regions. Yet, extralymphatic filariasis outside endemic areas is an extremely rare scenario.Case PresentationWe report a case of filariasis in the south of Spain. A previously healthy 49-year-old male patient reported a subcutaneous facial swelling. Surgery showed an encysted solitary filarial worm.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is a rare clinical manifestation of filariasis in non-endemical regions in Spain. Still, it should be kept in mind considering the increasing number of travelers and migratory phenomena, the changing climatic factors and the typical ultrasound findings.Keywords: Dirofilariasis, Subcutaneous dirofilariasis, Zoonotic diseases

Introduction

Filariasis is a group of chronic parasitic infestations that have been classified as “neglected tropical disease” by the World Health Organization, partly due to the low investment in research into new treatments [1]. Although the disease is usually limited to tropical areas, it is object of growing concern as new cases have been diagnosed in recent years in non-tropical countries, mainly in immigrants and travelers [2].

Classic filariasis that affect humans are caused by nematode worms from the Filaroidea family. There are other zoonotic filariases that affect humans very rarely, including Dirofilaria repens [3] which worms may parasite subcutaneous and intramuscular connective tissues of carnivorous animals (such as wolves or foxes) and dogs, the latter representing the major reservoirs of infestation. Microfilariae are released in the peripheral blood and are picked up by mosquito vectors, the intermediate host, during the blood meal [4]. Most of the infective larvae die shortly after the mosquito bite, with the infection resolving unrecognized and without causing any specific symptom. When symptomatic, filariasis most commonly presents as hydrocele or lymphedema involving the lower limbs.

We report a rare case of extralymphatic filariasis due to Dirofilaria repens diagnosed by clinical, ultrasonographic and histopathological examination.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old Spanish male patient with no relevant past medical history complained about a small painless swelling on his right cheek for 6 months. Three days before admission, he presented a sharp pain along with a threefold size increase of his right cheek swelling, measuring by that time around 3 cm. There were no other complaints. Physical examination confirmed the right cheek nodule; his general condition was otherwise good. All parameters in routine blood test, including leukocytes and reactive C protein, were unremarkable. Real-time high-resolution ultrasonography revealed a solitary well-defined subcutaneous cystic lesion in his right cheek with multiple linear echogenic images within. The echogenic structures exhibited a persistent twirling motion during the examination (video 1).

All symptoms resolved within 48 h after surgical removal. Additional empirical 2-weeks treatment with Doxicicline and Prednisone was delivered. The patient denied recent international travel. He had a domestic dog and admitted frequent mosquito bites throughout the year. Besides coprological and serologic tests, a chest X-ray along with a head and neck computed tomography scan were performed during admission, without any remarkable findings other than a facial subcutaneous cystic lesion with thin linear hypointensities. Microfilariae molecular analysis in bloodstream was not requested. The patient was outpatiently monitored for new symptoms, remaining asymptomatic to date.

Discussion

Dirofilariasis is a vector-borne zoonotic disease that mainly affects canids (the major reservoirs of infestation) and accidentally humans. Among nematodes with a zoonotic potential, Dirofilaria repens and Dirofilaria immitis play a significant role from a public health perspective [4]. Canine dirofilariasis by D. repens has been reported along the Mediterranean coast, still very few cases of microfilaremic dogs have been communicated in Spain and its prevalence is not uniform throughout the territory [5], [6]. Both humans and dogs acquire dirofilariasis infection in the same manner, namely after the bite of a mosquito species, mostly from the Culicidae family [7].

The life cycle of Dirofilaria repens entails larval, preadult and mature stages. The adult female worm can produce microfilariae within a period of nearly 6–9 months [3]. The presence of the bacteria Wollbacia sp has been proved to be required for successful molting and embryogenesis of filariae [8], suggesting its possible role as therapeutic target for filariasis.

In the main, infective Dirofilaria repens larvae in humans are detected by the immune system resulting in the destruction of the parasite and the bacteria Wollbacia sp prior to the infestation being recognized [9].

Human infection is uncommon, and most cases are asymptomatic [10], [11]. Rarely can Dirofilaria repens larvae survive and become adult worms in humans, with subcutaneous and ocular tissues inflammation as main clinical presentations. Only in some cases, microfilariae are present in blood stream and the number of parasites detected is low (1–12/mL) compared with dogs. If present, microfilaremia is transient, with short survival [12], [13], which is why humans are considered to be dead-end hosts for these helminths [4].

No predisposing factors are known to explain why in some cases larvae may develop further. There seem to be two immunological patterns [14]. Firstly, patients who tolerate the parasitic infection because they have lived in endemic regions. In this setting, clinical manifestations are negligible until the parasitic load is very high. Secondly, patients not being tolerant to the parasite. Such patients usually present severe symptoms with a very reduced filaremia.

To our knowledge, there are very few cases of confirmed Dirofilaria repens human infection in Spain [15], [16]. More recently, some imported and autochthonous cases of individuals infected by Dirofilaria repens presenting with cutaneous nodules have been communicated in our country [17], [18], [19], [20], [21].

Current regional epidemiological data remains unknown [6]. Subcutaneous dirofilariasis is claimed to be underestimated due to its infrequent presentation and spontaneous healing. Human cutaneous dirofilariasis can sometimes be challenging to diagnose, with nodules often mistaken for cancerous lesions. Only after removal of the nodule can the parasite be diagnosed [22].

Particularly outstanding is the fact of identifying a live adult extralymphatic filarial worm infestation in a facial subcutaneous nodule in a non-endemic region. Furthermore, the patient presented without eosinophilia, had no previous medical record, immune disorders or epidemiological remarkable data. No travel history was reported. However, the patient lived close to an inland fresh-water aquatic ecosystem and suffered frequent mosquito bites throughout the year, which has been reported to increase the risk of transmission of vector-borne diseases, such as filariasis affecting both animal and human populations [23], [24]. Filariasis distribution is known to be directly linked to the presence of vectors, whose life cycle is in turn closely associated with the existence of freshwater bodies (stagnant water areas) and climatic factors (humidity and temperature), as the molting period of larvae in the vector is shortened under these circumstances [25], [26], [22]. These factors may have contributed to D. repens infection in a non-endemic region in Spain.

Due to the rareness of Dirofilaria repens infection in humans, there are no guidelines as to which is the most appropriate approach for this condition. There is general agreement that no specific treatment is needed for subcutaneous nodules following a Dirofilaria repens infection other than nodule surgical removal with standard procedures according to the site of infection. As soon as Dirofilaria repens is removed, no further medical treatment is required [10], [11]. In our case, empirical oral treatment was delivered before diagnosis was confirmed. Doxycycline is primarily used to target Wolbachia, an endosymbiotic bacteria in multiple filarial species which is necessary for the successful molting of filaria. Additionally, oral steroid treatment with Prednisone was prescribed to control a possible inflammatory reaction linked to components of the dying microfilariae [27], [28].

Human population growth, increasing globalization as well as changes in socioeconomic patterns, travels, migratory trends and animal husbandry practices can result in epidemiological zoonosis changes [6], [29]. Besides, global warming and climate change affecting mosquito vectors may play a significant role in transmission dynamics of parasitic zoonoses in regions where they were previously absent. These factors may partly explain the increased prevalence of dirofilarial infections in non-endemic areas. Yet, some authors do believe that undiagnosed dogs perpetuating the life-cycle of D. repens may account for most of dirofilariasis zoonotic infection in non-endemic regions [6], [25].

Following the above-mentioned factors, it is crucial for physicians worldwide to be aware of the “filarial dance sign”, which is a highly specific ultrasonography finding of live adult filarial worms inside subcutaneous [30]. Additionally, awareness among veterinarians and dog owners are of utmost relevance to reduce the impact of these zoonotic agents. Likewise, public health experts should pledge to improve knowledge of D. repens infection in non-endemic areas. All new cases should be recorded to determine the current epidemiological situation and increase the recognition of this rare infection.
