Unmet healthcare needs, out-of-pocket payments and catastrophic health expenditures among hypertensive adults in Bangladesh
Authors: Md Mizanur Rahman, Md Safayet Hossain, Ryota Nakamura, Md Ashraful Alam, Syed Khurram Azmat, Motohiro Sato
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to examine unmet healthcare needs and the burden of out-of-pocket (OOP) payments in Bangladesh among hypertensive adults using the most recent survey data.MethodsA total of 5086 hypertensive patients aged 18 to 80 were recruited from 75 pharmacies in Bangladesh in 2023, 35 being located in urban areas and 40 in rural areas. Unmet healthcare needs was the primary outcome variable, while the incidence of catastrophic health expenditures (CHE) was the secondary outcome variable. A multilevel logistic regression model was performed to identify factors associated with unmet healthcare needs and CHE. A multilevel Tobit regression model was used to identify the determinants of OOP health expenditures.ResultsThe study indicated that the prevalence of unmet healthcare needs among hypertensive adults was around 26% and incidence of CHE was 46% at 10% threshold of total consumption in Bangladesh. The most common reason for unmet healthcare needs was affordability, long waiting times, lack of availability, and transportation issues etc. Unmet healthcare needs were more prevalent among men, individuals with no education, divorced/separated, non-Muslims and poor population. Regression models suggested that older people, men, those with higher education, Muslim, married people, larger household, overweight and obese people, and rural residents were more likely to burden of OOP expenses.ConclusionsHigh unmet needs and CHE prevalence in Bangladesh reveal inadequate health risk protection. Prioritizing affordability, addressing disparities, and strengthening financial risk protection can improve access and outcomes for hypertensive adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13121-w.Keywords: OOP payments, CHE, Unmet healthcare needs, Multilevel analysis, Tobit model, Bangladesh

Introduction

Universal health coverage (UHC) is a fundamental goal of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) that aims to provide everyone with essential healthcare services without financial hardship [1, 2]. By ensuring that individuals are protected from the financial risks associated with seeking healthcare, UHC can improve access to healthcare and reduce inequities in health and financial risk associated with healthcare costs [3]. However, challenges such as inadequate risk pooling mechanisms, reliance on out-of-pocket (OOP) payments, and socio-demographic and cultural factors continue to impede people’s ability to access healthcare when needed [4–9]. This leads to unmet healthcare needs and financial burdens, where individuals are unable to receive the quality care they require. To achieve equitable healthcare access for all, it is crucial to monitor and address these barriers using up-to-date survey data [10].

The health system in Bangladesh is underfunded and lacks health insurance coverage, and OOP is the main source of healthcare financing [11]. In Bangladesh, OOP payments account for 67% of health expenditures [12]. Bangladesh spends approximately 2.36% of its GDP on health including both public and private health expenditures, which is lower than the healthcare spending of other South-East Asia countries, such as the Maldives (10.01%), Thailand (5.16%), Sri Lanka (4.07%), India (3.28%), Nepal (5.42%), Bhutan (3.85%), Indonesia (3.71%), Myanmar (5.63%), and Timor-Leste (11.44%) [13]. Consequently, Bangladesh has a high incidence of catastrophic health expenditures (CHE), low coverage of essential health services, and wide inequities in many health indicators compared to other South Asian countries [14].

We conducted an electronic database search in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and CINAHL up to June 2022 to identify prior evidence from South Asia countries. From 3039 initial articles, 1984 were screened after duplicate removal. Only 24 articles from 2010 to 2024 reported unmet healthcare needs, forgone care, or care-seeking behavior. Of the 24 studies, nine were from India [15–24], six from Thailand [25–30], two each from Bangladesh [26, 31, 32], Nepal [33, 34], one from each of the Maldives [35], North Korea [36], and Myanmar [26, 37] where one study has considered multi-countries [26]. The 24 identified studies differed considerably in their measurement of unmet healthcare needs, forgone healthcare, and care-seeking behavior, using various types of questions, recall periods, and units of measurement, such as households or individuals. Moreover, high cost of hypertension treatment often results in unmet healthcare needs among hypertensive patients in Bangladesh. Additionally, lack of access to healthcare services further compounds the burden, indicating a crucial gap that requires immediate response to ensure effective hypertension management and equitable health outcomes. The lack of targeted surveys or questionnaires for this population reveals a research gap in comprehending their healthcare challenges. To understand the prevalence, risk factors, and reasons for unmet healthcare needs, we used the most common validated questionnaires used by OCED (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development) and other countries. Since many Asian countries, such as Bangladesh, are experiencing demographic shifts, obtaining data on unmet needs and their underlying causes is crucial for guiding the development of integrated long-term care systems and the implementation of health system reforms.

The objectives of this study were to assess the prevalence of unmet healthcare needs and the financial burden of OOP payments and catastrophic health expenditures among hypertensive individuals in Bangladesh. Additionally, the study aimed to identify the factors contributing to these unmet needs and financial burdens. The findings could inform policymakers and healthcare planners in devising strategies to improve access to affordable healthcare and reduce financial barriers for patients.

Methods

Settings

The study was conducted in two districts in Bangladesh: Rangpur and Chuadanga (Fig. 1). From each district, a subdistrict was chosen at random: Pirganj and Alamdanga. According to the Population Census 2022, Bangladesh had 165 million population, 31% lived in rural areas, and 69% in urban areas [38]. Chuadanga district had a population of 1,234,066, with 24% living in urban areas and 76% living in rural areas. In Rangpur, the population was 3,169,615, with 33% living in the urban area and 67% living in the rural. The average household size was about 4 people (3.78 in Chuadanga district and 3.80 in Rangpur). Pirganj is located in Rangpur District and covers an area of 409.37 square kilometres with a literacy rate of 50.3%. Alamdanga is located in Chuadanga District and covers an area of 380.38 square kilometres with a literacy rate of 44.5% [14].

Study area selection, Bangladesh

Sampling

We used a multistage random sampling method in this study to select the study sample. First, we selected two districts (Rangpur and Chuadanga) out of 64 districts of Bangladesh, considering the socio-economic profile of each district and the logistics side. Next, two subdistricts were randomly selected: Pirganj from Rangpur and Alamdanga from Chudanga. As the final step, we randomly selected 75 community pharmacies in Pirganj (38 pharmacies) and Alamdanga (37 pharmacies), serving as primary sampling units (PSUs). In rural areas, 3,600 hypertensive adults were recruited from 40 community pharmacies as part of an ongoing cluster-randomized controlled trial (cRCT) [39]. For urban areas, 1,486 hypertensive adults were recruited from 35 pharmacies.

Data collection

Data were collected based on the following eligibility criteria: participants had to be 18 years or older, have hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher and/or diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher [40–42] or be currently taking antihypertensive medication), speak the local language, live in the study area, have a mobile phone, and provide consent. The exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, advanced medical conditions, and cognitive or psychiatric disorders.

Data collection for this study was conducted through face-to-face interviews and structured questionnaires. The interview team consisted of those with postgraduate degrees in various fields relating to social science, public health, demography, and statistics, all of whom had extensive experience in surveys. Two experienced supervisors were present to oversee the survey implementation and to provide the guidelines to the enumerators. Prior to data collection, the team underwent comprehensive training, covering topics including questionnaire content, obtaining informed consent, and strategies for acquiring complete and valid data. The collected data included self-reported socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of the patients, household expenditure (food, health, housing, and other non-food expenditures), household income, and unmet healthcare needs. The data also included the patients’ disease history (respiratory, cardiovascular, diabetes, and hypertension); health and behavioral indicators, including blood pressure, fasting blood glucose level, weight, height, daily habits, physical activity, and smoking status; and health-related quality of life (based on the EQ-5D-5 L questionnaire). In the current research, we used the Bangladeshi version of the EQ-5D-5 L questionnaire, which was self-report administered. As a country-specific value set for Bangladesh has not yet been established, we estimated the EQ-5D-5 L index scores using the Indian value set. This is a common practice in the same type of studies, due to the broad social, cultural, and economic similarities between Bangladesh and India, which makes the Indian value set an acceptable proxy in our study context. EQ-5D-5 L health states and EQ-VAS scores were directly elicited from the respondents. The utility scores were anchored between 0 (worst health) and 1 (perfect health) and were based on self-reported health states. The complete questionnaire is provided in the Appendix (see Questionnaire).

Outcome variables

The primary outcome variable was unmet healthcare needs. OOP payments and CHE were secondary outcomes. Participants were asked whether they had needed medical treatment but did not receive it in the past 12 months (“yes,” “no,” and “don’t know”). If the answer was “no”, respondents were asked why they could not receive treatment. The reasons included treatment costs, health facilities not being available in the area, long waiting lists, distance or lack of transportation, not knowing any good doctors and specialists, not being available when required, being too busy with work, fear of doctors, hospitals, or treatments, and other reasons. For analysis, we recoded into a binary variable, where “yes” indicated the presence of unmet need, and both “no” and “don’t know” responses were coded as “no”. This decision was based on the very low frequency of “don’t know” responses, with only 12 respondents (0.24%), which made it impractical to analyze as a separate group. Household’s OOP payments refer to the amount of money the members of household paid out of pocket for health care services. OOP healthcare expenditure was considered ‘catastrophic’ if it exceeded 10% of the household’s consumption expenditure as its widespread use in similar low-and middle-income settings.

Predictor variables

The study included a number of predictor variables such as age (≤ 30, 31–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, ≥ 70), sex (male, female), education level (no education, primary, secondary, higher), religion (Muslim, Non-Muslim), marital status (married, others), body mass index (underweight, normal weight, overweight, obese), household size (1–2, 3–4, ≥ 5), expenditure quintiles (Q1 for poorest, Q2, Q3, Q4, and Q5 for richest), and residence (urban, rural).

The household expenditure quintile (from the lowest 20% to the highest 20%) was determined following Xu and colleagues [43].

Statistical analysis

First, we utilized descriptive statistics to summarize the sociodemographic and other characteristics of the participants. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, while continuous variables such as household expenditure and monthly out-of-pocket payments were summarized using means and standard deviations. Second, we performed a multilevel logistic regression model with random intercept terms for community pharmacy in the inferential analysis. Multilevel modelling techniques were used to analyze data collected from various levels, especially when our outcome was measured at the lowest level, i.e. no. This approach allowed us to assess individuals’ self-reported health while considering both personal factors including age as well as broader contextual factors such as household income quintile and geographic location. If each observation was independent (e.g., one observation per household or community), a simpler single-level regression could have been used. However, due to the uniform contextual effect, simple regression cannot be used within dependencies or between heterogeneities if the observations come from a cluster. Therefore, our model allowed us to examine the likelihood of unmet healthcare needs by assessing the odds in relation to selected covariates, thereby identifying significant factors associated with these unmet needs across different regions. We also conducted a multilevel Tobit regression analysis to examine the determinants of OOP health expenditures using the same predictor variables. Additionally, the risks of CHE were estimated using a multilevel logistic regression model. Lastly, a multinomial logistic regression model was used to examine the determinants of healthcare access burden. All statistical analyses were performed by Stata v17.0.

Participants’ ages ranged from under 30 (5%) to over 70 (10.2%), and 41% had no formal education while only 14% had higher education. Employment was higher among males (87%) than females (2%), and 71% of respondents lived in rural areas. The prevalence of unmet need was reported approximately 26.45%. The average monthly household expenditure was 13,708 BDT (1 USD = 104.39 BDT), with the average OOP health expenditure was 1,822 BDT.

Prevalence and determinants of unmet healthcare needs

Table 2 presents the prevalence of unmet healthcare needs by socio-demographic characteristics. The results show that unmet healthcare needs were significantly correlated with sex, education, religion, marital status, residence, body mass index (BMI), and household expenditure quintiles. Around 26% of individuals have unmet healthcare needs. The prevalence of unmet healthcare needs was significantly higher among certain socio-demographic groups including older individuals such as 32.6% for the age group 60–69 years and 37.4% for those aged ≥ 70 years and above compared to younger individuals (19.6% for the age group 31–39 years) and women (33%). Additionally, the prevalence was also significantly higher among individuals with no education (41.2%) than among those with higher education (7.47%). The highest prevalence was found in the Q1 (poorest) group at 50.6%, whereas the lowest was found in the Q5 (richest) group at 4.6%.

Table 2 also represents the odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the factors associated with unmet healthcare needs. Individuals in the age category 31–39 had 1.72 times (95% CI = 1.05–2.83) higher odds of unmet healthcare needs than those aged ≤ 30. Notably, men had 16% (OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.71–1.01) lower odds of unmet healthcare needs than women. The findings also indicate that individuals with no education, primary education, and secondary education have 3.75 times (95% CI, 0.54–0.96), 2.71 times (95% CI, 1.94–3.81), and 1.56 times (95% CI, 1.08–2.26) higher odds of unmet healthcare needs, respectively, compared to those with higher education. Furthermore, non-Muslims had 1.43 times (95% CI, 1.01–2.04) higher odds of unmet healthcare needs than Muslims. Unmet healthcare needs were also correlated with BMI categories, household size, and expenditure quintiles. Poorest people (Q1) were almost 10 times more likely to experience unmet healthcare needs than the richest (OR, 10.10; 95% CI, 7.00-14.40). In addition, the unmet healthcare needs of the Q2 were approximately 8 times (OR, 8.00; 95% CI, 5.65–11.33) greater than those of the wealthiest quintile.

Reasons for unmet healthcare need

Figure 2 presents the prevalence of specific reasons for unmet healthcare needs. Affordability was the most common barrier, affecting 75.4% of respondents. There were also several other reasons for unmet healthcare needs such as long waiting time (7.55%), not being available in the area (4.48%), “too far to travel/no means of transportation” (2.65%), not knowing any good doctors or specialists (2.48%), not being available at times (2.01%), and working too hard (1.95%). There were the fewest number of respondents (0.59%) who were afraid of a doctor, hospital, or treatment.

Prevalence and risk factors for CHE

Table 4 presents the prevalence and risk factors associated with CHE. The results showed that individuals aged 70 years and above had the highest prevalence of CHE at 54.53%, while those aged 30 years or younger had the lowest prevalence at 32.28%. Men had a slightly higher prevalence of CHE at 50.02% than females at 40.88%. Additionally, individuals with no education exhibited the highest prevalence of CHE at 50.29%, followed by primary education (46.41%) and higher education (37.18%). Conversely, those with secondary education had the lowest prevalence at 37.18%. Muslims had a slightly higher rate of CHE (46.20%) compared to non-Muslims (43.29%). Additionally, the prevalence of CHE was lower among married individuals (44.51%) compared to non-married individuals (55.04%). Normal-weight people had a lower CHE than underweight, overweight, and obese people, who had 49.12%, 48.33%, and 50.53%, respectively. Rural residents experienced a higher prevalence of CHE (54.03%) than urban residents (26.78%).

The multilevel logistic regression analysis identified several significant predictor variables for CHE including age, gender, education, household size, weight, household expenditure quintile, and place of residence. The odds of incurring CHE increased with age, with individuals aged 60–69 having a 1.92 times greater risk than those aged ≤ 30 (OR = 1.92; CI = 1.17–2.91). Lower education levels were associated with lower odds of CHE, with individuals with no education having a 61% lower risk (OR = 0.39; CI = 0.29–0.50) compared to those with higher education. Additionally, the wealthier population had a lower risk of CHE than the poorer population. Moreover, multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify risk factors associated with both unmet healthcare needs and CHE (see Appendix Table S1). The findings revealed that individuals with higher education levels have increased odds of facing both unmet healthcare needs and CHE. Additionally, those in the poorest quintile (Q1) have significantly higher odds of experiencing these challenges compared to the wealthiest group.

Discussion

To understand the health system’s performance, our study examined the prevalence of unmet healthcare needs, OOP expenditures, and CHE among hypertensive individuals in Bangladesh. The results revealed that around 26% of hypertensive adults had unmet healthcare needs, and 46% experienced CHE. The common reasons for unmet healthcare needs were long waiting times, lack of availability, transportation issues, and not knowing good doctors or specialists, while fear of doctors, hospitals, or treatment was the least reported. Factors such as age, gender, education, religion, marital status, household size, BMI, household wealth, and rural or urban residence were significantly associated with unmet healthcare needs, OOP health spending, and CHE.

This study indicated that unmet healthcare needs are higher in older individuals, indicating the vulnerability of older adults in terms of basic needs. This might be due to chronic health conditions, financial issues, limited mobility, social isolation, inadequate support systems, and challenges in accessing healthcare [44–47]. Aligning with a previous study, the results showed that males were less prone to experience unmet needs than females [48]. Moreover, individuals in the younger group (31–39 years) had higher odds of unmet healthcare needs than those aged ≤ 30 years. This may be because the working-age population has more difficulty accessing healthcare, possibly due to challenges in finding time to visit doctors compared to younger and older individuals. Furthermore, lower education levels are significantly correlated with higher unmet needs [19]. For example, people with no education have a higher risk of unmet healthcare needs than people with higher education, because individuals with low education often lack health literacy [46]. Again, this can be attributed to the lack of awareness about healthcare needs and lack of education. Our study data showed that a significant portion of hypertensive adults with lower education levels lacked health literacy compared to those with higher education (result not shown). Specifically, 56% of the lower-educated group were unaware that excess salt intake causes hypertension, compared to 5% in the higher-educated group. Similarly, 64% of the less-educated group did not know obesity was a cause, versus 3% of the educated group; 55% were unaware of the risks of a sedentary lifestyle, compared to 7%; 54% did not recognize alcohol consumption as a factor, compared to 8%; and 58% were unaware of the importance of daily exercise, compared to 7%. Additionally, 52% of the lower-educated group lacked knowledge about the impact of sleep deprivation, while this was true for only 9% of the higher-educated group.

Similar to previous study [49], different religious groups view healthcare differently and married individuals have family members’ support when seeking healthcare. Individuals with normal and overweight have better health outcomes and access to healthcare. Conversely, this study found that people who are underweight have a higher prevalence compared to overweight and obese, indicating nutritional deficiencies and socioeconomic factors play a significant role in accessing healthcare needs. This result is consistent with other studies [50]. Studies have shown that financial problems are a crucial component that have a well-documented association between unmet healthcare needs and lower income [51]. In keeping with the literature, the study findings showed that poorer individuals face significant financial barriers to accessing healthcare, including OOP expenses, limited healthcare infrastructure, inadequate insurance coverage, indirect costs, and lower health literacy [46, 48, 52]. This could be because most patients in Bangladesh are responsible for covering the costs of medications. This finding is in line with a study by Momtaz and colleagues which showed that older men in rural areas have a higher risk of facing unmet needs [53]. Despite the government’s efforts to expand healthcare services in rural and remote areas, these regions continue to face a significant shortage of healthcare personnel.

Our study revealed that Bangladeshi people have a high burden of OOP payments and around 46% experience CHE. These results are consistent with some previous literature [54–61]. The study results showed that OOP payments and CHE differ greatly based on sociodemographic factors. Specifically, the results align with prior studies in identifying sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, and rural residence as significant determinants of healthcare costs [51, 62, 63]. This suggests that accessibility not only depends on health coverage but also on socioeconomic factors that are associated with OOP payment and CHE. In general, OOP expenses significantly increase with age and are comparatively higher for elderly people which is almost 1.92 times than younger age groups [64]. Possible reasons for higher OOP payments with age include a higher prevalence of diseases necessitating costly treatments and potentially limited coverage by health insurance plans for elderly individuals. Furthermore, individuals with no education exhibited the highest prevalence of CHE, whereas those with higher education had similar prevalence of CHE, suggesting that prevalence does not significantly vary by education. Affordability was significantly associated with the increased risk of unmet healthcare needs among the poorest quintile. People in this group often lack the financial resources to afford essential medical services, resulting in delayed or neglected treatment [65]. High OOP costs can deter them from seeking necessary medical attention, leading to unmet healthcare needs [66]. It is clear from our study that the level of education and expenditure quintile are significant predictors of CHE as well as unmet healthcare needs. The study contributes significantly to the literature and has a few key messages. Firstly, a considerable proportion of people in Bangladesh are experiencing unmet healthcare needs. Secondly, socioeconomic and demographic characteristics do have an impact on basic unmet healthcare needs. Finally, the study results indicated that financial constraints are one of the top barriers to accessing healthcare facilities.

This study has some major strengths. Firstly, our study followed standard methodology to measure the prevalence of unmet healthcare needs. Secondly, this study benefits from a significantly larger sample size compared to previous regional studies, thereby enhancing the reliability and robustness of its findings. There are several limitations to the study. Although the sample size is large, the data have been taken from only two districts in Bangladesh. Despite the study only including two districts, the household sizes in rural and urban areas are quite similar to national surveys, ensuring that the results can be reliably generalised. Furthermore, this study solely deals with hypertensive patients, there is a possibility that the prevalence of unmet needs is likely to be much lower in the general population. However, the robustness of the findings is strengthened by the large sample size and their alignment with national demographic patterns.

Conclusion

The study reveals a substantial prevalence of unmet healthcare needs and catastrophic health expenditures among hypertensive adults in Bangladesh. Around 26% of individuals experience unmet healthcare needs, often due to barriers such as waiting times, lack of availability, transportation issues, etc. Moreover, 46% face catastrophic health expenditures driven by high OOP payments, inadequate health insurance coverage, and financial constraints. Older individuals, those with lower education levels, and rural residents are particularly vulnerable and require attention to improve healthcare access and financial protection. Socioeconomic factors such as age, gender, education level, household wealth, and rural or urban residence significantly influence healthcare spending patterns and the likelihood of financial hardship due to healthcare costs. Reducing out-of-pocket costs, strengthening the healthcare system, boosting health literacy, and increasing access to health insurance coverage, particularly for vulnerable populations, are all necessary legislative measures to address these issues. By addressing these disparities, the burden of high healthcare costs can be mitigated and may help improve the quality of life for hypertensive individuals in Bangladesh.
