Three delays of malaria care-seeking in Malawi: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bolanle Olapeju, Michael Bride, Tyson Volkmann, Shelby Cash, Edson Dembo, Michael Kayange, Austin Gumbo, Taonga Mafuleka, Jennifer Boyle, Alfred Mang’ando, Nyanyiwe Masingi Mbeye, Chime Mukwakwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify psychosocial and structural barriers to prompt malaria care-seeking in Malawi by applying the Three Delays Model (delay 1: deciding to seek care; delay 2: reaching a facility; delay 3: receiving quality care).Designcross-sectional study.SettingNationally representative data collected from Malawi communities between 25 May 2021 and 1 July 2021.Participants913 female caregivers who reported a child with fever in the past 2 weeks.Primary outcome measuresPrompt care-seeking for fever (same or next day) from a qualified health provider.ResultsPrompt care-seeking was primarily associated with delay 1 (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.58, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.78) and psychosocial (aOR 0.59; 95% CI 0.44 to 0.79) factors. Significant factors included incorrect knowledge of malaria symptoms, cause and diagnosis (aOR 0.71: 95% CI 0.53 to 0.97), negative attitudes towards care-seeking (aOR 0.58; 95% CI 0.41 to 0.82), incorrect knowledge of when and where to seek care (aOR 0.19: 95% CI 0.07 to 0.50) and far distance from a health facility (aOR 0.67, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.93).ConclusionDespite the availability of free malaria services, significant bottlenecks remain in the initial decision-making phase. To reduce malaria mortality, national programmes should prioritise social and behaviour change interventions that move beyond general awareness to target specific care-seeking attitudes and intra-household decision-making dynamics.

Introduction

Background

Malaria is an ongoing public health problem in Malawi, with over 4 million new cases reported in 2020.1 The government of Malawi has identified malaria as a priority disease burden in its ongoing national health strategic plan and aims to eliminate malaria by 2030.2 Malaria elimination hinges on proper identification and treatment of suspected malaria cases and requires concerted effort to address both geographic and financial challenges and inequities when it comes to access to malaria services.1 Thus, a key objective in the national malaria strategic plan is to increase the proportion of caregivers of children under 5 years of age who take action to seek appropriate malaria treatment within 24 hours of the onset of fever to 90% by 2030.2

Malawi is currently operating its universal health coverage agenda, extending malaria service provision to hard-to-reach areas and providing malaria services free of charge at all service delivery points under the public-funded healthcare system.3 4 In 2021, care seeking for fever occurred primarily from the public health sector (93%), including 30% who sought care from a government health centre and 42% from a government hospital.5 However, despite free malaria services for children under 5 years of age at both the community and facility levels, prompt care-seeking (within 24 hours of the onset of fever) occurred for less than half (46%) of the children under 5 years of age with fever in 2021.5 In addition, prompt care seeking was the highest in the more populous and economically advanced south (54%) compared with the north (44%) and central (37%) regions of Malawi.5

Localised qualitative research in Malawi describes care-seeking for childhood fevers in two phases. Caregivers first assess the severity of fever and then second decide to seek care.6 However, the degree to which this is generalisable to the Malawi population has not been quantitatively determined. Identified factors impeding prompt or appropriate care-seeking from a qualified provider included structural factors such as geographic location,7 wealth status,7 distance to a health facility,8 supply chain stockouts of rapid diagnostic tests or artemisinin combination therapy9 10 and perceived provider knowledge.11 Of note, these studies do not explore psychosocial factors such as caregivers’ knowledge12 13 and attitudes14 related to malaria, decision-making agency,15 perceptions of susceptibility,16 effectiveness of care-seeking14 and supportive social norms,15 which have been shown in malaria-endemic settings to impact care-seeking from a health facility. Specific to Malawi, there has been no strategic comparison of psychosocial versus structural factors in the context of free service provision.

Study aims and rationale

This study aims to identify and compare (1) relevant factors in the Three Delays Model and (2) structural versus psychosocial factors influencing care-seeking for children under 5 years of age with fever in the context of free service provision in Malawi. A context-specific understanding of the interconnections between structural and psychosocial factors can inform the design of relevant demand and supply-side interventions to improve care-seeking for febrile children under 5 years of age in Malawi.

Conceptual framework

The Three Delays Model outlined by Thaddeus and Maine in 199417 highlights three phases during which delays in maternal health service provision occur: (1) delay in the decision to seek care; (2) delay in arrival at a health facility and (3) delay in the provision of adequate care.18 While originally developed to explain maternal mortality in obstetric emergencies, we adapted the Three Delays Model to care-seeking for fever and posit that these delays can be attributed to both structural and psychosocial factors within each level.19 Recently, the Three Delays Model has been used to understand healthcare seeking for fever20 and injuries among children21 as well as the drivers of infant mortality.22 Across multiple countries, the third delay was the most important delay for infant mortality22 while in Tanzania, caregiver knowledge and access to care were major delay factors.21 The objective of this study is to apply the Three Delays Model to survey data to identify which stage of delay and what type of delay are associated with delayed care seeking.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study draws data from the Malaria Behavior Survey (MBS), a cross-sectional, nationally representative study funded by the US President’s Malaria Initiative and implemented by the Johns Hopkins Center for Communication Programs’ Breakthrough ACTION project and the Malawi National Malaria Control Program. The MBS follows a standardised format and has been conducted in several countries in Africa with the key objective to assess sociodemographic and psychosocial factors related to key malaria-related behaviours.23 The MBS was a cross-sectional survey of randomly selected household heads, women and men interviewed using structured questionnaires (one for each group of study participants). Eligibility criteria for the women included the following:

A total of 3862 households and 5485 individuals (4181 women and 1304 men) enrolled in the MBS survey. The analytic dataset for this study is all women enrolled who were caregivers of children under 5 years of age with a fever in the 2 weeks preceding the survey (sample size = 913).

Power calculation

The sample size of 913 caregivers provides 83% power to detect the prevalence of prompt care-seeking (defined as care-seeking the same or the next day at the onset of fever from a health facility, facility provider or community provider) assuming a prevalence of 48% among under-five children (based on data from the 2017 Malaria Indicator Survey),24 a delta of 5% and an alpha (type 1 error rate) of 0.05.

Data collection

Data were collected between 26 May 2021 and 1 July 2021. Participant selection occurred through a multistep sampling process consisting of (1) probability proportional to size selection of the enumeration areas, based on the population size; (2) a random selection of eligible households (with at least one woman of reproductive age (15–49 years); (3) within selected households, selection of all eligible individuals (all women aged 15–49 years old and, in one-third of households, a male partner (aged 18–59 years) of an eligible woman). Specifically, in a third of households, the data collector randomly selected from the list of household members an eligible woman who also had a male partner aged 15–59 years. The partner was then recruited into the study to complete the men’s survey.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Key variables

Key variables and survey questions are included in online supplemental table 1. The outcome variable was prompt care-seeking from a qualified provider defined as care-seeking the same or the next day as the onset of fever from a health facility, facility provider or community provider.

Predictor variables were classified in the study either as structural versus psychosocial factors or by which level of delay in the Three Delays Model they applied to, as highlighted in table 1. Since this study explores the adaptation of the Three Delays Model from maternal health to child health, a priori consideration of the theoretical model constructs guided the classification of predictor variables. Household wealth was based on validated principal component analysis of housing structures and household assets recommended for Demographic and Health Survey data.25 The principal component analysis of housing structures (roof, floor and wall materials) and household assets (radio, television, computer, refrigerator, internet access, electric fan, air conditioner, watch, simple mobile phone, smartphone/tablet, bicycle, motorcycle/scooter, animal drawn cart, car/truck/van, boat with motor, land and livestock) was performed. The principal component analysis was then divided into wealth quintiles.

Contextual variables included the following: household size (≤4 people versus >4 people), age (15–24 years, 25–34 years and 35+ years), religion (Christian vs not), whether the caregiver was pregnant (yes vs no), age of the child with fever in months (0–11 months, 12–36 months, 37–60 months) and number of children with fever in the household (one versus >1) and exposure to communication channels, phone, radio or television, as a measure of access to information as well as potential reach or suitability of behaviour communication message channels.

Data analysis

The study performed data management and analysis using Stata V.17 (Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA). The data were weighted using the svyset command in Stata to make the data representative of the study population (accounting for probabilities of cluster and household selection). This is similar to weighting procedures for nationally representative datasets such as DHS.25

Each predictor variable and wealth quintile was weighted as a point per the literature.26 A composite score for each delay was created based on the total number of factors present. The first delay could range from 0 to 9, while the second delay from 1 to 11 as each wealth quintile was considered as an additional point, and the third delay could range from 1 to 4. Each delay score was subsequently dichotomised based on the median for comparability. Similarly, composite scores for positive psychosocial factors (ranging from0 to 12) and positive structural factors (ranging from 0 to 12 as each wealth quintile was considered as an additional point) were calculated. These scores were then dichotomised based on the median for comparability.

χ2 tests and crude logistic regressions illuminated the association between the different factors with prompt care-seeking. Multivariable logistic regression models explored factors associated with prompt care-seeking, controlling for contextual variables. Multivariable logistic regression models also explored the association between prompt care-seeking and composite scores for each delay level, as well as psychosocial and structural factors. The analytical dataset has been included as online supplemental material 1.

Of the 4181 women enrolled in the MBS, 913 were caregivers of children under the age of 5 years with a fever in the past 2 weeks preceding the survey. Prevalence of three delays factors

Figure 1 describes the prevalence of the factors related to the three delays of care-seeking for febrile children. For the first delay—deciding to seek care—most caregivers had not completed primary education (65%) while many did not discuss malaria with family (55%) or have correct knowledge about malaria symptoms, cause and testing (47%). For the second delay—getting to a health facility—most caregivers lived in rural areas (92%), far (≥5 km, 30 min by foot or 10 min by car) from a health facility (60%), with no mode of transportation (36%) in their household. Factors related to the third delay—getting adequate care—were generally favourable. Only a third of caregivers perceived that health providers made parents pay for malaria testing and medication (37%).

Discussion

Overview

The Malawi MBS was a first-of-its-kind national survey, providing a robust investigation of demographic, structural and psychosocial correlates of malaria-related behaviours recorded to date. This study explored factors at each level of the Three Delays Model and compared the magnitude of psychosocial versus structural correlates of prompt fever care-seeking, a key behavioural indicator tracked globally to monitor the coverage and success of malaria interventions. Strikingly, we found that the variables most strongly and independently associated with prompt care-seeking were psychosocial, such as general knowledge of malaria and positive attitudes towards care-seeking. Specifically, knowledge of when and where to seek care for fever showed the strongest statistical relationship with prompt care-seeking. Proximity to a health facility was the only structural factor to remain significant in the multivariable analysis. Furthermore, when analysed as a standardised score, the group of psychosocial variables demonstrated a stronger association with prompt care-seeking than the group of structural factors. These observations suggest that in the Malawi context, the first delay—deciding to seek care—is a critical focal point. Prior research suggests this may relate to low perceived severity or a preference for home remedies for simple fevers.6

Our findings align with other studies adapting the Three Delays Model to paediatric care-seeking, where knowledge (first delay), transportation challenges (second delay) and perceived treatment availability (third delay) were identified as notable factors.21 22 While those studies employed qualitative methods to explore caregiver perceptions of delay in hours, this study adds value by using a nationally representative sample to delineate the relative statistical importance of psychosocial versus structural correlates.

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this study is the use of a theory-driven approach to uncover factors influencing care-seeking. In addition, data came from a nationally representative survey successfully implemented in other African countries. Our data permit a robust investigation of demographic, structural and psychosocial correlates of malaria-related behaviours. We expect that our study findings might be generalisable to similar malaria-endemic settings in sub-Saharan Africa with free malaria services for children under 5 years of age. Nonetheless, this study acknowledges some limitations. First, the study was cross-sectional in design, limiting the ability to make causal inferences, as the temporal sequence between the identified correlates and the behaviour cannot be established. For example, it is unclear whether positive attitudes lead to prompt care-seeking or if the experience of seeking care shapes subsequent attitudes. Next, all data were self-reported by caregivers, introducing potential recall and social desirability biases. Also, the study did not measure health facility or provider-level factors directly, relying instead on caregivers’ perceptions as a proxy for the availability of commodities and behaviour of providers. We also note that the sample consisted primarily of infants, which may reflect a high prevalence of viral infections in the first year of life and limits generalisability to older children. Additionally, the transportation variable only accounted for household-owned assets, potentially overlooking the role of public transport. Additional limitations of the Three Delays Model include a potential oversimplification of complex health-seeking behaviour, its limited ability to address structural prevention factors and its original focus on emergency obstetric contexts. Future research that addresses these limitations is needed to validate our study findings.

Interpretation of findings

Study findings can be used to advocate for increased focus of social and behaviour change (SBC) solutions among various stakeholders and tailored interventions using a mix of approaches to address significant factors. Study findings may also provide guidance on the development and implementation of future malaria SBC interventions in Malawi. Data suggest that psychosocial factors were the most important correlates of prompt care-seeking for fever. Thus, appropriately tailored interventions designed to address these specific variables may be linked to improved behavioural outcomes. SBC interventions are often integral to generating demand for prompt care-seeking27 28 and tailored SBC interventions typically employ a mix of user-centred approaches to address key correlates of care-seeking29 and address knowledge of when to seek care and negative attitudes related to care-seeking.15 In Malawi, potential channels for such engagement may include socially convened community radio listening sessions, drama enactments and phone messages.30 Interpersonal community engagement approaches are important in Malawi given the varied exposure to mass media channels.31

Furthermore, our research underscores a potential association between proximity to health services and prompt care-seeking in Malawi. In this study, caregivers only minimally (4.3%) sought services from community health workers, called health surveillance assistants (HSAs). In Malawi, HSAs are prioritised for hard-to-reach areas to limit barriers of transportation and distance. However, not all of them provide malaria testing and treatment as part of the health services included in their village health clinics.32 Further examination of the effectiveness of the HSAs may inform additional provider-level interventions as well as determine how to address geographic barriers impacting care-seeking. Potential supply side considerations include training HSAs with technical and interpersonal skills as well as equipping them with adequate supplies to diagnose and treat malaria.9 33 Such complementary supply-side solutions can complement demand generation interventions and encourage lasting behaviour change in Malawi as well as support for HSA/community-level interventions.10

This study provides a theory-driven understanding of the multifaceted factors influencing care-seeking behaviour in Malawi. Our findings can be used to inform the design and implementation of relevant structural as well as behaviour change interventions to improve care-seeking behaviours in similar malaria-endemic settings which provide free malaria testing and treatment services.
