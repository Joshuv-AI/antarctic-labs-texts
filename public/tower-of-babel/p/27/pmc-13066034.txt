Canonical BAF chromatin remodeling complex specifies stem cell fate via cell-type-specific co-factor recruitment
Authors: Mingyi Zhang, Jifan Feng, Tingwei Guo, Lin Meng, Fei Pei, Lu Gao, Yuchen Yang, Thach-Vu Ho, Yang Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Individual subunits within the canonical BAF (cBAF) chromatin remodeling complex are known to regulate stem cell behavior, with some functional redundancy across subunits. Yet, how the cBAF complex directs adult stem cell fate specification and maintains stem cell niches remains unclear. Using the adult mouse incisor, we show that cBAF specifies mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) fate by recruiting distinct transcriptional co-factors to shape cell-type-specific chromatin regulation. Through single-cell multi-omics and in vivo functional analyses, we identify ARID1-containing cBAF as an essential gatekeeper that maintains the dynamic balance between MSC self-renewal and differentiation. Specifically, cBAF–DLX2 interactions preserve niche identity by remodeling intronic chromatin accessibility of niche-defining marker Runx2, while cBAF–FOXO1 directly modulates promoter accessibility of lineage-regulating transcription factors, including STAT3 and TRP53, among others, to balance progenitor proliferation and differentiation. Functional perturbation of RUNX2 and TRP53 confirms their roles downstream of cBAF in niche maintenance and fate specification. Our findings establish cBAF as a central regulator of adult stem cell niches and lineage commitment, and highlight cofactor-dependent mechanisms that may have broader implications for tissue regeneration and BAF-associated disorders.

Introduction

Epigenetic regulation orchestrates gene expression programs essential for cellular identity, lineage specification, and tissue maintenance without altering the DNA sequence. The mammalian SWI/SNF complex, a critical epigenetic regulator also known as the BAF (BRG1/BRM-associated factor) complex, dynamically modulates nucleosome positioning and chromatin accessibility in an ATP-dependent manner, enabling transcription factors and RNA polymerase II to access DNA sequences and regulate gene expression1–5. Dysregulation of the canonical BAF (cBAF) complex is prevalent across diverse pathological contexts in humans. Notably, mutations in cBAF subunits are involved in ~20% of human cancers and are linked to developmental disorders such as Coffin-Siris syndrome and autism spectrum disorders, highlighting their pivotal roles in cancer suppression, embryonic development, and tissue specification4,6–14. Recent findings further demonstrate that cBAF acts as a negative regulator of T cell fate by governing enhancer landscapes in activated CD8 + T cells, thereby influencing transcription factor recruitment and suggesting a potential therapeutic target in immunotherapy15,16. Although individual roles of cBAF subunits have been studied across various biological contexts17–24, significant gaps remain in our understanding of the precise mechanisms by which cBAF modulates chromatin accessibility profiles and transcriptional programs in a cell-type-specific manner, particularly in adult tissues. Clarifying these mechanisms is vital not only to comprehend fundamental stem cell biology but also to identify novel therapeutic targets for diseases arising from aberrant chromatin remodeling or stem cell dysregulation.

The BAF chromatin remodeling complex exerts diverse, context-dependent functions across tissues, shaped by its interactions with lineage-specific transcription factors (TFs) and chromatin modifiers. In embryonic stem cells, the cBAF subunit BRG1 is recruited by core pluripotency factors (OCT4, SOX2, NANOG) and plays an important role in this transcriptional network essential for maintaining stemness25,26. In cancer, BAF acts as a tumor suppressor by partnering with key regulators, such as MYC and p53, to control gene expression and cell behavior27,28. In bone marrow, BAF is engaged by CUX1 to regulate hematopoietic lineage commitment10. These interactions confer transcriptional specificity, enabling BAF to direct tissue-specific gene regulation. Beyond these contexts, the TFs that interact with the BAF complex to control stem cell fate commitment warrant further investigation. FOXO transcription factors have been identified as BAF partners that facilitate chromatin remodeling during cellular stress responses and differentiation29. DLX family homeobox proteins are implicated in craniofacial and skeletal development, and their mutations are linked to neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism30. Whether FOXO and DLX proteins can serve as context-specific co-factors of cBAF in regulating stem cell fate and the molecular mechanisms underlying their cooperation remain largely uncharacterized.

Adult tissue homeostasis relies on the precise coordination of stem cell behavior within specialized microenvironments known as stem cell niches, where important interactions occur between niche cells, nerves, circulatory vessels, and other cellular components31–35. These niche interactions regulate critical processes, such as stem cell quiescence, proliferation, differentiation, and lineage commitment, to maintain continuous tissue renewal throughout an organism’s lifespan36–39. Regenerative tissues, such as the hair follicle, intestine, and mouse incisor, exhibit conserved and spatially organized hierarchies of stem cells, transit-amplifying cells (TACs), niche cells, and differentiated cells. These tissues thus serve as excellent models for investigating mechanisms governing adult stem cell regulation and fate determination36,40–42. The mouse incisor model provides a uniquely accessible system with specific cellular markers, precise spatial organization, and well-defined differentiation trajectories, making it ideal for studying the regulation of stem cell niches and lineage commitment (Fig. 1a). Previous studies established that ARID1A and ARID1B, mutually exclusive subunits of the cBAF complex, display distinct expression patterns within the mouse incisor. ARID1A promotes cell cycle exit in TACs, whereas ARID1B maintains stem cell quiescence17,19. These initial insights inspired us to ask questions about the crucial roles of cBAF within the stem cell tissue, such as how cBAF differentially regulates chromatin accessibility in stem cells, TACs, and niche cells; whether it recruits distinct transcriptional co-factors in distinct cell types; and how these interactions coordinate lineage-specific transcriptional programs. Thus, our study systematically investigates the cell-type-specific regulatory mechanisms by which cBAF recruits co-factors to orchestrate adult mesenchymal stem cell fate commitment. By integrating single-cell multi-omic profiling and rigorous in vivo functional assays in the adult mouse incisor model, we reveal that cBAF remodels chromatin accessibility differently across various cell types. Our integrative analysis has uncovered co-factor-guided regulatory mechanisms of cBAF with cell-type-specific functions. DLX2 and FOXO1, as co-factors of cBAF, help remodel chromatin accessibility profiles to regulate precise transcriptional programs, thereby maintaining niche cell identity and balancing TAC proliferation and differentiation. This co-factor-guided regulatory framework is crucial to the roles of the cBAF complex in specifying stem cell fate, preserving stem cell niches, and maintaining tissue homeostasis and regeneration. Taken together, our findings improve understanding of how epigenetic regulators differentially determine stem cell fate, and the identified downstream targets provide a potential foundation for future therapies targeting chromatin remodeling complexes in areas such as tissue regeneration, cancer, and developmental disorders.

Results

Loss of cBAF significantly impairs adult tissue growth, homeostasis, and regeneration

Dual loss of ARID1A and ARID1B causes dissociation of the cBAF complex43, making the Arid1a and Arid1b double-knockout mouse model valuable for studying the fundamental role of cBAF. We first confirmed the expression patterns of ARID1A and ARID1B, which are widely expressed in the proximal region of the incisor tissue, with ARID1A specifically expressed in the TAC region and ARID1B expressed in the MSC region (Figure S1a–d). To investigate how cBAF complex plays its functional roles in the stem cell microenvironment and regulates lineage commitment in adult tissue homeostasis and regeneration, we deleted both Arid1a and Arid1b specifically in the GLI1+ MSC lineage in the continuously growing mouse incisor, by generating tamoxifen-inducible Gli1-CreER;Arid1afl/fl;Arid1bfl/fl (DKO) mice. To study adult stem cell homeostasis, tamoxifen was administered at postnatal 4-5 weeks in mice, when mouse incisors are fully mature (Fig. 1b). We began this study by confirming that Arid1a and Arid1b were efficiently deleted in the dental mesenchyme and epithelium of the mouse incisor 5 days post-tamoxifen induction (dpt) (Figure S1e–h).

To investigate the role of the cBAF complex in mouse incisor growth and post-injury regeneration, we first performed a notch movement assay to evaluate the growth capacity of DKO mouse incisors. We found the growth rate of the incisor was markedly reduced in DKO mice compared to controls when measured 10 days after notch creation (Fig. 1c,d). We next conducted an incisor clipping assay to assess the tissue injury repair ability following the loss of the cBAF complex. The control group fully restored the clipped incisor length within two days, whereas DKO mice achieved less than 50% restoration at the same time frame (Fig. 1e, f). These findings indicate the essential roles of the cBAF complex in supporting tissue growth and regenerative capacity of adult mouse incisors.

Next, we examined changes in tissue morphology one week after loss of cBAF. We observed that the well-aligned odontoblasts and ameloblasts in the control were severely disrupted and disorganized in the DKO mouse incisors, and nuclei in the TAC region displayed a round morphology in the control, whereas they exhibited an elongated, oval-shaped morphology in the DKO mouse incisors (Fig. 1g). Moreover, significantly less dentin and enamel, formed by odontoblasts and ameloblasts, respectively, were deposited in the DKO incisors compared to the controls. Together, these severe phenotypic abnormalities support the essential role of the cBAF complex in maintaining adult tissue homeostasis.

Given that GLI1+ cells contribute to both mesenchymal and epithelial lineages in the mouse incisor, we further explored whether the observed dental mesenchymal defects in DKO mice could be attributed to changes in the dental epithelial cells. To specifically delete Arid1a and Arid1b in the epithelial lineage, we generated Sox2-CreER;Arid1afl/fl;Arid1bfl/fl mice. While we noted slight misalignment in the ameloblast layer of Sox2-CreER;Arid1afl/fl;Arid1bfl/fl mice, no morphological defects were observed in mesenchymal tissue in these mice when compared to controls one week post-induction (Figure S1i–j,l–m). Also, odontoblast marker Dspp expression showed no obvious difference between the Sox2-CreER;Arid1afl/fl;Arid1bfl/fl mice and controls (Figure S1k, n). These findings suggest that the loss of the cBAF complex in dental epithelial cells does not affect the mouse incisor mesenchyme, supporting the essential role of the cBAF complex specifically in dental mesenchymal cells for maintaining incisor growth, injury repair, and adult tissue homeostasis.

cBAF preserves the MSC-TAC-niche cell domains and progenitor cell fate commitment

The MSCs give rise to TACs in the proximal region of the mouse incisor, supporting tissue growth and regeneration. In this region, a specialized niche cell population, marked by co-expression of RUNX2 and GLI1 (RUNX2 + GLI1 + ), supports the transition from MSCs to TACs41. To reveal the cellular changes associated with the observed phenotypes after the loss of cBAF complex in the GLI1+ lineage, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on cells collected from the proximal ends of DKO mouse incisors at 4 days post-induction, when the MSC, TAC, and niche cell domains have been disturbed (Figure S2). We integrated the DKO (8182 cells) scRNA-seq data with our previously published control (9625 cells) scRNA-seq dataset and analyzed the cell clusters using Seurat software19,44,45. The cell clusters were annotated using published markers, and a large cell cluster was defined as dental mesenchymal cells (Figure S3a, b)19,46. We further subclustered the dental mesenchymal cells into five cell clusters: PDF (proximal dental follicle cells), MSC (mesenchymal stem cells), DP (dental pulp cells), TAC (transit-amplifying cells), and Pre-od (pre-odontoblasts) (Fig. 2a, and Figure S3c, d), corresponding to their distinct in vivo locations (Fig. 2b). Because RUNX2 + GLI1+ niche cells are a small subset of GLI1+ cells, it is difficult to precisely isolate the niche cell population in scRNA-seq data. Therefore, we did not include niche cells in the clustering; instead, we compared them using in vivo analysis. We compared the proportions of each cell type between control and DKO samples. TACs were found to be twice as numerous in the DKO samples compared to controls, whereas MSCs and the differentiated pre-odontoblasts and dental pulp cells showed slight reductions (Fig. 2c). To determine whether stem cell lineage differentiation is affected by the loss of the cBAF complex, we performed pseudotime analysis on mesenchymal cell clusters from scRNA-seq data using the Monocle2 algorithm in an unbiased manner47,48. This analysis revealed the single-cell trajectories over time as MSCs transition into TACs and subsequently differentiate into pre-odontoblasts and dental pulp cells (Fig. 2d, e). Throughout this differentiation trajectory process, the expression of the MSC marker Gli1 was initially high and gradually decreased, while markers for pre-odontoblasts (Notum) and dental pulp cells (Fmod) increased in later stages. The proliferation marker Mki67 peaked during the middle stages, preceding the rise in pre-odontoblast marker levels (Figure S3e). By comparing differentiation trajectories between control and DKO samples along the pseudotime, we observed an early accumulation of TACs in the DKO sample at a stage typically occupied by MSCs in the control samples. Additionally, there was a loss of differentiated pre-odontoblasts after the branching point, along with a reduction in differentiated dental pulp cells in the DKO samples compared to controls (Fig. 2e). We compared gene expression dynamics along pseudotime trajectories between control and DKO samples to elucidate the cause of the changes in lineage commitment. The pseudotime heatmap compared the expression enrichment of 772 genes linked to differentiation trajectories between the control and DKO (Fig. 2f). Notably, genes within the green module, which are usually activated at early pseudotime points in control samples, showed later activation in DKO samples, shifting their expression toward more differentiated cell populations following cBAF loss. Functional annotation revealed that these temporally dysregulated genes are predominantly involved in critical biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis (Figure S3f). We therefore sought to verify whether these cellular processes were detected in the DKO mice. We observed a progressive reduction in GLI1+ MSCs and, unexpectedly, a decrease in TACs in the DKO mice at 5 dpt compared to controls (Figure S4a, b). Because our transcriptomic analysis revealed altered expression of apoptosis-related genes, we performed a time-course TUNEL assay to determine whether hyperproliferation at 3 dpt ultimately led to cell death. Apoptosis was first detected at 4 dpt in the TAC region and became pronounced by 5 dpt (Figure S4c,d), suggesting that hyperproliferation may trigger apoptosis and consequently reduce the number of proliferating cells from 3 dpt to 5 dpt. Three distinct spatial cellular domains, including MSCs, TACs, and niche cells, are well-defined under normal conditions. To simultaneously visualize these spatial domains in vivo, we performed co-staining for β-Galactosidase (β-GAL), RUNX2, and EdU (a 2-h EdU pulse to label proliferating cells in S-phase) in Gli1-LacZ and DKO;Gli1-LacZ mouse incisors. We observed that the well-defined cellular domains are disrupted following the loss of the cBAF complex, with a reduced GLI1 + MSC population, widespread proliferating cells, and increased RUNX2+ cells (Fig. 2g). This data indicates that the cBAF complex is indispensable for maintaining the proper spatial organization of cell domains in the proximal end of the mouse incisor.

To further investigate how the cBAF complex regulates MSC fate commitment, we performed lineage tracing of GLI1+ MSCs using tdTomato reporter mice. We generated DKO;tdTomato mice and compared their MSC differentiation capabilities to those of Gli1-CreER;tdTomato control mice. Analysis of tdTomato fluorescence revealed significantly fewer labeled dental pulp cells in DKO;tdTomato incisors compared to controls (Fig. 2h), indicating compromised lineage differentiation of MSCs following loss of cBAF. Additionally, we specifically monitored the capacity of TACs to differentiate into odontoblasts by injecting EdU to label proliferating TACs during S-phase and analyzed them 48 h later. We performed co-staining for the odontoblast differentiation marker Dspp and EdU, and overlapped cells indicated that the labeled TACs differentiated into odontoblasts. In control incisors, we clearly observed Dspp + EdU+ odontoblasts differentiated from labeled TACs. However, in DKO incisors, this differentiation was disturbed, with minimal overlap between EdU and Dspp staining (Fig. 2i), indicating that the ability of TACs to differentiate into odontoblasts was completely impaired after the loss of cBAF. These findings suggest that the loss of the cBAF complex causes MSCs to fail in properly committing to differentiated lineages. These data collectively demonstrate that the cBAF complex is essential for organizing the stem cell microenvironment, including MSC, TAC, and niche cells, and for ensuring appropriate stem cell lineage progression, thereby supporting adult tissue homeostasis and regeneration.

cBAF remodels chromatin accessibility in a cell-type-dependent manner

The cBAF complex is an essential remodeler of chromatin structure, enabling RNA Polymerase II (RNAPII) and transcription factors (TFs) to access promoter and enhancer regions, thereby regulating downstream gene expression. However, how the cBAF complex precisely regulates chromatin accessibility across various cell types and whether it exerts cell-type-specific effects on maintaining homeostasis and facilitating injury repair in adult tissues remain unexplored in vivo.

To investigate how cBAF regulates chromatin accessibility in adult stem cells and their progeny at the single-cell level, we performed single-cell ATAC-sequencing (scATAC-seq) on the proximal regions of DKO mouse incisors 4 days post-tamoxifen induction. The scATAC-seq data from the DKO samples were compared with data from control mouse incisors in our previously published datasets19. We integrated and analyzed 5522 control and 5663 DKO cells using Signac49. Strikingly, the integrated UMAP plot showed a distinct separation of cell clusters between control and DKO samples, highlighting a significant reshaping of the chromatin accessibility upon loss of the cBAF complex (Figure S5a). Pseudobulk analysis revealed 56,119 peaks open in control samples but closed or reduced in the DKO (closed peaks), alongside 17,194 peaks opened or enhanced in the DKO (opened peaks) (Fig. 3a). Using ChIPseeker, we annotated these peaks and compared the genomic distributions of unchanged, closed, and opened peaks50,51. Notably, we found that changed peaks (including both opened and closed peaks) were more frequently located in intronic and distal intergenic regions (enhancers) than the unchanged peaks, suggesting that enhancers were preferentially affected by cBAF disruption (Figure S5b). Heatmap analysis further confirmed that both closed and opened chromatin regions were enriched at enhancers rather than promoters (Fig. 3b). To characterize cell-type-specific chromatin accessibility patterns, we annotated cell clusters in control and DKO mesenchymal populations based on putative gene activities and established cell-type markers (Fig. 3c, and Figure S5c). Comparative analysis revealed that the loss of cBAF led to a large number of peaks closed in the TAC region, including TACs and pre-odontoblasts. In contrast, MSC, PDF, and DP populations displayed comparable numbers of closed and opened peaks following cBAF loss (Fig. 3d). Integration of chromatin accessibility data with transcriptional profiles showed that genes associated with closed peaks were generally downregulated in DKO samples, while those linked to opened peaks were upregulated (Figure S5d, e). Further analysis of genomic distribution revealed that DKO samples exhibited stronger peak signals in promoter regions, while in the TAC populations, promoter regions displayed a mixed pattern with both enhanced and reduced peak signals (Figure S5f). Notably, enhancer regions across all populations showed a global reduction in accessibility upon loss of cBAF (Figure S5f). As chromatin accessibility at promoter regions reflects gene expression levels, we specifically compared gene expression associated with closed and opened promoter peaks. Promoter regions with reduced or closed peaks exhibited decreased gene expression, and vice versa (Figure S5g,h). These results suggest that the cBAF complex exerts differential regulatory effects on promoter and enhancer elements to fine-tune gene expression across distinct mesenchymal cell types.

To assess how the cBAF complex modulates chromatin accessibility in a cell-type-specific manner, we annotated closed and opened peaks across distinct mesenchymal populations. Using UpSet plots, we compared genes linked to differentially accessible regions among these cell types. For genes associated with closed peaks, 1077 genes were commonly affected across all five populations, with the TAC and pre-odontoblast clusters exhibiting the highest numbers of uniquely changed genes, highlighting a pronounced role for cBAF in regulating gene expression within the TAC region (Figure S6a). In contrast, 814 genes were commonly associated with opened peaks, with the differentiated dental pulp cell population showing the most distinct gene expression. MSCs and adjacent PDF cells also showed substantial transcriptional changes associated with increased chromatin accessibility (Figure S6b). These findings indicate that cBAF exerts distinct regulatory influences on chromatin accessibility and transcriptional output in a cell-type-dependent manner within the adult incisor mesenchyme.

We next investigated how cBAF differentially regulates distinct domains, focusing on the altered populations in the proximal region, including MSCs, TACs, and niche cells. To specifically investigate how the cBAF differentially regulates MSCs and TACs, we compared closed and opened chromatin peaks between MSCs and TACs. We identified that 1778 changed genes were associated with closed peaks and 1125 genes were associated with opened peaks across these two cell types. Notably, closed peaks were predominantly enriched in TACs, while opened peaks were more prevalent in MSCs (Figure S6c,e). To determine the functional implications of these accessibility changes, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on the annotated genes. Among the genes in MSCs associated with closed peaks, which reflect genes controlled by cBAF, we found enrichment for pathways involved in Wnt signaling, connective tissue development, mesenchyme development, and sensory nerve system development. Meanwhile, genes associated with closed peaks in TACs were enriched for ossification, neurogenesis, cell junction assembly, and adhesion, indicating potential regulation of these processes by the cBAF complex (Figure S6d). These GO analyses indicated that the cBAF complex exhibits distinct functional roles within MSC and TAC populations. The opened peak analysis further highlighted altered pathways involved in chromatin remodeling changes in both MSCs and TACs. The changes were linked to disrupted cell cycle transitions and Wnt signaling in MSCs, while showing enrichment for double-strand break repair, apoptosis, and mitotic phase transition in TACs, consistent with their increased apoptosis observed in vivo (Figure S6f). To confirm that the identified signaling pathways are indeed influenced by cBAF, we selected Wnt signaling and DNA damage for in vivo validation. In situ hybridization for Axin2, a readout of the Wnt pathway, revealed ectopic activation in MSCs and DP cells, along with upregulation in TACs, in the DKO samples compared to controls (Figure S6g), suggesting the role of cBAF complex in restricting Wnt signaling activation in TACs. Immunostaining for γH2AX, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks, showed significantly increased DNA damage in TACs of DKO samples compared to controls (Figure S6h), indicating that loss of cBAF triggers DNA double-strand breaks. These data confirm that cBAF plays distinct and critical roles in maintaining the chromatin accessibility of MSCs and TACs, as well as their associated cellular functions.

To investigate how the cBAF complex modulates chromatin accessibility and specifically regulates niche cells, we isolated RUNX2+/GLI1+ cells from the MSC population and performed comparative chromatin accessibility analysis. Heatmap profiling revealed approximately twice as many regions with reduced accessibility (closed peaks) as with increased accessibility (opened peaks) following cBAF loss (Fig. 3e). GO analysis of these differentially accessible regions showed substantial overlap with the signaling pathways affected in the broader MSC population (Fig. 3f). Notably, morphogenesis and cell projection organization were enriched among the top signaling pathways identified in closed peaks in niche cells, suggesting unique chromatin remodeling events in these cells. Furthermore, pathways associated with extracellular stimulus response and cilium organization were selectively impaired in the niche cell population, underscoring the critical role of cBAF in maintaining the functional integrity of the stem cell niche.

Together, these findings indicate that cBAF exerts context-dependent regulatory control over chromatin accessibility across distinct cell populations. This cell-type-specific modulation may be driven by differential recruitment of co-factors, enabling cBAF to finely tune transcriptional programs within distinct cell types and to preserve tissue homeostasis in adult regenerative tissues.

Transcriptional co-factors, FOXO1 and DLX2, determine the regulatory specificity of cBAF

To verify whether these TFs physically interact with ARID1A-containing and/or ARID1B-containing cBAF, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) to identify protein-protein interactions. To further confirm the functional binding activities of these TFs, we utilized TF footprinting analysis with scATAC-seq data. TF footprinting appears as induced transposase (Tn5) insertion rates at sites of direct protein-DNA interactions, providing precise single-cell-level evidence of TF occupancy52. Footprinting analysis revealed clear dips indicative of strong TF binding in both control and DKO samples. However, the control cell types (represented by yellow-green lines) displayed higher Tn5 insertion than the DKO cell types (represented by pink-blue lines), suggesting impaired binding activity of DLX2 and FOXO1 following cBAF loss (Fig. 4d). Since ARID1A and ARID1B are DNA-binding proteins that lack a specific binding motif, we analyzed the CUT&RUN-seq results to identify their tissue-specific binding sites within the proximal end of the adult mouse incisor (Figure S7d). Genomic distribution analysis showed that ARID1A and FOXO1 exhibit more promoter regulatory activity than ARID1B and DLX2, and our analysis revealed a subset of target genes shared by these factors (Figure S7e–g). To verify whether these co-factors could interact with ARID1A and ARID1B in specific cell types, we focused on the intersections between TAC-specific closed peaks and ARID1A binding sites, as well as MSC-specific closed peaks and ARID1B binding sites. We confirmed that FOXO1 and DLX2 co-occupy ARID1A binding peaks, indicating their collaborative regulatory role in TACs. Similarly, DLX2 co-localizes with ARID1B binding peaks in MSCs, supporting its function as a co-factor for ARID1B-containing cBAF in the MSC population (Fig. 4e, f). These findings suggest that cBAF recruits distinct co-factors to regulate chromatin accessibility profiles differently across various cell types.

cBAF complex with co-factor DLX2 safeguards stem cell niche composition

To further delineate how cBAF recruits distinct co-factors to define the niche cell-MSC-TAC domains, we revisited our previous findings that established individual roles for ARID1A and ARID1B. Specifically, ARID1A regulates TAC cell cycle exit, while ARID1B maintains MSC quiescence, as reported in prior work17,19.

Extending this work, we aimed to determine whether the cBAF complex specifically regulates the niche cell population. Given that we observed an increase in RUNX2+ cells in the DKO incisors compared with the controls, we first examined whether cBAF directly regulates Runx2 expression. Using CUT&RUN-seq data at the Runx2 gene locus, we observed that both ARID1A and ARID1B, with co-factor DLX2, show strong binding activity in the intronic region of Runx2 (Fig. 5a). We also performed FOXO1 and DLX2 CUT&RUN sequencing using DKO mouse incisor tissue and found that DLX2 binding activity was lost upon cBAF loss, confirming DLX2 as a cBAF-dependent cofactor in regulating Runx2 expression (Fig. 5a). To further validate the regulatory role of cBAF complex, we examined chromatin accessibility at the Runx2 locus using scATAC-seq data from control and DKO samples. Our data showed open chromatin peaks at the ARID1A/ARID1B/DLX2 binding site of Runx2 in control samples, while these peaks were significantly reduced in DKO cell clusters, indicating that the cBAF complex regulates the chromatin accessibility in the intronic region of Runx2 to suppress its expression (Fig. 5a, b). Additionally, the chromatin accessibility of the Runx2 promoter region increased in DKO samples, confirming elevated Runx2 transcriptional activity following the loss of the cBAF complex (Fig. 5a). We then confirmed Runx2 upregulation in vivo by immunostaining. The result showed obvious ectopic activation of Runx2 in mesenchymal cells, especially in MSCs, in the DKO compared to the control (Fig. 5c). To validate that the Runx2 intron is a functional regulatory element, we conducted a luciferase reporter assay. We detected significantly increased Gluc activity in the DKO incisor primary cell culture compared to controls (Figure S8a), indicating that cBAF suppresses Runx2 transcriptional activity by binding to the intronic region. To assess DLX2’s role in regulating Runx2 expression, co-staining of RUNX2 and Dlx2 revealed mutually exclusive expression patterns (Fig. 5d). Meanwhile, we knocked down Dlx2 with siRNA in control primary cells and observed an increase in Runx2 expression (Fig. 5e). To test whether ARID1A and ARID1B are functionally redundant in regulating Runx2 expression, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of DKO samples and conducted GSA analysis comparing them to our published control samples19. We also checked Runx2 expression from the published bulk RNA sequencing results for control and Gli1-CreER;Arid1afl/fl (Arid1a KO) mice, as well as for control and Gli1-CreER;Arid1bfl/fl (Arid1b KO) mice17,19. Runx2 expression did not show significant changes between control and Arid1a KO or Arid1b KO, but it showed significant upregulation in the DKO (Figure S8b). We also performed immunostaining for RUNX2 in control, Arid1a KO, and Arid1b KO incisors, confirming that there was no obvious difference in RUNX2 expression, which indicates that ARID1A and ARID1B functionally compensate to regulate Runx2 expression and preserve the stem cell niche composition (Figure S8c). We also reanalyzed previously published scATAC-seq data from control and Arid1b KO samples19, showing no significant differences in chromatin accessibility peaks at the Runx2 locus, especially at the ARID1A/ARID1B/DLX2 binding site and the promoter region between the control mesenchymal cell (Control-MC) and Arid1b KO-MC samples (Figure S8d, e). This result suggests that loss of ARID1B alone does not affect Runx2 expression.

Given that both Runx2 and Arid1b are located on chromosome 17, it was not feasible to generate DKO mice with a Runx2 heterozygous deletion for functional validation. To circumvent this issue and assess the functional significance of RUNX2 as a downstream effector of the cBAF complex, we performed local knockdown experiments. Specifically, lentiviral particles encoding Runx2-targeting shRNA or control shRNA were injected into the proximal region of mouse incisors at 4-5 weeks of age, followed by tamoxifen induction, and samples were collected at 5 dpt (Fig. 5f). Immunostaining confirmed efficient Runx2 knockdown in Runx2 shRNA-treated DKO;Gli1-LacZ incisors compared to those treated with control shRNA (Fig. 5g). Additional analyses using β-GAL and Ki67 staining demonstrated significant restoration of disrupted MSC and TAC populations upon Runx2 knockdown (Fig. 5g–i). These results indicate that RUNX2 is a critical downstream effector of the cBAF complex in maintaining niche cell function and regulating MSC and TAC populations.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the cBAF complex recruits DLX2 to regulate niche cell marker Runx2 expression. Within this regulatory complex, ARID1A and ARID1B exhibit functional redundancy, ensuring the appropriate expression levels of Runx2 to preserve stem cell niche integrity and sustain niche cell function.

cBAF-FOXO1 directly controls lineage-specific transcription factors for cell fate decisions

Since we observed severe phenotypes and cellular changes caused by disruption of the cBAF complex, we hypothesize that these changes arise not only from dysregulation of the stem cell niche but also from the disruption of other cellular processes. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether the loss of the cBAF complex directly causes the observed lineage commitment defects.

Since our scRNA-seq analysis revealed significant changes in genes involved in proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis along the cell differentiation trajectory (Fig. 2f, Figure S3f), we sought to clarify how the cBAF complex controls TAC fate. To test whether the cBAF complex regulates key TFs in vivo, we assessed chromatin accessibility at their gene loci using scATAC-seq data. Our analysis revealed a significant increase in chromatin accessibility in the promoter regions of these five crucial TFs in the DKO-MC sample, reflecting enhanced transcriptional activation following cBAF loss (Fig. 6d, Figure S9b). To further examine the direct regulatory interactions between cBAF complexes and these TF loci, as well as the co-factors FOXO1 and DLX2, we analyzed CUT&RUN-seq data between control and DKO samples. The data revealed predominant ARID1A and FOXO1 occupancy at the promoters of these TFs than ARID1B and DLX2 in the control sample, whereas FOXO1 and DLX2 binding activity showed varying levels of reduction in the DKO sample (Fig. 6e, Figure S9c). To further validate cBAF-mediated transcriptional regulation of these TFs, we selected Stat3 and Trp53 as representative targets and cloned their promoter-binding regions upstream of Gluc for a luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 6f). The DKO cells exhibited modest but significantly higher promoter-driven normalized Gluc activity than controls (Fig. 6g), indicating that cBAF binds to promoter regions to repress TF expression. These transcriptional binding activities highlight the essential and predominant role of ARID1A-containing cBAF in interacting with FOXO1 to control the expression of key TFs.

To validate the functional roles of these TFs, we comprehensively investigated the cBAF complex in adult mesenchymal tissue by analyzing bulk RNA-seq data and identified over 3000 genes that were significantly differentially expressed in the DKO compared to the control (Figure S10a). Using GO analysis, we further highlighted the top 10 altered signaling pathways (Figure S10b). Notably, several key pathways, including PDGF signaling, angiogenesis, the p53 pathway, and apoptosis signaling, are associated with TRP53. Given this connection, we chose TRP53 as a representative TF for rigorous testing of its functional role downstream of cBAF in regulating TAC fate by balancing proliferation, differentiation, or apoptosis. We generated a genetic rescue model by deleting one Trp53 allele on the DKO background (DKO;Trp53+/-). Following tamoxifen induction at 4-5 weeks of age, samples were collected at 5 dpt. At the cellular level, the disturbed TAC proliferation and impaired differentiation observed in the DKO incisors were partially rescued in the DKO;Trp53+/- model (Fig. 6h). Additionally, the increased apoptosis in the DKO sample was partially ameliorated by the reduction of Trp53 (Figure S10c). These data collectively indicate that TRP53 acts as a key downstream TF of the cBAF complex, with critical roles in mediating TAC behavior and cell fate commitment.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the cBAF complex orchestrates the mesenchymal stem cell microenvironment and regulates lineage commitment through multiple, functionally distinct mechanisms. Specifically, cBAF maintains stem cell niche populations by modulating DLX2-mediated repression of Runx2, thereby preserving the stem cell microenvironment. Concurrently, cBAF regulates global chromatin accessibility and transcription factor occupancy, thereby guiding key biological processes, such as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, in TACs. These results establish the cBAF complex as a central regulatory hub with numerous important cell-type-specific functions, achieved through distinct co-factors, including safeguarding stem cell niche composition, regulating progenitor fate commitment, and maintaining adult mesenchymal tissue homeostasis.

Discussion

Epigenetic regulation is essential for orchestrating adult stem cell behavior, maintaining the niche microenvironment, and coordinating progenitor cell fate decisions, all of which are crucial for sustaining tissue homeostasis and regeneration. While previous studies have highlighted the significance of epigenetic mechanisms in regulating adult regenerative tissues, such as the intestine, hair follicle, and mouse incisor, with conserved spatially organized hierarchies of stem cells, progenitors, and niche cells40–42,60–62, how chromatin remodeling specifically regulates niche cell populations to influence stem cell behavior remains incompletely understood. Previous studies have shown that Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) controls stem cell fate and niche-derived signaling in the gastrointestinal mesenchyme to support intestinal stem cell function63. Disruption of this epigenetic network leads to deregulated chromatin states in intestinal stem cells, which can drive pathologies such as colorectal cancer64. This underscores the importance of understanding how chromatin-based regulation contributes to stem cell niches. In the adult mouse incisor, studies have explored the role of RUNX2+ niche cells, which directly regulate IGFBP3 secretion to coordinate the MSC-to-TAC transition41. In this study, we provide in vivo evidence that cBAF directly regulates niche cells by suppressing Runx2 expression. Our scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq results reflect significant upregulation of Igfbp3 expression after cBAF loss, suggesting that more stem cells turn into niche cells. Nonetheless, we remain cautious about classifying the expanded RUNX2 + GLI1+ cells in the DKO as niche cells, since we lack additional niche cell markers to confirm this conclusion. Further research to more precisely identify niche cell types and clarify their roles will enhance our comprehensive understanding of the stem cell microenvironment in the mouse incisor.

As a key tumor suppressor, the cBAF complex is frequently mutated across a wide spectrum of human cancers, with alterations in its subunits found in ~20% of cases8,65,66. It is therefore imperative to identify emerging opportunities for targeted therapies related to cBAF subunit mutations linked to cancer. Therapeutic strategies targeting downstream pathways activated by cBAF disruption are essential to this endeavor. In this study, we identified several TFs that were significantly upregulated, including STAT3, TP53, YY1, RELA, and SP1, which serve as key downstream effectors of cBAF-dependent chromatin remodeling. These factors are known to regulate essential cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Importantly, their aberrant activation and overexpression have been widely implicated in cancer as drivers of tumor growth, immune evasion, and metastasis67–78. For example, the interplay between SP1 and STAT3 has been shown to promote tumor progression in pancreatic cancer71. Profiling the expression and activation status of these TFs in cBAF-mutant tumors may help refine target selection and identify vulnerabilities for therapeutic intervention, a promising strategy for countering the oncogenic effects of chromatin remodeling dysfunction. Such approaches could potentially provide insights for biomarker-guided therapies that specifically exploit cBAF-dependent transcriptional networks in cancer.

Mutations in ARID1 and other cBAF subunits have also been widely reported in other disease contexts, including developmental disorders such as Coffin-Siris syndrome and autism spectrum disorders79–83. These disorders are often characterized by dysregulated stem cell function, abnormal differentiation, and compromised tissue development, underscoring the broader significance of cBAF-mediated chromatin regulation in human biology. We identified DLX2 as a niche-specific transcriptional co-factor of the cBAF complex. DLX2 is a homeobox factor implicated in craniofacial, dental, and forebrain development84,85. Further dissecting the cBAF-DLX2 interaction network may reveal conserved and context-specific regulatory strategies that are essential for organ development and adult tissue homeostasis. Given DLX2’s integration into developmental gene regulatory networks, it may contribute to broader chromatin remodeling dysfunction and phenotypic variability in related disorders. Understanding the interplay between chromatin remodelers and lineage-specific TFs is key to uncovering the mechanisms of tissue development and regeneration. These insights extend beyond dental mesenchyme and offer a conceptual framework for how epigenetic regulators govern stem cell and niche cell interactions across regenerative systems, with broad implications for understanding and treating chromatinopathies, neurodevelopmental disorders, and cancer.

The cBAF, polybromo-associated BAF (PBAF), and non-canonical BAF (ncBAF) complexes play critical roles in dynamically regulating the accessibility of cis-regulatory elements, including enhancers, promoters, and silencers, across diverse biological systems86–88. Each of these complexes has a distinct genomic localization pattern and regulatory function. Prior studies have shown that cBAF is preferentially enriched at enhancer regions, whereas PBAF and ncBAF tend to localize at promoters and transcription start sites3,8,89,90, suggesting that the chromatin remodeling landscape is modular and context-dependent, with specific complexes fine-tuning transcriptional output to meet cellular needs. Our in vivo scATAC-seq analysis of adult mesenchymal tissues enhances this understanding by demonstrating that cBAF primarily regulates enhancer accessibility and does so in a cell-type-specific manner. These enhancer-driven remodeling events are essential for maintaining lineage fidelity and directing stem cell fate decisions. Enhancer-/silencer-mediated chromatin regulation has emerged as a conserved mechanism for achieving spatially and temporally precise gene expression91,92, and our findings underscore the importance of cBAF in implementing such control to govern adult stem cell fate decisions. Despite these advances, several important questions remain to be explored. For instance, does cBAF loss reshape higher-order genome architecture, such as A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), or enhancer–promoter loops? How does cBAF coordinate with other chromatin remodelers, histone modifiers, and architectural proteins (e.g., CTCF or cohesin) to control long-range regulatory interactions? Addressing these questions will be crucial for establishing a unifying framework that clarifies how chromatin remodeling orchestrates the regulation of adult stem cells and tissue homeostasis.

In conclusion, our study reveals how the cBAF chromatin remodeling complex directs adult stem cell fate decisions and niche composition through context-specific recruitment of transcriptional co-factors. We demonstrate that cBAF achieves these diverse functions by integrating partial functional redundancy among its subunits, ARID1A and ARID1B, alongside cell-type specificity conferred by cofactors such as DLX2 and FOXO1. By elucidating how transcriptional programs are shaped at the chromatin level within stem cell niches, our work establishes a co-factor-guided chromatin regulatory framework with the potential to drive future therapeutic strategies targeting chromatin remodelers and their downstream regulatory networks across diverse contexts, including cancer, developmental disorders, and tissue regeneration.

Methods

Ethics

This research was conducted in accordance with federal guidelines under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Southern California. Mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment, with comprehensive precautions taken to ensure their welfare. At specified time points, the mice were humanely euthanized using an overdose of carbon dioxide, followed by confirmation of death through cervical dislocation.

Animals

The following mouse lines were used and cross-bred in this study: Arid1afl/fl (JAX: 027717)93; Arid1bfl/fl (JAX: 032061)94; Trp53fl/fl (JAX: 008462)95; Gli1-CreER (JAX:007913)96; Sox2-CreER (JAX: 017593)97; Gli1-LacZ (JAX:008211)98; tdTomato (JAX: 007905)99. Male and female mice showed similar phenotypes and were both used in this study.

Tamoxifen administration

Tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, T5648) was dissolved in corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich, C8267) at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. It was administered to mice at one month of age via intraperitoneal injection on three consecutive days, each at a dose of 1.5 mg per 10 g body weight, to induce Cre activity. Mouse mandibles were collected at multiple time points for molecular, cellular, and phenotypic analyses.

Notch movement and clipping assays

Control and DKO mice one week after tamoxifen induction were used for these assays. After administering anesthesia, a notch was made in the enamel of the left mandibular incisor above the gumline using a carbide bur (Brasseler USA, 018554U0). Ten days later, the distance of notch movement was measured as an indicator of incisor growth.

For the clipping assay, the left mandibular incisors of anesthetized control and DKO mice were clipped along the gingival papilla one week after tamoxifen induction. Two days after clipping, the injury repair ability was evaluated by measuring the amount of incisor growth.

Histological analysis

The mouse mandibles were sampled and dissected, then fixed in 10 % formalin solution at 4 °C overnight and decalcified in 10 % EDTA/PBS solution for 2-4 weeks. Following decalcification, the mandibles underwent a series of ethanol and xylene treatments for dehydration and were subsequently embedded in paraffin. The samples were sectioned at 5 µm thickness using a microtome (Leica, RM2235 ccwUS) and subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining following the standard protocol for histological analysis.

Immunofluorescence and in situ hybridization

The mouse mandibles were collected, fixed, and decalcified as described for histological analysis. Prior to cryosectioning, the mandibles were dehydrated in sucrose/PBS solutions of increasing concentrations (15%, 30%, and sucrose/OCT) and then embedded in OCT compound. The samples were sliced into 8 µm sections with a cryostat (Leica, CM1850) and collected onto slides for immunofluorescence staining and in situ hybridization.

For immunofluorescence staining, the slides were incubated in a blocking solution (PerkinElmer, FP1012) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The slides were rinsed with PBST 3 times before incubation at room temperature for 2 h with Alexa-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200, Invitrogen). After that, the slides were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 62248) and then mounted. The primary antibodies used in this study were: ARID1A (1:100, Abcam, ab182561), ARID1B (1:100, Abcam, ab244351), Ki67 (1:100, Abcam, ab15580), β-galactosidase (β-GAL) (1:100, Abcam, ab9361), RUNX2 (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, 12556), and Phospho-Histone H2A.X (Ser139) (Cell Signaling Technology, 2577).

RNAscope in situ hybridization was performed using the RNAscope Multiplex fluorescent v2 kit (Advanced Cell Diagnosis, 323100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The probes used in this study were synthesized by Advanced Cell Diagnostics: Probe-Mm-Dspp (448301), Probe-Mm-Trp53 (402331), Probe-Mm-Dlx2 (555951), Probe-Mm-Foxo1 (485761), Probe-Mm-Axin2 (400331), and Probe-Mm-Stat3 (425641).

Imaging

The histological HE-stained images were captured with a fluorescence microscope in brightfield mode (Keyence, BZ-X810). The immunofluorescent and RNAScope in situ hybridization fluorescent signals on histological sections were captured with a fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X810).

EdU incorporation, staining, and TUNEL assays

Both control and DKO mice were administered a single intraperitoneal injection of 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) at a dosage of 25 µg/g body weight 2 h and 48 h before harvest. The mandibles were then dissected, fixed, decalcified, and sectioned as described above. EdU signal was detected using the Click-iT plus EdU cell proliferation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10637). Cell apoptosis was detected using the Click-iT plus TUNEL Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10617). Both procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All the images were captured with a fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X810).

Bulk RNA-sequencing analysis

The proximal mesenchymal tissue of the DKO mouse incisor was carefully dissected four days after tamoxifen induction. RNA was extracted following the protocol provided by the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, 74004). Afterward, the cDNA library was constructed and sequenced at the Molecular Genomics Core at the University of Southern California (USC). The raw sequencing data were preprocessed, aligned to the mouse mm10 genome, and analyzed together with published mouse incisor control RNA-seq data using Partek Flow to identify differential gene expression19. Significance was assessed at a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤ 0.1 and absolute fold change >1.5.

Mesenchymal cell isolation

Mesenchymal tissue from the proximal end of the mouse incisor was collected and mechanically fragmented into tiny pieces to facilitate cell digestion. A solution of 2 mg/mL collagenase type I (Worthington, LS004196) and 4 mg/mL dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich, 54905400) was added to the chopped tissue in α-MEM medium (Gibco, 12571063). After fragmentation, the tissue was incubated at 37 °C in a thermomixer (Eppendorf) at 600 rpm for 15 minutes to facilitate the digestion. The process was terminated by adding α-MEM medium with 10% FBS (Gibco, 16000044). The mixture was centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in α-MEM medium with 10% FBS for further processing.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single cells were isolated from the proximal mesenchymal tissue of DKO mouse incisors at four days post-tamoxifen induction, following the protocol outlined in the ‘Mesenchymal cell isolation’ section. These cells were processed using the 10X Chromium system and tagged with barcodes from the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ v3.1 kit, preparing them for single-cell RNA sequencing. The library sample was constructed following the guidelines in the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits User Guide (v3.1 Chemistry) and then sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq System at the Technology Center for Genomics and Bioinformatics, University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). Quality control, mapping, and assembly of the library count tables were executed using the CellRanger pipeline. A total of 8,182 cells from the DKO sample were sequenced. The raw read counts generated by the CellRanger count command were analyzed alongside published control scRNA-seq data using the Seurat v5 R package19,100.

Single-cell ATAC sequencing

Single cells were extracted from the proximal mesenchymal tissue of DKO mouse incisors at four days post-tamoxifen induction, following the protocol outlined in the ‘Mesenchymal cell isolation’ section. Following extraction, cells were lysed to release nuclei, and scATAC-seq libraries were then prepared using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell ATAC Library & Gel Bead Kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000176). The suspension of nuclei was loaded onto the 10X Chromium system for barcoding. Library construction was performed following the v1.1 Chemistry protocol of the Chromium Single Cell ATAC Library & Gel Bead Kit. Sequencing of the scATAC-seq libraries was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq System at the Technology Center for Genomics and Bioinformatics, UCLA. The raw data was processed and aligned to the reference genome mm10 post-sequencing using the ATAC CellRanger pipeline. Comparative analysis and peak calling were performed using Signac alongside published control scATAC-seq data19,52,101.

CUT&RUN sequencing and data analysis

Pulled mesenchymal tissue from the proximal end of the incisor was collected from control and DKO mice, respectively, and the tissue was digested as described in the ‘Mesenchymal cell isolation’ section. The CUT&RUN procedure was performed following the protocol for the Epicypher CUTANA™ ChIC/CUT&RUN kit (Epicypher, 14-1048), and library construction followed by the protocol of the Epicypher CUTANA™ library prep kit (Epicypher, 14-1001). The antibodies used for ARID1A were 1 μl of Abcam ab182561 and 2 μl of Cell Signaling Technology 12354. The antibodies used for ARID1B were 1 μl of Abcam ab57461 and 2 μl of Cell Signaling Technology 92964. The antibody used for FOXO1 was 2 μl of Proteintech 18592-1-AP. The antibody used for DLX2 was 2 μl of Abcam ab272902. The antibody used for IgG (control) was 1 μl of Epicypher 13-0042.

The CUT&RUN-seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq System at the Technology Center for Genomics and Bioinformatics, UCLA. CUT&RUN-seq reads were trimmed for adapters and low-quality sequences using Galore and subsequently mapped to the mouse mm10 reference genome using Bowtie2 with default parameters102. PCR duplicates were marked using Picard. Coverage tracks were generated using the bamCoverage tool from deepTools with the RPKM normalization method103, and peak calling was conducted using MACS2104. Peak annotation was performed with ChIPseeker (RStudio). Visualization of CUT&RUN peaks was carried out using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, Broad Institute). Motif analysis of enriched peaks was performed with HOMER (Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment). Each control CUT&RUN sequencing sample has two replicates, and each DKO CUT&RUN sequencing sample has one replicate.

Co-immunoprecipitation

The proximal ends of control mouse incisors were collected and lysed in IP lysis buffer. The soluble fraction was centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. The protein extracts were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against ARID1A (Abcam, ab182560), ARID1B (Cell Signaling Technology, 92964), or control IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, 3900) under constant rotation. On the following day, pre-washed Protein G Sepharose beads were added to the lysate-antibody mixtures and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. The immune complexes were then washed three times. Input samples (total protein extracts before immunoprecipitation) and immunoprecipitated proteins (IgG control and specific antibody-target complexes) were separated using 4–15% SDS–PAGE gels, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against FOXO1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2880), DLX2 (Abcam, ab272902), DLX1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 96585), DLX5 (Abcam, ab109737), and FOXO3A (Cell Signaling Technology, 2497), followed by detection using the Azure 300 imaging system (Azure Biosystems).

siRNA treatment of primary mesenchymal cells

Tissue from the proximal ends of control mouse incisors was dissected, minced, and plated on 6-well plates. Once cells had migrated out and reached confluence, they were harvested and replated at a density of 6 × 10⁴ cells per well into 24-well plates. The following day, cells were transfected with either control siRNA (Invitrogen, 4390844) or Dlx2 siRNA (Invitrogen, 4390771, ID s65010) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, 13778150), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was extracted 3 days post-transfection using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, 74034), and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to assess relative gene expression. The following primer sequences were obtained from PrimerBank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/) and used for qPCR reactions: Dlx2 (Forward: 5’-GTGGCTGATATGCACTCGACC-3’; Reverse: 5’-GCTGGTTGGTGTAGTAGCTGC-3’); Runx2 (Forward: 5’-GACTGTGGTTACCGTCATGGC-3’; Reverse: 5’-ACTTGGTTTTTCATAACAGCGGA-3’).

shRNA-lentivirus injection

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2.5% induction, 1.5% maintenance). After clipping hair from the cheek, the skin was aseptically prepared using alternating Betadine scrub and alcohol. A sagittal incision was made through the skin and masseter muscle to expose the incisor region. Two full-thickness bone defects, each 0.3–0.4 mm in diameter and spaced 3.0–4.0 mm apart, were created using a microsurgical drill equipped with a trephine bit. One defect was positioned at the proximal end of the mouse incisor, while the other was located in the dental pulp region directly overlying the incisor mesenchyme. For lentiviral injection, a 10 μl Hamilton syringe (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV) connected to a glass capillary micropipette was inserted into the bone defect at the proximal end of the incisor. Subsequently, 3 μl of lentivirus carrying Runx2 shRNA (Santa Cruz, sc-37146-v) or control shRNA (Santa Cruz, sc-108080) was injected into the proximal end of the incisor mesenchyme at a flow rate of 20 nl/s. Mice were euthanized at 9 days post-injection for downstream analyses.

Luciferase reporter assay

Tissue from the proximal ends of control and DKO mouse incisors was dissected, minced, and plated on 6-well plates. Once cells had migrated out and reached confluence, they were harvested and replated at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates. The Runx2 promoter, along with the cBAF binding sequence in the Runx2 intron (GeneCopoeia, CS-MPRM76664-LvPG04-01-50), the cBAF binding sequence in the Stat3 promoter (GeneCopoeia, CS-GS849T-LvPG04-01), and the cBAF binding sequence in the Trp53 promoter (GeneCopoeia, CS-GS850T-LvPG04-01) were cloned into the pEZX-LvPG04 vector, which contains Gaussia luciferase (Gluc) and secreted alkaline phosphatase (SeAP) in a dual-reporter system. These vectors were transfected using EndoFectin™ Lenti transfection reagent (GeneCopoeia, EF001) 48 h after plating cells, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of incubation, the transfected cells were changed to fresh medium. After another 48 h, the cell culture medium was collected for analysis using the Secrete-Pair dual-luminescence assay kit (GeneCopoeia, FL033) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The SeAP signal was used as an internal standard control for the relative Gluc activity (Gluc/SeAP ratio) analysis of all samples. At least three replicates were performed, and each replicate sample was read three times to calculate the average.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using GraphPad Prism10 software. To determine the significance of the differences between paired groups, at least three samples from each comparison group were included, and unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied. The results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Each experiment included at least three biological replicates with similar results.
