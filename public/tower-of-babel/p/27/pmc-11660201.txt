Non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease: The importance of physical activity and nutrition education—A randomized controlled study
Authors: Diler Us Altay, Yasemin Kaya, Duygu Mataraci Değirmenci, Emine Kocyiğit, Abdullah Üner, Tevfik Noyan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimNon‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is characterized by the accumulation of excess fat in the liver, causing liver cell damage, increased inflammation, and weight gain. Despite its high prevalence, diagnosis and follow‐up of the disease is difficult. Irisin, a slimming myokine produced in response to physical activity (PA), exhibits anti‐inflammatory and anti‐obesity effects. This study aimed to investigate changes in irisin levels, inflammation markers (tumor necrosis factor‐α [TNF‐α] and interleukin‐6 [IL‐6]), and myeloid differentiation factor‐2 (MD2) levels in NAFLD, as well as anthropometric and routine biochemical parameters, by providing PA recommendations and nutrition education (NE) to individuals diagnosed with NAFLD over a period of 12 weeks.MethodsThe study included 62 patients diagnosed with NAFLD who did not use alcohol. They were divided into groups: PA, NE, both (NE + PA), and untreated (control) patients. Patients receiving NE were provided with 1‐h NE sessions every 4 weeks for 12 weeks, and their personal information, nutritional status, 24‐h retrospective food consumption record, and anthropometric measurements were recorded at the beginning (day 0) and end (week 12) of the study. The PA group was recommended aerobic walking for 30 min, 5 days a week. At the beginning (day 0) and end (week 12) of the study, patients' anthropometric and routine biochemical tests were conducted, and irisin, MD2, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 levels were measured using the ELISA method.ResultsAll groups were similar in demographic characteristics and dietary habits. After 12 weeks, there were no significant differences in biochemical parameters among the groups. Glucose levels increased in the untreated group but decreased in the PA and PA + NE groups compared to baseline, with a significant decrease in the PA group. Insulin levels significantly decreased in the NE group. The PA + NE group showed decreased aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma‐glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein, and triglyceride levels and significant decrease in ALT levels. AST decreased significantly in the PA group while high‐density lipoprotein increased significantly. There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in irisin, MD2, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels. After 12 weeks, irisin levels significantly increased in nutrition and PA groups except the untreated group. There were no statistically significant differences in IL‐6 and MD2 levels compared with baseline after 12 weeks. PA recommendations alone were not effective in observing significant changes in anthropometric measurements in individuals with NAFLD. It was detected that only nutritional recommendations provided a significant decrease in body fat ratio but were insufficient for the change in other anthropometric measurements. In the group where NE and PA were recommended together, a significant decrease in anthropometric measurements was found. The NE group significantly reduced their energy and carbohydrates (%EI) intake after 12 weeks of intervention compared with the baseline.ConclusionNE and PA recommendations led to improvements in liver‐related biochemical parameters and significant reductions in anthropometric measurements among individuals with NAFLD. Moreover, patients receiving NE experienced a decrease in energy and carbohydrates intake as a percentage of total energy intake (%EI). Increased irisin levels in NE, PA, and NE + PA groups may have contributed to the decrease in body fat percentage.Keywords: Irisin, NAFLD, Nutrition education (NE), Physical activity (PA), Proinflammatory cytokines

Introduction

Non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is one of the most common chronic liver disorders worldwide 1 that occurs when there is an excessive accumulation of fat in the liver, such as triglycerides. Liver cell damage and inflammation often occur due to excess fat accumulation. 2 , 3 , 4 In Western society, with the negative changes in dietary habits and transition to a sedentary lifestyle, NAFLD, is observed in 20–30% of the general population. 5 Despite its prevalence, challenges persist in diagnosing and monitoring the disease. The gold standard method used in diagnosing and classifying the disease is liver biopsy. However, due to its invasive nature, there is a need for non‐invasive laboratory and imaging methods to be used instead for diagnosis. For this purpose, many imaging techniques and biomarkers are being investigated. 6 , 7 Although effective drug treatment has not yet been found, there are drugs in the research phase.

Research worldwide has also shown that medical nutrition‐based diets have been applied to patients in treating NAFLD, resulting in positive outcomes in most cases. One of the most essential activities that ensures balanced weight loss and energy restriction in the body is physical activity (PA). 8 These activities, which can be moderate in intensity and performed for half an hour daily, 5 days a week, can be increased in reasonable proportions. PA, whether focused on walking or aerobic exercise, effectively increases muscle mass and fat burning and controls insulin resistance. However, more than weight loss alone through exercise is required in the treatment process of NAFLD; nutrition education (NE) and medical diet therapy must also be implemented.

Fibronectin type III NE protein 5 (FNDC5) is an irisin type 1 membrane protein precursor that circulates at basal levels. It is secreted more by skeletal muscles during acute and intense exercise, enters circulation, and regulates lipid metabolism. Irisin may increase the expression of uncoupling protein in adipose tissue. The primary role of irisin is to stimulate the transformation of white adipose tissue cells into brown adipocytes. This process, called “browning” of white fat, consumes energy to produce heat. 9 The anti‐inflammatory, anti‐apoptotic, anti‐cancer, and anti‐oxidative properties of irisin have received a great deal of attention from the scientific society. 10

There are few studies regarding the significant mechanisms of irisin in forming NAFLD. However, experimental results have shown that the liver is a target organ of irisin. 11 Hou et al. conducted a study on obese mice, demonstrating that exogenous administration of irisin could reduce body mass and visceral fat levels and improve glucose and lipid metabolic disorders. 12 Zhang et al. demonstrated that intraperitoneal injection of recombinant irisin in mice fed a high‐fat diet reduced body weight and insulin resistance. 13 Irisin increases adipocyte energy expenditure, enhances the expression of metabolite intermediates and enzymes, and plays a role in inhibiting lipid accumulation. These experimental results indicate that irisin exhibits pleiotropic effects in improving human adipocyte metabolism. 14 However, some similar experiments showed different results. After treatment of adipocytes with irisin, irisin did not directly affect lipidosis and fatty acid metabolism by adipocyte. 15 In recent years, studies investigating circulating irisin concentrations in NAFLD patients have been encountered. For example, Shanaki et al. discovered that irisin levels in NAFLD patients were significantly lower than in controls. 16 On the other hand, another study examined the irisin levels in the liver tissues of NAFLD patients and showed that irisin levels were higher than in healthy individuals. 17 NAFLD patients (56 cases of non‐alcoholic fatty liver and 75 cases of non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis) were higher compared with the healthy control group the levels of irisin in the liver tissue. 18 In a recent mouse study, it is believed that the increased circulation of irisin due to exercise competes with myeloid differentiation factor‐2 (MD2) to bind to the TLR‐4 receptor, reducing inflammation and contributing to the improvement process of NAFLD. 19

This study aimed to investigate the changes in the exercise myokine irisin, inflammation markers (tumor necrosis factor‐α [TNF‐α] and interleukin‐6 [IL‐6]), and MD2 levels, as well as anthropometric and routine biochemical parameters, by providing PA recommendations and NE to individuals diagnosed with NAFLD over 12 weeks. Thus, the goal was to determine how PA and NE affect the elevated inflammation markers in NAFLD.

Materials and Methods

Study design

The current study was a randomized, controlled, single‐blind clinical trial. Participants were randomized to an NE group (NE, n = 15), a PA group (PA, n = 16), an NE + PA group (NE + PA, n = 14), and a control group (n = 16) for 12 weeks. The randomization was made using a random numbers system (research randomizer.org).

A questionnaire recorded the general characteristics, medical history, and nutritional habits of the patients. Data for the study were collected using face‐to‐face interviews, telephone recall, and questionnaire forms. Anthropometric measurements (body weight and height) and body composition analysis were performed; 24‐h dietary recalls and blood samples for measurement of biochemical parameters were taken twice at the baseline (day 0) and end (week 12) of the study.

This study was conducted in compliance with the standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and signed informed permission was obtained from all participants. The protocol study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Clinical Research at Ordu University (No: 2022/159).

Study participants

The study consisted of 62 patients who were referred to the Department of Internal Medicine and diagnosed with NAFLD based on biochemical and ultrasonography results by clinicians and who did not consume alcohol, forming the sample of the study. The sample size of the study was calculated using the G*power 3.1.9.4 program. The effect size for calculating the sample size was derived from the study “Irisin in patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease”, where the effect size was found to be 3.22. However, according to effect size guidelines, an effect size of f = 0.40 was accepted. The sample size for a four‐independent group test was calculated with a 5% error margin (α = 0.05) and 80% power (1 − β = 0.80).

Patients aged between 20 and 75 years with a body mass index (BMI) value between 25.0 and 40 kg/m2, who did not consume alcohol, were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were a history of alcohol use (more than 20 cc/day for women, more than 30 cc/day for men), those positive for HBsAg or anti‐HCV, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson's disease, malignancy, taking drugs such as corticosteroids, methotrexate, tamoxifen, and oral contraceptives, jejunoileal bypass or extensive small intestine resection, heart or kidney failure, other chronic liver diseases, pregnancy, lactation, and unwillingness to continue the study protocol.

Nutrition and PA interventions

In our study, patients in the NE group, accompanied by two expert nutritionists, received NE for 12 weeks, with 1‐h sessions every 4 weeks. Participants were not given specific instructions to limit their caloric intake. The three interventions were as follows. NE group: two professional nutritionists offered nutrition instructions to the patients, focusing on the sufficiency of their existing eating habits, adoption of healthy eating habits, optimal macronutrient and micronutrient balance, and healthy food preparation and cooking techniques. Patients in this group were instructed to consume fish and legumes at least two to three times a week and walnuts (30 g/day) by the Turkey Dietary Guidelines‐2022. 20 Participants were encouraged to increase the intake of whole grains, complex carbohydrate, low‐glycemic index foods, low dairy fat, healthy fat especially olive oil, white meat and also avoid the intake of sugar, unhealthy fats, refined carbohydrate, processed meat products, and fast foods. 21 NE + PA group: in addition to the dietary recommendations in the NE group, moderate walking in an aerobic environment (30 min a day, 5 days a week) was recommended for 12 weeks. Both groups were instructed to adhere to a balanced diet. The research dietitian provided detailed instructions to patients in both diet groups regarding dietary guidelines for managing NAFLD. These guidelines included limiting the consumption of saturated fats, avoiding processed and packaged foods, alcohol, instant beverages, carbonated and sugary drinks, and sweets high in sugar and fat. PA group: moderate walking in an aerobic environment (30 min a day, 5 days a week) was recommended for 12 weeks.

Anthropometric measurements and body composition

The body weight and body composition were measured using a bioelectrical impedance analysis device (TANITA, model MC‐780MA) while the patients were fasting, barefoot, and wearing light clothes. The height was measured with a stadiometer with 0.1 cm accuracy, and the body weight was measured with 0.5 kg precision. BMI was calculated using the following formula: BMI (kg/m2) = [body weight (kg) / (height [m])2] (https://www.cdc.gov/healthyweight/assessing/bmi/index.html).

Assessment of dietary intake

The assessment of dietary intake was conducted through the 24‐h dietary recall method, which research dieticians administered. BeBIS software assessed the subjects' total energy, macronutrient, and micronutrient intakes from their records (BeBIS, Nutrition Information System, Version 9) (https://bebis.com.tr/anasayfa).

Biochemical analysis

Blood samples were collected at baseline and the end of the study (12th week). Samples were stored at −80°C after centrifugation until analysis. Biochemical measurements including serum fasting glucose, insulin, hemoglobin, glycated hemoglobin, C‐reactive protein, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, triglyceride (TG), uric acid, vitamin B12, ferritin, thyroid‐stimulating hormone, irisin, MD2, TNF‐α, IL‐6 measured using the ELISA method were recorded.

Statistical analysis

The analysis results were loaded into the statistical program SPSS 21 (IBM software, Pointe‐Claire, Quebec, Canada) to check for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables are indicated as frequency (n) and percentage (%). The ANOVA test was utilized to compare independent parameters conforming to a normal distribution, the paired t‐test was employed for dependent‐paired comparisons and χ 2 test for categorical variables. The Kruskal–Wallis variance analysis, and dependent‐paired Wilcoxon test were used for independent parameters not adhering to normal distribution. The statistical differences within groups in repeated measurements were examined using the paired t‐test, the non‐parametric Wilcoxon paired two‐sample test, or McNemar's test. Results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in the statistical tests.

Results

The groups in this study are similar in terms of demographic characteristics and dietary habits (Tables 1 and 2). The ELISA results and routine biochemical parameters of NAFLD patients are presented in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in terms of irisin, MD2, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels. However, the irisin level increased significantly at the end of 12 weeks compared with that at the beginning in the NE, PA, and NE + PA groups, except for the group with no intervention (control). Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences in IL‐6 and MD2 levels at the end of 12 weeks compared with those at the beginning (Table 4). Furthermore, there were no statistically significant differences in biochemical parameters between the groups at the beginning of the study. Glucose levels increased in the control group and decreased in the PA and PA + NE groups compared with the beginning, with the decrease in the PA group being statistically significant. In the NE group, there was a statistically significant decrease in insulin levels. Moreover, in the PA + NE group; AST, gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total cholesterol, LDL, and TG levels decreased, and the decrease in ALT levels was significant. In the PA group, AST decreased significantly while HDL increased significantly (Table 3). In this study, it was observed that PA recommendations alone were not effective in observing significant changes in anthropometric measurements in individuals with NAFLD. It was detected that only NE recommendations provided a significant decrease in body fat ratio, but were insufficient for the change in other anthropometric measurements. In the NE + PA group, a significant decrease in anthropometric measurements was found. Accordingly, it may be recommended that PA recommendations be included along with NE in the treatment of individuals with NAFLD (Table 5). Results of the assessment of the nutritional intake of participants at baseline and at the end of the intervention are summarized in Table 6. The NE group significantly reduced their energy and carbohydrates (%EI) intake after 12 weeks of intervention compared with the baseline (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively). There was no significant difference in terms of energy, protein (EI%), total fat (EI%), saturated fatty acids (EI%), monounsaturated fatty acids (EI%), polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) (EI%), carbohydrates (EI%), fiber, n‐6, n‐3, and n‐6/n‐3 intake of participants in the PA, NE + PA, and control groups at the baseline and end of the study. There is a significant difference in intergroup of protein and PUFA intake after 12 weeks (P < 0.05). The findings from the evaluation of the participants' food group intake at the baseline and end of the intervention are presented in Table 7. After 12 weeks of intervention, both the PA and control groups showed a substantial increase in their consumption of fat and oils compared with the baseline results (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in the other food group intakes of participants in the NE, PA, NE + PA, and control groups at the baseline and end of the study (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In many studies, weight loss is beneficial for the treatment of NAFLD and non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis, particularly leading to improvements in liver biochemistry profiles. NE and PA recommendations caused a decrease in glucose, insulin levels, and liver function tests in NAFLD patient. In a study involving 25 obese patients, it was observed that after a 3‐month program involving dietary restriction and PA, not only did the patients aminotransferase levels improve but their total cholesterol and fasting blood sugar levels also improved. 22 Similarly, in our study after 12 weeks, glucose levels increased in the untreated group but decreased in the PA and PA + NE groups compared with baseline, with a significant decrease in the PA group. Insulin levels significantly decreased in the NE group. The PA + NE group showed decreased AST, GGT, ALP, total cholesterol, LDL, TG levels, and a significant decrease in ALT levels. AST decreased significantly in the PA group while HDL increased significantly.

In a program targeting weight loss, reducing carbohydrate and fat intake and increasing physical exercises should be addressed first. 23 There is not yet a common consensus on how much energy restriction should be and how much weight loss should be achieved over what time. The important thing, as explained by the American Gastroenterology Association (AGA), is to pay attention to BMI ratios in the treatment of NAFLD, just like in obesity. Obesity treatment is recommended for those with a BMI of more than 25 kg/m2. 24 Irisin improves mechanisms such as glucose homeostasis, insulin resistance and obesity, which play a role in NAFLD pathogenesis. 25 Nutrition and PA are associated with serum irisin levels in NAFLD. Irisin is an exercise‐associated myokine that regulates energy metabolism by causing the browning of white adipose tissue. 26 Acute or long‐term exercise increases the expression of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC‐1α) in skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle, supports the production of fibronectin‐domain‐containing protein 5 (FNDC5), and as a result, irisin is formed by proteolytic cleavage from FNDC5. 27 Irisin binds to the surface of white adipocytes, increasing the expression of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP‐1) and triggering the transformation of white fat cells into beige cells. 28 In recent years, there have been many studies investigating circulating irisin concentrations, known as exercise‐associated myokine, in patients diagnosed with NAFLD. In some studies, the level of irisin in circulation increased, in some it decreased, and in some it did not change at all. Zhang et al. showed that liver enzymes and liver triglyceride content were inversely proportional to irisin concentration in obese individuals. However, in contrast, Rizk et al. found a positive relationship between irisin level and BMI, serum triglycerides, homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance, and liver enzymes. Choi et al. also found the irisin level in NAFLD to be lower than in healthy individuals. 29 , 30 , 31 NAFLD begins with inflammation and excess lipid accumulation, activating hepatic fibrosis. Moreover, chronic inflammation causes insulin resistance in NAFLD disease. MD2 is an adapter protein that interacts with Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4). The MD2‐TLR4 complex recognizes lipopolysaccharide and activates the myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88) intracellular adapter protein, mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), and nuclear factor‐kB (NF‐kB) pathways and causes the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. As a result of exercise, FNDC5 and irisin levels increase. Irisin competes with MD2 to bind to TLR4 and, by preventing MD2‐TLR4 from binding, prevents the activation of intracellular pathways and thus the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. As a result, exercise reduced weight status and liver damage in mice with NAFLD. 19 According to our ELISA results, irisin levels increased significantly in patient groups where nutrition, PA, and both were applied. Accordingly, irisin could not bind to the TLR4 receptor (receptor resistance probably developed), its level increased in the blood, the MD2 molecule bound to the TLR4 receptor, activating intracellular signaling pathways, and the level of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α and IL‐6) increased in the blood.

In studies conducted with individuals with high body weight, it has been observed that NE and PA provide a significant decrease in anthropometric measurements. 32 , 33 , 34 In this study, it was observed that exercise recommendations alone were not effective in observing significant changes in anthropometric measurements in individuals with NAFLD. It was detected that only nutritional recommendations provided a significant decrease in body fat ratio, but were insufficient for the change in other anthropometric measurements. In the group where NE and exercise were recommended together, a significant decrease in anthropometric measurements was found. Accordingly, it can be said that providing NE alone is insufficient in the treatment of individuals with NAFLD. Therefore, exercise recommendations should be included in addition to NE.

Implementing optimal dietary modifications is a crucial aspect of managing NAFLD and its associated complications due to their proven impact on metabolic parameters. Randomized controlled trials have shown that a decrease in energy intake leads to better weight loss, reduced liver fat mass, lower levels of liver enzymes, and enhanced insulin resistance in patients with NAFLD. 35 , 36 A healthy diet, particularly a Mediterranean diet, is widely recognized as being important for NAFLD patients, even if the optimal macronutrient composition of their diet has not been determined. 37 A systematic review and meta‐analysis found that Western food patterns were associated with a 56% higher risk of NAFLD. However, the Prudent and Mediterranean dietary patterns were associated with a 22% and 23% lower risk of this illness, respectively. Our study found that the NE group observed a significant reduction in their energy and carbohydrates intake (%EI) after 12 weeks of intervention, compared with the baseline measurements (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01). There are significant differences in the intergroup protein and PUFA intake following a 12‐week period, with statistical significance (P < 0.05). Following a 12‐week intervention, it was observed that both the PA and control groups experienced a significant increase in their fat and oil intake when compared with the baseline values (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in the other food group intakes of participants in the NE, PA, NE + PA, and control groups at the baseline and end of the study (P > 0.05). Participants in the NE, PA, NE + PA, and control groups were not significantly different in their intakes of the other food groups at the end of the study (P > 0.05). There may be several reasons for this result. At first, the research participants may have been inclined to follow the provided NE, but their commitment to the nutrition education may have diminished as time went on. Second, it may indicate that continuous adherence to nutrition and PA interventions during the study period is difficult. Third, a 3‐day or longer dietary recall can immediately record food intake, allowing for the collection of more accurate data. Finally, the study group consisted of a rather limited number of participants. NE and PA recommendations led to improvements in liver‐related biochemical parameters and significant reductions in anthropometric measurements among individuals with NAFLD. Moreover, patients receiving NE experienced a decrease in energy and carbohydrates intake as a percentage of total energy intake (%EI). Increased irisin levels in NE, PA, and NE + PA groups may have contributed to the decrease in body fat percentage. As a result, NE and PA contributed to improving the quality of life of patients.
