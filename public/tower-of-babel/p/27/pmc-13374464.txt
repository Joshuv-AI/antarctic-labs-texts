Do all individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus prefer decentralisation in clinical trials? Findings from a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Julia Kopanz, Jorien Veldwijk, Bart Lagerwaard, Julia K Mader, Dietrich Tews, Thomas T van Sloten, Diederick E Grobbee, Mira G P Zuidgeest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore differences in preferences for participation in trials with varying degrees of decentralisation among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and to show how personal, disease specific, digital and trial experience factors influence these preferences as well as impact potential trial participation rates.DesignA discrete choice experiment was conducted, including 16 choice tasks, with each two trial options and an opt-out.SettingPreference data was collected in the Netherlands between July 2023 and July 2024.ParticipantsA total of 276 individuals with T2DM aged ≥18 years took part in the study.Outcome measuresTrial options comprised six attributes: contact with the study team, activities to perform yourself, use of digital technologies, number of scheduled contacts, trial duration, safety and efficacy of the drug. Preference heterogeneity was explored using a latent class analysis.ResultsFour classes were identified displaying differences in preferences for trial designs. Younger female participants were more likely to have a strong preference for decentralised trials in any form. Two different classes of elderly participants were identified: elderly males who were more likely to accept any trial design including decentralisation versus elderly who were more likely to be averse to participation in all trials. The latter class showed an increase in participation probability for a home-based trial including video consultation, but still was not very likely to join.ConclusionsDecentralisation in trials was overall preferred over site-based trials, but variation in preferences was identified and associated with age and gender. This may impact the choice for decentralisation in trials, especially since women and the elderly are often under-represented in trials. Understanding patient preferences may help to overcome participation challenges and to increase diversity in future trials.Keywords: Clinical Trial; Patient Preference; Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2

Introduction

Clinical trial participation is influenced by diverse factors, such as perceived personal health benefits, altruism, trust, time commitments, financial incentives, trial and personal characteristics.1,8 It is important to understand preference differences to integrate them in the design of future trials. This is of particular relevance to promote diversity in trials, as the under-representation of different groups still remains a problem in trials. For example, low numbers of elderly people, females and ethnic minorities were reported to enrol in trials.9,15 Barriers for trial participation are various and could be for example mistrust in research, trial burden, transport challenges, cultural obstacles.1 2 16 17

Although some studies looked into differences in preferences for clinical trials,4 8 ongoing innovations in the way we conduct clinical trials may change such preferences. Decentralised clinical trials (DCTs) move some or all trial activities to participants’ homes or direct vicinity, using digital technologies and innovations in trial operations.18 19 This way participants have to travel to the research site less (in a hybrid trial) or not at all (in a fully DCT).19,22 DCTs may thus offer greater flexibility and convenience, which may reduce the burden on trial participants.21 23 Nonetheless, little empirical information is available on which people would be more willing to participate in a DCT.

Given that some factors affecting conventional trial participation were identified to be distinct across participants of for example different age, race, education and income,624,26 there is a necessity to research such possible differences in preferences also in DCTs. Similarly to traditional trials, we found various factors to influence participation in DCTs,27 and revealed that individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in three European countries generally favoured decentralised trial designs over conventional on-site ones. However, we also found significant heterogeneity in these preferences within the study population, which needs to be further explored.28

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the differences in preferences for taking part in clinical trials with decentralised elements among individuals with T2DM, and to show how personal, disease specific, digital and trial experience factors impact those preferences. The secondary objective was to determine how these preference differences influence the acceptance of hypothetical clinical trial scenarios with different levels of decentralisation. T2DM was chosen for this research because individuals with this disease are often used to self-managing their disease at home and using digital technologies, or could have comorbidities such as mobility issues, making this therapeutic area particularly suitable for the decentralisation of clinical trials. The knowledge gained from this research will facilitate the design of future patient-centred clinical trials by providing insights into how a decentralised trial model might influence who might participate in a trial. Incorporating such preferences for trial participation in the set-up of trials could both increase equity and inclusiveness for certain under-represented groups, and lead to a better generalisability of trial findings to clinical practice.

Methods

Discrete choice experiment and survey

A large-scale discrete choice experiment (DCE) on preferences for participation in clinical trials with different levels of decentralisation was conducted among individuals with T2DM in the Netherlands. The DCE was planned, conducted and analysed according to international guidance reports.29,31 A DCE is a commonly used method to elicit preferences in healthcare.3032,34 In a DCE, participants are asked to make choices on a series of hypothetical choice tasks. Each choice task comprises two or more alternatives, which are described by different characteristics, that is, attributes, and their varying degrees, that is, attribute levels.32 35 36 As participants choose the most preferred alternative for each choice task, this enables the calculation of utility estimates using statistical methods based on the underlying random utility theory.32 37

In our DCE, the selection of attributes and levels (table 1) was based on comprehensive qualitative work. Potential attributes representing drivers for participation in clinical trials with different decentralisation levels were identified in focus groups conducted with people living with T2DM.27 Each focus group participant individually ranked the final list of potential attributes according to their importance. This ranking was subsequently used by the researchers to prioritise the potential attributes to the six most important attributes for use in the DCE. In a next step, the research team defined the levels for the attributes based on the information gained from the focus groups and the literature. Additionally, discussions were held with various stakeholders, that is, people living with T2DM, clinicians, ethicists, clinical trial managers, study nurses and researchers, to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the attributes, attribute levels and their definitions to ascertain that the language and wording chosen was understood by the anticipated participants.

Attributes and levels of the discrete choice experiment

For the design of the choice tasks, a Bayesian D-efficient design31 was generated using the Ngene software V.1.4.38 No interactions were included because there was no strong evidence to support them, and adding interactions would have made the model too complex for exploration purposes beyond this study’s objectives. To avoid unrealistic alternatives, the design included the restriction that trials that did not deploy digital technologies could not be fully decentralised and take place at home without study team visits. In the initial pilot study, the priors for all levels were set to zero, and priors were updated with results of pilot data. Each DCE choice task (eg, see figure 1) entailed two unlabelled alternatives, that is, trial A and trial B, which included trial options ranging from no decentralisation to full decentralisation and an opt-out option. A blocked design was generated with 48 unique choice tasks divided over three blocks. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the blocks and each participant was asked to complete a total of 16 choice tasks. At the start of the survey, participants were provided with the informed consent form in digital format. If participants were willing to voluntarily participate in the survey, their informed consent was obtained online by ticking a box. This was followed by an introduction to the topic of DCTs and the DCE choice tasks. Additional background questions concerning demographics, socio-economics, disease characteristics, trial experience, digital literacy, health literacy,39,41 recruitment and evaluation were asked. All answers to the questions were self-reported by the participants. The DCE survey was programmed in the SurveyEngine platform, pretested in think aloud rounds to ensure comprehension, readability, understandability and wording of the text provided. The survey was administered to patients online with data collection between July 2023 and July 2024. The study complied with ethical requirements and the DIscrete choice experiment REporting ChecklisT (DIRECT) was used for the reporting of this study.42 A detailed description of the set-up of the DCE study is provided elsewhere.28

Study participants, recruitment and sample size

Persons aged 18 years or older, who were diagnosed with T2DM at least 1 year before survey start and were living in the Netherlands were eligible to take part. Individuals were recruited by healthcare professionals (HCPs), general staff and/or researchers in various settings, for example, pharmacies, general practices, hospitals, outpatient departments, diabetes events. Recruitment was conducted through direct HCP outreach, face to face or by letters, and was supported by flyers and posters in the above-mentioned settings and posts on social media and relevant websites, applying convenience sampling. A sample of around 300 participants was aimed for, based on calculations using a general rule of thumb suggested by Johnson and Orme as per guidance considerations.30 43 44

Patient and public involvement

This study is part of the IMI Trials@Home Project and the study was supported by the Trials@Home Patient Expert Panel (PEP) members. The PEP members were actively involved in the discussion process concerning the attributes and levels for the DCE. Further, people living with T2DM participated in the focus groups to identify potential attributes and were involved in the pretesting of the DCE survey in the form of think aloud rounds.

Data analyses

All preference data was analysed using a latent class model.2945,47 This model allows determining preference heterogeneity. Based on the choice data, this model forms classes with similar preference structures, while preference structures between classes differ. Thereby, the classes are latent and participants are assigned to a class up to a probability of class membership.

Effect coding was used for all attributes because it enables calculation of a coefficient for the omitted level per attribute.29 48 49 An alternative specific constant was not included in the model as the conditional logit model used for data exploration showed this variable to be insignificant (ie, data showed no evidence for participants being inclined to choose the left over the right trial option, called left-right bias). In the final analysis only participants who completed at least one choice task were included.

To determine the optimal number of classes, model outputs with two to six classes were compared. For deciding on the final number of classes, for each model the Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion (online supplemental table 1) and deliberative assessment of interpretability were used. Generally adding classes will improve model fit but does not necessarily aid the explanatory power of the model nor does it add to clinical relevance. For each trial option and for the opt-out option, the utility can be calculated as illustrated in the equations 1–2. Thereby, ‘r’ stands for the participant, ‘c’ for the assigned class, ‘a’ for the choice of the alternative and ‘t’ for the choice task, with β1-β18 describing the coefficients of the attribute levels. While an alternative specific constant was excluded in the model, for the utility of the opt-out, a constant was included, namely β0, which describes the constant for choosing not to participate in either of the presented trial options.

To better understand which respondent is more likely to belong to which of the latent classes, a class membership model was fitted. In our work, the initial variable selection was based on literature,624,27 diversity considerations50 and the expected impact on participation in clinical trials with decentralised approaches. We tested the following characteristics for contributing to class membership: gender, age, living area, ethnic group, education, occupation, support in activities of daily life, previous trial participation, experience with performing diabetes related measurements, health literacy, average internet use for private purposes. In a first step, a univariate screening of all characteristics was performed, that is, each variable was tested individually for significance (p<0.05) in the class membership model. In a second step, all variables that significantly contributed to membership when tested individually were included together in the multivariable class membership model, that is, gender (‘male’ vs ‘female’), age, education (‘high (ie, tertiary and higher secondary education)’ vs ‘other (ie, all other entries)’), occupation (‘working (ie, in paid work, self-employed, some ‘others’)’ vs ‘non-working (ie, all other entries)’). Variables that did not contribute significantly to the multivariable class membership (p<0.05) were further removed using backward selection. Due to the limited prior knowledge on the research topic, this data-driven process was chosen for variable selection.

The beta coefficients per class of the final model were interpreted and used for further calculations. The relative attribute importance was calculated by the maximum utility difference between the most and least favoured level per attribute. To compare importances, each utility difference was divided by the sum of all utility differences.51 The class adjusted relative attribute importance explains the average attribute importance in the population. It was calculated by multiplying the relative importance of each class by the respective class share and by taking the sum of all these values.

Trial uptake probabilities were calculated for hypothetical trial scenarios with different degrees of decentralisation per class. For each scenario, the utility was calculated per class based on the beta coefficients of the final model and all estimated trial scenarios were compared to the opt-out option (Equation 3). Class adjusted trial uptake probabilities were calculated by accounting for class shares as described above.

Four hypothetical clinical trial scenarios were proposed: a baseline trial, a hybrid trial and two different fully DCTs. The baseline trial was modelled after a conventional trial. It was designed to take place fully at site requiring participants to travel 30 min, with monthly contact moments with trial staff over a 1-year trial duration, performing basic measurements themselves, using digital technologies for communication purposes only (eg, text messages, email, smartphone apps and video-calling tools), with an investigational drug that appears to be safe and to work. Attribute levels that remained the same for the other trial scenarios were: a scheduled contact moment once every 3 months, a 1-year trial duration and an investigational drug that appears to be safe and to work. The hybrid trial was designed to take place both at participant’s home and on site, with 30 min travel time, including basic and simple measurements and digital technologies for communication only. One DCT involved a home visit by HCPs, basic and simple measurements and digital technologies for communication only. The other DCT included home video contact, with basic, simple and complex activities and digital technologies for communication and measurements.

Additionally, the overall number of participants who always, never or sometimes opted out was calculated. Descriptive statistics were calculated for the other survey questions. Categorical variables were presented in frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were presented with mean, SD, median, IQR, minimum and maximum, depending on the distribution. All data were analysed in Stata (Version StataNow/SE 18.5). Results were considered statistically significant at the 5% level.

The attributes ‘contact with the study team’, ‘number of scheduled contacts’, ‘safety and efficacy of the drug’ significantly influenced the trial participation decision in all four classes, while the attribute ‘activities yourself’ did not. In class 2, the attribute ‘use of digital technologies’ significantly contributed to the choice, while the attribute ‘trial duration’ did not, whereas the opposite was observed for both attributes in all other classes.

When reviewing the preferences for attribute levels across the classes, a significant difference in preferences was observed. Participants in class 1 were averse to travelling a long time to the research site. Participants in class 2 preferred a combination of trial site and home. Participants in class 3 favoured a decentralised trial with video contact and participants in class 4 preferred any form of a fully decentralised trial. Contact with trial staff every 3 months was preferred in classes 1 and 2, while annual contact was favoured in class 3, and both annual and 3 monthly contact was preferred in class 4 over more frequent contact. In all classes, participants preferred a trial duration of 1 year compared to the other options and trials investigating a drug with (some) evidence of being safe and effective compared with trials with a less established safety profile.

In classes 1 and 2, participants preferred taking part in a trial as opposed to opting-out (ie, not taking part in the trial options presented), with a strong preference for participation in class 2. A priori to changing the attribute levels, participants in classes 3 and 4 favoured opting out, with a strong preference in class 3. Overall, 35% of participants never, 49% sometimes and 18% always opted-out.

The relative attribute importance for all classes as well as adjusted for class probability (ie, class size adjusted mean of the total sample) is shown in figure 2. When adjusted for class probability, the attributes ‘knowledge about the safety and efficacy of the drug’ and ‘contact with the study team’ were on average most important. Relative to the other attributes, the attribute ‘knowledge about the safety and efficacy of the drug’ was also most important in class 1, while the attribute ‘contact with the study team’ was more important in classes 2 and 4. In class 3, the most important attribute was ‘trial duration’. In classes 1, 3 and 4, the attributes ‘use of digital technologies’ and ‘activities that you need to perform yourself’ were least important for the trial participation decision, while this was the case for ‘use of digital technologies’ and ‘trial duration’ in class 2.

Trial uptake scenarios

Compared to all other hypothetical trial scenarios, the DCT including home video contact had the highest acceptance when adjusted for class probability and for all classes except class 2. In class 2, willingness to participate was highest for the hybrid trial. In classes 1 and 4, the willingness to participate was highly influenced by different trial designs, that is, changes in the attribute levels. Class 2 strongly favoured trial participation, and almost no impact of trial design on trial acceptance was observed. In class 3, participants generally had a low acceptance ranging from 3% to 18% willingness to participate with the highest uptake for the DCT with video contact (figure 3). Discussion

This is the first DCE exploring differences in preferences for trial designs that include decentralised elements among individuals with T2DM. Four classes were identified that all favoured trial designs involving decentralisation compared to a conventional trial. However, a priori to changing the attribute levels, the constant of class three indicates a clear preference to not participate in any trial. In all four classes, the contact with the study team, the number of scheduled contacts and the safety and efficacy of the drug significantly drove the trial participation decision, whereas this was not the case for trial activities to be performed by participants themselves, suggesting that individuals with T2DM are largely indifferent to conducting trial activities themselves. However, the four classes also showed differences in preferences and willingness to participate in trials. In particular, younger female participants (class 4) were more likely to prefer fully decentralised trials in any form and were averse to long travel to the research site. For this class, the location and type of contact with the trial site mattered most in their decision whether to take part in a trial. While trials in some disease areas are reported to have a sufficient number of women enrolled,52 many others still show an under-representation of women.1011 53,57 Designing a DCT that involves either contact via messages, video or a home visit may offer a solution for future trials to partially overcome this under-representation.

There are several barriers to women’s participation in clinical trials which are less related to decentralisation, such as insufficient information on research, eligibility criteria, old age, fewer women in trial leadership, but also barriers more relevant to decentralisation, such as travel burden or time commitment.5458,63 While some factors influencing trial participation do not differ between women and men,64 65 other aspects, such as the opinion of others, concerns about trial associated risks and particularly caregiving responsibilities, which are especially relevant for younger women, seem to impact women’s decisions more strongly.65,67 Approaches to increase women’s enrolment in clinical trials are manifold and include, for example, transportation services, compensation for care arrangements, flexibility in terms of timing, decrease of visits at the research site, home-based follow-up.59 61 68 69 DCTs provide the opportunities to implement a lot of these strategies and thereby enhance convenience and reduce trial burden.21,23 DCTs may therefore be particularly suitable for young women, as they are averse to long travel times and participation in DCTs may be more seamlessly integrated into their everyday responsibilities, such as caregiving tasks. Yet, sponsors and investigators should also address the other barriers, which cannot be solved by decentralisation.

Two distinct classes of elderly participants were identified in our study: elderly males who were more likely to participate in any trial (class 2) and elderly who were more likely to be averse to any trial participation (class 3). The former class preferred a combination of home and on-site contact with shorter travel, but had a high participation probability for any trial scenario. The latter class preferred a DCT with video contact, for which participation probability was also highest. Similarly, some other studies observed a general willingness to participate in trials in the elderly,70 71 whereas this willingness was reported to be lower in elderly people in others.4 72 Note that several systematic reviews in different disease areas report an under-representation of the elderly in trials.10 12 13 52 73 74 Barriers for older adults to enrol in trials are amongst others patient’s health condition, not meeting eligibility criteria, concerns about travelling to the research site, trial related burden and time commitments.75,77 The latter barrier was also reflected in the class of elderly with the trial duration being most important for their trial participation decision. Potential solutions to help mitigate barriers are numerous. For example, approaches to alleviate the transportation burden could consist of an organised transport service or recruiting older patients from their home.75 Again, not all barriers can be solved by DCT approaches, but the adoption of home-based decentralised elements can reduce both burden in terms of travel and time, supporting the convenience and ease of trial participation.21,23 This may be particularly likely to be appealing to elderly people to mitigate the travel burden they face in site-based clinical trials.78 79 This may partly explain the preference for a hybrid trial observed in the class of older men in our study. However, it is to be noted that this class showed a very high willingness to participate in any trial. Furthermore, it can be assumed that decentralisation of trials may help to motivate participation among some elderly who are reluctant to participate, as a DCT with video contact was the preferred option in the class of trial averse elderly. Nevertheless, this would not increase the participation willingness drastically, and trial investigators should try and also address the other aforementioned barriers to trial participation among elderly people.

Another under-represented group in clinical trials for many disease areas are people of diverse ethnic origins and race.1114 57 80,82 However, it was not possible to investigate the trial preferences of people with different ethnicity in our study, because only 13 participants were of ethnic background other than Dutch, which is a limitation of this study. While some studies in the literature report that racial and ethnic minorities are equally willing to participate in trials as non-Hispanic whites,83 other studies found a lower willingness for trial participation among ethnic minority groups.84 85 The decentralisation of clinical trials widens trial access and may increase diversity of trial participants.7986,89 Barriers such as travel and time constraints, which are also cited as obstacles for ethnic minorities1790,92 can be overcome with DCTs.21,23 Nevertheless, ethnic minorities may also face other barriers for trial participation, such as difficulties in trusting researchers, lack of information and language issues,1790,92 which need to be paid attention to as well.

A lack of contrast in some other characteristics in our study population made it not possible to investigate their influence on trial preferences. This applied to characteristics such as support needs in daily life activities, previous trial participation, internet use and health literacy. More contrast in those characteristics might have enabled a better understanding of which participants were more likely to belong to which class. Consequently, this might have allowed to describe class 1, which was not possible in this study so that no further conclusions could be drawn for this class, which was the smallest of all classes. It should be noted that a larger sample size might also have provided greater contrast in some variables, allowing the four classes to be described in more detail. Furthermore, external validation of the model was not performed due to a lack of other comparable datasets.

In terms of the generalisability of our findings, preferences might differ in other disease areas and types of clinical trials not focused on drug interventions, and need further investigations. The same holds true for people residing in other countries, as country specific disparities, such as differences in digital access and use,93 driving distance to a hospital,94 population density95 or clinical trial activities 96could potentially impact trial preferences. However, with regard to the representativeness of our study sample, compared to the general Dutch diabetes population, it was representative in terms of median age, but fewer women were included.97 Furthermore, our study sample was representative of the general population in terms of internet access and use.93 In addition, it is also to be noted that preference findings may not always be translatable to actual trial participation behaviour, and that the enrolment decision is influenced by many factors.

Conclusions

The results of this study showed that overall individuals with T2DM were more willing to take part in decentralised trial approaches as opposed to a conventional trial on site, but heterogeneity in their preferences for trial designs was found. Specifically, younger women were more likely to favour participation in any DCT, and among two classes of elderly, one class of elderly males was more likely to take part in any trial, while another class of elderly was more likely to be averse to trial participation. The latter class had the highest uptake for a decentralised video trial, but still wasn’t very likely to join. These findings suggest that although decentralisation was preferred overall, there is most likely no one-size-fits-all solution and some people are not prone to participate regardless of the trial design. However, the differences in trial participation preferences identified by gender and age are important, since women and elderly are often still under-represented in clinical trials. We, therefore, encourage to tailor future trials to the preferences of the intended trial population, which may result in fewer barriers and might positively impact the willingness to participate for some people, which in turn could improve diversity in trials and lead to more inclusive and equitable trials.
