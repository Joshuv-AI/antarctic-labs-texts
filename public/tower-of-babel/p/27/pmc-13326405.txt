Chromosome-level reference genome of a foundational California native legume, Acmispon strigosus
Authors: Lorena Torres Martínez, Merly Escalona, Erin Toffelmier, Courtney Miller, Ruta Sahasrahbudhe, Mohan P A Marimuthu, Oanh Nguyen, Noravit Chumchim, Eric Beraut, Samuel Sacco, William Seligmann, Colin W Fairbairn, Robert D Cooper, H Bradley Shaffer, Jessica Purcell, Joel L Sachs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Acmispon is a legume genus that has diversified within the California Floristic Province. Acmispon species live in a variety of habitats, including coastal sage scrub, deserts, grasslands, and woodlands, and form symbiotic associations with nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Here, we report the first chromosome-level assembly of Acmispon strigosus (strigose bird’s-foot trefoil or strigose lotus) as part of the California Conservation Genomics Project. Consistent with the reference genome pipelines of the California Conservation Genomics Project, we used Pacific Biosciences HiFi long reads and Hi-C chromatin-proximity sequencing technology to produce a de novo assembled genome. The assembly is 519 Mb in length, with a contig N50 of 22.97 Mb, scaffolded into seven pseudochromosomes. Using the NCBI EGAPx pipeline, we annotated a total of 21,347 genes resulting in a protein Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) completeness score of 91.5%. This is the first genome assembled for Acmispon and among the first genomic resources available for a native California legume. The assembly BUSCO completeness score of 94.8% makes it one of the most complete genomes for the tribe Loteae (Fabaceae). Generating whole-genome sequences will contribute to our general understanding of nitrogen-fixing legume’s adaptations to diverse soil and environmental conditions, interactions with nitrogen fixing Bradyrhizobium and Mesorhizobium symbionts, and the degrading effects of pollution-induced nitrogen deposition on the legume−rhizobium symbiosis in California. These data will also help to reconstruct phylogenetic relationships among Acmispon spp., which remain unresolved.

Introduction

Native legumes are indispensable elements of healthy ecosystems due to their ability to associate with rhizobia or nitrogen-fixing bacteria. In these common ecological associations, rhizobia induce the formation of root nodules, within which the bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen for the plant. In exchange, the legume provides photosynthates, which are the primary source of energy for rhizobia (Masson-Boivin and Sachs 2018; Sachs et al. 2018). After the plant sets fruit, the nodules senesce and break down, releasing the growth-promoting bacteria back into the soil where they can establish new associations (Sachs et al. 2009; Poole et al. 2018). Although the benefits of rhizobia for overall community function are well established, their role in native legume conservation has received relatively little attention.

The southwestern United States is experiencing longer and more severe droughts, and our understanding of the long-term effects on plant communities is generally limited (Copeland et al. 2016). Increased drought frequency, in combination with environmental pollution caused by fossil fuel combustion and agricultural practices, has increased atmospheric nitrogen deposition in California, leading to soils enriched with reactive nitrogen compounds such as NH3 and particulate ammonium (NH4+) (Bytnerowicz and Fenn 1996; Bytnerowicz et al. 2001). An abundance of chemically available nitrogen compounds for plants in natural soils that were historically nutrient-poor can disrupt the legume−rhizobia symbiosis (Regus et al. 2017). Genomic data of both the host and rhizobia are critical to our further understanding of how legumes adapt to these natural and anthropogenic pressures.

California has hundreds of native legume species, but to date, they lack any published genomic resources. The native legume Acmispon strigosus (Nutt.), Brouillet (strigose bird’s-foot trefoil or strigose lotus), is a foundational California native plant, distributed in grassland, sage-scrub, and desert landscapes varying from protected wilderness to urban edges (Wendlandt et al. 2019; Torres-Martínez et al. 2021; Jepson Flora Project 2025). A. strigosus plays a key role in changing soil microbial composition by selectively rewarding beneficial nitrogen-fixing microbes (Regus et al. 2015; Hollowell et al. 2016), and promoting the colonization of other native flora, especially in disturbed habitats. A. strigosus habitats range widely with respect to exposure to anthropogenic nitrogen deposition, one of the least understood and potentially most insidious factors affecting wild plant communities in California (Regus et al. 2017).

The ubiquity and ecological significance of A. strigosus make it an excellent model species to study the genomics of adaptation and symbiosis across California. A. strigosus has a short annual life cycle of approximately 3–4 months (Jepson Flora Project 2025), can reproduce both by outcrossing and selfing, and it is widely distributed across multiple ecoregions in California. Based on the genome size and chromosome counts of its closest European relative and model system, Lotus japonicus (bird’s-foot trefoil), we predict that the A. strigosus genome is between 470 and 480 Mb in length. L. japonicus has a chromosome number of 2n = 12 (Ito et al. 2000) and a genome size of 472 Mb (Sato et al. 2008), whereas A. strigosus has a diploid number of 2n = 14 (Grant 1995), which is the hypothesized ancestral karyotype of legumes (Fabaceae, 1n = 7; Lee et al. 2024). The retention of the ancestral chromosome number also makes A. strigosus an important model for studies of chromosome number and evolution within Loteae and may enable future work on the speciation and systematics of the group.

To date, strigose lotus is one of the most widely studied legumes within the Loteae tribe native to the American continent, mainly in terms of its characteristic association with rhizobia of the genus Bradyrhizobium (Sachs et al. 2009; Hollowell et al. 2016; Wendlandt et al. 2019). However, further ecological and evolutionary analyses are needed for the conservation of the species. The California Conservation Genomics Project (CCGP) aims to create multispecies landscape genomic analyses across California to detect hotspots of genetic diversity at the regional level (Fiedler et al. 2022; Shaffer et al. 2022; Toffelmier et al. 2022). As part of the CCGP, we assembled a chromosome-level genome of A. strigosus. This genome assembly will provide a foundational resource for future studies of the conservation and evolutionary dynamics of native California legume species. These data will allow us to examine molecular mechanisms of local adaptation, the evolutionary dynamics of A. strigosus, and its relationship with diverse rhizobia symbionts in the face of climate change and increased nitrogen deposition.

Methods

Biological materials

Mature pods of line AcS079 were collected in the spring of 2013 in a canyon near the Whitewater River (33.9797167; −116.6570000) between the San Bernardino and San Jacinto Mountains (Fig. 1A−D). We chose this collection site because it is near the center of the species range and within the most common microhabitat for the species. Inbred lines were created in the Sachs Laboratory at the University of California Riverside, under greenhouse conditions. To obtain a high-quality reference genome, we used a third-generation inbred line. Homozygosity and genetic uniformity can help produce a more complete assembly, especially in plants where repetitive sequences and structural variants are abundant (Kong et al. 2023). A third-generation inbred line (u01.g3.r02.01) was grown over the fall of 2020 under axenic conditions (Fig. 1F and G). Seeds were grown on an equal mix of coarse and fine calcined clay (ProLeague, Quickdry; Turface Athletics, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). To obtain optimal growth, young seedlings were inoculated with an equal combination of three Bradyrhizobium strains collected from across California.

High-molecular-weight genomic DNA isolation

High-molecular-weight (HMW) genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from 800 mg of leaf tissue following the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method (Inglis et al. 2018) with minor modifications as described by Anghel et al. (2022). The DNA purity (260/280 = 1.83 and 260/230 = 2.31) was assessed by absorbance ratios on a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer. The DNA yield (6 μg) was quantified using the QuantiFluor ONE dsDNA dye assay (Promega, Madison, WI). Forty-nine percent of the total fragments were 40 kb or longer (Femto Pulse system, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

HiFi library preparation and sequencing

A HiFi SMRTbell library was constructed using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit v2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA; Cat. #100-938-900) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HMW gDNA was sheared to a target DNA size distribution between 15 and 18 kb using a Diagenode’s Megaruptor 3 system (Diagenode, Belgium; cat. B06010003). The sheared gDNA was concentrated using 0.45X of AMPure PB beads (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA; Cat. #100-265-900) for the removal of single-strand overhangs at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by further enzymatic steps of DNA damage repair at 37 °C for 30 min, end repair and A-tailing at 20 °C for 10 min and 65 °C for 30 min, and ligation of overhang adapters v3 at 20 °C for 60 min. The SMRTbell library was purified and concentrated with 1X AMPure PB beads for nuclease treatment at 37 °C for 30 min and size selection using the PippinHT system (Sage Science, Beverly, MA; Cat #HPE7510) to collect fragments greater than 7–9 kb. The 15–20 kb average HiFi SMRTbell library was sequenced at the UC Davis DNA Technologies Core (Davis, CA) using one 8 M SMRT cell, Sequel IIe sequencing chemistry 2.0, and 30-h movies on a PacBio Sequel II sequencer.

Omni-C library preparation and sequencing

The Omni-C library was prepared using the Dovetail Omni-C Kit (Dovetail Genomics, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with slight modifications. Leaf tissue was thoroughly ground with a mortar and pestle, while cooled with liquid nitrogen. Nuclear isolation was then performed using published methods (Workman et al. 2018), chromatin was fixed in place in the nucleus and digested under various conditions of DNase I until a suitable fragment length distribution of DNA molecules was obtained, and chromatin ends were repaired and ligated to a biotinylated bridge adapter, followed by proximity ligation of adapter-containing ends. Following ligation, crosslinks were reversed, the DNA was purified from proteins, and biotin that was not internal to ligated fragments was removed. An NGS library was generated using an NEB Ultra II DNA Library Prep kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA) with an Illumina compatible y-adaptor, and biotin-containing fragments were captured using streptavidin beads. The postcapture product was split into two replicates prior to PCR enrichment to preserve library complexity with each replicate receiving unique dual indices, and the library was sequenced at the Vincent J. Coates Genomics Sequencing Lab (Berkeley, CA) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA) to generate approximately 100 million 2 × 150 bp read pairs per GB genome size.

Transcriptome library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from three different plant tissues (leaves, roots, and flowers) using a Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, The Netherlands), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA libraries were then prepared using the KAPA mRNA HyperPrep Kit (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced with 100 bp reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to generate approximately 50 M reads per library.

Nuclear genome assembly

The genome assembly followed the CCGP assembly pipeline version 5.0, as outlined in Table 1, where we list the tools and nondefault parameters used in the assembly process. We removed the remnant adapter sequences from the PacBio HiFi dataset using HiFiAdapterFilt (Sim et al. 2022) and generated an initial diploid phased assembly using HiFiasm (Cheng et al. 2022) in HiC mode with the filtered PacBio HiFi reads and the Omni-C short-reads to generate two assemblies, one per haplotype. We then aligned the Omni-C data to both assemblies following the Arima Genomics Mapping Pipeline (https://github.com/ArimaGenomics/mapping_pipeline) and scaffolded both assemblies with SALSA (Ghurye et al. 2017; Ghurye et al. 2019).

The assemblies for both haplotypes were manually curated by iteratively generating and analyzing their corresponding Omni-C contact maps. To generate the contact maps, we aligned the Omni-C data with BWA-MEM (Li 2013), identified ligation junctions, and generated Omni-C pairs (Lee et al. 2022) using pairtools (Open2C et al. 2024). Then, we generated multiresolution Omni-C matrices with cooler (Abdennur and Mirny 2019) and balanced them with hicExplorer (Ramírez et al. 2018). We used HiGlass (Kerpedjiev et al. 2018) and the PretextSuite (https://github.com/wtsi-hpag/PretextView; https://github.com/wtsi-hpag/PretextMap; https://github.com/wtsi-hpag/PretextSnapshot) to visualize the contact maps. We identified misassemblies and misjoins in these contact maps and modified the assemblies using the Rapid Curation pipeline from the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute, Genome Reference Informatics Team (https://gitlab.com/wtsi-grit/rapid-curation). Some of the remaining gaps (joins generated during scaffolding and/or curation) were closed using the PacBio HiFi reads and YAGCloser (https://github.com/merlyescalona/yagcloser). We checked for contamination using the BlobToolKit Framework (Challis et al. 2020).

Genome annotation

We annotated the reference assembly using the NCBI Eukaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline v0.4.1-alpha (hereafter, “EGAPx”) which is published in the NCBI RefSeq Database (O'Leary et al. 2015) and accessible through the NCBI github page (“ncbi/egapx”). Annotation features were identified by aligning transcripts and proteins from related taxa in the RefSeq database using BLAST (Camacho et al. 2009). Novel, species-specific RNA-Seq reads generated from three tissue types were also aligned to the assembly using the alignment software STAR (Dobin et al. 2012). Additional features were predicted using HMM-based gene models using the NCBI Gnomon software. We evaluated the quality and completeness of our annotation by comparing the longest protein for each annotated coding gene with fabales (odb10) using BUSCO v5.7.1 (Manni et al. 2021). We report the number of annotation features and the BUSCO results in Table 2.

Mitochondrial genome assembly

We assembled the mitochondrial genome of A. strigosus from the PacBio HiFi reads using the reference-guided pipeline MitoHiFi (Allio et al. 2020; Uliano-Silva et al. 2023). A mitochondrial sequence of Lotus japonicus (NCBI:NC_016743.2; Kazakoff et al. 2012) was used as the starting sequence. After completion of the nuclear genome, we searched for matches of the resulting mitochondrial assembly sequence in the nuclear genome assembly using BLAST+ (Camacho et al. 2009) and filtered out contigs and scaffolds from the nuclear genome with a percentage of sequence identity >99% and a size smaller than the mitochondrial assembly sequence.

Chloroplast Genome Assembly and Annotation

The chloroplast sequence for A. strigosus was generated with Oatk (Zhou et al. 2025). We used the chloroplast genome assembly from Acmispon glaber (NCBI:PQ539367.1; Samigullin et al. 2025) as a guide for validation, aligned the generated sequence against the guide using lastz (Harris 2007), and visualized the alignment using (Wilson et al. 2001). The strand direction and start position of the resulting assembly were verified with ChloroAnno (https://github.com/Hua-CM/ChloroAnno), annotated using the Unix version of CPGAVAS (Liu et al. 2012), and visualized using the online version of OGDRAW (Greiner et al. 2019).

Genome size estimation and quality assessment

We generated k-mer counts from the PacBio HiFi reads using meryl (https://github.com/marbl/meryl). The k-mer counts were then used in GenomeScope2.0 (Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020) to estimate genome features, including genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content. To obtain general contiguity metrics, we ran QUAST (Gurevich et al. 2013). To evaluate genome quality and functional completeness we used BUSCO (Manni et al. 2021) with the Embryophyta ortholog database (Embryophyta_odb10), which contains 1,614 genes. Assessment of base-level accuracy (QV) and k-mer completeness was performed using the previously generated Meryl database and Merqury (Rhie et al. 2021). We further estimated genome assembly accuracy via BUSCO gene set frameshift analysis using the pipeline described in Korlach et al. (2017). Measurements of the size of the phased blocks were based on the size of the contigs generated by HiFiasm on HiC mode. We follow the quality metric nomenclature established by Rhie et al. (2021), with the genome quality code x.y.P.Q.C, where x = log10[contig NG50]; y = log10[scaffold NG50]; P = log10 [phased block NG50]; Q = Phred base accuracy QV (quality value); and C = % genome represented by the first “n” scaffolds, following a karyotype of 2n = 14 for this species (n = 7), estimated as a mode from the ancestral species number of chromosomes (Genome on a Tree—GoaT; tax_name: A. strigosus); Challis et al. (2020). Quality metrics for the annotation were calculated on the assembly for haplotype one.

Results

Genome size estimation and quality assessment

The Omni-C library generated 70.96 million read pairs and the PacBio HiFi library generated 1.89 million reads. The PacBio HiFi sequences yielded ~56X genome coverage and had an N50 read length of 13,126 bp; a minimum read length of 84 bp; a mean read length of 13,158 bp; and a maximum read length of 54,045 bp (see Supplementary Fig. S1 for read length distribution). Based on the PacBio HiFi data, Genomescope 2.0 estimated a genome size of 548.06 Mb, a 0.125% sequencing error rate, and 0.147% heterozygosity. The k-mer spectrum shows a bimodal distribution with a major coverage peak at ~40-fold coverage and a minor peak at ~22-fold coverage (Fig. 2A).

Nuclear genome assembly

The final genome assembly (drAcmStri1) consists of two phased haplotypes. Both assemblies are similar in size but not equal to the estimated genome size from GenomeScope2.0, as has been observed in other taxa (see Pflug et al. 2020, for example).

The haplotype one assembly (drAcmStri1.0.hap1) consists of 1,931 scaffolds spanning 519 Mb with a contig N50 of 22.97 Mb, a scaffold N50 of 67.38 Mb, a largest contig size of 64.22 Mb, and a largest scaffold size of 100.03 Mb (Table 2; Fig. 2B). The haplotype two assembly (drAcmStri1.0.hap2) consists of 599 scaffolds spanning 510.34 Gb with a contig N50 of 36.42 Mb, a scaffold N50 of 76.37 Mb, the largest contig size of 65.12 Mb and the largest scaffold size of 101.4 Mb (Table 2, Fig. 2C).

The assembly included most of the A. strigosus gene space. The haplotype one assembly has a BUSCO completeness score for the Embryophyta gene set of 94.8% (91.30% single-copy, 3.50% duplicates), a base pair QV of 58.74, a kmer completeness of 92.22%, and a frameshift indel QV of 50.93 (Table 2). The haplotype two assembly has a BUSCO completeness score for the Embryophyta gene set of 99.6% (96.00% single-copy; 3.60% duplicates), a base pair QV of 64.23, a kmer completeness of 96.63%, and a frameshift indel QV of 51.13 (Table 2).

During manual curation, we made a total of 42 joins (22 on haplotype one and 20 on haplotype two); 10 breaks (6 on haplotype one and 4 on haplotype two) based on the Omni-C contact map signal, and we were able to close a total of 15 gaps (8 on haplotype one and 7 on haplotype two; see Supplementary Fig. S2). No further contigs were modified or removed. The Omni-C contact maps show highly contiguous assemblies with chromosome-length scaffolds, suggesting that the A. strigosus genome is organized into seven chromosomes based on the number of major bins along the diagonal axis of the plot (Fig. 2D and E; see Supplementary Table S1). Assembly statistics are reported in Table 2 and represented graphically in Fig. 2. We have deposited the genome assembly in NCBI GenBank (see Table 2 and section Data availability for details).

Genome annotation

Sequencing of mRNA libraries for SAMN41406329, SAMN41406330, and SAMN41406328 yielded 44 M, 54 M, and 46 M paired end reads, respectively. Our final genome annotation for A. strigosus included 21,347 genes, with a fabales BUSCO completeness of 91.5%. A list of annotation statistics and BUSCO score breakdowns is reported in Table 2.

Mitochondria and chloroplast genomes

The final mitochondrial sequence of A. strigosus is 212,676 bp long, with a base composition of A = 27.46%, C = 22.56%, G = 22.56%, T = 27.41%. It consists of 19 unique transfer RNAs, and 13 protein-coding genes.

The final chloroplast assembly of A. strigosus is 151,249 bp long, with a nucleotide content of A = 32%, C = 17.86%, G = 18.02%, T = 32.12% (Supplementary Fig. S4). The large-single-copy region is 84,360 bp long, the small-single-copy region is 18,172 bp long, and the inverted repeat (IR) is 24,357 bp long (Supplementary Fig S3). This plastome contains a total of 232 genes including rRNAs and tRNAs (not counting one of the IR) (see Supplementary Figs S3 and S4).

Discussion

We report the first reference and chromosome-level genome of a native California legume, A. strigosus. We were able to fully assemble the seven reported chromosomes of the species (Grant 1965, 1995), supporting the accuracy of our assembly. The karyotype number of A. strigosus represents the ancestral number for Fabaceae (Lee et al. 2024; see Supplementary Table S2), making this genome an important resource to understand chromosome evolution within Fabaceae. Acmispon belongs to the Loteae tribe within the robinioid clade for which only the genome of Lotus japonicus has been sequenced (Bruneau et al. 2013). In comparison with the earliest available L. japonicus genome (~472 Mb; Sato et al. 2008), the genome of A. strigosus reported here is 1.16 times bigger and has higher gene coverage based on BUSCO scores (94.8% vs 91.3% in L. japonicus). However, L. japonicus ecological variants can have genome sizes of up to 512 Mb (Kawasaki and Murakami 2000), and a more recent, higher-quality chromosome-level assembly is now available for L. japonicus (Li et al. 2020). The number of highly conserved, single-copy genes in A. strigosus was slightly lower than that reported for L. japonicus (1,244 vs 1,473). The BUSCO completeness, which reflects the assembly completeness was 94.80% and 99.60% in our two A. strigosus haplotypes, whereas the recent assembly for L. japonicus was 92.7%, and, the contig N50 of the L. japonicus genome was almost an order of magnitude smaller (2.81 Mb; Li et al. 2020) than that for A. strigosus (22.97 Mb). These findings support the effective CCGP methodological approaches of using a combination of PacBio HiFi and Omni-C Illumina reads to assemble reference genomes in California native plant species; other plants studied by the CCGP have reported similarly robust patterns of genome completeness scores (Adams et al. 2023; Chaturvedi et al. 2024).

The A. strigosus genome reported here is among the most complete and high-quality genomes available for Fabaceae (see Supplementary Table S3). The haplotype 2 assembly of A. strigosus had a BUSCO completeness score of 99%, surpassing the highest BUSCO score of 98.8% reported for the Adzuki bean, a legume with a similar genome size as A. strigosus (Chu et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). The GC content reported in Fabaceae genomes ranges between 36.6% and 37.9% (see Supplementary Table S3), and the A. strigosus GC content falls at the lowest end of this range (36.6%). However, the BUSCO completeness score reported here for the A. strigosus genome annotation was somewhat lower than that of other legume species, but it is similar to the annotation BUSCO score reported for L. japonicus (Supplementary Table S3), with only 1.3% fewer (~6,644 genes) protein-coding genes. Our annotation completeness score was higher than those recently reported for other CCGP project plant species (e.g. 89.88% in Azolla caroliniana, Song et al. 2025), confirming the effectiveness of our pipeline.

The chloroplast genome of A. strigosus was between 430 and 957 bp smaller than the chloroplast genomes of Acmispon americanus, Acmispon parviflorus and A. glaber that were recently assembled by (Samigullin et al. 2025) using Illumina short-read data. Overall, the chloroplast genome of A. strigosus had similar features and size of those reported for other members of Loteae (Samigullin et al. 2025).

The high-quality nuclear, mitochondrial, and chloroplast genomes reported here for A. strigosus will provide an important genomic resource for resolving the phylogenetic relationships among members of Loteae, which have been highly debated, and they will be an important resource enabling comparative genomic analysis with other Fabaceae species to study the evolution and genetic basis of symbiosis and host specificity. These genomic resources will also contribute to the ongoing efforts by our and other research groups to disentangle the molecular mechanisms of native legumes and their symbionts to respond to climate change and anthropogenically enhanced nitrogen deposition. In addition, as the only legume species included within the CCGP, these genomic resources will contribute to the planned comparative landscape genomic studies across California to identify areas that harbors the greatest multispecies genetic variation at a regional level (Shaffer et al. 2022; Toffelmier et al. 2022).
