Introduction

The topic of drones has grown significantly in recent times, both in the military and civilian sectors, especially in the civilian sector, which represents community service. Its most essential applications include agriculture, firefighting, goods delivery, search and rescue, and reconnaissance. It also protects gas and oil pipelines. These applications are of great importance in human life, particularly in securing food, ensuring survival, and accessing energy resources, which are key requirements and goals of sustainable development1.

Drones typically fall into three categories: rotary-wing, flapping-wing, and fixed-wing aircraft. Each type has its own application and importance. Rotary-wing aircraft are used for tasks requiring short-range, small-area operations, such as firefighting, photography, and geospatial surveying. Flapping-wing aircraft are used for reconnaissance and photography because they are quiet, fast, and maneuverable. Fixed-wing aircraft offer high stability, enabling long-range operations such as reconnaissance, search and rescue, and pipeline monitoring2. From the foregoing, the importance of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), particularly fixed-wing drones, is evident. Therefore, it is essential to design a wing that aligns with the assigned tasks to achieve optimal performance and fulfill their objectives at the lowest cost and with the highest efficiency. Thus, drone wings must be explicitly designed for drone applications, following a methodology that considers the drone’s intended use3.

Lee et al.4 used numerical timing analysis to reduce the drag load of long-range electric drones. X-Foil was used to analyze the drone’s aerodynamics and its wing using the Reynolds number, and Gambit was used to generate the geometry and create a mesh for the drone and its wing. Three different aspect ratios (12.5, 15.6, and 20.0) were used on the drone’s wing. The results showed that the total drag force was reduced by 18% with a high aspect ratio of 20 compared with a low aspect ratio of 12.5. The use of aspect ratios also reduced the drag force by 3.4%. Reza et al.5 used X-Foil 5 to analyze the aerodynamics of a drone’s wing and fuselage at high lift and low speeds. The Eppler, Selig, and Wortman wing types were analyzed in this software using the RQ-7 Shadow drone. The results showed that at low speeds and high lift, the performance analysis and comparison under these conditions are encouraging and essential for the future of drones or drone engineering. Alsahlan and Rahulan6 conducted an analytical study of several previously used wing types, comparing their results for thickness, angle of attack, lift-to-drag ratio, and angle of attack. They then proposed a new wing design for the aircraft, which was developed using the Requirements software and aerodynamically analyzed with Xfoil. The Bezier Curve was used to specify the wing section’s curvature and thickness. The newly designed wing was designated ZMR, with a number indicating the wing thickness ratio. The results showed that the new ZMR19 wing exhibits the highest lift-to-drag ratio at an angle of attack of 6 degrees, whereas the ZMR26 wing exhibits behavior similar to that of the ZMR16 wing. Premkartikkumar et al.7 analyzed the performance of a range of conventional drone wings using X-Filler 5 and ANSYS software. They used sections of the Selig 1223, Eppler 423, CH10, and Wortmann FX wings. The results showed that the Selig 1223 wing exhibited the highest lift-to-drag ratio. Based on these results, the Selig 1223 wing served as a model for the design and modification of a new wing. Modifications were made to this wing, including changes to its thickness and length, resulting in three new wing types: SMOD1, SMOD2, and SMOD3. These modifications and wing designs were then implemented using X-Filler 5 and analyzed using ANSYS aerodynamics. The results for the new wings showed that the second new wing, SMOD2, achieved the best lift force and lift coefficient, and that the lift-to-drag ratio was also the highest for this wing. Tang et al.8 compared two types of drone wings: a conventional wing and a modified wing with a wavy or undulating surface. The wing was analyzed using ANSYS Fluent in both 2D and 3D. The study employed a Reynolds number of 4000 and angles of attack ranging from -0 to 6 degrees, with both wings set to the same angle. The results showed an increase in lift for the wing with a wavy (undulating) surface due to negative pressure in the wave region. The results also indicated an increase in drag due to the vortices generated by the waveform or concavity on the wing surface. Aabid et al.9 analyzed wingtip CH10 using the ANSYS Fluent and compared it to wingtip NACA 2412 using numerical analysis. Star angles from 0 to 14 were applied, and a Reynolds number of 500,000 was used for a wingtip length of 1 meter. The results showed that the Transient SST model yielded improved predictions of wingtip behavior at lower Reynolds numbers. Durmuş10 studied four types of drone wing sections: the Eppler 339, the MH60, the TL54, and the TSIG12%. The Xfiler 5 software, based on XFoil, was used to model the drone sections and to simulate and analyze the wings using aerodynamics. The results showed that the lift-to-lift ratio was higher for the TL54 and MH60 wings at angles of 3 to 5 degrees, whereas the MH60 wing achieved the highest lift-to-lift ratio at 32.7 degrees, corresponding to the angle of attack.

Zhang11 presented an analysis of drone wings using aerodynamic variables for the NACA 2401, NACA 4412, and NACA 643 wings. These results show the advantages and disadvantages of the aforementioned wing types, helping the engineer select the appropriate wing type and reducing time, resources, effort, and cost.

Elshewey et al.12 used machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict drag coefficient (CD) and lift coefficient (CL) in aerodynamic modeling. The primary goal is to identify optimal models that can be used to predict CD and CL using error measures and coefficient of determination (R). The prediction of CD and CL was done using linear, Ridge, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Support Vector Regressor, and XGBoost. Experiments showed that XGBoost and Random Forest (RF) performed better than CD and CL predictions. XGBoost achieved a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.0002 and a R of 0.9916 for CD prediction, followed by RF by 0.0002 and 0.9915. This method increases the prediction of aerodynamic coefficients.

Khan et al.13 present a novel study of drone wing segment types: the NACA 2412, NACA 4415, and NACA 0012. CFD simulation was performed using the ANSYS Fluent 2022 R2 software, and wing performance analysis was conducted using the Xfoil software. Artificial intelligence (AI) was employed using a neural network, and the program was trained on CFD data. The AI analysis using a neural network revealed that the NACA 4415 wing achieved the highest lift-to-drag ratio at an attack angle of 11.19 and a Reynolds number of 770801. Furthermore, the results for the NACA 4415 wing showed a delayed onset of adjacent-layer detachment and improved response control. Using CFD data and artificial intelligence, the best wing for the reference aircraft was identified as the NACA 4415.

Elshewey et al.14 presented the proposal of a machine learning framework to make precise predictions of the aerodynamic lift-to-drag ratio (Cl/Cd) of multi-stepped airfoils in different flow conditions. Data were gathered by experimental wind-tunnel measurements of several step configurations and a stacked ensemble model using XGBoost, Support Vector Regression (SVR), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) with a Random Forest meta-learner was created to predict data. The test R and tenfold cross-validation R of the proposed model were found to be 0.9951 and 0.9872 0.0043, respectively, which was better than that of individual regressors. It is a data-driven, fast method to traditional CFD simulations and allows predicting aerodynamic performance reliably and efficiently airfoil optimization.

Previous studies indicate that most existing designs are for passenger transport aircraft, cargo planes, and other drone-specific applications. Therefore, a specific design approach or philosophy must be adopted to select the optimal drone wing based on its intended purpose or application, aiming for optimal performance at the lowest cost, in the shortest time, and with the highest precision. In this study, we will apply Began’s law to airfoil design for search-and-rescue UAVs, depending on the specific application at low Reynolds numbers (, and ). Began’s theory, or constructivist law, is considered one of the most important and modern methods in fluid dynamics design. This is because it deals with fluids through the philosophy of flow evolution15. This evolution depends on the fluid’s flow path within the system’s flow field to achieve an optimal trajectory and, consequently, optimal system performance.

Previous research in low Reynolds number using UAV airfoils is hampered by disjointed geometry analysis, single-methodology (either CFD or empirical tools) use, and lack of unified frameworks to connect geometry, flow physics and predictive modeling. Traditional optimization tools, including gradient-based or evolutionary, are usually computationally intensive, sensitive to local optimism and, to a large extent, uninterpretable in physical terms, as geometry is treated as a variable in mathematical terms and not a design entity driven by a flow. In order to fill these gaps, the current research proposes an integrated methodology that integrates constructal theory, CFD, and ANN where constructal theory offers a physics-based explanation of geometric evolution, CFD allows high-fidelity aerodynamic assessment, and ANN can be used to conduct predictive analysis in the design space efficiently. It has provided a unified, physics-aware and computationally efficient methodology that provides a bridge between aero physics knowledge and data-driven optimization, which is a new contribution to the current disjointed methodologies. Airfoil analysis for search and rescue UAVs

The latest research findings indicate that appropriate airfoil selection can increase flight endurance by up to 30 percent while ensuring the rigid stability criteria required for search-and-rescue applications. Proper choice of an airfoil is essential in designing efficient UAV wings. Optimization of aerodynamic parameters directly affects mission success, payload capacity, and efficiency16. The significant profiles of the multidimensional NACA have been selected for thorough analysis. Such profiles constitute unique design philosophies and performance attributes applicable to search-and-rescue UAV operations. Constructal Law is a physics law: Finite-size flow systems must evolve with time (to live) by such a way that they afford easier and easier access to their currents (to be aero-dynamic)17. The Constructal Law is applied using the Constructal Design Method (also referred to as Design with Constructal Theory).

Search and Rescue (SAR) missions are varied and primarily classified by terrain or the circumstances under which the search is conducted. The following are examples of SAR operations. Mountain rescue is a SAR operation that occurs primarily in rugged mountainous terrain. Ground search and rescue: This entails searching for lost or distressed persons on land or in inland waterways. It includes searches for individuals who have become lost or even abducted due to various causes, such as domestic violence, mental disorders, accidents, and more. Urban search and rescue (US & R or USAR) is commonly referred to as Heavy Urban Search and Rescue (HUSAR). It entails the search and rescue of individuals trapped in building rubble or in other urban and industrial hazards. It involves multidisciplinary teams because the work is highly complex and requires specialized expertise. Air-sea rescue (ASR): A joint SAR operation for survivors of emergency water landings and individuals who have survived the loss of their seagoing ship. It may entail seaplanes, helicopters, submarines, rescue boats, and ships. Military and civilian teams are involved in such rescue operations. Other technological advancements, such as the helicopter, have enabled hovering, transforming air-sea rescue. Combat search and rescue is conducted on the battlefield to rescue people in distress, typically in war-torn areas18.

Moreover, aviation laws impose restrictions on drones, including airspace, flight altitude, and operating conditions. For example, drones are not permitted to fly above the operator’s visual horizon or to operate in certain restricted airspace. However, drone-based search-and-rescue missions have inherent limitations. The main drawback of drones is their reliance on batteries with limited capacity, which can sustain flight for only a short period19. Although the functionality may be improved, a heavier payload may drain the power source more quickly, thereby reducing operational flight time. Additionally, unfavorable weather, e.g., high winds, heavy rain, or high temperatures, may adversely affect flight performance and stability. Such rules can ensure the safety and legality of drone operations, which can be particularly challenging in the context of SAR, especially when drones are required to operate in isolated or restricted areas.

Computational domain

The flow domain consists of a semicircle of radius 20 m and a rectangular domain with a length of 60 m. At the center of the semicircle, the airfoil. It is located. Boundaries were established, including a velocity inlet and a pressure outlet at the inlet and outlet, respectively, and the airfoil is treated as a no-slip wall. The upper and lower surfaces were taken as wall boundaries. The coarse model is typical, and the wall movement is assumed to be stationary. The wall is subjected to a no-slip boundary condition22. Based on Fig. 3, the outlet is chosen as the pressure outlet boundary condition. This is the state implemented at the point where the domain emits the system23. It was in accordance with the inlet velocity boundary condition, as specified in Fig. 3. To obtain a wide range of angles of attack, the fluid flow velocity components are reversed24. All perimeter measurements are presented in meters, as shown in Fig. 3. The computational domain boundaries were moved sufficiently away from the airfoils to avoid boundary effects in the simulation results.

Mesh generation

A domain is usually rectangular, with grid points assigned to every grid line, making them easier to identify. This space is referred to as an unstructured grid, or a mesh (2D). Defining edge sizing yielded a robust mesh around the airfoil of interest, as shown in Fig. 4. To provide a fair comparison, the meshing of all airfoils was performed using the same sizing functions and methods. It is necessary to intercept wakes at both the leading and trailing edges. Thus, to achieve high-quality resolution at the trailing edge, the mesh must be close to the tight cells in terms of mesh size. The field is referred to as an unstructured grid or a triangular mesh (2D). A fine or dense mesh is required to resolve the behavior of viscous shear layers within the boundary layer to obtain more accurate results 25. The wall is maintained below 1 to accurately resolve the flow around the airfoil, as required by the turbulence model used. Moreover, lastly, a grid with approximately 550000 mesh cells was generated. Nevertheless, a fine mesh everywhere in the domain leads to more grid points and ultimately to the CPU time. It is advisable to make the grid size close to the wall to introduce accuracy. It is known as clustering and is simpler to implement when a structured mesh is used26.

Solution convergence

A numerical method based on iterations to generate a solution of the grid is called solution convergence, in which the error approaches zero. Optimizations are performed iteratively until a predetermined convergence criterion is met27. The default convergence criterion in FLUENT is for the energy equation and for the rest of the equations. Once the predetermined convergence criterion is met, the iteration process is complete. This paper disabled convergence checking and assessed convergence by analyzing residual levels (Fig. 6). The pressure contours of the three-degree-of-freedom (m, p, t) airfoil geometries are illustrated in the following Figs. 18, 19 and 20. The low and high pressures were noted on the lower and upper surfaces, respectively, thereby contributing to the increase in lift. The profile with the greatest camber was the NACA 4412, which exhibited the most significant pressure difference, indicating it produced the greatest lift among the NACA 2412, NACA 4612, and NACA 4415 profiles. The low-pressure zones on the upper surface increased at all profiles at AoA = 4 at the leading edge. This led to another upsurge in the lift. The NACA 4412 profile exhibited the most significant pressure difference, indicating excellent lift performance at this AoA. The upper surface experiences higher pressure because, according to Bernoulli’s principle, the static pressure is highest at the AoA condition, where the velocity is lower on the upper surface.

The Streamline patterns and velocity profiles of the three degrees of freedom (m, p, t) airfoil geometries are provided in Figs. 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 and 26, which provide a holistic picture of the aerodynamic performance of the three degrees of freedom (m, p, t) airfoil geometries. The leading edge showed a considerable increase in velocity on the upper surface, and the streamlines became more compressed and denser. A steep velocity gradient at the top surface, along with the associated velocity field, indicated an increase in lift. However, even with some flow separation at the trailing edge, this shows the onset of aerodynamic inefficiency at higher angles of attack. The velocity profile was also used to support this, showing that the velocity gradient is steeper at the upper surface and, hence, greater lift. The NACA 4412 had the simplest streamlines and the highest velocity regions among the other two airfoils. A high-velocity gradient on the upper surface and slight flow separation to the rear were observed. A combination of all these factors made the NACA 4412 the most efficient in lift production, particularly. Starting with NACA 2412, a slightly cambered airfoil, the streamline patterns possessed apparent succession in the flow behaviour. It was also observed that at the trailing edge of the bottom surface, flow separation occurs and the streamlines are compressed, indicating higher velocity there. This was determined by the velocity profile, which gave relatively high velocities at the lower surface and quite low velocities at the upper surface.

Above the attack angles, the flow has progressive tendency towards separation especially on the suction side whereby the adverse pressure gradients are stronger. In the case of the investigated airfoils, the separation starts around the trailing edge and slowly proceeds upstream with the increase in the angle of attack. This is in agreement with the low-Reynolds-number aerodynamics, in which boundary layer is more prone to premature detachment.

The angle of the stall is found to be between about 10 -12, and the lift coefficient ceases to be nearly linear, and the drag becomes much greater. It is notable that the superior airfoil configuration exhibits a late formation of the separation and a more gradual passage to stall which shows a better pressure recovery and a more consistent behavior of the boundary layer in contrast with the baseline airfoil. These attributes imply that the optimized geometry will maximize aerodynamic performance in the working pre-stall regime, and will retain more controlled separation behavior at elevated angles of attack.

It is particularly applicable to search-and-rescue UAVs, which can operate at varying speeds and altitudes depending on mission requirements.

The paper develops a physics-based, hybrid methodology to the optimization of airfoil of low Reynolds number with constractal theory, CFD, and ANN-based surrogate modeling, where constractal is used to provide systematic geometric development, CFD is used to provide consistent aerodynamic analysis, and ANN is used to provide fast and reliable performance prediction throughout the design space. These findings substantiate the claim that this combined methodology allows quantifiable enhancements in aerodynamic efficiency in the pre-stall regime of application to SAR-UAV systems, as well as greatly decreasing the computational cost as compared to the traditional optimization techniques. The main contribution is that physical design reasoning is combined with data-driven modeling to provide a scalable, interpretable, and computationally efficient alternative to existing optimization methods of aerodynamic design.

Recommendation of future work

Given the importance of Constructal’s law in design and optimization, and after its application in this study to an airfoil section, the researchers propose applying this law to the complete wing design in three dimensions and to the angle of inclination of this wing in conventional point-blank aircraft. Furthermore, in the future, this law could be applied to the overall design of the aircraft, including the airframe and fuselage, through practical automation to achieve optimal aerodynamic performance.
