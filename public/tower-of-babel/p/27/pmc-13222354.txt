Copper-sensitive OsSPL9 TF regulates expression of indica rice domestication-associated miRNAs and phenotypes
Authors: Steffi Raju, Chenna Swetha, Chitthavalli Y. Harshith, Padubidri V. Shivaprasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Domestication of indica rice facilitated better harvest and yield, however the molecular mechanisms that drove multitude of the associated phenotypes is poorly understood. A few genetic and epigenetic mechanisms have been attributed to indica rice domestication; however, upstream regulators of these variations are unknown. Here, we identified a copper (Cu)-dependent regulatory module, involved in the regulation of OsSPL9 TF and two classes of RNAs under its control. Differential accumulation of Cu-associated micro(mi)RNAs and Cu-associated protein-coding RNAs were a major portion of the differences between wild (Oryza nivara) and cultivated rice lines. We identified OsSPL9 as an upstream regulator of these changes through genetic and molecular analysis as well as by using Cu stressed conditions. OsSPL9 bound to the promoters of these genes through a conserved GTAC enriched motif. Although OsSPL9 might have Cu-independent roles, Cu-associated miRNAs and their targets acted as a regulatory loop with OsSPL9. Mis-expression of SPL9 alone, or any individual Cu-associated miRNA, also altered levels of other Cu-miRNAs and their cognate targets. OsSPL9-mediated regulation was closely linked to Cu accumulation and metabolism, indicating previously unappreciated roles of metal ions in mediating domestication-associated phenotypes. Our study facilitates a better understanding of the crosstalk between genetic and epigenetic regulation that contributed to indica rice domestication.

Introduction

Domestication is a phenomenon of the evolution of crops assisted by cultivation, and it has induced diverse changes in plants, often referred to as domestication-associated traits1. Although genomic loci attributed to these phenotypes have been characterised to a greater extent from several studies in diverse crops, the mechanisms underlying this process have not been well understood. This difficulty was largely attributed to the fact that not all phenotypic changes in the course of domestication could be attributed to protein-coding genes2. Specifically, a majority of the QTLs attributed to distinct crop phenotypes did not overlap with specific protein-coding genes, and this led to the hypothesis that small (s)RNAs or other epigenetic regulators might be playing a dominant role in establishing domestication-associated phenotypes3–7. sRNAs as major regulators of gene expression and phenotypes in plants were well elucidated, along with their role in inducing heritable epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation and histone modifications8–11. Indeed, several landmark studies have revealed that there is a major influence of sRNAs, including miRNAs, in regulating gene expression changes resulting in crop domestication phenotypes. In agreement with this, a significant number of yield-related QTLs across crops overlapped with sRNAs, their targets or players involved in epigenetic modifications12–15.

The cultivated rice is thought to have originated from two wild relatives of cultivated rice, namely, Oryza nivara and O. rufipogon16. Among these, indica rice is of generally shorter stature with morphological and physiological features distinct from those of japonica and javanica rice lines. Several studies identified genetic and epigenetic regulators of indica rice domestication. Well-known phenotypes and associated genes identified in indica rice include an increase in grain shape17, grain colour, heading date18, loss of shattering19, cold tolerance20 and thermo-tolerance21. Several sequence variations, including SNPs in coding as well as regulatory regions, transposon insertions and gene duplications appear to have contributed to indica rice domestication3,22,23. Among the epigenetic factors, sRNAs and methylation have been documented to influence rice domestication phenotypes.

A major domestication-associated phenotype determinant in indica rice is through miRNA-mediated laccase silencing12. The miR397 is a Cu- dependent miRNA24,25, and it can regulate the expression of Cu-binding proteins such as laccases post transcriptionally26,27. More than 25 QTLs implicated in rice domestication overlapped with miR397 or laccases, indicating their importance in the domestication process12. Several reports further indicated that miR397 levels can also reinforce phenotypes that are associated with yield, such as grain weight28, seed number26, grain yield29, seed size30 and circadian rhythm31. The role of miR397 to regulate these diverse phenotypes associated with yield was also conserved across other food crops, such as wheat32. This indicates the potential role of miRNAs as emerging QTLs across food crops in regulating phenotypes associated with domestication.

The class of Cu-dependent miRNAs includes miRNAs such as miR397, miR528, miR408 and miR39833–36. However, the role of miRNAs other than miR397 in the context of domestication is not understood. miR528 is a conserved monocot-specific miRNA36,37, which is known to regulate flowering time in rice38, tillering phenotypes in switchgrass39 and broad-spectrum antiviral resistance in rice40. miR408 has the potential to influence grain yield and photosynthesis in rice41, and might contribute to anthocyanin biosynthesis42. The role of miR398 was majorly studied in the context of abiotic stress responses in rice43,44. How exactly Cu or these Cu-dependent miRNAs play a role in influencing these phenotypes is unknown. This mode of regulation through miRNAs are not well-known for other nutrients such as zinc, iron, manganese etc. Since copper can form highly co-ordinated complexes with other proteins might have necessitated a tight regulation through miRNAs33. Interestingly, in plants grown in high iron-containing soil, the OsSPL9 knockout plants activated Cu-responsive genes rescuing the defects in vegetative tissues, indicating a complex interaction of the OsSPL9 module in regulating multiple nutrient deficiencies45.

Among the genes that regulate domestication-associated phenotypes are TFs or their cofactors2,3,46–49. Interestingly, some or most of these Cu-dependent miRNAs are under the regulation of a Squamosa promoter-like TF, named SPL7 in Arabidopsis50,51, and at least one miRNA is under the control of OsSPL9 in rice40,52–55. Among Brassicales, SPL7 ensures Cu acquisition and localised deposition in seeds to enable its dispersal56 and also mediates the crosstalk between sucrose signalling, growth and copper homoeostasis as documented in Arabidopsis57,58. Systemic signalling of copper occurs through AtSPL7, especially near the vasculature, enabling root-to-shoot communication and long-distance signalling. Several speculative models regarding AtSPL7 activity indicated the binding properties of the SBP domain to both zinc and copper, in Cu-deficient and Cu-sufficient conditions, respectively59. Rice OsSPL9 is known to regulate antiviral response through miR528-mediated post-transcriptional gene silencing of ascorbate oxidase, rendering immunity40, and also as a direct/indirect determinant of grain size and yield through unknown mechanisms53. In addition, studies have shown Cu-dependent leaf phenotypes in OsSPL9 knockout plants and the role of OsSPL9 in regulating Cu-dependent genes.55

The process of domestication in food crops led to major shifts in the nutritional requirements due to altered growth habitats and conditions60. It is well-established that dependency on metalloproteins acting as enzymes and other catalysts likely ensured better survival and fitness in life forms, including crops61–63. To ensure the copper demand is met in individual tissues or organs, copper transporters appear to get activated upon Cu-deficiency to ensure appropriate Cu redistribution specifically in organelles63. Allowing a permissible range of nutrient accumulation seems to be of utmost importance to balance out excessive metals to avoid mis-incorporation and toxicity within cells. Nutrient balancing during domestication was also essential to mediate phenotypic plasticity in plants over the course of selection, eventually leading to altered phenotypes64. How metal ions such as Cu and other micronutrients and their upstream regulators might have emerged in a context where such nutrient dependency has evolved is not understood.

Here, we show that Cu-responsive miRNAs and their targets are a substantial portion of the changes between cultivated and wild rice lines. Through genetic and molecular approaches, we show that OsSPL9 is the upstream regulator of these miRNAs and other differentially expressing genes between wild and cultivated lines. Using ChIP-seq, we identified that OsSPL9 binds to GTAC-enriched motifs of Cu-associated genes. Several rice domestication-associated phenotypes under the control of SPL9 and miRNAs were altered under limiting and excess Cu conditions, indicating how Cu might have influenced these phenotypic changes. These results indicate potential contributions of altered metal ion availability and their accumulation, influencing crop phenotypes in agricultural systems.

Results

Substantial changes in micronutrient-related genes between wild and cultivated rice

In order to identify differentially expressing genes between wild and cultivated rice line Pusa Basmati 1 (henceforth PB-1) across various growth stages, we performed RNA-seq analysis and obtained a mapping efficiency of 95% to the rice genome (IRGSP-1.0) (Supplementary Table 1). Nearly 25% of the genes that showed higher expression in O. nivara when compared to PB-1 were associated with disease resistance and secondary metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Such a relationship has been well-explained across several wild species and domesticated crops65–68. Among the prominent genes that exhibited reduced expression in cultivated rice PB-1 included disease resistance-associated MYBs, WRKY, RALF and bZIP TFs (Fig. 1A lower panel). About 713 genes out of 1335 DEGs had higher expression in PB-1 (Fig. 1A upper panel). As expected, growth-related and yield-associated genes, such as OsGLW769 and OsCLV2c70, were upregulated in PB-1, along with some of the most prominent macro and micronutrient and metal ion-associated genes. It is well known that improved nitrate metabolism is a major change responsible for increased yield across cultivated lines71 and in agreement with this, around 70 nitrate-responsive genes showed higher expression in PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Similarly, iron-responsive genes also exhibited higher expression in PB-1 importance of which is unknown (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Additionally, the expression of several phosphate-responsive genes was high in wild rice when compared to PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1E). For example, a central regulator of phosphate signalling in rice named OsPHR2 and a high affinity phosphate transporter-OsPT4 were upregulated 2-fold and 16-fold, respectively, in O. nivara (Fig. 1B). As implicated previously, phosphate levels and expression of phosphate-responsive genes are important factors in disease resistance72,73. Among the genes involved in micronutrient and metal ion response, a major category of upregulated genes in cultivated lines were Cu-binding proteins such as uclacyanins (Fig. 1B). Among the 6 categories of Cu-responsive genes classified based on functions in rice74, major categories of highly expressed genes in PB-1 included those involved in photosynthesis, metabolism and transport. Very few genes among Cu-responsive genes were upregulated in O. nivara, and they included categories such as response to stress and defense as indicated earlier (Fig. 1C). The expression levels of Cu-responsive genes (as listed in ref. 74 and about 128 Cu-associated genes (curated from RAPDB) were upregulated in cultivated rice when compared to O. nivara (Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Data 1, 2). This indicates that Cu-responsive modules were among the major DEGs between O. nivara and PB-1. Among these Cu-associated genes are a family of laccases that were previously implicated in indica rice domestication12. In agreement with the large number of Cu-associated DEGs identified between wild and cultivated rice, a large number of QTLs (from Gramene QTL database) for all traits also overlapped with Cu-responsive genes (25% of all QTLs). In comparison, among the 327 well-characterised QTLs for nitrogen-use efficiency75, there was an overlap of 30.8% (Fig. 1D). These results indicate a previously unappreciated contribution of Cu in mediating domestication-associated changes.

We hypothesised that the observed differences in gene expression for Cu-responsive genes might be due to differential absorption/accumulation of Cu between wild and cultivated species. Several studies indicated altered accumulation of nutrients between wild and cultivated crops that resulted in above and below-ground growth phenotypes in wheat76. Recently, it was also reported in durum wheat that a 2-fold increase in DEGs occurred depending on nitrogen availability during domestication77. It is well-known that Cu in vegetative tissues is inhibitory to herbivores as well as other biotic stress factors54,78,79 and several studies suggest that certain hyperaccumulator plants ward off pathogens and pests by selective allocation of transition metals80. Although several heavy metals, such as arsenic, are known to accumulate in rice grains81, genes associated with arsenic were not enriched among the DEGs between the wild and cultivated lines. While these results are in agreement with the general assumption that wild species are able to accumulate bivalent anions and cations, they do not provide insights why Cu-associated genes are among the most DEGs or which tissues copper accumulates between wild and cultivated lines.

Cu-associated miRNAs and their targets are differentially expressed in wild and cultivated rice

miRNAs are well-known regulators of a large family of genes, and they fine-tune gene expression during development and stress responses5,8,82. Several domestication-associated DEGs and phenotypes are regulated by miRNAs and other sRNAs in rice3,12,83–86. Based on degradome analysis87, we identified several differentially expressing miRNAs between wild and cultivated rice that targeted genes such as Os5NG4 and R2R3MYB. In order to get a comprehensive view of nutrient-associated miRNAs and their target genes, we analysed sRNA datasets across different tissues of wild and cultivated rice lines. Several nutrient associated miRNAs88 were unchanged in seedling, flag leaf and panicle tissues across wild and cultivated lines (Fig. 2A). These include miR390 which is cadmium-responsive89, miR395, sulfate-responsive miRNA25, miR156 responsive to phosphate and potassium90, miR393 associated with nitrogen91, and miR169,171 and 172 that are associated with Fe accumulation92. The miRNAs that showed differential expression between wild and cultivated rice varieties were miR397, miR398, miR408 and miR528 across several different tissues such as seedling, flag leaf and panicles (Supplementary Fig. 2A). This set of miRNAs is known to establish Cu homoeostasis and is known to differentially express upon sensing Cu levels33,35. In addition to PB-1, another cultivated indica rice line, white Ponni, also showed patterns similar to PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 2A), indicating that the differences in miRNA levels are more general. Higher expression of Cu-miRNAs was also observed in O. rufipogon, the ancestor of several indica and japonica accessions. For example, O. sativa japonica Nipponbare, a cultivated japonica rice, accumulates reduced Cu-associated miRNAs such as miR408 (5-fold reduction when compared to O. rufipogon), and miR528 (30-fold reduction when compared to O. rufipogon) (Supplementary Table 6; data reanalysed from ref. 93. However, another Cu-dependent miRNA, miR397, did not show a reduction between wild relatives— O. rufipogon and Nipponbare (Supplementary Table 6). Also, miR397b, which is highly expressed in O. nivara is less abundant in O. rufipogon, indicating a significant difference between indica and japonica rice lines. This indicates that the roles of Cu-dependent miRNAs in general and miR397-related domestication-associated phenotypes in particular might not be entirely similar between indica and japonica rice lines. These differences between subspecies are interesting, and it is possible that they might have evolved to match the locations/habitats in which they were domesticated.

To further confirm the differential accumulation of miRNAs, we performed a sRNA northern blotting that indicated high expression of Cu-miRNAs in all tissues of O. nivara (Fig. 2B). We used the psRNATarget tool94,95 to identify the putative targets of these Cu-miRNAs and found additional, previously unknown, targets such as members of the SOD and uclacyanin families that are also Cu-associated genes. Previously known as well as newly identified targets of these miRNAs also showed differential expression between wild and cultivated rice lines as seen in RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 2C). The targets and miRNA levels showed negative correlation, indicating the miRNA-mediated regulation of these Cu-responsive target genes.

To further confirm differential targeting of these Cu-associated miRNAs, degradome sequencing87 was employed. As expected, targets of these miRNAs, such as laccases, uclacyanins, multicopper-oxidases and Cu/Zn-SODs, had higher degradome reads, often in category 0, in O. nivara when compared to PB-1, indicating a much stronger targeting in wild rice due to higher levels of miRNAs (Fig. 2D). A miR528 target named multicopper-oxidase had 164 degradome reads from wild rice and 73 reads from cultivated rice. Similarly, Cu/Zn-SOD had 425 degradome reads in category 0 from O. nivara when compared to 121 reads of category 2 in PB-1 flag-leaf and panicle tissues. The targets of these miRNAs are known to regulate several yield-associated phenotypes, and these modules include miR397:laccases12,26,28–30, miR528: RFI238, miR408:Uclacyanins41, and miR398:Cu/Zn-SOD44 (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

OsSPL9 is an SBP-domain containing TF upstream of domestication-associated miRNAs

miRNA-TF associated networks are well studied in plants96–99. TFs associated with basal metabolism and development remain conserved across land plants, but several nutrient-related TFs were co-opted with the downstream gene regulatory networks to elicit specific responses in changing environmental conditions100. In Arabidopsis, the Cu-responsive miRNAs (miR397, miR398, miR408, miR857) are regulated by the SBP-domain containing TF named AtSPL751. To identify the rice homologue of AtSPL7, we performed a phylogenetic analysis for the SPL family of genes in Arabidopsis and rice (Fig. 3A), having 16 and 19 members, respectively101. This analysis indicated that OsSPL9 is homologous to AtSPL7. Interestingly, OsSPL9 in rice was identified as a regulator of miR52840. To study further the role of OsSPL9 in cultivated indica rice, we generated overexpression (OE) and knockout (ko) lines of OsSPL9. SPL9 constitutively expressed under Zm: Ubi promoter was tagged with 3xFLAG in the N-terminal (Supplementary Fig. 3A), and the transcript/protein levels were validated (Supplementary Fig. 3B–D). We obtained 8 independent transgenic lines upon transforming scutella-derived PB-1 calli (Supplementary Table 4). Interestingly, the SPL9 OE plants showed more number of tillers (Supplementary Fig. 3E, H and Supplementary Data 12), as well as reduced yield mainly due to reduced panicle size and abnormal grain filling (Fig. 3B). They also exhibited reduced seed length and width (Fig. 3C, D and Supplementary Fig. 3F, I).

We also generated spl9ko lines using a gRNA targeting the first exon (Supplementary Fig. 4A) and obtained 5 independent ko lines (Supplementary Table 4), among which altered editing of SPL9 led to reduced levels of its transcript (Supplementary Fig. 4D). The CRISPR-Cas9-mediated ko of SPL9 TF rendered a 2nt-deletion resulting in a truncated protein of 118 amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). The spl9ko plants were largely indistinguishable in vegetative growth when compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 3E); however, they exhibited compromised yield phenotypes such as reduced panicle size (Fig. 3B). Seeds of spl9ko were also smaller and had abnormal shape (Fig. 3C, D and Supplementary Fig. 3F, I). These results indicate that mis-expression of SPL9 leads to alterations in yield-related phenotypes, such as seed length, grain filling (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Data 3, 13, 14 and Supplementary Fig. 3I, J), indicating the importance of optimal levels of SPL9 required for normal growth and development.

In order to understand if SPL9 is indeed upstream of Cu miRNAs, we performed northern blot analysis in these mis-expression lines and observed that all 4 miRNAs, but not other miRNAs, are upregulated at least 2-fold in SPL9 OE lines (Fig. 3E). The miRNA accumulation was also conserved across the vegetative leaf stages of the SPL9 OE lines (Supplementary Fig. 3G). Most importantly, levels of these 4 miRNAs were undetectable in spl9ko−/− and at intermediate levels in spl9ko heterozygous lines as seen in northern blot analysis (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 4C), together indicating that OsSPL9 is the upstream regulator of these miRNAs. Since we observed differential Cu accumulation between wild and cultivated lines, we explored if SPL9 mediated regulation of miRNAs gets altered under different levels of Cu. In wild-type PB-1, Cu-deficient conditions did not alter levels of these miRNAs, while Cu-excess conditions abolished the expression of all these miRNAs (Fig. 3F). spl9ko lines did not express these miRNAs in both deficient and excess conditions, indicating that SPL9 is the sole regulator of these miRNAs under varying Cu conditions (Fig. 3F). The SBP domain showed sequence conservation across the rice lines (Supplementary Fig. 4E) and the SPL9 transcripts did not accumulate differently upon varying Cu concentrations in PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4F). However, to check the response of SPL9 protein levels in response to high copper we performed western blotting and we observed that the high Cu led to reduced protein levels of SPL9 (Fig. 3G) as also observed previously54, and this might be why miRNA expression was abolished under high Cu conditions. Interestingly, we see that the SPL9 transcript levels are high in flag leaves and panicles of O. nivara compared to PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4G). All these results conclusively indicate that SPL9 regulates the expression of Cu miRNAs involved in Cu-responses. OsSPL9 likely played a major role in establishing domestication-associated yield phenotypes by regulating the targets of these Cu-associated miRNAs.

SPL9 binds to Cu-associated gene promoters enriched with the conserved GTAC motifs

Although the SPL family of TFs in Arabidopsis are known to bind to GTAC-containing motifs, the global profile of regions bound by OsSPL9 or its other monocot homologues is unknown. Although it was shown that OsSPL9 can bind to the miR528 promoter in ChIP-qPCR40, it is unknown if it can also bind to promoters of other Cu-associated miRNAs and Cu-associated genes. Since SPL9 regulates the expression of miRNAs, we profiled SPL9-bound regions in wild-type and under Cu-stressed conditions.

ChIP-seq was performed using seedling tissues using the Illumina platform, and we obtained more than 20 million reads mapping to IRGSP-1.0. We obtained more than 8000 peaks enriched with SPL9 binding. Among these peaks, most were located in intergenic regions, especially among promoters (62.11%), while distal intergenic regions (36.26%) peaks were also found in introns and exons (Fig. 4A, B). Preferred binding to genic promoters by OsSPL9 is in agreement with similar observations in Arabidopsis58. The consensus motifs across the peaks were analysed, and these had multiple GTAC motifs as expected, usually in the distal intergenic regions. Cu-sufficient conditions also showed wider stretches of GTAC motifs in SPL9-bound genomic regions (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, several Cu-associated genes, as well as several stress- associated genes, had SPL9 peaks in their promoter as well as in distal regions validated by ChIP sequencing and ChIP qPCR (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 7A–I). SPL9 appears to be regulated by itself as it bound to GTAC motifs in its promoter (Supplementary Fig. 5A). To further validate the binding of SPL9 to its consensus GTAC motif, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) using GTAC motif-containing probes of the key copper associated genes and targets of miRNAs. OsSPL9 regulates the expression of Cu-associated miRNAs and protein-coding genes

To further understand the fundamental role of SPL9 TF in regulating gene expression under homoeostatic conditions, we generated RNA-seq datasets (Supplementary Table 1) from both spl9ko and SPL9 OE lines. In SPL9 OE, 698 genes were upregulated and 773 genes were downregulated (Fig. 5E). The upregulated genes belong to categories involved in Cu-chaperone associated superoxide activity, nutrient reservoir activity, enzyme inhibitor activity etc. and some of these were downregulated in spl9ko as expected (Fig. 5F). Since OsSPL9 has specific roles in Cu-deficiency responses and under copper deprivation the SPL7 undergoes post-transcriptional regulation with changes in its target activity55,102, we analysed the transcriptomic responses upon low Cu and excess Cu conditions in cultivated rice. The DEGs obtained in both categories were correlated with the transcriptomic responses of spl9ko. Interestingly, we observed that spl9ko mimics an excess Cu transcriptome (Fig. 5G).

The spl9ko lines showed 1217 upregulated and 1376 downregulated genes; the latter set of DEGs included copper transporter, COPT3 and several metallothionein proteins (Fig. 5H). This is in agreement with observations in Arabidopsis SPL7 where the protein activated the expression of Cu-transporters, such as COPT1 and COPT251. Increased Cu-miRNA target expression in spl9ko lines was also observed. The miRNA targets such as CuZnSOD2, CuZnSOD3, SOD4, Uclacyanin 8, Uclacyanin 9, Uclacyanin 15, Uclacyanin 23, Uclacyanin 30 and Laccase 14were upregulated several-fold in spl9ko (Fig. 5H). Among these, most of them showed reduced expression in O. nivara, while their levels were upregulated in spl9ko, when compared to PB-1 (Fig. 5I and Supplementary Data 4). The major family of DEGs in cultivated rice, compared to wild rice, also had Uclacyanins. This set of genes was not observed in Arabidopsis upon spl7ko as indicated51. All these results conclusively indicate the importance of Cu-dependent processes mediated by OsSPL9 in homoeostasis and under varying Cu levels.

We further overlapped the upregulated and downregulated genes of spl9ko with SPL9 TF-bound 8362 peaks identified in Cu-sufficient (optimal Cu) conditions. Upon overlapping spl9ko upregulated genes with SPL9 peaks, we obtained 57 genes corresponding to 158 peaks (Supplementary Fig. 8A), all showing SPL9 binding in the distal regions of the genes (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C). The 57 upregulated genes in spl9ko were also bound by SPL9; they were probably negatively regulated, as these genes failed to respond to low Cu conditions in PB-1 and in spl9ko (Supplementary Fig. 8D). These genes were associated with defense response and protein desumoylation (Supplementary Fig. 8E), and they were also directly or indirectly Cu-associated. The SPL9-bound upregulated genes included genes such as Iron-sulfur cluster protein, Uclacyanin 23, etc (Supplementary Fig. 8F), and they showed peak occupancy in the TES regions of coding sequences.

Upon overlapping spl9ko downregulated genes with SPL9 peaks, we obtained 66 genes corresponding to 208 peaks (Supplementary Fig. 9A), all showing a higher SPL9 binding in the distal regions of the genes (Supplementary Fig. 9B, C). In low Cu conditions, these 66 genes remained largely downregulated in spl9ko in comparison to PB-1. However, under excess Cu treatment, these 66 genes were predominantly downregulated in both spl9ko and PB-1. This conclusively indicated that these 66 genes are positively regulated at the transcriptional level by OsSPL9 in optimal Cu-conditions, and might have important roles to play under varying concentrations of Cu (Supplementary Fig. 9D). In agreement with this prediction, gene ontology analysis revealed that these SPL9-bound downregulated genes (66) in spl9ko were associated with suberin biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, fatty acyl biosynthetic process, etc. (Supplementary Fig. 9E), and genes of these categories were among the DEGs upregulated in O. nivara when compared to PB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). It is important to note that these categories of genes are mediators of largely Cu-associated processes. Genes such as heavy metal- associated protein 2 (HMA2) and Pollen fertility restorer 2 were downregulated, and they had SPL9-bound peaks close to TSS and TES (Supplementary Fig. 9F).

Cu-associated miRNAs form a feedback loop

Several studies have shown that the stability of the miRNAs is regulated by their targets, association with Argonaute proteins or their modifications103, indicating the presence of a feedback loop. A number of targets associated with Cu-binding and/or associated genes are regulated by the miRNAs downstream of SPL9. However, the role of individual miRNAs or their targets in regulating the Cu-responses remained unclear. It is also intriguing that Cu, an element, the accumulation of which might vary between two nearby sites, was central to these regulatory modules. We hypothesised that regulatory roles played by individual Cu-miRNA, its target(s) and Cu-availability/accumulation might be strongly linked in such a way that small perturbations do not lead to a drastic growth penalty. In order to understand the crosstalk by SPL9-mediated regulation of miRNAs and its targets, we generated individual knockouts of these domestication-associated miRNAs in cultivated rice background (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11C). The knockout of miR397 resulted in a 4 nt deletion in the stem loop of the precursor miRNA, perturbing the formation of mature miR397 (Supplementary Fig. 10A). miR528ko mutant harbours a single nucleotide insertion (Supplementary Fig. 10B) and the knockout of miR408 using CRISPR led to 8 nt deletion in the stem-loop region of the miRNA (Supplementary Fig. 10C). The mature miR408 with this mutation will be only 18 nt long when compared to 21 nt in WT. Estimation of the Cu accumulation revealed that in the mature leaves of miRNA knockout lines of miR528 and miR397, there was a reduction in Cu accumulation (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Data 5) similar to SPL9 perturbed lines. This suggested a strong feedback control between individual miRNAs and their regulator OsSPL9, as it indicates that the SPL9-regulated phenotypes are dependent on SPL9-regulated miRNAs. On a similar note, the panicle length, seed filling and seed length were reduced in each of these knockout lines, suggesting that individual miRNA knockouts phenocopied spl9ko lines (Fig. 6F–I, Supplementary Data 6–9, 15, 16 and Supplementary Fig. 11A, B, E, F).

The perturbation of individual miRNAs led to changes similar to their upstream regulator SPL9; it is likely that each Cu-associated miRNA module functions in a feedback regulation with SPL9 and, in turn, other SPL9-dependent miRNAs. This probed us to test the accumulation of other SPL9-dependent, domestication-associated miRNAs in individual miRNA knockout lines. Interestingly, we observed that the individual miRNA knockouts of miR397 and miR528 also led to a reduction in all the other domestication-associated miRNAs in mature leaves (Fig. 6J and Supplementary Fig. 11C, D). For example, the miR397bko not just led to a reduction in the levels of miR397b but also the other Cu-associated miRNAs such as miR528, miR408 and miR398. Such a relationship was observed partially or fully with other Cu-associated miRNA knockouts. For example, miR408ko did not alter the levels of other Cu-associated miRNAs but only that of miR408. However, similar to miR397ko, miR528ko accumulated reduced levels of miR528 and also the other Cu-associated miRNAs. This is indicative of a strong self-regulatory module where the regulation of miRNAs is not only dependent on each other but also on the targets or the set of Cu-genes regulated by them. Since such feedback is not reported so far, it will be interesting to identify how upstream regulators of Cu-miRNAs influence miRNA biogenesis machinery. Expression levels of genes involved in miRNA biogenesis remain unperturbed under varying Cu-levels (Supplementary Fig. 10D). In hen-1 mutants, the transcripts of Cu-binding proteins such as LAC5 and LAC8 accumulated independent of copper supply and to higher levels compared to wild type104. It is possible that the stability of proteins, especially those involved in miRNA accumulation, might be under the control of proteases as hypothesised previously for SPL7102. Future studies integrating Cu transport, its compartmentalisation, tissue/cell-specific accumulation of Cu and activities of Cu-dependent proteins, preferably with genetic mutants, might provide further mechanistic understanding.

Discussion

Cereal crops grown in agricultural ecosystems are genetically distinct from wild relatives and are subjected to varied water and fertiliser supplies. Ability to acquire and utilise the minimal requirements for plant growth, must have contributed to the process of domestication in these crops, to enable them to have high photosynthetic rate, broader leaves, and increased growth rate110–112. Increased growth might have demanded more nitrogen and phosphorus uptake and utilisation113, when compared to other elements such as Cu and micronutrients that are part of defense proteins and promoters of secondary metabolites114. Micronutrients such as cobalt is also essential for plants in the form of metalloproteins such as carboxypeptidases and ureases115. Uptake and accumulation of several of these elements, including metal ions, were altered in many agronomic crops during domestication116. Studies have suggested that genetic as well as epigenetic factors alter the histone marks at the level of transport, metabolism as well as signalling for macronutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus117. Cu, being a micronutrient, an essential cofactor for a wide range of cellular enzymes and proteins, has importance in several key processes. Cu-proteins are functionally redundant to existing iron enzymes and have evolved much later upon oxygenation, indicating their possible redundant but equally important roles in the metalloproteomes118. It is therefore not surprising that Cu build-up and its utilisation and incorporation appear to be a major determinant for traits somewhat similar to the well-established macronutrient nitrogen119–125. Even though Zn, Fe and Mn-associated metalloproteins exist, there is no clear association of them being key regulators of domestication-associated traits126. It is evident that Cu might have a bigger unappreciated role in plant growth and development than previously envisioned. Accumulation of high Cu in wild relatives is in agreement with their ability to have stronger disease resistance traits127. Further studies are required to understand the nature of its accumulation in tissues, subcellular localisation, stored and utilised form of Cu, as well as some understanding of the Cu cycle and its homoeostasis within wild and cultivated rice plants. Temporal Cu-supplementation experiments at specific developmental stages, such as grain-filling and pre-flowering is required to study specific domestication traits. Additionally, cross-species analysis to study the comparative evolution of the Cu-SPL9 pathway in other domesticated crops, such as wheat, barley, maize and sorghum, would be interesting. Several aspects of Cu-dependent processes that contributed to rice domestication, specifically involving SPL9, require further experimentation.

The TFs function by binding to contact sites in the enormous stretch of DNA to promote the recruitment of other factors such as co-activators/repressors to regulate the gene expression128–130. SPL family of TFs are well known in rice to modulate yield associated traits, for example, OsSPL14 is important for regulating plant architecture131–133, and SPL12 and miR529-regulated SPLs such as SPL2, 16, 17, 18 are essential for maintaining grain size and yield134,135. Maize SPL homologue regulates kernel row number136. Among dicots, too, SPLs are well-known regulators of growth137. Since Cu metabolism in rice is connected closely with yield-related traits and miRNAs form feedback to regulate them to a very refined layer through OsSPL9, it is clear that SPLs have the potential to modulate Cu-mediated processes. Similar to other SPL members50,51,138,139, OsSPL9 also preferred GTAC motifs. We have also observed that there are additional sequence motifs where SPL binding can be seen, and it will be interesting to further explore if these additional motifs mediate specific functions. OsSPL9 has auto-regulatory activity and it binds to regulatory elements in its own promoter, this mode of autoregulation has been reported for TFs such as MYB41140, BBM1141 and WRKY6142. In this study, the perturbation of OsSPL9 or even altering the amount of Cu available mimicked the accumulation of miRNAs/target levels in wild or cultivated rice. This conclusively indicates that Cu-dependent SPL9-mediated regulation is central to gene expression differences between wild and cultivated rice lines.

Homologue of OsSPL9 in Arabidopsis, SPL7, is known to interact with another TF named HY5, thereby integrating the light and Cu cues. The pathway is also regulated by miR408 such that it reallocates more Cu to chloroplasts143. The sequence-specific DNA-binding activity of the OsSPL9 TF indicates the ability to regulate the miRNA genes as well as several Cu proteins/ transporters in a synchronous manner. This required optimal levels of OsSPL9 as reported here, since Cu-stress altered binding to target sites. It will be interesting to study if OsSPL9 integrates other cues, such as light and temperature, to modulate binding to target sites. Some of the downstream targets of OsSPL9 seem to be working in this fashion. For example, Cu-associated miR528 targets RFI2 (red and far-red insensitive 2), a RING-domain zinc finger protein that modulates light sensing38. Natural variations in the miR528-SOD interaction has been documented in C3 and C4 photosynthetic monocot species39. Additionally, Arabidopsis miR408 encoded peptide miPEP408 accumulated differentially during light transitions144. miR398 is a heat-responsive miRNA that facilitates thermotolerance among diverse plants145. It is possible that through these regulatory loops, OsSPL9 regulation might be controlled by other environmental cues apart from natural variation, aging and light as reported previously38.

Since OsSPL9 appears to be a major regulator of yield-related traits, it is possible that its homologues in other cereals also regulate Cu-dependent yield-related processes. Cu deficiency and Cu levels through YSL proteins are well known to affect seed size, spikelet numbers and floret formation in monocots146. Although such studies are yet to be carried out in cereals other than rice, the CRR1 gene, a homolog of OsSPL9 and AtSPL7 in Chlamydomonas, regulates Cu uptake and its utilisation147,148. The CRR1 in algae binds to the regulatory regions of copper-deficiency genes such as CYC6 and CPX1147, similarly, in our biochemical assays, we see a conserved binding affinity for OsSPL9 to the GTAC motifs of Cu-responsive genes, such as copper transporters and miRNAs. In moss, Cu-mediated transcriptional suppression is through a SBP transcription factor, PpSBP2, in a GTACT motif-dependent manner149. In maize, miR528 is known to regulate laccases and affect the lodging resistance150, but the upstream players are unknown. In crops like banana, the miR528-PPO module involved in fine-tuning ROS levels is under the regulation of a miR156 targeted SPL4151. The evolutionary conservation of CRR1/SPL7/SPL9 family across algae, dicots and monocots indicates an evolutionary pathway linking metal homoeostasis and plant development. Our observation that SPL9 OE has partial phenotypic rescue, while the spl9ko did not show a rescue, indicates a dual functionality of the SPL9 TF, which integrates the nutrient homoeostasis with the developmental pathways. Further studies, including Cu-insensitive variants of SPL9, Cu-chelation and SPL9 activity, along with ortholog complementation experiments, might help to dissect the transcriptional roles of SPL9.

In this study, we observe feedback between individual miRNAs and OsSPL9 in a Cu-dependent manner. Several studies have pointed out feedback mechanisms where the miRNA machinery is regulated by a miRNA itself, such as DCL1 and AGO1, negatively regulated by miR162 and miR168, respectively152. In the context of nutrient stress, such as phosphate, the non-protein gene IPS1 acts as a target mimic to sequester miR399, leading to PHO2, its target upregulation153. Target-directed miRNA decay, which leads to miRNAs of distinct families getting degraded, has been reported from metazoans154,155. In SPL9 mis-expressed lines, or in miRNA ko lines, levels of miRNAs other than Cu-miRNAs were unchanged. Among individual miRNA ko plants, the absence of a miRNA might have triggered downregulation of other miRNAs in the pathway, probably by altering functions of a Cu-mediated process downstream of the core miRNA biogenesis machinery. Interestingly, Cu-miRNAs are associated with multiple regulatory pathways, and the self-regulatory module that we observe in our study represents a functional regulatory interaction rather than an isolated feedback loop. This mode of regulation for Cu miRNAs was previously reported, where the northern blots confirmed that miR398 and miR408 levels were strongly reduced in farnesyl transferase mutants. Importantly, miRNA levels are rescued upon SPL7 complementation in Arabidopsis156. It is also possible that levels of Cu incorporated by any of the miRNA targets in the miRNA ko line, in specific cell types, might influence levels of Cu available for other Cu-associated proteins.

The miRNAs and TF responding to varying levels of Cu might have been a key for survival, as Cu is an important micronutrient that serves as a cofactor for several enzymes and proteins35. Apart from SPL9 as a sole regulator, MYB84 has also been reported to regulate COPT2 and HMA5 in rice to improve the Cu distribution in rice grain and yield157. The evolution of such a system involving TFs might be a result of the habitat in which these lines evolved and in soils enriched with specific metals. Several metabolic pathways and metabolites are triggered upon heavy metal stress in cultivated rice158–160. However, O. nivara has higher accumulation of metabolites such as anthocyanins that can incorporate high amounts of heavy metals161–164 to sequester them in vacuoles and less sensitive organelles/tissues. A recent study has also shown that SPL9 has a role in alleviating Fe-associated copper starvation45. Over the course of domestication, the modern crop varieties have been prone to grow in nutrient-rich environments165,166. It is likely that OsSPL9 played a crucial part in this selection and adaptability to agricultural systems.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The plant materials used for the study were Oryza nivara and Oryza sativa indica PB-1, which were grown in a growth chamber 24 °C/70% RH with a 16 h–8 h light–dark cycle. The plants grown in the growth chamber were further transferred to a greenhouse maintained at 28 °C with a natural day-night cycle. The other mutants in the study are mentioned in the Supplementary Table 4.

Binary vector construction and Agrobacterium mobilisation

For the generation of overexpression lines of SPL9, the CDS of the gene Chr 5: 19923167-19932333 (Os05g0408200) was amplified and cloned with an N-terminal 3xFLAG tag into a derivative of pCAMBIA1300 driven by a maize Ubiquitin1 promoter. The spl9ko mutant was generated using CRISPR-Cas9 mutagenesis, and the gRNA was designed using CRISPR-PLANT167. A similar strategy was followed for the miRNA knockouts as well. The gRNA sequences and the reverse primers for all constructs are mentioned in the Supplementary Table 5. The amplicon harbouring the gRNA sequence was cloned and inserted into plasmid pRGEB32168. Screening of mutants was performed by PCR and sequencing to identify the indel (insertion/deletion) in the targeted region. The gRNAs were designed using CRISPR-PLANT tool and Class 1.0 gRNAs with no off-targeting ability under the stringency criteria were chosen for the study (Supplementary Fig. 15A). These gRNAs were further subjected to RAPDB BLAST and only single hit was found across the genome for the respective miRNA (Supplementary Fig. 15B). Additionally, the miRNA ko lines were further sequenced for indel in other miRNA regions using primers flanking the respective genomic loci and no indel was found for any miRNA other than for the gRNA: target miRNA (Supplementary Fig. 15C).

The constructs were mobilised into Agrobacterium using the electroporation method. All the binary plasmids were mobilised into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 harbouring the extra-virulence plasmid pSB1. Embryogenic rice calli were infected with Agrobacterium strain containing binary plasmids using established methods169,170, and the calli were selected and regenerated on hygromycin-containing medium.

Stress experiments of the seedlings

The basal ½ MS media was reconstituted, Cu-deficient media (0 mg/l) was prepared by omitting copper from the standard formulation and supplemented with increasing gradients of CuSO4 concentration—for Cu-sufficient (0.025 mg/l), Cu-excess (2.5 mg/l) and Cu-high (8 mg/l). The rice seedlings were germinated on magenta (Sigma) boxes with the media described above and grown for 21 days in growth chambers maintained at 24 °C/70% RH with a 16 h–8 h light–dark cycle were used for stress experiments. The samples were collected post-stress administration and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction and other molecular analysis. For ChIP-seq analysis, the samples were crosslinked and processed. The phenotyping of the seedlings was performed by counting the root length, shoot length, number of lateral root hairs and total number of crown roots. The box plots were plotted using the R-package ggplot2, and the statistical significance was computed using Student’s t-test. The phenotyping data are included in Supplementary Data 3, 6–10 and 12–16.

Cu estimation in seedlings

The rice seedlings grown in the growth chamber, supplemented with Cu at various concentrations, were collected at the end of the stress regimen of 21 days. For copper estimation in mature leaves, plants grown in greenhouse conditions were used. The tissues were dried for a period of 3–5 days at 37 °C to remove moisture. At the end of the drying period, the samples were finely ground to a powder using a shredder. Around 200 mg of the ground powder was weighed, and 10 ml of concentrated HNO3 was added. The tissue was digested overnight in a fume hood. Post overnight incubation, 1 ml of 4% H2O2 was added to the acid-digested tissue. Post digestion, the tissues were then put in an acid digestor to facilitate maximum extraction of the elements. The samples were filtered using a filter paper and collected. The samples were analysed along with Cu standards in an ICP-OES as described in ref. 54.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 21-day-old whole seedlings. A total of 1 µg RNA was taken as input, and RNA integrity was verified with Agilent Tapestation. RNA was enriched for poly-A fraction, and the library was prepared using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep kit (E7765L) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed in a paired-end fashion on the Novaseq 6000 platform. The resulting reads from the library were trimmed using Trimmomatic171 and mapped to the rice genome (IRGSP 1.0) using Hisat2172. The library statistics and the alignment percentage are mentioned in the Supplementary Table 1. The output files were later processed using Samtools173 and the Cufflinks package174,175. The DEG calling was performed using Cuffdiff, and the upregulated and downregulated genes were classified based on log2Fold change cut-off as >1 and <−1. The p-value was taken as less than 0.05 for significant DEGs. For the volcano plot, the ggVolcano176 tool was used. For the heatmap, the pheatmap package in R was used. The scatter plots were made using ggplot2 after creating the matrix for the gene expression using bedtools multicov as mentioned in ref. 177. GO analysis was performed using Shiny GO v0.75178. The gene-IDs were used from RAPDB with a preference for biological processes with an FDR cut-off of 0.05.

sRNA northern hybridisation

sRNA northern blots were performed based on the previously established protocols5,179. Around 10 µg of TRIzol extracted total RNA from 21-day-old seedling tissues was electrophoresed on a denaturing 15% acrylamide gel. The RNA gel was blotted onto Hybond N+ membrane (GE Healthcare) and UV-cross-linked. The hybridisation of the membrane was performed with the T4 PNK end-labelled oligonucleotides ([γ-32P]-ATP) in Ultrahyb buffer (Invitrogen). The blots were scanned using a Typhoon scanner (GE Healthcare). After the scanning, blots were stripped at 80 °C in stripping buffer, and repeat hybridisations were done with subsequent probes.

Phylogenetic analysis

Amino acid sequences in Arabidopsis and rice were downloaded from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) and RAP-DB (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/), respectively. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was generated based on the alignment using MEGA v6.06 with bootstrap of (1000 repetitions).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-seq) and analysis

ChIP was performed as described earlier180–182. About 1–1.5 g of the 21-day old seedling tissue grown in respective copper conditions were collected and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde with intermittent mixing and quenched with 0.125 M glycine in 1× PBS. The tissues were finely ground in liquid nitrogen, and the nuclei isolation was performed. The nuclei were lysed and sheared using the Covaris ultrasonicator till the fragments were of 150–350 bp in size. The chromatin post shearing was incubated overnight with Anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads-40 μl (Sigma-M8823) at 4 °C. The purified IP products were taken for library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA library prep kit with sample purification beads (NEB E7103L) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries (with replicates) were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Supplementary Table 3).

The datasets from the sequencing were used for cutadapt-based adaptor trimming. The alignment was performed using Bowtie2183, and the alignment parameters were as follows: -v 1 -k 1 -y -a --best –strata. The PCR duplicates were removed, and the alignment files were converted to coverage files and normalised with input samples, using deeptools184,185. The ComputeMatrix tool was used to compute the average signals over the annotated regions, and plotProfile was used to generate the metaplots. The peak calling was performed using MACS2 with the narrow peak calling option. The consistency across replicates was validated using BEDTools intersect. Intervene was used to obtain the intersections with the RNA-seq data as performed earlier186.

For the ChIP-qPCR, the immunoprecipitated DNA was taken as the template. Around 1–1.5 µg of total DNA was used for qPCR. The qPCR reaction was set up using SYBR-containing reaction mix (Solis Biodyne), and the samples were loaded as triplicates in a 96-well plate. The values are plotted across two different regions, where P1—with enrichment or presence of peaks in ChIP-seq and P2—with no enrichment or absence of peaks. The Ct-values from the resulting plate were used to estimate the 2−∆CT. The triplicate values were used for plotting, and the p-value was calculated using Student’s t test.

sRNA sequencing and analysis

sRNA datasets were obtained from ref. 12. The size-based fractionation and library preparation were performed as previously described187. The library statistics and alignment percentage are as mentioned in Supplementary Table 2. These reads were further subjected to quality check using FastQC, adaptor removal and size selection using UEA sRNA workbench188. The 21–22 nt size classes of the miRNA was categorised to the IRGSP1.0 genome using Bowtie 2, using the following parameters: -v 1 -m 100 -y -a --best –strata. The miRNA abundance was profiled using the miRProf tool189, and the differential expression of the reads were plotted. Only the mapped reads were used for further analyses, including differential expression analyses.

Degradome analysis

The degradome library preparation and sequencing are as mentioned in ref. 87,187. In order to predict the potential miRNA targets, the GSTAr script from CleaveLand was used. The degradome reads were aligned to all rice transcripts from MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project Version-7 using Bowtie with 2 mismatches. The calculation of the fragment abundance of the reads and category for each position in every gene was performed. Later, the merging of the potential targeting information and read alignments from the genome was done to obtain the list of valid miRNA targets. The list of potential targets had target cut site, category and abundance. Category 0 is when more than 1 read aligns at the cut site, and is the only position where maximum reads have aligned. Category 1 is when more than one read aligns, and is also where the maximum number of reads align and is among the other equal maximum peaks. Category 2 is when more than one read aligns at the cut site, and the depth is above the average depth (average of abundances at all positions that have at least one read) and below the maximum abundance. Category 3 is when more than one read aligns at the cut site, and the depth is below or equal to the average depth (average of abundances at all positions that have at least one read). Category 4 is when only one read aligns at the cut position87.

Western blotting

The mature leaves and seedling tissues were used for protein extraction and western blotting. The samples were finely ground using liquid nitrogen, and extraction was performed using extraction buffer (0.05 M Tris pH 8.0, 0.2 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton-X-100, 1 mM PMSF, PVP). Around 200 mg of the whole seedling or mature leaves was taken for the native extraction. The protein extract from the samples was electrophoresed and stained using a CBBS to normalise the loading. Normalised amounts of proteins were electrophoresed and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was hybridised with Anti-FLAG M2 monoclonal antibody (Sigma F1804) used at a dilution of 1:1000 and detected using HRP-based chemiluminescent detection system.

RT-qPCR

Around 1–1.5 µg of total RNA from the seedling or leaf was used for cDNA synthesis and RT- qPCR. The qPCR reaction was set up using SYBR-containing reaction mix (Solis Biodyne), and the samples were loaded as triplicates in a 96-well plate. The Ct-values from the reaction plate were used to estimate the 2−∆CT with reference to GAPDH as an internal reference. Further, the standard deviation and the standard error were calculated. The p-values obtained based on Student’s t test across 3 replicates were used for statistical significance. Each experiment consists of 3 independent biological replicates.

Recombinant protein purification

pET vector harbouring 6XHis-tag at the N-terminal end was used for expression of the coding sequence of OsSPL9. Rosetta Gami DE3 (Novagen, Madison, WI, USA) cells were used for transformation with p plasmids containing OsSPL9. A single colony was inoculated to raise a primary culture of 10 ml in LB medium. The next day, 1% of the primary culture was inoculated to obtain a secondary culture of 1.5 L, and the culture was grown at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.6. Protein expression was induced by treating with 0.3 mm of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and grown at 37 °C for 2 h. Cells were pelleted at 5422 g for 1 h at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until further use. Cells were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 8, 300 mm NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mm β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME), 1 mm phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.01% IGEPAL, 0.5 mg/ml lysozyme and complete protease inhibitor tablets containing EDTA (Roche). Cells were lysed by sonication (40% amplitude, five cycles of 10 s pulse-on, 15 s pulse-off for 2 min) and were centrifuged at 17,000 rpm at 4 °C for 1 h. The supernatant fraction was filtered through 0.45 μm filters. The filtrate was passed through Econo-columns (Bio-Rad) containing Ni-NTA agarose beads (Qiagen), equilibrated with buffer-A (50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 8, 300 mm NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mm β-ME). The flow-through was passed through the column again. Beads in the column were washed with buffer-A and then with buffer-B (50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 8, 600 mm NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mm β-ME), and then again with buffer-A. Proteins were eluted using 250 mM imidazole prepared in buffer A. Protein yield and purity were assessed by CBB staining.

EMSA

Recombinantly purified 6XHis-OsSPL9 protein was used for EMSA reactions. Single-stranded DNA probes, along with their reverse complements, were obtained from Eurofins (probes listed in the Supplementary Table 5). The positive strand of DNA was radioactively labelled using a terminal transferase kit from New England Biolabs (NEB, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA. Radio-labelled DNA probes were incubated with 1 μg of OsSPL9 protein in EMSA binding buffer (50 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 50 mm NaCl, 200 mm KCl, 5 mm MgCl2, 5 mm EDTA, 5 mm ZnSO4, 5 mm DTT, 0.025 mg/ml BSA) for 30 min at 30 °C. The reaction mixtures were run on a 6% native-PAGE and exposed under a phosphorescent screen. The phosphor screen was imaged under a GE Healthcare Typhoon scanner.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical comparisons were performed using the R package or Excel, with a significance threshold α = 0.05. The two-tailed Student’s t test was used to compare two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for box plots showing gene expression across samples. A minimum of 3 biological replicates were used for all statistical analyses.
