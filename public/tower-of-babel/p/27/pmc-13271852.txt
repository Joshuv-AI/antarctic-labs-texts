Mechanical Thrombectomy for Internal Carotid Artery Occlusion with Accessory Middle Cerebral Artery: A Case Report
Authors: Ryo Hiramatsu, Ryokichi Yagi, Yuichiro Tsuji, Junzo Nakao, Masao Fukumura, Yoshiki Fujikawa, Akihiro Kambara, Ryo Kayama, Hironori Yamada, Masahiro Kameda, Naosuke Nonoguchi, Motomasa Furuse, Shinji Kawabata, Toshihiro Takami, Hiroyuki Ohnishi, Masahiko Wanibuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe accessory middle cerebral artery (AMCA) is a relatively rare normal variant of the middle cerebral artery (MCA). Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion with AMCA is very rare, and the initial diagnosis can be difficult. We illustrate a case of ICA occlusion with AMCA in which delayed diagnosis led to delayed MT.Case PresentationAn 80-year-old man suddenly presented with only mild sensory aphasia. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score was 6. Head MRI showed new punctate acute ischemic lesions. Head MRA demonstrated the left ICA occlusion and visualization of the left MCA via the anterior communicating artery. Since there were few findings that would positively suggest cardioembolic cerebral embolism, including the absence of atrial fibrillation, the patient was diagnosed with the progression of ICA stenosis to acute occlusion. Antiplatelet therapy was initiated, but 10 h later, the patient developed a progressive stroke and underwent MT, which resulted in complete recanalization, during which an AMCA was identified. The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital with a modified Rankin Scale of 4.ConclusionA high-signal-intensity area in the main MCA on FLAIR MRI, suggesting flow stagnation, along with absent signal in the same region on time-of-flight MRA, suggesting occlusion, may be a clue to the diagnosis of ICA occlusion with a normal MCA variant.

Introduction

The accessory middle cerebral artery (AMCA) is a variant vessel that branches from the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and runs through the sylvian fissure along the middle cerebral artery (MCA). Most AMCAs are smaller in diameter than the main MCA and are thought to primarily perfuse the frontal lobe.1–4) Although several reports of bifurcation aneurysms thought to be caused by hemodynamic stress have been published,4,5) reports describing mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for acute ischemic stroke associated with AMCA are extremely rare, with only 7 cases having been reported,6–12) of which 4 were cases of internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion with AMCA.8,9,11,12) As in the present case, patients with ICA occlusion and the affected-side AMCA often have poorer functional outcomes after MT compared with cases of AMCA occlusion without concomitant ICA occlusion, because recanalization may take longer owing to anatomical differences in the vascular pathway from the ICA to the MCA.8,9,11) However, in cases of ICA occlusion with AMCA, head MRA may reveal the AMCA via the anterior communicating artery (Acom A), which may lead clinicians to mistake the AMCA for the main MCA, making initial diagnosis and determination of treatment strategies difficult. We present a case of ICA occlusion with AMCA that resulted in a poor functional outcome because of a delayed treatment decision.

Case Presentation

An 80-year-old man with no past medical history and independence in activities of daily living suddenly developed mild sensory aphasia at 1:00 p.m. on the previous day. His sensory aphasia did not improve until the next day, so he initially presented to another hospital with his family. Head MRI–diffusion weighted imaging (MRI-DWI) showed new punctate acute ischemic lesions mainly in the territory of the left anterior choroidal artery (Fig. 1A). Head MRA demonstrated a left ICA occlusion. In addition, the left MCA was visualized by cross-flow via the Acom A (Fig. 1B). He was transferred to our hospital (Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University Hospital) 24 h after the onset of symptoms. At the time of transfer to our hospital, the patient’s blood pressure was 172/111 mmHg; no atrial fibrillation (Af) was observed on the electrocardiogram; the D-dimer level was 1.4 μg/mL on blood testing; and the Japan Coma Scale (JCS) score was 0. He had no significant past medical history and was taking no regular medications. Neurological findings included mild sensory aphasia without motor deficits, and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was 6. After initiating intravenous fluid administration in the emergency room, the patient’s mild sensory aphasia improved, with the NIHSS score decreasing from 6 to 2. Angiography was performed to assess cerebral hemodynamics. On angiography, consistent with the MRA findings, the left ICA was occluded (Fig. 2A) and the left MCA was well visualized distally due to flow via Acom A (Fig. 2B and 2C). Acute cerebral infarction was mainly observed in the territory of the left anterior choroidal artery with a spotty pattern. Furthermore, because angiography confirmed sufficient collateral circulation, cardioembolic cerebral embolism could not be completely ruled out, but at this stage, progression of ICA stenosis to acute occlusion was suspected. Since the patient’s NIHSS score improved to 2, MT was deferred, but instead dual antiplatelet therapy and adequate fluid management were initiated. However, 10 h later, despite continued medical treatment, he developed impaired consciousness and his NIHSS score worsened to 15. An immediate head MRA findings remained unchanged (Fig. 1C); MRI-DWI revealed new acute ischemic lesions (Fig. 1D), but the neurological findings showed paralysis of the right upper limb with NIHSS subscore of 4 on item 5, and a decreased level of consciousness with JCS score of 20 (NIHSS score was 14). These findings suggested additional large-vessel occlusion beyond the anterior choroidal artery territory. At this stage, it was determined that the patient was refractory to medical management, and endovascular treatment was initiated (Fig. 3).

Treatment progress timeline.

Under local anesthesia, an 8-Fr-long sheath was placed in the right femoral artery. An 8-Fr OPTIMO (Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was guided into the left ICA. After balloon inflation of the OPTIMO, an APRO (Kaneka, Osaka, Japan) was advanced using the Sofia (Terumo Neuro, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA) Non-wire Advancement techniKE (SNAKE).13) When an APRO made contact with the thrombus, it was manually aspirated using a 20-mL syringe, retrieving the thrombus. Three attempts were performed, resulting in the retrieval of a large amount of thrombus. Angiography after balloon deflation showed visualization of the main MCA and AMCA, with a migrated thrombus confirmed proximally in the AMCA and distally in the main MCA (Fig. 2D). It was only at this stage that we determined that this was a case of ICA occlusion with AMCA. A Vecta 46 (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was guided to the location of the thrombus of AMCA using an SL-10 (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) and a 0.014-inch microguidewire. The proximal thrombus of the AMCA was then manually aspirated using a 20-mL syringe to retrieve, and the distal thrombus of the main MCA was spontaneously recanalized, achieving complete recanalization (Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 3) (Fig. 2E and 2F). Angiography after complete recanalization confirmed that the anterior choroidal artery branched from the ICA, not the main MCA (Fig. 2G), and that the LSA branched from the AMCA (Fig. 2H). A head MRI-DWI performed the day after MT revealed extensive cortical infarction (Fig. 1E). After MT, a Holter electrocardiogram revealed paroxysmal Af, leading to a diagnosis of cardiogenic cerebral embolism in this case. A direct oral anticoagulant was initiated. In the initial head MRI-FLAIR, a high-signal area was observed in the main MCA (Fig. 4A), and the signal in the same area disappeared in the time-of-flight MRA (TOF-MRA) image (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, in the head TOF-MRA image after complete recanalization, a high-signal area corresponding to the affected-side main MCA was confirmed in the same region (Fig. 4C). The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital on the 22nd hospital day with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) of 4.

Discussion

The AMCA, first described by Crompton in 1962, is a vessel originating from the ICA between the origin of the anterior choroidal artery and the terminal bifurcation of the ICA, and it courses parallel to the MCA trunk within the sylvian fissure.14) Subsequently, Teal et al.15) defined those branching from the ICA as duplicated middle cerebral arteries and those branching from the ACA as AMCA; this classification remains widely used today. The incidence of AMCA is estimated to be 0.3%–2.7% in autopsy cases and 0.26%–4.0% in cerebral angiography, making it a relatively rare occurrence.16) Most AMCAs are smaller in diameter than the main MCA and predominantly supply the frontal lobe.1–4) Furthermore, in autopsy cases, it has been reported that the lenticulostriate artery branches from the AMCA.4,17) Reports of MT for acute ischemic stroke involving AMCA are extremely rare. Including this case, only 8 cases have been reported to date,6–12) 5 of which involved ICA occlusion with AMCA (Table 1). The outcomes of the 5 cases of ICA occlusion with AMCA were poorer than those of the other 3 cases. In 5 of ICA occlusions with AMCA, an mRS ≥3 at 90 days was observed in 75% of reported cases, while both reported non-ICA occlusion cases had an mRS score of 0. Therefore, early diagnosis of ICA occlusion with AMCA and prompt MT are crucial.

Normal variants of the MCA pose one of the current challenges in MT for acute ischemic stroke. MT has become the standard treatment for acute ischemic stroke caused by large-vessel occlusion, but it is not applicable to all stroke subtypes.18) Clinicians should consider normal variants of the MCA in such cases, especially when there is a discrepancy between clinical symptoms, as indicated by the NIHSS score, and imaging findings, or when complete occlusion of the ICA does not correlate with the patient’s clinical symptoms.7)

In the other 4 published cases of ICA occlusion with AMCA, excluding the present case, the MCA was frequently visualized on imaging despite relatively severe neurological symptoms, suggesting that the presence of an AMCA or duplicated MCA should be considered in such situations.8,9,11,12) In contrast, in the present case, neurological symptoms were mild despite occlusion of the left ICA. However, similar findings may also be observed in ICA occlusion without AMCA when the Acom or posterior communicating artery (Pcom A) is well developed, making differentiation challenging.

In this case, the electrocardiogram obtained on admission to our hospital did not demonstrate Af, and blood testing revealed a D-dimer level of 1.4 μg/mL, which was not elevated. Although these findings were not sufficient to rule out a cardioembolic etiology, at this stage we diagnosed large-artery atherosclerosis due to progression of ICA stenosis to acute occlusion. Subsequently, the patient developed a progressive stroke and underwent MT, during which the presence of an AMCA was revealed. Complete recanalization was achieved, but the functional outcome was poor at discharge (mRS 4). The essence of this case was acute left ICA occlusion due to the cardioembolic cerebral embolism. Because the thrombus was located at the bifurcation of the left ICA and main MCA, blood flow to the AMCA via the Acom A was maintained, resulting in only minor stroke symptoms. Therefore, looking back at our initial diagnosis of this case, we misdiagnosed acute ICA occlusion due to cardioembolic cerebral embolism as progression of ICA stenosis to acute occlusion, and additional evaluation for a cardioembolic source should have been pursued. Furthermore, the usefulness of MT for minor stroke remains unclear, and several cohort and observational studies have not demonstrated its superiority over medical treatment, which may have contributed to the delay in intervention.19) In addition, when the imaging findings from the initial consultation were reviewed, findings suggesting normal variants of the MCA were also confirmed. The initial head MRI- FLAIR revealed a high-signal area in the main MCA, indicating blood flow stagnation (Fig. 4A), and the signal in this area disappeared on TOF-MRA images (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, head TOF-MRA images after complete recanalization confirmed the high-signal area corresponding to the main MCA in the corresponding regions (Fig. 4C). These imaging findings may provide useful clues for differentiating between ICA occlusion with AMCA and ICA occlusion without AMCA in cases where the Acom A or Pcom A is well developed.

Conclusion

We report a case of ICA occlusion with AMCA in which MT was delayed due to difficulty in the initial diagnosis. In cases of ICA occlusion in which the affected-side MCA is visualized by the cross-flow through the Acom A despite a low NIHSS score, a high-signal-intensity area in the main MCA on FLAIR MRI, suggesting flow stagnation, along with absent signal in the same region on TOF-MRA, suggesting occlusion, may be a clue to the diagnosis of ICA occlusion with a normal MCA variant.
