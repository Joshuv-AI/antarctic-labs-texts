Region-specific vulnerability to vascular risk factors and modifiable protective influences on white matter hyperintensities
Authors: Mingxi Dang, Dandan Wang, Yaojing Chen, Zhanjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegion-specific vulnerabilities to single and combined vascular risk factors and the role of modifiable behaviors in white matter hyperintensity (WMH) progression remain unclear. We aimed to characterize neuroanatomical patterns of WMH vulnerability and to identify subgroup-specific factors associated with lower burden and slower progression.MethodsWe included 1146 participants (60.9% female; age 45–86 years) from the Beijing Aging Brain Rejuvenation Initiative (2008–2023), all of whom underwent standardized magnetic resonance imaging together with clinical and behavioral assessments. We used age- and sex-specific centile curves of WMH burden to examine associations with vascular risk factors, their combinations, and behavioral profiles.ResultsHere we show that vascular risk factors have region-specific associations with WMH burden: hypertension is linked mainly to periventricular frontal regions, whereas smoking is linked to deep subcortical regions. Combined risk factors show broader involvement, especially in periventricular frontal regions and the corpus callosum. Better control of some vascular risk factors is associated with lower burden (e.g., diabetes: approximately 2.0 mL lower; hyperlipidemia: approximately 2.3 mL lower) and slower progression (e.g., controlled total cholesterol: 0.176 vs. 0.939 mL/year, p = 0.007), and behavioral associations differ across risk profiles. More frequent leisure activity is associated with lower burden mainly in obesity, whereas lower depressive symptoms are associated with lower burden or slower progression in some vascular-risk subgroups.ConclusionsVascular risk factors and behavioral factors show subgroup- and region-specific associations with WMH burden and progression. These findings may help refine risk stratification, improve understanding of vulnerability patterns, and inform future intervention studies. Subject terms: White matter disease, Cerebrovascular disorders, Cerebrovascular disorders, Dementia

Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH), among the most prominent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) markers of cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD), are associated with increased risks of stroke, dementia, and mortality1. Management of modifiable vascular risk factors (VRFs), such as hypertension and diabetes, is therefore considered a central component of cSVD prevention and care2. However, important gaps remain in understanding the region-specific vulnerability of white matter to distinct VRFs and to combinations of coexisting VRFs, limiting more refined risk stratification and mechanistic interpretation. In addition, the associations of socioeconomic, behavioral, and emotional factors with WMH burden in individuals already carrying VRFs remain insufficiently characterized, restricting the development of more personalized non-pharmacological strategies.

Modifiable VRFs are major contributors to WMH through microvascular structural and functional alterations that promote white matter injury. Hypertension shows the strongest and most consistent association with WMH presence and severity3, whereas the effects of diabetes, smoking, obesity, and hyperlipidemia have been more heterogeneous, with prior studies reporting positive4–7, null8,9, or even inverse associations10,11. These discrepancies may reflect unmodeled spatial heterogeneity in VRFs-related white matter damage, as distinct VRFs exhibit unique pathophysiological mechanisms targeting discrete brain regions12. Another limitation of the existing literature is the insufficient consideration of combined VRF effects. In older populations, VRFs frequently co-occur13–15, and such coexistence may aggravate vascular and clinical burden16,17. For example, hypertension and diabetes may jointly amplify microvascular injury18, but their combined associations with WMH topography and burden remain incompletely understood.

There is also growing interest in how behavioral and psychosocial factors—including socioeconomic conditions19–21, leisure activities22,23, and emotional states24–26—relate to brain aging and cerebrovascular health. While the concept of lifelong brain health promotion is widely endorsed27, evidence linking these factors to lower WMH burden or slower progression in established at-risk populations remains limited and is inconsistently reflected in practice recommendations2. For example, although physical activity is broadly recommended for reducing obesity-related health risks28, it remains unclear whether its associations with WMH are similar across individuals with hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking exposure, or obesity, or whether different behavioral domains (e.g., social activity vs. emotional well-being) show subgroup-specific patterns. This lack of stratification hampers the delivery of personalized behavioral advice.

The Beijing Aging Brain Rejuvenation Initiative (BABRI, 2008–present) cohort provides an opportunity to address these questions through a community-based longitudinal design in China29,30, integrating standardized neuroimaging, comprehensive vascular risk factor phenotyping, and real-world assessments of behavioral and psychological factors. In the present study, we show that different VRFs are associated with distinct region-specific patterns of WMH vulnerability, whereas coexisting risk factors are associated with more extensive white matter involvement. We further find that better VRF management and favorable behavioral or emotional profiles are associated with lower WMH burden or slower progression in specific at-risk subgroups. Together, these findings support a stratified view of WMH vulnerability and indicate that both vascular management and personalized behavioral strategies are relevant to brain health promotion.

Methods

Study design and participants

Participants were derived from the BABRI database29, which included 12,219 individuals as of December 2023. Recruitment was conducted through community health centers, and all participants received complimentary cognitive assessments, MRI scans, and personalized health reports. MRI was performed as part of a standardized community-based research protocol rather than in response to clinical symptoms, physician referral, or other clinical indications. Baseline inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥24; (2) age ≥45 years; (3) ≥6 years of education; (4) absence of major neurological or neuropsychiatric disorders, verified through electronic medical records and structured interviews; and (5) availability of a complete 3T MRI protocol, including T1-weighted and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) sequences. After applying these criteria, 1146 participants comprised the baseline cohort (Fig. S1). The relatively limited MRI sample reflects practical feasibility constraints, MRI contraindications, and the requirement for complete imaging sequences suitable for WMH quantification in older community participants. To assess the representativeness of the MRI subgroup, baseline characteristics were additionally compared between participants with and without available MRI data (Table S1).

For the longitudinal progression analysis, we included all baseline participants who completed follow-up MRI scans, resulting in 205 participants (median interval: 3.5 years; interquartile range [IQR]: 2.7–4.6 years), representing the full available follow-up cohort. The smaller longitudinal MRI subgroup reflects the additional challenges of repeated neuroimaging follow-up in older adults, including participant return, MRI eligibility, and sequence completeness at follow-up. To further assess the representativeness of the longitudinal MRI subgroup, we compared baseline characteristics between participants with and without follow-up MRI data (Table S2).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of Beijing Normal University (approval No. ICBIR_A_0041_002_02). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of vascular risk factors

This study assessed traditional VRFs, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and obesity. Information on these factors was obtained from participant self-reports and supplemented by medical records. For participants with hypertension, treated systolic and diastolic blood pressure values (in mmHg) were recorded. For participants with diabetes and hyperlipidemia, fasting blood samples were collected to assess treatment status using serum biomarkers, including fasting plasma glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol. These measures were obtained using an automatic biochemistry analyzer (OLYMPUS AU400, Japan) and commercially available diagnostic kits (Beckman Coulter, Ireland). Smoking status was defined as a history of regular smoking (≥1 cigarette/day for ≥ 6 months), including both current and former smokers. Obesity was defined according to the Chinese body mass index (BMI) classification standard (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²), where BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). All VRFs were coded as binary variables (1 = present, 0 = absent).

Collection of multidimensional behavioral information

Behavioral information was systematically collected across three domains: socioeconomic status, lifestyle, and emotional state. Socioeconomic status was assessed using years of education, occupational level, monthly income, and subjective economic status. Occupational level was quantified using an occupational prestige index ranging from 0 to 131. Monthly income was evaluated on a graded scale from 1 to 13, with higher scores indicating higher income. Subjective economic status was self-rated on a four-point scale corresponding to poor, lower-middle class, upper-middle class, and wealthy.

Lifestyle information comprised leisure activity and life regularity. Leisure activity was assessed using a personal information questionnaire including 23 self-reported items covering common leisure activities over the previous 12 months. Based on established frameworks32–34, these activities were categorized into mental, physical, and social domains, and aggregate scores were calculated for each domain. Life regularity was assessed in two dimensions: dietary regularity and sleep regularity. Dietary regularity was evaluated using a four-point scoring scale based on breakfast habits, meal frequency, meal timing, changes in portion sizes, and overeating tendencies. Sleep regularity was assessed using four questions addressing bedtime, wake-up time, sleeping later than midnight, and napping for more than 1 h.

Emotional state was evaluated using life satisfaction, depressive symptoms, and loneliness. Life satisfaction was assessed using the Face Scale35, which presents seven facial expressions ranging from very happy to very sad. Depressive symptoms were measured using the 30-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)36, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30 and higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Loneliness was assessed using the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness Scale37, which includes 20 items and yields scores ranging from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness.

MRI acquisition

Imaging data were collected on a Siemens Trio 3T MRI scanner using T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR sequences. Participants were positioned supine, and their heads were stabilized with shoulder straps and foam pads to minimize motion during scanning. High-resolution T1-weighted images were acquired using a sagittal three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequence with the following parameters: 176 sagittal slices, repetition time (TR) = 1900 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.44 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, flip angle = 9°, inversion time = 900 ms, field of view = 256 × 256 mm², and acquisition matrix = 256 × 256. T2-FLAIR images were acquired with TR = 9000 ms, TE = 81 ms, slice thickness = 3 mm, flip angle = 150°, and 25 slices.

Lesion segmentation and quantification

WMH were segmented from T2-FLAIR images using the Lesion Growth Algorithm implemented in the Lesion Segmentation Tool (LST v2.0.15) for Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12 v7771). T1-weighted images were segmented into cerebrospinal fluid, white matter, and gray matter, and the resulting tissue probability maps were coregistered with FLAIR intensities to generate lesion belief maps. A lesion probability threshold of κ = 0.2 was applied to derive participant-specific WMH masks and lesion volumes38. WMH masks were then normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using a T1-guided nonlinear registration pipeline implemented in Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs v2.2.0). All lesion masks underwent visual quality control, with lacunes at WMH margins excluded.

WMH burden was quantified both globally and regionally. For regional quantification, the brain was segmented according to anatomical lobes and proximity to the ventricular surface (Fig. S2). A ventricular distance map was generated using the distancemap function from FMRIB Software Library (FSL v6.0.5.1), and a 10-mm threshold was used to define periventricular and deep white matter regions. Lobar white matter segmentation was performed using the Geodesic Information Flows (GIF) algorithm, delineating the frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, insula, subcortical, and corpus callosum regions. By combining the two distance-based layers (periventricular and deep) with the anatomical regions, a total of 14 regions of interest (ROIs) were defined. The deep frontal region was further parcellated into anterior and posterior subdivisions along the anterior–posterior axis using the midsagittal plane as a reference. Because the corpus callosum is almost entirely periventricular, no deep corpus callosum ROI was defined. Regional WMH burden was calculated as the lesion volume relative to the total volume of the corresponding ROI. Intracranial volume (ICV) was calculated as the sum of gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid volumes. Total brain volume (TBV) was defined as the sum of gray matter and white matter volumes, and brain parenchymal fraction (BPF) was calculated as TBV/ICV. These measures were used as covariates in the primary and sensitivity analyses, as described below.

Establishing normative modeling

Generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) were used to construct age- and sex-specific normative centile curves for global and regional WMH burden. This framework enables estimation of an individual’s WMH burden relative to a reference population with similar demographic characteristics and provides a flexible approach for modeling nonlinear age-related variation39–41. Briefly, GAMLSS specifies the outcome distribution as a function of parameters governing location, scale, skewness, and kurtosis. In the present study, cubic splines were used to model age-related changes in WMH burden. To identify the optimal configuration of model parameters, the full dataset was randomly divided into training (60%) and validation (40%) subsets, minimum validation global deviance was used for model selection42, and the most suitable outcome distribution was selected according to the Bayesian information criterion43. Goodness of fit was evaluated using quantile–quantile plots and residual distribution metrics, including skewness, kurtosis, and the Filliben correlation coefficient.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, GAMLSS was applied to model the distribution of WMH burden across age, stratified by sex. For each participant, an estimated centile was derived based on age, sex, and WMH burden, representing the proportion of peers with lower WMH values. To facilitate downstream statistical analyses and visualization, these centiles were subsequently transformed into z-scores, such that the 50th centile corresponded to a z-score of 0. Positive z-scores indicate greater-than-expected WMH burden relative to the normative reference, whereas negative values indicate lower-than-expected burden. This approach allows for individual-level characterization of WMH burden while accounting for nonlinear effects of age and sex, providing a normative framework for subsequent association analyses44.

Statistics and reproducibility

To investigate the independent and combined effects of VRFs on WMH accumulation, participants were stratified into a free-risk group (no VRFs), five single-VRF groups (hypertension, smoking, diabetes, obesity, or hyperlipidemia alone), and ten combined-VRF groups representing all unique pairwise combinations of these five risk factors [C(5,2) = 10; Fig. S3], yielding fifteen risk-factor groups in total. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to compare WMH deviation z-scores, derived from the GAMLSS-estimated age- and sex-specific centiles, between each risk-factor group and the free-risk group. Although age and sex were incorporated in the normative modeling, they were additionally included as conservative covariates in ANCOVA to reduce potential residual confounding across subgroups and to account for residual age- or sex-related variation in downstream subgroup comparisons. ICV, which was not included in the GAMLSS models, was also adjusted for to account for head-size-related variability. To account for multiple comparisons across brain regions and risk-factor groups, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 5% using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure45. Given the large number of statistical tests performed, these analyses were considered exploratory and intended to identify potential regional specificities of WMH accumulation. Voxel-wise WMH probability maps were additionally analyzed using SPM12 with the same covariate adjustment, using a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05 corrected by Gaussian random field (GRF) theory.

To evaluate associations of risk-factor management with WMH burden and progression, participants with hypertension, diabetes, or hyperlipidemia were further classified into well-controlled and poorly controlled subgroups according to prespecified clinical thresholds. Specifically, hypertension was categorized using the blood pressure target recommended for older adults in the Chinese Guidelines for Hypertension Management, with <150/90 mmHg considered well controlled46. Diabetes was considered well controlled when fasting blood glucose was <7.0 mmol/L and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was <7.0%47. Hyperlipidemia was considered well controlled when lipid levels fell within the normal range, defined as total cholesterol <6.2 mmol/L, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol >1.0 mmol/L, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol <4.1 mmol/L, and triglycerides <2.3 mmol/L48. ANCOVA was used to compare baseline WMH deviation z-scores between groups. Longitudinal WMH volume trajectories were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance, with age, sex, and ICV included as covariates and follow-up interval additionally included in longitudinal models.

Multidimensional behavioral factors, encompassing socioeconomic, lifestyle, and emotional domains, were also evaluated within VRF-defined at-risk populations. Participants were stratified into high- and low-score groups according to the median of the corresponding composite scores. ANCOVA was used to compare baseline WMH deviation z-scores between groups, adjusting for age, sex, and ICV. To extend the cross-sectional analyses, linear regression models were used to quantify the associations between baseline behavioral scores and annual WMH progression rates, with adjustment for age, sex, and ICV.

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings under alternative covariate specifications. These included cross-sectional models adjusted for ICV only, as well as longitudinal models in which ICV was replaced by TBV or BPF to account for global brain atrophy. The baseline analyses included 1146 participants with complete MRI and phenotypic data, and the longitudinal analyses included 205 participants with follow-up MRI. Each participant contributed one observation to the cross-sectional analyses, and repeated measures were defined as baseline and follow-up MRI assessments in the longitudinal subgroup. All statistical analyses were performed using R and SPM12.

Results

This study included 1146 participants aged 45–86 years (mean age, 66 years) of whom 698 were female (60.9%; Table 1). Among the investigated VRFs, hypertension was the most prevalent (46.8%), followed by hyperlipidemia (40.6%), diabetes (23.7%), obesity (22.1%), and smoking (21.0%). VRF multimorbidity was common in this cohort, as reflected by the frequent co-occurrence of multiple risk factors (Fig. S3): the hypertension–hyperlipidemia dyad was the most common combination (22.6%), followed by hypertension–diabetes (15.0%). Notably, only 9.5% of participants with hypertension had no other coexisting VRFs, whereas 23.1% of the overall cohort had no VRFs. The frequency distribution of WMH lesion damage across single- and combined-VRF groups suggested region-specific and graded patterns of WMH accumulation (Fig S4).

Region-specific effects of VRFs on WMH

Normative centile curves generated via GAMLSS revealed accelerated age-related increases in WMH burden (Fig. S5), with model validity supported by approximately normally distributed residuals in Q–Q plots (Fig. S6) and residual distribution metrics (Table S3). The distribution of WMH deviation z-scores demonstrated distinct patterns: single VRFs were associated with more focal WMH accumulation, whereas co-occurring VRFs were associated with more extensive lesion patterns (Fig. S7). Region-specific analyses across 15 groups (5 single-VRF groups and 10 unique pairwise combinations) identified distinct patterns of association (Fig. 2). For example, hypertension showed the strongest associations with periventricular frontal WMH burden (F = 17.317, p = 3.979E-05, p-FDR = 0.001), whereas smoking showed predominant associations with deep regional WMH abnormalities (F = 12.274, p = 0.001, p-FDR = 0.006).

Combined VRFs were associated with broader lesion distributions, with the most pronounced patterns observed in the periventricular frontal lobe and corpus callosum. Specifically, hyperlipidemia co-occurring with hypertension or diabetes was associated with higher WMH burden compared to risk-free individuals in periventricular frontal (F = 9.089, p = 0.003; F = 10.629, p = 0.001), periventricular parietal (F = 9.005, p = 0.003; F = 10.558, p = 0.001), corpus callosum (F = 9.327, p = 0.002; F = 8.801, p = 0.003), and deep posterior frontal regions (F = 9.854, p = 0.002; F = 8.007, p = 0.005). The hypertension–diabetes combination was associated with extension of lesions into periventricular subcortical and temporal regions, as well as deep anterior frontal and parietal regions (all p-FDR < 0.05), together with higher total WMH volume (F = 21.262, p = 5.327E-06). In addition, smoking co-occurring with hypertension or hyperlipidemia showed spatial concentration in the periventricular frontal region (hypertension: F = 7.012, p = 0.008; hyperlipidemia: F = 6.991, p = 0.009) and corpus callosum (hypertension: F = 6.851, p = 0.009; hyperlipidemia: F = 7.893, p = 0.005). By contrast, obesity co-occurring with hypertension or diabetes was associated with more diffuse periventricular and deep lesion patterns (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1).

Voxel-wise analyses corroborated these spatial patterns with greater anatomical precision (Fig. S8, GRF-corrected). Isolated hypertension showed peak effects in the periventricular frontal lobe, whereas combinations with smoking, diabetes, or obesity extended abnormalities into deep frontal and parietal regions. Obesity showed predominant involvement of periventricular frontal and callosal regions, with broader extension into deep frontal, parietal, temporal, and insular areas when co-occurring with other VRFs. Notably, isolated diabetes showed preferential lesion accumulation in deep posterior frontal regions, suggesting regional vulnerability that was less apparent in the region-based analyses.

Associations of VRF management with WMH

Stratification by clinical guideline targets showed that diabetic and hyperlipidemic older adults with better fasting glucose and lipid control had lower WMH burden than their suboptimally managed counterparts (fasting glucose: F = 7.633, p = 0.01; total cholesterol: F = 7.633, p = 0.006; triglycerides: F = 5.651, p = 0.018). The corresponding adjusted mean differences in WMH volume, derived from covariate-adjusted models, were approximately 2.0 mL for fasting glucose and 2.3/1.7 mL for lipid-related measures (Fig. 3A).

The longitudinal subgroup comprised 205 participants with eligible follow-up MRI scans; their baseline and follow-up characteristics are summarized in Table S4. Longitudinal analyses suggested slower annual WMH progression in well-controlled lipid groups than in poorly controlled groups (total cholesterol: 0.176 vs. 0.939 mL/year, triglycerides: 0.22 vs. 0.708 mL/year; Fig. 3B). In the primary models adjusted for age, sex, ICV, and follow-up interval, this difference was significant for total cholesterol (F = 8.106, p = 0.007) and borderline for triglycerides (F = 4.018, p = 0.051). Corresponding effect sizes and annual WMH progression as a percentage of baseline WMH volume are provided in Table S5. Overall, these longitudinal differences were modest in magnitude and should be interpreted cautiously, despite statistical significance in some comparisons. Notably, although blood pressure control was not associated with lower baseline WMH burden, lower systolic blood pressure was associated with slower lesion progression (standardized β = 0.676, p = 0.020; Fig. S9).

Associations of healthy behaviors with WMH

Beyond VRF management, more frequent physical activity, greater social engagement, lower depressive symptom burden, and higher educational attainment were associated with WMH burden across high-risk groups, although the magnitude of these associations varied by risk profile (Fig. 4A). Specifically, obese older adults with more frequent physical activity (F = 15.308, p = 0.0001) or social participation (F = 14.186, p = 0.0002) exhibited lower WMH burden than their less active counterparts, with adjusted mean differences of approximately 2.6 mL and 1.4 mL, respectively. Lower depressive symptom burden was also associated with lower WMH burden in participants with diabetes (F = 7.844, p = 0.005), hyperlipidemia (F = 7.438, p = 0.007), and hypertension (F = 3.694, p = 0.055).

Longitudinal analyses further suggested that lower depressive symptoms were associated with slower WMH progression in participants with hypertension (standardized β = 0.219, p = 0.035; Fig. 4B) and showed a borderline association in those with hyperlipidemia (standardized β = 0.196, p = 0.056), whereas leisure activity frequency was not significantly associated with progression. These regression models were adjusted for age, sex, and ICV. Overall, these longitudinal associations were modest and should be interpreted cautiously.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses. In the cross-sectional analyses, models adjusted for ICV only (without age and sex) yielded attenuated regional associations but preserved key spatial patterns (Fig. S10), whereas the associations of VRF management and healthy behaviors with WMH deviation z-scores remained broadly consistent (Figs. S11and S12). These findings support the robustness of the main results while also highlighting the stabilizing role of age and sex adjustment in the region-specific analyses.

In the longitudinal analyses, replacing ICV with TBV or BPF yielded similar overall results. Specifically, the association for total cholesterol remained significant under both alternative adjustments (TBV-adjusted: F = 7.008, p = 0.011; BPF-adjusted: F = 6.141, p = 0.017), whereas the triglyceride association was significant with TBV adjustment (F = 4.430, p = 0.041) and borderline with BPF adjustment (F = 3.942, p = 0.053) (Table S6). Likewise, the association between lower depressive symptom burden and slower WMH progression remained significant in hypertension (TBV: standardized β = 0.215, p = 0.038; BPF: standardized β = 0.210, p = 0.048) and borderline in hyperlipidemia (TBV: standardized β = 0.189, p = 0.068; BPF: standardized β = 0.192, p = 0.072) under both TBV- and BPF-adjusted models. Taken together, these analyses support the overall robustness of the main findings across different model specifications, although some longitudinal associations remained modest in magnitude.

Discussion

This study established a normative model for age-related WMH trajectories and extending our previous work38 by identifying two main findings: (1) region-specific patterns of WMH vulnerability associated with VRFs across the brain, and (2) potential synergistic interactions between VRFs in relation to WMH accumulation. In addition, we identified subgroup-specific associations between modifiable factors and WMH burden. Better glycemic and lipid control, greater engagement in leisure activities, higher educational attainment, and lower depressive symptom burden were associated, to varying degrees, with lower WMH burden in specific high-risk subgroups. Longitudinal analyses further suggested that better lipid control and lower depressive symptom burden—particularly among hypertensive and hyperlipidemic individuals—were associated with slower WMH progression.

The differential susceptibility of brain regions to VRFs likely contributes to variation in WMH distribution across white matter territories12, and our findings further support the biological heterogeneity of WMH lesion patterns. Previous studies have shown that hypertension exerts the strongest influence among single VRFs, predominantly exacerbating periventricular over deep WMH49. Our findings are consistent with this pattern, particularly in the periventricular frontal region, which may be especially vulnerable because of its location within watershed territories supplied by long penetrating arterioles with limited collateral circulation50. Apparent discrepancies across prior reports linking hypertension preferentially to deep WMH12,51 may partly reflect differences in parcellation strategies. For example, some clustering approaches merge deep white matter with periventricular border regions into broad “deep frontal” clusters, whereas coarse dichotomization into periventricular versus deep WMH may obscure more selective regional associations12,51. These considerations suggest that finer anatomical subdivision, particularly of periventricular frontal regions, may improve the detection of region-specific vascular associations.

Consistent with prior evidence linking greater VRF burden to higher WMH load9, our findings further suggest that specific combinations of VRFs may be associated with more extensive and anatomically distinct lesion patterns. Hypertension-dominant comorbidities—especially in combined with diabetes—were associated with greater periventricular frontal WMH burden and broader involvement of adjacent watershed territories, in line with previous reports of multiplicative vascular injury52. At the same time, metabolic comorbidities, particularly diabetes combined with obesity, were associated with more diffuse whole-brain WMH accumulation, potentially through insulin resistance-related mechanisms53. This pattern is consistent with broader associations reported in the Framingham cohort54. In addition, the corpus callosum and periventricular frontal regions appeared particularly susceptible to combined VRFs, which may relate to their location within microvascular watershed zones characterized by limited collateral supply and increased blood–brain barrier vulnerability under multifactorial insults55,56.

Regarding vascular and brain health, our findings add to emerging evidence that multidomain behavioral and psychosocial factors—including physical exercise, social activities, and emotional regulation—may be associated with lower WMH burden, while also suggesting that these associations vary across vascular risk profiles57. In particular, lower depressive symptom burden was associated with slower WMH progression in hypertensive individuals and showed a borderline association in hyperlipidemic individuals, in keeping with prior studies linking depression-related interventions to slower WMH progression58,59. These longitudinal associations remained broadly similar after additional adjustment for baseline TBV or BPF, suggesting that the observed patterns were not solely explained by global brain structural decline. Nevertheless, some effects were attenuated and remained modest in magnitude, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation.

Nevertheless, because behavioral measures were self-reported and assessed contemporaneously with MRI, reverse causality cannot be excluded. For example, greater WMH burden may itself contribute to depressive symptoms or reduced activity levels60, rather than these factors serving solely as antecedent exposures. Although the longitudinal analyses provide partial support for a prospective association, they do not fully resolve the possibility of bidirectional relationships. Future studies with repeated behavioral assessments and longer follow-up will be needed to better disentangle directionality.

Notably, we also found that diabetic and hyperlipidemic individuals meeting guideline-based targets (fasting glucose <7.0 mmol/L; total cholesterol <6.2 mmol/L) had lower WMH burden than their poorly controlled counterparts. However, prior studies have reported inconsistent associations of glycemic and lipid control with WMH burden61,62. Taken together, these observations support the need for targeted randomized controlled trials to clarify the causal impact of VRF interventions on WMH and may help inform future biomarker-guided and subgroup-specific interventional study designs.

It should also be noted that, while cSVD is a multicomponent syndrome, the present study focused specifically on WMH burden and progression. This focus was motivated by both the clinical relevance of WMH63,64 and methodological considerations. In community-based samples, WMH volume provides a continuous imaging phenotype with sufficient dynamic range for GAMLSS-based normative modeling, whereas other cSVD markers, such as lacunes or cerebral microbleeds, are typically lower-frequency discrete events and are therefore less well suited to this approach. Future studies incorporating a broader range of cSVD markers will be important for determining whether the region-specific patterns identified here generalize across other manifestations of small vessel injury.

This study has two key strengths. First, normative modeling of individual WMH trajectories reduced age- and sex-related confounding and enabled the identification of spatially specific VRF associations beyond simple group averages. Second, the integration of cross-sectional and longitudinal data provided additional insight into how multidimensional factors may relate to cerebrovascular health in aging and suggested subgroup-specific associations with WMH progression.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, some participation-related selection bias cannot be excluded because MRI participation and repeated follow-up depended on participant willingness, scanner eligibility, and sequence completeness, although MRI acquisition in BABRI was research-based rather than clinically indicated, making symptom-driven enrichment less likely. Second, VRFs and their management status were modeled as binary variables, which improved interpretability but may have oversimplified exposure duration, severity, cumulative burden, and variability. Future studies with repeated vascular measurements are needed to better capture these aspects of vascular risk. Third, the regional and subgroup analyses were exploratory and involved a large number of comparisons; thus, despite FDR correction, type I error cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, our sample size was insufficient to reliably examine higher-order interactions among three or more coexisting VRFs. Finally, the longitudinal sample was relatively small, and although the overall pattern of findings was broadly preserved in sensitivity analyses, some associations were modest in magnitude and attenuated after alternative model adjustment, warranting cautious interpretation.

Conclusions

Our study identified region-specific patterns of association between VRFs and WMH, and suggested that behavioral factors may be differentially related to WMH burden and progression across at-risk subgroups. These findings may help refine risk stratification, improve mechanistic understanding of WMH vulnerability, and inform the design of future interventional studies.
