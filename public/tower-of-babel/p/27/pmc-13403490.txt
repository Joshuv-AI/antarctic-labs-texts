ESR1-Guided Endocrine Switching in HR-Positive Metastatic Breast Cancer: From Detection to Risk-Adapted Decision in SERENA-6
Authors: Atlal Abusanad
Published: 2026

BACKGROUND

Aromatase inhibitor (AI) therapy combined with cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibition is the standard first-line treatment for hormone receptor–positive (HR-positive) metastatic breast cancer without HER2 overexpression or amplification.[1–3] Despite initial disease control, most patients eventually experience progression. Acquired resistance to aromatase inhibition is frequently mediated by mutations in the estrogen receptor gene, ESR1, resulting in ligand-independent estrogen receptor signaling and reduced sensitivity to estrogen deprivation. The most common clinically relevant hotspot mutations include D538G and Y537 substitutions (particularly Y537S, Y537N, and Y537C), with less frequent alterations such as E380Q.[4] In PALOMA-3, ESR1 mutations were identified in 25.3% of plasma samples from patients with endocrine-resistant disease.[5] To overcome this mechanism of resistance, selective estrogen receptors degrader (SERD) was used. The FALCON trial demonstrated that deeper estrogen receptor (ER) blockade with fulvestrant that is injectable SERD improved progression-free survival (PFS) over anastrozole in postmenopausal, endocrine-naïve metastatic disease, particularly in patients with nonvisceral involvement.[6] Other trials have demonstrated that fulvestrant as an effective second-line option for patients with HR-positive advanced breast cancer who experience disease progression following AI therapy.[7,8]

Although conducted in the pre-CDK4/6 era, studies of fulvestrant in the first- and second-line treatment of HR-positive metastatic breast cancer without HER2 overexpression or amplification showed that ER degradation can outperform estrogen deprivation alone and may remain effective after progression on AI therapy.[6–8]

Several orally bioavailable SERDs have been developed to address the limitations of injectable fulvestrant. Their efficacy has been demonstrated compared with fulvestrant, particularly in the presence of ESR1 mutations. Most trials adopted a molecularly nonselective design, enrolling patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative for overexpression or amplification disease who progressed on first-line endocrine therapy, with or without CDK4/6 inhibitors, followed by stratified analyses according to ESR1 mutation status.[9,10] In contrast, molecularly selected designs, as used in PADA-1 and SERENA-6, restrict intervention to patients with circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-detected ESR1 mutations, aiming to test whether targeted endocrine switching at molecular progression improves outcomes.[11,12] PADA-1 is an open-label phase 3 trial[11] randomized patients on AI plus palbociclib who developed a rising ESR1 mutation in blood without radiographic progression to either continue the same therapy or switch the endocrine partner to fulvestrant while maintaining palbociclib. Early switching improved median PFS from 5.7 to 11.9 months (hazard ratio [HR], 0.60; 95% CI, 0.42–0.85). The SERENA-6 trial[12] enrolled patients with HR-positive advanced breast cancer without HER2 overexpression or amplification who had received at least 6 months of first-line AI plus CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy and had no evidence of disease progression, patients underwent ctDNA testing every 2–3 months. Only patients in whom an ESR1 mutation was detected in ctDNA, in the absence of concurrent progression, proceeded to randomization. Eligible patients were randomized to either replace the AI with the oral (SERD) camizestrant while continuing the same CDK4/6 inhibitor or continue the AI plus the CDK4/6 inhibitor. Postprogression treatment was left to investigator discretion and was not protocol mandated.

At the interim analysis, switching to camizestrant resulted in longer PFS compared with continued AI therapy (median, 16.0 vs 9.2 months; HR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.31–0.60).[12] It also met its PFS2 end point.[12] Median PFS2 was 25.7 months with camizestrant plus a CDK4/6 inhibitor compared with 19.1 months with continued AI plus CDK4/6 inhibitor (HR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.46–0.86).[12] Patient-reported outcomes showed delayed deterioration in global health status and quality of life.[13] Overall survival data remain immature, with approximately 30% maturity at the time of analysis and final results anticipated in 2028. Adverse events included visual disturbances with camizestrant (34% vs 16%), most frequently photopsia (20% vs 8%), and were predominantly grade 1–2.[12] Bradycardia was also observed more frequently with camizestrant (7.7% vs 0%), raising concern when combined with QT-prolonging agents such as ribociclib. Of note, a limited number of patients received this combination in SERENA-6. These findings establish ESR1 mutation emergence as a clinically actionable event and confirm that endocrine resistance can be detected before overt progression.

The key difference is that PADA-1 established proof of concept, whereas SERENA-6 extended the strategy using a next-generation oral SERD and a broader CDK4/6 inhibitor backbone. Both trials intervene at molecular progression not clinical progression. However, neither directly answers whether switching at ESR1 detection is superior to a planned switch to SERD at radiographic progression, because delayed SERD use was not the randomized comparator. Therefore, both support ESR1-guided early switching as a PFS-improving strategy, but the long-term sequencing question remains unresolved.

What SERENA-6 Establishes and What Remains Unresolved?

SERENA-6 provides prospective evidence that molecular progression can precede clinical progression by several months and that intervention at this stage can delay radiologic failure.[12] The trial validates the use of serial ctDNA testing as a tool to identify acquired endocrine resistance while patients remain clinically stable. It also confirms that continued CDK4/6 inhibition with a change in endocrine backbone is feasible and associated with maintained quality of life (Table 1). Importantly, the trial isolates the effect of acting at ESR1 emergence rather than comparing ESR1-mutant and ESR1-wild-type disease, as all randomized patients had an ESR1 mutation.

Several questions remain unresolved. First, SERENA-6 is not a pure timing trial, as the control arm did not mandate switching to camizestrant or another SERD at progression, and postprogression therapies were heterogeneous. Therefore, the study cannot distinguish early from delayed camizestrant use. In addition, PFS was measured from the time of molecular detection, a nonstandard endpoint that requires cautious interpretation. PFS2 has similar limitations, as it is influenced by subsequent therapies and does not isolate the effect of the study drug or address whether switching at ESR1 detection is superior to switching at radiographic progression.

Second, overall survival benefit has not been demonstrated. In HR-positive metastatic breast cancer, survival end points are influenced by multiple subsequent therapies, but for a strategy that introduces serial molecular surveillance and earlier drug exposure, overall survival data are necessary to justify broad adoption of labor- and resource-intensive interventions.

Third, ESR1 mutation detection does not uniformly identify patients at immediate risk of progression. In the control arm, median PFS after ESR1 detection exceeded 9 months, indicating that a substantial proportion of patients remained stable for extended periods despite molecular resistance. ESR1 mutation therefore represents an early resistance signal rather than a binary indicator of poor prognosis.

Fourth, routine implementation of serial ctDNA testing raises practical considerations, including assay availability, turnaround time, cost, and interpretation of low-allele-fraction mutations. Data on ESR1 mutation kinetics and emergence timing are not available yet from the trial.

Risk-Adapted Use of ESR1 Detection

A key implication of SERENA-6 is the need to a tailored approach to ESR1 mutation detection.[12] ESR1 status alone might be insufficient in guiding the timing and intensity of intervention. Therefore, a risk-adapted approach may allow better alignment between biology and treatment (Fig. 1).

Factors that may identify patients at higher risk after ESR1 detection include molecular features such as high mutant allele fraction, rapidly rising allele fraction on serial testing, multiple concurrent ESR1 mutations, or co-occurring alterations in parallel pathways; clinical features such as visceral disease, symptoms, high disease burden, or short duration of benefit from prior therapy; and temporal features such as early emergence of ESR1 mutation relative to treatment initiation.

Under such a framework, patients with low-risk profiles might continue current therapy with close monitoring, patients with intermediate risk might benefit from early endocrine switching while maintaining CDK4/6 inhibition, and patients with high-risk features could be considered for early switch combined with clinical trial enrollment or other escalation strategies. This approach is consistent with the data generated by SERENA-6 but requires prospective validation.

CONCLUSION

SERENA-6 establishes emergent ESR1 mutation detected by ctDNA as an actionable resistance event and demonstrates that early endocrine switching can delay progression without compromising quality of life. However, the absence of mandated postprogression sequencing and immature survival data limit conclusions regarding long-term benefit.

Precision oncology is not about targeting every alteration immediately but about using the right target at the right moment. The question is no longer whether ESR1 mutation matters but how to use this information to individualize care. Future efforts should focus on defining which patients benefit from early intervention, how best to sequence SERDs, and whether molecular surveillance improves outcomes beyond delaying radiographic progression. Until then, ESR1-guided switching should be applied selectively, informed by molecular burden, clinical context, and patient priorities.
