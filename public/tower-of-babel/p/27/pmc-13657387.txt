Guardians of Integrity: Establishing an Independent Artificial Intelligence Monitoring Board
Authors: Yasin Khadem Charvadeh, Yuan Chen, Katherine S Panageas
Published: 2026

The Rising Prevalence and Challenges of Artificial Intelligence in Clinical Research and Practice

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly becoming integral to clinical research and health care. To support its responsible adoption, we propose the establishment of an independent AI Monitoring Board (AIMB), as a dedicated oversight mechanism, analogous to a Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB). Machine learning (ML) algorithms can analyze large, complex data sets to enable more personalized care and improve patient outcomes.1,2 However, the expanding use of AI in health care also raises important ethical, legal, and practical challenges that require robust oversight.3,4

The need for clear standards and guidelines to govern AI tools has been widely emphasized in the literature.5-8 Although there is broad consensus on foundational ethical principles including transparency, justice, nonmaleficence, accountability, and privacy,5 significant challenges remain in translating these principles into routine practice. This gap between ethical theory and implementation stems from several interconnected factors. First, high-level ethical principles are often difficult to operationalize, with considerable variation in how they are interpreted and applied across settings.5,6 This ambiguity is further compounded by the lack of clearly defined goals, strong accountability mechanisms, and validated implementation strategies.6 Second, regulatory frameworks have struggled to keep pace with the rapid evolution of AI, particularly in areas such as data quality, data security, algorithm validation, and accountability for self-learning systems.9

Recent guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has begun addressing these challenges through risk-based approaches to AI governance in health care.8 For example, the FDA's 2025 draft guidance outlines principles for AI use in regulatory decision making, including bias assessment, performance monitoring, transparency, and documentation. However, the guidance remains largely principles-based and provides limited direction on how institutions should operationalize these recommendations. Key implementation questions remain: Who independently verifies that bias assessments are rigorous and ongoing? Who is responsible for monitoring performance after deployment? How should institutions adjudicate trade-offs among fairness, accuracy, and clinical utility? These challenges closely parallel the circumstances that led to DSMBs. Although clinical trial protocols often included safety-monitoring plans, implementation was often inconsistent without independent oversight. DSMBs emerged not because safety principles were lacking, but because dedicated structures were needed to apply them consistently, transparently, and accountably. Similarly, the central challenge in AI governance is no longer defining ethical principles but establishing governance mechanisms that translate them into reliable and trustworthy practice.

These implementation challenges become particularly evident in real-world health care, where ethical principles often collide with practical complexities. AI's reliance on large, high-quality data raises persistent concerns regarding fairness, privacy, data governance, and regulatory compliance.10 Although AI has demonstrated considerable promise in oncology, ensuring equitable performance across diverse patient populations and minimizing algorithmic bias remain major challenges.11 The complexity of these issues is illustrated by sex-based prediction models. The FAIRS (Framework for Algorithm Inclusion Review for Sex) framework by Goodman et al.12 highlights that incorporating sex into predictive algorithms can either mitigate or perpetuate disparities, depending on how such variables are conceptualized and implemented. For example, algorithms predicting complications after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) may classify women as being as higher risk. Although these differences may reflect biologic variation, they may also capture downstream effects of systemic inequalities, including delayed diagnosis, differential referral patterns, or unequal access to higher-quality care. If such predictions are subsequently used to guide treatment decisions, they may inadvertently reinforce or exacerbate the disparities they seek to address. For example, if higher predicted risk discourages surgical intervention among women, the algorithm could perpetuate existing sex-based differences in treatment. Distinguishing clinically meaningful differences from embedded algorithmic bias is one of the central tensions in the implementation ethical AI. Addressing these challenges requires moving beyond high-level ethical principles toward implementation mechanisms that include independent review, transparent reporting of model performance, ongoing monitoring, and accountability throughout an AI system's lifecycle.13 To bridge the gap between principles and practice, we propose an independent AIMB to support the safe, ethical, and effective use of AI in clinical research and health care. Rather than replacing existing regulatory frameworks,14,15 the AIMB would complement them by providing multidisciplinary oversight and a structured approach for evaluating AI systems after deployment.

AI Monitoring Board

The proposed AIMB is modeled on DSMBs, which provide independent oversight of participant safety and trial conduct in clinical research.16 AIMBs could operate as institution-specific boards within large academic medical centers, shared regional boards serving smaller hospitals and community practices, or potentially national bodies. Regardless of structure, their core function would be to uphold safety, fairness, transparency, and accountability. Unlike clinical trials, AI systems undergo a continuous lifecycle that extends from development through deployment and ongoing refinement. This lifecycle includes a pretraining phase involving problem definition, data collection and curation, and model design, followed by a training and testing phase focused on model development, validation, and performance evaluation. The AIMB would oversee these stages to ensure that AI systems are developed, implemented, and monitored according to consistent ethical and scientific standards.

Importantly, the AIMB would not constitute an additional regulatory layer beyond existing FDA guidance but would serve as the institutional mechanism for operationalizing regulatory recommendations. As with DSMBs, the AIMB would translate high-level guidance into routine practice through independent review and ongoing evaluation. Regulatory frameworks define what should be monitored; the AIMB would establish how that monitoring is implemented, verified, and acted upon in specific local contexts.

Pretraining Phase

The pretraining phase establishes the foundation for an AI system by ensuring that data are representative and accurate. Decisions made at this stage are critical to developing models that are fair, reliable, and generalizable to real-world clinical settings.

Data quality and curation are especially important because many AI models are trained on nonrepresentative populations.11 Poorly curated data sets can introduce algorithmic bias,14 measurement error,17,18 missing data,19 or ethical concerns. These biases may reflect systemic inequalities, nonrepresentative sampling, or demographic imbalances in data collection, potentially worsening disparities in diagnosis and treatment for marginalized groups. To reduce these risks, data sets should reflect the intended clinical conditions and patient populations, while addressing measurement error and missingness. Techniques like fairness audits, data augmentation, synthetic data generation, and appropriate data imputation methods can help identify and reduce bias.

Emerging threats extend beyond traditional sources of bias. Data poisoning attacks, for example, intentionally introduce false data into the training set, causing models to learn incorrect associations,20 and as a result, when deployed, the model may make false predictions. Das et al21 demonstrated that compromised clinical large language models (LLMs), like BioGPT, could generate flawed treatment recommendations for breast cancer. To address these risks, the AIMB should establish standards for data validation, data provenance tracking, and security. Data provenance tools can identify unauthorized modifications,22 while blockchain approaches can offer tamper-proof audit trails.23

AIMB oversight during pretraining should be primarily documentation-based and milestone-driven, rather than requiring real-time approval of individual development decisions. Developers should document data sources, curation methods, model selection rationale, and known limitations. The AIMB would review this documentation at key development milestones and before clinical deployment, with the depth of review scaled to application risk. Documentation requirements should be proportionate: early-stage exploratory and pilot studies would require streamlined review to avoid impeding innovation, whereas autonomous clinical decision-support systems would undergo more rigorous predeployment review. For LLMs and foundation models, where comprehensive characterization of training data is often infeasible, oversight should focus on output validation, behavioral testing across diverse clinical scenarios, and postdeployment monitoring. The AIMB would establish documentation and evidence standards appropriate to the model architecture, intended use, and level of clinical risk.

Once data are collected and validated, pretraining proceeds to AI model design: defining the clinical problem, selecting appropriate algorithms, and setting performance goals. Algorithm selection is particularly important in health care, where interpretability affects clinical adoption, trust, and accountability. Although traditional statistical models are often viewed as more interpretable, clinicians frequently rely on model outputs rather than underlying mechanics across model types. As AI systems take on more autonomous clinical roles, developers should incorporate mechanisms to explain or justify recommendations, whether through inherently interpretable models or post hoc explanation methods.

Training and Testing Phase

During training and testing, the AI model is trained on curated data, tuned, and evaluated for real-world performance. This phase is critical for ensuring the algorithm development meet standards for safety, fairness, and accuracy while identifying bias, overfitting, and other performance limitations. Models should be optimized and tested on representative data sets, with fairness metrics used to assess performance across diverse populations.

Like the pretraining phase, modern risks such as evasion attacks also threaten the integrity of the testing phase in ML models.24 Evasion attacks involve adversaries subtly manipulating input data to force the model to produce incorrect results. During testing, when models are evaluated on data meant to represent real-world scenarios, such manipulations can lead to faulty conclusions about the model's performance and reliability. Effective external oversight can help detect and mitigate adversarial manipulation of test data, safeguarding the evaluation process.

AI systems may require updating or retraining as clinical environments change, data drift, where the statistical shifts in data properties over time can reduce model accuracy and fairness, introduces new biases. Although continual learning enhances adaptability, frequent large-scale updates can hinder thorough oversight. Incremental, controlled updates help maintain manageable reevaluation schedules. The reevaluation process should replicate initial assessments, focusing on fairness, bias detection, and alignment with objectives. By embedding these checks into the retraining lifecycle, the risks introduced by evolving data and operational changes can be effectively managed without requiring constant surveillance.

Establishing a Multidisciplinary AIMB

Given the complexity and risks of AI-driven medical technologies, the AIMB should be multidisciplinary, drawing on expertise needed to support robust oversight, as emphasized in prior work.25-27 This approach would help balance technical innovation with patient safety and fairness. Ideally, the AIMB would include clinicians, ethicists, patient advocates, statisticians, AI experts, legal and policy experts, and technology developers and regulatory specialists. At a minimum, the AIMB should include quantitative expertise to assess model reliability, fairness, and robustness. A de minimis structure should comprise three members: a clinician with domain expertise, a statistical scientist, and an AI/data science expert. This triad ensures representation of clinical context, methodologic rigor, and technical depth.

Algorithm Ownership, Proprietary Evaluation, and Balancing Transparency With Commercial Interests

Algorithm ownership and proprietary evaluation are critical in clinical research and health care. When AI algorithms are codeveloped by universities, technology companies, and health care providers, clear ownership documentation is essential. The AIMB can ensure compliance with licensing agreements and evaluate proprietary ML models by testing outputs against benchmarks without needing full code access. Balancing transparency with commercial interests is essential. Although full disclosure may not always be feasible, providing sufficient transparency to support regulatory review and approval can help build trust among stakeholders. Compliance with GDPR and HIPAA protects patient data, and ethical considerations like accessibility and reducing health care disparities must be prioritized.

Certification and Scalable Implementation

A national certification infrastructure could complement institutional AIMBs and strengthen AI oversight. Modeled on programs such as ONC Health IT Certification Program, this framework could establish standardized benchmarks and tiered certification levels based on the AI system risk and complexity, institutional AIMBs would then assess how certified tools perform in their specific local context and patient populations. Challenges to such an approach include ensuring affordability and accessibility for smaller health systems, preventing test recognition attacks that allow systems to game certification scenarios, and maintaining certification current as models evolve. Despite these challenges, a layered approach combining national certification with institutional AIMB oversight offers a feasible path toward comprehensive AI governance.

In conclusion, an AIMB can help ensure that clinical AI algorithms are safe, effective, ethical, and legally compliant, protecting patient welfare while supporting responsible innovation. As AI becomes more widespread, it is essential to advance fairly and transparently, focused on improving patient care, particularly for higher risk applications. Through rigorous review, the AIMB can foster innovation uphold ethical standards and strengthen clinician and public confidence in AI's role in medicine.
