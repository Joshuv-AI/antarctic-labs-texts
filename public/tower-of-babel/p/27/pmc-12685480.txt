Fourth‐Generation Ceramic‐On‐Ceramic THA With Anatomic and Tapered Femoral Stems: 11‐Year Follow‐Up
Authors: Liang Yongjian, Li Hao, Jun Fu, Guoqiang Zhang, Libo Hao, Wei Chai, Erlong Niu, Jiying Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTotal hip arthroplasty (THA) with Ceramic‐on‐Ceramic (CoC) components achieved excellent outcomes. However, the long‐term outcomes of anatomic and tapered stems are controversial in clinical practice, and the difference in the survival rates between the tapered stems and anatomical stems over the long term remains unknown.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed to evaluate the 11‐year follow‐up outcomes of anatomic and tapered femoral stems. Between January 2009 and December 2011, a total of 1438 patients with COC were included in this study initially. Among these hips, 30 patients (30 hips) experienced death, and 254 hips (17.6%) were lost to follow‐up. Finally, a total of 591 hips with Corail stem and a total of 334 hips with Ribbed stems were included in this study. The outcomes were evaluated by the modified Harris hip score (mHHS), the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), and a questionnaire assessing articular noises.ResultsFor the Corail stem, the survival rate with aseptic loosening or revision of any component for any reason as the endpoint was 99.1% at 11 years. The survival rate with reoperation for any reason as the endpoint was 98.8% at 11 years. For the Ribbed stem, the survival rate with aseptic loosening or revision of any component for any reason as the endpoint was 98.8% at 11 years. In patients with the Corail stem, the preoperative modified Harris hip score (mHHS) score, with a mean of 43.8 points, significantly improved to a mean of 93.5 points at the final follow‐up assessment (p < 0.001). In patients with the ribbed stem, the preoperative mHHS score, with a mean of 40.9 points, significantly improved to a mean of 92.8 points at the final follow‐up assessment (p < 0.001) during the follow‐up period. The incidence of squeaking and squaking in the Corail group was significantly higher than that in the ribbed group (squeaking: 22.7% vs. 6.9%; squaking: 17.4% vs. 4.2%). The incidence of postoperative thigh pain was 4% in patients with the Corail stem, significantly lower than that in patients with the ribbed stem (17.4% vs. 4%; p < 0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, CoC THA with Corail and Ribbed stems exhibits excellent clinical outcomes at the long‐term follow‐up. However, the incidence of postoperative thigh pain in the Ribbed group is significantly higher than that in the Corail group, while the incidence of squeaking was lower.

Background

Despite the success of total hip arthroplasty (THA) in addressing debilitating hip conditions, a significant challenge impacting its long‐term success is polyethylene (PE) wear and particle‐induced osteolysis, leading to aseptic loosening and eventual prosthesis failure [1, 2]. To mitigate these issues and prolong implant lifespan, advancements in bearing materials have been made, including the introduction of highly cross‐linked polyethylene and ceramics [3, 4, 5]. Previous research has showcased the effectiveness of third‐generation alumina ceramic (BIOLOX forte; CeramTec, Plochingen, Germany) in reducing osteolysis; however, concerns persist regarding potential fractures and articular noise [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

In the early 2000s, the fourth‐generation ceramic known as BIOLOX delta (produced by CeramTec) was introduced, which incorporated zirconia into the alumina matrix to mitigate complications after THA [11]. This innovative ceramic material exhibited enhanced tribological properties, including low surface roughness, exceptional hardness, scratch resistance, and high wettability in laboratory tests. Consequently, it facilitated the use of larger diameter femoral heads in conjunction with thinner acetabular liners, ultimately enhancing the range of motion and reducing the incidence of dislocation [12].

In accordance with the fundamental principles of cementless femoral stem design, the key geometric rationales can be categorized into three distinct types: anatomic, straight, and tapered [13] designs. Anatomic stems aim to closely mimic femoral cavity contours, but anatomical variations and post‐implantation local stress distribution directly affect postoperative femur‐stem integration, bone remodeling, and mechanical transmission efficiency—potentially causing hip prosthesis aseptic loosening.

The Ribbed prosthesis (Ribbed Hip system, Waldemar Link, Hamburg, Germany), specifically, adopts an anatomical S‐shaped design to facilitate the insertion of the largest feasible stem size while minimizing rotational forces that could compromise prosthetic stability [14, 15]. Furthermore, the stem incorporates deep grooves to enhance its elastic modulus, mitigating the stress shielding effect or excessive stiffness of the proximal femur caused by the metallic implant. Additionally, an anchoring screw can be threaded through a bore in the lateral fin and into the greater trochanter, serving to alleviate compressive loads on the calcar during the initial postoperative period [15]. Today, the merits of utilizing HA‐coated implants for cementless fixation are widely acknowledged. In 1986, the notion of utilizing an extensive hydroxyapatite (HA) coating for the secure fixation of a tapered femoral stem (Corail) was introduced. The Corail stem, now in its third‐generation iteration, offers a comprehensive range of sizes spanning from 95 to 170 mm and incorporates a stepped geometry design. This innovative feature is intended to enhance compression loading, diminish shear forces, and expand the range of motion, thereby optimizing performance [16].

To our knowledge, no prior cohort studies have conducted a long‐term comparative analysis of the outcomes associated with these two distinct types of femoral stems, anatomic and tapered designs. Ribbed and Corail are both coated with hydroxyapatite while the geometric rationales are different. To compare the long‐term outcomes of the anatomic and tapered stems, we used these two types of stems as the samples. With the same CoC (BIOLOX delta) and acetabular compartments, we performed a comprehensive investigation to assess [1] the difference in the 11‐year follow‐up survival rates of Ceramic‐on‐Ceramic (CoC) total hip arthroplasty (THA) between tapered stems and anatomical stems; [2] the difference in the clinical outcomes between the tapered stems and anatomical stems.

Materials and Results

Patients

After obtaining approval from the Ethics Institutional Review Board of the Chinese People's Liberation Army General Hospital, we conducted a retrospective analysis of our institution's THA registry (IRB number: S2021‐066‐01). This registry meticulously tracks all patients who have undergone total hip arthroplasty (THA), with standardized postoperative follow‐up visits scheduled at 3, 6, and 12 months post‐surgery, followed by subsequent evaluations every 2 to 3 years. In cases where patients were unable to attend in‐person follow‐up appointments, they were instructed to submit a validated outcomes form and radiographs via mail.

Surgical Technique

All surgical procedures were carried out by a team of four highly experienced senior surgeons within a single medical facility. General anesthesia was administered, and the posterolateral approach was employed for the surgeries. Each patient received a cementless hip prosthesis, equipped with a BIOLOX Delta CoC bearing (manufactured by CeramTec, Germany). The acetabular components, which were also cementless (Betacup, LINK, Germany), were implanted using the press‐fit technique, without the use of any additional screws. For the femoral components, either Ribbed or Corail, the femoral medullary cavity was first reamed, followed by the press‐fit implantation of the components. Depending on their individual postoperative evaluations, patients were gradually allowed to bear full weight, typically after a minimum period of 6 to 8 weeks.

Functional Assessments

At each visit, the modified Harris hip score (mHHS) was documented, alongside the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index score [17]. During the final follow‐up session, a dedicated evaluation was conducted to assess the presence of squeaking or any other noises emanating from the joints. This assessment encompassed a thorough examination of the incidence, type, frequency, intensity, and temporal pattern of the noises, as well as the specific activities that triggered their occurrence, utilizing a questionnaire [18].

Radiographic Evaluation of Acetabular Components

The postoperative and most recent radiographs were scrutinized by two impartial investigators who had no involvement in the initial surgical procedures. The baseline for all comparative analyses was established using the antero‐posterior (AP) and lateral pelvic radiographic views obtained 3 days after surgery. The inclination and anteversion angles of the acetabular cup were precisely measured utilizing OrthoView software (version 7.0.3, Materialize, Leuven, Belgium). Furthermore, the presence of radiolucencies and osteolysis around the acetabular cups was evaluated in accordance with the DeLee and Charnley zonal classification system [19], while similar assessments for the femoral components were conducted using the zonal classification proposed by [20].

Follow‐Up Endpoint

The aseptic loosening or revision of any component for any reason was set as the primary endpoint. The reoperation for any reason was set as the secondary endpoint.

Statically Analysis

Based on the nature of the data, variables were categorized into continuous and dichotomous types. For continuous variables, a normal distribution test was employed to assess their distribution. If the data followed a normal distribution, they were summarized using means; otherwise, medians were calculated. Depending on the fulfillment of specific conditions, either the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test or the student's t‐test was utilized to detect differences among groups. Dichotomous data were presented as frequencies and compared using the chi‐squared test. Following these transformations, logistic regression analyses were conducted. Kaplan‐Meier survivorship analysis and Cox multivariable regression were used to evaluate the survivorship. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM; version 26.0), and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients' Characteristics

A total of 591 hips with Corail stem and a total of 334 hips with Ribbed stems were included in this study. The inclusion process of patients was summarized in Figure 1. There were 213 (36%) and 119 (35.6%) women in the Corail and Ribbed cohort, respectively. The age in the Corail cohort was 67.7 ± 13.5 years and the age in the Ribbed cohort was 69.4 ± 13.0 years (P < 0.0001). The body mass index (BMI) was 24 ± 4 kg/m2 and 24 ± 4 kg/m2 in the Corail and Ribbed cohort, respectively. The constitution ratio of Ankylosing spondylitis and Osteoarthritis in the Corail group was significantly higher than that in the Ribbed group (Ankylosing spondylitis: 22% VS 10.2% p < 0.0001; Osteoarthritis: 10.3% VS 6%, p = 0.025). The constitution ratio of Osteonecrosis in the Ribbed group was significantly higher than that in the Corail group (46.5% vs. 57.5%; p = 0.001). The constitution ratio of ASA scores (grade 2) in the Ribbed group was significantly higher than that in the Corail group (75.1% vs 67.7%, p = 0.017). The constitution ratio of ASA scores (grade 3) in the Corail group was significantly higher than that in the Ribbed group (8.8% VS 4.5%, p = 0.015). The constitution ratio of 36 mm femoral head (grade 3) in the Corail group was significantly higher than that in the Ribbed group (97.5% VS 93.7%, p = 0.005). Survival Analysis

The details about the demographic characteristics of the Corail and Ribbed groups were summarized in Table 1. In the Ribbed group, over an average follow‐up period of 118 months, a total of 7 reoperations were performed. In the Ribbed group, over an average follow‐up period of 114 months, a total of 2 reoperations were performed.

For the Corail stem, the survival rate with aseptic loosening or revision of any component for any reason as the endpoint was 99.1% at 11 years (See Figure 2A). The survival rate with reoperation for any reason as the endpoint was 98.8% at 11 years (See Figure 2B). For the Ribbed stem, the survival rate with aseptic loosening or revision of any component for any reason as the endpoint was 98.8% at 11 years (See Figure 2A). The survival rate with reoperation for any reason as the endpoint was 99.4% at 11 years (See Figure 2B). For the 2 endpoints, no significant difference in the survivorship was observed between the Corail and Ribbed groups (Reoperation: p = 0.248; Aseptic loosening or revision: p = 0.743).

The mean WOMAC score at the final follow‐up was 14.8 points (Tables 2 and 3). Additionally, the range of motion (ROM) improved from a preoperative average of 65.2 degrees to 114.5 degrees at the final follow‐up (p < 0.001, Table 4). The incidence of postoperative thigh pain was 4% in patients with Corail stem, significantly lower than that in patients with ribbed stem (17.4% vs. 4%; p < 0.001).

Radiological Assessment

In Corail group, the average inclination among the 591 hips was 42.5 degrees and the average anteversion was 13.8 degrees. In Ribbed group, the average inclination among the 334 hips was 43.4 degrees and the average anteversion was 12.6 degrees. All 925 hips, which represent the entire patient cohort, exhibited satisfactory bony ingrowth of the acetabular component. There was no observed migration or osteolysis of the acetabular component in any of the patients.

Radiolucent lines adjacent to the Corail stem were seen in 71 hips (12.7%), including 67 hips in Gruen Zone 1, 4 hips in both Gruen zone 7 and Gruen zone 1. Radiolucent lines adjacent to the Ribbed stem were seen in 5 hips (1.5%), including 3 hips in Gruen Zone 1, 2 hips in both Gruen zone 7 and Gruen zone 1. Radiolucent lines were < 2 mm and did not progress in any hip.

In Corail group, heterotopic ossification was seen in 14 hips, including 7 hips in Brooker grade 1, 1 hip in Brooker grade 2 and 6 hips in Brooker grade 3. In Ribbed group, heterotopic ossification was seen in 8 hips, including 4 hips in Brooker grade 1, 2 hip in Brooker grade 2 and 3 hips in Brooker grade 3. Lesser trochanter bone resorption was seen in 6 hips (1.8%) with Ribbed stems. In hips with Corail stem, no Lesser trochanter bone resorption was observed. Discussion

In this study, we have revealed the long‐term follow‐up outcomes of CoC hips implanted with either tapered stems (Corail) or anatomical stems (Ribbed). To our knowledge, this is the first study comprehensively comparing the long‐time outcomes of tapered and anatomical in a large CoC cohort with the same bearings and acetabular components. The same type of bearings and acetabular components also increases the robustness of our comparison results potentially. CoC THA with Corail and Ribbed stems exhibits excellent survival and clinical outcomes at the long‐term follow‐up. The incidence of postoperative thigh pain in Ribbed group is significantly higher than that in the Corail group while the incidence of squeaking was lower. Despite that no revisions were performed because of squeaking or thigh pain in our cohorts, the outcomes of the types of femoral stems should be taken into account before chosen.

CoC THA with Corail and Ribbed stems exhibits excellent clinical outcomes. Some literature reports that the first generation of anatomical stems performed poorly [13]. In our study, regardless of the LCU stem or the Corail stems, during the 11‐year follow‐up, the patients' hip pain and limited mobility symptoms were significantly relieved after total hip arthroplasty. In the Ribbed group, the HSS scores were improved from 72.4 points to 92.8 points after THA and the ROM was improved to 92.8° postoperatively. In the Ribbed group, the HSS scores were improved from 72.4 points to 92.8 points after THA and the ROM was improved to 92.8° postoperatively. In the Corail group, the HSS scores were improved to 92.8 points and the ROM was improved to 92.8° postoperatively. Moreover, the survival rate with aseptic loosening or revision of any component for any reason as the endpoint was comparable in the LCU group and Corail group. This indicates that the overall outcomes of LCU and Corail stems are similar in the 10‐year follow‐up period. Heekin et al. studied 100 hips managed with a cobalt‐chromium anatomic prosthesis with sintered porous coating and found a 5% failure rate by 5 years, with 15% of the patients having thigh pain [21]. Kim et al. found a clinical failure rate of 9% and thigh pain in 28% (32 of 116 hips) at a mean of 6 years [22]. This indicates the advancement of anatomical stems in recent years. The differences in prognosis are mainly concentrated in minor aspects such as thigh pain, squeaking, and the locations of radiolucent lines revealed by radiographic assessment [23, 24].

The Incidence of Squeaking in Corail Group Was Higher Than That in the Ribbed Group

The squeaking hip is a unique phenomenon observed only in total hip replacements that utilize hard‐on‐hard bearings. In our study, we observed that the morphology of the femoral stem can be a cause of squeaking hip after THA. The incidence of squeaking in the Corail group was significantly higher than that in the Ribbed group. This may be related to the fact that the anatomical stems can better adapt to the morphology of the femur compared to the biological stems [15]. In this study, no patients considered revisions because of squeaking, and no association was observed between the presence of squeaking and impaired hip function, which is different from previous reports. Although implant‐dependent, surgeons should counsel patients about the potential for a higher risk of squeaking in CoC THA with tapered stems (Corail stem), which may occur years after the procedure [18, 25].

The Incidence of Thigh Pain in Ribbed Group Was Higher Than That in the Corail Group

In this study, neither stem type is completely free of thigh pain or stress‐shielding in the follow‐up period. However, compared to patients with Corail stem, patients with Ribbed stems are more likely to suffer from thigh pain after total hip arthroplasty in the long‐term follow‐up period. The literature indicates that thigh pain is a symptom of bone resorption [26, 27]. Moreover, this may be related to the micro‐motion between the bone‐prosthesis interface caused by the larger surface area of the Ribbed stems compared to that of Corail stems. Inconsistent with previous literature, the anatomical stems (Ribbed) were associated with a higher rate of thigh pain [24]. Hence, modifications to the stem design to enhance rotational stability and a better understanding of cementless fixation have the potential to lead to improved outcomes.

The rates and locations of femoral stem‐associated loosening were different in these two types of femoral stems. In patients with Ribbed stems, trochanter bone resorption was more likely to be observed. In patients with Corail stems, radiolucent lines in the Gruen zone 1 were more likely to happen. This is related to bone remodeling caused by the stress shielding effect, which was various in different types of stem designs. In the femur with the Ribbed stem, the stress transferred to the lesser trochanter is weaker than that with the Corail stem. While in the femur with the Corail stem, the stem transferred to the bone around Gruen zone 1 is weaker than that with the Ribbed stem.

Considering the potential loss to follow‐up (attrition bias), a sensitivity analysis was performed. Firstly, patients who died and were dropped out were included in the sensitivity analysis. Then, Worst/Best‐Case Scenario Analysis was performed to test the robustness of the results. We used the incidence of squeaking and thigh pain after THA as the tested outcomes, and we found that the incidence of squeaking in the Corail cohort was higher than that in the Ribbed cohort whether in the Worst‐case scenario analysis or in the Best‐Case Scenario Analysis. Besides, similar to our previous results, the incidence of thigh pain in the Ribbed group was significantly higher than that in the Ribbed cohort in the Worst/Best‐Case Scenario Analysis. Taken together, these results further enhance the robustness of our conclusions through sensitivity analysis (Appendix 1). Ribbed stem is a type of anatomic stem, which can match the femoral cavity tightly and cause higher pressure in the femoral cavity. Elevated pressure within the femoral cavity and the stress transferred to the distal femoral stem may be the cause of thigh pain after THA with Ribbed stem. Lower survival of anatomical stems can be associated with the stress shielding and bone resorption in the proximal femur, which can cause loosening and sunken potentially. Hence, based on our results, we can suggest that operators can choose anatomical stems (Ribbed stems) in patients who cannot suffer from noise after total joint arthroplasty.

Another concern associated with the ceramic bearings is ceramic fractures. Previous studies reported that the rate of fracture for ceramics of the third generation was 0.18% while it was 0.0013% for the fourth‐generation ceramics. No fracture of the fourth‐generation ceramics was observed in our cohort at a mean of 11 years follow‐up, which is similar to the findings from Kim et al. [28]. While some surgeons reported the incidence of ceramic fractures during liner insertion, it indicates that the ceramic fractures of fourth‐generation ceramics are very rare clinically, but those associated with liner mal‐seating are concerning, and particular care should be taken to avoid incomplete seating during liner insertion [29].

There are still some limitations in this study. Firstly, loss to follow‐up remains a concern in this long‐term follow‐up study. There was a 12% missing rate in this study. However, our missing rate is similar to that of other 10‐year follow‐up studies (70% to 93%, mean: 82%) [30]. This limitation has the potential to bias our clinical results. Secondly, this is a single‐center follow‐up study and the results need to be further confirmed in other joint centers. Hence, the generalizability of the study results to other medical centers needs further validation. Thirdly, there was selection bias in the retrospective study when the patients were included. The preference of different operators for different stems at different times remains. This bias can potentially influence our results. Lastly, the further long‐term clinical outcomes of the Corail and Ribbed stems can be evaluated in the future, which can illuminate the outcomes of Corail and Ribbed stems over a longer period.

In conclusion, CoC THA with Corail and Ribbed stems exhibits excellent survival and clinical outcomes at long‐term follow‐up. We believe that delta ceramic bearings continue to provide an option for the young and more active patient. The incidence of postoperative thigh pain in the Ribbed group is significantly higher than that in the Corail group, while the incidence of squeaking was lower. Despite that, no revisions were performed because of squeaking or thigh pain only in our cohorts; the outcomes of the types of femoral stems should be taken into account before being chosen.
