Trajectory of Kidney Function in T1D over Time: A Scoping Review
Authors: Favian Co, Reid Whitlock, Arvind Katta, Carolina Aldworth, Jon Mares, Zihe Zheng, Mauricio Ferri, Nicole Askin, Navdeep Tangri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 1 diabetes (T1D) is associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Contemporary data on the natural history of kidney function decline and the effect of therapies on slowing disease progression in T1D-related CKD are limited. We conducted a scoping review to describe the natural trajectory of kidney function and its effects in individuals with T1D in the USA.MethodsIn our scoping review of observational studies and grey literature, we searched EMBASE (Ovid), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature [CINAHL; Elton B. Stephens Company (EBSCO)], MEDLINE (Ovid), Scopus (Elsevier), Global Health (Ovid), and the Food and Drug Administration from inception to 25 April 2025. The study population included adults (age ≥ 18 years) with T1D in the USA. Our outcomes were change in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), MACE (myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular death, unstable angina, or heart failure hospitalization), and healthcare resource use (HCRU). We screened 6437 abstracts and selected 22 texts that matched our criteria.ResultsIn one cohort, more than 50.0% of individuals with T1D developed moderately increased albuminuria (UACR > 30–299 mg/g) after 20 years and two cohort studies in T1D observed a progressive annual eGFR decline of 3 mL/min/1.73 m2. Five cohort studies found higher proportions of individuals with T1D experienced MACE or kidney failure versus those without T1D. Cross-sectional studies estimated total annual cost of HCRU in the USA at $17–27.8 billion for those with T1D, with annual per-patient costs ranging from $73,534–$112,833 for kidney failure and $40,889–$86,417 for components of MACE.ConclusionsT1D is associated with a high burden of CKD, MACE, and healthcare costs. A clearer understanding of the trajectory of kidney function decline in T1D would help providers and policymakers understand the impact potential therapies to treat T1D-related CKD may have on reducing the risk of kidney failure, MACE, and HCRU.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13300-026-01899-9.Keywords: Type 1 diabetes, Natural history, UACR, eGFR, MACE, Pharmacotherapies

Key Summary Points

A significant proportion of individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D) will develop moderately increased albuminuria.Individuals with T1D experience a higher progressive annual kidney function decline than the general population.Patients with T1D will experience a high burden of major adverse cardiovascular events, healthcare costs, and resource use.A clearer understanding of the trajectory of kidney function decline in T1D would help providers and policymakers understand the impact potential therapies to treat T1D-related chronic kidney disease may have on reducing the risk of kidney failure and major adverse cardiovascular events.

Introduction

Diabetes is distinguished by hyperglycemia, which is driven by complications in insulin secretion, insulin activity, or both [1]. The two main classes of diabetes are type 1 diabetes (T1D), and (2) type 2 diabetes (T2D) [2]. T1D is normally diagnosed in childhood and caused by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells [3]. Patients with T1D must be administered exogenous insulin. In contrast, T2D is normally diagnosed in adulthood and is marked by insulin resistance and/or unusual insulin secretion [4]. Patients with T2D may require exogenous insulin if blood glucose levels cannot be controlled by dietary means or oral hypoglycemic agents [5].

Both T1D and T2D are associated with microvascular and macrovascular complications, and increased risk of mortality [6, 7]. Microvascular complications affect several tissues, including the kidneys. However, macrovascular complications affect arteries and veins, including the cardiovascular system [8].

Diabetes is the primary cause of kidney failure [9]. Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) occurs in 20–40% of individuals with T1D and is characterized by albuminuria, reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), high blood pressure, and higher cardiovascular risk [9, 10].

Overall, there is a paucity of data on the natural history of changes in kidney function in those with T1D compared with T2D as well as fewer treatment options to slow CKD progression. Studies have reported that approximately 85% of patients with DKD and moderately increased albuminuria [urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) > 30–299 mg/g] develop severely increased albuminuria (UACR ≥ 300 mg/g) within the next 10–15 years [11]. Further, 50% of individuals with T1D and severely increased albuminuria progress to kidney failure within 10 years, and at least 75% within 20 years, compared with only 20% of those with T2D and macroalbuminuria [12, 13]. Although the association between T1D and CVD is well known [14, 15], the specific rate and trajectory of decline in kidney function over time in that association is less understood.

Understanding the natural history of kidney function decline in patients with T1D can help healthcare providers and policymakers identify clinical phenotypes based on patterns of T1D-related CKD and devise treatment plans that slow or halt kidney function decline. As such, we conducted a scoping review to investigate the natural history, cardiovascular burden, regulatory considerations, and health care resource use (HRCU) outcomes associated with CKD in individuals with T1D in the USA.

Methods

Literature Search

Articles published on EMBASE (Ovid), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature [CINAHL; Elton B. Stephens Company (EBSCO)], MEDLINE (Ovid), Scopus (Elsevier), and Global Health (Ovid) were captured. Additionally, grey literature published by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) was included. The search included articles published from inception to 25 April 2025. We adapted a search strategy from our prepared protocol which included keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to type 1 diabetes, urine albumin to creatinine ratio, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. The full search strategy used for each database is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Study Selection

Citations were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) adults (age ≥ 18 years) with T1D and (2) observational studies in a USA setting. We also included materials from our search of the FDA related to pharmacological safety in T1D-related CKD. We excluded articles where the study population was on kidney replacement therapy. There were no restrictions due to language or sample size.

We aimed to describe temporal changes in kidney function in patients with T1D and the associated cardiovascular events and HCRU. Our outcomes included (1) changes in eGFR, (2) changes in UACR, (3) major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and (4) HCRU including costs of treatment, hospitalizations, and emergency room visits. We defined MACE as a composite outcome of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, cardiovascular death, unstable angina, or heart failure hospitalization [16]. We also evaluated the individual components of MACE. Additionally, we sought citations that compared all outcomes in T1D to those with T2D.

Once the search results were inputted into Covidence by the medical librarian, one reviewer (F.C.) screened through the abstracts and selected articles for full text review. From there, the full texts were reviewed to be selected for data extraction. The reviewer consulted with the senior author (N.T.) before finalizing all articles for inclusion at each stage.

Data Extraction

We extracted the author, title, journal, year of publication, study setting and duration, diagnosis of diabetes, and relevant outcomes with magnitude of change and association.

Ethics Statement

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

The citations were imported into Covidence. The search captured 10,190 citations. After removing duplicates, 6437 abstracts were screened. In addition to the captured citations, three articles were manually added. Following full-text screening, 22 articles satisfied the inclusion criteria. Discussion

In this scoping review of the natural trajectory of kidney function and its effects in individuals with T1D in the USA, we found consistent themes that showed many patients with T1D experience significant increases in UACR and decreases in eGFR throughout their diabetes duration which over time may lead to experiencing kidney failure and MACE. These are contributing factors to an environment where the estimated annual HCRU cost of individuals with T1D is $27.8 billion in the USA. Notable findings include cohort studies which have found that more than 50% of individuals with T1D develop moderately increased albuminuria after 20 years, a progressive annual decline of eGFR in individuals with T1D up to 3 times higher than what would normally be expected [19, 22, 23], and high rates of MACE. The higher rates of kidney failure and cardiovascular events in patients with T1D contribute to high per-person healthcare costs and total HCRU costs of $18–28 billion a year in the USA. These facts underscore the urgency to ensure individuals with T1D have access to therapies that can slow the decline of kidney function and reduce the risk of MACE.

Our review could not find direct comparisons of kidney function decline trajectory between T1D and T2D in an American setting. One observational study found that individuals with diabetes using insulin experienced a greater decline in eGFR relative compared to individuals with diabetes using oral medications (unadjusted: −1.8 versus −0.7 mL/min/1.73 m2; adjusted: −1.1 versus −0.3 mL/min/1.73 m2) [25]. Although this may be an indicator that CKD progresses faster in those with T1D versus T2D, there was not enough information in that study to make that conclusion, as the proportion of those with diabetes using insulin who were diagnosed with T1D was not provided. A Danish study found similar trajectories in T1D and T2D that were higher than a normal annual decline of 1 mL/min/1.73 m2. The mean annual eGFR declines in patients without albuminuria, moderately increased albuminuria, and severely increased albuminuria after 10 years of CKD G3 diagnosis were 1.9, 2.3, and 3.3 mL/min/1.73 m2 in patients with T1D and 1.9, 2.1, and 3.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 in patients with T2D, respectively [40].

Over the past decade, more novel therapies, including GLP-1 receptor agonists, nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and SGLT-2 inhibitors have been proven to slow the progression of kidney function and reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in individuals with CKD and T2D [41–46]. However, their safety and effectiveness in T1D have yet to be firmly established. There are key differences in the pathophysiology of the types of diabetes due to the complete inability of those with T1D to produce insulin, such as increased hepatic glucose production, suppressed capacity of the kidneys to absorb glucose, and inhibited lipid and protein metabolism [47]. Additionally, T2D represents over 90% of all patients with diabetes [48] and therefore attracts more investment in research compared to T1D.

T1D is also associated with an increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis, other autoimmune diseases, and severe hypoglycemia compared with T2D, which manifests in more cautious approaches to safety in T1D [49–51]. For example, current FDA guidance issues warnings for SGLT2 inhibitor use in patients with T1D due to the increased risk for diabetic ketoacidosis [35, 38]. However, it is promising that phase 3 clinical trials evaluating new medications in T1D and CKD are either ongoing [52, 53] or have been recently completed, such as the FINE-ONE trial investigating finerenone and participants with T1D and CKD, which found that finerenone was associated with a 25% greater reduction of UACR after 6 months compared with placebo [54].

Our scoping review showed that the early stages of CKD can quickly progress to major adverse kidney and cardiovascular events in patients with T1D. In one study, 33% of individuals progressed to severely increased albuminuria from moderately increased albuminuria over 10 years [55]. Whereas another study found that, in patients with T1D and diabetes diagnosis younger than 30 years of age, 8.7% of patients with T1D and CKD progressed to kidney failure over 6 years [21]. These results are consistent with results found in two analyses of retrospective cohorts of adults with T1D. One study found that 8% developed CKD over a median duration of 24 years, and another found that 9% of those with rapid decline of eGFR developed CKD in at least 6 years [56, 57]. Both studies showed a significant number of individuals with rapid eGFR decline.

Our scoping review also found those with T1D and CKD experienced higher rates heart failure, angina, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular events, and coronary heart disease than those without. Furthermore, there was evidence to suggest a higher likelihood of sudden cardiac arrest for those with T1D compared with T2D. However, the nature of the relationship between annual eGFR decline and MACE could not be quantified. An analysis of the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) attempted to analyze this temporal relationship. The study results showed that early rapid eGFR loss in T1D was predictive of CKD, but not MACE, in later years [58].

Longer diabetes duration, with moderately or severely increased albuminuria, was associated with higher mortality risk from cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, and stroke relative to patients without albuminuria in both T1D and T2D, possibly indicative of a biomarker as a risk factor for MACE for both types of diabetes [59, 60]. Similarly, a low eGFR forecasted the risk of heart failure endpoints [26]. In an analysis of the Swedish Diabetes Registry where 2.4% of individuals with T1D with a mean age of 41 years were hospitalized with heart failure (HF) over a median of 7 years, the risk of hospitalization for HF was at least twofold higher at eGFR between 45 and 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and at least threefold higher at eGFR less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 relative to an eGFR of at least 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 [61]. In a study of patients with T1D in Finland, the relative risks for CHD, stroke, and any CVD in those with CKD versus those without were 10.3, 10.9, and 10.0, respectively, over a 24-year follow-up period [62].

Clinical variables such as hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) and hypoglycemia are vital endpoints that reflect the therapeutic benefits of new T1D therapies [63]. Regulatory assessments prioritize these outcomes, as an HbA1C of less than 7% is associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular mortality [64]. However, hypoglycemia remains a critical safety endpoint [36] that can cause sweating, shakiness, dizziness, blurred vision, confusion, and in extreme cases, seizures, coma, or death [65]. Therefore, understanding the balance between achieving glycemic control and minimizing hypoglycemia is essential for the safe management of T1D.

While the above endpoints are crucial, managing T1D with insulin replacement therapy presents significant challenges. Insulin replacement therapies are modalities to control blood glucose levels in individuals with T1D [66]. Hypoglycemia is the predominant adverse event in insulin therapy [67]. Additionally, insulin promotes weight gain, which may decrease the dosage of insulin in individuals with T1D [67]. Noninsulin adjunct medications should (1) mitigate hypoglycemia, (2) prevent insulin-driven weight gain, and (3) promote cardiovascular outcomes [68]. The prospective adjunct therapies for patients with T1D and CKD are nonsteroidal MRAs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) [69, 70].

Our search of FDA literature captured SGLT2 inhibitors but not nonsteroidal MRAs or GLP-1 RAs. There are data available for some of these medications in T1D. A meta-analysis of sotagliflozin as adjunct therapy in patients with T1D reported a reduction in blood glucose levels, blood pressure, and weight; however, the risk of diabetic ketoacidosis needs to be assessed [71]. Similarly, dapagliflozin adjunct therapy in patients with T1D decreased HbA1C and weight; however, diabetic ketoacidosis was higher [72]. Empagliflozin reduced HbA1C [73–75] and weight [73–75], ketoacidosis increased with higher dosage [73], and it did not contribute to increasing the risk for hypoglycemia [75]. A meta-analysis of liraglutide as adjunct treatment in T1D reported reductions in HbA1C, weight, and risk of severe hypoglycemia; however, the odds for nausea and vomiting increased [76]. Additionally, liraglutide was not associated with diabetic ketoacidosis [76]. When exenatide was used as adjunct therapy in patients with T1D, gastrointestinal adverse events, primarily nausea, were reported [77].

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to assess the natural longitudinal trajectory of kidney function and UACR in individuals with T1D in the USA. This scoping review has limitations. Many of the studies we assessed did not differentiate between T1D or T2D or intentionally combined data in individuals with T1D and T2D. As a result, we had to exclude studies where we could not report on T1D specifically. Additionally, we could not definitively quantify the extent to which the burden of CKD, MACE, and HCRU is increased in individuals with T1D compared with the general population due to the limited availability of direct longitudinal comparative evidence. However, the available evidence does suggest that individuals with T1D are disproportionately affected by faster progression of CKD and its downstream effects including MACE and healthcare costs.

Conclusions

T1D is associated with a high burden of CKD, MACE, and healthcare costs, which underscores the need for therapies to treat T1D-related CKD. A clearer understanding of the natural trajectory of the loss of kidney function in patients with T1D would help providers and policymakers better understand the long-term impact potential therapies to treat CKD may have on reducing the risk of kidney failure, MACE, and HCRU in this population.
