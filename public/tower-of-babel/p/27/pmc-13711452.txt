Differences in patterns of relapse and survival after relapse between lobectomy and sublobar resection for stage IA non–small cell lung cancer
Authors: Masahiro Adachi, Yukihiro Yoshida, Chihiro Takemura, Masaya Yotsukura, Tomohiro Haruki, Hidehito Horinouchi, Yasushi Goto, Shun-Ichi Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn the Japan Clinical Oncology Group 0802 trial, lung cancer-specific mortality was comparable between lobectomy and segmentectomy despite a higher rate of locoregional relapse after segmentectomy. We evaluated relapse patterns and postrelapse survival following lobectomy or sublobar resection.MethodsThis single-institution retrospective study included patients who underwent surgery for primary non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) between 2003 and 2022 and subsequently developed relapse. Overall survival was defined as the period from surgery (OS1) or from the date of relapse diagnosis (OS2) to death from any cause.ResultsAmong 362 patients with clinical stage IA NSCLC who developed relapse, isolated locoregional relapse accounted for 110 of 297 relapsed patients (37.0%) after lobectomy and 36 of 65 relapsed patients (55.4%) after sublobar resection. Among 59 patients treated with local consolidative therapy for isolated locoregional relapse, the 4-year OS2 rate was 76.4% (95% CI, 64.9%-89.8%). After inverse probability of treatment weighting, the 7-year OS1 rates were 49.3% (95% CI, 42.2%-57.6%) after lobectomy and 64.9% (95% CI, 52.4%-80.3%) after sublobar resection, and the 4-year OS2 rates were 49.6% (95% CI, 42.4%-57.9%) and 62.7% (95% CI, 49.5%-79.3%), respectively.ConclusionsIn this exploratory cohort of patients who developed postoperative relapse, relapse patterns differed between surgical groups, with isolated locoregional relapse representing a larger proportion of relapse in the sublobar resection group. Long-term survival was observed in selected patients treated with modern postrelapse therapies, including local consolidative therapy. These findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating.Key Words: sublobar resection, locoregional relapse, relapse pattern, local consolidative treatment, postrelapse therapy, oncologic outcomes, optimal surgical procedure

Central Message

Among patients with postoperative relapse, selected patients whose relapse was amenable to salvage treatment achieved long-term survival.

Perspective

The JCOG0802 trial showed comparable lung cancer-specific mortality despite a higher relapse rate after segmentectomy than after lobectomy. Our study suggests that long-term survival after relapse may be achieved in selected patients treated with modern postrelapse therapies, underscoring the need to balance oncologic curability with patient-specific factors to select the optimal surgical procedure.

Two randomized controlled trials, the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) 0802 and the Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) 140503 trials, demonstrated that sublobar resection is noninferior to lobectomy for patients with small-sized (≤2 cm) peripheral non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).1,2

In the JCOG0802 trial, although the segmentectomy group showed a higher incidence of relapse—particularly locoregional relapse, which was approximately twice as frequent as that after lobectomy—the 5-year overall survival was significantly higher in the segmentectomy group than in the lobectomy group.1 The recent 10-year follow-up data from JCOG0802 confirmed a higher rate of locoregional relapse after segmentectomy without a significant difference in lung cancer-related mortality.3

This apparent discrepancy raises an important clinical question regarding how relapse patterns and the postrelapse clinical course may influence long-term outcomes. We hypothesized that parenchyma-preserving surgery might allow patients to better tolerate subsequent treatment after relapse, thereby contributing to favorable outcomes. To explore this hypothesis, we conducted a single-center retrospective observational study to compare patterns of relapse and survival after relapse between patients with NSCLC who underwent lobectomy and those who underwent sublobar resection.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This single-center retrospective observational study included patients who underwent surgical resection for primary NSCLC at the National Cancer Center Hospital between 2003 and 2022 and subsequently developed relapse. Exclusion criteria were neuroendocrine tumors, incomplete resection, receipt of neoadjuvant therapy, and pneumonectomy. The patient selection process is shown in Figure 1. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the National Cancer Center (protocol No. 2018-045), and informed consent was waived due to the study's retrospective design.

Patient flow diagram of the study cohort.

End Point

The primary end point was overall survival after relapse (OS2). The secondary end points included overall survival from surgery (OS1), relapse patterns, and the initial treatment after relapse. This study was designed as an exploratory analysis of patients who developed postoperative relapse.

Surgical Procedures and Definitions

Surgical procedures included lobectomy or sublobar resection (defined as segmentectomy or wedge resection). Clinical and pathological stages were assigned according to the TNM classification system, using the sixth edition for surgical cases from 2003 to 2009, the seventh edition for cases from 2010 to 2016, and the eighth edition for cases from 2017 to 2022. Patterns of relapse were categorized as locoregional, distant, or both according to the JCOG0802 trial protocol.1 Locoregional relapse included recurrence at the bronchial stump or surgical margin, ipsilateral lung parenchyma, ipsilateral pleura or pleural effusion, ipsilateral hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes, as well as contralateral hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes. Relapse was determined primarily based on radiologic findings, with or without pathological confirmation.

Postrelapse Treatment

Initial treatment after relapse was classified as local consolidative therapy (LCT), tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), chemotherapy, and/or immune checkpoint inhibitor (Chemo/ICI), or best supportive care (BSC). In the prior clinical trial, LCT was defined as surgery, radiotherapy, or chemoradiotherapy4; in the present study, we additionally included radiofrequency ablation as a form of LCT. Detailed definitions of relapse patterns, molecular analyses, pulmonary function testing, and performance status assessment are provided in Appendix E1.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Patients with missing values were excluded from each respective analysis (complete-case analysis). OS1 was the time from surgery to death from any cause. OS2 was defined as the time from the date of relapse diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the date of last follow-up were censored. Survival outcomes for OS1 and OS2 were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was performed to adjust for baseline differences between the lobectomy and sublobar resection groups. Given the limited number of patients in the sublobar resection group, we intentionally restricted the propensity score model to a small set of clinically relevant preoperative covariates, including age, sex, forced expiratory volume in 1 second, radiologic tumor size, histology (adenocarcinoma vs nonadenocarcinoma), and year of surgery (early vs late era), to maintain model stability and reduce the influence of extreme weights. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model, and stabilized weights were applied. Patients with propensity scores outside the range of 0.05 to 0.95 were excluded (trimming) to improve covariate overlap and reduce the influence of extreme weights. Weighted survival analyses were then conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Student Version 17.0 (SAS Institute Inc) and R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Cohort

Among 730 patients, 362 had preoperative clinical stage IA NSCLC and constituted the primary study cohort for the present analysis. Of these, 297 patients underwent lobectomy and 65 underwent sublobar resection, including 33 segmentectomies (Video 1) and 32 wedge resections (Table 1, Tables E1 and E2). Patients in the sublobar resection group were older at the time of surgery and at the time of relapse and had substantially fewer lymph nodes resected compared with those in the lobectomy group. Among patients who developed relapse, isolated locoregional relapse accounted for a greater proportion of relapse in the sublobar resection group (55.4% vs 37.0%), whereas any distant relapse accounted for a larger proportion of relapse in the lobectomy group (63.0% vs 44.6%). Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation testing was performed in 296 patients, and EGFR mutations were identified in 145 cases (49.0%). Details of molecular testing are provided in Table E3.

Relapse Pattern and Survival in Clinical Stage IA NSCLC

Locoregional relapse occurred in 146 patients, distant relapse in 84 patients, and both locoregional and distant relapse in 132 patients (Tables E4 and E5 and Figure E1). The 7-year OS1 and 4-year OS2 were 60.8% (95% CI, 52.4%-70.5%) and 62.3% (95% CI, 54.0%-71.8%), respectively, in patients with isolated locoregional relapse; 39.6% (95% CI, 28.3%-52.1%) and 48.9% (95% CI, 38.6%-61.9%) in those with distant relapse; and 52.1% (95% CI, 43.6%-62.3%) and 45.2% (95% CI, 36.5%-56.0%) in those with both locoregional and distant relapse (Figure 2, A and B).

Postrelapse Treatment and Survival After Locoregional Relapse

Among 146 patients who developed isolated locoregional relapse, initial postrelapse treatment consisted of LCT in 59 patients, TKIs in 44 patients, Chemo/ICI in 38 patients, and BSC in 5 patients (Table E6). Among patients treated with LCT, the most common modalities were chemoradiotherapy (n = 31) and radiotherapy (n = 24), with a small number of patients undergoing surgery (n = 4). Among the 31 patients treated with chemoradiotherapy, 8 subsequently received maintenance durvalumab. Twelve patients in the Chemo/ICI group received an ICI-containing regimen as first-line treatment for relapsed lung cancer. Forty-seven lobectomy group patients and 12 sublobar resection group patients underwent LCT for isolated locoregional relapse (Table E5).

In descriptive analyses, the 4-year OS2 was 76.4% (95% CI, 64.9%-89.8%) among patients who received LCT, 65.7% (95% CI, 50.7%-85.1%) among those treated with TKIs, and 40.8% (95% CI, 27.0%-61.6%) among those treated with Chemo/ICI (Figure 3, B). All patients who received BSC died within 4 years after relapse. Similar trends were observed for OS1 (Figure 3, A). Among patients who received LCT or TKIs, no statistically significant differences in OS1 or OS2 were observed between treatment groups (log-rank P = .520 and .866, respectively).

Survival According to Surgical Procedures before IPTW

We compared survivals between patients who underwent lobectomy and those who underwent sublobar resection for clinical stage IA NSCLC. The median follow-up periods for OS1 and OS2 were 5.0 years and 2.6 years, respectively. Median time to relapse was 1.8 years in the lobectomy group (n = 297) and 1.6 years in the sublobar resection group (n = 65). The 7-year OS1 and 4-year OS2 rates were 52.9% (95% CI, 46.9%-59.6%) and 53.2% (95% CI, 47.3%-59.9%) in the lobectomy group, and 54.3% (95% CI, 42.4%-69.6%) and 51.3% (95% CI, 38.6%-68.2%) in the sublobar resection group (log-rank P = .718 for OS1 and 0.876 for OS2) (Figure 4, A and B).

Survival According to Surgical Procedures After IPTW

After IPTW adjustment, baseline characteristics between the lobectomy and sublobar resection groups were substantially balanced, with all standardized mean differences below 0.1 (Figure E2). The 7-year OS1 and 4-year OS2 rates were 49.3% (95% CI, 42.2%-57.6%) and 49.6% (95% CI, 42.4%-57.9%) in the lobectomy group, and 64.9% (95% CI, 52.4%-80.3%) and 62.7% (95% CI, 49.5%-79.3%) in the sublobar resection group. In the weighted cohort, there were no significant differences in OS1 or OS2 between the 2 surgical procedures (hazard ratio, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.46-1.28; P = .317 for OS1 and hazard ratio, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.47-1.29; P = .329 for OS2, using lobectomy as the reference) (Figure 5, A and B).

Discussion

In this exploratory cohort restricted to patients who developed postoperative relapse, locoregional relapse accounted for a greater proportion of relapse after sublobar resection. Long-term survival after relapse was observed in selected patients, particularly among patients with locoregional relapse amenable to salvage treatment, irrespective of the initial surgical procedure. Although patients who received LCT showed favorable survival after relapse, confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Our findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating that postrelapse treatment accounts for the comparable lung cancer-specific mortality reported in JCOG0802. Rather, they provide a descriptive, hypothesis-generating perspective on the postrelapse clinical course.

Historically, postoperative relapse of lung cancer was associated with a poor prognosis. For example, a Mayo Clinic report of patients treated between 1997 and 2001 demonstrated a median survival after relapse of only 8.1 months.5 In contrast, our findings suggest that survival outcomes after postoperative relapse have substantially improved in the modern treatment era. Molecular targeted therapies have markedly prolonged survival in patients with driver gene alterations, particularly EGFR mutations, which were prevalent in our cohort. These observations are consistent with prior reports.6 The small number of patients who received an ICI-containing regimen precluded a separate analysis of the survival impact of ICI in this study.

In patients with clinical stage IA disease, isolated locoregional relapse accounted for approximately 40% of all relapses and was associated with significantly better survival than distant or combined relapse. One plausible explanation is that a substantial proportion of these patients were eligible for potentially curative LCT. These findings contrast with the results of the CALGB 140503 trial, in which more than 50% of relapses were distant.2 This discrepancy may be attributable to the higher proportion of women and adenocarcinoma in our cohort,7 the mediastinal and hilar lymph node assessment performed before randomization in CALGB 140503, and the fact that our study population reflects real-world clinical practice.8 Nevertheless, the potential for effective postrelapse treatment should not be interpreted as justification for indiscriminate selection of sublobar resection, and careful patient selection remains essential to minimize the risk of locoregional relapse.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective observational study restricted to patients who developed postoperative relapse. Therefore, it could not estimate the incidence of locoregional or distant relapse by initial surgical procedure, and the observed associations between relapse patterns, postrelapse treatment, and survival may have been influenced by selection bias. Second, the study period spanned nearly 2 decades, during which staging systems, indications for sublobar resection, molecular diagnostics, and systemic therapies for recurrent NSCLC evolved substantially. Missingness in molecular and programmed death-ligand 1 data was likely nonrandom and may have introduced bias. Furthermore, limited nodal evaluation in many patients who underwent sublobar resection may have affected the accuracy of pathological staging and contributed to stage migration. Although we adjusted for major baseline differences using propensity score-based weighting and performed an additional sensitivity analysis, the adjustment was limited by the small number of patients in the sublobar resection group. Therefore, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, including comorbidity burden, performance status, radiologic characteristics, surgical margin status, and molecular characteristics, cannot be excluded. Third, the number of patients in the sublobar resection group was relatively small, limiting statistical power. In addition, the limited sample size did not allow for reliable separate analyses of segmentectomy and wedge resection, which may have different oncologic implications. Therefore, the findings for the sublobar resection group should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as procedure-specific conclusions for either segmentectomy or wedge resection. Fourth, relapse classification was based primarily on radiologic assessment without systematic pathological confirmation in this retrospective cohort and may therefore have been subject to misclassification. Imaging modalities, including positron emission tomography/computed tomography, and surveillance practices evolved over the study period, potentially affecting the timing and accuracy of relapse detection. We also acknowledge that the favorable survival observed among patients with locoregional relapse may partly reflect era-related differences, because advances in imaging may have facilitated earlier detection of locoregional relapse, whereas postrelapse treatment options improved substantially over the study period. Different TNM staging editions were used across the study period. Moreover, the relapse pattern categories were clinically heterogeneous and may have oversimplified biologically distinct relapse entities. Treatment selection was influenced by clinical factors such as relapse pattern, performance status, and molecular characteristics; therefore, confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Finally, follow-up duration may have differed across treatment eras, which could have influenced survival estimates (Video 2).

Conclusions

In this exploratory cohort restricted to patients who developed postoperative relapse, locoregional relapse represented a larger proportion of relapse in the sublobar resection group than in the lobectomy group. Long-term survival after relapse was observed in selected patients. Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, they highlight the need to balance oncologic curability with patient-specific factors when selecting the optimal surgical approach for clinical stage IA NSCLC (Figure 6).

Detailed Classification of Locoregional Relapse Patterns

We added a detailed classification of locoregional relapse patterns, including same-lobe recurrence (including the surgical stump), other ipsilateral lobe recurrence, lymph node recurrence, and other patterns, in Table E4. Relapse patterns and initial postrelapse treatment according to surgical procedure are presented in Table E5.

Multivariable Cox Proportional Hazards Analysis for Overall Survival From Surgery and Overall Survival After Relapse

We performed multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses for both overall survival from surgery (OS1) and overall survival after relapse (OS2) to complement the IPTW-based Kaplan–Meier analyses. The models included preoperative variables (age, sex, forced expiratory volume in 1 second, tumor size, histology, and year of surgery). The results showed no significant difference between lobectomy and sublobar resection for either OS1 (hazard ratio [HR], 0.89; 95% CI, 0.58-1.37; P = .60) or OS2 (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.58-1.37; P = .59), which were consistent with the IPTW findings, supporting the robustness of our conclusions (Table E9).

Diagnostic Evaluations of the IPTW Approach

We performed diagnostic evaluations of the IPTW approach. The distribution of stabilized weights and the overlap of propensity scores between treatment groups were examined to assess the positivity assumption (Figures E5 and E6). Initial analyses identified extreme weights associated with propensity scores near the boundaries. To address this issue and improve covariate overlap, patients with propensity scores outside the range of 0.05 to 0.95 were excluded (trimming). After trimming, the resulting weights were well behaved, with a maximum of 3.29 and a mean close to 1.0, indicating stable estimation. These findings suggest that the positivity assumption was reasonably satisfied and that no individual observation exerted disproportionate influence on the weighted survival estimates.

Prognosis According to Clinical Stage

The distribution of preoperative clinical stage was as follows: cStage I (n = 508), cStage II (n = 141), and cStage III (n = 81) (Table E7). The median time from surgery to relapse was 1.8 years (interquartile range [IQR], 1.0-3.3 years) in cStage I, 1.2 years (IQR, 0.5-2.1 years) in cStage II, and 0.8 years (IQR, 0.5-1.2 years) in cStage III. As the clinical stage increased, the time from surgery to relapse tended to be shorter. In contrast, the proportion of locoregional relapse was higher in patients with earlier-stage disease: 36.0% (183 out of 508) in cStage I, 34.8% (49 out of 141) in cStage II, and 30.9% (25 out of 81) in cStage III.

The median follow-up periods for OS1 and OS2 were 4.4 years and 2.2 years for the 730 patients. The 7-year OS1 and 4-year OS2 rates were 49.2% (95% CI, 44.5%-54.3%) and 48.9% (95% CI, 44.3%-54.1%) in cStage I; 33.3% (95% CI, 25.1%-44.1%) and 40.4% (95% CI, 32.2%-50.7%) in cStage II; and 32.6% (95% CI, 20.3%-52.3%) and 49.0% (95% CI, 37.9%-63.3%) in cStage III, respectively (Figure E3). Both OS1 and OS2 were more favorable in patients with cStage I NSCLC than in those with cStage II or III disease.

Prognosis According to Surgical Period

The prognosis was evaluated by stratifying patients into 2 groups based on the surgical period: those who underwent surgery before 2009 and those who underwent surgery from 2009 onward, corresponding to the initiation of patient enrollment in the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) 0802 trial. Patient characteristics according to the surgical period are summarized in Table E8.

Among the 154 patients who underwent surgery between 2003 and 2008, the 7-year OS1 rate and 4-year OS2 rate were 41.4% (95% CI, 34.1%-50.3%) and 40.4% (95% CI, 33.1%-49.2%), respectively. In contrast, among the 576 patients who underwent surgery between 2009 and 2022, the 7-year OS1 rate and 4-year OS2 rate were 45.5% (95% CI, 40.9%-50.7%) and 49.2% (95% CI, 44.6%-54.1%), respectively (Figure E4). Both OS1 and OS2 tended to be improved in patients who underwent surgery between 2009 and 2022 (P = .153 for OS1 and P = .078 for OS2). These findings suggest that advances in postrelapse treatment after the initiation of the JCOG0802 trial may have contributed to improvements in both postrelapse survival and overall survival following surgery in patients with relapsed NSCLC.

Sensitivity Analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the trimmed IPTW-adjusted survival analyses restricted to patients who underwent surgery between 2015 and 2022, when molecular diagnostics, positron-emission tomography/computed tomography, and modern postrelapse therapies were more widely available. We selected 2015 as the cutoff because immune checkpoint inhibitors became available for the treatment of NSCLC in Japan with the approval of nivolumab in 2015, marking the beginning of the contemporary treatment era.

In this contemporary cohort, driver mutation testing was performed in 124 of 138 patients (89.9%) in the lobectomy group and 48 of 53 patients (90.6%) in the sublobar resection group. Similarly, PD-L1 expression was assessed in 113 patients (81.9%) and 48 patients (90.6%), respectively (Table E10). In the unadjusted analysis of this contemporary cohort, the 7-year OS1 rates were 49.1% (95% CI, 39.5%-61.1%) in the lobectomy group and 56.4% (95% CI, 43.8%-72.6%) in the sublobar resection group (log-rank P = .522). The corresponding 4-year OS2 rates were 57.3% (95% CI, 48.5%-67.7%) and 53.9% (95% CI, 40.8%-71.2%), respectively (log-rank P = .366) (Figure E7).

In the trimmed IPTW-adjusted analysis, the 7-year OS1 rates were 46.5% (95% CI, 35.6%-60.6%) in the lobectomy group and 62.0% (95% CI, 48.3%-79.5%) in the sublobar resection group. The corresponding 4-year OS2 rates were 54.4% (95% CI, 44.9%-66.0%) and 59.6% (95% CI, 45.3%-78.4%), respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed between surgical procedures for either OS1 (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.51-1.66; P = .787) or OS2 (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.58-1.81; P = .948) (Figure E8). Overall, the findings were consistent with the primary analyses, demonstrating no significant differences in OS1 or OS2 between lobectomy and sublobar resection after restricting the cohort to patients treated in the contemporary era.

Appendix E1. Patients and Methods

Surgical Procedures and Definitions

Predicted values for preoperative pulmonary function parameters were calculated using reference equations proposed by the Japanese Respiratory Society.E1 Performance status was evaluated using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS) scale, and the ECOG-PS at the time of relapse was used for analysis.

Definitions of Relapse

Locoregional relapse was defined as relapse within the thoracic cavity related to the surgical field, including the bronchial stump or surgical margin, ipsilateral lung parenchyma, ipsilateral pleura or pleural effusion, ipsilateral hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes, as well as contralateral hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes. Distant relapse was defined as relapse outside the locoregional area, including metastases to the contralateral lung or pleura, brain, bone, adrenal glands, liver, supraclavicular lymph nodes, pericardium, or other extrathoracic organs.

Postrelapse Treatment Categories

Systemic therapy, including chemotherapy and/or immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—namely, chemotherapy plus ICIs, chemotherapy plus other agents, or ICIs plus other agents with or without chemotherapy—was categorized as chemotherapy/ICI. Treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), either alone or in combination with other agents, was classified as TKI.

Molecular Analysis and Programmed Death-ligand 1 Expression

Molecular alterations were evaluated using pretreatment biopsy specimens or resected tumor samples with single-plex or multiplex companion diagnostic assays or next-generation sequencing. At a minimum, EGFR mutation status was assessed, and cases without detectable EGFR mutations were classified as EGFR-negative. For molecular data, the frequency of each genetic alteration was calculated among patients who underwent testing, with both the number and proportion of mutation-positive cases reported. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression was evaluated using the 22C3 assay. PD-L1 expression was categorized as <1%, 1% to 49%, ≥50%, or unmeasured, and was reported using the total number of patients in each surgical group as the denominator.
