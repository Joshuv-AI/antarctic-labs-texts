Structural insights into heme-iron dependent N–N bond formation enzyme LnzB
Authors: Huisi Huang, Lu Wang, Pujian Chen, Tao Yang, Chengjun Zhu, Shaoyang Li, Yang Zhou, Yi Tan, Zhengqiu Li, Hong Zhang, Jianwei Chen, Zhi-Min Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen-nitrogen (N-N) bond formation is integrated into the biosynthetic pathways of various classes of natural products, some of which exhibit intriguing biological activities. While recent studies have identified several distinct groups of enzymes responsible for N-N bond formation, the underlying catalytic mechanisms are largely unknown. Here, we report the dimeric structure of the N-N bond forming enzyme LnzB (Streptomyces spp.), which relies on a heme-iron to catalyze the formation of intramolecular N-N bonds using N-hydroxyornithine as a substrate. The structure reveals the molecular architecture of its active sites and heme-interacting pocket. In combination with MD simulation, site-directed mutagenesis, and kinetic activity assays, we have identified key residues responsible for ligand binding and N-N bond formation activity. Phylogenetic analysis and structural comparison reveal that LnzB and its homologues may have evolved from the transcriptional regulator PaiB by altering the substrate binding pocket. Our study extends the limited knowledge of N-N bond formation catalyzed by a heme iron-dependent enzyme in natural products.Subject terms: X-ray crystallography, Enzyme mechanisms, Biosynthesis

Introduction

As a central feature of many functional groups, including hydrazines, diazos, pyrazoles, and N-nitroso compounds, nitrogen–nitrogen (N–N) bonds have been found in numerous synthesized chemical compounds and hundreds of natural products with diverse structures (Fig. 1a)1. Many of these compounds exhibit significant biological activities2, making them effective clinical treatment drugs. For example, streptozotocin (SZN, also known by the trade name Zanosar), an N-nitrosourea natural product isolated from Streptomyces achromogenes, has been used as chemotherapy to treat pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors3,4. However, due to the relatively high electronegativity of nitrogen, N–N bond construction remains challenging in organic chemistry, especially when in relation to intermolecular connections5, thus hindering the extensive exploration of these compounds in disease therapy.

With the rapid growth in the number of natural products containing N–N bonds, the biosynthetic enzymes responsible for constructing these different N–N bonds are just beginning to be identified and have attracted much attention for their potential use in biosynthesis. Recently, the enzymatic activity of several proteins that construct various groups containing N–N bonds has been revealed1,6,7. ATP-dependent enzyme CreM and its homologues utilize nitrous acid to construct diazo during the biosynthesis of cremeomycin8–11. This diazo-forming strategy is also shared by a transmembrane protein AzpL during the biosynthesis of alazopeptin12. Another distinct way to form N–N bond was found in some metalloenzymes, which use N-hydroxylated amino acids, usually N-hydroxylysine (L-N6-OH-Lys, 1) or N-hydroxyornithine (L-N5-OH-Orn, 2), as substrates (Fig. 1b)13–19. Such as a class of di-domain N–N bond formation enzymes, consisting of an N-terminal cupin domain and a C-terminal methionyl-tRNA synthase (MetRS) homolog, are widely distributed in bacterial species14–17. Preliminary studies based on in vitro biotransformation experiments have shown that the MetRS-like domain catalyzes the nucleophilic attack of N-hydroxy group to the amine group of another amino acid to form an ester intermediate (3), in the dependence of ATP and Mg2+; while the zinc-binding cupin domain further catalyzes the intermolecular arrangement of the ester intermediate through zinc-assisted N–O bond cleavage and N–N bond formation (4)14. Using L-N5-OH-Orn as substrate, another metalloenzyme, KtzT (Kutzneria sp. 744), relies on a heme iron to polarize the N–O bond to form an intramolecular N–N bond. The resulting product, a six-membered cyclic and hydrazine-bearing piperazic acid (Piz, 5)13, could be further incorporated into the biosynthetic pathways to increase the structural rigidity and biological activity of the final natural products19. By genomic signature-based screening of bacterial DNA libraries, a recent study has identified 62 bacterial strains that contain PiZ-bearing natural products and KtzT-like proteins19, highlighting the wide-spread of this unique N–N bond formation way in bacterial biosynthesis.

To illustrate the catalytic mechanism underpinning the heme iron-dependent N–N bond formation enzymes, we here report the crystal structure of LnzB (Streptomyces spp.), a homolog protein of KtzT. The structure of LnzB is a homodimer with each monomer adopting a typical split-barrel fold. Two heme molecules, with their 6-propionate groups flipped up to the substrate binding side, were defined unambiguously in the two binding pockets located symmetrically on the homodimer interface. In combination with molecular docking and kinetic experiments, we identified key residues responsible for heme positioning and substrate recognition. Additionally, structure comparison and phylogenetic analysis revealed a close evolutionary relationship between KtzT-like proteins and the transcriptional regulator PaiB, which plays critical roles in the negative control of sporulation and degradative enzyme production. We further solved the crystal structure of PaiB-heme complex, which exhibits obvious differences from LnzB in the conformation of heme and key residues involved in substrate binding. Our work will deepen the understanding of N–N binding enzymes in natural product biosynthesis and potentially provide clues to engineer them for new bioactive substances with valuable applications.

Results

Identification of LnzB as a highly-expressed KtzT-like enzyme

To understand the molecular mechanism underlying the activity of heme iron-dependent N–N bond formation enzymes, we tried to recombinantly express KtzT in E. coli in the presence of 5-Aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA), the committed intermediate of the heme biosynthesis pathway. However, the low yield and significant amounts of proteolytic degradation products make it difficult to purify high-quality KtzT. We then turned to KtzT homologs and found that LnzB, which shares about 50% sequence identity with KtzT, exhibited particularly excellent soluble expression and could be readily prepared with high homogeneity. The gene of LnzB was discovered in two different Streptomyces spp. strains with distinct origins: PC5 was isolated from a dung beetle, whereas SNJ018 was collected from marine sediment. Both strains produce two novel 31-membered Piz-containing cyclic decapeptides, lenziamides A and lenziamides B (Fig. 1a), that exhibit selective anti-proliferative activities against cancer cells compared to normal cells19.

Purified LnzB is brown in color with a maximum absorbance (λmax) of 406 nm (Soret peak) in the ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating the presence of heme. The heme level was quantitatively determined by pyridine hemochromagen assay, which showed that only 22.5% of LnzB is loaded with heme (Supplementary Fig. 2), similar to that of KtzT (22.0%)13. We also performed heme titration using LnzB expressed in the absence of 5-ALA. As the concentration of heme increased, the Soret peak became significantly stronger (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating that more heme was bound to the protein. The absorption peaks at 504 nm and 632 nm in the Q-band suggest that the heme-iron is in the ferric state, whereas in the presence of dithionite, the heme-iron could be reduced to the ferrous state (absorption peak at 556 nm in the Q-band). However, a previous study has shown that the oxidation state of heme does not affect the catalytic capability of N–N bond formation13. We conducted an in vitro reaction experiment to explore the enzymatic activity of LnzB (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1b). To exclude the effect of insufficient heme binding on their activity, purified LnzB was incubated with fourfold the molar amount of heme before assessing their enzymatic activity against L-N5-OH-Orn. Boiled LnzB and free heme were used as negative controls. The results showed that a new compound with the same retention time and mass spectrum as the Piz standard was produced in the presence of LnzB, supporting that LnzB is a KtzT-like N–N bond formation enzyme. Surprisingly, boiled LnzB also showed a small peak of the product. It is possible that LnzB is a relatively stable protein, and small amounts remain active after high-temperature treatment.

Crystal structure of LnzB-heme complex

Feather-shaped crystals of LnzB were initially grown in the presence of L-ornithine and optimized to diffraction quality when using cupric chloride as an additive. The structure was determined at a resolution of 2.30 Å (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 1), which contains two homodimers of LnzB in an asymmetric unit. In each homodimer, the two LnzB molecules are related by a non-crystallographic twofold axis, with the typical split-barrel fold of each monomer tightly packaged them together (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). The structure of LnzB monomer is composed of three domains: a large domain (LD) that contains the six-stranded β-barrel fold flanked by two short helices (residues 1–129 and 169–190), an intermediate domain (ID) resembling a helix-loop-helix pattern (residues 130–168), and a C-terminal domain (CD) that possesses a helix-turn-helix fold (residues 169–221). At the interface of the homodimer, two pronounced heme-binding clefts are located, each with a heme b embedded diagonally in a deep and narrow pocket (Fig. 2b, c). Even though the crystals were obtained in the presence of L-ornithine, we were unable to assign it in the structure due to the lack of electron densities in the substrate binding pocket.

Though the two heme b binding pockets are contributed by both monomers in the structure, the heme molecule in each pocket closely leans to the monomer contributing a proximal histidine (His72) as an axial ligand to coordinate the heme iron (Fig. 2b). A water molecule coordinates the iron atom at a distance of ~3.0 Å on the distal side of the heme ring. Generally, the heme-propionates have been regarded as functioning as anchors for the heme group by forming interactions with the polar protein matrix. This is true for the 7-propionate in the LnzB-heme structure, as it is out-stretched and interacts extensively with surrounding residues through a series of hydrogen bonding interactions, including direct hydrogen bonding with the backbone amide of Asn74 and the side chain of Asn77, and water-mediated hydrogen bonding with Tyr7 and His39 (Fig. 2d). In contrast, few interactions were observed between the 6-propionate and LnzB. The peripheral side chain of 6-propionate is flipped to the distal side of the heme ring, presumably to avoid steric clashes with the side-chain of Asn74 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). This flipped conformation makes 6-propionate part of the substrate binding pocket, implying that it may play a critical role in substrate recognition during the catalytic process of N–N bond formation. The propionate groups of heme have been discovered in other proteins to play distinct roles. Such as in Cytochrome P450cam, which relies on a Cys-coordinated heme-iron to catalyze hydroxylation of d-camphor, the 7-propionate functions as a gate for controlling the access of water to the substrate binding site20, while the 6-propionate participates in the fixation of the heme-binding site and stabilization of the Fe-Cys coordination21. Our study provides a new example to study the diverse roles of the propionates in heme-binding proteins.

The substrate binding pocket is open to the solvent in the structure and much shallower than the heme binding pocket, allowing the substrate to easily access the catalytic center (Fig. 2c). Besides 6-propionate, residues from both molecules in the dimer are involved in forming the substrate binding pocket. However, they are predominantly derived from the large domain of the monomer on the heme ring’s distal side, including Tyr7’, Tyr105’, Thr113’, Lys184’, and Glu188’ (Fig. 2e). Noticeably, the side chains of Lys184’ and Thr113’ form a hydrogen bond near the heme-iron coordinated water, and may serve as a proton donor system for the catalytic process. The other monomer contributes two residues, Tyr161 and Leu165, from its intermediate domain. Sequence alignment reveals that almost all these residues involved in heme binding and substrate pocket formation are highly conserved among KtzT-like proteins (Fig. 3).

Identification of key residues for substrate binding and catalysis

Despite numerous attempts, we could not obtain a co-crystal structure of LnzB with either the native substrate L-N5-OH-Orn or the product Piz (Supplementary Fig. 4c). To gain mechanistic insights, we docked L-N5-OH-Orn within the substrate binding pocket in which the N atom of the N-O bond replaces the iron-coordinated water (Fig. 4a). This model was further validated through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Supplementary Fig. 5). The root mean square deviation (RMSD) values exhibits minimal fluctuations around a stable baseline, suggesting the docked L-N5-OH-Orn is in an energetically favorable conformation. In this model, L-N5-OH-Orn is directly recognized by the heme-6-propionate and Tyr161 from the heme-bound monomer via hydrogen bond interactions. The side chains of Tyr7’, Tyr105’, Thr113’, Lys184’ and Glu188’ together form a hydrophilic surface to accommodate L-N5-OH-Orn. Additionally, L165 interacts with the side-chain of L-N5-OH-Orn through van der Waals forces.

To investigate which residues are critical for the N–N bond formation activity of LnzB, we performed site-directed mutagenesis and tested the mutant proteins for their activity in quantitative LC-MS assays (Fig. 4b). Selected mutants were further analyzed in kinetic experiments (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 6 and 7). We first investigated the impact of Tyr7, which is located on the very N-terminal loop of LnzB. Tyr7 was substituted with phenylalanine (Y7F) and alanine (Y7A), respectively, to assess the effect of the hydroxyl group and the aromatic ring on LnzB activity. Interestingly, even when expressed in LB cultures supplemented with 5-ALA, the Y7A mutant was much weaker in color than WT LnzB. Consistently, the heme-loading level of the Y7A mutant is notably decreased to 7.8% (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicative of impaired heme-binding capacity. The catalytic activity of the Y7A mutant is significantly impaired compared to WT (3.2-fold increase of KM, 39.0-fold decrease of kcat), whereas the Y7F version (2.3-fold decrease of KM, 2.7-fold decrease of kcat) exhibits similar activity of WT LnzB (Fig. 4b, c, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). These results revealed an indispensable role of the aromatic nature of Tyr7 in ensuring efficient heme binding of LnzB.

To probe the contribution of other residues (Tyr105’, Thr113’, Lys184’, and Glu188’) on the hydrophilic surface to substrate binding and catalysis, the LnzB variants Y105A’, T113A’, K184A’, and E188A’ were created. The hydrogen bond observed between Lys184’ and Thr113’ is apparently essential for the N–N formation activity of LnzB, since disruption of the hydrogen bonding by either the K184A’ or the T113A’ mutation results in a dramatic decrease in catalytic activity (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). However, kcat was severely decreased for both mutants (151.4 times for K184A’ and 74.8 times for T113A’) (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7). Given the conservation of Lys184’ and Thr113’ in KtzT-like proteins, we speculate that this hydrogen bond not only contributes to the construction of the substrate-binding pocket, but may also be directly involved in the catalytic process. E188’ is located at the entrance of the substrate binding pocket (Fig. 2e). Though not directly interacting with L-N5-OH-Orn in the model, replacing it with alanine (E188A’) led to an obvious reduction in kcat (60.3-fold). Y105A’ (2.9-fold increase of KM, 7.9-fold reduction of kcat) had moderately reduced catalytic activity compared to WT LnzB. Like the T7A mutant, Y105A’ also contains a low level of heme (11.5%) and could absorb free heme from the solvent, demonstrating that Y105’ and Y7 may regulate the integration of heme into the binding pocket of LnzB (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Next, we investigated the effect of residues on the heme-coordinating monomer (Asn77, Tyr161 and Leu165) on the activity of LnzB. The L165A mutant retained significant catalytic activity, whereas the activity of the Y161A mutant was drastically compromised. Detailed kinetic analysis revealed that the KM of Y161A was increased 6.6 fold and kcat was reduced 44.2 fold (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7). This result agrees with the model in which Tyr161 hydrogen bonds directly with the carboxyl group of L-N5-OH-Orn, and indicates the critical role of Tyr161 for the substrate specificity of LnzB. We also generated an N77A mutant to explore the relevance of this heme-interacting residue to activity. The function of N77A was moderately affected (19.2-fold reduction of kcat), suggesting that the stabilization of heme by Asn77 also contributes to the N–N bond formation activity of LnzB.

We also confirmed that all the mutants are well folded as the WT LnzB by CD spectra (Supplementary Fig. 8). These relative activities and kinetic data support that Tyr7’, Thr113’, Tyr161, Lys184’ and Glu188’ are catalytically essential residues: the Thr113’-Lys184’ pair may serve as a proton donor, participating directly in the catalytic process; Tyr7’ plays critical role in heme uptake; while Tyr161, in cooperation with 6-propionate, is responsible for substrate recognition and orientation. Meanwhile, other residues, including Try105’ and E188’ maintain a hydrophilic environment around the substrate binding pocket.

These structural insights and mutagenesis data allowed us to propose a catalytic mechanism for LnzB (Supplementary Fig. 9). Upon positioning L-N5-OH-Orn in the correct orientation collectively by heme-6-propionate and residues from both monomers, the water molecule initially coordinating with heme-iron is removed from the catalytic center by the N atom in the N-O bond. The Lys184’-Thr113’ pair then acts as a proton donor for the hydroxyl group of L-N5-OH-Orn and polarizes the N-O bond to generate a new water molecule. The departure of this water molecule from L-N5-OH-Orn results in a positively charged nitrogen atom, which was then attacked by the α-amino group of L-N5-OH-Orn to form the six-membered Piz ring. This step may be facilitated by the interaction between the 6-propionate and the α-amino group of L-N5-OH-Orn. After Piz leaves active site, the water molecule may take over the heme-iron coordinating position again, waiting for a new substrate to bind.

Phylogenetic relationship between PaiB and KtzT-like proteins

A search on the Swiss-Model for close sequence homolog of LnzB in the Protein Data Bank achieved the best hit to a transcriptional regulator PaiB from Bacillus stearothermophilus (PDB code: 2OL5), suggesting a close phylogenetic relationship between the two protein families. PaiB is essential for cell growth and negative control of sporulation and degradative enzyme production22. The sequence similarity between archaeal PaiB and bacterial PaiB is about 50-55%, higher than the ~45% between PaiB and KtzT (Supplementary Fig. 10). A phylogenetic tree inferred based on available KtzT proteins and representative PaiB proteins support a monophyletic group consisting of bacteria-originated KtzT and PaiB proteins, with the PaiB proteins of archaeobacteria forming a basal lineage (Fig. 5a). The phylogenetic relationship suggests that the KtzT-like proteins should have gained their novel function via an evolutionary process of the ancestral PaiB gene.

Interestingly, some of the critical residues in KtzT-like proteins involved in heme or substrate binding are also highly conserved in PaiB proteins, including the heme-iron coordinating His72, the catalytic pair T113 and K184, and the heme-7-propionate interacting residue Asn77 (Supplementary Fig. 10). We thus tested its capability of PaiB to catalyze N–N bond formation. The purified PaiB is brown in color and shows a characteristic maximum absorbance (λmax) for b-heme in the UV–Vis spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 11a), supporting that PaiB is also a heme-containing protein. However, LC-MS did not identify the expected product PiZ (Supplementary Fig. 11b), implying that residues around the active site of LnzB that are different from those of PaiB have allowed it to evolve the activity to catalyze the formation of N–N bond.

Structure comparison between LnzB and PaiB

To further characterize the differences between the active sites of LnzB and PaiB, we determined the crystal structure of PaiB-heme complex at a resolution of 2.4 Å (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 2). Consistent to the observation of UV–Vis spectrum, a b-heme could be unambiguously assigned in each of the two active sites (Fig. 5b), further confirming PaiB as a heme-containing protein. A structure overlap revealed that the dimers of LnzB and PaiB superimpose well, giving a RMSD of 1.76 Å over the Cα atom of 309 residues. Three major differences occur around the active site: (1) In LnzB, the N-terminal loop (residues 1–8) extends towards a long cleft on the dimer interface and contributes to the catalytic activity via Tyr7. In contrast, Tyr7 in not well conserved in PaiB proteins (Supplementary Fig. 10), usually replaced by a residue with large hydrophobic side chain, such as Phe or Met; and this N-terminal loop in PaiB becomes highly dynamic with most the residues untraceable (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 12). (2) Heme-6-propionate in PaiB forms a hydrogen bond with Tyr2 and therefore no longer flips to the substrate binding side. Another reason for the conformation change of 6-propionate could be the substitution of LnzB-Asn76 by PaiB-Ala56, thus eliminating the potential steric conflict between LnzB-Asn76 and 6-propionate (Fig. 5d). (3) The α-helix containing Y161 in LnzB is involved in both heme and substrate binding, but in PaiB it is not only tilted out to the solvent, but also replaces LnzB-Y161 with methionine (Fig. 5e). Taken together, these structural differences suggest that KtzT-like proteins have undergone systematic engineering around the heme and substrate binding pockets on the conserved split-barrel-fold during evolution to acquire N–N bond forming activity.

Discussion

With the rapid increase in the number of N–N bond-containing natural products identified, mechanistic studies of N–N bond-forming enzymes are still limited. In this work, we reported the crystal structure of LnzB, which, together with mutagenesis and enzymatic studies, provides critical information on heme coordination and substrate binding in this unique heme-iron dependent N–N bond formation enzyme family. Noticeably, the two propionate groups of heme play different roles. The side chain of 7-propionate reinforces the heme–protein matrix contact, thus facilitating the fixation of heme in the proper conformation, while 6-propionate is involved in substrate recognition directly. This study enriches our understanding of the diverse roles of the heme propionates.

The catalytic process of LnzB relies on the cooperation of three structural elements: the N-terminal loop, the hydrogen bond between the Lys-Thr pair, and the flip-up heme-6-propionate. On the substrate side, the N-O bond is indispensable for the efficient interaction of L-N5-OH-Orn with LznB. Upon reaching the active site, the nitrogen atom in the hydroxylamine of L-N5-OH-Orn has to replace the water molecule to coordinate with heme iron, positioning the hydroxyl group close to the Lys-Thr pair. The catalytic process might be initiated by the Lys-Thr pair providing a hydrogen atom to the hydroxyl group, generating a water molecule. It appears that the catalytic mechanism of LnzB may share similarities with the engineered heme enzyme ParPgb-HYA-5213 (Pyrobaculum arsenaticum), which contains an acid (Asp57)–base (Gln60) catalytic pair to facilitate efficient N-O bond cleavage in hydroxylamine for benzyl C-H primary amination and olefinic amino hydroxylation23,24, with a water molecule as a byproduct. Recently, the structure of LnzB homolog protein Pips and KtzT were published, which shares the same Lys-Thr pair, substrate binding residues and the up-flipped 6-propionate, supporting a strictly conserved catalytic mechanism in these heme-iron-dependent N–N bind formation enzymes25,26.

Methods

General methods

L-ornithine and L-N5-OH-Orn were purchased from Bidepharm (Shanghai, China). Piz was purchased from ivsci (Shanghai, China). Code-optimised LznB was synthesized from Tsingke (Tianjin, China) for better expression in E. coli. Kanamycin (50 μg/ml) purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. was used for the selection of recombinant strains.

Protein purification

Genes of LnzB and PaiB were cloned into a modified pRSF-Duet vector that contains a His6-SUMO tag ahead the multicloning site, respectively. The recombinant protein was overexpressed in BL21(DE3) cells, which were induced by the addition of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.4 mM when the cell density reached an OD600 of 0.6. At the same time, 1.0 mM of heme precursor 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) and 1 mM of (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2 were added to the culture. The cells were further incubated at 16 °C overnight. The His6-SUMO-fused proteins were first purified using a nickel column, then the tag was cleaved by ubiquitin-like-protease 1 (ULP1). The tag-free target proteins were further fractioned by ion-exchange (Q, GE Healthcare) and size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column (GE Healthcare). Purified proteins were concentrated to about 30 mg/mL and stored at −80 °C in a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, pH8.0, 100 mM NaCl and 5 mM dithiothreitol. The mutations were generated by site-directed mutagenesis and the mutants were expressed and purified in the same way as the WT LnzB.

Crystallization and structure determination

The crystals of LnzB and PaiB were generated by the hanging drop diffusion method at 20 °C by mixing 2 μl of protein-compound solution and 2 μl of reservoir solution. The reservoir solution for LnzB contains 0.4 M sodium phosphate monobasic, 1.6 M potassium phosphate dibasic, 0.2 M NaCl and 0.1 M imidazole, pH8.0. The reservoir solution for PaiB contains 200 mM NaF and 20% PEG 3350.

For cryoprotection, crystals were soaked in the reservoir solution supplemented with 15-20% (v/v) glycerol. The data, collected on beam line BL10U2 at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, were indexed, integrated and scaled using HKL3000 package27. The structures were solved by molecular replacement using PHASER28. A model generated by Alphafold2 was used as the searching model for LnzB. The crystal structure of PaiB (PDB code 2OL5) was used as the search model for PaiB. Following iterative manual rebuilding and refinement were performed with the use of COOT29 and PHENIX30, respectively.

HPLC-MS based enzymatic activity assay

LnzB and its mutants were incubated with the heme at a molar ration of 1:4 at 4 °C for 1 h, and then clarified by centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 5 min to remove the precipitate. The reaction was performed in a total volume of 100 μl containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 μM LnzB or the mutants, and 1 mM L-N5-OH-Orn, at 25 °C for 40 min. 100 μl of the reaction mixture was mixed with 200 μl of acetonitrile, chilled at −20 °C for 10 min and centrifuged at 12,000 r.p.m. for 5 min. 150 μl of the supernatant was withdrawn and mixed with 10 μl of borate buffer (0.2 M, pH 8.0) and 20 μl of Fmoc-Cl (20 mM in acetonitrile) for 5 min, followed by addition of 20 μl of 0.1 M 1-aminoadamantane for incubation for another 10 min. 20 μl of the resultant supernatant was subjected to LC–MS analysis. LC–MS analysis was carried out on 8060 LC-MS/MS system (SHIMADZU) operated in negative ion electrospray modes, using an Agilent Eclipse Plus C8 (3.5 um, 4.6 mm × 30 mm) column. Elution was performed at 0.4 ml min−1 with a mobile-phase mixture consisting of a linear gradient of water and acetonitrile ((v/v): 50:50 to 0:100, 0 to 1.2 min; 0:100, 1.2 to 2.8 min; 0:100 to 50:50, 2.8 to 3.1 min; 50:50, 3.1 to 4.6 min), both of which contain 0.05% (v/v) acetic acid.

Determination of kinetic parameters

The kinetic parameters of LnzB and its mutants were measured in the same way as mentioned in the HPLC-MS-based enzymatic activity assay. The reactions were performed in a final volume of 200 μL system containing various concentrations of substrate. The concentration of LnzB and its mutants in the reaction mixture was 0.1 μM for wild-type LnzB and Y105A mutants, 0.5 μM for Y161A and E188A, and 1.0 μM for K184A, Y7A, and T113A mutants. Enzyme kinetic parameters (Km, kcat, and kcat/Km) were analyzed by the Michaelis–Menten equation by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism 10.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations

The Glide Extra-precision (XP)31 of Schrodinger (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024) has been employed to perform the molecular docking. The protein (LnzB) was prepared by Protein Preparation Wizard, including the addition of hydrogen atoms, assignment of the physiological pH, elimination of water molecules that are not involved in ligand binding, and energy minimization. A radius of the default 10 Å was selected for the active site cavity during receptor grid generation.The ligand (L-N5-OH-Orn) was prepared by LigPrep (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024). Molecular docking of L-N5-OH-Orn into LnzB was performed with default settings and the model exhibiting the lowest binding free energy was selected for binding interaction analysis.

Three independent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for the complex were conducted using Desmond32 (https://www.deshawresearch.com/resources_desmond.html). The Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations 4 (OPLS4) force field was employed, and the Simple Point Charge (SPC) water model was selected as the water model. An orthorhombic water box was constructed with a minimized volume to solvate the entire complex, which was neutralized with sodium (Na+) and chloride ions (Cl−). The solvated system was minimized and relaxed using the built-in protocol, and then submitted to 100 ns production MD simulations under NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 atm. The RMSD analysis was conducted using built-in tools in Maestro (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024).
