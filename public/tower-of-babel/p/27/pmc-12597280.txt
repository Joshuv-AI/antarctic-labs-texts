Protocol for in vitro transfection of mRNA-encapsulated lipid nanoparticles
Authors: Kounghwa Youn, Soyeon Yoo, Il Kwon Hwang, Hyunchae Gil, Hayoung Park, Haein Lee, Sunmin Oh, Gyochang Keum, Eun-Kyoung Bang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLipid nanoparticle (LNP) platforms are promising for mRNA therapeutics; however, while highly effective in vivo, commercial mRNA-LNPs show reduced transfection efficiency in vitro under serum-starved conditions, limiting mechanistic studies and cell engineering applications. Here, we present a new standard for in vitro transfection of mRNA-LNPs, including cell culture preparation, mRNA-LNP treatment in complete media, and mRNA expression level quantification. This protocol improves efficiency and reproducibility in in vitro screening and ensures consistency in the assessment of mRNA-LNPs.

Before you begin

We provide the protocol for the transfection of mRNA-encapsulated lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNPs) into nine commonly used cell lines: HEK293, Huh-7, HeLa, HepG2, MCF-7, U-87 MG, HT22, raw264.7, and SH-SY5Y. Depending on cell type, the appropriate media can be selected for adaptation. The mRNA-LNPs in this protocol were prepared based on previously reported methods.1,2,3 All procedures were performed under standard biosafety level 1 conditions with appropriate personal protective equipment, and waste was disposed of according to institutional guidelines. The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in the Graphical Abstract.

Innovation

While mRNA-LNPs perform excellently in vivo, they often exhibit drastically reduced transfection efficiency in vitro when using the well-established serum-starved method.4 Thereby, reducing the correlation between in vitro and in vivo data, compromising the reliability of LNP screening and mechanistic studies. Although several studies have reported using complete media for mRNA-LNP transfection, detailed protocols are rarely provided, making reproducibility difficult. In many cases, optimization is only demonstrated in hepatic cell lines, where mRNA-LNP uptake is inherently more efficient. This protocol introduces a simple and reproducible innovation. The protocol eliminates the need for serum-starvation and simplifies the workflow. Compared with serum-starved methods, this approach demonstrates 4- to 26-fold higher transfection efficiency across multiple cell types. Expected outcomes

This protocol significantly enhances the in vitro transfection efficiency of mRNA-LNPs. Lipofectamine was used as a positive control using the conventional protocol (Table 3 and Supplementary Protocol 1). This protocol eliminates the starvation steps and enables direct mRNA-LNP treatment in serum containing complete media compared with the conventional method. Figure 1 illustrates a simplified workflow of the protocol. A detailed step-by-step comparison is presented in Table 3. mRNA-LNPs were prepared in-house using a 50:10:38.5:1.5 molar ratio (SM-102:DSPC:cholesterol:DMG-PEG2000)—a composition widely used in commercial mRNA vaccines.5 The in vivo efficacy of this commercial formulation has already been confirmed in previous studies,1,3,17 making it a suitable benchmark for protocol validation.Figure 1Simplified workflow of the protocolA step-by-step schematic illustration of the experimental procedure for practical use.

In this protocol, complete medium was used for mRNA-LNP transfection, and the choice of serum supplier influences the outcomes. Additionally, the use of conditioned or fresh media may affect transfection efficiency. A direct comparison of media containing 10% FBS from two different manufacturers (Company W (Co. W) and Company G (Co. G)) revealed variations depending on whether the medium was conditioned or fresh, and these effects may differ depending on cell type (Figures 4B and S6). Thus, the optimal combination of supplier and medium conditions is important, but may vary case by case. Liver tropism has been previously reported for the commercial LNP formulation used in this study. Consistent with this finding, we observed that conditioned medium was preferable for hepatic cell lines, whereas fresh medium was advantageous for non-hepatic cell lines. This phenomenon can be interpreted in relation to protein corona formation.18,19 In hepatic cells, conditioning facilitates the formation of an appropriate protein corona, thereby enhancing transfection efficiency. In contrast, in non-hepatic cells, the conditioned medium interfered with optimal protein corona formation, leading to reduced efficiency. Thus, the observed outcomes were a consequence of the liver tropism of the tested LNPs. Different LNP formulations with distinct organ tropisms may yield opposite results, underscoring the critical importance of selecting the appropriate medium conditions for a given mRNA–LNP system. When uncertainty exists regarding the optimal media, a 1:1 mixture of conditioned and fresh media may serve as a practical compromise; indeed, our recent report demonstrated transfection results obtained under such mixed medium conditions.20

Compared to physical methods such as electroporation, the delivery of mRNA via LNPs is particularly important for maintaining cell viability. For instance, in the context of mRNA-based Chimeric Antigen Receptor T cell engineering, the use of mRNA-LNPs results in higher cell viability than electroporation.21 Our overall results demonstrated that mRNA-LNP transfection using this protocol exhibits a superior transfection profile compared to serum-starved conditions. Therefore, along with the growing interest in mRNA therapeutics, this protocol is expected to be useful for various applications, including gene-editing tool development, delivery and therapeutic mechanism studies, cell-based therapy, and other mRNA-based applications. To screen mRNA-LNPs when the target tissue is known, the use of matched cell lines may provide relevant in vitro screening models. However, high in vitro performance does not always predict selective in vivo delivery; thus, in vitro results should be interpreted in the context of in vivo complexity. In cases where the delivery target is not clearly defined, selecting cell lines align with the experimental goal is essential for obtaining meaningful data. Nevertheless, by enabling robust and reproducible transfection across diverse cell types, this protocol facilitates more reliable in vitro screening of mRNA-LNPs, mechanistic studies, and cell engineering strategies in contexts where serum-compatible high-efficiency delivery is critical.

Limitations

While conditioned media enhance mRNA-LNP transfection efficiency, several limitations must be considered. Batch-to-batch variability poses a major challenge, as secreted bioactive molecules vary depending on cell density, passage number, and incubation time, leading to inconsistent results. Additionally, uncontrolled factors within conditioned media, such as metabolic byproducts and degraded biomolecules, may introduce cytotoxic effects, further complicating standardization.

Conditioned media may alter cell physiology, potentially affecting transfection efficiency through mechanisms unrelated to nanoparticle uptake.

This protocol shows a consistent improvement in transfection efficiency across several cell lines, especially compared to that of serum-starved conditions, which often result in little transfection. However, similar to Lipofectamine and other transfection agents, commercial LNPs do not always function equally well in all cell types. This is a limitation of the intrinsic properties of the LNPs and how different cells take them up.

Finally, cell-type specificity remains uncertain, as different cell lines may respond variably, affecting viability and reproducibility.

Addressing these challenges may exhibit variable responses, impacting both cell viability and reproducibility through compositional analysis, controlled supplementation, and broader validation is essential for optimizing this approach in standardized experimental settings.

If the target organ or cell type for delivery is not clearly defined, it remains difficult to determine whether the transfection trends observed in one or two selected cell lines accurately reflect in vivo delivery pattern. However, for applications with specific delivery targets, such as cancer cells, or cases where in vitro research is essential, this protocol serves as a foundation for high-throughput in vitro screening to optimize nanoparticle formulations for targeted delivery.

This protocol is not guaranteed to be applicable to all cell lines, particularly primary and immune cells. Nevertheless, compared to the serum-starved conditions, we consistently observed the enhanced transfection efficiency of mRNA-LNPs in complete media with hundreds of mRNA-LNPs, even when they exhibit a different pattern of transfection efficiency. The manufacturer of the serum and medium, decision regarding the use of fresh or conditioned medium, number of cells seeded, and incubation time need to be further validated for each mRNA-LNP and cell line. In addition, variability between serum batches may influence reproducibility across experiments.

Finally, high-throughput in vitro screening may require substantial amounts of complete medium, increasing experimental costs.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The cells tested in this protocol are adherent, whereas suspension cells require modifications in the protocol.

Potential solution

For cells other than those specified, we recommend using the complete growth medium appropriate for that cell line. For suspension cells, the main difference is that the trypsinization step is unnecessary and therefore omitted. In addition, minor modifications of the transfection steps are recommended. For transfection in conditioned complete medium, add 50–100× mRNA-LNP stock solution to each well after overnight incubation of seeded cells. For transfection in fresh complete medium, add the 50–100× mRNA-LNP stock solution immediately after seeding. For transfection in mixed complete medium, seed cells at half the final medium volume, incubate overnight, and then fill the remaining half of the final volume with fresh complete medium, followed immediately by 50–100× mRNA-LNP stock solution. Compare this procedure with step 7 and steps 9–10 of this protocol.

Problem 2

The use of complete media is challenging for large-scale screening due to batch-to-batch variability and the logistical difficulty of maintaining consistent conditions across multiple experiments.

Potential solution

To enable scalability, a defined and optimized formulation of complete media should be developed. It is critical to identify the source of serum. This would allow for consistent application in high-throughput settings while preserving the transfection-enhancing properties. Additionally, automation and bioreactor-based media preparation could help ensure uniformity across experimental batches. Refer to the expected outcome section.

Problem 3

High doses of mRNA-LNPs may decrease cell viability. Although this protocol suggests an optimal treatment range of 0.5–1.0 ng/μL, the actual working concentration may vary depending on the specific LNP formulation and cell line used.

Potential solution

If reduced cell viability is observed using this protocol, we recommend decreasing the mRNA–LNP concentration or replacing the used medium with fresh growth medium 6 h post-transfection. As cell growth rate and conditions affect seeding density at transfection, seeding density should be adjusted. Refer to Step 11 of this protocol.

Problem 4

Excessive cell density can lead to reduced transfection efficiency. Even when seeding density is appropriately adjusted, rapid cell growth may result in high density at transfection, reducing efficiency.

Potential solution

We recommend testing this protocol at different seeding densities to determine the optimal conditions for the specific cell types used. We recommend to adjust the cell density to reach ∼60% confluency at the time of mRNA-LNP treatment. Refer to Step 4 of this protocol.
