How does knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams fuel creativity among members and teams? a cross-level analysis of mechanism and outcomes
Authors: Yu Su, Jin Ma, Zixuan Zhang, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough team knowledge heterogeneity is widely viewed as a catalyst of creativity, empirical evidence—particularly across levels—remains limited. Drawing on social cognitive theory, this study proposes a multilevel model in which team knowledge heterogeneity enhances team and member creativity through team creative efficacy and creative self-efficacy, respectively. Using a two-wave, multisource design with 38 venture team leaders and 221 members in China, this study tests the hypothesized cross-level relationships via hierarchical linear modeling. Results indicate that team creative efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and team creativity, whereas creative self-efficacy mediates its relationship with member creativity. Moreover, team knowledge heterogeneity exerts a cross-level effect on member creativity through team creative efficacy. By unpacking distinct within- and cross-level mechanisms, this study advances understanding of how team knowledge heterogeneity fosters creativity in venture teams and offers theoretical and managerial implications.Keywords: Venture team, Knowledge heterogeneity, Creative self-efficacy, Team creative efficacy, Member creativity, Team creativity, Cross-level analysis

Introduction

The entrepreneurship literature has generally been concerned with the impact of the lead founder, who plays an active role in facilitating the creation and development of new ventures. In this vein, substantial credit is often ascribed to the lone genius of the lead founder [7]. However, as entrepreneurship-related research has matured, scholars have increasingly recognized that the vast majority of new entrepreneurial firms are not only founded and led by solo entrepreneurs but are also led by teams [24, 58, 61, 114]. For example, according to Beckman’s [8] research, 90% of surveyed new entrepreneurial firms are not run by one entrepreneur but by teams.

Indeed, there is strong evidence demonstrating that venture teams are common in the process of founding and developing and play a dominant role in the performance of entrepreneurial firms [17]. However, other studies have failed to provide evidential support for a strong team-success link [26, 27]. Although partially contradictory, these results seem to indicate that team-based foundations may be a key condition for venture firms to succeed, and the activities and interactions among team members may catalyze the performance of venture firms in the process of creativity development.

Increasing workforce heterogeneity and deepening the pool of knowledge become the key measures of modern organizations to enhance their creativity so as to connect with the turbulent business environment [102, 118, 119, 121]. Given that heterogeneous teams with various opinions and ideas could more be intended to enhance their creativity and innovation via team interaction to improve entrepreneurial success [82, 116]. Nevertheless, the cliché of double-edged swords regarding team heterogeneity prevails, thus the implications of team heterogeneity are still controversial [9, 84, 93], which calls for further research on team heterogeneity-creativity link [92]. To heed this call, considering knowledge-based heterogeneity as the most important and salient type [45, 78, 87, 95], this study attempts to examine the relationship between knowledge-based heterogeneity in teams and creativity-related outcomes.

Furthermore, although prior studies have explored the link between team heterogeneity and creativity (e.g., [75]), they seem to mostly target macrolevel creativity (e.g., Da’as, [25], Guo43], Lia, [67], Nijstad, [78], Park, [79], Pollok, [87], Shin, [95], Wang, [111], Wang, [112] and fail to simultaneously be sufficiently concerned with the distinctiveness and effectiveness of team knowledge-based heterogeneity in relation to individual creativity. Indeed, these two kinds of creativity at different measurement levels describe level-specific organizational phenomena. Therefore, this study argues that to deepen the understanding of the link between team heterogeneity and creativity, it is of prime importance to decompose creative outcomes into individual- and collective-level creativity (e.g., team member creativity and team creativity). Therefore, these two levels of creativity are introduced into an integrated model to explore how team knowledge-based heterogeneity influences level-specific creativity simultaneously.

In addition, most previous studies on knowledge-based heterogeneity have been conducted almost exclusively on top management teams (TMTs) in mature firms [75] or R&D teams [78, 95] instead of other kinds of teams. In particular, a scarce body of studies in the domain of knowledge-based heterogeneity involves venture teams. In light of the suggestion of Wiersema and Bird [115], considering that how team members notice and respond to their heterogeneity may be contingent on the context, caution should be taken in generalizing the results from one type of team to another type of team. To answer this call, this study aims to explore how the knowledge-based heterogeneity in venture teams influences creativity-related outcomes.

Moreover, the prior literature on team heterogeneity omits the idea that there is a black box in the heterogeneity-creativity relationship, and the underlying mechanism through which team heterogeneity could lead to creativity-related outcomes has rarely been explored [65, 95]. In this respect, prior literature mainly focuses on the underlying mechanism in terms of team-interaction-related variables (e.g., exchange of information, communication, learning, motivation and negotiation) in the process of team heterogeneity being transformed into team creativity [11, 28]. However, few studies have explored the impact of creativity-based group beliefs on team creativity [95]. Creativity requires individuals and teams to take risks, and team members need to gain the necessary confidence when facing certain difficulties and problems [1].

In particular, as venture teams are more sensitive to the awareness of risk, they require more common beliefs and confidence. Therefore, the role of team creative efficacy in venture teams is more important than creative self-efficacy. To explore these beliefs, according to Chen and Kanfer [21], this study argues that parallel motivational processes simultaneously exist at both the individual level and team level. Thus, this study intends to open the black box by reasoning and testing whether both individual-level creative self-efficacy and team-level team creative efficacy are underlying mechanisms through which knowledge-based heterogeneity could be conducive to creativity-related outcomes (e.g., individual and team creativity) to further advance the creativity literature.

This study attempts to make contributions to the literature concerning team heterogeneity and creativity by exploring the following. First, to broaden the understanding of the team heterogeneity-creativity link, this study highlights knowledge-based heterogeneity as distinctive team diversity in creativity research to explore its influence on both individual-level and team-level creativity in venture teams. Second, drawing on social cognitive theory (SCT), this study introduces creative efficacy beliefs as mediators in the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and creative outcomes. Third, creativity self-efficacy and team creativity efficacy have rarely been simultaneously explored as parallel mediators of the link between team heterogeneity and creativity at both levels. This study answers the calls to identify and examine both same-level and cross-level mechanisms in the team heterogeneity-creativity link. Finally, venture teams have been under-investigated considering the importance of knowledge-based heterogeneity and creativity in venture teams. Figure 1 depicts our hypothesized model.

Theory and hypotheses

Social cognitive theory

Drawing on Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), originally articulated by Bandura [4], which has sought to elucidate how human behavior is generated, sustained, and reinforced over time. Social cognitive theory departs from stimulus–response explanations by positing that individuals are not passive recipients of environmental influences; rather, behavior is shaped through active observation, cognitive interpretation, and self-regulatory processes. Central to this framework is self-efficacy, defined as individuals’ beliefs in their capabilities to formulate goals, monitor their progress, and flexibly adjust their actions in response to feedback and situational demands [4]. As emphasized in subsequent work, self-efficacy functions as a pivotal motivational mechanism through which individuals translate intentions into sustained action [99]. Within the creativity literature, social cognitive theory has provided a particularly influential theoretical lens for explaining heterogeneity in employees’ creative behaviors [5].

Information and decision-making theories

The information and decision-making theories offer a robust explain for understanding that heterogeneous teams can provide diverse information, and through the interactions among members, these teams are more likely to achieve the integration of ideas, experiences, and resources, which is conducive to team creativity [113]. Drawing on information and decision-making theories, it is generally assumed that greater heterogeneity in team members’ disciplinary backgrounds and areas of specialization expands the breadth of information, knowledge bases, and cognitive perspectives available within the team [95]. From this standpoint, teams characterized by higher levels of heterogeneity are expected to possess richer informational resources and more diverse interpretive frames, which, in turn, would ostensibly enhance their creative potential. Consequently, prior theorizing has often implicitly suggested a monotonic relationship whereby increasing heterogeneity is presumed to be uniformly conducive to higher levels of creativity.

Team knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams and creativity

The concept of venture teams has induced considerable debate among the academic community. According to Kamm et al., [58], venture teams are defined as “two or more individuals who jointly establish a firm in which they have a financial interest”. Moreover, according to Gartner et al., [34], this definition has been broadened to involve individuals who have a direct influence on entrepreneurial firms’ strategic choice. Specifically, Ucbasaran et al., [105] combine both descriptions by noting that the equity stake condition should be made stricter and that an individual could be regarded as a member of the venture team when he or she has imposed a minimum equity stake.

Venture teams differentiate themselves in terms of intrateam relationships, resource attributes, cognitive characteristics, etc. Based on the resource-based view, venture teams can be divided into homogeneous teams and heterogeneous teams. Accordingly, a higher heterogeneity of venture teams means that team members have more diversified knowledge, experience and skills and resources and capabilities [46, 50, 55, 57]. In heterogeneous teams, members with different opinions and ideas are more likely to stimulate their creativity and innovation through team interaction to promote entrepreneurial success [82, 116].

To better understand the heterogeneity-creativity link, it is recommended that the specific type of team heterogeneity that is most associated with the outcomes of interest be examined because all types of team heterogeneity are not equal (e.g., [75]). In this respect, the knowledge-based heterogeneity of teams is considered one of the most important and salient types of heterogeneity for team creativity [45, 78, 87, 95]. Knowledge-based heterogeneity, defined as variation in team members’ task-relevant knowledge and skills [120], provides diverse perspectives, fostering creative activities such as idea recombination and novel problem-solving [1, 74, 77]. Drawing on information and decision-making theories, greater heterogeneity equips teams with broader information and cognitive resources, enhancing creativity [6, 78, 82, 95, 113].

Translated to venture teams, these findings suggest that in the process of creating value, knowledge-based heterogeneity could aid team members in producing different perspectives and seeking solutions to problems, uncertainties, and challenges by offering various information and resources. As such, the knowledge-based heterogeneity in venture teams could facilitate team members to internalize their willingness to be involved in creative efforts and activities. Based on this reasoning, this study proposes the following:

Hypothesis 1

Team knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams is positively related to individual-level team members’ creativity.

According to information and decision-making theories, knowledge heterogeneity accelerates cognitive processing and enhances information utilization [113]. Team members’ differing knowledge backgrounds foster varied task understandings that promote team creativity [48], as individuals with diverse knowledge, skills, perspectives, and thinking styles serve as sources of team creativity [93]. In interdisciplinary teams, integrating rich domain-specific experiences, technical skills, and professional knowledge optimizes decision-making and generates creative thinking, substantially enhancing team creativity [22, 95]. These findings indicate that team creativity depends on information sharing and knowledge integration, achieved by heterogeneous teams through internal interactions that synthesize diverse individual attributes. In venture teams, members’ heterogeneous industry experience, information, skills, and professional backgrounds foster diverse perspectives that stimulate creative breakthroughs and enhance team creativity potential. Accordingly, this study proposes that knowledge-heterogeneous venture teams exhibit higher creativity than homogeneous ones.

Hypothesis 2

Team knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams is positively related to team creativity.

Creative efficacy beliefs as mediators

Drawing on social cognitive theory [4, 5], efficacy beliefs drive effort and persistence, enhancing creativity [32, 97, 100]. Thus, this study proposes that individual creative self-efficacy and team creative efficacy mediate the effects of team knowledge heterogeneity on creativity at both levels [95, 99].

Creative self-efficacy as mediator

Creative self-efficacy reflects individuals’ confidence in their ability to be creative, shaped by contextual cues from their work environment [3, 23, 41, 100]. Team knowledge heterogeneity—an organizationally bound attribute [59, 83]—provides diverse experiences and perspectives, fostering interactions that allow members to pool cognitive resources, enhancing creative self-efficacy and supporting superior problem-solving and performance [45, 99].

Compared with homogeneous teams, knowledge-heterogeneous teams expose members to diverse information and perspectives, elevating creative expectations and strengthening beliefs in their capacity to solve problems innovatively. As a contextual resource, knowledge heterogeneity encourages engagement in creative problem solving and fosters confidence through support and cognitive stimulation. Prior research consistently links creative self-efficacy to individual creativity [101], demonstrating that it shapes creative expectations, motivates idea generation [41, 99, 108], and sustains effort toward creative performance [3, 100].

Translating the above findings to venture teams, considering the proposed positive link between knowledge-based heterogeneity and individual creativity, this study poses that team knowledge heterogeneity, as a working context, could effectively foster the creative self-efficacy of team members and that creative self-efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and team members’ individual creativity. As such, a higher level of knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams might instill team members feelings of being motivated to exert more effort in achieving creative goals, thus enhancing their creative outcomes. Based on the above reasoning, this study proposes the following:

Hypothesis 3

Creative self-efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and individual-level team members’ creativity.

Team creative efficacy as mediator

Team creative efficacy—members’ shared confidence in their collective creative capability—promotes creativity at both individual and team levels [32, 95, 96]. Rooted in social cognitive theory [5], it reflects collective beliefs about achieving creative outcomes. Given limited research linking it to team creativity [95, 103], this study proposes that team creative efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and team creativity.

Unlike individual self-efficacy, collective efficacy is an emergent group-level construct rather than an aggregation of individual beliefs [31]. Through intrateam interactions, teams develop shared interpretations of collective capability that guide joint action [32, 76] and differ fundamentally from isolated individual perceptions [70]. Whereas group potency reflects shared beliefs in general effectiveness [16, 44], team creative efficacy captures collective confidence in generating novel and useful ideas. Domain-specific efficacy better predicts corresponding outcomes than general efficacy [38]. Thus, understanding team creativity requires examining content- and level-specific efficacy beliefs rather than broad collective confidence [38, 99].

Social cognitive theory suggests that collective efficacy derives from contextual cues [4, 70]. Knowledge-based heterogeneity, as a salient team context, shapes such beliefs through interaction. By integrating diverse expertise, members recognize their pooled cognitive resources and develop shared confidence in their collective capacity to achieve creative goals [37]. Teams high in knowledge-based heterogeneity are better positioned to leverage divergent expertise and cognitive resources, fostering shared beliefs in their capacity to attain collective creative goals. By integrating members’ unique perspectives through dynamic interaction, such teams align individual distinctiveness with collective identity, thereby strengthening confidence and expectations regarding their creative capabilities (i.e., team creative efficacy).

Prior research links team efficacy to team performance [42, 70]. Extending this logic, team creative efficacy should enhance team creativity by strengthening motivation and creativity-relevant processes. Efficacy beliefs shape choice, effort, and persistence [5] and constitute a core determinant of work motivation [4, 97], particularly for creative outcomes [32]. Because creativity requires risk taking, extra effort, coordination, and persistence amid obstacles [36], higher team creative efficacy could translate into elevated team creativity. Because motivation underpins effective cognitive processing yet remains underexamined in diversity research [106], team creative efficacy may shape creativity-relevant processes. Creative outcomes depend on high-quality interaction [39]. As an emergent state and input to subsequent processes [72], team creative efficacy might foster engagement in creativity-conducive processes [29, 32].

In venture teams, strong collective beliefs in creative capability motivate members to share, integrate, and recombine ideas into novel and useful outcomes. By instilling confidence and persistence, team creative efficacy sustains engagement in creativity-conducive processes. Accordingly, higher team creative efficacy is likely to translate into greater involvement in creative processes and, ultimately, elevated team creativity.

Hypothesis 4

Team creative efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and team-level team creativity.

Drawing on the top-down effect of team-level creativity on employee performance [15, 21, 42], this study poses that team-level factors (e.g., TCE) might have a potential positive effect on individual-level creativity. Specifically, according to Bandura [5] and Ford [32], employee motivation with respect to creativity could be enhanced by team efficacy. In essence, team creative efficacy sets collective beliefs and expectations with regard to creative achievements that could encourage individuals to exert greater effort to engage in creative activities [5]. Therefore, team members with a high level of beliefs and expectations concerning their team’s creative capability are likely motivated to break through in terms of improvements and to persevere when exposed to obstacles [95, 96]. Moreover, in terms of individual interactions [15, 95], individuals with strong beliefs and expectations with regard to their team’s creative capability (e.g., team creative efficacy) are more confident and willing to exert consistent effort toward creative activities and are more likely to initiate creativity by sharing and exchanging their information and ideas [37, 80].

Building on this reasoning, this study posits that team knowledge heterogeneity enhances team creative efficacy, which in turn promotes individual member creativity. By cultivating collective creative-specific efficacy, knowledge heterogeneity motivates sustained creative engagement. Accordingly, team creative efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and individual creativity.

Hypothesis 5

Team creative efficacy mediates the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity and individual-level team members’ creativity.

Methods

Research setting, sample and procedure

This study conducted the research survey at six large, government-sponsored, high-tech entrepreneurial and creative parks, located in several cities (e.g., Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhuhai) of Guangdong province, China. With permission from directors of administrative offices in multiple entrepreneurial and creative parks, venture teams were randomly sampled and contacted. Each selected team received survey packages containing a cover letter and self-administered questionnaires distributed to both team leaders and team members. The cover letter explained the study’s purpose, practical relevance, confidentiality assurances, and the importance of accurate responses. All questionnaires were completed anonymously and returned directly to the research team in sealed envelopes. Leader and member surveys were matched using identification codes. To enhance response rates, each participating team was offered a detailed feedback report summarizing the research findings.

In this study, team leaders were lead entrepreneurs (e.g., CEOs) or co-founders responsible for strategic direction, operational coordination, and boundary-spanning activities. Team members were senior executives (e.g., COOs, CTOs, marketing managers) involved in strategic decision-making and venture operations. Consistent with Ucbasaran et al., [105], individuals were considered venture team members if they held a minimum equity stake. Given their roles and ownership positions, these members were directly engaged in innovation-related decisions, including technology adoption and new product or service development.

Sampled ventures operated across diverse knowledge-intensive industries (e.g., software, biopharmaceuticals, new energy). Of 275 members in 47 teams, 221 matched member–leader responses from 38 teams were retained (80.3% response rate). Team size ranged from 3 to 14 members (M = 5.82). All team members participated in the survey, allowing team-level constructs to be derived from complete within-team responses and enhancing the reliability of the aggregation procedure.

This study employed a two-wave survey design with a two-month interval to mitigate common method bias. At Time 1, team members reported demographics, team knowledge heterogeneity, creative self-efficacy, and team creative efficacy. At Time 2, team leaders provided team characteristics (team age and size) and evaluated member and team creativity. Among participants, 57.5% were male and 42.5% female; 52.5% were aged 30–39, and 42.1% held a bachelor’s degree; 71% were male leader and 29% female leader; team size ranged from 3 to 10 members (M = 5.8). The issue of potential common method bias was minimized by means of the different sources of survey data [86], including team member surveys and leader surveys, demonstrating that common method bias was unlikely to pose a serious threat to the validity of the findings in this study.

Measures

According to the translation-back-translation procedure [13], bilingual experts translated English to Chinese and then back to English. The scale measure items in this study were assessed on a 7-point Likert-type scale, with scores ranging from 1 (I do not agree at all) to 7 (I completely agree).

Team knowledge heterogeneity

Consistent with prior conceptualizations of cognitive heterogeneity in teams [55, 93], the present study operationalized the focal construct as cognitive team knowledge heterogeneity. Specifically, a three-item scale adapted from Campion et al., [16] and Jehn et al., [55] was employed to assess the extent to which team members perceive heterogeneity in knowledge, expertise, and informational backgrounds within their team. Individual responses were aggregated to the team level to capture shared perceptions.

The emphasis on cognitive heterogeneity, rather than objective compositional indices (e.g., functional or demographic diversity), is theoretically grounded in creativity research. Creativity fundamentally involves cognitive processes [93], and deep diversity—such as cognitive diversity—has been shown to stimulate information elaboration, idea recombination, perspective integration, and experimentation [46, 50, 55, 57, 106]. Accordingly, members’ perceptions of knowledge differences constitute a more proximal predictor of team creative processes than objective heterogeneity alone.

Team members evaluated perceived knowledge heterogeneity within their teams. A sample item was “Your entrepreneurial team members have a variety of different educational backgrounds and experiences”. Because team knowledge heterogeneity was theorized as a team-level construct, individual ratings were aggregated. Following LeBreton and Senter [66], within-team agreement and between-team variance were assessed. The mean rwg(j) was 0.69 (SD = 0.36). Consistent with Bliese [10], ICC(1) and ICC(2) were calculated, yielding ICC(1) = 0.17 and ICC(2) = 0.55 (F = 2.22, p < 0.001). These statistics supported sufficient agreement and justified aggregation to the team level.

Creative self-efficacy

Creative self-efficacy was measured with 3 items of the scale adopted from Tierney and Farmer [99]. Team members were asked to rate the extent to which the statements accurately described their efficacy with respect to creative work. A sample item was “I have confidence in my ability to solve problems creatively”.

Team creative efficacy

Team creative efficacy was assessed using a three-item scale adapted from Tierney and Farmer [99] and Shin and Eom [96], reframing creative self-efficacy items to capture collective efficacy beliefs. A sample item is: “Members of my team have confidence in their abilities to solve problems creatively.” This measure captures members’ perceptions of shared confidence in the team’s creative capabilities and has been validated in prior research (e.g., Cai et al., [15], Chen, [19], Kim, [60], Shin, [95], Shin, [96]. Consistent with its team-level conceptualization, individual ratings were aggregated. Within-team agreement and between-team variance supported aggregation (mean Rwg(j) = 0.69, SD = 0.33; F = 2.51, p < 0.001; ICC(1) = 0.21; ICC(2) = 0.60).

Member creativity

Member creativity was measured with the 4 items of the scale adopted from Tierney and Farmer [101]. Team leaders were asked to report how extensively they felt the individual creativity of each team member was. A sample item was “This team member seeks new ideas and ways to solve problems”.

Team creativity

Team creativity was measured with a 6-item scale adopted from George and Zhou [36]. Team leaders were asked to report how extensively they felt team creativity given that team leaders are considered reliable sources of team-level information [85]. A sample item was “Your team often comes up with new and practical ideas to improve performance”.

Controls

Prior studies indicated that several variables might influence creativity, which were taken as control variables in this work to avoid research bias. At the individual level, team members’ gender, age, and educational level were taken as control variables (e.g., [41, 90, 93, 94]). At the team level, team tenure (in years) was taken as control variable [62].

Analytic strategy

Given the nested structure of the data and the multilevel nature of the hypotheses, this study employed hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) with restricted maximum likelihood estimation [89, 110]. HLM appropriately addresses the statistical limitations of traditional single-level techniques when analyzing nested data [49] and enhances external validity by accounting for both team- and individual-level influences across multiple organizations.

For team-level hypotheses (H2 and H4), where team knowledge heterogeneity, team creative efficacy, and team creativity operate at Level 2, this study conducted hierarchical regression analyses at the team level. For cross-level hypotheses, this study first examined between-team variance in the dependent variables to justify multilevel modeling. This study then followed a three-step procedure: (1) testing the cross-level effect of team knowledge heterogeneity (Level 2) on member creativity (Level 1; H1); (2) examining its effect on creative self-efficacy (Level 1), controlling for team creative efficacy (Level 2); and (3) assessing the joint effects of creative self-efficacy (Level 1) and team creative efficacy (Level 2) on member creativity, controlling for team knowledge heterogeneity (H3, H5).

Moreover, according to Preacher et al.,’s [88] recommendation, a parametric bootstrapping procedure was applied to test for mediation and cross-level mediation. A Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 replications was conducted to estimate the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval (CI) around the level-specific indirect effect (e.g., Koopman et al., [62], Lanaj, [64], Matta, [73].

Convergent and discriminant validity

Following Fornell and Larcker [33], all constructs demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity: Cronbach’s alphas and composite reliability exceeded 0.70, square multiple correlation (SMC) values surpassed 0.50, and both factor loadings and average variance extracted (AVE) met recommended thresholds, supporting internal consistency and convergent validity (see Table 2). Following Anderson and Gerbing [2], a series of confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) compared the proposed five-factor model with alternative three- and four-factor models. The five-factor model demonstrated superior fit (χ²[142] = 209.26, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.962, RMSEA = 0.046, SRMR = 0.041), supporting discriminant validity ([51]; see Table 3).

To further address concerns regarding small-sample instability, additional robustness analyses were conducted. Two teams characterized by minimal membership (three members each) and comparatively lower agreement were excluded, and aggregation statistics were recalculated. The ICC(2) for cognitive team knowledge heterogeneity increased slightly to 0.57, indicating stable team-mean reliability. Importantly, the substantive findings remained unchanged. Cognitive team knowledge heterogeneity continued to exhibit significant positive associations with team creativity (γ = 0.619, p < 0.001) and team creative efficacy (γ = 0.597, p < 0.001). Moreover, the team-level indirect effect of cognitive team knowledge heterogeneity on team creativity via team creative efficacy remained significant (indirect effect = 0.369, 95% CI [0.298, 0.446]), thereby providing continued support for H3. Collectively, these analyses indicate that the reported team-level relationships are robust to concerns regarding ICC(2) magnitude and small team sample size.

Post-hoc analysis

An ICC(2) (team knowledge heterogeneity) of approximately 0.55 is sometimes regarded as modest for team-mean reliability. However, in organizational research—particularly in studies involving small, naturally bounded groups such as entrepreneurial teams—team size is often limited, which mechanically constrains ICC(2) estimates [10]. Comparable multilevel studies conducted in similar contexts have reported ICC(2) values within a similar range (e.Chen and Bliese, [18], Gilson, [40], Jiang, [56], Liao, [68], Lin, [69], Xu, [117]. Although relatively lower ICC(2) values may reduce statistical power to detect emergent group-level effects [10], they do not preclude aggregation when the construct is theoretically conceptualized at the collective level and when between-group variance is statistically meaningful [18, 63, 68, 109]. In the present study, cognitive team knowledge heterogeneity was explicitly theorized as a team-level construct; thus, aggregation was retained in accordance with both conceptual and methodological guidance.

Discussion

Theoretical implications

With these findings, this study makes theoretical contributions to the literature on team heterogeneity and creativity. First, this study advances the team heterogeneity and creativity literatures by focusing on knowledge-based heterogeneity in venture teams. Following Pelled [81] and Williams and O’Reilly [116], this study contends that heterogeneity dimensions should be selected based on conceptual alignment with focal outcomes. In entrepreneurial contexts, knowledge heterogeneity is especially salient, as it supplies the diverse perspectives, expertise, and skills critical to enabling creative processes and outcomes at both individual and team levels. While prior work has primarily examined team heterogeneity’s macro-level effects on creativity [43, 75, 78, 79, 95, 111], micro-level mechanisms—particularly individual member creativity—remain underexplored. Drawing on information/decision-making theory [113], this study demonstrates that knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams operates as a cross-level catalyst, simultaneously enhancing both individual- and team-level creativity, thereby extending theory on heterogeneity’s multi-level role in entrepreneurial creativity processes.

Second, this study introduces creative efficacy beliefs—both at individual (creative self-efficacy) and team (team creative efficacy) levels—as novel mediators linking team knowledge heterogeneity to creativity outcomes. Grounded in social cognitive theory, this study posits that heterogeneous knowledge exposure bolsters members’ and teams’ efficacy beliefs, which in turn sustain effort and persistence necessary for creative idea generation [4, 32, 97]. Whereas most heterogeneity research has emphasized team processes as mediators [53], surprisingly few studies have examined efficacy-related emergent states [72]. By empirically verifying these mediating pathways, our work addresses long-standing calls to unpack the black box of the heterogeneity–creativity relationship [65], advancing understanding of psychological mechanisms in entrepreneurial team creativity.

Notably, our findings reveal that team knowledge heterogeneity shapes creative outcomes via creative self-efficacy and team creative efficacy, addressing longstanding calls to examine efficacy beliefs as intervening mechanisms [35, 92]. By identifying these emergent states as key mediators, the study enriches heterogeneity research and extends entrepreneurial team literature by empirically establishing creativity-specific efficacy beliefs as critical pathways through which diverse knowledge fosters individual and collective creative capabilities [4, 122].

Third, this study not only theoretically developed the cross-level model regarding creativity at both levels by integrating literatures on diversity, efficacy, as well as creativity, but also empirically demonstrated the mediating roles of creative efficacy beliefs on the relationship between knowledge-based team heterogeneity and creative outcomes with ongoing teams. To extend previous studies on how team-level factors could be conductive to individual-level creativity (e.Chen et al., [20], Hirst, [47], Liu, [71], this study mitigates the research gap in this regard from the multilevel perspective by simultaneously exploring the level-specific mechanisms (e.g., individual-level creative self-efficacy and team-level team creative efficacy) through which the knowledge-based heterogeneity of venture teams leads to creativity at both the individual level and the team level. Thus, with respect to the relationship between team heterogeneity and individual/team creativity, our findings highlight the value of knowledge-based heterogeneity in creativity research both individually and collectively.

Although organizational studies note that team-level efficacy might play an important role in fostering individual-level outcomes [91], the paucity of such research has empirically explored the cross-level mechanisms that transfer the impact of team heterogeneity to individual creative outcomes. To answer the call to empirically verify the top-down effects of team-level properties on individual-level creativity [20, 52, 104], our findings identify that team-level creative efficacy could play a cross-level mediating role in the relationship between team heterogeneity and individual creativity. As such, the theoretical argument in social cognitive theory [4, 5] has been strengthened by our findings. In this regard, when a team has a higher level of creative-specific efficacy, team members are more likely to exert efforts toward creative-related achievements [42]. Members’ strong association with the team could encourage their creative contribution to team outcomes [12]. In particular, given that there is debate with regard to the importance of various types of efficacy in the creativity literature (e.g., Walumbwa et al., [107], our findings extend social cognitive theory in an organizational context by indicating that both individual-level and team-level creative-specific efficacy could be conducive to level-specific creativity.

Finally, our study investigates the team heterogeneity-creativity link in venture teams with a field nested dataset from venture teams, especially in the domain of high-tech industries in China. In this case, our findings provide some of the first empirical evidence of how team knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams facilitates team creativity potential at both the individual level and team level, considering that prior studies on the team heterogeneity-creativity relationship have mainly concerned working teams (e.g., [43, 79, 98, 111]), R&D teams (e.g., Shin and Zhou, [95], and top management teams (TMTs) (e.g., Buyl et al., [14].

Practical implications

In light of our multilevel model analysis, this study provides practical implications. First, for the pre-founding stage of new venture teams, with the aim of aiding venture team members in challenging the status quo and helping venture teams integrate and collaborate to generate and realize creative ideas, venture team leaders should recognize the value of knowledge-based heterogeneity in the current business environment by fostering venture teams with heterogeneous compositions in terms of knowledge and information. Second, for established venture teams, knowledge heterogeneity alone does not ensure creativity. Our findings underscore efficacy beliefs as the key mechanism translating diversity into creative outcomes. To fully leverage heterogeneous expertise, leaders should cultivate and reinforce shared confidence in the team’s creative capabilities, thereby unlocking the creative potential embedded in diverse cognitive resources. Third, our findings further underscore the value of creative efficacy at both individual and team levels. Leaders should model and reinforce creativity-supportive behaviors, clarify shared goals, and strengthen collective confidence in achieving them. Cultivating strong team creative efficacy can enhance members’ creative contributions both collectively and individually.

Limitations and directions for future research

Although the hypotheses of this study were grounded in theory and the findings supported our proposed theoretical model, some limitations need to be considered. First, a further limitation concerns the operationalization of team knowledge heterogeneity. This study focuses on cognitive team knowledge heterogeneity as perceived by team members rather than objective compositional heterogeneity. Although this approach is theoretically consistent with prior research, perceptual assessments may also share variance with other perceptual team constructs. Future research could incorporate both objective compositional indicators and perceptual assessments of knowledge heterogeneity to disentangle their unique effects on influencing team creativity and related outcomes.

Second, although this study employed multi-source data (leader and member surveys) to mitigate common method bias, creativity was assessed via supervisory ratings rather than objective indicators. Given the cross-industry nature of our venture team sample, comparable objective measures (e.g., patents, new product introductions) were infeasible [123]. While proxy ratings are widely accepted in creativity research [30, 95], future studies could strengthen causal inference by incorporating archival outputs, expert judgments, or peer evaluations where feasible.

Third, another limitation concerns the temporal design of the study. Because the focal predictor and mediating variables were measured within the same survey wave, the temporal ordering of the proposed mechanism cannot be definitively established. Although the theoretical model specifies directional relationships, stronger causal inference would require multi-wave data in which predictors and mediators are assessed at distinct time points. Future research should adopt longitudinal or time-lagged designs to more rigorously establish temporal precedence and strengthen causal claims.

Conclusions

Drawing on social cognitive theory, this study explores the relationship between team knowledge heterogeneity in venture teams and individual/team creativity as well as individual/team creative efficacy beliefs to extend the understanding of knowledge-based heterogeneity as a specific team composition by 2-wave longitudinal research design. Our findings can be applied by venture practitioners in terms of building entrepreneurial teams and managing workplace creativity given the dynamism of entrepreneurial environments.
