Initial leukemic epigenomic state determines hypomethylating agent response

Vizeacoumar, Mackenzie MacAuley, Emilie Haniak, Charles Craddock, Paresh Vyas, Luca Malcovati, Rena Buckstein, Michelle Moksa, Martin Hirst, Franco J. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypomethylating agents (HMAs) are a mainstay of therapy for myeloid cancers, but genetic biomarkers do not predict who will respond to treatment. Using a variety of single-cell sequencing approaches to define the epigenomic state of responder and nonresponder leukemic cells, we demonstrate that leukemic stem cells (LSC) exist in at least two different epigenomic states: a hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)-or multipotent progenitor (MPP)-like state that is sensitive to HMAs, independent of genetic mutations, or a lymphoid-primed MPP (LMPP)-like nonresponder state. Hypomethylation and chromatin accessibility at ZNF143- and CTCF-binding sites results in activation of HOXB4, which defines the HSC/MPP-like state and HMA-sensitivity. Our study provides evidence that the epigenomic state of the LSC is a major determinant of response to HMAs, and demonstrates that a routine clinical assay can identify patients who will respond.

Introduction

Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) are among the most common hematopoietic malignancies, with approximately 30–40% of MDS progressing to AML1,2. Hypomethylating agents (HMAs) are a core therapy for MDS/AML, either as a single agent or as a backbone for combination therapies3–5. More than 50% of MDS/AML patients receiving single-agent HMA show primary non-response, but several months of therapy are generally required before this can be determined3,4. Despite extensive investigation, a widely reproducible genetic biomarker for HMA responsiveness has not been identified6–10.

Studies examining HMA response have rarely examined the primitive leukemic stem cell (LSC) compartment, which initiates and maintains the malignancy11–16. The lineage (lin)–CD34+CD38– compartment, which is enriched in LSC, may immunophenotypically resemble either hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) or multipotent progenitor (MPP) cells, or a downstream progenitor - lymphoid-primed MPP (LMPP)11,14,17. As the epigenomic state is the key determinant of cell type18, we postulated that differences in the LSC epigenome may underlie the observed heterogeneity in response to HMAs.

Here we use various single-cell epigenome sequencing approaches19,20 to demonstrate that LSCs exist in at least two different epigenomic states: an HSC/MPP-like state that is sensitive to HMAs, independent of genetic mutations and a LMPP-like nonresponder state. Hypomethylation and chromatin accessibility at ZNF143- and CTCF-binding sites result in activation of HOXB4, which defines the HSC/MPP-like state and HMA-sensitivity. Our study provides evidence that the epigenomic state of the LSC is a major determinant of response to HMAs, and demonstrates that a routine clinical assay may identify patients who will respond.

Results

HMA responsiveness is defined by distinct epigenomic states of primitive leukemic cell populations

We investigated the response to HMAs in 4 MDS and AML patient cohorts. Clinical and genetic data and response to HMAs for the cohorts are listed in Supplementary Dataset 1–4 and Supplementary Figs. 1, 2. To determine whether the epigenomic state of the LSC compartment was a determinant of 5-azacytidine (Aza) response, we performed single cell whole genome bisulfite sequencing (scWGBS) using post-bisulfite adapter ligation19 on index-sorted, lineage-negative (lin-) CD34+CD38- cells from bone marrow of 7 treatment-naïve patients, 4 responders and 3 nonresponders to HMA treatment according to their clinical response (cohort 1, Supplementary Dataset 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Nonresponders were enriched in lin-CD34+CD38-CD45RA+ cells within the CD99+ fraction21,22 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4a), suggestive of a malignant LMPP-like population (Fig. 1d). Applying a methylation-based classifier derived from normal HSPC23 to the leukemic cells revealed that LMPP-like cells were virtually exclusive to the nonresponder cluster (Fig. 1e, f), and this pattern persisted even when the nonresponder patient containing the highest predicted proportion of LMPP-like cells (patient 437) was excluded from the clustering (Supplementary Fig. 4b–d). Common lymphoid progenitor (CLP)-like cells were also overrepresented in Aza-nonresponder samples (Fig. 1e, f), consistent with the lymphoid output potential of LMPP24. In contrast, responder cell populations were enriched in HSC- and MPP-like leukemic cells (Fig. 1e, f).

Interestingly, patient 449, an Aza nonresponder, was the source of almost all the misclassified nonresponder cells within the responder cluster (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Consistent with the preceding data, LMPP and CLP-like cells were predicted only within the nonresponder cluster in this patient sample (Supplementary Fig. 5b), suggesting the co-existence of Aza-sensitive and -resistant clones in this MDS patient. Clonal tracking using mitochondrial variants identified three clonotypes in patient 449, two in the nonresponder cluster (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 5c–g), confirming the presence of distinct clonal populations segregating by response, prior to Aza treatment.

To confirm epigenomic state differences of primitive cells between Aza responders and nonresponders using an alternate method, we performed DOGMA-seq20 on 12 additional patient samples prior to Aza treatment (cohort 2, Supplementary Dataset 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Classification of the stem/progenitor population (Supplementary Fig. 6b) using an RNA-seq based cell-type classifier25 confirmed that LMPP-like cells were restricted to the nonresponder patient samples (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Consistent with the LMPP-like cell bias in nonresponders, CLP-like and mature lymphoid populations were also significantly overrepresented in Aza nonresponder samples (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Using scADT-seq to define the lin-CD34+CD38- population in cohort 2, we see similarly discrete responder and nonresponder cell clusters by scRNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 7d), as seen with scWGBS in cohort 1 (Fig. 1a). In contrast, in the blast population (lin-CD34+CD117+), separation between responders and nonresponders is less discrete (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

To determine whether response to another HMA, decitabine, was also dependent on the epigenomic state of the primitive cell population (cohort 3, Supplementary Dataset 3), we performed a single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (scATAC-seq)26 on sorted lin-CD34+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 8). Concordant with the Aza results, chromatin accessibility profiles separated responder and nonresponder cells into distinct clusters (Fig. 1i).

To determine whether methylation analysis of bulk CD34+ samples would return similar results, we compared the differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between leukemic responder and nonresponder lin-CD34+CD38- cells in the current study with bulk CD34+ MDS responder vs nonresponder samples in an independent study27. DMRs from the current study overlapped significantly with the latter study, although there were substantially fewer DMRs in the bulk CD34+ cell data (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). We also queried publicly available bulk RNA-seq data from the BEAT-AML cohort28. Although patient samples with a higher predicted fraction of LMPP cells, by CIBERSORTx cell type deconvolution29, trended towards increased resistance to Aza ex vivo, the correlation was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 10). These findings highlight the utility of single-cell sequencing to identify differences only seen in rare leukemic stem/progenitor cell populations.

Normal LMPP are resistant to HMA compared to MPP

DMRs between leukemic responder and nonresponder cells in the current study overlapped significantly with DMRs between normal MPP and LMPP24 (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Given the correlation of DMRs between normal MPP and LMPP and HMA responders and nonresponders, respectively, we asked whether normal LMPP would also be resistant to HMA compared to MPP (Fig. 1k). Normal MPP treated with Aza or vehicle showed increased cell death in response to Aza compared to LMPP (Fig. 1l and Supplementary Fig. 12a). Proliferation rates were similar between normal MPP and LMPP (Supplementary Fig. 12b), and similarly, Aza-responsive patient cells did not show altered cell cycle kinetics compared to nonresponsive patient cells (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Hence resistance to Aza is associated with an epigenomic state associated with LMPP independent of cell proliferation rate.

Aza responsiveness of leukemic cells is determined by ZNF143 activity

Consistent with this finding, cells with reduced relative methylation from the biphenotypic sample (449) clustered with the responder population (Supplementary Fig. 13b). To identify transcription factors (TFs) that might modulate Aza responsiveness, we compared the average methylation of cohort 1 samples at transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) of responders and nonresponders across 1034 ChIP-seq experiments30, resulting in 64 TFs with > 4% increased methylation in nonresponders at a padj-value threshold of <0.1 (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b and Supplementary Dataset 5). Hierarchical clustering of the cells based on average methylation within the binding sites of these 64 TFs resolved responder from nonresponder cells (Supplementary Fig. 15), and UMAP dimension reduction using only these binding sites again clearly differentiated responder from nonresponder cell populations (Fig. 2c). Further, using the same binding sites on normal HSPC showed significant clustering of normal LMPP and CLP23 with the nonresponder leukemic population (Fig. 2c). Binding sites for factors associated with three-dimensional chromatin structure (CTCF, ZNF143 and cohesins) were among the most significantly hypomethylated in responders (Fig. 2d), and clustered in a single sub-branch of the TF hierarchy (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Dataset 5).

The above findings were corroborated in cohort 2, assayed by DOGMA-seq, where cells of responding patients had increased chromatin accessibility relative to nonresponders within regions regulated by the 64 TFs, including CTCF, ZNF143 and cohesins (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). Further, both transcription factor network analysis of gene body and promoter accessibility, and UMAP dimension reduction of TFBS accessibility placed CTCF and ZNF143 within the same network or cluster, along with several cohesins (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). Binding sites of ZNF143 and 24 more of the 64 TFs were also significantly hypomethylated in normal MPP compared to LMPP (Supplementary Dataset 6 and Supplementary Fig. 19), again underscoring the similarities between normal MPP and LMPP, and responder and nonresponder LSC, respectively.

Given that binding sites of the 64 TFs were differentially hypomethylated in responders, and most TFs preferentially bind hypomethylated DNA, we expected that knockout of these TFs would increase resistance to Aza. We identified Aza-sensitive and -insensitive cell lines in DepMap (Supplementary Fig. 20a)31, and verified selected Aza-sensitive (OCI-AML3 and SKM-1) and -resistant (THP-1 and OCI-AML2) lines and the reported Aza-sensitive line, MOLM-13 (Supplementary Fig. 20b)32–35. We performed a CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screen targeted at the 64 TFs in MOLM-13 and SKM-1 cell lines, which identified 8 TFs that induced resistance to Aza in both cell lines when knocked out (Supplementary Fig. 20c). Co-ranking based on differential cell viability after Aza treatment revealed ZNF143 as inducing the greatest resistance, and also identified CTCF and the cohesin subunit, SMC1A (Fig. 2f). We confirmed decreased Aza sensitivity in three cell lines following shRNA-mediated knockdown of ZNF143 (Fig. 2g, and Supplementary Fig. 20d). Similarly, knockdown of CTCF also resulted in reduced sensitivity to Aza (Supplementary Fig. 20e, f). Importantly, overexpression of ZNF143 resulted in increased sensitivity to Aza treatment in the two resistant cell lines as well as OCI-AML3 (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 20g, h).

To further explore ZNF143-CTCF interactions, we performed a HiChIP39 experiment combining ChIP-seq of CTCF with 3D chromatin conformation capture in the OCI-AML3 cell line with or without knockdown of ZNF143 (Supplementary Fig. 21a–c). As noted in the mouse hematopoietic system37, there was minimal alteration of large-scale chromatin architecture, specifically A/B compartmentalization, with ZNF143 knockdown (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 22a–c). In contrast, we found realignment of TAD structures (Supplementary Fig. 23a, b) and fewer, longer chromatin loops globally after ZNF143 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 23c, d). Coverage of CTCF-ZNF143 co-binding sites decreased with ZNF143 knockdown (Fig. 3d), and there was substantial restructuring of promoter-enhancer loops with loss of loops that were anchored at both ends by CTCF and/or ZNF143 binding sites and gain of loops where only one anchor contained these binding sites (Fig. 3e).

ZNF143 regulates expression of HOXB4 and drives Aza responsiveness

To determine which transcriptional programs were regulated by differences in epigenomic states, we used GREAT (Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool)40,41 to analyze DMRs and DARs between responders and nonresponders from cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Regions hypomethylated in responders were associated with RNA PolII-dependent transcription and myeloid development and differentiation, whereas regions hypomethylated in nonresponders were highly enriched for terms associated with lymphoid differentiation and function (Supplementary Fig. 24a, b), consistent with nonresponders having a lymphoid-biased cell state (Fig. 1). A similar lymphoid bias was noted with DARs in nonresponders, in patient cohort 2 (Supplementary Fig. 24c, d).

GREAT analysis of hypomethylated regions in responders of cohort 1 identified HOXB3 and HOXB4 as the most likely genes to be regulated by these regions (Fig. 3f). Similarly, GREAT analysis of DARs in cohorts 2 and 3 predicted HOXB4 and HOXB5, as well as HOXB3 in cohort 2, to be the most likely genes regulated by these regions (Fig. 3g, h). In contrast, no genes were recurrently identified within hypermethylated or hypoaccessible regions in responders relative to nonresponders (Supplementary Fig. 24e, f). The anterior HOXB genes were also among the most significant genes associated with DARs in cohorts 2 and 3 (Fig. 3i, j), and differential gene expression in cohort 2 (Fig. 3k). Of the anterior HOXB gene cluster, only HOXB4 was consistently identified across all comparisons (Fig. 3f–k), and importantly, was also among the differentially expressed genes and TFs in normal MPP vs LMPP (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Fig. 24g, h), consistent with the role of HOXB4 in normal primitive HSPC42–44. Data from 25 AML cell lines in DepMap31 corroborated these findings, with only HOXB4 and HOXB3 having a significant correlation with Aza sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 25a). Expression of CTCF and ZNF143 were both uncorrelated with Aza resistance (Supplementary Fig. 25a), consistent with their role being dependent on differences in epigenomic states in Aza-responder compared to Aza-nonresponder LSC, rather than these factors being differentially expressed. The DMRs (cohort 1) and DARs (cohort 2) immediately upstream of HOXB4 overlapped H3K27 acetylation peaks in normal CD34+ HSPC (EGAD00001003963; GSE231486)45,46. H3K27 acetylation HiChIP loops (GSE231486)45,46 at this location suggest coregulation of the anterior HOXB cluster in normal CD34+ cells. CTCF (GSM935407), ZNF143 (GSM935568) and SMC3 (GSM935310)47 co-binding sites were also observed upstream of HOXB4, supporting active enhancer or promoter activity in this region in normal progenitors regulated by ZNF143/CTCF/cohesin (Supplementary Fig. 25b).

Given that HOXB4 was the only gene predicted to be differentially regulated in HMA-responders across all comparisons, as well as in normal MPP (Fig. 3f–m), we asked whether HOXB4 was regulated by ZNF143. Knockdown and overexpression experiments confirmed that ZNF143 positively regulated HOXB4 gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 25c, d and Supplementary Fig. 20d, g). Knockdown of either ZNF143 or HOXB4 increased resistance to Aza (Fig. 2g and Fig. 3n and Supplementary Fig. 25e), while overexpression of either gene had the opposite effect, sensitizing OCI-AML3 and the resistant leukemic cell lines, OCI-AML2 and THP-1 to Aza (Fig. 2h and Fig. 3o and Supplementary Fig. 25f). Together these findings support the idea that the epigenome, as defined by DNA methylation and chromatin structure and accessibility, permits ZNF143 to activate HOXB4 and render primitive hematopoietic cells sensitive to Aza.

To directly demonstrate that methylation of the HOXB locus regulates HOXB4 expression and sensitivity to Aza, we used a dCas9-DNMT3A approach to selectively methylate the ZNF143/CTCF-binding region associated with the chromatin loop that was lost upon ZNF143 knockdown (Fig. 5b). The resulting increase in methylation was of a similar magnitude to the differential methylation seen in Aza-responders relative to -nonresponders (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 28a, b and Fig. 2a). The increased methylation in the HOXB regulatory region reduced HOXB4 gene expression and increased resistance to Aza (Fig. 5d, e). In contrast, selective demethylation in the same region using a Dox-inducible dCas9-TET1 catalytic domain construct increased HOXB4 expression and increased sensitivity to Aza (Fig. 5f–i and Supplementary Fig. 28c, d). These results support the assertion that methylation of this ZNF143/CTCF binding site in the HOXB cluster regulates HOXB4 expression to modulate Aza responsiveness.

CD45RA-positive leukemic cells are associated with resistance to Aza

Given that CD45RA was the primary marker differentiating responders from nonresponders in cohort 1 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4a, consistent with an LMPP-like phenotype, we investigated whether CD45RA expression could be used as a predictive marker to determine Aza response in AML. We prospectively obtained bone marrow samples from 23 newly diagnosed AML patients (patient cohort 4; Supplementary Dataset 4), and assayed the lin-CD34+CD38-CD99+ LSC compartment to enumerate CD45RA positivity (Supplementary Fig. 29a, b). After treatment of AML samples with Aza in vitro, we determined that a threshold of 25% CD45RA-positivity would predict response (Supplementary Fig. 29c and Supplementary Dataset 8).

To determine whether the CD45RA threshold would predict Aza response in the context of a human microenvironment, we adopted a human embryonic stem cell-derived marrow organoid model (Fig. 5j, k and Supplementary Fig. 29d). We implanted 5 samples with > 25% CD45RA expression in the lin-CD34+CD38-CD99+ compartment and 5 samples with < 25% CD45RA expression, into human marrow organoids. Treatment of organoids containing patient leukemic cells with Aza in this system showed that samples with ≤ 25% CD45RA+ cells in the LSC compartment had significantly higher cell death compared to those with > 25% CD45RA+ cells (Fig. 5l), with no change in the cellular microenvironment of the marrow organoids (Supplementary Fig. 29e).

Discussion

Although previous studies have suggested that transcriptomic data of the AML bulk population can predict sensitivity to Aza, there has been no exploration of the mechanisms of this association15,16. Our results lead us to propose a model where the LSC of Aza responders and nonresponders are comparable to normal MPP and LMPP, respectively, based on their immunophenotype, global DNA methylation patterns, chromatin accessibility and gene expression (Fig. 5m). The LSC reflect the Aza response of their corresponding normal counterparts, where normal MPP are more sensitive to Aza compared to normal LMPP. We further show that this leukemic cell state difference is determined by the differential binding of ZNF143 to its target sites, resulting in altered chromatin structure and differential expression of HOXB4. Modifying ZNF143 binding, either by knocking down, or overexpressing ZNF143, or by changing the methylation levels of its binding site in the HOXB cluster, alters cell state and sensitivity to Aza in both leukemic and normal cells.

ZNF143 is a chromatin-looping factor that colocalizes genome-wide with CTCF and cohesin36,49,50. It has been shown to regulate pathways critical for murine hematopoiesis by providing sequence specificity at promoters connected with distal regulatory elements36,37. There is discordant data about whether ZNF143 also regulates higher-order chromatin structure36,37,49. Our studies show that in human hematopoietic cells, ZNF143 not only specifies promoter loops, but also regulates TAD boundaries, with a major impact on chr 17 TADs, where HOXB4 is located. These findings suggest that ZNF143 plays a crucial role in shaping both the epigenomic landscape and cell state of leukemic cells. This function appears to be inherited from the normal stem/progenitor population, from which specific LSCs with HSC/MPP characteristics originate. Consequently, normal HSC/MPP cells are sensitive to HMA treatment, whereas normal LMPP cells exhibit resistance. Furthermore, the HMA sensitivity of both leukemic and normal cells can be modulated by altering ZNF143 levels or its key target, HOXB4.

Although adjusting levels of ZNF143 or HOXB4 alters HMA responsiveness, the downstream cellular output as a result of these manipulations is different. Knockdown of either ZNF143 or HOXB4 leads to a relative increase in LMPP-like cells, but only ZNF143 depletion leads to increased downstream monocytic and lymphoid output. This suggests that the mature cell output of LMPP is decoupled from the cell state that determines HMA responsiveness, with a ZNF143-dependent, but HOXB4-independent mechanism for monocytic- and lymphoid-biased output. These findings also suggest that the therapy-resistance described for leukemias with monoblastic/monocytic bias51, may be a reflection of LMPP-like LSC derivation that is an epiphenomenon rather than a specific driver of resistance. As HMAs are the backbone of several combination therapies, these data establish a framework for investigating resistance to current and new drugs used in combination with HMAs52,53.

Our observation that LSCs from treatment-naïve patients who respond to Aza exhibit hypomethylated genomes raises important questions about the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of Aza. It is possible that hypomethylated responder cells possess a chromatin structure that facilitates Aza-mediated demethylation at alternative CpG sites, which remain inaccessible within the hypermethylated epigenomic landscape of nonresponders. However, it is also possible that Aza is cytotoxic for specific cell types defined by a hypomethylated genome. Elucidating these mechanistic differences could have significant implications for drug development and underscores the need for further investigation into the epigenetic changes that occur with treatment.

The inherent difference in Aza sensitivity between normal MPP and LMPP suggests that the epigenomic state of the LSC may be the critical determinant of HMA responsiveness, rather than any particular driver mutation, although several have been proposed6–10. Our work clearly demonstrates the key role that epigenetic mechanisms play in cellular responsiveness to HMA. While initiating oncogenic mutations occur in a stem cell, subsequent genetic alterations often arise in a downstream self-renewing progenitor11,17,54. A machine learning approach has suggested that MDS patients with 3 or more driver mutations are more likely to be resistant to HMAs9, which would be consistent with the accumulation of mutations in downstream progenitors such as LMPP11,54, and may be one explanation for this finding.

The idea that LMPP-derived LSC represent a cell or epigenomic state that is inherently resistant to HMAs leads to the potential for identifying HMA-nonresponsive patients using a routine clinical flow cytometry assay. Although CD45RA is not a cell surface marker that is routinely interrogated in clinical labs, it could easily be incorporated into standard clinical flow cytometry panels. Our results suggest that CD45RA surface expression may be a potential biomarker to identify cases that would be resistant to HMA, but this would require prospective validation. However, given the potential for heterogeneity of epigenetic clones, as we have seen, one caveat is that small resistant clones may not be detected at diagnosis, but could quickly expand with treatment. Our findings also imply that compounds that alter the epigenomic state, potentially by activating ZNF143 or HOXB4, may prove efficacious for HMA combination therapy approaches.

Ethics approvals

Consent to publish the deidentified data was obtained, and all study protocols and patient specimen collections were approved by the University of British Columbia–BC Cancer Research Ethics Board (Vancouver, British Columbia; protocol H19-01600).

Reagents and cell lines used

5-Azacytidine was acquired from Sigma (Cat# A2385) dissolved in DMSO at 100 mM, aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C. Doxycycline was acquired from Stemcell Technologies (Cat# 72742), dissolved in DMSO at 10 mg/ml, aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C. Resazurin sodium salt was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Cat# R7017). A stock solution of 440 µM was prepared in PBS and stored at 4 °C. The MOLM-13 and SKM-1 cell lines were provided by Devendra Hiwase (South Australian Health and medical Research Institute, Adelaide, Australia). OCI-AML3 and OCI-AML2 cell lines were purchased from DSMZ (Germany), and THP-1 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MOLM-13, OCI-AML3, OCI-AML2 and THP-1 were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). THP-1 medium was supplemented with 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol. SKM-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20% FBS. All cultures were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C in room air supplemented with CO2 to achieve a final concentration of 5%.

Lentiviral vectors cloning

The shRNA lentiviral constructs that targeted ZNF143 (sh1b & 1c - TRCN0000012934 and TRCN0000012935, respectively) were obtained from the Broad Institute RNAi Consortium shRNA Library in the lentiviral vector pLKO.1. The puromycin resistance cassette was replaced by eGFP or mCherry using restriction enzymes BamH1 and KpnI. shRNA constructs targeting HOXB4 (713 & 714) were a kind gift from Andrew Weng’s lab (BC Cancer Research Institute, Vancouver, Canada). As control, a pLKO.1 vector containing a non-human targeting sequence (shCTR) was used. Lentiviral constructs overexpressing ZNF143 in the pLX317 vector, TFORF1373 and TFORF#1374, were purchased from Addgene (Addgene #143954 and Addgene #149355). pLX317 GFP (Addgene #13681) was used as a control. For the HOXB4 overexpressing lentiviral construct, HOXB4 was amplified by PCR using hHOXB4-C-DYK plasmid (GenScript OHu29112) as template and cloned into lentiviral backbone pMND-YFP (a kind gift from Connie Eve’s lab in BC Cancer Research Institute, Vancouver, Canada) between AscI and PacI sites. pMND-YFP was used as a control.

Lentivirus production and transduction

High-titer lentivirus was produced by transient transfection of 293 T/17 cells (purchased from ATCC) with 3rd-generation packaging/envelope vectors pRRE (Addgene # 12251), pREV (Addgene # 115989), and pMD2.g (Addgene # 12259) along with lentiviral constructs using the CalPhosTM mammalian transfection kit (Clontech # 631312). For ZNF143, high-titer lentivirus was made using 2nd-generation packaging/envelope vectors psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and pMD2.g. Media were changed 8h-overnight after transfection and viral supernatant was harvested 2 days after transfection, followed by ultracentrifuge concentration (25,000 rpm for 90 min at 4 °C using a Beckman SW32Ti rotor). Pellets were resuspended in 90 ml of PBS. The titers obtained were typically in the range of 109 transducing units per ml as measured by transducing 150,000 Hela cells (transduction and culture media for Hela cells were DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS), followed by flow cytometry analysis of > 20% GFP /mCherry+/dsRed+ at a 1:50000 dilution (Virus titer = dilution factor × GFP/YFP/mCherry+ × cell number at transduction ÷ 100).

For lentiviral transductions of cell lines, 2 × 106 cells per cell line were suspended in 4 ml of appropriate medium and 6–8 µl of concentrated virus was added. Polybrene was added to the cell-virus mixture at a concentration of 8 µg/ml to enhance viral transduction. The cells were incubated with the virus for 6–16 h after which the cells were washed once with sterile PBS and resuspended in 8 ml of appropriate culture medium and allowed to grow for 72 h. The transduced live GFP+ or mCherry+ cells were then sorted using BD FACS machines (FACSFusionTM, FACSAriaIIITM or FACSMelodyTM). The sorted cells were then used either for further drug treatments or RNA extractions.

For lentiviral transduction of cord blood, cells were seeded into 96-well round-bottom plates at a cell density of 100,000 cells/100 µl. Plated cells were pre-stimulated in StemSpanTM SFEM (STEMCELL Technologies #09650) supplemented with StemSpan™ CC100 (STEMCELL Technologies #02690), 750 nM SR1 (STEMCELL Technologies #72344) and 35 nM UM171 (STEMCELL Technologies #72912) overnight. The next day the CD34+ CB cells were incubated with Cyclosporin H (Cedarlane laboratories # C988920-5) to increase transduction efficiency and incubated overnight55. CD34+ CB cells were then transduced by direct addition of concentrated viral supernatants (100 µl total cell mixture with addition of < 5 µl concentrated virus). After 6 h of virus exposure, cells were washed once with PBS and transferred to culture medium. Following 48 h of culture, GFP+ MPP and LMPP were isolated by sorting on a Fusion sorter using the purity mode. All cultures were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C in room air supplemented with CO2 to achieve a final concentration of 5%.

RT-qPCR

RNA was isolated from BM cells or cell lines using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) or TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was prepared using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase and oligodT primers (Invitrogen). qPCR was performed using PowerSyber Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and HT7900 sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems) as per the manufacturer’s specifications. Human GAPDH or human HPRT served as a reference control, and differences in mRNA expression levels were calculated as fold-changes by the ΔΔCt method. The primers pairs used for each gene were: hZNF143 (fwd: ACCCAGATTGCAGTTCAGCTTGG & rev: GGCTTCATGGATTTCTGCTGCC), hHOXB4 (fwd: ACGTGAGCACGGTAAACCC & rev: TTGGTGTTGGGCAACTTGTG), hHPRT (fwd: TGACCTTGATTTATTTTGCATACC & rev: CGAGCAAGACGTTCAGTCCT), hGAPDH (fwd: TGATTCCACCCATGGCAAATTCC & rev: GCTCCTGGAAGATGGTGATGGATT).

Western blot

Protein lysates were prepared with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). Cell extracts were centrifuged at 21,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min. Protein concentration was measured by the DC Protein Assay Kit II (BioRad). Supernatants were boiled for 5 min with Laemmli buffer. Samples containing 20–50 μg protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using BioRad mini transblot cell. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk or BSA in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20. Primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-ZNF143 (L-26) (Santa Cruz sc100983, 1:200), anti-HOXB4 (ThermoFisher Scientific PA5-23366, 1:200), anti-GAPDH (New England Biolabs #2118, 1:5000). Blots were imaged using Western Lightning Plus–ECL (PerkinElmer) and BioRad ChemiDoc Imaging system.

Flow cytometry

For progenitor or mature population analysis of primary patient samples or cord blood, cells were initially blocked in human blocking buffer (containing 4% Human serum, 1% human CD32 Ab in PBS) for 30 min on ice, followed by staining with the appropriate primary antibody cocktail (Supplementary Dataset 9) for 60 min on ice. The stained cells were then washed with flow buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS) and either stained for AnnexinV or resuspended in the flow buffer as detailed above for analysis.

For Annexin V staining, cells (either after primary antibody staining or directly from culture) were washed in 1x Annexin V binding buffer (10 mM HEPES; 140 mM NaCl; 2.5 mM CaCl2; pH 7.4), and resuspended in 100 µl 1 x Annexin V binding buffer mixed with BUV385 or APC-conjugated Annexin V antibody at a 1:20 dilution. Following 15 min incubation at room temperature, an additional 200 µl 1xAnnexin V binding buffer containing PI or DAPI at 1:1000 dilution was added, and samples were analysed by flow cytometry.

The analysis of the flow plots was done using the FlowJo software v10.9. The positive and negative populations were gated based on the use of FMO (fluorescence minus one) for each marker. In cases where there were limited cell numbers and staining for FMO was not possible, the gating for positive samples was done using the negatively stained sample, where the voltage for negative samples was set within 10−3 (Log10) and all populations above this threshold were considered positive. Gating strategies are shown in Supplementary Fig. 30.

Patient sample processing

Freshly obtained patient BM samples were obtained from Vancouver General Hospital after obtaining appropriate consent. BM samples were suspended in PBS containing 2% FBS to make the volume up to 3 ml. RBC were lysed by adding 6 ml of sterile ammonium chloride, mixed thoroughly and incubated on ice for 7 min. After 7 min, the lysis was stopped by the addition of double the volume (12 ml) of PBS containing 2% FBS. The lysed samples were then spun down, supernatant removed and resuspended to proceed for live cell counting using Trypan blue. The cells were then either viably frozen or used for drug treatment and/or flow cytometry analysis. Viably frozen patient samples were gently thawed by dropwise addition into PBS containing 20% FBS supplemented with DNase (100 µg/ml final concentration).

Patient samples were cultured in the following medium – Stemspan serum free medium (STEMCELL Technologies # 09650) + Penicillin/Streptavidin + 10 ng/ml hIL3 (STEMCELL Technologies # 2503) + 50 ng/ml hSCF (STEMCELL Technologies # 78062) + 10 ng/ml hIL6 (STEMCELL Technologies # 78050) + 50 ng/ml hFLT3L (STEMCELL Technologies # 78009) + 10 ng/ml G-CSF (STEMCELL Technologies # 78012.1) + 25 ng/ml TPO (STEMCELL Technologies # 78210.1). All patient samples used in this study were obtained prior to initiation of any treatment. For those patients whose post 5-Azacytidine treatment data was available they were classified as responders or non-responders based on their clinical response. Patients with complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), complete remission with incomplete hematologic response (CRi) or hematologic improvement (HI) were classified as 5-Azacytidine responders. Patients with stable disease (SD) or refractory disease (RD) were classified as 5-Azacytidine non-responders.

Drug treatment

Cells were seeded at a concentration of 10,000-30,000 cells per well in 100 µl of appropriate medium in a 96-well U-bottom plate. Appropriate 5-Azacytidine working stocks were made in PBS from the stock solutions detailed above such that 2–5 µl of working stock added to the wells gave a final concentration of 1 or 5 µM. For 0 µM 5-Azacytidine, working stocks were prepared similar to the 5-Azacytidine working stocks but using 100% DMSO in place of the drug stock. Fresh 5-Azacytidine was added every day, by spinning the cells down in the plate, replacing 80% of the medium with fresh medium and adding freshly prepared working stocks as outlined above. Cell lines were treated with 5-Azacytidine for 7 days, patient samples were treated for 3 days.

For Doxycycline treatments, cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 100,000 cells per well in 1 ml. Working stocks were made in PBS from a doxycycline stock solution such that the addition of 2 µl of working stock in 1 ml would give a final concentration of 1 µg/ml. The cells were treated with doxycycline for 1 week, with fresh doxycycline added every 2 days. After 1 week of doxycycline treatment, half the cells were harvested for RNA and DNA extractions and the remaining cells were seeded as outlined above to proceed with 5-Azacytidine treatment.

IC50 curves

Cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells/well in 100 µl of culture medium in a 96-well U-bottom plate and treated as outlined above for a period of 3 days. On day 3, 10 µl of Resazurin (440 µM) was added to each well, making the final concentration 44 µM. The cells were incubated with Resazurin at 37 °C for 3-4 h after which the fluorescence was measured using a fluorescent plate reader at 530/580 nm.

ROC curve analysis of CD45RA expression in patient samples

Freshly obtained bone marrow (BM) samples from patients at Vancouver General Hospital were processed after obtaining informed consent, as described earlier. For flow cytometry analysis, 300,000–1,000,000 cells per sample were stained for CD45RA and other surface markers using a BD Symphony flow cytometer. In parallel, 60,000–100,000 cells per sample were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with 1 or 5 µM of 5-azacytidine for 72 h. Post-treatment, samples were classified as 5-azacytidine-sensitive or resistant based on a ≥ 2-fold increase in cell death after drug exposure. To assess the predictive value of CD45RA expression for 5-azacytidine response, we performed ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve analysis using GraphPad Prism 9. ROC curves were generated by comparing the CD45RA expression profiles of 5-azacytidine-sensitive versus resistant samples. The optimal cut-off for CD45RA expression, which maximized both sensitivity and specificity for predicting treatment response, was determined using the Youden Index and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as a quantitative measure of how well CD45RA expression could discriminate between sensitive and resistant samples.

Incucyte measurements

2000 cells per population per well were seeded in 100 µl of culture medium in a flat-bottom 96-well NUNC plate (Cat# 167008) known to be compatible with the Incucyte readouts. IncucyteTM cytotoxic green reagent (Sartorius) was added to the cells at a 1:4000 dilution, and the plate was placed inside the Incucyte SX5 live-cell analysis system (Sartorius). Using the Incucyte setup and analysis software, the Incucyte was set to take images every 12 h for a total of 14 days at 10X magnification using phase contrast imaging to measure cell confluence and the green channel to measure dead cell counts. The next day, 5-Azacytidine treatment was started by the addition of 2 µl freshly prepared working stock to get a final concentration of 1 µM. Fresh 5-Azacytidine was added daily without changing media to avoid disturbing the cell distribution in the plate.

Cord blood cells culture

Anonymized normal human cord blood samples from normal full-term Caesarean section deliveries were obtained with informed consent from the Stem Cell Assay lab (BC Cancer). These were obtained as > 95% pure CD34 + pooled collections obtained using a two-step Rosette-Sep/EasySep human CD34-positive selection kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 17896) according to the manufacturer’s protocols and/or FACS sorting. Viably frozen CD34+ cells were thawed by dropwise addition into Iscove medium + 10% FBS supplemented with DNase (100 µg/ml final concentration). After thawing, the CD34+cord blood cells were either prepared for isolation of MPP and LMPP using a Fusion sorter (Becton Dickenson) or plated for lentiviral transductions.

Cord blood and stromal cells co-culture experiment

10,000 MS-5 feeder cells were plated in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1 x Penicillin-Streptavidin-Glutamine (PSG) in 96-well tissue culture treated, collagen coated plates, and allowed to adhere to the plate overnight. The next day, before adding CD34+ cord blood cells, the DMEM medium was replaced by a cord blood differentiation medium – alpha MEM containing 2 mM glutamine, 10−4 M β-mercaptoethanol, 7.5% FCS + 50 ng/ml hSCF + 10 ng/ml hFLT3L + 10 ng/ml hIL-7 (STEMCELL Technologies # 78053.1) + 3 U/ml EPO (STEMCELL Technologies # 02625). For measuring the mature cell output of sorted cord blood cells, 500 cells were seeded on the MS-5 feeder cells and grown for 2 weeks. After one week, half the medium was replaced with fresh medium, carefully without disturbing the cord blood cells or the feeder cells. At the end of two weeks, all the cells from the well, including the MS-5 feeder cells, were trypsinized, harvested and processed for flow cytometry analysis. The hematopoietic cells were identified from the feeder cells based on the expression of hCD45.

Cloning of sbTET-GFP-dCAS9-DNMT3a or sbTET-dCAS9-TET1CD construct for site-specific methylation

The genes encoding the dCAS9-DNMT3A and dCAS9-TET1CD fusion proteins were PCR amplified using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB #M0493S) from the Lentiviral construct Fuw-dCas9-DNMT3a (Addgene # 84476) and Fuw-dcas9-Tet1CD (Addgene #84475), respectively with oligonucleotides (IDT) containing the Kozak sequence and flanking inducible, directional SfiI cloning sites with buffer sequences on either end (Supplementary Dataset 10). Separately, the double-stranded PCR product and a vector containing a sleeping beauty transposon with inducible SfiI cloning sites and cassettes for constitutive expression of GFP, rtTA, and a puromycin resistance gene (pSBtet-GFP, Addgene # 60495)56 were digested for 8 hours with SfiI (NEB # R0123S). The resulting products were then gel purified to remove the end fragments from the PCR product, and the luciferase stuffer from the backbone. The digested PCR product and backbone vector were then ligated overnight at 16 °C with T4 DNA ligase (NEB # M0202S), transformed into DH5α competent cells (ThermoFisher # 18258012), plated on ampicillin agar, followed by colony selection, mini-prep, and validation by bidirectional Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Dataset 10). DNA was subsequently obtained by maxi-prep from a single, verified stock of transformed DH5α cells. A total of 2 × 107 OCI-AML3 cells were harvested from a growing culture, washed with PBS, and re-suspended in electroporation reagent. A 10:1 ratio DNA mixture containing the sbTet-trTA-dCAS9-DNMT3a-GFP-Puro or sbTet-trTA-dCAS9-TET1CD-GFP-Puro transposon and the pCMV-SB100X transposase (Addgene # 34879), along with a vector-only control, was prepared and added to the cell suspension. Electroporation was performed as per manufacturer instructions with a Neon Transfection System (ThermoFisher #MPK5000) in a 10 ml syringe at 1600 V, 10S width, 3 pulses. Cells were immediately decanted into warmed RPMI media and placed in an incubator. The cells were not disturbed for 72 h and were subsequently cultured with minimal manipulation for a period of 2 weeks. Live, GFP+ cells were then FACS-sorted (BD FACS AriaIII), and expanded for 1 week. Puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich # P8833-25MG) selection was then performed over 5 days at a 1 µg/ml concentration, and the resultant live cells were established as a stably expressing cell line. Stable GFP expression was validated by flow cytometry, and doxycycline-inducible dCAS9-DNMT3a or dCAS9-TET1CD expressions were confirmed by qPCR.

gRNA design and preparation for site-specific methylation

A multiplex gRNA expressing lentiviral vector was utilized for targeting the region of interest with dCAS9-DNMT3a or dCAS9-TET1CD. The ENCODE regulatory region (EH38E1868216, chr17: 48,602,682-48,602,876) was chosen for targeting using two gRNAs. To design the gRNAs, the GRCh38 coordinates for the targeted region were entered into the CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNA generation tool CHOPCHOP website interface version 3.0 and selected for the “repression” setting57. Modified oligonucleotides (IDT) with 5’-phosphorylation or treated with PNK (NEB # M0201S) containing the gRNA sequences as well as flanking overhang sequences for cloning into separate expression vectors were generated (Supplementary Dataset 10)58. The complementary oligonucleotides were annealed in a thermocycler with a slow temperature gradient, using the program: 96 °C for 300 s, followed by 85 °C for 20 s, 75 °C for 20 s, 65 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, 45 °C for 20 s, 35 °C for 20 s and 25 °C for 20 s with a − 0.3 °C /s rate between steps to generate a double-stranded product. The vectors containing the expression vectors under control of four different promoters (pmU6, Addgene #53187; phU6, Addgene #53188 phH1, Addgene #53186; ph7SK, Addgene #53189) were digested with BbsI (NEB #R0539S) and gel purified. The annealed, double-stranded oligonucleotide and the corresponding digested vector were then ligated overnight at 16 °C with T4 DNA ligase (NEB #M0202S). The ligated product was transformed into DH5α competent cells (ThermoFisher #18258012), plated on kanamycin agar, followed by colony selection, mini-prep, and validation by Sanger sequencing with a M13 reverse primer. After confirming the correct insert, 200 ng of each gRNA expression vector was combined with 200 ng of a vector containing a Golden Gate cassette and dsRED (Addgene #84034), with the addition of 1 µl of T4 DNA ligase (NEB #M0202S), 1 µl BsmBI FastDigest (Thermo Scientific #FD0454), and 2 µl of 10X T4 Ligase Buffer (NEB #B0202S) in a 20 µl reaction volume. A thermocycler program of 37 °C for 10 min, 16 °C for 15 min, 37 °C for 30 min, and 80 °C for 5 min was then used, with 5 µl of the ligation reaction used to transform STBL3 competent cells (ThermoFisher # C737303), plated on ampicillin agar, followed by colony selection and mini-prep. The simultaneous presence of all four inserts was confirmed by PCR using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase across the Golden Gate destination cassette, which produced four distinct bands on electrophoresis (Supplementary Dataset 10).

Pyrosequencing

gDNA extracted from cells was bisulfite converted using the Thermo Scientific EpiJET Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Cat# K1461) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The region of interest was PCR amplified from the bisulfite converted DNA using the Qiagen PyroMark PCR kit (Cat# 978703) as per the manufacturer’s protocol using primers designed for the E1868216 region. CRISPR knockout screen

MOLM-13 and SKM-1 stably expressing Cas9 were generated using lentiviral transduction, and transduced cells were selected over a period of 2 weeks. The CRISPR screening was done in collaboration with Franco Vizeacoumar (Saskatchewan Cancer Agency, Saskatchewan, Canada). In brief, lentivirus containing 2 sgRNAs per TF to be screened were prepared prior to infecting the Cas9 expressing cell lines. The Cas9 expressing MOLM-13 and SKM-1 were seeded in 96-well plates (5000 cells/ well) in duplicate. One plate was treated with 5-Azacytidine, and the other plate was used as a control treated with equivalent amounts of DMSO. Puromycin was used as a selection marker to select for live cells containing the sgRNA. Live cells were counted using an automated cell counter at Day 0, Day 3 and Day 5 of treatment. To identify TFs that play a role in resistance to 5-Azacytidine, the fold-change was calculated in live cell counts after 5-Azacytidine treatment over no treatment for each TF KO individually. This live cell fold-change was normalized to the fold-change of the control cells (without any KO). The log transformed normalized values were then plotted for each cell line. To obtain co-rank values for both cell lines tested, for each cell line, the TF were ranked based on their log transformed normalized values with the highest value ranked as 1. A “rank value” was calculated for each TF for each cell line by dividing the rank by the total number of TF analyzed. Co-rank values for each TF was obtained by taking the average of “rank values” for both cell lines.

scWGBS sample preparation

Cryopreserved MDS patient bone marrow (BM) samples were thawed and stained with patient BM samples panel of antibodies (Supplementary Dataset 9), along with a human lineage antibody cocktail. Lineage negative CD34 positive CD38 negative live single cells were index sorted into a 384-well plate using FACS (BD FACS AriaFusion), snap frozen and kept at − 80 °C until library construction. Bulk flow events were recorded prior to index sorting. The single-cell post-bisulfite adapter ligation (scPBAL) libraries were constructed following the protocol previously described5 and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500. Only 7 out of 10 samples in patient Cohort 1 (Supplementary Dataset 1) were sequenced with this assay.

scWGBS data processing

Methylation data was processed using a single-cell Post-Bisulfite Adapter Ligation (scPBAL) pipeline previously described by Hui, et al.19. Adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore v0.4.0 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) and then aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome using NovoAlign v3.02.10 (http://www.novocraft.com/). Duplicate alignments were marked using Picard v1.3.1 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/), and methylation states called using NovoMethyl v1.01 (http://www.novocraft.com/). Copy number calling was conducted using Control-FREEC v11.660 in 5 Mb windows. Subsequent analysis only includes single cells with > 100,000 CpGs. Three wells of 10-cell pools, 3 negative control wells, and 2 positive control wells were filtered out.

scWGBS data analysis

Global methylation was calculated by averaging the methylation across all CpGs in each cell. Average methylation per genomic region was calculated by averaging the methylation across all CpGs in each genomic region as annotated by annotatr v1.20.0 R package61 using the hg19_basicgenes built-in annotation. A mixed model ANOVA was used to measure the significance of the differential methylation between 5-azacytidine response groups across each of the functional genomic regions. Average methylation per transcription factor binding site was calculated by averaging the methylation across all CpGs in all cells within each transcription factor binding site defined by ChIP-seq experiments from the Locus Overlap Analysis (LOLA) database v17020630. The mean methylation by response was used to calculate the difference in methylation between responders and nonresponders. A mixed model implementation with pbkrtest v0.5.1 R package62 was used to calculate the statistics. To obtain the top differentially methylated transcription factor binding sites, the non-treated experiments were filtered for > 4 % hypomethylation difference with a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value < 0.1, yielding 64 transcription factors. For each transcription factor, the most significant experiment was selected (Supplementary Dataset 5). Differentially methylated loci and regions were called using the DMLtest (Wald test p-value threshold of 0.001) and the callDMR function of the DSS v1.8.2 R package63, with 4 responders and 3 nonresponders in patient cohort 1. As a control, custom scripts were written to randomly reassign all cells into seven groups without replacement, representing patients. Groups were randomly assigned as four “responders” and three “nonresponders”, reflecting the metadata in cohort 1. Differential methylation was calculated across each of the annotated genomic regions as described above using a mixed model ANOVA. This process was iteratively repeated 100 times to bootstrap the significance distribution.

Clonotype inference from scWGBS data

Mitochondrial variant analysis was performed on scWGBS alignments from cohort 1 using _mgatk_ v0.6.764 for cells passing prior filtering. Variance-to-mean ratio (VMR) and strand concordance was calculated for each called variant. VMR was calculated by dividing the variance in heteroplasmy observed across all cells containing the variant by the mean heteroplasmy. The strand concordance was defined by a Pearson’s R correlation statistic between the number of forward and reverse reads supporting the variant in each cell. Variants confidently detected in 5 or more cells with a VMR > 10-2 and strand concordance > 0.65 were retained. Clonotypes were determined by performing hierarchical clustering on the single-cell VAFs of the filtered variants.

Normal MPP vs. LMPP analysis

Similar procedure as described for patient cohort 1 was executed using the previously published dataset GSE87197 from Farlik et al.23. Average methylation per transcription factor binding site was calculated by averaging the methylation across all CpGs in all cells within each transcription factor binding site defined by ChIP-seq experiments from the LOLA database. The mean methylation by cell type was used to calculate the difference in methylation between MPPs and LMPPs. The Wilcox test was used to calculate the statistics. To obtain the top differentially methylated transcription factor binding sites, the non-treated experiments were filtered for > 1% hypomethylation difference with a Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value < 0.05. For each transcription factor, the most significant experiment was selected. To identify methylation profiles for normal hematopoietic cells, differentially methylated loci and regions between MPPs and LMPPs were called using the DSS R package, as described above for patient cohort 1.

Overlap analysis of normal and patient differential methylation

To determine the significance of differentially methylated transcription factor binding sites, a Fisher’s exact test was used. To determine significance of overlaps between differentially methylated regions in patient responder vs. nonresponder and normal MPPs vs LMPPs, overlapPermTest from regioneR 1.26.1 R package65 was used (Fig. 1i).

scWGBS cell type prediction

The training regions from the methylation cell type classifier in the above mentioned dataset (https://medical-epigenomics.org/papers/BLUEPRINT_methylomes/data/cell_type_predictor_README.html) were lifted over from GRCh38 to GRCh37 using rtracklayer v1.54.0 R package66. The average methylation per lifted over region was re-calculated using the dataset GSE87197 from Farlik et al.23 using bedtools map v2.27.167. The average methylation across these training regions was calculated for each single cell in patient cohort 1 in the same manner, as input to the classifier.

scWGBS CNV analysis

Copy number variants were called using Control-FREEC. Cells were filtered for abnormality by selecting for cells with more than 100 5 Mb windows with a copy number greater or less than 2. To determine the cell cycle phase of each single cell, the K562 dataset from Hansen et al.68 with the provided plasticity mask (lifted over to GRCh37) was used with a Fisher’s exact test. Only cells determined by this test to be in G0 were included in the copy number analysis.

Differential gene expression analysis between normal MPP and LMPP

The normal dataset from Karamitros et al.24 was used for normal cell type analysis. The raw FASTQs were downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive (E-MTAB-5456) and processed using Salmon v1.6.069. A GRCh38 cDNA reference (Ensembl v99) was built using salmon index -t Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.cdna.all.fa.gz -i hg38_cDNA_index -k 21. Transcript abundances were obtained using salmon quant -i hg38_cDNA_index -l A --validateMappings, in single-end mode. To correct for low count numbers, the HSC and MPP populations were combined into a single MPP population. To correct for heterogeneity between pools, ComBat_seq from the sva v3.42.0 R package70 was utilized. DESeq2 v.1.34.0 R package71 was used for differential expression analysis between MPPs and LMPPs. Transcription factor genes were identified using the Human Transcripton Factors database v1.0172.

10x Multiome-seq

Cryopreserved AML patient bone marrow samples were thawed and stained with the human lineage antibody cocktail, along with the cell viability dye 7-AAD (StemCell Technologies, Cat#75001.1), and FACS sorted for lineage-negative live cells. The nuclei were isolated from the sorted cells following the 10x Genomics demonstrated protocol Nuclei Isolation for Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression Sequencing, the single cell multiome ATAC and gene expression sequencing libraries were constructed following the user guide from 10x Genomics and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Only 3 of 12 samples in patient cohort 2 (Supplementary Dataset 2) were sequenced with this assay.

DOGMA-seq sample preparation and library construction

The DOGMA-seq protocol previously described20 was used to generate a mitochondrial DNA-included single cell ATAC-seq (mtscATAC-seq) library, a single cell Gene Expression library and a single cell Antibody Derived Tag (ADT) library from lineage negative cells from each AML patient sample. In brief, the cryopreserved AML patient bone marrow samples were thawed and stained with FVS 575 V, followed by blocking and staining with human lineage antibody cocktail and 13 TotalSeq A antibodies from BioLegend (Supplementary Dataset 10), incubated on ice for 30 min and washed 3 times with staining buffer (2% BSA, 0.01% Tween-20 in PBS). Fixed with 0.1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 5 minutes and quenched by adding glycine at 0.125 M final concentration, FACS sorted for lineage negative live cells, mildly lysed using LLL lysis buffer and proceeded with single cell library construction. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 or NovaSeq X Plus. Only 9 of 12 samples in patient cohort 2 (Supplementary Dataset 2) were sequenced with this assay.

DOGMA-seq data processing

Raw sequencing data for scATAC-seq, scRNA-seq and scADT-seq were processed from base calls to FASTQs using Illumina’s bcl2fastq software v2.17. scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq reads for all libraries were aligned to a U2AF1 fixed version of the GRCh38 reference genome73 using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger ARC command cellranger-arc count v2.0.1 (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell-ranger-arc/latest). scADT-seq data was processed using kallisto v0.48.074 command kallisto bus -x “0,0,16:0,16,28:1,0,0” --fr-stranded followed by bustools v0.41.075 command bustools correct -w 737K-arc-v1.txt and bustools count --genecounts v0.41.0 in conjunction with featuremap.py FeatureBarcodes.csv from kite (https://github.com/pachterlab/kite) to obtain protein abundances, as described by Mimitou et al.20.

DOGMA-seq data analysis

DOGMA-seq data was imported into R v4.1.3 for analysis using the Seurat v4.1.1 R package76 with the function Read_10x_h5. Cells were filtered for quality, selecting for cells between the 5% and 95% quantile in the following metrics: number of RNA features, number of ATAC features, mitochondrial ATAC depth, mitochondrial RNA depth, number of ATAC peak region fragments, transcription start site enrichment score (TSSEnrichment), and nucleosome signal (NucleosomeSignal) using the subset function. Doublets were removed using scDBblFinder v1.8.0 R package77 and RNA contamination removed using SoupX v1.6.2 R package78. Cells were normalized, scaled, and clustered using Seurat’s workflow, including the RunUMAP function with the R-native UWOT using the cosine metric. scATAC-seq data was further processed with the Signac v1.7.0 R package79, calling peaks using MACS2 v2.2.7.180. Cell types were predicted for each library based on single-cell gene expression with the Azimuth v0.4.5 R package76, using the human bone marrow reference. Cell cycles were predicted using the CellCyclingScoring function. All libraries from patient cohort 2 were integrated using the Harmony v1.0.3 R package81, obtaining an integrated UMAP projection containing all libraries for each assay, including ADT. In the case of ATAC data, common peaks across all samples were used for integration. Gene accessibility was inferred from these common peaks using the GeneActivity function. To filter for the leukemic stem cell compartment, cells were filtered for CD34 and CD99 gene activity. When compared to gene expression, gene accessibility yielded higher barcode retention concordance with CD34+CD99 + cells, as determined by ADT (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Gene accessibility and expression filtering thresholds were determined in a method similar to that of flow cytometry gating (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

Differential DOGMA-seq analysis

Differential analysis on both scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq were conducted using Seurat’s FindMarkers function using logistic regression with Bonferroni correction. Differential peaks were annotated with genomic features (using hg38_basicgenes built-in annotation) and transcription factor binding sites from ENCODE, Codex, Cistrome, and custom (GSM2274676, GSM2274677, GSM2687534, PMID 23353889, PMID 23727019) collections in the LOLA database using the annotatr v1.20.0 R package. To determine the significance of the number of hyperaccessible vs hypoaccessible regions, Cohen’s d effect size was computed from a left-tailed t test and a right-tailed t test against a normal distribution of average log2 fold change using the rstatix v0.7.1 R package (https://github.com/kassambara/rstatix). Cohen’s d effect size was interpreted as stated in Cohen 199282: “negligible”: |d | < 0.2; “small”: |d | < 0.5; “medium”: |d | < 0.8; “large”: |d | ≥ 0.8. As a control, all cells were divided randomly into 12 equal groups to simulate individual patients. These groups were then randomly divided into 2 groups to simulate a response. Differential peaks were called between these 2 groups to yield non-significant Cohen’s d effect sizes in all genomic features and all transcription factor binding sites across 10 iterations (Supplementary Fig. S10C).

DOGMA-seq transcription factor network analysis

Transcription factor network analysis was conducted using SCENIC v1.3.1 R package83 with a custom implementation of GRNBoost2 from Arboreto v0.1.684 in Python v3.10.5, using normalized gene accessibility derived from the scATAC-seq modality. Regulons in an incidence matrix were converted to an edgelist and subsequently used to create the network graph in igraph v2.0.3 R package (https://github.com/igraph/igraph) using the function graph_from_data_frame. The Layout for this graph was determined by the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm using the layout_fr function at 40,000 iterations with a start temperature of 20.

DOGMA-seq copy number alteration calling

Copy number alterations were called from the scATAC-seq modality using epiAneufinder v1.0.385 with default parameters (bin size = 100,000 bp). A gain karyotype was called in bins where ≥ 10% of the cells indicated a gain. A loss karyotype was called in bins where ≥ 15% of the cells indicated a loss. To call a karyotype for each chromosome arm, gain or loss bins must cover ≥ 40% of the arm, and overall read coverage of the arm must be ≥ 50% for a confident call. In silico karyotypes called are shown in Supplementary Dataset 2, where recurrent gains or losses present in ≥ 50% of the samples are excluded.

mt-scATAC-seq

Cryopreserved AML patient bone marrow samples were thawed and stained with the patient BM samples panel of antibodies and human lineage antibody cocktail, FACS sorted for lineage negative CD34 positive live cells. The mitochondrial single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (mt-scATAC-seq) protocol previously described64 was modified and used to generate the mt-scATAC-seq libraries. In brief, the cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 575 V (FVS 575 V, BD Biosciences, Cat#565694) before incubating with the above antibodies, and fixed with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 minutes, quenched by 0.125 M glycine after antibody staining, followed by FACS sort for lineage negative CD34 positive live cells. The sorted cells were then mildly lysed in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.1% NP-40 and 1% BSA on ice for 3 minutes, washed in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2 and 1% BSA, then resuspended in Diluted Nuclei Buffer and proceeded following 10 x Chromium Next GEM Single Cell ATAC Regent Kits v1.1 User Guide. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 at the School of Biomedical Engineering Sequencing Core at the University of British Columbia. All samples in patient cohort 3 (Supplementary Dataset 3) were sequenced with this assay.

mt-scATAC-seq data processing

Raw sequencing data was processed from base calls to FASTQs using Illumina’s bcl2fastq software v2.17. Reads for all libraries were aligned to a U2AF1 fixed version of the GRCh38 reference genome73 using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger ATAC command cellranger-atac count v2.1.064.

mt-scATAC-seq data analysis

An scATAC-seq only analysis was conducted on cohort 3 in the same manner as the scATAC-seq data in cohort 2. mt-scATAC-seq data was imported into R v4.1.3 for analysis using the Seurat v4.1.1 R package with the function Read_10x_h5. Cells were filtered for quality, selecting for cells between the 5% and 95% quantile in the following metrics: number of ATAC features, mitochondrial ATAC depth, number of ATAC peak region fragments, transcription start site enrichment score (TSSEnrichment), and nucleosome signal (NucleosomeSignal) using the subset function. Doublets were removed using scDBblFinder v1.8.0. Cells were normalized, scaled, and clustered using Seurat’s workflow, including the RunUMAP function with the R-native UWOT using the cosine metric. Peaks were called with MACS2 v2.2.7.1 in the Signac v1.7.0 R package. All libraries from patient cohort 3 were integrated using the Harmony v1.0.3 R package, obtaining an integrated UMAP projection containing all libraries. Common peaks across all samples were used for integration. Gene activity was inferred from these common peaks using the GeneActivity function. Differential analysis on both the peak and gene activity data were conducted using Seurat’s FindMarkers function using logistic regression with Bonferroni correction.

GREAT Analysis

Genomic coordinates of the regions that were > 40% differentially methylated between 5-Azacytidine responder and nonresponder patients in cohort 1 were entered into the Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT) web-based platform (version 4.0.4)40. Functions of the regions entered were predicted using the “Basal plus extension” setting, including curated regulatory domains. Similar analysis was conducted on regions that were hyperaccessible between 5-Azacytidine responder and nonresponder patients in cohort 2, and between Decitabine responder and nonresponder patients in cohort 3. Significant biological processes and genes affected by the entered regions were obtained using a region-based binomial option. The binomial FDR was calculated from the binomial p- value which indicates the probability that a random gene is associated with the given genomic coordinates. The binomial fold-enrichment was calculated as the observed/expected ratio.

HiChIP library construction and next-generation sequencing

OCI-AML3 cells were transduced with pLKO.1 shZNF143-GFP or pLKO.1 shControl-GFP lentivirus. GFP-positive cells were FACS sorted and collected. HiChIP libraries with CTCF antibody (Cell Signaling, 3418) pull-down was performed using Dovetail HiChIP MNase Kit (Cantata Bio, 21007) following the manufacturer’s instructions. 1 μL of MNase Enzyme Mix was used to digest 10 million cross-linked positive transduced cells. HiChIP sequencing libraries were constructed using Dovetail Library Module for Illumina (Cantata Bio, 25004 and 25010) following manufactory instructions. Pooled libraries were sequenced to 1100 million 150 bp PE reads on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Illumina bcl2fastq2 conversion software (v2.17) was used for library de-multiplexing, and data quality was confirmed following kit specifications.

HiChIP data analysis

Paired end reads were aligned to the human genome GRCh38 using bwa mem v.0.7.17 with flags “-5SP -TO”86. Aligned reads were parsed, sorted, de-duplicated and split using pairtools v1.0.287 to generate a “.pairs file” containing pairwise genome contact information, and a de-duplicated “.bam” file, sorted and indexed with samtools v1.16.188. The “.pairs” file was used to generate hic contact matrices using the Juicer Tools v.1.22.0189 pre function and A/B compartments were called on the resulting Pearson’s contact matrix at 50 kb and 1MB resolution using the eigenvector function. TADs were called using the TopDom tool implemented in the HiC-DC + v1.14.0 R package90 at 50 kb binsize with window size = 1090,91. Coordinates of TFBS, enhancers and promoters were extracted from the LOLA database30. CTCF binding sites were expanded to comprise the union of the LOLA CTCF binding sites with the binding sites called from the control OCI-AML3 HiChIP sample using MACS2 v2.2.7.180. Read coverage was normalized to 1x sequencing depth, and enrichment over CTCF and ZNF143 binding sites was calculated and plotted using deeptools v.3.5.292 bamCoverage, computeMatrix and plotHeatmap functions. Significant chromatin loops were identified from the 5 kb resolution contact matrix at an FDR < 0.01 using FitHiChIP v11.093 with the following parameter options: IntType = 3; BinSize = 5000; LowDistThr = 5000; UseP2PBackgrnd = 0; BiasType = 1.

Promoter and enhancer analysis

Significantly hypomethylated regions ( > 10% hypomethylation; log10(1 + |areaStat | ) > 2) from patient cohort 1 and significantly hyperaccessible regions (average log2 fold change > 2; padj < 0.001) from cohort 2 were annotated for specific genomic features (promoters, enhancers, and TADs) and ZNF143 and CTCF binding sites using the annotatr v1.20. R package. Promoter annotations were the built-in hg19_genes_promoters annotations for patient cohort 1 and hg38_genes_promoters for patient cohort 2. Enhancer annotations were CD34+ EP, downloaded from Enhancer Atlas (http://www.enhanceratlas.org/data/AllEPs/hs/CD34+_EP.txt), lifted over to hg38 when analyzing patient cohort 2. TADs called from the HiChIP analysis were lifted over to hg19 when analyzing patient cohort 1. A region was determined to be a co-binding site if it was annotated as both a ZNF143 binding site and a CTCF binding site. Pearson’s Chi-squared test with a Cramer’s V effect size measurement with the Funder and Ozer 201994 interpretation (“tiny”: |V| < 0.05; “very small”: |V| < 0.1; “small”: |V| < 0.2; “medium”: |V| < 0.3; “large”: |V| < 0.4; “very large”: |V| ≥ 0.4) was used to assess significant differences in overlap frequencies between genomic features. The functions cramers_v and interpret_cramers_v from R package effectsize v0.8.895 was used.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). A two-tailed Student’s t test with Welch’s correction was employed to compare two groups. One-way or two-way ANOVA analysis corrected by the Sidak multiple comparison test was used for multiple group comparisons. For all data, statistical significance was considered at p ≤ 0.05 or FDR ≤ 0.01. The error bars represent SEM in all figures unless otherwise stated.
