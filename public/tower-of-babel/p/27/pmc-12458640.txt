Synergy between TLR3-ligand and IFN-α in the transient sensitization of “Cold” tumors to PD-1 blockade and the induction of systemic immunity
Authors: Kathleen M Kokolus, Connor J Huck, Eoghan L Connors, Eduardo Cortes-Gomez, Jianmin Wang, Natasa Obermajer, Per H Basse, Pawel Kalinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProgrammed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)-blocking immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are effective against highly immunogenic “hot” tumors containing high numbers of cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CTLs), but not against poorly immunogenic “cold” tumors. We previously reported that the combination of TLR3 agonist rintatolimod with interferon (IFN)-α selectively induces CTL-attracting chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL5) in the tumor microenvironment (TME), but not healthy tissues, raising the possibility of their systemic application to promote local CTL attraction to TME.MethodsWe used mouse colorectal cancer (CRC) cells implanted in syngeneic mice to test the effects of chemokine modulatory regimen (CKM) in combination with PD-1 blockade applied at different schedules. Tumor-bearing mice were treated and monitored for survival. In addition, we evaluated the reliance of CKM+αPD-1 treatment on various immune cell subsets. Finally, we observed treatment-induced changes in immune markers within TME.ResultsHere, we report that both local or systemic application of CKM, a combination of rintatolimod and IFN-α, but not each of them individually, induces intratumoral CTL attractants and sensitizes PD-1-resistant MC38 and CT26 tumors to PD-1 blockade. The CKM/αPD-1 combination promotes intratumoral influx of BATF3-positive cDC1s and CTLs, inhibits tumor growth and prolongs survival, inducing cures in 20–100% of animals, depending on the tumor model and stage, and the route of delivery (local or systemic). CKM/αPD-1-treated mice develop local and systemic tumor-specific CTL responses and resistance to tumor re-challenge. The effectiveness of CKM requires its application at the time of or directly before PD-1 blockade and is strongly reduced by even a 24-hour delay in αPD-1 administration. CKM-driven intratumoral CTL accumulation and antitumor effects require host’s BATF3+cDC1s, CD8+T-lymphocytes, and CXCR3 and CCR5 (receptors for CXCL9/CXCL10 and CCL5).ConclusionsThe ability of systemic CKM to eliminate the PD-1-resistance of cold tumors indicates that intratumoral CTL accumulation, rather than tumor immunogenicity, is the key factor limiting the therapeutic effectiveness of ICI. These data suggest a broad therapeutic potential of TME-reprogramming strategies.Keywords: Colorectal Cancer, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors, Immunomodulation, Tumor Microenvironment

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Immunotherapies work best in “hot” tumors infiltrated with high numbers of cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CTLs). Data from our human ex vivo tumor explants and clinical trials demonstrated that the chemokine modulatory regimen (CKM) combining rintatolimod with interferon-α reprograms the tumor microenvironment by upregulating expression of CTL-attracting chemokines (ie, CCL5, CXCL9/10) while reducing expression of regulatory T cell(Treg)-attracting chemokines (ie, CCL22).

Current data suggest that systemic CKM can be widely used to sensitize “cold” tumors to immune-based therapies, even if tumors cannot be directly accessed. The demonstration that the effects of CKM require its administration at the same time or directly before αPD-1 treatment helps to design effective therapeutic regimens involving CKM.

Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) which block the interaction of T cell-expressed checkpoint molecules, such as programmed-death 1 (PD-1), with their ligands programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and programmed death-ligard 2 (PD-L2) within tumor tissues, have become the standard of treatment in many advanced cancers and significantly extended survival in patients with previously hard-to-treat malignancies,1,3 including metastatic, microsatellite instability–high/mismatch repair–deficient colorectal cancer (CRC).4 Since ICIs reactivate the immune system to specifically destroy cancer cells and promote long-lasting immune memory, responders often achieve durable tumor control.5 However, the therapeutic benefit of ICIs is currently limited by less than 25% of patients responding .6 7 Three major factors have been shown to predict the responding patients: high tumor mutational burden (TMB),8,10 presence of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)11,17 and intratumoral expression of PD-1 ligands.18 19 Although such factors can be interconnected with high TMB load driving TIL infiltration and TIL infiltration promoting interferon (IFN)-γ-driven induction of αPD-L1 and PD-L2 on cancer cells, local stroma, and myeloid cells,20 the relation between these factors and their relative values remains controversial.21,26 Interestingly, a recent study demonstrated that artificial increase in TMB is not sufficient to enhance ICI responsiveness of mouse tumors,27 raising a possibility that TIL content and PD-1 sensitivity may be regulated at least partially independently from the TMB. However, a recent study of personalized messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines demonstrated a dramatic ability of multiepitope vaccination to enhance the clinical effectiveness of neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade to prevent cancer recurrence,28 providing a promise of combinatorial approaches.

The accumulation of immune cells within the tumor is predictive of clinical outcomes, including in the CRC setting.29 30 Since tumors lacking sufficient cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) have been classified as “cold” and minimally responsive to immune-based treatment approaches,31 strategies to increase infiltration of CTLs into the tumor microenvironment (TME) have become increasingly sought.21 32 Our method to promote CTL infiltration, chemokine modulatory regimen (CKM),33,44 combines a type I IFN with toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) ligands, such as poly-IC36 37 39 43 or rintatolimod,43 which show synergistic activity in inducing selective production of CTL attractants, such as CXCL9, CXCL10 or CCL5, but not regulatory T cell (Treg) attractant CCL22.36 As a selective TLR3 ligand,45 46 rintatolimod does not activate NFkB, thus avoiding tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α induction,43 toxicity45 46 and NFkB/TNF-α/PGE2-dependent suppressive aspects of inflammation.43 In addition to reprogramming of the stromal and myeloid component of the TME, TLR3-ligation can contribute to dendritic cell (DC) maturation and T-cell stimulation,45 47 acting in concert with IFN-α.36 48 49

Notably, CKM effectiveness depends on the baseline activation of NFkB in the target tissues preferentially inducing the desirable CTL-attracting chemokines in the TME36 41 43, rather than healthy tissues, suggesting its potential to achieve local intratumoral effects, even after its systemic application. The strong synergy between the CKM components and intratumoral NFkB, allows the CKM combination to be effective in the TME at much lower concentrations than those needed to induce CTL attractants by IFN-α or rintatolimod alone,36 43 thus avoiding systemic toxicities and resulting in very good toxicity profiles of CKM when used jointly with chemotherapy or before PD-1 blockade.44 50 51 The reliance of CKM on baseline activation of NFkB, used by tumors to sustain viability and attract tumor-protecting suppressive cells,36 results in its Trojan Horse character, converting tumor-associated survival feature into a vulnerability.

Our recent clinical trials (NCT02432378, NCT03599453, NCT04081389) in patients with ovarian cancer and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) showed high tolerability of local and systemic CKM, and their ability to reprogram the TME for selective CTL influx.44 50 51 Despite these favorable immune effects, subsequent application of PD-1 blockade with an average delay of 1 week after CKM (NCT03599453 trial in TNBC) showed only modest clinical efficacy with 50% of long-term survivors and only one objective clinical response among six patients.44 Driven by these observations, we evaluated the mechanisms involved in CKM’s effectiveness and the impact of its timing relative to ICI. We show that CKM synergizes with PD-1 blockade, inducing cures in mouse CRC models, but requires concurrent application of PD-1 blockade. In addition to CXCR3-dependent and CCR5-dependent CTL attraction to the TME, the effectiveness of CKM+αPD-1 therapy involves conventional dendritic cells (cDC1s) and results in the induction of systemic immunity to tumor re-challenge.

Methods

Mice

Female 6–8 weeks old C57BL/6J (JAX stock #000664), BALB/cJ (JAX stock #000651), B6.129P2-Ccr5tm1Kuz/J (CCR5 KO; JAX stock #005427), and B6.129P2-Cxcr3tm1Dgen/J (CXCR3 KO; JAX stock #005796) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and experiments began within 2 weeks of arrival. Female 6–8 weeks old C.B-Igh-1bIcrTac-Prkdcscid/Ros (SCID) and NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice were bred by the Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center Comparative Oncology Shared Resource. Batf3 (Batf3 KO) mice were a gift from Dr Oscar Colegio and bred at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center. All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions in a high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA)-filtered ventilated rack system at the Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center Comparative Oncology Shared Resource. Mice were housed five or less per cage with Enrich-o’Cobs ¼ (The Andersons) enrichment. Sterile pellet Teklad Global 2018SC Rodent Diet (Inotiv) and water were provided ad libitum. Mice were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 24°C±2° (~70°F) and 50% average humidity (range 30–70%). All in vivo experiments were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center.

Cell lines

MC38 (Kerafast) and CT26 (American Type Culture Collection) were obtained directly from the manufacturer and stored frozen in aliquots after <5 passages. Cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 with GlutaMAX (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and other additives according to manufacturer recommendations. MC38 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), non-essential amino acids (NEAA), sodium pyruvate, l-glutamine, and antibiotics (penicillin streptomycin and gentamycin sulfate) and CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with GlutaMAX (Gibco) media enriched with 10% FBS, HEPES, NEAA, sodium pyruvate, l-glutamine, and penicillin streptomycin. Prior to inoculation, cells were harvested at ∼90% confluency at <10 passages from the original stock. For in vivo work, freshly harvested tumor cells were suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and injected subcutaneously into the left flank or intraperitoneally. Subcutaneous injections of both CT26 and MC38 cells were administered at 500,000 cells per 50 µL PBS for initial inoculation. Intraperitoneal injections of MC38 were given at 500,000 cells per 200 µL PBS. For rechallenge, mice surviving following treatment of intraperitoneal tumors were inoculated with 1,000,000 MC38 cells per 50 µL PBS subcutaneously.

Treatment strategies

Local and systemic treatments were modeled by administering treatment intraperitoneally to mice bearing intraperitoneal and subcutaneous tumors, respectively. Local delivery is intended to route drug directly to the tumor (although some drug may also enter circulation), while systemic delivery intends to deliver the drug to the TME via circulation. To model early-stage CRC, treatments were administered beginning on day 3 post-implantation. To model late-stage CRC, treatments were administered to mice bearing palpable tumors at~10–14 days after inoculation (tumor volume ∼20–100 mm3). Rintatolimod (provided by AIM ImmunoTech) was administered in 50 µg doses, and IFN-α (Miltenyi Biotec) was administered in 10,000 IU doses, which we identified as optimal.42 For PD-1 blockade, αPD-1 (clone RMP1-14; Leinco Technologies) was administered in 200 µg doses. For concomitant administration, three cycles consisting of two doses of CKM (50 µg rintatolimod and 10,000 IU IFN-α) 1 day apart were given, followed by 200 µg αPD-1 the next day. For sequential treatment, two doses of CKM (50 µg rintatolimod and 10,000 IU IFN-α) were given 1 day apart followed by three doses of αPD-1 (200 µg) 1, 5, and 10 days after the second CKM dose. When administering CKM or αPD-1 alone, doses were administered on the corresponding days these treatments were given in the combinatorial schedule of CKM+αPD-1. For mice receiving PBS control, 200 µL sterile PBS was administered on all days and treatment was administered to comparative groups of mice. When any combination of rintatolimod, IFN-α, and αPD-1 was given together, they were administered in a single intraperitoneal injection. All treatments were administered intraperitoneally in a total volume of 200 µL PBS. Tumors were measured using digital calipers and tumor volumes (mm3) were calculated as length×width×width/2. Following the initiation of treatment, tumors were measured at least two times a week until size reached 1,000 mm3 at which point measurements were taken daily. The endpoints for survival studies included the tumor volume reaching >1,500 mm3, mice becoming lethargic, or ascites development prohibiting movement. Endpoints were noted when subcutaneous tumors reached 1,500 mm3 or the longest diameter reached 2 cm for subcutaneous tumors. For intraperitoneal tumors, endpoints were noted when the diameter or shape of the abdomen has clearly changed (abdominal distension). All mice were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation at endpoints in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center.

CD8+ T-cell depletion

To deplete CD8-positive cells, CD8 antibody (Clone: 53–5.8; Bio X Cell) was administered on days 2 and 3. Beginning on day 10, the antibody was administered three times every 2 weeks until tumors were cured, or mice reached their endpoint. Control mice were treated with IgG antibody (Clone: HRPN; Bio X Cell) were treated on the same days. All antibody was administered intraperitoneally in 200 µL PBS.

Evaluation of gene expression

The expression of mRNA was analyzed using TaqMan-based real-time quantitative PCR. For in vitro analysis, MC38 cells were cultured and subsequently treated in DMEM media supplemented as described above. For ex vivo analysis of splenocytes, spleens were excised from mice following treatment, digested into a single-cell suspension, and red-blood cell lysed before incubation and treatment in AIM-V supplemented with penicillin streptomycin. Splenocytes and cancer cell lines were treated with 50 µg/mL rintatolimod, 10,000 IU/mL IFN-α, or a combination of both. For ex vivo total TME analysis, tumors were excised from mice following treatment. Spleen and tumor samples were processed in RLT buffer (Qiagen), and homogenizing tubes were used for digestion of tumors. Supernatants were collected and total RNA was extracted using RNeasy kit (Qiagen). For RNA, 250 ng per complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis reaction was used to generate cDNA with a QScript cDNA Synthesis kit (QuantaBio) and the resulting cDNA was diluted 5× in DNase/RNase-free water for mRNA expression analysis. TaqMan primers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) included: Batf3 (Mm01318274_m1), CD8α (Mm01182107_g1), CCL5 (Mm01302427_m1), CXCL9 (Mm00434946_m1), CXCL10 (Mm99999072_m1), and GZMB (Mm00442837_m1).

ELISpot

Spleens and lymph nodes from MC38-bearing mice were excised from mice following treatment, digested into a single cell suspension, and red-blood cell lysed with ACK buffer (Gibco). Samples were enriched for CD8 cells using EasySep Mouse CD8α2 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL). Lymph-node cells and CD8-selected splenocytes were incubated overnight at a density of 2×106 cells/mL in CTL-Test Medium (ImmunoSpot) with low-dose (50 IU/mL) IL-2 (McKesson). Post incubation, CD8+ splenocytes and lymph node cells were harvested, counted, and added to a 96-well plate at a concentration of 1×105 cells/well. Cells were treated with media (control), 4T1 (negative control cell line), or MC38 to determine MC38 antigen specificity. Spots were counted to calculate the total number of splenic antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and lymph node cells.

Splenic MC38 antigen-specific CD8+ T cells were quantified via IFN-γ production. A 96-well filtration plate MultiScreen HA (Millipore) was coated with 200 µL/well anti-mouse IFN-γ mAb (AN18) unconjugated (Mabtech) overnight at 4°C under light protection, washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and blocked with 200 µL RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin, at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 2 hours. Interleukin (IL)-2-stimulated CD8-selected splenocytes and lymph node cells were incubated in a 96-well culture plate coated with IFN-γ antibody overnight at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Positive spots stained with an IFN-γ antibody were counted using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) plate reader (ImmunoSpot) and quantified using ImmunoSpot V.5.1 software (CTL).

ELISA

Spleens were excised from mice, digested into a single-cell suspension, red-blood cell lysed with ACK buffer (Gibco), and enriched for CD8 cells using EasySep Mouse CD8α2 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL). CD8+ splenocytes were incubated in CTL-Test Medium (ImmunoSpot) with low-dose IL-2 overnight at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. CD8+ splenocytes were then co-cultured with live MC38 cells (on target), live 4T1 (off target), or additional media (no target). Following a 48-hour incubation, supernatants of the respective co-cultured samples are collected and stored for further analysis. ELISA plates were coated with primary IFN-γ antibody (R&D Systems) at 1 mg/mL overnight at room temperature. Plates were washed and blocked with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 2 hours. Samples were loaded (200 µL/well) at 1× and 5× dilutions and incubated for a minimum of 2 hours at room temperature on a plate rocker. Cells were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) three times before loading biotinylated secondary antibody at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The plate was washed before streptavidin-HRP conjugate (R&D Systems) incubation for 30 min. After the addition of TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), the reaction was stopped with 2% H2SO4 solution. Absorbance at 450 nm and 540 nm was measured on Epoch Microplate Reader (Agilent).

Flow cytometry

Tumors were harvested from mice and dissociated with a GentleMacs Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotech) using the protocol and reagents from the mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech). Debris Removal Solution (Miltenyi Biotech) was used to generate a stable cell pellet. Tumor cells were red-blood cell lysed with ACK buffer (Gibco) and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 2.5×107 cells/mL. Cells (200 µL) were added to a 96-well plate, stained with LIVE DEAD Fixable Blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific), then resuspended into PBS containing 0.1% sodium azide (VWR chemicals), 2% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 2 min before cells were resuspended in PBS containing Fc block (Bio X Cell). Samples were stained with BUV615-CD45.2 (clone 104: Cytek Biosciences), PeCy7-CD3 (clone 17A2; Cytek Biosciences), BUV737-CD4 (clone RM4-5; Thermo Fisher Scientific), SuperBright 645-CD8α (clone 53–6.7; Thermo Fisher Scientific), BV570-Ly6G (clone 1A8; BioLegend), BUV563-F480 (clone BM8; Thermo Fisher Scientific), VF450-CD11b (clone M1/70; Cytek Biosciences), BV711-CD11c (clone N418; BioLegend), RB545-MHC class II (clone M5/114.15.2, BD Biosciences), BUV805-CD69 (clone H1.2F3; BD Biosciences—Optibuild), APCfire810-CD279 (clone 29F.1A12; BioLegend), BV480-TIGIT (clone GIGD7; Thermo Fisher Scientific), RBV705-CD80 (clone 16–10 A1; BD Bioscience), and BV785-CD206 (clone C068C2; BioLegend) with Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Bioscience). Samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 2 min before cells were fixed with Fix Buffer (BD Bioscience) followed by permeabilization with BD Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Bioscience) and stained with BUV496-Ki-67 (clone SolA15; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data was obtained using the Aurora flow cytometry analyzer (Cytek) located in the Flow and Image Cytometry Shared Resource at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center and analyzed using FlowJo V.10.8.1 software (BD Biosciences).

Statistics

One-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post-test for multiple comparisons was used to compare readouts from three or four groups and unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction were used to compare readouts between two groups. All p values<0.05 are reported and indicated in the figures. Kaplan-Meier survival plots and log-rank tests were used to display and analyze the time from the beginning of treatment to death or the date of sacrifice. All tests were two-sided. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.10 or higher.

Results

Systemic CKM induces CTL-attracting chemokines in the TME, promoting CTL

To determine the impact of CKM on mouse CRC, MC38 cells were treated with rintatolimod, IFN-α, or their combination (CKM). Expression of CCL5, CXCL9 and CXCL10, chemokines known to attract CTLs, was significantly upregulated following CKM treatment (figure 1A). IFN-α alone upregulated expression of CXCL9 and CXCL10 to a similar extent as the combinatorial CKM, but the induction of CCL5 was strictly dependent on the exposure to both CKM components, indicating that optimal TME modulation requires a combinatorial approach.

To assess if similar changes can be induced in vivo, mice with established subcutaneous MC38 tumors were treated with one dose of CKM intraperitoneally. Tumors were harvested 3 days after treatment. As predicted by our in vitro data, systemic CKM enhanced local intratumoral expression of CTL-attracting chemokines CCL5 and CXCL10 with an additional trend towards increased CXCL9 expression (figure 1B). In addition, systemic CKM promoted intratumoral increases of CTL markers, CD8α and granzyme B (figure 1B). In contrast, CKM did not impact splenic expression of CD8 or granzyme B in naïve mice, nor were intrasplenic levels of CCL5, CXCL9, or CXCL10 influenced by CKM (online supplemental figure S1). Lack of the chemokine induction in the spleen is aligned with our previous observation of the dependence of CKM effectiveness on intratumoral NFkB activation,36 and the resulting tumor selectivity of this treatment.36 41 43

Our previous work has shown that a combination of TLR3 ligands (poly:IC or rintatolimod) and type 1 IFN (IFN-α) reprograms the TME of various human and mouse tumors.36 37 39 42 43 We tested this concept in vivo by testing the antitumor efficacy of CKM, and its components, IFN-α and rintatolimod, in MC38-bearing mice. While there was no notable impact of either IFN-α or rintatolimod monotherapies, we found a modest, yet significant (p=0.0196), extension of survival in mice treated with CKM compared with untreated mice (figure 1C and online supplemental figure S2). Next, we added αPD-1 to CKM and each component of the combinatorial treatment. While αPD-1 alone, IFN-α+αPD-1, and rintatolimod+αPD-1 had no effect on survival, survival was strongly enhanced when CKM was combined with αPD-1 (figure 1C, online supplemental figure S2). Strikingly, the PD-1 blockade was exclusively effective in promoting the survival benefit when combined with the combinatorial CKM, but not with IFN-α or rintatolimod alone, consistent with the need for both of these factors, to induce optimal chemokine modulation in the TME.36 43 These findings demonstrate a synergistic effect, which requires all three components of the CKM+αPD-1 combination therapy, for effective tumor control.

Local or systemic CKM converts nominally PD-1-resistant tumors into PD-1-responsive ones

To evaluate the impact of the timing of PD-1 blockade relative to CKM, we compared two treatment schedules. As shown in figure 2A, in the “Concomitant” schedule CKM was administered directly before αPD-1 in each of three cycles and in the “Sequential” schedule the CKM component was administered first, followed by three subsequent αPD-1 injections. In addition, we also compared the effectiveness of local (figure 2B) and systemic (figure 2C–E) treatments by inoculating tumors via intraperitoneal or subcutaneous routes, respectively. Local treatment of early-stage MC38 tumors, starting 3 days after inoculation, resulted in complete abrogation of tumors in all mice receiving either concomitant or sequential treatments (figure 2, online supplemental figure S3). In this model, both concomitant and sequential CKM+αPD-1 regimens significantly extended the survival beyond that of CKM alone (p<0.001), αPD-1 alone (p<0.001), or PBS (p<0.01).

Because the systemic treatments are more clinically feasible, we next evaluated the antitumor effectiveness of CKM+αPD-1 being administered distantly. In the systemic model of early-stage subcutaneous tumors receiving intraperitoneal treatment, both concomitant and sequential schedules showed better efficacy than monotherapies (figure 2C and online supplemental figure S4). In this setting, concomitant drug administration did result in tumor control in all mice, and in about 70% of mice receiving sequential treatment. Both regimens significantly prolonged survival compared with CKM (p<0.05) and PBS (p<0.01), while concomitant treatment also led to significantly longer survival than αPD-1 alone (p<0.01). When treating established tumors, both the concomitant and sequential CKM+αPD-1 treatments resulted in at least 50 days survival in half of the treated mice, significantly higher than control mice (figure 2D and online supplemental figure S5). As predicted by the requirement for synergistic action of both IFN-α and rintatolimod in the induction of CTL attractants (figure 1A), the therapeutic effects required combined application of both rintatolimod and IFN-α, in addition to PD-1 blockade.

The efficacy of CKM+αPD-1 observed in the MC38 model, most commonly used to test immune therapies,52,55 was also observed in the fully PD-1-resistant weakly immunogenic CT26 model, even on its sequential application. It uniformly delayed the progression and induced cures in 25% of the animals with CT26 tumors, which were fully resistant to PD-1 blockade alone (figure 2E and online supplemental figure S6). CKM+αPD-1 administration prolonged survival compared with all controls (p<0.0001).

CKM+αPD-1 treatment induces the influx of PD-1+ CTLs and cDC1s

Using multispectral flow cytometry, we evaluated immune cell composition of the TME (online supplemental figure S7). Following treatment with CKM, we observed significant increases in infiltrating lymphocytes including CD8+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and B cells, while CD4+ T cells showed a downward trend (figure 3A). Among myeloid cell populations, we observed no change in neutrophils in CKM-treated tumors, while DCs were significantly elevated (figure 3B). We also looked at subpopulations of macrophages and we saw decreases in pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages, while M2-like macrophages remained the same (figure 3B), a finding consistent with the ability of rintatolimod to avoid the induction of PGE2, arginase and iNOS,40 56 57 the factors mediating the myeloid immunosuppression, in mouse and human cancers.58 59 When further examining the CD8+ T-cell population in the CKM-treated tumors, we found no change in activation marker CD69, but a significant increase in CD8+ T cells expressing PD-1 following CKM (figure 3C). In addition, the percentage of Ki67-expressing CD8+ T cells increased following CKM treatment suggesting that CKM induces effector cell proliferation (figure 3C). Overall, these data demonstrate that CKM promotes accumulation of both DCs and activated CD8+ T cells in the TME and suggest that these immune cell subsets play a prominent role in the efficacy of CKM+αPD-1 therapy observed in vivo.

Both the chemokine-driven CTL attraction to “hot” tumors60 and local antigen uptake and cross-presentation and induction of systemic immunity depend on a specialized subset of BATF3+ cDC1s.61,64 In accordance with their role in the induction of tumor-specific immunity, we observed strongly elevated levels of not only total intratumoral DCs (figure 3B), but also elevated BATF3 levels in the TME as well as in splenocytes of the CKM-treated mice (figure 3D).

CKM+αPD-1 induces durable systemic tumor-specific CTL responses

To explore the long-term, durable immune effects of CKM+αPD-1 therapy, we next rechallenged mice who experienced curative CKM+αPD-1 therapy as defined by complete tumor regression. The CKM/αPD-1-treated mice became fully resistant to tumor re-challenge when inoculated with two times the original number of MC38 cells (figure 4A), suggesting that, in addition to local attraction of CTLs, CKM may also facilitate the induction of tumor-specific systemic immunity.

The analysis of splenocytes and lymph nodes of the CKM/αPD-1-treated mice demonstrated strong increases in the numbers of tumor-specific splenic and nodal CD8+ T cells that specifically recognize the MC38 cells, with no or only marginal responses to unrelated (4T1) cancer cells (figure 4B–D). Interestingly, the percentage (~67%) of responding mice corresponds with our observations of long-term responses from treating early-stage MC38 tumor with sequential administration of CKM+αPD-1 (figure 2D). The mice which experienced the largest tumor regressions also had the highest production of IFN-γ (figure 4C). In addition, CD8+ splenocytes from long-term (over 90 day) survivors who experienced tumor regression following CKM+αPD-1 treatment exhibited MC38-antigen specific IFN-γ production (figure 4D). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that CKM+αPD-1 therapy can induce favorable immune changes that occur quickly after treatment but also result in long-lasting immunity.

CKM/αPD-1 efficacy requires CTLs, BATF3+ cDC1s, and chemokine receptors CXCR3 and CCR5

T cells and DCs, especially BATF3+ cDC1s, which specialize in cancer antigen cross-presentation,60 61 63 play a pivotal role in antitumor immunity.64 To explore their roles in the systemic CKM+αPD-1 treatment, we used a series of genetically deficient animals. We found no effect of CKM+αPD-1 treatment in the MC38-bearing immunodeficient SCID mice (figure 5A and online supplemental figure S8), which lack T cells,65 or NSG mice66 which lack T and NK cells (figure 5B and online supplemental figure S9), showing the key roles of these immune subsets in the therapeutic effectiveness of the CKM/αPD-1 combination. To evaluate the role of CD8+ T cells in the efficacy of CKM+αPD-1, we performed antibody depletion of CD8 cells, observing complete elimination of the treatment efficacy (figure 5C and online supplemental figure S10). In support of the key role of endogenous BATF3+ cDC1s,61 62 we also observed (figure 5D and online supplemental figure S11) the complete abrogation of the therapeutic efficacy of CKM+αPD-1 combination in BATF3 KO mice, which lack cDC1s.63

To evaluate the roles of the CKM-induced chemokines in the antitumor efficacy of the CKM+αPD-1 combination, we compared its effectiveness in the WT animals and the animals lacking CCR5, the ligand for CTL-attracting chemokine CCL5,67 or lacking CXCR3, the ligand for CTL-attracting chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10.68 We found that mice lacking CCR5 show a partially reduced response to CKM+αPD-1 therapy, compared with WT C57BL/6 mice, (figure 5E, online supplemental figure S12). However, the mice lacking CXCR3 showed complete abrogation of the therapeutic effects of the CKM+αPD-1 therapy (figure 5F, online supplemental figure S13). These findings indicate a minor role for the CCR5 - CCL5 interaction and the key role of the CXCR3 - CXCL9/10 axis in the efficacy of this treatment.

Transient character of the CKM-induced sensitization to PD-1 blockade

To test the duration of the CKM-induced window of sensitization of tumors to PD-1 blockade, we compared the effectiveness of αPD-1 administered at the same time or at different time points after CKM completion of treatment. As shown in figure 6 and online supplemental figure S14, the immediate implementation of PD-1 blockade, directly after CKM completion allowed us to achieve close to 90% response rate. Unexpectedly, the effectiveness of CKM-induced responsiveness to PD-1 blockade diminished almost immediately after treatment showing strong reduction (~60–70%) when αPD-1 was administered 1 day after CKM. Further reduction of tumor regression was seen when αPD-1 was delayed until 3 days after administration, and the efficacy was virtually eliminated when αPD-1 was delayed by 5 days (figure 6). These data indicate that αPD-1 should be administered together or immediately after CKM for the optimal clinical benefit in the upcoming clinical trials.

Discussion

Our study shows that CKM, a combination of a TLR3 agonist (rintatolimod) with type-1 IFN (IFN-α), but not each of these factors alone, can be used to promote antitumor effectiveness of PD-1 blockade against nominally PD-1-resistant tumors. This treatment strategy is associated with favorable immune changes in the TME, including the induction of CTL attractants, CD8α, granzyme B, and TNF-α, and the enhanced levels of PD-1-expressing CTLs and BATF3+ cDC1s. Our findings provide direct in vivo evidence that the synergy between TLR3 ligands and IFN-α, we originally identified in human tumor explants ex vivo,36 43 can be used to reprogram the TME in vivo for therapeutic purposes, while avoiding the need for high doses of IFN-α known to mediate the undesirable systemic induction of chemokines and systemic toxicities.

The addition of CKM to PD-1 blockade allowed tumor control in nominally PD-1-resistant tumors. Both local and systemic administration of this combinatorial regimen resulted in durable tumor regressions, even in late-stage tumors which showed a striking response (~50% cures). The ability of CKM+αPD-1 treatment to induce durable tumor-specific responses and immunity to tumor rechallenge suggests a potential benefit of this combination in neoadjuvant settings.

While CKM+αPD-1 therapy also induced survival benefit in mice bearing CT26 tumors, cures were achieved in a lesser proportion of mice than observed in MC38-bearing mice. The CT26 tumors are considered less immunogenic, and thus less sensitive to PD-1 blockade, than MC38.69 However, even the less effective sequential regimen promoted a striking survival prolongation of the CT26-bearing animals, despite complete resistance to αPD-1 alone. In-depth analysis of the differences between these two CRC models could provide important context to the mechanics behind the antitumor benefits of CKM+αPD-1 treatment.

Our data show a requirement for two chemokine receptors in the effectiveness of CKM+αPD-1 treatment. We observed a partial dependence on CCR5 (figure 5E), the ligand for CTL-attracting chemokine CCL5.67 CCR5 has been shown to be a necessary signaling component for macrophage-driven expression of inflammation in response to double-stranded RNA,70 and since our treatment regimen contains rintatolimod, a double-stranded RNA which activates TLR3,46 it is not surprising that CCR5 deficiency impedes the effects of CKM+αPD-1 therapy. While CCL5/CCR5 signaling is essential to the recruitment of CTLs to the TME,71,74 it has also been implicated in the recruitment of Tregs.75 This consideration might suggest that Treg depletion may be further enhancing the effects of CKM; however, we did not observe any Treg enhancement in our two recent clinical trials.44 50 In addition, the data from Ron Germain’s laboratory indicated its key role in guiding DC-T cell interaction during the induction of primary immune responses,76 and multiple other studies have indicated its role in attraction of additional DCs to the sites of inflammatory reactions or tumor growth.74 77 Follow-up experiments are required to determine the impact of CCR5 depletion on the immunosuppressive TME and the DC system following CKM+αPD-1 treatment.

This study demonstrates a notable reliance on chemokine receptor CXCR3 (figure 5F), which serves as a ligand for T-cell attracting chemokines CXCL9/10/11 and is prominently expressed on activated T and NK cells.68 While CXCR3’s absence perturbs T-cell trafficking into the TME, some T cells can infiltrate the tumor in mouse models and show little changes in lymphoid tissues.78 The complete abrogation of CKM+αPD-1 efficacy in CXCR3 KO mice indicates the dominant impact of CXCR3 in this model. In addition to its well-documented role in the intratumoral accumulation of CTLs, CXCR3 has been shown to augment the ability of tissue-localized CD8+ T cells to find abnormal cells and enact effector functions in tumor79,82 and viral models83 84 and to facilitate autoimmune reactions in murine graft-versus-host disease models.85 Further studies are needed to understand if similar mechanisms are at play in mouse tumor models.

The ability of the CKM to promote the intratumoral influx of CTLs and other type-1 immune cells (NK and NK-T cells), which all express CXCR3 and CCR536, was predicted by our preclinical ex vivo studies36 43 and phase 1 clinical studies in patients with ovarian and breast cancer.44 50 51 While the increases in some of the immune cell subsets observed after CKM treatment were subtle, their roles were supported by the depletion studies. Several scenarios can explain the ability of these small, yet significant, changes in immune cell numbers to contribute to substantial therapeutic impact. Potential unique activation status, memory differentiation, or reduced exhaustion and resistance to suppression of the newly infiltrating cells needs to be explored. The ability of CKM to promote intratumoral increases in cDC1s, tumor-specific systemic immunization and protection from tumor re-challenge represents the unexpected aspects of our findings, which mechanism still needs to be fully understood. The current data suggests the cDC1 dependence of these effects, but the underlying mechanism of their accumulation or induction in the tumor tissues remains to be determined.

Similarly unexpected was the strict timing requirement of CKM in relation to the αPD-1 treatment. Our initial expectation for the combined CKM and αPD-1 therapy was that CKM-induced susceptibility to PD-1 blockade would benefit from a delay between CKM and αPD-1 administration, allowing time for the influx of PD-1-expressing CTLs. In contrast, our data demonstrate that the therapy regimen worked best when αPD-1 was administered either together or immediately after CKM (figure 6, online supplemental figure S14). The simplest explanations for these findings are either a transient character of the CKM-induced CTL accumulation or a rapid inactivation of the CKM-mobilized CTLs in the suppressive TME in the absence of PD-1 blockade. However, it is also possible that only a closely associated timing of the CKM and PD-1 blockade allows for the optimal mobilization of cDC1s or their optimal activity in the induction of tumor-specific CTLs or support for pre-existing antigen-specific cells in the TME. The roles of the early PD-1 exposure and tumor-intrinsic and CKM-induced DC-activating and CTL-activating and suppressive immune mediators and metabolic factors in the above scenarios remain to be established.

In accordance with the lack of overt toxicity of any of the regimens tested in the current study (and our IND enabling studies), our clinical trials of local or systemic CKM administered together with chemotherapy of ovarian and breast cancers or before PD-1 blockade therapy of advanced breast cancer44 50 51 have shown very good tolerability of these combination regimens. The toxicity and potential efficacy of sequential and concomitant regimens of CKM/αPD-1 is being compared in our clinical trial NCT05756166 in patients with advanced breast cancer. Although mouse models of PD-1 blockade therapies have been widely recognized as poor predictors of human toxicities,86,88 our clinical trial should provide the most reliable insights into this question.

Independently of their mechanism, these narrow time constraints have immediate implications for the design of clinical trials with efficacy endpoints. Our recently completed trial NCT03599453 in six patients with metastatic TNBC demonstrated consistent strong CTL increases in the biopsies obtained within 48 hours post-CKM (average 10.3× increase), compared with pretreatment biopsies. However, only one of six patients demonstrated an overt tumor response (a near-complete clinical response) even if all patients received follow-up pembrolizumab within 2 weeks of CKM termination (1 week delay on average). Although the long-term outcomes of that small study were highly encouraging, with 50% of patients surviving at least 4 years, and the responding patient remaining disease-free at the 4-year follow-up mark, our recently initiated trial NCT05756166 is a regimen-escalation study progressing from sequential (with immediate introduction of PD-1 blockade after CKM) to a concomitant application of CKM and pembrolizumab.

In summary, our current study demonstrates the need for a concomitant or back-to-back CKM/αPD-1 application to benefit from their optimal therapeutic synergy and indicates the key roles of CTLs and their two chemokine receptors, CXCR3 and (to a lesser extent) CCR5, in the mechanism of the therapeutic action of this combination. At the same time, our data indicate an unexpected dependence of this combination on the BATF3+ cDC1s, providing for a new biomarker for prospective clinical trials and additional targets to enhance the therapeutic effectiveness of CKM-based treatments.
