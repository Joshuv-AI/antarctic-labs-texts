Digital annealing optimization for natural product structure elucidation
Authors: Chien Lee, Pei-Hua Wang, Yufeng Jane Tseng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe digital annealer (DA) leverages its computational capabilities of up to 100 000 bits to address the complex nondeterministic polynomial-time (NP)-complete challenge inherent in elucidating complex structures of natural products. Conventional computational methods often face limitations with complex mixtures, as they struggle to manage the high dimensionality and intertwined relationships typical in natural products, resulting in inefficiencies and inaccuracies. This study reformulates the challenge into a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization framework, thereby harnessing the quantum-inspired computing power of the DA. Utilizing mass spectrometry data from three distinct herb species and various potential scaffolds, the DA proficiently locates optimal sidechain combinations that adhere to predefined target molecular weights. This methodology enhances the probability of selecting appropriate sidechains and substituted positions and ensures the generation of solutions within a reasonable 5-min window. The findings underscore the transformative potential of the DA in the realms of analytical chemistry and drug discovery, markedly improving both the precision and practicality of natural product structure elucidation.

Introduction

The digital annealer (DA) [1], commercialized by Fujitsu, is a computing architecture that draws inspiration from quantum annealers. It utilizes the Markov chain Monte Carlo technique to generate search points and explore nearby optimal solutions through specialized hardware. Starting with 1024 bits in the first-generation DA chip, the third-generation chip now boasts 100 000 bits for optimization problems. The DA is based on the simulated annealing algorithm and incorporates several features to improve solution quality [2], facilitating the parallel evaluation of candidate solutions and faster optimization.

DA has been used to solve problems in different fields. In the studies of Javad-Kalbasi and Naghsh [3, 4], a DA generated high-quality solutions and outperformed some existing solvers when solving well-known vertex cover problems and the maximum clique problem. In another study by Matsubara et al. [2], the DA outperformed the well-known exact solver CPLEX regarding efficiency and solution quality for the problem instances. Moreover, Şeker et al. [5] conducted a comprehensive study on the performance of the DA. They compared it with state-of-the-art solvers for combinatorial optimization problems (COPs) for three types of problems. Their findings revealed that the DA can provide good-quality solutions compared to those of other solvers, and its runtime is not affected by an increase in the problem instance size. In this study, we devise a novel approach incorporating digital annealing technology to address the challenge of structure elucidation.

The quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem is an nondeterministic polynomial time (NP)-hard combinatorial optimization challenge that poses significant difficulty for all existing algorithms and computers. DAs, however, are specifically designed to handle QUBO problems, taking them as input and searching for optimal solutions [6]. To transform a problem into a QUBO problem, the decisions required in the original problem are converted into a binary vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $x$\end{document}, representing the solution of the QUBO problem. The cost functions and constraints are encoded into a matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $Q$\end{document}, encompassing the coefficients of the variables’ linear and quadratic terms. DAs then find the optimal solutions by minimizing the energy function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${x}^T Qx$\end{document}.

Mass spectrometry (MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) are the two major experimental methods used to provide data for analysis in the elucidation process. MS experiments can provide data on molecular weight, fragmentation patterns, and molecular formulas, which can be used in structure matching but are insufficient in elucidating unknown compounds. High-resolution NMR can provide a spectroscopic view of the target compounds, but structure elucidation with NMR data depends greatly on the purity of the target compound [7]. When combining MS and NMR data, computational systems are needed to analyze the data to successfully identify the structures of unknown compounds.

The proposed solution process of the structure elucidation problem.

Harn et al. [8] developed NP-StructurePredictor, a system that utilizes liquid chromatography-MS (LC–MS) data to predict the structures of natural products. Combining seed scaffolds and potential sidechains to match the LC–MS data, Su et al. [9] further converted the matching procedure into a formal computational problem known as the chemical constituents–core combinatorial problem (CSCCP).

The CSCCP is an NP-complete [9] COP and is thus difficult to solve within a reasonable timeframe. Several algorithms were developed to speed up the solution-finding process. NP-StructurePredictor uses a branch-and-bound (BB) method to substitute sidechains onto scaffolds for structure prediction. IDPforCSCCP, a novel dynamic programming approach in [9], significantly improves analyzability and running time. However, the runtime of IDPforCSCCP is still dependent on the accuracy of floating numbers that describe molecular weight information, resulting in a pseudopolynomial runtime in small-scale experimented cases. There is, however, the potential for the emerging field of quantum computing [10] and quantum-inspired algorithms to address COPs.

LC–MS experiments were conducted to obtain possible molecular weights, and a list of candidate scaffolds and possible sidechain information was obtained to determine the chemical structure of a natural product mixture. Based on the acquired data, we encoded the problem as a QUBO model and input it into Fujitsu’s third-generation DA. The solutions found by the DA were further analyzed to identify the correct structure. The main problem-solving architecture is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

In this section, we present the problem statement that underpins our research objectives. We first describe the dataset we employed, followed by a definition of the CSCCP. Next, we provide an overview of the algorithms that aim to solve the CSCCP introduced by [9]. Finally, we propose a QUBO formulation of the CSCCP that can be processed as input by the DA. The QUBO formulation solves the COP by leveraging the capabilities of digital annealing technology.

Datasets

The experiments utilized a dataset of natural products from four different herbs: Cuscuta chinensis, Ophiopogon japonicus, Polygonum multiflorum, and Angelica sp. The same dataset was used in [9], and no modifications were made to the statistics of the dataset itself, as our primary objective was to develop a new methodology and make a fair comparison with previous approaches. Using experimental procedures from the Natural Product Laboratory of the Taiwan Medical and Pharmaceutical Industry Technology and Development Center (PIDTC), a list of target molecular weights was obtained from each herb’s LC–MS spectra, and a list of seed scaffolds was identified. Our algorithm aims to generate potential chemical structures by substituting sidechains onto the seed scaffolds, accounting for the occurrence probabilities and weights of the sidechains. To obtain sidechain information, we utilized pre-generated statistics from several databases, including the natural products found in the Dictionary of Natural Products® (DNP), the natural product subset of ZINC [11], and the Traditional Chinese Medicine Database (TCMD) [12]. To verify the accuracy of the generated structures, the Natural Product Laboratory of PIDTC identified the true chemical structures using liquid chromatography with tandem MS (LC–MS/MS). These identified structures served as the correct structures for our experiments.

Considering the limited usage quota of DAs, our structure searching efforts were focused on natural products of O. japonicus, P. multiflorum, and C. chinensis. The RDKit [13] Python package calculated the molecular weights of scaffold structures. Marvin [14] drew, displayed, and characterized chemical structures.

Dynamic programming algorithms for chemical constituents–core combinatorial problem

The DPforCSCCP algorithm [9] is a dynamic programming algorithm that solves the CSCCP in pseudopolynomial time. This algorithm takes as input a scaffold configuration, sidechain information (including probabilities and weights), the total weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${W}_t$\end{document}, and the parameter r. The CSCCP cost is defined as C(s, w, r), which is a function of the rth highest value of the probability product of the sidechains when considering the substituted positions 1 to S, satisfying the requirement that the sum of the weights of the selected sidechains equals w. The DPforCSCCP algorithm sequentially fills in the dynamic programming table of C by iterating through the weights and substituted positions starting from the initial indices. The final output of the algorithm is the set of values C(S, 1:Wt, R).

The DPforCSCCP algorithm operates in pseudopolynomial time by treating floating point weights as integers, which enables the algorithm to iterate through the weight values when filling in the dynamic programming table. The conversion from floating-point numbers to integers is accomplished by multiplying by a factor of 10D, where the parameter D controls the algorithm’s precision. When D is too large, the complexity of DPforCSCCP may be worse than that of brute-force search. As suggested in the original paper, this problem can be addressed by designing the iterative DPforCSCCP (IDPforCSCCP) algorithm, which gradually increases the parameter R’, representing the number of top solutions. When R’ is sufficiently large, IDPforCSCCP can obtain all the values that DPforCSCCP can produce without encountering precision problems.

Problem-solving architecture

The CSCCP acts as a subproblem in the broader challenge of fully elucidating the chemical structure of a natural product. The DPforCSCCP (IDPforCSCCP) algorithm is applied, generating a comprehensive set of potential molecular structures across various possible molecular weights and scaffolds to derive the best guesses of the chemical structures. These potential structures are subsequently filtered using specific criteria to identify the most likely candidates, which are then presented as the final predictions.

In this study, we develop an algorithm integrating a digital annealing subroutine into the structural elucidation process. The subroutine is specifically designed to address a modified version of the CSCCP and takes a fixed molecular weight, a scaffold with pre-specified configurations, and corresponding sidechain information as input. Similar to the joint algorithm that utilizes DPforCSCCP (IDPforCSCCP), the possible molecular weights, scaffolds, and configurations are iterated through a standard program. In each iteration, the digital annealing routine is called upon to maximize the product of the sidechain occurrence probabilities, and the valid solutions found are returned. As its name suggests, a QUBO is characterized by single and quadratic terms of variables, with each term associated with a constant stored in the matrix Q. Its equivalent form, the Ising model, considers solving the same problem but with the variables selected from {−1, 1} rather than {0, 1}. The Ising model is highly significant and has numerous applications in mathematics and physics [16]. Modern annealing technologies, including digital and quantum annealers, accept Ising (QUBO) models as input, as they are designed as Ising machines to solve problems formulated as Ising models [17].

Since the scaffold is predetermined, it is possible to subtract its weight from the total molecular weight and match the resulting weight with the weight of the combination of sidechains, which we denote as Wt. Hydrogen weight removal

It is important to carefully consider the weight of the hydrogen atoms at the substituted positions to calculate the correct molecular weight after subtracting the scaffold weight. In particular, it is necessary to subtract the number of hydrogen atoms at each substituted position from the scaffold weight. For instance, suppose we have a scaffold with weight w and two substituted positions, where the first position has one hydrogen atom attached. Still, the second position has two hydrogen atoms attached. To determine the scaffold weight that should be subtracted from the target weight, we need to subtract the total weight of the hydrogen atoms at the substituted positions, which is (1 + 2) times the weight of a single hydrogen atom, from the scaffold weight: (w - (1 + 2) * (weight of a hydrogen atom)).

Experimental settings

Fujitsu’s third-generation chip [1], which has been recently introduced, is used in this study. DA3 was used to examine the success rate to find the correct structures, and DA3C was used to perform the runtime analysis. It lets users separate the cost and penalty terms and directly specify one-hot constraints. Penalty_auto_mode was used throughout the study. In this mode, the priority of the penalty function will gradually increase in the annealing process. Therefore, DA3 and DA3C promise to give final solutions without violating the constraints formulated in the penalty term.

Possible weights of a natural product of O. japonicus and the experimentally determined structures.

One-hot-one-way groups are set for all the variables representing the sidechains attached to the same atom. We set both A and B to 1000 for the penalty term coefficients. This decision was made after finding that setting A and B to 1 resulted in penalty energies that were difficult to interpret as reasonable numbers. Some penalty energies obtained were as low as −1 or − 2, which was unexpected in our experimental settings. Through multiple trials, we discovered that the penalty energies DA reported were all integers. The penalty energy returned by DA became a common logarithm of the original value when the penalty energy was smaller than one but greater than zero when the total weight was approaching the peak without violating the one-position-one-sidechain rule. This hindered our ability to explore the problem space effectively. In contrast, setting A and B to 1000 resulted in penalty energies equivalent to 1000 times the originally expected energy. This approach was accurate and reliable, enabling us to proceed confidently.

To eliminate erroneous solutions, we established a threshold of 100 in the penalty energy value to determine the acceptance of a solution. Ideally, the appropriate solution should possess a weight that precisely matches the target weight. However, due to our reliance on empirical atomic weight calculations, the values obtained were not precise enough to eliminate the penalty energy. Consequently, a positive acceptance value must be specified to validate the solutions.

Slow linear growth of runtime as the search space increases

As we anticipate that digital annealing may offer an alternative solution, assessing the runtime needed for the DA to produce a valid solution is crucial. As the QUBO formulation permits each variable to have two potential values, the search space increases exponentially relative to the number of variables. Hence, the runtime is expected to rise as the number of variables being considered increases. To investigate the relationship between runtime and the number of variables, we conducted experiments on 152 selected configurations across the three herb species, ensuring that the target structures were within their search space. DA experiments were conducted three times for each configuration to obtain the average runtime.

We defined valid solutions as those that select sidechains whose combined molecular weight, when added to the scaffold’s molecular weight, differed from the peak by less than one AMU. The results of the runtime analysis are depicted in Fig. 5. In the lower-left corner of Fig. 5, four configurations with a maximum of 11 variables demonstrate that the DA found a valid solution in less than 1 s. Without these extreme cases, Fig. 5 shows slow linear growth in runtime as the number of variables increases (R2 = 0.56). If extreme cases are included, the runtime grows logarithmic with a higher R2 value (0.64). A gradual increase in runtime is observed over all the data points. This discrepancy between the runtime between data points with similar numbers of variables may result from the probabilistic nature of DA’s search method.

Digital annealer identified all the target structures

DA successfully identified all the correct structures for O. japonicus, and it found two additional valid structures that did not violate the constraints of the QUBO formulation. For the P. multiflorum and the C. chinensis datasets, the DA identified 57% and 80% of the validating structures, respectively.

However, when we inspected the four cases in which DA failed to identify the correct structures, we found that the difference between the molecular weights of the validating compounds and the peaks was more than 1.8 AMUs (see Supplementary Data). Related classical algorithms other than brute force (BF) also have trouble identifying these structures [9]. The DA identified all the validating structures for P. multiflorum and C. chinensis when the difference between the peaks and the molecular weights was less than 1.8 AMU. For those validating structures whose molecular weights were close to the peaks, the DA achieved a 100% success rate for all the structures in the datasets.

Solutions can be ranked using the third-generation digital annealer

Since the solutions that DA produces are sorted by the probability of occurring within a configuration, manually ranking all the identified solutions becomes necessary. All solutions generated using different configurations must be considered for each weight peak observed in the MS data to enrich the diversity of solutions. Since the third-generation DA provides separate objective and penalty function energy in the returned data, we can rank the solutions based on penalty energy to match the weight value closer. We subsequently sorted the objective energy within the same penalty energy level to determine the final ranking. Therefore, developing a protocol that can rank the returned solutions more effectively becomes essential if the number of solutions increases.

Advantage over mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry-based database search methods

MS/MS-based database search methods are highly effective for identifying known compounds in curated spectral libraries. However, their applicability is limited when analyzing novel compounds absent from such databases. In contrast, our method employs a combinatorial approach to systematically generate and evaluate plausible sidechain substitutions on a given molecular scaffold. This allows our approach to predict novel structures not present in MS/MS databases or conventional natural product libraries, thereby significantly expanding the search space and enhancing the likelihood of identifying previously uncharacterized natural compounds.

Limitations of the digital annealer and the chemical constituents–core combinatorial problem algorithm

Despite its benefits, the digital annealing process has some limitations. In certain cases, the correct structure may possess a lower objective energy but a higher penalty energy (Table 3) if the difference between the peaks and the molecular weights was larger than 1.7 AMUs, resulting in a lower ranking than other incorrect valid structures. The DA is most suitable for tasks where a valid structure is deemed a correct solution. Nevertheless, DA can rapidly provide a reasonable list of possible structures with high probability and facilitate structure elucidations in subsequent experiments.

All solutions generated using different configurations must be considered for each weight peak observed in the MS data to increase the diversity of solutions. When aiming to identify valid structures, the digital annealing framework is a straightforward and user-friendly tool for solving the problem.

The CSCCP algorithm can successfully identify the natural structures with commonly seen sidechains given target peaks and possible scaffolds. However, if the scaffold is unknown, the search space will encompass all the scaffolds in the natural product dataset and become time-consuming. Novel compounds with unseen sidechains and scaffolds are also impossible to be identified.
