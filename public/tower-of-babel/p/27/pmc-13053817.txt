Defect Size-Based Comparative Analysis of Treatment Modalities for Esophagojejunal Anastomotic Leakage Following Gastrectomy
Authors: Ba Ool Seong, Ji Yong Ahn, Juno Yoo, Chang Seok Ko, Sa-Hong Min, Chung Sik Gong, Beom Su Kim, Moon-Won Yoo, Jeong Hwan Yook, Hee Jin Choi, In-Seob Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeEsophagojejunal anastomotic leakage (EJAL) represents a severe postoperative complication following total or proximal gastrectomy. Treatment strategies include conservative management, endoscopic interventions, and surgery; however, comparative data remain limited. This study aimed to compare clinical outcomes of different strategies to identify the optimal approach based on anastomotic defect size.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed 100 patients diagnosed with EJAL between January 2015 and October 2024. Patients were categorized into four groups: conservative management, endoscopic vacuum-assisted closure (E-VAC), other endoscopic treatments, and surgery. The primary outcomes were leakage duration and length of hospital stay after EJAL diagnosis, whereas the secondary outcome was time to C-reactive protein normalization. Subgroup analyses were performed according to defect size.ResultsAmong the 100 patients, 76 were male and 24 were female, with a mean age of 65.7 years. Conservative treatment was the most common modality (53%), followed by other endoscopic treatments (19%), E-VAC (14%), and surgery (14%). In patients with a defect size <1 cm, conservative treatment was associated with significantly shorter leakage duration (P=0.035) and earlier resumption of diet (P=0.029) compared with endoscopic treatment. Among those with defects ≥2 cm, E-VAC demonstrated the most favorable median outcomes across all variables; however, statistical significance was not achieved because of the small sample size.ConclusionsConservative treatment appears to be the most effective treatment strategy for EJAL with anastomotic defects <1 cm. For larger defects (≥2 cm), E-VAC may offer clinical benefit, although further studies are needed to confirm its efficacy. These findings highlight the importance of individualized treatment selection based on defect size.

INTRODUCTION

Esophagojejunal anastomotic leakage (EJAL) is a serious complication of total or proximal gastrectomy for gastric cancer, with an incidence of 1.9%–14.6% [123456]. EJAL is associated with a higher risk of respiratory complications, intra-abdominal infections, and abscess formation, all of which contribute substantially to postoperative morbidity [345678]. These complications often necessitate prolonged antibiotic therapy and additional interventions, resulting in an extended hospital stay [89]. Furthermore, EJAL delays the initiation of oral intake and adjuvant chemotherapy, potentially increasing postoperative mortality, particularly in patients with uncontrolled infection or advanced-stage disease [1011]. Therefore, early diagnosis and appropriate management of EJAL are crucial for improving patient outcomes following gastric cancer surgery.

Various treatment strategies have been proposed for EJAL. Conservative management—including fasting, intravenous administration of antibiotics, and parenteral nutrition—may result in spontaneous resolution of the leakage [121314]. In addition, percutaneous catheter drainage (PCD) may be employed to control fluid collections or abscesses adjacent to the leakage site in selected cases [1516]. Endoscopic interventions, such as endoscopic vacuum-assisted closure (E-VAC), stent placement, clip closure, and glue application, have gained popularity in recent years because they allow direct visualization and minimally invasive treatment [171819202122232425]. In particular, E-VAC has emerged as a preferred modality in many centers [22232425]. Surgical intervention is typically reserved for patients with hemodynamic instability or refractory leakage [13].

Although several studies have reported the success rates of individual treatment modalities through single-arm analyses or comparative studies between 2 specific methods, comprehensive analyses that integrate and directly compare all major treatment options remain scarce. Moreover, studies limited to gastric cancer often have small sample sizes and include heterogeneous anastomotic sites, thereby reducing the generalizability of their findings. Consequently, robust evidence to guide the optimal choice of treatment for EJAL in gastric cancer remains insufficient.

Therefore, this study aims to compare the clinical outcomes of various treatment modalities for EJAL following gastrectomy and to determine the most effective treatment strategy based on defect size.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea (IRB No. 2024-0925). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study design.

Data collection

This retrospective observational study reviewed the medical records of 100 patients diagnosed with EJAL following total or proximal gastrectomy for gastric cancer or gastric-origin gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) at Asan Medical Center between January 2015 and October 2024.

Data were collected on the following variables: age at surgery, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status score, body mass index, tumor type, type of gastrectomy (total vs. proximal), surgical approach (open vs. laparoscopic), extent of lymph node dissection, duration of surgery, tumor location, histology, Lauren classification, pathologic morphology, number of metastatic and harvested lymph nodes, tumor stage according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer [26], and postoperative day of EJAL diagnosis.

EJAL was diagnosed based on the presence of contrast leakage on fluoroscopic upper gastrointestinal (UGI) series using Gastrografin. Patients with suspected leakage on computed tomography without confirmatory finding on UGI series (n=68) were excluded from the study.

In most open surgeries, esophagojejunal anastomosis was performed using a circular stapler, whereas a linear stapler was consistently used in all laparoscopic procedures. Overall, 93 patients underwent stapled anastomosis (65 with linear staplers and 28 with circular staplers), 4 patients underwent linear stapling followed by manual closure of the entry site, and 3 patients underwent a fully hand-sewn anastomosis. For reinforcement, additional sutures were routinely applied in cases using circular staplers and were selectively performed in cases using linear staplers.

The anastomotic defect size was categorized as ≤1, 1–2, or ≥2 cm. In patients who underwent endoscopic evaluation (n=43), the defect size was directly measured by the endoscopist. For patients without endoscopic confirmation (n=57), the size was estimated by comparing fluoroscopic findings with those of cases with known endoscopic measurements (Fig. 1).

The duration of leakage was defined as the period from the diagnosis of EJAL to confirmed resolution, which was defined as the absence of leakage on follow-up UGI series. In cases in which patients were discharged before radiologic confirmation of complete resolution, the date of discharge was used as a proxy for the date of leak resolution. Overall, 28% of patients did not undergo confirmatory testing to verify complete resolution of the leakage (Group 1: 15/53 [28.3%]; Group 2: 4/14 [28.6%]; Group 3: 7/19 [36.8%]; Group 4: 2/14 [14.3%]).

Patients were categorized into 4 groups based on the primary treatment modality following EJAL diagnosis: conservative treatment, E-VAC, other endoscopic treatments, and surgery. The conservative treatment group included patients managed with fasting, total parenteral nutrition, antibiotic therapy, and PCD if required. The other endoscopic treatment group included patients who underwent endoscopic stent placement, clip closure, or glue application, excluding E-VAC. The surgical intervention group comprised patients who underwent reoperation, such as primary repair, colon interposition, or surgical drainage. For the nine patients who received multiple therapies (9%), classification was based on the first modality implemented. Among these patients, four underwent stent placement following reoperation, and three underwent E-VAC after reoperation. Additionally, one patient received E-VAC after treatment failure with stent placement, and another received E-VAC after unsuccessful conservative management.

The primary endpoints were the duration of leakage and length of hospital stay after EJAL diagnosis. The secondary endpoint was time to normalization of C-reactive protein (CRP) levels.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Treatment outcomes were analyzed using linear regression models after log transformation of the outcome variables to approximate a normal distribution. Statistical adjustments were made to account for defect size and its potential influence on treatment selection.

RESULTS

Clinicopathologic characteristics and surgical information of patients

Among the 100 patients treated for EJAL, 76 were male and 24 were female. The mean age at the time of surgery was 65.7 years. Ninety-eight patients were diagnosed with gastric cancer, whereas two were diagnosed with gastric GISTs. Total gastrectomy was performed in 95 patients, and proximal gastrectomy with double-tract reconstruction was performed in 5 patients. Laparoscopic gastrectomy was performed more frequently than open surgery (67% vs. 33%). The most common tumor location was the high body of the stomach (47%), and involvement of the gastroesophageal junction was observed in 17% of cases. According to pathologic staging, gastric cancer stages were distributed as follows: stage I, 51%; stage II, 26.5%; stage III, 21.5%; and stage IV, 1% (Table 1).

Treatment distribution and outcomes

Conservative treatment was the primary modality in 53 patients (53%), followed by E-VAC in 14 patients (14%), other endoscopic treatments in 19 patients (19%), and reoperation in 14 patients (14%). Among the 19 patients who received other endoscopic treatments, 10 underwent clipping, 5 received stent placement, and 4 underwent glue injection. In the conservative treatment group, 10 patients additionally required PCD, whereas no patients in the other three groups received PCD. Instead, drain tubes placed during the initial gastrectomy were maintained in 23 patients in the conservative group, 9 in the E-VAC group, 8 in the other endoscopic treatment group, and all patients in the reoperation group. The highest proportion of intensive care unit admissions was observed in the surgical intervention group (64.3%), and one patient in this group died because of recurrent pneumonia during treatment. Escalation of antibiotic therapy occurred more frequently in the surgery and other endoscopic treatment groups than in the E-VAC and conservative treatment groups (Table 2).

The distribution of anastomotic defect sizes varied across treatment groups. A defect size <1 cm was observed in more than 50% of patients in the conservative and other endoscopic treatment groups, whereas a defect size >1 cm was present in nearly 90% of patients in the E-VAC and reoperation groups. Given this discrepancy, statistical adjustments were performed to account for defect size and its potential impact on treatment selection and clinical outcomes.

Among the 100 patients included in the analysis, the conservative treatment group demonstrated favorable outcomes in all primary and secondary endpoints, including duration of leakage, length of hospital stay following EJAL diagnosis, time to resumption of a soft fluid diet (SFD), and time to CRP normalization. Although overall P-values were statistically significant across all variables, pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences only between the conservative treatment and surgical intervention group.

Comparison of treatment outcomes in patients with a defect size of <1 cm

To minimize bias related to defect size and treatment selection, subgroup analyses were performed for patients with anastomotic defect sizes of <1 cm, between 1 and 2 cm, and ≥2 cm. Among patients with a defect size of <1 cm, 30 received conservative treatment, 2 were treated with E-VAC, 11 received other endoscopic treatments, and 1 underwent reoperation. To enhance statistical power, the E-VAC and other endoscopic treatment groups were combined into a single endoscopic treatment group, and the reoperation group was excluded from the analysis. The conservative treatment group showed more favorable outcomes across all measured variables, with statistically significant differences in leakage duration (P=0.035) and time to resumption of an SFD (P=0.029) (Table 3). Length of hospital stay and time to CRP normalization were also shorter in the conservative treatment group; however, the differences did not reach statistical significance.

Comparison of treatment outcomes in patients with a defect size of ≥2 cm

Among patients with a defect size ≥2 cm, 3 received conservative treatment, 6 underwent E-VAC, 4 received other endoscopic treatments, and 7 underwent surgery. Because of the small sample size, statistical significance could not be determined; therefore, only the median values of the outcome variables were analyzed. The E-VAC group demonstrated the most favorable outcome in terms of hospital stay. When the reoperation group was excluded, the E-VAC group also demonstrated better outcomes in leakage duration and time to SFD resumption compared with the conservative and other endoscopic treatment groups. Compared with patients who underwent surgery, the E-VAC group demonstrated a shorter hospital stay despite a longer duration of leakage (Table 6).

Comparison of body weight changes following EJAL treatment across modalities

To assess the nutritional impact of EJAL and potential differences in outcomes according to treatment modality, changes in body weight before gastrectomy and after completion of EJAL treatment were analyzed. The mean body weight loss was 5.2 kg in the conservative treatment group, 3.5 kg in the E-VAC group, 5.2 kg in the other endoscopic treatment group, and 10.3 kg in the reoperation group. All groups showed significant weight loss following treatment (P≤0.001), and the magnitude of weight change also differed significantly among the groups (P=0.003). In pairwise comparisons, significant differences were observed between the reoperation group and each of the other groups: conservative (P=0.012), E-VAC (P=0.002), and other endoscopic treatment (P=0.015). No significant differences were observed among the remaining treatment groups (Table 7).

DISCUSSION

The present study compared the treatment outcomes across multiple therapeutic modalities for EJAL following gastrectomy and demonstrated that the choice of treatment modality significantly influenced clinical outcomes, including duration of leakage, length of hospital stay after EJAL diagnosis, time to resumption of a SFD, and time to CRP normalization. Notably, among patients with a defect size of <1 cm, conservative treatment emerged as the most effective strategy, showing significantly better outcomes in terms of leakage duration and time to resumption of oral intake. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that small anastomotic defects often heal spontaneously with supportive management [13827].

Conversely, conservative treatment is generally selected for patients with relatively small defects, less severe intra-abdominal infection, and better overall clinical condition. As defect size increases, more invasive treatment strategies are often required, which may influence patient outcomes. Similarly, the present study may have been subject to selection bias, as patients with smaller and more stable leaks were more likely to be managed conservatively. Therefore, a subgroup analysis was conducted for patients with a defect size ≥1 cm. Among these patients, the conservative treatment group exhibited significantly better clinical outcomes than the reoperation group. However, no statistically significant differences were observed among the conservative, E-VAC, and other endoscopic treatment groups when the reoperation group was excluded. Consequently, definitive conclusions regarding the optimal treatment for defects ≥1 cm could not be established. To further explore treatment efficacy in patients with larger defects, an additional analysis was performed for those with a defect size ≥2 cm. Although statistical significance could not be achieved because of the small sample size, median outcome values indicated that the E-VAC group achieved the most favorable results. While the limited number of patients precludes firm conclusions, these findings suggest that E-VAC may offer clinical benefits for large anastomotic defects (≥2 cm).

E-VAC promotes healing by applying continuous negative pressure to the leakage site, which facilitates drainage, reduces local contamination, and maintains a clean environment [23]. This technique has been widely adopted in colorectal and esophageal surgeries because of its ability to improve perfusion and accelerate wound healing [2829303132]. Similarly, several studies have reported E-VAC as a feasible and effective treatment option for EJAL following gastrectomy. Nevertheless, compared with conservative management, E-VAC is more invasive, requiring continuous placement of a vacuum-assisted drain tube through the nasal passage. This approach may cause patient discomfort and necessitate frequent endoscopic assessments, thereby increasing medical costs. Considering these factors, the finding that conservative treatment outperformed other modalities for defects <1 cm has important clinical implications.

Treatment selection in this study was primarily guided by the patient’s systemic condition. Hemodynamically unstable patients were prioritized for reoperation, whereas stable patients underwent conservative management, E-VAC, or other endoscopic interventions depending on the operator’s preference. Since 2022, E-VAC has increasingly become the preferred first-line endoscopic option at our institution, whereas clipping has been reserved for clearly small defects. These institutional practices may have influenced the distribution of treatment modalities. To minimize potential bias arising from these factors, subgroup analyses were performed according to defect size, on which the present results are based.

Our findings provide valuable insights into the optimal management of EJAL. Conservative treatment should be considered the first-line option for small defects (<1 cm), whereas E-VAC may be beneficial for patients with larger defects ≥2 cm. Although statistical significance was not observed in this subgroup because of the limited sample size, the potential advantage of E-VAC warrants further investigation in larger, multicenter studies. These results highlight the need for individualized treatment strategies based on defect size and patient condition.

An issue requiring clarification is that the duration of EJAL was longer than the time to resumption of a SFD. The other endoscopic treatment group included patients who underwent stent placement, and in this subgroup, diet was resumed despite the presence of residual leakage because of the sealing effect of the stent. In the conservative and E-VAC groups, some patients did not undergo confirmatory testing to verify complete leak closure, and the date of discharge was used as a surrogate endpoint for leak resolution, potentially underestimating the true duration of EJAL.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, particularly in treatment allocation. Second, the relatively small sample size—especially in the subgroup analyses—limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, measurement error may have occurred. In patients without endoscopic evaluation, defect size was estimated fluoroscopically by comparing the extent of contrast leakage with that of cases with endoscopically measured defects. Although this indirect method was practical, it may have introduced inaccuracies that could have affected the subgroup results. Fourth, long-term outcomes such as anastomotic stricture formation, nutritional status, and delays in adjuvant chemotherapy were not evaluated. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods are necessary to validate these results and establish more definitive treatment guidelines.

In conclusion, conservative treatment should be considered the primary approach for EJAL with anastomotic defects <1 cm. For larger defects (≥2 cm), E-VAC demonstrated favorable outcomes in exploratory analyses and may represent a potential therapeutic option; however, further well-designed studies are needed to confirm its efficacy.
