Investigation of the Relationship Between Brain‐Derived Neurotrophic Factor and Some Biochemical Parameters in Dairy Cows During the Transition Period
Authors: Mustafa İssi, Cennet Nur Ünal, Sümeyye Babacan, Meltem Cankara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe transition period covers a period of ±21 days based on the calving day. This period is characterised by marked changes in the endocrine status of the animal, increased nutrient demand of the developing foetus and decreased feed intake. In this study, it was aimed to evaluate brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) concentrations in dairy cattle during the transition period in the prenatal and postpartum periods and to evaluate the relationship with biochemical parameters. Blood was collected from healthy cows at 254 ± 3 days prepartum and 21 ± 3 days postpartum. Metabolic profile tests aspartatamino transferase (AST), alaninamino transferase (ALT), gamma‐glutamine transferase (GGT), total protein, cholesterol, urea, creatinine, calcium and phosphorus (P) parameters—were measured calorimetrically in biochemical analyser. Beta hydroxy butyric acid (BHBA) and glucose were measured with a handheld device. Serum BDNF was analysed with a bovine‐specific ELISA kit. In conclusion, serum BDNF measurement showed a similar increase among metabolic profile parameters, but no statistical correlation was detected, and it was concluded that it would be useful to evaluate negative energy balance in a larger population and with daily measurements.

Introduction

The transition period covers a period of ±21 days based on the calving day. The animal's endocrine status significantly changes during this time, the growing foetus's nutritional needs increase and feed intake decreases (Ünal et al. 2024). While cows’ consumption of dry matter drops during the transition phase, their needs for energy and calcium for milk production increase. In this condition, tissues consume more oxygen through normal cellular respiration during times of increased metabolic demand to provide the energy required for the onset of lactation, resulting in negative energy balance (NEB) (Caixeta and Omontese 2021). After calving, many cows enter an NEB period when the energy demand for milk synthesis is not met by feed intake. In order to meet the increased energy demands, the body tries to obtain reserves, especially from adipose tissue (Abuelo et al. 2019). The metabolic and immunological changes that occur during this period, such as immunosuppression, NEB, systemic inflammatory response, oxidative stress, etc., lead dairy cows to various challenges (physiological, nutritional, psychological, management) that require rapid and effective adaptive responses within a few weeks (Trevisi and Minuti 2018). Correct transitional practices, such as appropriate nutrition, housing, calving management against the adaptive changes that occur both at the beginning of lactation and in the following days, can provide lactating dairy cattle with a healthy and productive period, while inadequate transitional practices can lead to numerous problems such as clinical disease and reduced milk production (Mezzetti et al. 2021).

Brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is a member of the neurotrophin growth factor family. BDNF is synthesised in several areas of the hypothalamus, including the paraventricular nucleus, ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus, dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus and lateral hypothalamic area (Cavaleri et al. 2023). BDNF is expressed mainly in the central nervous system but also highly abundantly in muscle, liver and adipose tissue (Arévalo and Deogracias 2023). In addition to its central nervous system effects, BDNF is also known for its peripheral functions affecting glucose metabolism, energy expenditure and food intake. In recent years, BDNF has attracted attention due to its role in energy homoeostasis (Noble et al. 2011). The local presence of BDNF in the subcutaneous adipose tissue of lactating cows has been demonstrated and it has been suggested that it may be associated with lipogenesis and lipolysis occurring during lactation in these cows (Colitti et al. 2015). Krabbe et al. (2007) found that when hyperglycaemic clamp was applied to diabetic patients, BDNF concentrations decreased significantly and played a role in glucose homoeostasis. Genzer et al. (2017) showed that BDNF binding to hepatocytes is involved in the activation of catabolic pathways such as fatty acid oxidation and consequently in the increase of glycogen stores while gluconeogenesis is inhibited. In addition, BDNF has been reported to increase insulin sensitivity and parasympathetic tone by acting in the brain and periphery (Marosi and Mattson 2014).

Biochemical analyses are performed in dairy farms to evaluate metabolic and nutritional disorders and to monitor the condition of animals. In addition, biochemical analyses are used as a reliable test for the optimisation of herd management at relatively low cost, to enable future attempts to achieve maximum yield, and for the early detection of nutritional deficiencies or metabolic diseases (Radostits et al. 2007; Găvan et al. 2010).

BDNF is a parameter secreted from various tissues and plays an important role in different mechanisms. It is known to play important roles in glucose metabolism and energy balance. It is known that the transition period puts a strain on cattle in terms of energy and lipid metabolism. However, there are no studies evaluating BDNF concentrations in cattle during the transition period. The aim of this study was to determine BDNF concentrations in transition dairy cows during the prenatal and postnatal periods and to evaluate their relationship with biochemical parameters.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval

The study was initiated after the permission of Fırat University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee Regulation (F. Ü. HADYEK Session Number: 2024/04‐03).

Animal Selection

The study was conducted in a commercial dairy farm in Elazığ province. A total of 10 Simmental dairy cows in the last 3 weeks of pregnancy (254 ± 3 days) according to the records of artificial (DeLaval herringbone parlor HB50, DeLaval International AB, Tumba, Sweden) insemination were used in the study. The animals were housed in half‐open stall paddocks. The cows were fed anionic in the prepartum period and cationic in the postpartum period with total mixed rations containing chaff, soybean meal, corn flakes, corn silage, bypass oil, alfalfa grass, sugarcane, cottonseed meal, vitamins and minerals in various concentrations according to the prepartum and postpartum energy requirement of the animal with ad libitum access to water. Cows had an average age of 3.30 ± 1.15 years, lactation number of 2.10 ± 0.99 and body condition score of 3.10 ± 0.21.

Information about the animals was obtained from the automation system of the facility and BMI was determined using a scale between 0 and 5 developed by Edmonson et al. (1989). Animals with diseases such as metabolic acidosis, difficult parturition, retensio secundinarum, mastitis and metritis were excluded from the study. New cows with similar characteristics from the same farm were added to replace the excluded animals until the number was completed.

Collection of Blood Samples

Cattle included in the study were followed up in the prepartum and 3 weeks postpartum period. Blood samples were collected from healthy cows (Gül 2022) at 254 ± 3 days prepartum and 21 ± 3 days postpartum.

For aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), total protein (TP), cholesterol, urea, creatinine, calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P) and BDNF analyses, 10 mL blood samples were collected in yellow‐capped gel tubes (BD Vacutainer, Plymouth, UK) using a standard jugular venepuncture technique with appropriate restraint. After clotting, blood samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and the sera were obtained and stored at −20°C until analysis.

For β‐hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA) and glucose analyses, one drop of blood was taken from the jugular vein according to the technique and was evaluated during sampling.

Metabolic Profile Tests and BDNF Analysis

After thawing the sera at room temperature, AST, ALT, GGT, TP, cholesterol, urea, creatinine, Ca and P parameters were measured calorimetrically in a biochemical analyser (Mindray‐BS400, China).

Serum BDNF was analysed with a bovine‐specific ELISA kit and reader (BioTek Elx800, BioTek Instruments, Highland Park, Winooski, VT, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

BHBA and glucose analyses were performed on the days when blood samples were taken in the prepartum and postpartum periods. The handheld device was calibrated by attaching a different calibrator kit for each parameter. Then, one drop of blood was taken from the vena jugularis and glucose concentrations were analysed with the Free Style Optium Neo H device (Abbott Diabetes Care Ltd, Witney, UK) according to the technique. Measurements were performed with disposable β‐ketone (FreeStyle Optimum β‐Ketone, Abbott Diabetes Care Ltd.) and glucose (FreeStyle Optimum Glucose, Abbott Diabetes Care Ltd.) test strips. Within the scope of this study, relevant measurements were performed once.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS 26 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). The Shapiro Wilk test was used to evaluate whether the data were normally distributed. Data before and after birth were compared with the dependent samples t‐test. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and minimum/maximum values. The data obtained from the study were analysed by the Spearman correlation test. Statistical significance level was accepted as p value < 0.05.

Discussion

The aim is to keep the cows healthy for high milk yield. Their nutrition and metabolism are extremely important for the health status of the herd. The transition period is considered to be the period when metabolic and nutritional problems are most common in cows. Metabolic profile analysis is the analysis of blood biochemical parameters used to evaluate and prevent metabolic and nutritional disorders in dairy cattle (Puppel and Kuczyńska 2016). The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between metabolic profile analyses and BDNF concentrations in dairy cows in the transition period of 254 ± 3 days prepartum and 21 ± 3 days postpartum.

It has been reported that blood BHBA and glucose concentrations are used in the evaluation of NEB, which is common in the transition period (Suthar et al. 2013; Trevisi and Minuti 2018). Acetoacetic acid and BHBA and their decarboxylation products, isopropanol and acetone, form ketone bodies and it has been reported that BHBA measurements are preferred because they are more stable in blood compared to others (Radostits et al. 2007; Sun et al. 2015). In this study, BHBA levels were determined as 0.42 ± 0.03 mmol/L and 0.53 ± 0.05 mmol/L in the prepartum and postpartum periods, respectively, for the evaluation of NEB. According to the results obtained, although BHBA mmol/L levels increased in the postpartum period, there was no statistically significant difference compared to the prepartum period (p = 0.093) and < 1.2 mmol/L was observed in the reference range reported for healthy cattle (Garro et al. 2014; McArt et al. 2015).

It has been reported that high BHBA mmol/L concentrations after parturition predispose dairy cows to transitional diseases such as mastitis, metritis and retensio secundinarum (Hammon et al. 2006). Neutrophil dysfunction and immunosuppression as a result of decreased chemotactic response of polymorphonuclear leukocytes and total leukocytes with increasing BHBA mmol/L concentrations are thought to be the mechanism leading to this predisposition (Kremer et al. 1993). It is known that hyperketonaemia is associated with NEB (Issi et al. 2016; Uztimür et al. 2023) and can be minimised with correct nutritional management (Baird 1982). Although it is reported that hyperketonaemia is common in the first month after birth, studies (Geishauser et al. 2001; Duffield 2003) have found that it is more common in the first 2 weeks, although the cause is uncertain. For this reason, the fact that no statistical difference was observed in BHBA mmol/L concentrations in the study suggests that the sampling time was selected relatively close to the end of the transition period. In addition, the fact that the animals included in the study were from a facility where the feeding program was applied according to the lactation period may be an indication that NEB did not occur. In addition, the fact that BHBA mmol/L concentrations did not increase in the postpartum period and therefore ketosis was not observed can be considered as a result of the fact that NEB did not occur in the study animals and the correct feeding strategies were applied.

In ruminants, glucose is known to be an important energy source for milk production during the transition from the dry period to lactation and for the foetus during pregnancy (Radostits et al. 2007; Uztimür and Ünal 2024). On the day of calving, there is a physiological increase in blood glucose as a result of the increased concentration of glucagon, catecholamines and glucocorticoids and then it is used to provide energy requirements in the body. Glucose requirement is tried to be satisfied by gluconeogenesis for the milk lactose required during the lactation period and mobilisation of body fat reserves to meet the energy requirement during this period (Radostits et al. 2007; Fiore et al. 2015). As a result of the demand for glucose, decreases in blood glucose levels can be observed with a decrease in the amount of glucose that other tissues in the body can use after birth (Bicalho et al. 2017). In the present study, glucose concentrations were determined as 47.60 ± 1.50 and 52.20–2.29 mg/dL in the prepartum and postpartum periods, respectively, although there was no statistically significant difference (p = 0.088). These values were within the reference range reported for cows (47.7–71.5 mg/dL) (Lager and Jordan 2012). The increase in postpartum glucose concentration was similar to that found by some researchers (Vazquez‐Anon et al. 1994; Seifi et al. 2007).

It is stated that the increase in glucose concentrations in the postpartum period may be due to the increase in feed intake and improvement in energy status as lactation progresses (Seifi et al. 2007). In this study, a positive correlation was found between prepartum glucose and cholesterol (r = 0.647) and prepartum cholesterol and postpartum glucose (r = 0.632) concentrations. This correlation is similar to the results found by Djoković et al. (2015) in early lactation and late lactation cows. It is suggested that these results may be due to the possible effects of the early lactation period on liver metabolism. In studies conducted on cattle in the transition period, Seifi et al. (2007) analysed different cholesterol measurements such as 60.12–57.6 mg/dL, Avcı and Kızıl (2013) analysed 107.1–73.7 mg/dL and Singh et al. (2020) analysed 65–114–63–253 mg/dL. In the presented study, cholesterol concentrations measured in the prepartum and postpartum periods (105.60 ± 9.22 and 104.30 ± 9.05 mg/dL, respectively) were within the reference range (Lager and Jordan 2012; Cozzi et al. 2011), but no statistically significant difference was detected (p = 0.920).

It has been reported that AST, ALT and GGT activities in dairy cows during lactation and dry period show significant differences according to lactation stage (Stojević et al. 2005). They reported that AST, ALT and GGT levels used as liver function tests can be evaluated. Among these researchers, Issi et al. (2016) found AST 66.20 U/L, Elshahawy and Abdullaziz (2017) found prepartum and postpartum ALT 24.66–26.27 U/L and AST 65.93–49.53 U/L, respectively. As can be seen in Table 1, there were no differences in AST, ALT and GGT concentrations in the prepartum and postpartum periods, similar to those reported by Elshahawy and Abdullaziz (2017) and those analysed in this study, but the measured values were found to be within the reference range (Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine n.d.). The AST and ALT concentrations determined in this study were within the reference range, indicating that the cows had healthy, non‐damaged livers. In addition, in the present study, a positive correlation was observed between prepartum ALT and AST (r = 0.639) in accordance with the reports of Đoković et al. (2014) and Puppel and Kuczyńska (2016), while a positive correlation was found between postpartum GGT and prepartum AST (r = 0.707) and ALT (r = 0.729).

It is known that renal function, assessed by urea and creatinine measurements, is affected by metabolism‐associated changes during the stages of lactation. In ruminants, it is stated that serum urea concentrations increase during lactation due to dietary protein intake and increased needs, while creatinine concentrations show higher levels during late pregnancy and early lactation (Piccione et al. 2012). Avcı and Kızıl (2013) found a difference between creatinine concentrations in the prepartum and postpartum periods, but urea concentrations did not change. In this study, urea concentrations in the prepartum and postpartum periods were 29.39 ± 2.53 and 32.01 ± 1.97, respectively, and although they were comparatively lower in the prepartum period, there was no statistically significant difference with the postpartum period (p = 0.237). As can be seen in Table 1, creatinine concentration was also higher in the prepartum period (1.29 ± 0.05), but there was no statistically significant difference with the results of the postpartum period (1.16 ± 0.03) (p = 0.058). Piccione et al. (2012) reported values compatible with the findings obtained in this study and stated that the reason for the differences in the mentioned measurements was that serum urea levels were affected by the protein content in the diet, while creatinine was affected by the organic waste load of the calf, especially in the late gestation period of the cow.

Calcium and P concentrations are known to be vital macrominerals for the normal functioning of animals and are reported to be important for the health and productivity of the cow in the early lactation after calving as Ca and P mechanisms are significantly affected in transition cows (Moreira et al., 2009). It is reported that the concentrations of these macrominerals are related to each other and hyperphosphatemia increases the risk of hypocalcaemia in cows (Tsiamadis et al. 2016). In the present study, there was no statistically significant difference between prepartum and postpartum Ca concentrations (8.51 ± 0.13 and 8.27 ± 0.10 mg/dL, respectively) (p = 0.135). It has been reported that Ca concentrations are lowest in the first 12–24 h after delivery, but are also affected by nutritional age, number of lactations and environmental factors (Tsiamadis et al. 2016; Goff 2014). In this study, although hypocalcaemia did not occur following birth during the observation period, it was determined that Ca concentrations measured on the 21st day after birth were within the reference range (Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine n.d.).

Phosphorus concentration in the prepartum period (9.23 ± 0.83 mg/dL) was statistically significantly (p = 0.045) higher than that in the postpartum period (7.98 ± 1.00 mg/dL). In this study, prepartum P concentrations were above the reported reference range (Fielder 2022), and it is suggested that the decrease in the postpartum period may be related not only to milk production but also to decreased feed intake at calving, increased plasma corticosteroids and more generally to intracellular and extracellular redistribution (Goselink et al. 2019).

The immunohistochemical detection of BDNF, a member of the neurotrophin family, in subcutaneous adipose tissue of lactating cattle has been demonstrated and it has been suggested that it may be related to energy and fat metabolism. In addition, it has been reported that BDNF contributes through its receptors during oocyte maturation and early embryo development in buffalo ovarian follicles (Goselink et al. 2019), plays a role in shaping animal behaviour (Zielke et al. 2011) and improves glucose and lipid metabolism, controls energy balance and feeding behaviour in humans. In this study, there was no statistically significant difference between prepartum and postpartum BDNF concentrations (0.20 ± 0.01 and 0.22 ± 0.00 pg/mL, respectively) (p = 0.524). This may be due to the fact that no harmful effects of NEB were observed in cows throughout the study, metabolic profile parameters were within the reference range, few animals were used in the study, sampling time and analysis method. Table 1 shows that although there was no statistical difference in the postpartum period measurements of BHBA and glucose as an indicator of the evaluation of NEB in the animals included in the study, a slight increase was also observed in BDNF concentration.

In the study, samples were collected at two points during the transition period. While our aim was to evaluate prenatal and postnatal periods, collecting samples throughout the transition period could be beneficial in providing more information about BDNF and metabolic profile parameters. A power analysis was not conducted before the study commenced. This is primarily because the study was conducted in a farm environment and individual animals were assessed and included. The aim of this study is to assess methodological feasibility and lay the groundwork for subsequent, more comprehensive studies. Therefore, repeating the study with larger samples and appropriate statistical planning would be beneficial in future studies.

As a result, it was determined that serum BDNF did not show variability at the measured time points similar to other metabolic parameters. Although a similar increase was observed between BDNF measurement and metabolic profile parameters, no statistical correlation was detected. As a result, it was concluded that it would be useful to evaluate NEB in a larger population and with daily measurements.
