Prenatal diagnostic value of medium-coverage WGS and clinical Exome sequencing in fetal congenital heart disease
Authors: Jing Li, Yuanhang Zhu, Yuan Tian, Ying Li, Yunxiao Zhi, Xuezhe Ouyang, Xuewen Yang, Lei Xu, Qian Zhang, Wei Victor Zhang, Ling Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital heart disease (CHD) is a leading cause of infant morbidity and mortality in China, creating a significant healthcare burden. While prenatal diagnosis via fetal echocardiography is routine, it is prone to missed diagnoses, highlighting the need to explore genetic mechanisms for improved diagnostics and management.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 412 fetuses diagnosed with CHD through echocardiography between December 2021 and March 2024. All parents received prenatal genetic counseling and provided informed consent for ultrasound-guided procedures. Genetic testing included Medium-Coverage Whole Genome Sequencing (CMA-seq) and Clinical Exome Sequencing (CES). CHD cases were stratified into simple or complex forms according to established pediatric cardiology criteria. Pathogenic variants were detected with CMA-seq and CES.ResultsGenetic abnormalities were found in 36.4% (150/412) of fetuses (P/LP + VUS), while the P/LP-only diagnostic yield was 19.4% (80/412). Detection was higher in non-isolated CHD cases (47.3%) compared to isolated cases (31.3%). Aneuploidies such as Trisomy 21 and 18 were commonly detected. Pathogenic copy-number variants (CNVs) were most frequent in right- and left-ventricular outflow tract obstructions. CES enhanced the detection of single nucleotide variants, particularly in complex CHD.ConclusionIntegrating CMA-seq and CES enhances prenatal CHD diagnosis by uncovering genetic etiologies, improving accuracy, and supporting personalized management strategies.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) refers to structural or functional abnormalities of the heart or great vessels due to underdevelopment during early embryonic development. Approximately 1.35 million children with CHD are born worldwide each year [1]. CHD has become a widely occurring congenital birth defect and is one of the major causes of noninfectious death in newborns [2]. The incidence of CHD varies in different countries or regions, with a prevalence rate of about 8.2‰ among live births in Europe, while in China the prevalence rate is slightly higher, at around 8.98‰ [3] and is increasing annually. The total economic cost of newly diagnosed CHD over a lifetime amounts to 12.6 billion yuan each year, posing a substantial economic burden on the healthcare system and families [4].

Fetal CHD is classified based on the complexity of the structural abnormalities into simple and complex types. Clinically, simple CHD is more common, while complex CHD, although accounting for less than 1/5 of cases, is a significant risk factor for stillbirth, neonatal disability, or death [5]. The primary method for diagnosing fetal CHD is fetal echocardiography. However, due to factors such as fetal position in utero and other maternal factors during pregnancy, there is still a 43%-96% possibility of missed diagnoses [6,7]. Understanding the etiological mechanisms of CHD is crucial for developing targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies and improving prognosis. Known factors include maternal age or disease, genetic factors, and environmental exposures, with genetic factors playing a significant role in the pathogenesis of fetal CHD [8–10]. Genetic factors include chromosomal aneuploidy, copy number variants (CNVs), and single-gene mutations.

Genetic testing methods for fetal CHD primarily include chromosomal karyotype analysis and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). With the increasing use of Clinical Exome Sequencing (CES) technology in prenatal diagnosis, exome sequencing (ES) is sequentially used to detect single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) in fetuses with abnormal prenatal ultrasound findings and normal results from traditional methods, expanding the scope of genetic variation research [11]. However, this stepwise procedure has a relatively long turnaround time (4–8 weeks), impacting the timely provision of genetic counseling and diagnostic results [12].

With the promotion of new technologies in the clinical field, our hospital has recently adopted medium-coverage whole-genome sequencing (CMA-seq) for CNV detection in prenatal diagnosis, replacing CMA/single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays [13]. This technology offers high throughput, high resolution, and relatively low cost. Additionally, based on family-based CES, we identify SNVs/indels, focusing on essential genes associated with known diseases in clinical practice. Many genes have been reported, such as cardiac transcription factor GATA regulating myocardial cell proliferation and differentiation [14], enzyme gene MTHFR related to folate/homocysteine (Hcy) metabolism [15], and the transcription factor Nrf2 altering cardiovascular hemodynamics [16]. To better understand the genetic landscape of fetal CHD and improve diagnostic strategies, we conducted a retrospective study of 412 fetuses with echocardiographically identified CHD using this combined genomic approach. We hypothesize that CMA-seq with CES will enhance diagnostic yield in prenatal CHD evaluation and provide a more comprehensive understanding of its genetic basis.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant recruitment

This retrospective study included 412 fetuses diagnosed with congenital heart disease (CHD) by fetal echocardiography between December 2021 and March 2024 at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2021-046-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnoses were made by two qualified physicians, meeting the International Pediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code (IPCCC) standards, and confirmed with CHD through color Doppler echocardiography and surgical diagnosis; (2) Fetal parents agreed to invasive prenatal diagnosis through prenatal genetic counseling and signed relevant informed consent forms. Exclusion criteria: (1) Pregnant women with contraindications to invasive sampling or who refused diagnostic investigations; (2) Fetal cardiac phenotypes involving only arrhythmias or pericardial effusion. Exclusion of pregnant women with coagulation dysfunction, infection at the puncture site, or history of exposure to teratogens; (3) Pregnant women with psychiatric disorders unable to complete the investigation.

All participants received prenatal genetic counseling and underwent ultrasound-guided transabdominal amniocentesis between 20 and 25 weeks of gestation. About 20 mL of amniotic fluid was collected using a 22 G needle. No procedure-related complications were observed. All samples were tested with CMA-seq. In selected cases, CES was additionally performed based on ultrasound findings or clinical indication.

Echocardiographic data and classifications

Pregnant women were examined using GE Voluson E8/E10 ultrasound systems. Following American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) and International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) ­guidelines, All fetal echocardiograms were independently interpreted by two senior fetal cardiologists (each with >8 years of dedicated fetal‑cardiac scanning experience) who were blinded to the genetic results. Diagnoses were coded according to the IPCCC and ICD‑10. Whenever the two primary readers disagreed, the images were re‑evaluated jointly until a consensus decision was reached. CHD was categorized as isolated (simple or complex) or non-isolated (with extracardiac malformations), based on the Ministry of Health of China’s guidelines, as follows: (1) Isolated CHD: include simple CHD and Complex CHD, simple CHD refers to a single intracardiac lesion that generally does not cause hemodynamic changes. Cases involving one or more types of complex CHD were classified as complex CHD. Multiple simple CHD cases were not considered complex CHD; (2) Non-isolated CHD: CHD with extracardiac malformations: Includes malformations in systems other than the circulatory system.

Whole Genome sequencing

Medium-coverage whole genome sequencing (5–8x coverage, also known as CMA-seq) was used to detect CNVs. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and sequenced on the AmCareSeq-2000 platform (AmCare Genomics Lab, Guangzhou, China) with >100 million raw reads per sample. Sequencing reads were cleaned by removing read when a base quality less than QC20. The sequencing reads were aligned to the reference human genome version hg19 using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner(BWA). CNVs were identified using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline (AmCare Genomics Lab, Guangzhou, China) based on read counts and Z-scores, with a bin size of 10 kb. The interpretation of CNVs was based on multiple sources: the Database of Genomic Variants (DGV, http://dgv.tcag.ca/dgv/app/home), DECIPHER (Database of Chromosomal Imbalance and Phenotype in Humans Using Ensembl Resources; https://decipher.sanger.ac.uk/), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM;https://www.omim.org/), peer-reviewed literatures and an internal database comprising data from 100,000 Han Chinese individuals maintained by AmCare Genomics Laboratorys. Variants detected by CMA-seq and CES were classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) and variants of uncertain significance (VOUS).

Clinical Exome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted in the same manner as in CMA-seq, followed by solution hybridization using custom-designed Amcare probes (AmCare Genomics Lab, Ltd., Guangzhou, China) to enrich target sequences. This included approximately 5000 clinically relevant pathogenic genes’ exonic regions [17]. The genes were selected based on their documentation in OMIM, the Human Gene Mutation Database(HGMD), and peer-reviewed literature. Known pathogenic variants in deep intronic and other non-coding regions of the targeted genes were also included. The enriched DNA samples were indexed and sequenced using the AmCareSeq-2000 sequencer (AmCare Genomics Lab, Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The average coverage depth was 200x, with over 98% of the target regions being covered by at least 20 reads. Sequence variants were identified using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline (AmCare Genomics Lab, Guangzhou, China), which includes AmCare’s proprietary algorithms for extracting Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) variant sites and the use of the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) HaplotypeCaller for insertions and deletions (InDels), followed by filtering with Variant Quality Score Recalibration (VQSR). Sequence variants were annotated using population and literature databases, including the Genome Aggregation Database (GnomAD; https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), OMIM, etc. Variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 1% in gnomAD East Asian were excluded. Variant pathogenicity was assessed according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). The conservation and pathogenicity of the variants were predicted using software like SIFT, Polyphen2, MutationTaster, FATHMM, among others. Sanger sequencing was applied to validate suspicious variant sites, and this was also done for the fetus and its parents.

Pregnancy counseling approach

Our hospital offers a comprehensive counseling strategy for parents facing the difficult decision of terminating a pregnancy due to fetal CHD. Our multifaceted approach includes educating parents about the specific CHD, its severity, and potential impacts on the child’s future health and development. A team of fetal cardiologists, geneticists, and ethicists provide a balanced view on medical interventions and the child’s long-term prognosis, considering quality of life. Recognizing the importance of family and societal values in China, we include considerations of the social and familial impacts of the decision. Parents are encouraged to think about the diagnosis’s effects on family life, caregiving, and psychological and financial implications. Genetic counseling is vital, particularly for suspected hereditary CHD, to understand recurrence risks and genetic testing options. Our non-directive counseling respects parents’ autonomy, supporting them without pressure, ensuring decisions align with their values and beliefs.

Statistical analysis

Proportions were compared using Pearson χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are presented as median (inter‑quartile range) and were compared with the Mann‑Whitney U‑test. A two‑sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in SPSS v26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

General cohort information

This study included 412 fetuses with CHD. The most common defect was septal defects (24.03%, 99/412), predominantly ventricular septal defects (VSD) (19.17%, 79/412). Left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) was the second most common (21.12%, 87/412), with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) (10.19%, 42/412) and coarctation of the aorta-interrupted aortic arch (CoA/IAA) (8.01%, 33/412) being notable subtypes. Right ventricular outflow tract obstruction (RVOTO) included 70 cases (16.99%), mainly hypoplastic right heart syndrome (HRHS) (11.41%, 47/412). Conotruncal defects (CTD) had 49 cases (11.89%), including tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) (4.13%, 17/412). Anomalous pulmonary venous connection (APVC) and heterotaxy (Hex) were recorded in 38 (9.22%) and 22 cases (5.34%), respectively. Isolated CHD was present in 281 cases (68.20%), while 131 cases (31.80%) had extracardiac abnormalities, as shown in Table 1.

Frequency of genetic variants in different congenital heart disease categories

Genetic abnormalities were detected in 36.4% (150/412) of fetuses. Septal defects had a 26.3% (26/99) positivity rate, with VSD at 25.3% (20/79). LVOTO showed a 31.0% (27/87) positivity rate, including HLHS at 23.8% (10/42) and CoA/IAA at 36.4% (12/33). RVOTO exhibited a 44.3% (31/70) rate, with HRHS at 42.6% (20/47). CTD had a 40.8% (20/49) positivity rate, with TOF at 41.2% (7/17) and PMVSD + CoA at 50% (7/14). APVC showed 39.5% (15/38), and Hex had the highest at 59.1% (13/22). Isolated CHD had a 31.3% (88/281) positivity rate, while non-isolated CHD had a significantly higher rate of 47.3% (62/131) (p < 0.05). Non-isolated RVOTO and APVC showed significantly higher rates of 60% (15/25) and 72.2% (13/18), respectively, compared to isolated cases (35.6% and 10%). Non-isolated CoA/IAA also had a higher rate (66.7%, 4/6) compared to isolated cases (29.6%, 8/27), as shown in Table 1.

Chromosomal abnormalities in fetuses with cardiac and extra-cardiac findings

CMA-seq identified aneuploidies in fetuses with cardiac and extra-cardiac abnormalities. Trisomy 21 was found in 11 cases, associated with septal defects (3), conotruncal defects (2), RVOTO (2), APVC (1), LVOTO (1), and other defects (2). Five of these cases also had extra-cardiac abnormalities, including renal, neurological, and lymphatic issues. Trisomy 18 was detected in 4 cases, each with cardiac and extra-cardiac abnormalities, such as renal and nervous system malformations and cystic hygroma. One case of Trisomy 14 was identified with septal defects. Sex chromosome aneuploidies included one case of chrY duplication with RVOTO and potential right renal agenesis, and one case of chrX duplication with septal defects, as shown in Table 2.

Impact of Exome sequencing on detection rates of genetic variants in CHD categories

CES notably improved variant detection across CHD subcategories. In terms of variant count, LVOTO had 16 additional SNVs identified through CES, particularly in CoA/IAA (8 variants) and HLHS (5 variants). RVOTO, especially HRHS, showed 11 additional SNVs, and septal defects had 6 new variants. When considering the number of newly diagnosed cases, CES identified 6 additional positive cases in septal defects, 10 in LVOTO, and 9 in RVOTO. Overall, CMA-seq alone yielded a detection rate of 8.50% (35/412), while CES alone increased the detection to 10.92% (45/412), as detailed in Tables 5 and 6.

Discussion

This study represents the first large-scale retrospective research in Zhengzhou, a key city in China’s Central Plains, focusing on fetuses prenatally diagnosed with congenital heart disease (CHD). The Central Plains region, a historical and cultural hub with deep-rooted family values, emphasizes maternal–child health, reproductive planning, and genetic risk reduction. Its tradition of early fetal screening, strong public health infrastructure, and increasing uptake of advanced molecular diagnostics reflect a long-standing commitment to reducing birth defects and congenital conditions [18,19]. Economic development and advanced medical resources in Zhengzhou further bolster these practices [20]. This study utilizes advanced CMA-seq combined with CES for prenatal CHD diagnosis, crucial for genetic syndrome identification, prenatal counseling, and prognosis evaluation.

The clinical relevance of CNVs is related to the functional impact of microdeletions and/or microduplications, with a higher likelihood of phenotypic effects when mutation regions occur in key genes and important regulatory areas [21]. Consequently, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) remains the most widely used first-line test for prenatal CNV detection. Wu et al. applied CMA to 158 fetuses with aortic-arch anomalies and reported a 12.7% yield for pathogenic microdeletions/duplications [22]. In our unselected cohort the corresponding yield was 8.5% (35/412)—slightly lower, likely because 68.2% of our fetuses had isolated CHD and 50.2% had simple lesions, diluting the proportion of syndromic cases. A meta-analysis of 1 131 pregnancies by Jansen et al. showed a 7% overall CMA yield, with clear stratification between isolated (3.4%) and non-isolated CHD (9.3%), mirroring our figures [23]. Similar gradients have been confirmed in North-American and European registries, where detection climbs to 12–15% when extracardiac malformations or increased nuchal translucency coexist [24–26]. Moreover, guidelines now recommend reflex low-pass whole-genome sequencing when ultrasound demonstrates complex cardiac anatomy because it can uncover sub-100 kb CNVs that arrays may miss, boosting diagnostic yields by a further 3–5% [27]. Together, these data underscore the importance of phenotype-directed choice of CNV methodology in prenatal CHD evaluation.

Among the aneuploidies, trisomy 21 was the most common, representing 61.1% (11/18). In our cohort, the predominant cardiac phenotype in trisomy 21 fetuses was septal defects (3/11, 27.3%), followed by conotruncal and right-ventricular-outflow-tract (RVOT) lesions. This distribution is consistent with patterns reported in previous large-scale prenatal and postnatal studies of congenital heart disease in trisomy 21 populations [28–30]. We also observed a disproportionately high CNV burden in RVOTO relative to other CHD subgroups. Previous outcome studies indicate that when CHD co-occurs with extracardiac malformations, gene-dosage imbalances—rather than single-gene point mutations—drive pathogenesis [31,32]. Our data support this view: non-isolated cases harbored significantly more CNVs than isolated cases. Leveraging CMA-seq as a first-line test is therefore justified; its ∼10-kb resolution exceeds the 5-Mb limit of karyotyping and outperforms many array platforms. Indeed, Shaffer et al. detected pathogenic CNVs < 10 Mb in 10.6% of neonates with prenatal cardiac anomalies [33], while we identified 15/27 clinically significant CNVs < 5 Mb and 6 < 100 kb, underscoring the added value of sequencing-based CNV detection (Supplementary Table 1). Finally, the well-known association between conotruncal defects (CTD) and 22q11.2-deletion syndrome was replicated: three fetuses carried 22q11.2 deletions, two classified as CTD, reinforcing the dosage-sensitive role of TBX1 in outflow-tract morphogenesis [26,34,35].

Exome sequencing has also been recommended in recent years as a diagnostic method for prenatal ultrasound anomalies. In this study, CES, as previously described [36], is a custom-designed panel including OMIM, HGMD, and newly peer-reviewed literature identified human pathogenic genes, covering all exonic regions and 30 bp of exon-intron boundaries, as well as known pathogenic deep intronic variants [37,38], with an average sequencing depth of 200×, capable of detecting some CNVs (results consistent with CMA-seq omitted). CES produced its highest diagnostic yields in RVOTO, LVOTO, CTD and septal‑defect groups, consistent with prospective European and US data reporting an 8–15 % incremental yield over array techniques for outflow‑tract or multiple‑lesion CHD [39]. From a developmental standpoint, the outflow tract is shaped by second heart field progenitors and neural crest cells regulated by Notch and TGF-β signaling. Mutations in pathway genes such as NOTCH1, SMAD6, JAG1, and DLL4 can disrupt valve morphogenesis and ventricular alignment. These mutations, often missense or truncating, affect protein function without altering copy number, which may explain the predominance of SNVs over CNVs in these lesions [40,41]. In heterotaxy (Hex), no pathogenic CNVs were identified, but multiple likely pathogenic variants were detected in axonemal dynein genes, including DNAH1, DNAH5, and DNAH10. These results strengthen existing evidence that ciliary dysfunction contributes to laterality defects [42–44]. Pathogenic SNVs were more common in non-isolated CHD (16.8%) than isolated cases (13.5%), highlighting the increased genetic complexity when extracardiac anomalies are present. Our findings are comparable to the study by Li et al. [45], which showed a 10% diagnostic yield from WES in fetuses with negative CMA, though they did not find a significant difference between isolated and non-isolated cases. These discrepancies may reflect differences in case selection or variant interpretation frameworks.

In conclusion, integrating CMA and CES in prenatal CHD diagnosis is crucial for identifying genetic variations. This approach enhances genetic counseling and informed decision-making for prospective parents, aligning with the cultural emphasis on family health in the Zhengzhou Central Plains region.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective nature may introduce selection bias, and despite a relatively large sample size, it may not capture the full spectrum of genetic variations associated with CHD. High-resolution methods like CMA-seq and CES might miss certain mutation types, such as deep intronic variants and epigenetic modifications. Interpreting variants of unknown significance is challenging and requires further research. Additionally, understanding the functional roles of novel variants in heart development and managing fetuses with positive ultrasound but negative genetic findings pose significant challenges. Future research should focus on larger cohorts and diverse genetic testing methods to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Our study confirms that chromosomal aneuploidies, pathogenic copy‑number variants and single‑nucleotide variants all contribute substantially to the genetic architecture of fetal congenital heart disease. Integrating CMA-seq and CES significantly enhanced the detection of genetic abnormalities, particularly in complex CHD cases. This comprehensive approach to prenatal diagnosis aligns with the cultural emphasis on family health in the Zhengzhou Central Plains region. Our data indicate that a combined CNV and CES is a valuable, evidence‑based option for prenatally identified CHD cases in which the likelihood of an underlying genetic defect is elevated by sonographic or clinical features.
