Myeloid cAMP Reduction Shifts Rhinovirus‐Induced Airway Inflammation From Neutrophilic to Eosinophilic by Suppressing M1‐Interstitial Macrophages
Authors: Seong Ok Park, Erdenebileg Uyangaa, Hyo Jin Kim, Hee Won Byeon, Jin Young Choi, Koanhoi Kim, Jihyung Lee, Samuel Bertin, Eyal Raz, Seong Kug Eo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAsthma exacerbations caused by human rhinovirus (hRV) infection are characterized by airway neutrophilia and reduced corticosteroid response, leading to significant healthcare costs and lung function impairment. The Gαs subunit of the trimeric G protein regulates immunopathological conditions by modulating cAMP levels. We aimed to investigate the impact of myeloid cAMP levels on neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation caused by hRV infection.MethodsWe generated mice with myeloid cell‐specific deletion of the Gαs subunit by targeting the LysM gene, leading to a specific reduction of cAMP in myeloid cells. Neutrophilic asthma exacerbation was induced by hRV infection during allergen challenge, and cytokine production in BALF and lung tissue was assessed, along with histological examinations.ResultsMyeloid Gαs ablation was found to shift airway inflammation from a neutrophilic to an eosinophilic phenotype during hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation. This change led to mucus hypersecretion and Th2‐type inflammation, and enhanced CD4+ Th2 effector cell expansion. In chronic asthma with repeated allergen and hRV exposure, myeloid Gαs ablation caused mixed Th2‐ and Th17‐biased inflammation with increased neutrophil and eosinophil infiltration and collagen deposition. Myeloid Gαs deficiency hindered NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby suppressing M1 polarization of interstitial macrophages during eosinophilic inflammation. cAMP levels in macrophages were likely associated with M1 polarization, as cAMP analogs regulated NLRP3 inflammasome activation via PKA and EPAC pathways. Finally, adoptive transfer of cAMP analog‐treated macrophages reversed eosinophilic to neutrophilic inflammation in myeloid Gαs‐ablated mice following hRV infection.ConclusionsThese results highlight the essential role of macrophage cAMP in steroid‐resistant, neutrophil‐dominant airway inflammation during hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation.

Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic respiratory disease characterized by airway inflammation, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, recurrent wheezing, coughing, and shortness of breath [1]. It affects individuals of all ages, particularly children. Approximately 334 million people worldwide suffer from asthma, with numbers expected to rise to 400 million by 2025 [2]. Asthmatic airway inflammation is heterogeneous, involving various phenotypes and endotypes due to complex interactions between risk factors and disease determinants [3]. The primary phenotype is marked by Th2‐type responses, including eosinophil infiltration, excessive mucus secretion, allergen‐specific Th2 reactions, and airway remodeling after allergen sensitization [4].

Despite optimal medical therapy, asthmatic patients frequently experience acute exacerbations and loss of disease control, leading to increased morbidity, healthcare costs, and progressive lung function decline [5, 6, 7, 8]. Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and long‐acting β‐agonists (LABA) reduce the frequency and severity of exacerbations but do not entirely prevent them [6]. Triggers include respiratory viral infections, allergens, pollutants, and exercise, with human rhinovirus (hRV) being the most common cause, responsible for up to 80% of exacerbations in children and 50% in adults [9, 10, 11, 12]. hRV, a single‐stranded RNA virus in the Picornaviridae family, infects the respiratory tract, causing colds, bronchiolitis, pneumonia, and asthma exacerbations [13]. Its pathogenesis involves multiple mechanisms, including airway inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired antiviral responses [12]. A key mechanism is neutrophilic airway inflammation [14, 15, 16]. Evidence suggests that Th2‐mediated inflammation in allergic asthma patients reduces type I IFN responses in peripheral blood mononuclear cells [17, 18], plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) [19], and bronchial epithelial cells after viral infection [20, 21]. IgE binding to FcεR inhibits antiviral IFN‐α production from pDCs, increasing susceptibility to hRV‐induced wheezing and exacerbation [19]. Additionally, hRV infection can elevate CXCL8 and CXCL10 levels in adults with mild asthma, leading to neutrophilic inflammation and worsening symptoms [22, 23]. Immune cell dysregulation can cause severe complications, like asthma exacerbation and COPD, through amplified antiviral Th1 responses and synchronized allergen‐specific Th2 expansion [24]. Other mechanisms include increased IL‐25 and IL‐33 production by epithelial cells [25, 26]. and enhanced expression of the hRV receptor CDHR3 in the airway epithelium [27, 28]. Therefore, targeted therapies are more needed to prevent and manage hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation by addressing the complex interactions between immune cells, cytokines, and related molecules.

Besides neutrophils, macrophages are critical effector cells in hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation [29, 30]. These phagocytic cells clear hRV, activate T cells, regulate cytokine production, and resolve inflammation [31]. Macrophages consist of about 70% of lung immune cells [32, 33] and can polarize into different phenotypes (M1 and M2) in response to signals during hRV‐induced exacerbation [34]. M1 macrophages, activated by hRV infection, contribute to airway inflammation and symptom exacerbation [35]. Depleting macrophages reduces hRV‐induced inflammation and hyperresponsiveness in OVA‐treated mice [36], and hRV colocalizes with CD68+ and CD11b+ airway macrophages in humans [36, 37]. While alveolar macrophages (AM) are the dominant airway subpopulation, other macrophage subpopulations in lung tissues have gained attention. Interstitial macrophages (IM), located between the alveolar and vascular endothelia, also play a role in asthma [38, 39]. AM are relatively homogeneous, while IM comprises at least two subsets defined by CD206 expression [40]. During inflammation from pollutants and allergens, resident AM are often replaced by monocyte‐derived inflammatory macrophages (InfM) and possibly IM [40]. This occurs alongside the accumulation of other inflammatory cells like neutrophils and eosinophils. IM can expand under CpG DNA stimulation, reducing allergic reactions via IL‐10 production [40] and inhibiting LPS‐induced dendritic cell maturation and migration [41]. These findings suggest IM functions in type II inflammation. However, the roles of AM, IM, and InfM in hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation remain unclear.

The stimulatory Gα subunit (Gαs) of the trimeric G protein Gs plays a central role in GPCR‐mediated signal transduction by activating adenylyl cyclase (AC) and increasing cAMP synthesis [42, 43]. Intracellular cAMP levels in innate and adaptive immune cells affect inflammatory and autoimmune diseases in animal models [42, 43]. Reduced cAMP levels in CD4+ T cells, as seen in GnasΔCD4 conditional KO (cKO) mice, impair Th17 and Th1 differentiation, suggesting that cAMP in CD4+ T cells is proinflammatory by altering Th subset differentiation and activation [44]. Inhibiting cyclic nucleotide phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4), an enzyme highly expressed in dendritic cells (DC), improves autoimmune diseases and suppresses human Th1 cell polarization by increasing cAMP levels [45, 46]. Reduced cAMP levels in DC provoke Th2 polarization and yield an allergic phenotype in mice with the Gαs subunit deleted in DC (GnasΔCD11c) [47], indicating that cAMP levels in DC play a crucial role in Th2‐mediated immunopathologies.

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that cAMP levels in myeloid cells, particularly macrophages, could affect neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation caused by hRV infection by regulating macrophage activation in the lung. To test this, we generated mice with a myeloid cell‐specific deletion of the Gαs subunit targeting the LysM gene (LysM‐Cre Gnasfl/fl, i.e., GnasΔLysM). We found that specific ablation of Gαs in myeloid cells caused a shift from neutrophilic to eosinophilic airway inflammation during hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation, accompanied by mucus hypersecretion and Th2‐type cytokines. Loss of myeloid Gαs suppressed M1 macrophage polarization, particularly in interstitial macrophages (IM), during eosinophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation caused by hRV infection. This suppression was associated with impaired NLRP3 inflammasome activation in Gαs‐deficient macrophages through protein kinase A (PKA) and exchange protein‐activated cAMP (EPAC) pathways. Finally, our results revealed that the adoptive transfer of macrophages treated with a cAMP analog reversed eosinophilic airway inflammation to neutrophilic inflammation in myeloid Gαs‐ablated mice. Therefore, this study suggests that macrophage cAMP levels play a pivotal role in steroid‐resistant, neutrophil‐dominated airway inflammation during hRV‐triggered asthma exacerbation.

Results

Generation and Immune Phenotypes of GPCR Gαs‐Deleted Mice in Myeloid Cells

To investigate the impact of myeloid intracellular cAMP levels on neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation following hRV infection, we generated GnasΔLysM cKO mice with a specific deletion of the Gαs‐encoding gene Gnas in myeloid cells using the Cre‐loxP system targeting LysM [48] (Figure S1A). These mice exhibited reduced expression of Gαs in myeloid‐derived cells, including Ly‐6G+ granulocytes, CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages, and Ly‐6C+ monocytes, but not in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 1A and Figure S1B), with Gnas deletion efficiency of 83%–90% in mature macrophages, near 100% in granulocytes, and around 16% in DCs [49]. Also, CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages isolated from the spleens of GnasΔLysM cKO mice produced lower levels of cAMP compared to macrophages isolated from Gnasfl/fl mice (Figure S1C). GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed no significant difference in the spleen's total number of myeloid and lymphoid cells, except for Ly‐6C+ monocytes, compared to control Gnasfl/fl mice (Figure S1D). There was also no change in serum Ig isotype production (Figure S1E), and histological examination revealed no alterations in the colon, lung, or spleen tissues (Figure S1F).

Loss of Myeloid Gαs Subunit Shifts hRV‐Exacerbated Asthma From Neutrophil to Eosinophil Dominance

In addition to Th2‐mediated eosinophilic inflammation, neutrophilic influx in the airways is induced by environmental stimuli like pollutants, cigarette smoke, and respiratory viral infections [5, 6, 7, 8]. hRV exposure is a leading cause of steroid‐resistant, neutrophilic inflammation in asthmatic patients during exacerbations, and a mouse model for this condition has been described (Figure S2A–C) [50, 51]. In particular, chronic neutrophilic asthma exacerbation induced by repeated exposure to hRV and allergens does not show an improvement in response to steroid treatment (Figure S2D–F). Using this model, we investigated the impact of myeloid intracellular cAMP levels on neutrophilic airway inflammation during hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation. Gnasfl/fl control and GnasΔLysM cKO mice were sensitized with OVA and then challenged with OVA. Asthma exacerbation was induced by hRV infection, and airway inflammation was assessed by analyzing leukocytes in BALF at 1 and 2 days post‐infection (dpi) (Figure 1B). Gnasfl/fl control mice showed evident neutrophilic infiltration after OVA and hRV exposure, while GnasΔLysM cKO mice had increased eosinophil infiltration and decreased neutrophils (Figure 1C). BALF lymphocytes were significantly increased in GnasΔLysM cKO mice after hRV infection. The relative ratio of BALF leukocyte subsets indicated a shift from neutrophilia to eosinophilia in GnasΔLysM cKO mice after OVA and hRV exposure. In contrast, Gnasfl/fl control mice had a higher proportion of neutrophils (Figure 1D). In GnasΔLysM cKO mice, hRV‐induced eosinophilia led to the observation of changes in ILC2. As a result, GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited a slight increase in ILC2 following hRV and OVA exposure compared to Gnasfl/fl control mice (Figure S3A,B). Notably, an increase in both ILC2 distribution and total cell count in the BALF was observed (Figure S3A), suggesting that ILC2 may play a role in eosinophilic asthma exacerbation following hRV infection in GnasΔLysM cKO mice. In addition, airway responsiveness, measured by Penh values in conscious unrestrained mice, showed an increase in GnasΔLysM cKO mice after OVA and hRV exposure compared to Gnasfl/fl controls (Figure 1E). Histopathological examinations showed more exacerbated inflammation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice than controls, with increased leukocyte infiltration in the peribronchial and perivascular areas (Figure 1F). GnasΔLysM cKO mice also had higher inflammation scores (Figure 1G). These results suggest that Gαs subunit deficiency in myeloid cells shifts neutrophilic airway inflammation to enhanced eosinophilic inflammation during asthma exacerbation following hRV infection.

Myeloid Gαs‐Ablated Mice Exhibit Th2 Inflammation in Th17‐Type Asthma Exacerbation by hRV

During hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation, cytokine analysis elucidates the roles of Th1, Th2, and Th17 (and Th9) responses [4]. To characterize eosinophil‐dominated exacerbation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice, we measured Th subset‐related cytokines in BALF 1 and 2 days after OVA and hRV exposure. GnasΔLysM cKO mice produced significantly more Th2 cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13) but not Th1 (IFN‐γ) or Th17 (IL‐17) cytokines compared to Gnasfl/fl controls (Figure 2A). Increased IL‐25 and IL‐33 levels were also observed, which activate not only ILC2 but also Th2 cells [25, 52]. However, after the OVA challenge alone, the levels of cytokine production were comparable in both Gnasfl/fl and GnasΔLysM cKO mice, suggesting that cAMP deficiency in myeloid cells does not influence eosinophilic inflammation induced solely by allergens. Th2 cytokine expression in lung tissues of GnasΔLysM cKO mice supported Th2‐type eosinophil‐dominated exacerbation (Figure 2B), while Th1 and Th17 cytokines were decreased. Goblet cell metaplasia and mucus secretion, indicative of allergic airway inflammation [53], were assessed by PAS staining. GnasΔLysM cKO mice had more mucus‐producing cells in lung airways and higher PAS scores post‐OVA and hRV exposure than Gnasfl/fl controls (Figure 2C,D). No significant differences in mucus‐producing cells were observed after the OVA challenge alone. GnasΔLysM cKO mice also showed significantly increased expression of Muc5ac and Muc5b mRNAs in lung tissue 2 days post‐OVA and hRV challenge (Figure 2E), subsequently confirmed by immunohistochemistry for Muc5AC and Muc5B proteins (Figure 2F and Figure S4A). Notably, the production of Muc5B protein was particularly evident in lung tissues of GnasΔLysM cKO mice post‐OVA and hRV challenge. These results suggest that reduced cAMP levels in myeloid cells shift hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation from Th17‐associated neutrophilic inflammation to Th2‐driven eosinophilic inflammation, accompanied by increased mucus production.

Myeloid Gαs Ablation Enhances CD4 + Th2 Cell Expansion During Th17‐Biased Responses Following hRV Exposure

Neutrophilic asthma exacerbation induced by hRV infection is known to require viral replication [51]. To determine whether eosinophilic asthma exacerbation triggered by hRV and OVA exposure in GnasΔLysM cKO mice similarly depends on viral replication, we compared responses to live and UV‐inactivated hRV. OVA‐sensitized Gnasfl/fl control and GnasΔLysM cKO mice were challenged with either live or UV‐inactivated hRV in combination with OVA, and eosinophilic inflammation was assessed. Exposure to UV‐inactivated hRV did not result in significant changes in eosinophilic inflammation compared to OVA challenge alone in either genotype (Figure S5A), suggesting that the eosinophilia observed in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following hRV infection is dependent on active viral replication. To evaluate whether differences in viral replication contribute to the observed phenotype, we measured hRV RNA levels in lung tissue. Comparable levels of hRV RNA were detected in both Gnasfl/fl and GnasΔLysM cKO mice (Figure S5B), indicating that viral replication occurs to a similar extent in both strains. Therefore, while viral replication is necessary for the induction of eosinophilia in GnasΔLysM cKO mice, the exacerbated eosinophilic inflammation is unlikely to result from differences in viral replication between the genotypes.

Subsequently, to further investigate the Th2‐type, eosinophil‐dominated airway inflammation observed in GnasΔLysM cKO mice after OVA and hRV exposure, we examined CD4+ Th subsets in BALF, lung tissues, and MLN 1 dpi in response to stimulation with PMA and ionomycin. Compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed increased frequencies and total count of CD4+ Th2 cells producing IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 following exposure to OVA and hRV, while the levels of IFN‐γ+CD4+ Th1 and IL‐17+CD4+ Th17 cells were decreased (Figure 3A,B). Both mouse types had similar levels of CD4+Foxp3+ Treg cells, indicating that the regulatory T cell population remained unaffected. These findings are consistent with the observed Th2‐type, eosinophil‐dominated airway inflammation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice post‐OVA and hRV challenge. Moreover, GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited higher frequencies and total count of IL‐4+, IL‐5+, and IL‐13+ CD4+ Th2 cells in MLN, compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, suggesting that reduced cAMP levels in myeloid cells influence the distribution and/or migration of Th2 cells during asthma exacerbation (Figure 3A,B). CD4+ Th2 cells, primed with OVA protein absorbed to alum, are activated in MLN and migrate to inflamed lung tissues, coordinating asthmatic immune responses during OVA challenge [54]. To further investigate CD4+ Th2‐biased responses in inflamed lung tissues of GnasΔLysM cKO mice after OVA and hRV exposure, we stimulated lung and MLN leukocytes with OVA protein and measured Th subset‐related cytokines. GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited markedly enhanced production of Th2‐related cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13) from CD4+ T cells derived from lung tissues and MLN, whereas Th1 (IFN‐γ) and Th17 (IL‐17) cytokine production was decreased (Figure 3C).

Given the strong correlations between allergen‐specific IgE, IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a responses and Th subsets in allergic asthma progression [55], we measured these Ig levels in sera. GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed increased OVA‐specific IgE and IgG1 levels, particularly after OVA and hRV challenge, while IgG2a levels were decreased, indicating a suppressed Th1 response (Figure 3D). Collectively, these results suggest that the deficiency of the GPCR Gαs subunit in myeloid cells promotes a skewed distribution and/or migration towards CD4+ Th2 effector cells, leading to Th2‐type, eosinophil‐dominated airway inflammation during asthma exacerbation triggered by hRV infection.

Myeloid Gαs Ablation Provokes Mixed Th2/Th17 Airway Inflammation With Repeated Allergen and hRV Exposure

Long‐term recurrent exposure to allergens can drive neutrophil‐dominated chronic airway inflammatory responses, resulting in epithelial destruction and eventual airway remodeling in asthma [56, 57]. While the immunopathological processes of Th2‐mediated chronic asthma are somewhat understood, the chronic exacerbation of asthma inflammation with hRV infection remains unexplored. Given that decreased cAMP levels in myeloid cells shift neutrophilic inflammation to eosinophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation after allergen and hRV challenge, it is essential to examine the impact of reduced cAMP on chronic asthma exacerbation with hRV infection. To this end, we subjected Gnasfl/fl control and GnasΔLysM cKO mice to recurrent OVA and hRV exposure. Mice were sensitized with 100 μg of OVA in 2 mg of alum via the intraperitoneal (i.p.) route, followed by intranasal (i.n.) challenges with 50 μg of OVA for 3 consecutive days per week over 5 weeks. Chronic asthma exacerbation was induced by infecting the mice with hRV (1 × 107 TCID50) during the third OVA challenge each week. Inflammation was assessed by analyzing leukocytes in BALF 1 day after the final challenge (Figure 4A). Our results revealed that GnasΔLysM cKO mice had an increased number of total leukocytes and neutrophils in BALF following repeated OVA challenges and hRV infection compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, with higher numbers of eosinophils, despite the predominance of neutrophils (Figure 4B). Also, we observed neutrophil‐dominated chronic airway inflammation after repeated OVA challenge and hRV infection, as indicated by leukocyte subset proportions (Figure 4C). In addition, airway hyperresponsiveness, assessed by Penh values in conscious, unrestrained mice, was more pronounced in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following OVA and hRV exposure compared to Gnasfl/fl controls (Figure 4D). Histopathological examination showed more severe inflammation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice, characterized by increased cellular infiltration in the peribronchial and perivascular areas and enhanced thickness of the airway epithelium after repeated OVA and hRV challenges (Figure 4E). These findings were confirmed by histopathological scoring of inflammatory cell infiltration and epithelial thickness (Figure 4F). Gnasfl/fl control and GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed no significant changes in histopathological scores after repeated OVA exposure alone. Cytokine analysis of BALF indicated significant increases in Th2‐type cytokines (IL‐4 and IL‐5), Th1‐type cytokines (IFN‐γ), and Th17‐type cytokines (IL‐17) in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following repeated OVA and hRV exposure, with no changes in IL‐13 and IL‐33 levels (Figure 4G). Enhanced expression of these cytokines' mRNAs in lung tissues further supported these findings (Figure 4H). Additionally, GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited significantly increased levels of OVA‐specific IgE, IgG, and IgG1 in sera but decreased IgG2a levels, indicating a suppressed Th1 response (Figure 4I). Collectively, these results suggest that decreased cAMP levels in myeloid cells provoke mixed Th2‐ and Th17‐type chronic airway inflammation following repeated allergen and hRV exposure. This mixed inflammation is characterized by both eosinophil and neutrophil infiltration, leading to a complex exacerbation of asthma with severe histopathological changes.

Myeloid Gαs Deficiency Leads to Increased Mucus Production and Collagen Deposition With Repeated Allergen and hRV Exposure

To further characterize the mixed Th2‐ and Th17‐type chronic inflammation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice, we performed PAS staining to detect mucus‐producing cells in the airway. GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited significantly increased mucus hypersecretion compared to Gnasfl/fl controls (Figure 5A). In Gnasfl/fl control mice, chronic inflammation resulting from repeated OVA and hRV exposure led to reduced mucus secretion compared to that observed with repeated OVA exposure alone. This was confirmed by scoring the frequency of PAS‐positive cells, with GnasΔLysM cKO mice showing higher scores (Figure 5B). Moreover, GnasΔLysM cKO mice repeatedly challenged with OVA alone had the highest mucus secretion and expression of Muc5ac and Muc5b mRNAs (Figure 5C), as confirmed by immunohistochemistry for Muc5AC and Muc5B proteins in lung tissues (Figures 5D and S4B). As expected, GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed higher production of Muc5AC and Muc5B proteins in the airway area compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, with the highest frequency of Muc5AC and Muc5B‐producing cells observed in mice repeatedly exposed to OVA alone. Repeated allergen challenges promote airway remodeling, such as collagen deposition and epithelial proliferation [58]. Masson's trichrome staining revealed denser and thicker collagen layers around the airways in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following repeated OVA and hRV exposure, compared to Gnasfl/fl control mice (Figure 5E,F). These findings indicate that the deficiency of myeloid Gαs subunit leads to exaggerated mucus production and collagen deposition following repeated allergen and hRV exposure, contributing to chronic airway inflammation and remodeling.

Loss of Myeloid Gαs Subunit Suppresses M1 Polarization of Interstitial Macrophage During Eosinophilic Airway Inflammation

Macrophages, comprising about 70% of lung immune cells, play a crucial role in the progression of allergic asthma, with M1/M2 polarization closely linked to disease severity [32, 33, 59]. Furthermore, hRV colocalizes with CD68+ lung macrophages in OVA‐sensitized and hRV‐infected mice [36, 37]. Deleting the Gαs‐encoding gene Gnas in myeloid cells primarily affects Ly‐6G+ granulocytes, Ly‐6C+ monocytes, and F4/80+ macrophages. Given the short lifespan of granulocytes, we focused on the role of lung macrophages in shifting neutrophilic to eosinophilic inflammation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following allergen and hRV challenges. Three macrophage types were observed to be mainly detected in BALF and lung tissues after OVA and hRV exposure [60, 61]: Siglec‐F+Ly‐6C− alveolar macrophages (AM), Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− interstitial macrophages (IM), and Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C+ inflammatory macrophages (InfM) (Figure 6A). GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed increased levels of Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− IM in both BALF and lung tissues compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, especially after OVA and hRV exposure (Figure 6B). These mice also exhibited higher Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− IM following OVA protein challenges alone. Next, M1/M2 polarization was assessed to understand macrophages' role in eosinophilic asthma exacerbation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice. GnasΔLysM cKO mice displayed suppressed M1 polarization in macrophage subpopulations after OVA and hRV exposure, indicated by reduced IL‐12p40 and iNOS production in response to brief LPS stimulation (Figure 6C,D). Notably, Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− IM in BALF and lung tissues from GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed the most significant suppression of M1 polarization compared to other macrophage subpopulations. In line with suppressed M1 polarization of IM, there were fewer Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− IM producing IL‐12p40 and iNOS in BALF and lung tissues of GnasΔLysM cKO mice 1 day after OVA and hRV exposure (Figure 6E,F). Additionally, GnasΔLysM cKO mice had fewer Siglec‐F+Ly‐6C− AM and Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C+ InfM producing IL‐12p40 and iNOS in lung tissues compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, indicating that decreased cAMP levels in myeloid cells also suppress M1 polarization in these macrophage subsets. To further investigate, we isolated Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− IM, Siglec‐F+Ly‐6C− AM, and Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C+ InfM from lung tissues to examine M1/M2 macrophage effector molecule expression. AM, IM, and InfM from GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited lower expression of M1 macrophage effector molecules than those from Gnasfl/fl control mice, while M2 effector molecule expression remained unchanged (Figure 6G). Specifically, after OVA and hRV exposure, IM and AM from GnasΔLysM cKO mice had significantly lower M1 effector molecule expression. In summary, these results indicate that the ablation of the Gαs subunit in myeloid cells results in suppressed M1 polarization of lung macrophages, particularly Siglec‐F−Ly‐6C− IM, during asthma exacerbation by hRV infection, thereby contributing to eosinophil‐dominated airway inflammation.

Impaired Inflammasome Activation and M1 Polarization in Gαs‐Deficient Macrophages Through PKA and EPAC

Inflammasome activation plays a critical role in airway diseases such as asthma [62, 63, 64] and is required for hRV‐induced airway inflammation in allergen‐sensitized hosts [62]. IL‐1β production via NLRP3 inflammasome activation is particularly significant in patients with neutrophilic and Th2/Th17‐predominant asthma [64]. Given that IL‐1β influences macrophage polarization [65], we assessed the levels of IL‐1β and IL‐18 in BALF from Gnasfl/fl control and GnasΔLysM cKO mice at 6, 12, and 24 h following OVA and hRV exposure. GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited significantly reduced production of both IL‐1β and IL‐18 compared to Gnasfl/fl controls, particularly following combined OVA and hRV exposure (Figure 7A). Notably, the reduction in IL‐1β was more pronounced at the early time point (12 h), coinciding with impaired inflammasome activation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice. The expression of inflammasome‐related mRNAs, including Nlrp1, Nlrp3, Nlrc4, and Aim2, was also decreased in the lung tissues of GnasΔLysM cKO mice 12 h post‐exposure (Figure 7B). Immunoblotting further confirmed suppression of inflammasome activation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice, showing reduced expression of NLRP3 (110 kDa), active caspase‐1 (20 kDa), and mature IL‐1β (17 kDa) in lung tissues compared to Gnasfl/fl controls following OVA and hRV exposure (Figure 7C). Even in response to OVA challenge alone, GnasΔLysM cKO mice demonstrated lower NLRP3 inflammasome activity relative to Gnasfl/fl controls. Confocal microscopy supported these findings, revealing reduced co‐localization of F4/80 and NLRP3 in GnasΔLysM cKO mice (Figure S6A). These results indicate that ablation of the Gαs subunit in myeloid cells impairs NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby reducing IL‐1β production. To further investigate, we used BMDM from GnasΔLysM cKO mice. As expected, these macrophages showed markedly reduced IL‐1β production upon stimulation with LPS, ATP, and nigericin (Figure 7D), which activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [66]. Suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation in Gαs‐deficient macrophages was confirmed by immunoblotting, as evidenced by reduced levels of mature IL‐1β (17 kDa) in the culture supernatant and decreased expression of NLRP3 (110 kDa) and active caspase‐1 (20 kDa) in the cell lysates of BMDM (Figure S6B). Additionally, Gαs‐deficient macrophages exhibited impaired M1 polarization, as indicated by reduced IL‐12p40 and iNOS production in response to LPS and IFN‐γ (Figure 7E). These BMDM also displayed lower M1 macrophage effector molecules expression than Gnasfl/fl controls, while M2 effector molecule expression remained unchanged (Figure 7F). This suggests that reduced cAMP levels in BMDM diminish NLRP3 inflammasome activation, resulting in suppressed M1 macrophage polarization.

To explore the role of cAMP, we treated Gαs‐deficient BMDM with cell‐permeable cAMP (8‐CPT‐cAMP). 8‐CPT‐cAMP treatment restored IL‐1β production in response to LPS, ATP, and nigericin (Figure 7G). Additionally, 8‐CPT‐cAMP reinstated M1 polarization (Figure 7H), normalizing M1 effector molecule expression in Gαs‐deficient BMDM (Figure 7I). We also used specific inhibitors to examine the effects of cAMP's major signaling pathways—PKA and EPAC. Both PKA (H‐89) and EPAC inhibitors (CE3F4) significantly suppressed IL‐1β production in Gαs‐competent BMDM following LPS, ATP, and nigericin stimulation (Figure S7A). This suppression was accompanied by reduced M1 polarization and effector molecule expression (Figure S7B,C). Combined treatment with PKA and EPAC inhibitors significantly suppressed inflammasome activity and M1 polarization in BMDM, indicating that cAMP influences NLRP3 inflammasome activation and M1 polarization through PKA and EPAC pathways. Collectively, macrophages derived from GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibit reduced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and M1 macrophage polarization due to decreased cAMP levels, mediated via PKA and EPAC pathways. This inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activity and M1 macrophage polarization likely contributes to the eosinophilic airway inflammation observed following OVA and hRV exposure.

Regulation of Eosinophilic hRV‐Induced Asthma Exacerbation With Artificial cAMP‐Treated Macrophages

Artificial cAMP (8‐CPT‐cAMP) treatment restored impaired inflammasome activation and M1 polarization in Gαs‐deficient macrophages, mediated by PKA and EPAC pathways. To investigate the in vivo relevance of this finding, we examined whether adoptive transfer of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages could restore eosinophilic asthma exacerbation to neutrophilic asthma exacerbation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following OVA and hRV infection, similar to the response observed in Gnasfl/fl controls. To this end, we administered 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated BMDM intranasally to OVA‐sensitized Gnasfl/fl and GnasΔLysM cKO mice during the OVA challenge 1 day before hRV infection (Figure 8A). Somewhat interestingly, the administration of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient BMDM resulted in the suppression of eosinophil infiltration and a significant increase in neutrophil infiltration in BALF of GnasΔLysM cKO mice (Figure 8B). Conversely, administering 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated macrophages to Gnasfl/fl control mice did not induce changes beyond those observed with untreated macrophages, predominantly showing neutrophil infiltration. This shift from eosinophilic to neutrophilic airway inflammation due to the administration of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages was further confirmed by examining the distribution of leukocyte subpopulations in BALF (Figure 8C). Subsequent histopathological analysis of lung tissues revealed pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration in peribronchial and perivascular areas when untreated Gαs‐deficient macrophages were administered to GnasΔLysM cKO mice. In contrast, the administration of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages to GnasΔLysM cKO mice resulted in inflammation levels comparable to those in Gnasfl/fl control mice (Figure 8D,E). Additionally, the administration of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages to GnasΔLysM cKO mice suppressed Muc5ac and Muc5b mRNA expression, aligning with levels observed in Gnasfl/fl control mice (Figure 8F). This was corroborated by PAS staining, which indicated a reduction in goblet cell metaplasia and mucus production (Figure 8G). To further support the findings of the conversion from eosinophil to neutrophil airway inflammation and reduced mucus production, levels of Th2, Th1, and Th17 cytokines in BALF were examined. As anticipated, the administration of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages to GnasΔLysM cKO mice resulted in decreased secretion of Th2 cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13), while the production of Th17 cytokine IL‐17 and Th1 cytokine IFN‐γ increased (Figure 8H). Additionally, the secretion of epithelial cell‐derived ILC2‐stimulating cytokines IL‐25 and IL‐33, induced by hRV infection, was reduced upon administration of 8‐CPT‐cAMP‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages to GnasΔLysM cKO mice. Serum Ig isotype levels also showed that administering these macrophages to GnasΔLysM cKO mice decreased OVA‐specific IgE and IgG1 levels to those comparable to Gnasfl/fl control mice. In contrast, OVA‐specific IgG2a production, stimulated by Th1 cytokines, increased (Figure 8I). Collectively, these findings suggest that the administration of artificial cAMP (8‐CPT‐cAMP)‐treated Gαs‐deficient macrophages to GnasΔLysM cKO mice induces a restoration from OVA and hRV‐induced Th2‐type eosinophilic airway inflammation to Th1/Th17‐type neutrophilic airway inflammation.

Discussion

cAMP, formed by AC activation via the Gαs subunit of GPCR, is known as the original “second messenger” and has recently been recognized for its role in regulating immunopathological diseases by modulating immune cell functions, such as CD4+ T cells and DCs [44, 47]. However, the role of intracellular cAMP in macrophages and its effect on immunopathological diseases have not been studied. Using GnasΔLysM cKO mice, we found that decreased cAMP in myeloid cells shifted neutrophilic airway inflammation to eosinophil‐dominated inflammation in hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation. While Gnasfl/fl control mice developed Th17‐type inflammation, GnasΔLysM cKO mice showed mucus hypersecretion, Th2‐type cytokine production, and enhanced CD4+ Th2 expansion. Additionally, GnasΔLysM cKO mice exhibited mixed Th2‐ and Th17‐biased inflammation with neutrophil and eosinophil infiltration and collagen deposits in chronic asthma exacerbation induced by repeated allergen and hRV exposures. Mechanistically, reduced cAMP in macrophages was likely associated with impaired NLRP3 inflammasome activation via PKA and EPAC signaling pathways, thereby suppressing M1 polarization, particularly in IM. Adoptive transfer of cAMP‐treated macrophages reversed eosinophilic to neutrophilic inflammation in hRV‐induced exacerbation. These results suggest intracellular cAMP in macrophages is crucial for developing neutrophilic airway inflammation during hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation through NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent M1 polarization.

Neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation caused by hRV infection is a major cause of intractable morbidity, increased healthcare costs, and progressive lung function loss [67, 68]. The role of cAMP in this process has yet to be documented, and few reports describe macrophages' role in hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation [35, 36, 37]. Our study demonstrates that neutrophil‐dominated exacerbation relies on cAMP presence in macrophages. Depletion of macrophages in OVA‐sensitized mice inhibited airway inflammation post‐hRV infection, highlighting macrophages' role [36]. While M2 polarization may enhance eosinophilia [35, 37], our findings focus on M1 polarization inducing neutrophilic inflammation. This discrepancy may be due to varying hRV infection doses, which can induce different inflammation phenotypes [35, 36, 37, 50, 51]. We induced Th17‐type, neutrophil‐dominated inflammation using a stable hRV dose [50, 51]. Our findings are supported by reports that hRV infection in macrophages can induce M1 polarization through inflammasome activation [62, 63]. Macrophages are the most abundant cell group in lung tissues and are implicated in asthma via M1/M2 polarization [32, 35, 59]. The contribution of interstitial macrophages (IM) to asthma phenotype has recently been elucidated [40, 41]. Our study highlights IM's role in neutrophil‐dominated exacerbation. Gαs expression is absent in both macrophages and neutrophils when LysM is targeted in Cre‐LoxP recombination [49]. Since neutrophils are not infected by hRV and have short lifespans, macrophages play a significant role in hRV‐induced exacerbation [69]. Although our study suggests that cAMP reduction suppresses M1 polarization of IM, it also weakens M1 polarization of other macrophage subpopulations like AM and InfM. Future research should further explore IM's contribution to hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation through cAMP reduction and M1 polarization attenuation.

Phenotypic changes in asthmatic diseases with varying cAMP levels in immune cells have shown contrasting results [70]. For instance, increasing cAMP in DC via PDE4 inhibitors or AC stimulation reduces inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α) but increases anti‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐10) [71], potentially worsening Th2‐type asthma. Conversely, GnasΔCD11c cKO mice with reduced cAMP in DC develop Th2‐type eosinophilic asthma even without allergen sensitization [47], indicating that decreased cAMP in DC favors Th2‐type eosinophilic inflammation. Also, cAMP levels in conventional DC2 support Th17‐inducing properties in neutrophilic airway inflammation [47]. This process occurs through the cAMP‐PKA‐CREB signaling pathway, which regulates DC2 by repressing IRF4 and KLF4 [72]. Our results align with these contexts: cAMP reduction in myeloid cells shifted hRV‐induced neutrophil‐dominated exacerbation to eosinophil‐dominated exacerbation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice. This suggests that myeloid cAMP reduction can drive Th2‐biased asthma, even amidst Th17‐type inflammation caused by hRV infection. Repeated allergen exposure in asthma induces airway remodeling and chronic inflammation with increased neutrophil accumulation, leading to steroid‐resistant and irreversible airway changes [56, 57]. Research on chronic asthma exacerbation with both allergen and hRV exposure is limited. Interestingly, myeloid cAMP reduction also appeared to suppress irreversible neutrophil‐dominated inflammation in chronic exacerbation following repeated allergen and hRV exposure. Although GnasΔLysM cKO mice exposed to both allergen and hRV showed more neutrophilia than those exposed to allergen only, they still had higher eosinophilia compared to Gnasfl/fl controls. Ultimately, cAMP reduction in myeloid cells drives Th2 responses by suppressing Th17 responses, even in environments promoting chronic Th17 inflammation from repeated allergen and hRV exposure.

A mechanistic finding of this study is that reduced cAMP levels in macrophages are associated with impaired inflammasome activation, leading to a shift from hRV‐induced neutrophilic asthma exacerbation to eosinophilic airway inflammation. Although studies directly linking intracellular cAMP levels to inflammasome activation are limited, multiple reports indicate that elevated cAMP suppresses inflammasome activity, including activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. For instance, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) has been shown to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation in human monocytes by increasing intracellular cAMP via engagement of the PGE2 receptor subtype 4 (EP4) [73]. Similarly, forskolin, a known cAMP elevator, has been reported to suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation [74], while activation of the calcium‐sensing receptor (CASR) promotes NLRP3 activation through increased intracellular Ca2+ and decreased cAMP levels [75]. These findings appear to contrast with our results. Although the underlying reason for this discrepancy remains unclear, it may reflect differences in cell types or the genetic backgrounds of the experimental models. Nevertheless, our study is the first to suggest that cAMP reduction—resulting from deletion of the Gαs subunit of GPCRs in macrophages in GnasΔLysM cKO mice—may be linked to impaired NLRP3 inflammasome activation. These mice secreted less BALF IL‐1β and IL‐18 after allergen and hRV challenges, indicating impaired inflammasome activation. Experiments with BMDM derived from GnasΔLysM cKO mice further supported this finding. Although these results differ from previous in vitro studies using cAMP inducers, restoring intracellular cAMP with a cAMP analog induced inflammasome activation in macrophages from GnasΔLysM cKO mice. Intracellular cAMP is synthesized by AC activation functions through PKA and EPAC stimulation [76]. Therefore, the inhibition of inflammasome activation by PKA and EPAC inhibitors is likely linked to impaired cAMP sensor activation in macrophages from GnasΔLysM cKO mice. Furthermore, intranasal administration of macrophages treated with cell‐permeable cAMP to GnasΔLysM cKO mice shifted eosinophil‐dominated inflammation (induced by OVA and hRV infection) to neutrophil‐dominated inflammation, similar to that observed in Gnasfl/fl control mice. This indicates that cAMP in macrophages is critical in hRV‐induced neutrophilic asthma exacerbation through inflammasome activation and M1 macrophage polarization mediated by the PKA and EPAC pathways.

Inflammasome activation is essential for inducing airway hypersensitivity by hRV infection [62, 63]. Our results suggest that cAMP reduction in macrophages shifts hRV‐induced, neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation by impairing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent M1 polarization. A known correlation exists between inflammasome activation and M1/M2 macrophage polarization in asthma. M2 polarization of lung macrophages, especially IM, facilitates allergen‐induced Th2‐type inflammation, with the expression of markers like CD206, arginase‐1 (Arg‐1), and CCL17/CCL22/CCL24 [77]. Macrophage polarization also affects ILC2 activation, influencing asthma progression [33]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation has been shown to induce M2 polarization, increasing IL‐4 production and exacerbating allergen‐induced asthma [78]. While M2 polarization promotes Th2‐type airway inflammation, there is limited evidence linking macrophage polarization and hRV‐induced neutrophilia in asthma exacerbation. Hong et al. found that allergen and hRV infection in IL‐4R KO mice induced neutrophil‐dominated exacerbation via M1 polarization, producing TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, and IL‐12p40, supporting our findings [35]. Decreased cAMP in macrophages, mainly IM, likely leads to eosinophil‐dominated exacerbation by inhibiting M1 polarization and impairing NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Our study did not find evidence of M2‐biased polarization in cAMP‐deficient macrophages, as markers like Arg‐1, Fizz1, Ym1, and CD206 remained unchanged. Additionally, cAMP‐deficient macrophages did not show enhanced M2 polarization under IL‐4 and IL‐13 stimulation (Data not shown). Our results were further supported by the finding that the adoptive transfer of cAMP‐treated macrophages reversed eosinophilic to neutrophil‐dominated exacerbation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice following allergen and hRV exposure. These findings prove that eosinophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation in GnasΔLysM cKO mice is due to inhibited M1 macrophage polarization through weakened NLRP3 inflammasome activation by decreased cAMP.

hRV is a ubiquitous virus known to cause upper respiratory tract infections and is a major trigger of steroid‐resistant asthma exacerbation [9, 10, 11, 12]. However, the mechanisms of hRV‐induced neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation need further elucidation. Typically, inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) combined with long‐acting beta2‐agonists (LABA) are prescribed as first‐line therapy for asthma patients [2]. Despite this, about 35%–50% of asthma patients still exhibit inadequate responses to this treatment. The efficacy of LABA/ICS therapy is believed to be mediated by increased intracellular cAMP [79]. LABA induces smooth muscle relaxation by stimulating the PKA and EPAC pathways through elevated cAMP levels [80]. Recent studies show that LABA preferentially increases cAMP levels in lung macrophages of severe asthma patients, affecting their function [81]. Repetitive LABA prescription and hRV exposure during seasonal exacerbations may result in persistent cAMP accumulation in lung tissue, leading to steroid‐resistant, neutrophilic asthma exacerbation, rendering LABA/ICS therapy ineffective. Our study suggests that cAMP levels in myeloid cells, particularly macrophages, play a crucial role in the progression of steroid‐resistant, neutrophil‐dominant asthma exacerbations. Therefore, elucidating the precise mechanisms that regulate intracellular cAMP levels in macrophages and understanding its role in inflammasome activation may be important for developing effective treatments for steroid‐resistant, neutrophil‐dominant asthma exacerbations.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

All animal experiments described in the present study were conducted at Jeonbuk National University according to the guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) of Jeonbuk National University and were pre‐approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments of Jeonbuk National University (approval number: JBNU‐2019‐00079). The animal research protocol in this study followed the guidelines set up by the nationally recognized Korea Association for Laboratory Animal Sciences (KALAS). In this study, the institutional bio‐safety committees (IBC) of Jeonbuk National University approved all necessary experimental bio‐safety protocols.

Animals, Cells, and Viruses

Gnasfl/fl (Gnastm5.1Lsw/tm5.1Lsw, C57BL/6 [H‐2b] background) mice, in which the Gnas gene for Gαs is flanked by Loxp sites, were obtained from Dr. Raz's laboratory (Univ. of California, San Diego, CA) kindly gifted by Dr. Lee Weinstein (NIH, Bethesda, MD), as described elsewhere [47]. GnasΔLysM conditional knockout (cKO) mice, in which the Gnas gene is deleted in myeloid cells, were generated by crossbreeding Gnasfl/fl mice with LysM‐Cre transgenic mice (B6.129P2‐Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo/J, The Jackson Lab., Bar Harbor ME) based on the Cre‐loxP recombination system [48]. All mice were genotyped and bred in the animal facilities of Jeonbuk National University. Minor‐group hRV 1B was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (VR1645; Middlesex, UK) and passaged six times in the Ohio‐HeLa human epithelial cell line (ECACC84121901; European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, Salisbury, UK) before purification. Ohio‐HeLa cells grown to around 90% confluency were infected with hRV 1B in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 U/mL). Six to seven days post‐infection (dpi), cultures of Ohio‐HeLa cells showing an 80%–90% cytopathic effect were harvested. The virus was then concentrated through ultracentrifugation using 30% sucrose cushions, and the viral stock was stored at −80°C until use in vivo. The hRV 1B was titrated on the Ohio‐HeLa cells using standard methods for cytopathic effect.

Abs and Reagents

Unlabeled and fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies used for flow cytometric analysis and immunoblotting, as well as chemical reagents, were obtained from BD Biosciences (San Diego, CA), eBioscience (San Diego, CA), BioLegend (San Diego, CA), Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA), Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX), and Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) (Table S1).

Mouse Model of Neutrophil‐Dominated Airway Inflammation Exacerbated by hRV Infection

Mouse models of neutrophil‐dominated asthma exacerbation by hRV infection were modified [50, 51]. Gnasfl/fl and GnasΔLysM cKO mice were sensitized with 100 μg of OVA absorbed in 2 mg of alum via i.p. injection. After 10 days, mice were challenged with 50 μg of OVA in 30 μL of PBS for 3 consecutive days via the i.n. route under mild anesthesia. The third OVA challenge for neutrophil‐dominated asthma included hRV (1 × 107 TCID50). UV‐inactivated hRV (1200 mJ/cm2, 30 min) was used for a negative control in some experiments. To establish a chronic model, mice were sensitized twice with 100 μg of OVA absorbed in 2 mg of alum. Ten days later, they were challenged with 50 μg of OVA for 3 consecutive days via the i.n. route. For hRV‐induced exacerbation in the chronic model, the third OVA challenge included hRV (1 × 107 TCID50), repeated five times every 7 days.

Quantitative Real‐Time RT‐PCR (qRT‐PCR) Analysis

The mRNA expressions of cytokines, inflammasome‐related molecules, and mucus‐producing genes were assessed by real‐time qRT‐PCR using total RNA extracted from lung tissue with Wizol reagent (Wizbiosolutions Inc., Seongnam, Korea). The RNA was reverse transcribed using High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kits (Wizbiosolutions Inc), and the cDNA was subjected to qRT‐PCR using a CFX96 Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). The reaction mixture included two μl of cDNA, 10 μL of 2× SYBR Premix Ex Taq (WizBio Solutions), and 200 nM primers (Table S2) in a final volume of 20 μL. The reactions were denatured at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 20 s. Melting curve analysis was performed from 65°C to 95°C, with fluorescence measured every 5 s. Control samples without template DNA were included in each assay. All measurements were duplicated, and the amplified product authenticity was confirmed by melting curve analysis. Cytokine and chemokine expressions were normalized to GAPDH levels. Data were analyzed using Bio‐Rad CFX Manager software, version 2.1.

Collection and Analysis of Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF)

BALF was collected 1 or 2 days after the last OVA challenge and hRV infection [82]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with zolazepam (Zoletil 50; Virbac, Carros, Francs) before thoracotomy. The trachea was exposed and punctured, followed by inserting a 21‐gauge infusion set connected to a syringe containing 0.6 mL of PBS to flush the lung. This process was repeated twice with 1.0 mL of PBS each time. The first BALF was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min and used for cytokine ELISA. Cells from the first, second, and third BALF were combined for leukocyte analysis. Differential leukocytes were assessed by cytospin, stained with Wright‐Giemsa, and counted blindly under light microscopy (Olympus Corp.) based on cell morphology.

ELISA for Cytokines and Igs

The levels of IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, IL‐25, IL‐33, IL‐17A, and IFN‐γ in BALF were measured using sandwich ELISA following the manufacturer's protocols (eBioscience, BD Bioscience, Invitrogen). Nunc MaxiSorp 96‐well plates (ThermoFisher) were coated with 50 μL of Capture Ab in coating buffer overnight, washed with PBST, and blocked with ELISA diluent for 2 h. BALF samples and recombinant cytokine standards were added and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing, biotinylated detection antibodies were added and incubated for 2 h at 37°C; then streptavidin‐HRP for 40 min at 37°C. Color development was achieved with TMB substrate, stopped with 1 M phosphoric acid, and OD values were read at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Cytokine concentrations were calculated using SoftMax Pro3.4. OVA‐specific IgE, IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a levels in sera were determined by ELISA on Nunc MaxiSorp plates coated with 50 μL of OVA protein (100 ng/well).

Measurement of Airway Hyperresponsiveness

Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) was measured in conscious, unrestrained mice using barometric whole‐body plethysmography (Buxco; EMKA Technologies) while inhaling methacholine (Sigma‐Aldrich). AHR is expressed as enhanced pause (Penh) values [50]. Mice were placed in a whole‐body chamber, and basal readings were averaged over 5 min. Increasing doses of methacholine (0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/mL) were aerosolized for 1.5 min, with readings taken and averaged for 5 min after each nebulization.

Histological Examinations, Periodic Acid‐Schiff (PAS), and Masson's Trichrome Staining

Histopathological examinations were performed on left lung lobes perfused with 10% neutral buffered formalin. Lung tissues were paraffin‐embedded, sectioned (10 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) solution 2 days after the last OVA challenge. Slides were scanned and analyzed using a slide scanner (Motic Digital Pathology, Kowloon, Hong Kong). Lung inflammation and goblet cell hyperplasia were graded with a semiquantitative scoring system [14, 81]. Inflammation was evaluated on a 5‐point scale: 0 (standard) to 5 (high cell influx, significant pathology). Goblet cell grading was based on PAS‐positive cell percentage: 0 (< 0.5%) to 4 (> 75%). Five fields per section were counted, and mean scores from 5 to 6 mice per group were calculated. Analyses were conducted blindly, with slides presented randomly. Masson's trichrome staining visualized collagen deposition following repeated allergen exposure [80].

Immunohistochemistry

Following deparaffinization, tissue sections underwent immunohistochemistry for Muc5AC and Muc5B proteins. After antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 3% H2O2 for 15 min and washed with PBS. Endogenous avidin and biotin were blocked using Super Block blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primary antibodies for Muc5AC and Muc5B were applied overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. After PBS rinsing, sections were incubated with secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature, then developed with diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride solution (Vector Laboratories) for 0.5–1 min. After washing in distilled water, sections were counterstained with VECTOR methyl green and cover‐slipped using a mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Slides were scanned and analyzed using a slide scanner (Motic Digital Pathology).

Flow Cytometric Analysis for Macrophage Subpopulations in BALF and Lung Tissues

Analysis of macrophage subpopulations in BALF and lung tissues was conducted using flow cytometry. The leukocytes prepared from BALF and collagen‐digested lung tissues were washed and resuspended (0.5–1 × 106 cells) in 100 μL of FACS staining buffer (PBS, 2% BSA, 0.05% NaN3). The cells were then stained with an Ab cocktail, including fluorescence dye‐conjugated Abs against CD45, Ly6‐G, CD11c, CD64, Siglec‐F, and Ly‐6C at 4°C for 40 min. After staining, the cells were washed twice with FACS buffer by spinning at 1200 rpm and 4°C for 3 min. To analyze M1 polarization of macrophage subpopulations in BALF and lung tissues, the leukocytes derived from BALF and lung tissues were briefly stimulated with LPS (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 5 h and subjected to intracellular staining for IL‐12p40 and iNOS after surface staining, as described elsewhere with some modification [83]. Following fixation, the cells were resuspended in PBS and then analyzed using a FACSCalibur equipped with CellQuest (Becton Dickson Medical Systems, Sharon, MA, USA) and FlowJo (Tree Star, San Carlos, CA, USA).

Immunoblot Analysis for Inflammasome Activation

To assess inflammasome activation, protein lysates and culture supernatants were collected from lung tissues and BMDM obtained from Gnasfl/fl and GnasΔLysM cKO mice. Samples were subjected to electrophoresis on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies specific for NLRP3, pro‐caspase‐1/caspase‐1, pro‐IL‐1β/IL‐1β, and β‐actin. After thorough washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged with the ChemiDoc system (Fusion Fx7, Vilber, France).

Confocal Microscopy for Inflammasome Activation

After fixation, the slides were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X‐100 and blocked with Super Bloc blocking buffer before being incubated with primary antibodies against NLRP3 (Cell signaling technology) and F4/80 in a humidified chamber. After washing, secondary FITC‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG (Southern Biotech) was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Following washing, the slides were sealed with 4,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma‐ Aldrich). Images were taken using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 980 with Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) and analyzed using ZEN 3.5 blue edition.

Allergen‐Specific Cytokine Production in BALF, MLN, and Lung Tissues

After heart perfusion to remove blood cells, lung lobes were collected, chopped, and homogenized through a 100‐μm mesh sieve. The tissue was digested with 0.5 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Life Technologies) in HBSS for 1 h at 37°C with vibration. The digested tissue was passed through a 20‐gauge needle and filtered through a 45‐μm mesh to create a single‐cell suspension; then centrifuged at 1500 rpm and 4°C for 5 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in NH4Cl‐based RBC lysis buffer for 10 min, filtered through a 70‐μm strainer, and washed. BALF cells were obtained by flushing lung tissues with PBS after thoracotomy. Leukocytes from mediastinal lymph nodes (MLN) were harvested by pressing them through 100‐μm strainers. Leukocytes from BALF, MLN, and lung tissues were plated in 96‐well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) in complete RPMI with 2% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 U/mL), and stimulated with OVA (500 μg/mL) at 37°C for 72 h. Culture supernatants were used for cytokine ELISA to evaluate CD4+ Th responses.

Analysis of CD4 + Th Subsets in BALF, MLN, and Lung Tissue

To monitor CD4+ Th subsets in MLN, lung tissues, and BALF, leukocytes were briefly stimulated with PMA (100 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1.0 μg/mL) in the presence of monensin (2 μM) at 37°C for 5 h [14, 80, 82]. The cells (106/well) were then surface‐stained with FITC‐anti‐CD4 at 4°C for 30 min, washed, and fixed. After washing with permeabilization buffer (eBioscience), the cells were stained with PE‐anti‐IFN‐γ, IL‐5, Foxp3, PerCP‐anti‐IL‐13, and APC‐anti‐IL‐4, IL‐17A at room temperature for 30 min. For Treg cells, leukocytes were surface‐stained with FITC‐anti‐CD4 on ice for 30 min, fixed with permeabilization buffer at 4°C for 6 h, and stained with PE‐anti‐Foxp3 at RT for 30 min. The cells were resuspended in PBS and analyzed using a FACSCalibur with CellQuest (Becton Dickson) and FlowJo (Tree Star).

Purification of Lung Macrophage Subsets and Analysis of M1 Polarization

Macrophage subset cells from Gnasfl/fl and GnasΔLysM cKO mice were purified 2 days after the final OVA challenge and hRV infection. Lung tissues were perfused and digested to yield a single‐cell suspension. The isolated leukocytes were stained with fluorescently labeled Abs specific to CD45, CD11c, Ly‐6G, CD64, Siglec‐F, and Ly‐6C and then separated into alveolar macrophages (AM), interstitial macrophages (IM), and inflammatory macrophages (InfM) using flow cytometry on a FACSAria (Beckton‐Dickinson). Each macrophage subtype was individually sorted, pelleted, and subjected to RNA extraction using an RNA isolation kit (Macherey‐Nagel). The total RNA was reverse transcribed using High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kits, followed by real‐time qPCR. The expression of M1 polarization markers was normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH.

Transfer of Artificial cAMP‐Treated Macrophages

The adoptive transfer of macrophages treated ex vivo with a synthetic cAMP (8‐CPT‐cAMP) analog into GnasΔLysM cKO mice was performed as previously described with modifications [47]. BM cells were harvested from femurs and tibiae of GnasΔLysM cKO mice and cultured in RPMI medium with 10% FBS, 10% penicillin–streptomycin, 2 mM L‐glutamine, 50 μM 2‐mercaptoethanol, and 10 ng/mL GM‐CSF for 7 days. BMDM were treated with 8‐CPT‐cAMP for 24 h, harvested with Cell Stripper buffer (Corning), washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in PBS. BMDM (1 × 106) in 30 μL were transferred intranasally to GnasΔLysM cKO mice 1 day before hRV infection. Recipients were then assessed for hRV‐induced asthma exacerbation via differential cell count in BALF, lung histology, serum immunoglobulin isotypes, and cytokine production.

Statistical Analysis

All data are expressed as the average ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For the ex vivo experiments and immune cell analyses, statistically significant differences between groups were analyzed using unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐testing. For multiple comparisons, statistical significance was determined using one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. The statistical significance of the in vivo cytokine gene expression was evaluated using unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐testing. A p‐value ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. All data were analyzed using GraphPadPrism 9.0.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc.).
