Executive summary

The review presents digital forensic science as a science-intensive field that is subjected to the evolution of technology. Platform evolution and organisational capability have taken the discipline well beyond traditional examination of devices. The strategic significance of the changes extends across justice systems and into national security, incident response, inter-jurisdictional investigations, regulatory compliance, platform governance and AI-assisted decision systems.

The five narratives

During the period 2023-2025, the literature revealed five digital forensic science narratives that reflect the discipline's evolution:

Authenticity at scale refers to verifying previously overwhelming volumes of evidence, detecting manipulation or synthetic content and maintaining evidential integrity across platforms. Performing these functions at increasing scale means digital forensic science is moving towards the use of AI/LLM-enabled triage (filtering, clustering and presenting relevant evidence), prioritisation (ranking evidence by likely probative value) and interpretation (summarising, classifying or contextualising evidence to support human reasoning and decision-making). Its significance is that scale can now be increased with AI-enabled tools opening the possibilities to examine much larger volumes of digital evidence. However, this increased scale is only defensible if three conditions are met: 1) humans makes the final decision, 2) AI outputs are explainable and 3) decisions and AI interactions are logged to preserve auditability, reproducibility and defensibility. When governance trails behind the adoption of AI and automation technologies, organisational risk is created through exposure to hallucination, bias, prompt leakage, opaque reasoning and error amplification.

Defensibility first means validation, reproducibility and accreditation-readiness are design requirements for the entire method. They are no longer retrospective quality checks. Admissibility, contestability and stakeholder confidence depend on validated workflows, documented limitations, version control, auditable decision logs and transparent communication of uncertainty. When governance fails, the impacts can include fragmented ownership which presents as confused accountability and divided responsibilities, in addition to weakened change control presenting as the informal evolution of methods which fail to be documented or validated in a consistent or complete manner. Any of these failings can contribute to challenges to admissibility.

Cloud/SaaS-by-default evidence refers to the technological evolution leading to cloud platforms, SaaS applications and provider-managed services becoming the primary evidence sources. They are the default and dominant source of digital evidence from user activity, where providers generate and store logs, evidence is accessed by APIs, exports and legal means. The evidence is retained, structured and controlled by the service provider. As cloud/SaaS systems are now the primary sources of digital evidence, digital forensic science must focus on lawful access, provenance, retention policies, API-mediated collection and provider-specific acquisition workflows as core concerns. Access depends on legal processes, warrants and provider policies in an environment when inter-jurisdictional issues are routine. Cloud-centric evidence creates an organisational risk that arises from dependence on the provider. It is the providers who control access, retention of data is variable, inter-jurisdictional legal barriers complicate retrieval, logs may be incomplete or abstracted and evidence in shared environments cannot be independently verified.

Ephemeral telemetry refers to the volatile evidence in live memory (RAM), IoT/OT sensor data streams, containerised systems and edge environments which must often be captured before it disappears. RAM evidence disappears when the process ends or the system reboots or memory is reallocated. IoT/OT devices have minimal telemetry buffer capacity and may not store historical data. Containerised systems are ephemeral by design from which the artefacts disappear when the container stops. Edge devices operate with limited storage and prioritise performance. Therefore, the opportunity to capture volatile evidence is short and needs to be prioritised. Consequently, forensic readiness, rapid preservation, pre-arranged access and validated live acquisition methods are now core capabilities for defensible digital forensic science practice. Organisations face risk to their capability when they rely entirely on post hoc collection models as evidence from contemporary systems is volatile, distributed and quickly overwritten.

Blockchain investigations require attribution and evidential integrity to develop together for both cryptocurrency tracing and blockchain-based evidence management to ensure that both are analytically sound and legally defensible. Attribution involves clustering addresses, linking wallets to entities and analysing transaction patterns to identify the beneficial ownership. Whereas evidential integrity concerns verifying blockchain data, maintaining chain-of-custody and documenting node, provider and tool behaviour. Attribution and integrity represent a unified capability as attribution without integrity is untrustworthy and integrity without attribution is incomplete. Blockchain investigations are moving to structured, scalable, auditable and tamper-evident investigative processes that provide defensible findings. However, risk arises when attribution heuristics are used without statements of confidence or when blockchain immutability is treated as a technological guarantee of evidential integrity. Attribution heuristics are probabilistic, not deterministic. Therefore, without confidence limits, attribution is subject to being over-claimed and legally fragile.

Strategic implications

The major finding is that digital forensic science has fundamentally changed. No longer is it a device-centric, craft-based exercise. Digital forensic science is an integrated system-level practice where evidence is fragmented, provider-dependent, transient and increasingly analysed through automated or probabilistic tools. The change is a structural transformation, not incremental. Evidence is dispersed across cloud, SaaS, containers, IoT and edge for which access is mediated by providers. The digital traces are transient or volatile. Analysis is automated and probabilistic.

The primary strategic implication is that technical progress alone is insufficient as tools are no longer the centre gravity of digital forensic science - the workflow is. Reliability depends on method design, transparency of reasoning, documentation and version control, governance and oversight, all supported by organisational structures that support defensibility.

This fundamental change presents new, system wide risks including:

Legal and governance issues are now central to central to digital forensic science. The forensic workflow has the legal requirements (admissibility and privacy) and governance structures (provenance and access pathways) built in from beginning to end. Admissibility depends on traceability of every step of the workflow, decisions and actions are logged as auditable standard measures and reviewable by independent experts. Admissibility is earned through transparent, governed workflows. Privacy is an evidentiary quality attribute achievable through collection minimisation, targeted acquisition to avoid indiscriminate collection and proportionality. This aligns forensic practice with legal expectations and public trust requirements. Coordination between legal, technical and governance teams avoids unlawful access, inadmissible evidence or systemic risk when dealing with inter-jurisdictional access to evidence, hacked or leaked material, OSINT and platform-managed evidence.

Quality implications are substantial as legacy quality assurance models are under strain. Static validation and the periodic audit cycle is not suited to changing, AI-enabled workflows. IT environments undergo frequent software updates, evolving cloud/SaaS platforms and AI-enabled workflows with shifting behaviour. The consensus is that static validation is obsolete and quality must become continuous and adaptive. Tool validation, which tests whether a specific tool works as intended, is distinguished from method validation, which tests whether the entire workflow, including tools, reasoning, data sources and decision making, is reliable and reproducible. Further, governance, ensuring the method is controlled, documented, versioned and accountable, is distinguished from accreditation, which ensures the organisation meets a standard. Unlike traditional, static digital forensic tools, AI-enabled tools are probabilistic, change over time, depend on representativeness of training data, produce outputs that require interpretation and can introduce bias or draft. Therefore, AI models should be governed and validated as part of the methods within the full workflow and subject to accountability and reviewability.

Introduction

Every three years, the Interpol International Forensic Science Managers Symposium is held at the Interpol Directorate in Lyon, France. The centre piece of the symposium comprises 16 triennial reviews of the relevant literature that has been published over the previous three years, representing the main forensic evidence types. The reviews are presented in oral form at the symposium and in written form.

The purpose of the reviews is to provide a strategic resource for those forensic science service providers who are looking for guidance in developing, perhaps even beginning, their forensic science capability. As digital forensic science is moving rapidly due to the advances in consumer and business technology, it is challenging for even well-resourced organisations to keep pace with the changes. It must seem to be almost insurmountable for those organisations who are not as well resourced. Taking a lead from the key message of the opening remarks of the 2025 symposium, “Our mission is not excellence for the few, it is capability for all”, this review sets out to provide guidance to all organisations who see digital forensic science as an important capability within their law enforcement organisations and justice systems.

This review examines the recent evolution of digital forensic science to become a science-intensive field shaped by global practice, governance demands and capability development requirements. The literature reveals a new reality, that digital forensic science is becoming more integrated, more system-oriented and more dependent on validated methods, reproducibility, formalised SOPs and governance. The review is framed around that reality.

The conceptual architecture is organised through five narratives that were revealed by the literature and capture the dominant shifts across the field – authenticity at scale, defensibility first, cloud/SaaS-by-default evidence, ephemeral telemetry and blockchain investigations. These strategic narratives provide the framework for understanding how technological change is reshaping digital forensic science practice, risk and organisational responses across the field. The operational architecture is organised through five policy and action domains – strategy, leadership and management, capability development, capacity building and governance. These two structures operate together. The narratives identify the major patterns of change. The policy and action domains organise what those changes require of organisations, systems and leaders.

A structured analytical and synthesis approach has been applied that draws together findings across technical areas through structured extraction, thematic coding and alignment to the five narratives and five policy and action domains. The corpus was built from Google Scholar searches for the period covering the beginning of the last calendar quarter of 2022 to the end of the third calendar quarter of 2025. The search terms used were “digital evidence”, “digital forensics” and “digital forensic science”, resulting in 1723 identified references. Articles that were published in a language other than English were translated using ChatGPT and provided some diverse and regional perspectives.

The remainder of the review is structured as follows. Section 3 discusses the five narratives, section 4 discusses the shift towards science-intensive evidence as it impacts on policies and actions, then section 5 addresses legal frameworks and governance. The technical domain is discussed in section 6, section 7 addresses emerging threats and challenges followed by a discussion of quality and standards in section 8. Section 9 presents the conclusion and a strategic framework for action. The strategic framework links technological change to governance, legal defensibility and future capability design, providing a practical guide for organisations operating in contemporary digital evidence environments, culminating in recommendations for agencies, managers and practitioners.

The five narratives

Analysis of the digital forensic science literature corpus for 2023-2025 reveals five narratives which represent the synthesised insights from across the spectrum.

Digital forensic science practices are being transformed by technological changes that have the effect of increasing data volumes, platform change and justice system expectations.

Authenticity at scale describes how traditional manual analysis of digital traces has now moved to AI/LLM-enabled triage and decision-making on prioritisation. This move towards automation requires auditable, explainable processes with human responsibility retained as the ultimate authority.

Defensibility first puts validation, reproducibility and accreditation readiness front and centre as doctrinal design principles for digital forensic science practice. Governance and quality management are directly linked to the mitigation of admissibility risk.

SaaS-by-default acknowledges cloud and SaaS telemetry as primary evidence sources. This changed emphasis of the evidence source has a consequential impact on digital forensic science workflows, where log-focused and jurisdictionally compliant processes facilitate lawful access to evidence.

Ephemeral telemetry refers to the operational challenges presented by volatile IoT/OT and live memory states. Evidential data rapidly decays after it has been created, highlighting the need for preparedness and time-sensitive preservation.

Blockchain investigations integrate cryptocurrency tracing with provenance and chain-of-custody controls. These investigations are moving toward standardised, scalable and auditable evidential and investigative pipelines.

Narrative 1: authenticity at scale (AI/LLM enabled analysis)

The most obvious and anticipated narrative to emerge during the review period is the impact of artificial intelligence/large learning models (referred through the text as AI/LLM) on digital forensic science. When the breakthroughs in AI technologies emerged, the application to this field was a natural progression for many reasons, such as new ways in which to deal with the vast and ever growing volumes and variety of seized data; while on the human side, digital forensic scientists were already familiar in dealing with data at its most fundamental level. However, several issues became immediately apparent which conflicted with the requirements of digital forensic science within the justice system. Research over the past three years has done much to address many of these issues.

AI/LLM-enabled analysis has now found an important role as an assistive layer in the triage of seized data that provides significant efficiency gains in artefact review. The ultimate decision making remains the responsibility of and within the authority of the expert. In effect, digital forensic science analysis has moved from manual reviewing to model supported prioritisation [[1], [2], [3]].

The reported efficiency improvements are contingent of the use of domain-specific datasets and the integration into procedural workflows. The recent introduction of new data sets (e.g., ForensicsData) and cloud-based collection models highlight the importance of task-specific prompt engineering, operational safeguards and structured output formats. These innovations ensure that the resultant analyses are transparent, auditable and systematically documented for subsequent case management [4,5].

The foundational requirements for the admissibility of AI/LLM-enabled analysis are explainability and human-in-the-loop control. The studies emphasise the necessity for the documentation of:

•decision rationales

decision rationales

•systematic review checkpoints and

systematic review checkpoints and

•mandatory supervisory approval for the release of analytical results.

mandatory supervisory approval for the release of analytical results.

Collectively, these controls serve to mitigate the risk of biases inherent in the models and processes which would adversely influence charging or attribution decisions [1,2,6].

Although not the emphasis of this review, it is important to mention multimedia authenticity as it is an important application of digital forensic science AI/LLM-enabled analysis demonstrating significantly improved speed and detection accuracy, however, these processes are especially sensitive to changes in content and adversarial manipulation. Consequently, it is necessary to implement processes in the workflow that involve corroborative analysis and rigorous validation. This is especially true when the analytical processes are operating beyond the original training datasets [7,8].

The second aspect of authenticity at scale addresses cloud/Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) evidence. The ubiquity of cloud-based evidence has led to the normalisation or standardisation of API-driven log acquisition and the integration of AI-assisted triage across multi-tenant environments. Consequently, an admissible chain of custody will comprise documented model interactions, including which models, prompt content and versions were used, the resultant artefacts and the traditional collection metadata [5,6,9].

Blockchain is a technology that can provide the capacity for automated event logging at scale. Therefore, it is a highly suited to the verification of AI-assisted decision processes through blockchain-based custody and notarisation. Events and actions such as recording provenance, routing and tamper-evident proof of history across heterogeneous tools and agencies can be automatically recorded [1,9,10].

To support authenticity at scale, other processes must shift. The focus of validation is transitioning from single, tool-specific validations to continuous, pipeline-level monitoring. The pipeline begins with pre-deployment benchmarking, progressing to systematic post-deployment monitoring for model drift. The validation procedures are analogous to those that are employed for accredited laboratory instruments [1,2,11].

Working within the justice system, authenticity at scale means that digital forensic scientists must be increasingly cognisant of and ensure that they operate within operational guidelines defined by the jurisdictional legal framework. In practical terms, this means that the warrants and scope of AI-assisted processing must adhere to strict controls, especially particularity and minimisation. Further, provenance records must be comprehensively detailed to elucidate, limit and reproduce each analytical step [2,5,6].

Most of the researchers who addressed the issue of authenticity at scale using AI/LLM-enabled analysis also considered the risks associated with the shift to these strategies and procedures. Some of the noted risks include:

Narrative 2: defensibility first (validation, reproducibility, accreditation)

Previous reviews (for example, see the previous two Interpol digital evidence reviews) have noted the ongoing and vigorous debate around, particularly, accreditation and validation [12,13]. The debates concerned conflicting opinions on several issues, some of which can be broadly categorised as:

The above points represent a broad summary, an inexhaustive list, of the previously identified issues that concern defensibility. The current review notes a more mature and open-minded approach to defensibility, for which there are many reasons cited. Similarly to the Narrative 1 (AI/LLM-enabled analysis), research over the past three years has done much to address many of the previous issues and, perhaps, digital forensic science is now at a tipping point.

The most impactful element is the growing understanding that defensibility is now a design requirement and is not a retrospective quality check. Many authors (a small sample of whom are cited here) discuss their research (whether through experimentation, casework or review) of the ways in which method validation, reproducibility planning and accreditation-readiness shape workflow design addressing, for example, memory examination and mobile/application digital traces. However, many go further to address strategic planning for defensibility including leadership KPIs and change-control/versioning which are tied to accreditation scope, including traceability from case results to validated method versions [2,3,[14], [15], [16], [17]].

The movement towards fundamental defensibility is demonstrated by the convergence of validation standards on “models as methods”. This progression is characterised by the development of ISO 17025-compliant documentation, the use of representative datasets, followed by rigorous review conducted by a responsible human expert. This is especially the case when AI/LLM models are applied to casework. In addition, studies of steganalysis and faked images revealed the need for defined performance scope with documented limitations [2,14,15,17,18].

Failures of governance are both predictable and preventable. For example, fragmented digital forensic science capability ownership and custodianship lead to validation inconsistencies and ineffective change management. In contrast, digital forensic science defensibility is significantly improved when version control for datasets and for analytical methods, in addition to the maintenance of auditable decision logs are subject to governance controls. Within the scope of governance, the principle of defensibility first is supported by publishing method validation studies which can be accessed by the justice system [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]].

A foundational element of defensibility is the principle of reproducibility, which has been a challenging concept for digital forensic science. Reproducibility is contingent on the availability of reference corpora and verification systems. The literature emphasises laboratory level programmes that use standardised, reuseable test datasets and workflow-specific validation frameworks. These might include, for example, OCR, data fusion pipelines, authentication, steganalysis etc. These capabilities would be systematically integrated with inter-laboratory collaborative trials to obtain a confident assessment of uncertainty and reproducibility [[26], [27], [28], [29]].

Accreditation is the catalyst for strengthening organisational capacity and capability. A consistent library of SOPs and shared datasets facilitate faster onboarding and interchangeability between teams. There is a critical imperative for the organisational leadership to:

•allocate resources to teams responsible for validation and

allocate resources to teams responsible for validation and

•align training schedules and change management practices with accreditation milestones [15,24,26,[29], [30], [31], [32], [33]].

Defensibility in the court room requires the transparent communication of uncertainty and methodological limitations. Recent decisions regarding the admissibility of media and AI-based evidence that have converged with assessments of digital evidence regulation have revealed the need for digital forensic science practice to articulate confidence limits, ensure dataset representativeness while also specifying the operational constraints of automated methods [16,18,20,[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]].

Integrating AI/LLMs into digital forensic science workflows intensifies the burden of formal validation tasks. For example, LLM-enabled review and authentication tasks introduces explainability and bias risks. Several researchers have proposed:

Establishing defensibility first as a principle of digital forensic science requires a system-wide view that integrates governance, quality, capability and accreditation. In such a system, ownership (span of control), versioning and audit are elements of an integrated framework in combination with documented validation, proficiency testing and reference corpora that are all aligned with ISO 17025. Where organisations successfully embed and operationalise this framework, reproducibility and reliability lead to demonstrably improving organisational competence that result in improved justice system stakeholder confidence. Conversely, error rates will increase and admissibility of evidence will be challenged more frequently in the absence of such an integrated framework [14,15,[40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]].

Narrative 3 - SaaS-by-default evidence (cloud-native, lawful access, jurisdiction)

Digital forensic science is no longer “device forensics”. Cloud and Software-as-a-Service have become primary evidence sources. Now it is about identifying and acquiring evidence from multiple sources. The default work of an investigation now includes evidentiary data from cloud and SaaS platforms, although not to the exclusion of other sources. Cloud and SaaS platform data comprises provider telemetry and orchestration pipelines. Contemporary research highlights the adoption of horizontally scalable, cloud native methodologies for unified data ingestion, retention, analytics and audit. The approaches align Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) log warehousing with forensic requirements and evidential lifecycle management, thereby enhancing evidence integrity and traceability [6,[46], [47], [48]].

The legal defensibility of “SaaS by Default” is determined by the robustness of the warrant process, the management of inter-jurisdictional procedures and platform-specific acquisition protocols. Contemporary jurisprudence underscores the complexities of distributed cloud artefacts and provider-specific processes. This complexity is further heightened by significant variability in the admissibility and authentication of leaked or compromised data, which reinforces the necessity of pre-established legal, digital forensic science, incident response and privacy frameworks to guide evidence handling and compliance [6,46,47,[49], [50], [51], [52]].

Governance frameworks that integrate compliance auditing with forensic procedures at the time of data ingestion provide a robust operational model for defensible cloud logging. The integrated framework enables a coherent, single cloud pipeline of evidence acquisition, data retention, analysis and policy verification. Such an approach will enhance traceability, enforce chain-of-custody and ensure auditability at scale. Several authors express the opinion that these frameworks are critical for maintaining evidential integrity and supporting defensibility in cloud environments [46,47,[52], [53], [54]].

The inherent retention fragility and service provider variability compel early preservation of cloud evidence and the adoption of standardised methods. Reviews of cloud investigations reveal the transient nature of log data and diverse provider policies. These findings support the importance of standardised frameworks and reliable adapters, particularly for for O365/Google Workspace/IDP telemetry within warrant-scoped minimisation [46,47,51,52].

Operational orchestration through end-to-end pipelines reduces tool drift and enforces precise scope control over cloud sourced artefacts. Empirical models illustrate the effectiveness of managed workflows, which systematically package case-relevant outputs. This approach mitigates the risk of error propagation and supports repeatability when logs and SaaS exports require subsequent querying [46,47,51,52,55].

Jurisdictional and forum-specific admissibility tests are aligning around the need for transparency of provenance and method limitations. Guidance from judicial and arbitral bodies emphasise the importance of explicitly addressing the authenticity, reliability and scope of platform-generated data. The recommendations include documentation of heuristics, statements of uncertainty and auditable export chains [6,[50], [51], [52]].

The use of domain ontologies and CASE/USO standards facilitates systematic evidence exchange through the ordered transfer of provider log data into the investigative reasoning process. Extensions that have been developed for smart city environments demonstrate how narrative-ready mappings from heterogenous telemetry sources translate into structured representations of roles, threats and evidence categories [46,47,51,52,56].

The effective management of SaaS by default requires an integrated strategy encompassing log-centric pipelines, cross functional leadership involving legal, digital forensic science, incident response and privacy experts. The essential capabilities include the deployment of provider-specific adapters and orchestration systems integrated with centralised infrastructure for data normalisation and warehousing. The system is supported by robust governance structures, specifically concerning chain-of-custody aware logging [6,46,47,50,[57], [58], [59]].

Narrative 4: ephemeral telemetry (IoT/OT, live memory forensics)

The emergence of ephemeral telemetry as an important source of probative digital evidence has led to a shift in incident reconstruction. The priority now is preparedness for time-sensitive evidence capture rather than post-hoc data acquisition. The research reveals that volatile digital traces in IoT/OT ecosystems and live memory will rapidly decay or be overwritten due to routine system operations. Consequently, rapid preservation methods are de rigeur for digital forensic scientists’ tool kit who also need other established prearranged operational windows for lawful capture [[60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65], [66], [67]].

Reconstructing transient system states, such as cryptographic keys, active runtime objects and inter-process links, is dependent on live memory forensic science processes. Importantly, these activities, in the form of digital traces, are not stored on disk. Kernel data type inference and memory-safe acquisition techniques have been demonstrated to improve artefact recovery rates and to mitigate the risk of tool drift in heterogenous environments. However, validated methods and standardised procedures are essential [[60], [61], [62],66,[68], [69], [70]].

IoT/OT data pipelines must accommodate device heterogeneity and the distinct telemetry semantics prescribed by individual vendors. Reviews and empirical frameworks have converged to adapter-based architectures which serve to facilitate the normalisation of logs and artefact states across disparate platforms. This normalisation enables the integration of timeline data and selective minimisation of evidence [63,[70], [71], [72]].

The reliability of evidence acquisition and subsequent integrity verification is impacted by the platform service provider's policies and actions. Frequent platform update cycles and unpredictable data retention policies impact the efficacy of evidence acquisition. In case studies of motor vehicles and dash cam, it has been found that the authenticity of the collected digital traces was compromised by delayed extraction. With data being used and generated by cloud-managed applications and containerised services, evidence is now cloud/SaaS-based by default. This has introduced complexity in defining the boundaries of a digital crime scene. Two case studies, one each of Kubernetes and Wordpress deployments, illustrate that ephemeral data and orchestrated rollouts can result in the relocation of probative digital traces from local systems to orchestrator-level logs and service backends. Therefore, forensic readiness in cloud environments requires integration of provider-specific telemetry and API-level export routines that require warrant-scoped and provider-specific acquisition pathways and supporting documentation [6,46,66,74,75].

Within IoT/IIoT and vehicle ecosystems, digital evidence is distributed across edge devices, infotainment subsystems and cloud services. Therefore, defensible event reconstruction requires cross-layer correlation, i.e. the evidence of individual computing operations that is located across multiple devices and systems. This challenge is addressed by contemporary forensic frameworks that link metadata provenance to device identifiers and the associated network context. The frameworks feature verifiable exchange transactions that preserve evidence integrity across stakeholders [11,70,71,76,77].

These contemporary forensic frameworks are scalable, however, that scalability depends on sound governance that is fit for purpose and the resources allocated to purpose. A structured governance framework that includes SOPs, systematic adapter maintenance and validations based on verified, representative reference corpora is invaluable. When present, they consistently demonstrate reduced onboarding time and greater reproducibility in forensic reconstruction tasks. The alternative, i.e. using ad-hoc processes and practices correlate with uncertainty and admissibility challenges [46,47,52,62,68,76,78].

Volatility is a fundamental design challenge with ephemeral telemetry. Therefore, forensic readiness requires validated live-memory acquisition methods, the deployment of provider-specific adapter pipelines, in addition to robust cross-layer correlation across devices and systems. These technical requirements must operate within a governance framework that appropriately resources a validation program, with SOPs developed and maintained that include documented uncertainty. In the absence of an integrated system as described, the capability will be subject to persistent challenges from evidence loss, tool drift and admissibility [6,52,60,62,66,75].

Narrative 5: blockchain investigations (crypto tracing, blockchain evidence management)

Contemporary blockchain investigations tend to split into two distinct problem spaces, attribution (linking blockchain activity to real world actors) and evidential integrity (defensibility). Cryptocurrency forensic methods, including cryptocurrency tracing (comprising cluster heuristics and off-chain attribution) are systematically integrated with provenance controls. This approach preserves auditability at scale. The shift to this standardised, evidential pipeline approach is recent and replaces the previous ad-hoc wallet analytics approach [45,70,[79], [80], [81], [82], [83]].

Permissioned-ledger chain-of-custody frameworks have been designed and implemented to record the continuity, attribution and audit of digital evidence. Many proposals were put forward, including blockchain-based evidence registers and custody ledgers, which operate on the principle that each instance of evidence acquisition, transformation and transfer is immutably documented as a digitally signed transaction. Implementing a permissioned-ledger, chain-of-custody system strengthens tamper-evident measures, thereby providing assurance of evidence integrity. In addition, the system ensures accurate chronological access recording across stakeholders [9,70,[84], [85], [86], [87], [88]].

Privacy-preserving controls can no longer be considered to be optional protective features retrofitted to digital evidence systems. Instead, they are now considered to be necessary elements of the architecture. One such element is advanced cryptography which can enable lawful and precise evidence acquisition without unnecessary data exposure. Another important element is zero-knowledge-proof (ZKP)-enabled evidence acquisition allows a system to prove the evidence meets the warrant criteria without revealing the underlying data, hence preserving privacy. Applying proxy re-encryption allows encrypted evidence to be re-encrypted for a new authorised party, which enables controlled, auditable sharing with role specific disclosure. These actions can take place on permissioned blockchains and self-sovereign identity frameworks which allow, for example, investigators, judges etc, to authenticate (without centralised identity control) to receive evidence within the scope of the warrant. These strategies align with and support the principles of proportionality of evidence subjected to digital forensic science processes and to cross-jurisdictional cooperation [19,45,86,[89], [90], [91]].

Defensibility of digital evidence depends on governance frameworks that integrate technical provenance with established forum standards. Studies of admissibility in arbitral practice note the importance of systematically documented authenticity verification, methodological transparency and defined parameters for the acceptance of platform or ledger-generated data. Similarly, several studies of legal reviews noted the need for pre-established digital forensic science incident response and legal protocols to guide the consistent handling and evaluation of blockchain evidence [50,86,[92], [93], [94]].

It becomes apparent in this review that effective digital forensic science depends on coordinated workflows across tools, teams and stages that are built on formally defined data structures. To this end, contemporary digital evidence registers are no longer limited to being just passive storage systems. Contemporary digital evidence registers function as integrated operational platforms that manage the entire evidence lifecycle, from initial ingestion to retention to disposition. Not only does the platform store the evidence, it also operates as a platform for the analytical functions and, importantly, reinforces the organisation's evidence management policies. Further, within the workflow, ontology-aligned models, such as CASE/UCO and “bag-of-evidence”, enable efficient inter-agency cooperation to accelerate evidence analysis and interpretation without weakening provenance integrity [81,[95], [96], [97]].

Despite the noted advances in distributed evidence systems, some limitations persist including issues with interoperability, validators5 and the consensus overhead. The literature refers to recent surveys and evaluations which note a range of challenges:

To address these challenges, several studies propose standardised export protocols, independent verification and auditing of smart contract logic while consideration should be given to the development of inter-jurisdictional SOPs [88,98,99].

Research has established that the defensibility of blockchain investigations improves when tracing analytics is integrated in permissioned, privacy-aware custody systems that are managed under a governance framework that comprises standardised SOPs and legal guidelines. In the absence of such systems, recurring challenges over evidential authenticity, failures of data minimisation and fragile protocols for inter-jurisdictional cooperation will continue to be encountered. In summary, the research findings have reached a consensus that embedding evidential tracing with privacy-aware custody architectures adhering to governance requirements is highly impactful on the integrity and effectiveness of blockchain investigations [45,50,70,79,81,92].

There are almost as many definitions of the above policies and activities as there are organisations in the world today. Therefore, it is important to define what is being referred to in this section. For the purposes of this review, the following definitions are enlisted:

Strategy

Strategy is the continuous process by which an organisation makes coherent, evidence informed decisions about where to act, how to act and with explicit limitations in order to fulfil its mission within environments characterised by uncertainty, operational limitations and dynamic change.

Leadership and management

Leadership and management comprise the structured coordination of people, organisational culture, resources and decision-making processes that are required to translate strategic intent into actions that are lawful, ethical and effective.

Capability development

Capability development refers to the systematic design, validation and progressive refinement of the scientific, technical, procedural and human competencies that enable an organisation to perform its functions to an accepted, demonstrable standard of reliability and defensibility.

Capacity building

Capacity building refers to the establishment and sustainment of the operational scale, resilience and operational throughput necessary to deliver established capabilities reliably and equitably over time.

Governance

Governance comprises the legal, ethical, scientific, technical and organisational frameworks that provide oversight, assurance and accountability for the exercise of authority and the deployment of technology and evidence. It defines the structures through which decisions are reviewed, risks are managed and the integrity of processes is assured.

Collectively, these five policy and activity domains form an interdependent system, although they do not comprise an exhaustive list. Within science-intensive digital forensic science, deficiency in any single domain has the potential to compromise the reliability of evidentiary processes and, by extension, the integrity of justice outcomes. A robust system does not lie in technological sophistication alone. A robust system relies on the disciplined integration of scientific methodologies, organisational decision making and legal accountability.

Strategy

Strategic approaches are reorienting from traditional, artisanal casework towards frameworks grounded in scientific rigour and pipeline-centric methodologies for digital forensic science processes. Organisations are increasingly prioritising algorithm-assisted analysis, platform-mediated artefacts and provenance-rich exchange. This shift enables evidentiary trust to scale in accordance with increasing volume and complexity (Narratives 1, 3 and 5) [49,50,[100], [101], [102]].

The adoption of cloud and Software-as-a-Service platforms as the default evidentiary environment necessitates strategic planning across the complete workflow. The end-to-end process encompasses ingestion, retention and analytics. These plans must explicitly incorporate warrant procedures and inter-jurisdictional procedures and constraints. Contemporary strategies will ensure that provider telemetry, export mechanisms and log normalisation are elevated to essential strategic assets rather than ad-hoc responses (Narratives 3 and 2) [49,50,52,74,91].

Defensibility functions as a key design feature. Strategic frameworks integrate method governance, such as validation summaries and scope limitations, with decisions regarding the deployment of AI/LLMs and automated pipelines. Programmatic validation, statements of uncertainty and transparency concerning datasets and tools are implemented at early stages in the workflow to mitigate potential risks of disputed evidence (Narratives 1 and 2) [15,32,36,39,51,[103], [104], [105], [106]].

Volatility is now established as a fundamental planning assumption. Strategic plans are designed to incorporate rapid acquisition windows for live memory and transient IoT/OT telemetry. The plans feature pre-arranged legal triggers and platform-specific adapters designed to mitigate data loss resulting from TRIM operations and retention cycles (Narratives 4, 2 and 3) [60,61,63,[107], [108], [109]].

The focus of on-chain investigations is reframed from the employment of analytical tools to investigative pipeline design. The strategies focus on integrating attribution heuristics with permissioned chain-of-custody registers in addition to a schema-first approach to data exchange. These strategies are designed to strengthen continuity, audibility and the chronological sequencing of information across multiple stakeholders, therefore resulting in a more robust integrity and reliability of the investigative process (Narrative 5) [45,70,81,84,86,110,111].

Building strategic capabilities in digital forensic science centres on the development of established reference corpora, workflow-specific verification frameworks and modular adapters. These underpinning elements collectively enable repeatable AI-LLM-enabled triage, ensure the reproducibility of associated tools and enable the design of provider-specific pathways at scale. This approach supports the systematic advancement of organisational strategy that is aligned to the collective, contemporary direction of digital forensic science (Narratives 1, 2, 3 and 4) [14,15,22,100,112,113].

Integrated cross-functional leadership and legal-DFIR frameworks are elevated into organisational strategy. This is evident through the establishment of adjudicative guidance on leaked or compromised data in addition to the formulation of forum rules for authenticity. The guidance and rules inform and drive predetermined workflows that link acquisition, analytics and policy compliance checks (Narratives 2, 3 and 5) [32,50,51,73,93,103].

Risk management strategy centres on the explicit recognition of uncertainty and the delineation of scope limits. Organisations systematically formalise evidence minimisation, ensure dataset representativeness and establish clear separation of model roles. The objective of these measures is to preserve reviewability and proportionality within both cloud-based and blockchain-related contexts (Narratives 1, 2, 3 and 5) [15,32,50,70,114,115].

Executives are responsible for defining and establishing role clarity within the governance framework, ensuring comprehensive documentation and communication protocols that withstand judicial scrutiny. Capability development

In contemporary digital forensic science, capability development initiatives now focus on pipeline-ready methodologies as opposed to ad-hoc tools. Programmes now establish validated workflows, systematically documenting scope limits and associated uncertainties. Further, method change-control procedures are integrated with accreditation procedures. This strategic shift in approach ensures that evidential reliability remains robust and scalable as caseload volume and case complexity continue to increase (Narratives 1 and 2) [14,15,22,114,115].

Agencies are institutionalising reusable reference corpora and verification frameworks for their digital forensic science capabilities. The specific functions include, for example, optical character recognition (OCR), memory acquisition and cloud log parsing, although many agencies are going further. These frameworks support reliable AI/LLM-enabled triage processes and systematic tool improvements. This approach ensures that tool upgrades do not degrade performance standards and they are maintained without regression (Narratives 1, 2, 3 and 4) [27,68,[133], [134], [135]].

Cloud/SaaS is now established as a primary capability domain. The processes of intake, retention and analytics are systematically engineered and integrated from beginning to end. They employ provider-specific adapters, clear pathways for warrant administration, then specify and control minimisation. The final process performs auditable export. This beginning to end approach for cloud/SaaS transforms the platform telemetry into a governed evidentiary environment (Narrative 3) [47,74,91,108,118].

Volatility is regarded as a design feature within system architecture. In order to build capability to meet the challenge of volatility, resources are allocated towards rapid data capture windows, memory-safe acquisition methods in combination with integrated IoT/OT adapters. When fully implemented, these strategies minimise the risks associated with TRIM/retention churn and unpredictable platform updates. Further, capability roadmaps are developed to coordinate data capture toolkits with proficiency testing and the continuous revision of SOPs (Narrative 5) [61,62,107,127,136].

The evolution of blockchain (also referred to as “on-chain”) investigations has seen a shift from discrete investigative tools to integrated investigative pipelines. Contemporary capability investments integrate attribution heuristics with permissioned chain-of-custody registers and implement robust selective disclosure controls. The integrated approach also includes schema-driven data exchange frameworks, such as alignment with CASE/UCO standards, to ensure the preservation of evidentiary continuity and chronological sequencing across multiple stakeholders (Narrative 5) [45,70,71,81,137,138].

Integrated model-role separation and explainability mechanisms within AI/LLM processes ensure that training data, prompts and outputs are traceable, verifiable and reproducible. Effectively implemented capability roadmaps will mitigate the risks associated with tool drift and enable consistent, repeatable analysis (Narratives 2, 3 and 4) [22,108,118].

Governance-coupled capability integrates chain-of-custody-aware logging, versioned SOP libraries and documentation standards specifically designed for legal proceedings within the capability acceptance criteria. Governance

The governance of cloud-native evidence requires a seamless integration of legal authority and technical integrity. The prevalence of platform-mediated digital traces has promoted the importance of log integrity as an element of governance. Integrity assurance is dependent on verifiable logging of every access, modification or transfer of evidence. Further, the logs must be tamper-evident and reviewable. Claims of integrity must be independently reviewable by third parties, including by courts and oversight bodies. The key principle is that assurance is demonstrable without relying on internal trust. Historically, evidential integrity relied on physical custody with locked rooms or sealed media and restricted access. The systematic integration of attribution heuristics, such as address clustering, behavioural patterns and off-chain correlations, in addition to tracing methodologies such as transaction graph analysis and flow tracking, with permissioned chain-of-custody registers and defined disclosure protocols, is becoming more pervasive. Significantly, governance of blockchain-based chain-of-custody solutions improves audibility and accountability, rather than simply providing data immutability. Ephemeral telemetry generated within IoT and edge computing environments establishes a direct relationship between data provenance and governance. Evidential reliability is contingent on models that are capable of preserving event context and linkages across dynamic network layers. Consequently, effective governance defines specific governance responsibilities, including systematically capturing provenance data, establishing correlation rules and documenting defensible uncertainties associated with the evidence (Narratives 2, 3 and 4) [83,108,121,[177], [178], [179]].

Legal guardrails for digital evidence: admissibility, privacy and cross-border access

Legal frameworks and governance conceptualise legal guardrails as a purposefully restrictive mechanism that ensures investigative capabilities (for example, remote or cloud-based acquisition, automation and large-scale analytics) subordinate to statutory authority and judicial standards of evidentiary integrity. It can be concluded from the published literature of this review period that the shift towards science-intensive digital forensic science is not a purely technical development. Instead, it is a policy challenge in which the legitimacy of investigative practices is dependent on the alignment of technical advancements with explicit legal mandates, procedural fairness and demonstrable provenance controls (Policy/Action domains: Strategy; Governance) [15,50,100,114,115,180,181].

Governance guardrails function as assurance obligations that are focused on traceability, auditability and reviewability. They emphasise the importance of demonstrable compliance rather than assuming it. The literature indicates that leadership failure is evident when legality is viewed only as a compliance afterthought rather than as an integrated design element. Treating compliance as an afterthought leads to weak admissibility positions where courts are placed in the position of having to infer, for example, incomplete chain-of-custody narratives instead of relying on reliable information from strong, systematic controls (Policy/Action domains: Governance; Leadership and management) [46,83,86,90,91,[182], [183], [184]].

Lawful access to encrypted data is framed as a strategic “guardrail stress test”. Investigative requirements are balanced against proportionality and necessity. The feasibility of compelled decryption is examined specifically against the risk of normalising systemic vulnerabilities which can be exploited by adversaries at a later time. Key-extraction approaches are positioned as legally consequential as they transfer the burden of trust from devices and users to system processes. Therefore, explicit authorisation thresholds are required, in parallel with transparency of technical limitations and defensible minimisation to prevent excessive collection. These guardrails will serve to mitigate the potential for the risk of later exclusion (Policy/Action domains: Strategy; Governance; Capability development) [180,185,186].

Studies presented during the review period conceptualise access to encrypted data as inseparable from process integrity. A technically successful data extraction does not satisfy legal guardrails unless the procedure is demonstrably repeatable, reviewable and documented with sufficient detail to withstand adversarial scrutiny. Therefore, capability development must emphasise forensic transparency including exhaustive method disclosure, clear statements of estimated error and reproducibility. As a reflection of their importance, these documents should be regarded as a legal imperative, not merely being of scientific value. The alternative leads increased admissibility risk when the processes of extraction cannot be explained meaningfully to the court (Policy/Action domains: Capability Development; Governance) [180,186,187].

The admissibility standards for illegally obtained digital evidence, specifically data that has been hacked or leaked, are widely regarded as structurally unstable across different legal systems. This instability arises as exclusion of such evidence is variously justified on the basis of public policy considerations, the deliberations of tribunals or the application of balancing tests, which will differ between jurisdictions. Consequently, it is critical for strategic governance to anticipate where the evidential value of “proof” may carry less weight, or where enforcement of awards or judgments may be resisted, in the event that the provenance of the evidence is compromised. This can also occur even when the authenticity of the evidence is technically uncontested, making the legality of evidence acquisition a core evidentiary criterion and not a simple investigative ethics issue. The research studies of hacked and leaked evidence indicate that a management obligation exists to formalise decision-making criteria. The addressed criteria include materiality, proportionality, degree of illegality, party involvement and public domain status. The absence of clear organisational doctrine will result in inconsistent practices and unpredictable judicial findings. Therefore, to maintain jurisprudential balance, operational policies, including escalation protocols and documentation templates aimed at preserving contestability and procedural efficiency, are to be implemented (Policy/Action domains: Leadership and Management; Governance; Strategy) [49,50,75,183,188].

The admissibility of OSINT is increasingly recognised as a governance issue. Verifiable authenticity, preservation integrity and sound interpretive methodologies to the standards of regulatory frameworks must be demonstrated by OSINT if it is to be admissible and credible. The literature indicates that OSINT is prompting legal systems to formulate clear standards for data capture tools, manage metadata effectively and establish verification procedures. Further, it is notable that “open sources” cannot simply be “uncontested evidence” under adversarial conditions (Policy/Action domains: Governance; Capability Development) [49,183,[188], [189], [190], [191]].

A consistent finding is the inadequacy of controls when investigators, forensic experts and the courts lack a shared understanding of how digital traces can be manipulated, decontextualised or authenticated. Consequently, building capacity is framed as a critical component of the broader legal system's infrastructure with training for the judiciary, prosecutors and defence counsel specifically targeted at assessing indicators of authenticity and provenance. In the absence of such structured, system-level initiatives, even robustly governed evidence pipelines carry a higher risk of misinterpretation, improper weighting or inadmissibility (Policy/Action domains: Capacity Building; Governance) [30,182,192,193].

Protections articulated in human rights evidentiary rules adapt to the complexities of electronically stored information. Traditional evidence categories, such as hearsay and documentary evidence, are imperfect when applied to digital evidence. This leads to the suggested need for legal reforms, such as specific electronic evidence statutes, to mitigate the risk of unsafe judicial and regulatory findings. Effective reforms will prescribe minimum standards for admissibility that focus on reliability, analytical methods, storage integrity and originator identification (Policy/Action domains: Strategy; Governance) [182,183,[194], [195], [196]].

Data protection regulations, most famously the GDPR but also others, limit and manage sharing of “personal data”, including as digital evidence. The classification of “personal data” forms the legal determination for its handling and claims of anonymisation must account for the risk of re-identification to reach the threshold of legal validity. Consequently, privacy engineering practices that meet legal standards should be included in capability development initiatives (Policy/Action domains: Governance; Capability Development) [[197], [198], [199], [200]].

Privacy-preserving digital forensic science balances investigative needs with individual rights. Excessive disclosure of personal data can undermine the admissibility and credibility of evidence. Therefore, technical measures such as selective disclosure and data minimisation in combination with legal controls such as demonstrable proportionality are presented. From this integrated approach, privacy then becomes the central evidentiary attribute in legal and regulatory contexts (Policy/Action domains: Governance; Capability Development) [89,197,201,202].

The use of blockchain technology in chain of custody processes is seen as a means of strengthening the integrity of evidence management through tamper-evident and auditable systems. However, the preponderance of the relevant literature recognises that the concept of immutability, which is inherent to blockchain, does not equate to admissibility in legal proceedings. Effective governance also addresses identity management, access controls and interpretation of ledger records within a legal framework. In the absence of these measures, blockchain becomes an opaque technical artefact rather than a trusted and reliable assurance mechanism (Policy/Action domains: Governance; Capability Development) [45,74,86,87,91,98,203].

Evidence submission frameworks built on zero-knowledge-proof enabled private blockchain to advance the concept of compliance mechanisms by integrating authenticity verification with the protection of source anonymity. This is critical in situations such as whistle blower contexts. The implication is that future legal standards may need to formally acknowledge cryptographic “proof of integrity” or “proof of provenance” as a functional analogue to conventional evidence disclosure while still preserving contestability, i.e. ensuring that such submissions remain subject to challenge and scrutiny. From a strategic perspective, it is incumbent on organisational leadership to establish policies that delineate situations under which privacy-preserving verification methods is acceptable and how courts should interpret cryptographic attestations (Policy/Action domains: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capability Development; Governance) [45,89,90,201,202,204,205].

Human rights and ethical boundaries

Human rights and ethical parameters are consistently framed as a foundational condition for science-intensive digital forensic science. As investigative reach expands through cloud acquisition, remote access and automated analysis, the principal concern becomes whether technical capability is bounded by explicit legal authority and by demonstrable standards of necessity and proportionality. This is a concerted shift to fill the previous situation of simply following what is convenient for the organisation. Therefore, strategy is conceptualised as “rights-aware design”, where legal and ethical safeguards are integrated into the entire evidence management process, rather than being considered only at the point of disclosure (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance) [182,183,[207], [208], [209], [210], [211], [212]].

Privacy is regarded as a fundamental evidentiary quality attribute rather than simply a compliance requirement. Several articles establish that admissibility and credibility are intrinsically linked to demonstrable data minimisation, targeted acquisition and the separation of irrelevant personal information. Over-collection is a factor that can undermine proportionality, therefore it elevates the risks of exclusion, suppression or reputation. Accordingly, capability development focuses on implementing technical solutions that demonstrate restraint, such as scope-limited data collection, metadata preserving filtering techniques and enforcing auditable access controls. These initiatives are supported by documentation practices that can be tested in an adversarial process (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development) [19,161,197].

In practice, hacked for leaked digital information is a paradigmatic challenge for human rights compliance. Even in situations in which such materials appear to be probative, decision makers must consider the victim's privacy interests in addition to the legal and ethical ramifications associated with an unauthorised acquisition. Therefore, organisational leadership must establish clear operational guidelines that systematically translate discretionary balancing tests (including materiality, degree of intrusiveness, public interest considerations and available alternatives) into operational doctrine. Without such clarity, organisational practice risks becoming inconsistent and courts are left to judge fairness from incomplete evidentiary trails and fragmented decision-making processes (Polices/Actions: Leadership and Management; Governance) [50,75,183,209,210,[213], [214], [215], [216]].

The issue of accessing data across national borders and through third parties is often framed as an ethical boundary problem. The argument is primarily due to, in part or wholly, irreconcilable jurisdictional disparities. Such disparities can lead to data that has been lawfully acquired and preserved in one jurisdiction deemed to be unlawfully acquired by the receiving jurisdiction. To mitigate this risk, protocols for mutual legal assistance, provider request and evidence preservation should be aligned to standards that protect individual rights. Developing organisational capabilities will enable the preservation of provenance and contestability. This approach ensures that legality and authenticity are linked and travel together, rather than being litigated as separable attributes (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capability Development) [91,180,193,197,200,217].

Incorporating privacy-preserving digital forensic science into organisational processes reflects a practical manifestation of human rights considerations. The advanced analytical mechanisms include selective disclosure, cryptographic access controls and privacy-preserving computation which are employed to demonstrate proportionality and to minimise secondary harms. Concurrently, these mechanisms will support accountability through comprehensive auditability. From a governance perspective, the implication is that privacy engineering practices are transparent and comprehensible within judicial settings. Therefore, threat models, minimisation strategies and disclosure protocols all need to be documented to enable review and potential challenge (Policy/Action: Governance; Capability Development) [201,202,218,219].

The employment of AI-enabled decision support systems presents a risk to human rights. One such vulnerability occurs when surveillance converges with comprehensive, indiscriminate data acquisition, then collides with procedural fairness. Learning from the lessons of predictive policing models, there is a demonstrated need to limit the application of high-impact, automated inferences from systems that have not been subjected to independent method validation nor are accountable. Effective leadership should establish expectations for transparency and sound governance that ensures human oversight of algorithmic outputs for investigative targeting and the weighting of evidence (Policy/Action: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Governance) [181,197,[220], [221], [222], [223], [224], [225], [226]].

Explainability and reviewability are controls for ensuring fairness. When analytical results cannot be satisfactorily explained, defence counsel is placed in the unfair position of being unable to effectively challenge the methods used to obtain those results nor identify potential errors. The same principles apply to results generated from AI/LLM systems or complex toolchains. Such a scenario increases the risk of an unsafe court outcome. Therefore, in order to mitigate these risks, a digital forensic science service's methodological capabilities are designed to deliver reproducible results with defined error parameters and intelligible reporting. For the other elements of the criminal justice system, the judiciary, counsel and investigators should also participate in capacity building initiatives that equip them with the ability to evaluate technical claims rather than deferring to a technical authority as their only course of action (Policy/Action: Capability Development; Capacity Building; Governance) [114,116,132,140,210,[227], [228], [229], [230]].

Generative-AI systems introduce new ethical considerations in the context of digital evidence. Routine digital traces, such as search results, summarised content and triage outputs, can be compromised by opaque model behaviour, data leakage or hallucinated information. Even when evidence has been lawfully obtained, there is a risk of privacy exposure along with that of questionable reliability of analytical results. Unless provenance integrity, context and their model's interactions are logged, this risk will persist (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development) [4,222,[231], [232], [233]].

The integrity of evidentiary processes and rights protections may be undermined by deepfakes and synthetic media. Ethical responsibility does not end with identifying manipulation but extends to mitigating the risks of unfair inferences resulting from unverified evidence authenticity. Therefore, mitigating the risks associated with synthetic media is achieved by setting standards for validation and thresholds for expert testimony within a system that is transparent about its detection capabilities [[234], [235], [236]].

OSINT is another evidence type that is legally and ethically challenging. Privacy concerns, contextual dependencies and the risk of misrepresentation remain present despite its publicly available status. Acquisition and preservation processes applied to OSINT are still required to meet acceptable standards as described in governance documentation. Further, the importance of large-scale reliability and disclosure practices cannot be understated, especially regarding matters involving transnational crime. These measures meet the public's expectations of the universal right to a fair trial and confidence in evidence that relies on scientific methodologies (Polices/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capacity Building) [93,188].

Finally, complex ethical challenges surround whistleblowers and confidential sources who require protection, yet their evidence is subject to the principle of contestability. Zero-knowledge proofs and privacy enhancing blockchain were proposed as strategies for simultaneously demonstrating both integrity and anonymity. Therefore, circumstances where cryptographic attestations may replace conventional provenance disclosure practices should be defined in governance frameworks, including:

•policies concerning anonymity thresholds

•criteria for disclosure exceptions and

•the legal interpretation of cryptographic evidence. (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Leadership and Management) [86,89,202].

International standards and compliance requirements

International standards and compliance frameworks serve as the foundational infrastructure that enables the inter-jurisdictional admissibility and transferability of digital evidence. By standardising locally generated digital traces, such as images, logs, open-source intelligence captures in addition to outputs from forensic tools, these frameworks ensure that claims are globally intelligible, verifiable and auditable. This harmonisation mitigates evidentiary fragmentation and lowering transaction costs of mutual legal assistance, joint operations and cooperation with the private sector (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance) [129,188,193].

Compliance is a form of court-legible assurance, rather than an administrative formality. Within a compliance framework, accreditation and validation processes cluster around governance requirements that require laboratories to demonstrate control over their methods, define tool limitations and maintain transparent decision-making processes. Even greater importance is assumed when the laboratory's outputs are subject to adversarial evaluation, which aligns with the broad direction of the review's literature. Organisational legitimacy is demonstrated through its compliance practices rather than relying on traditional institutional authority (Policies/Actions: Governance; Leadership and Management) [33,116,229].

Tool testing is the practical operationalisation of international standards. Baseline testing is the process of translating abstract requirements, such as reliability, repeatability and known limitations, into reviewable artefacts, including test plans, established datasets, test reports and documented conditions. This testing stabilises and reinforces the evidentiary validity of outputs generated by both commercial and open-source tools (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance) [26,116,131,237,238].

When applying open-source tools to digital forensic science processes, the admissibility of their outputs is largely determined by the adoption and employment of ISO-compliant acquisition and processing standards. Data integrity, repeatability and transparency in the way that the evidence is managed, tested and disclosed, i.e. the consistency with ISO-IEC standards and guidelines, will determine legal acceptance. The adoption of and consistency with ISO-IEC standards and guidelines acknowledges that contemporary legal challenges are being directed at software opacity (“closed box” testing and lack of transparency) and uncontrolled tool behaviour (verification and validation); whereas, legal challenges previously focused on the human who was handling the evidence (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development) [26,93,131].

Procedural volatility presents the most significant ethical and legal risks when working with OSINT in mass-atrocity and international criminal investigations. These risks are largely due to the challenge of achieving standardisation at the scale of these matters. User-generated digital evidence is often abundant in these investigations. However, its reliability is frequently uncertain and tribunals have limited precedents regarding its admissibility and use. The proposed remedy is the implementation of governance structures that would institutionalise standardised protocols for the collection, verification and disclosure of evidence among all relevant actors. In so doing, the right to a fair trial is protected and consistency and comparability is enhanced across cases (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capacity Building) [183,[188], [189], [190],241,242].

In the context of cross-border data governance, privacy and data-protection guidance, such as that articulated by GPPR-style regimes, serve as critical jurisdictional constraints. These compliance limitations may restrict the use of data that is otherwise, technically, available, therefore, introducing risks and complexities. Anonymisation, purpose limitation and lawful basis requirements become central governance challenges as they determine whether evidence can be lawfully shared across borders and whether its acquisition could be deemed as a violation of rights. By establishing such compliance limitations, privacy regimes can render otherwise accessible data as unusable or legally precarious. Consequently, these factors ultimately impact on the admissibility and legitimacy of data, highlighting the importance of privacy and data-protection practices in international operations (Policies/Actions: Governance; Strategy) [30,197,199,217,243].

The rapid evolution of cloud-based imaging and API-mediated acquisitions has significantly challenged digital forensic science standards, effectively serving as a stress-test for traditional validation approaches. Cloud service providers frequently change their services, configurations and processes which can compromise the efficacy of known datasets. Consequently, minimum testing expectations have moved from relying on static, pre-deployment assurances such as one-off laboratory validation, to empirical validation at the time of use conducted by experienced analysts. Therefore, the levels of acceptable risk and documentation should be decided as policy at senior leadership levels. Further and perhaps even more importantly, senior leaders are also responsible for establishing the protocols and thresholds for suspending services pending re-evaluation (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Leadership and Management; Governance) [51,116,240].

International standards are legitimate instruments only if the justice system is equipped to critically assess them. In addition, explainability and reviewability are legal security concepts for ensuring fairness. Accordingly, capacity building in digital forensic science must consider the whole justice system that comes into contact with digital evidence. Therefore, capacity building extends beyond the scientific expert and should include judicial officers and legal counsel. Extending capacity in this way will prevent the term “validated” from becoming a rhetorical device that prevents methods from being subject to judicial review. This is especially germane to cases in which tool-generated evidence is complex or probabilistic (Policies/Actions: Capacity Building; Governance) [26,33,93,189,230,244].

Traditional chain-of-custody documentation is perceived as deficient, especially in the management of digital evidence. As discussed in more detail elsewhere in this review, blockchain and Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI) technologies are being applied to engineer compliance properties into chain-of-custody solutions. The properties in question are integrity, traceability, verifiability and audibility, which become challenging and lacking standardisation in circumstances involving distributed, multi-actor evidence lifecycles. The absence of established standards is identified as a primary driver for this shift with technical mechanisms increasingly positioned as governance solutions rather than simply efficiency tools. Consequently, blockchain and SSI are positioned as essential frameworks for ensuring chain-of-custody processes where traditional methods fall short (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance) [83,86,245,246].

The increasing integration of scientific methodologies into digital forensic science elevates measurement science principles, such as managing uncertainty, identifying systematic and instrument related errors and the analysis of error propagation, becoming essential components within compliance frameworks. International standards are now the vehicle by which uncertainty is managed and communicated. The research finds that, in the absence of explicit reasoning regarding validity and errors, assertions of confidence are frequently misconstrued as assertions of accuracy. Such misinterpretations present risks in judicial settings and in cases relying on inter-jurisdictional collaboration (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Capacity Building) [115,[247], [248], [249]].

Lawful access is an autonomous compliance pillar subject to its own governance, controls and assurance requirements. It is not satisfied by technical success. However, the adoption of standardised technical and harmonised requirements enables predictable and rights-bound access, especially during inter-jurisdictional investigations. Therefore, implementing such autonomous compliance interfaces in place of ad hoc procedures will lower the risk of unlawful data acquisition or disputes over evidence validity. In complex legal environments, such as those encountered in cross-jurisdictional investigations, the various stakeholders will need to ensure ongoing collaboration and that policies are aligned to ensure there is an effective implementation of the necessary architecture (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Leadership and Management) [30,180,217,250].

Governance structures for evidence management

Governance structures for evidence management are now recognised as critical system-level assurance mechanisms, well beyond the previously limited role of only meeting documentation requirements. Given the scale and heterogeneity of digital traces, evidentiary defensibility necessitates pre-defined accountability, distinct role separation combined with auditable decision-making processes across the evidence lifecycle. The lifecycle begins with creation, then progresses to acquisition, preservation, storage, analysis, sharing, retention and finally to disposition. This systematic approach ensures evidentiary defensibility and consistency, replacing the traditional reliance on the discretionary actions of individual examiners (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capability Development; Capacity Building; Governance) [81,91,113,213].

From a governance perspective, the principle of ‘defensibility first’ dictates that legal compliance is an engineering requirement within evidence management systems. Therefore, the various elements, such as change control, systematic versioning of methods and datasets in concert with beginning to end traceability, form the organisational foundations. Consequently, these elements ensure that system reliability can be assessed as part of the judicial process (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capability Development; Capacity Building; Governance) [14,91,113,114,251,252].

Introducing AI/LLM technologies into digital forensic science presents numerous governance issues. For example, integrating AI/LLM technologies into triage processes inserts delegated judgement into the process which is, therefore, an issue of governance. Model outputs increasingly shape task prioritisation and the tempo of investigative activities, whereas governance frameworks require defined owner and operator accountability. Further, good governance requires decision logging that separates recommendations proposed by models from those arising from human judgement. In addition, controls are required to manage the handling of privacy-impacting data generated by consumer and enterprise LLM systems (Polices/Actions: Governance; Leadership and Management) [45,181,221,222,253].

Governance structures should address categories of contested provenance, for example, evidence that has been hacked or leaked. This is accomplished by specifying admissibility-relevant tests for integrity, authenticity and lawful seizure. Further, the structures account for disclosure protocols and processes for reviewable evidentiary decision-making and are consistent across portfolios who may each be working to a different set of priorities (Policies/Actions: Governance; Leadership and Management) [49,50,81,181,197,213].

Chain of custody governance is inseparable from technical timestamping, integrity verification and the use of structured metadata schemas. Distributed digital traces and their repeated copying, a feature of contemporary digital forensic science, especially in IoT/OT ecosystems, have rendered the traditional notion of “original evidence” somewhat moot. To respond appropriately to these challenges, organisations should develop frameworks that integrate innovations in auditable chain of custody systems. These innovative systems would feature log chronology, transfer accountability and provenance in addition to complying with privacy regulations and practices (Policies/Actions: Governance) [81,86,91,156,252].

When blockchain is implemented as an integrity service for evidence registers, the register becomes a socio-technical control rather than a technological guarantee. Its evidentiary value is dependent on key management, privacy-focused design and independent auditability. Further, the methods for preserving evidence should be transparent to the judicial process and align with established chain-of-custody norms (Policies/Actions: Governance) [91,111,203].

Governance structures in blockchain investigations formalise the communication of probabilistic attribution and heuristic clustering when presented as evidence. The protocols will include documented confidence thresholds, methodological limitations and escalation procedures for legal review. The volatility of crypto ecosystems and the complexities of inter-jurisdictional exchange elevate the risk of over-interpretation, highlighting the importance of these governance features (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capability Development) [114,158,180,254].

The default approach to evidence management is now SaaS which means that governance is provider-mediated, no longer device-centric control. Therefore, coordination across the organisation's various functions that are involved in digital forensic science, i.e. digital forensic science, DFIR, legal and privacy teams, becomes essential. In addition, similarly to lawful access, protocols for inter-jurisdictional handling need to be pre-negotiated. Therefore, it is necessary to adequately resource forensic readiness to ensure that the organisation's relevant capabilities are able to withstand provider changes and API volatility (Policies/Actions: Leadership and Management; Governance; Strategy) [91,121,180].

Implementing a governance-focused model for cloud logs and enterprise telemetry requires evidence-warehouse controls that are the equivalent to those employed in laboratory settings. A successful implementation will be predicated on and support the “collect once, use many times” principle. The key elements comprise normalisation pipelines, access controls, query audit trails and integrity attestations. However, the design phase should note the caution that the enhanced capacity for data reuse does not increase the risk of uncontrolled secondary disclosure or silent integrity drift (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Capacity Building; Governance; Leadership and Management) [91,121,197].

The collection of vehicle telemetry data is becoming routine. However, these collections contain, for example, locational data and digital traces generated by LLMs which are more forcefully colliding with privacy and data protection obligations, therefore heightening governance requirements. Therefore, the principles of proportionality, retention minimisation and definitional clarity around “personal data” and anonymisation limits must be integrated into the evidence handling policies of governance frameworks. Such measures will meet the expectations of the judicial system that personal data will not be used in breach of prevailing regulatory and judicial standards (Leadership and Management; Governance) [52,197,222,255].

Governance of evidence management in IoT environments begins at the point of creation and includes early preservation. At these early stages is where evidential fragility and operational risk is greatest, especially in safety-critical systems, due to the volatile, distributed configuration of these systems. Therefore, preservation infrastructures and procedures, comprising integrated storage and integrity controls, require validated procedures. Further, role-based escalation protocols should be supported by documented justification for intervention (Policies/Actions: Leadership and Management; Governance) [70,71,84,156,179].

The contemporary reality is that probative digital evidence is, by default, managed by third parties or private persons. The search, seizure and disclosure process is adapting to that technical reality which should be reflected in governance arrangements, especially as the probative digital traces can be intermingled with privileged and privacy-protected material. Therefore, the inclusion of standardised processes for filtering and review with delineated decision-making authorities must feature within the governance documentation. The public health context has more experience and therefore a more mature approach to the technology-enabled practices in the management of sensitive data. Digital forensic science can draw from that knowledge base to ensure that sensitive data is acceptably and effectively managed (Policies/Actions: Governance; Leadership and Management) [114,197,213,256].

The policy intent of governance is actualised through organisational design. Common risks, such as ambiguous lines of accountability, deficient change control and inconsistent validation procedures, are mitigated by distinguishing between functional roles, including evidence custodianship, analytical decision-making, tool maintenance and oversight of legal and privacy elements. An additional benefit is realised with capacity building which is strengthened on the accessibility and transferability of SOPs and governance templates (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capacity Building; Governance) [91,113,121].

Organisational-level system-assurance depends on governance that ensures uncertainty is both transparent and reviewable, which is accomplished by documenting expert judgement and method limitations. The growing reliance on automation in digital forensic science processes and distributed evidence ecosystems is accompanied by an increased risk that closed box decisions will be perceived as arbitrary, not based on scientific principles. Consequently, method validation and uncertainty management practices based on transparency principles are essential components of governance that promotes sustained admissibility (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance) [14,15,50,114].

AI/LLM-enabled digital forensic science

Over the review period, 66 articles relevant to AI/LLM were reviewed. The collection's prevailing focus was on capability development which highlights a rapid acceleration of methodological innovation. However, consistent deficiencies emerged in the:

•depth of validation

depth of validation

•extent of cross platform coverage and

•clarity of governance.

clarity of governance.

These deficiencies suggest the need for transition strategies that place equal emphasis on strengthening assurance capacity in parallel with advancing technical capabilities (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Capability Development).

AI/LLM enablement is operationalised through pipelines, with integrated models, that fuse text, image, audio and structured data. These integrated pipelines accelerate multiple tasks within the digital forensic science process, including automated pattern recognition and classification, triage, semantic grouping and early lead identification. The model-enabled workflow aligns with the prevailing research findings of the importance of repeatable methodologies, scalability and interoperability. Case studies in automation illustrate how the combination of multimodal LLMs with OCR and user interface analysis enables the execution of plain-language instructions on real devices that result in repeatable digital trace-to-action mappings. However, divergences between emulator and user devices in addition to inconsistencies within user interfaces can undermine attribution unless validation is conducted on representative devices (Policies/Actions: Capability development; Capacity Building; Governance) [154,155,253,257,258].

Defensibility of AI-enabled analysis is predicated on treating AI-enabled outputs as the results of established methods. The assurance of these methods involves validation using representative datasets and have explicitly documented uncertainties and limitations. Following the principle that documented accreditation-compliance and transparent evaluative reasoning are prerequisites for judicial confidence in AI-assisted analysis, the traceability between the reported findings and the specific model versions employed to obtain those results must be documented and reproducible (Policies/Actions: Governance’ Capability development) [14,31,40,114,115,[259], [260], [261]].

The principal governance framework for deploying AI/LLM-enabled workflows is based on the owner-operator model. Therefore, governance requirements include recorded provenance of training data, controlled change management, continuous monitoring for model drift and auditable logging. Human oversight is demonstrated using decision records that delineate examiner judgement and decisions from model suggestions. Such records also serve to mitigate the limitations of explainability, particularly when findings are contested (Policies/Actions: Governance’ Leadership and Management) [5,141,233,248,262].

The integration of LLM-enabled mobile applications introduces new avenues for evidence collection in addition to new risks. Some services, such as ChatGPT and Copilot, preserve conversational records and metadata directly on Android and iOS devices themselves, often in plaintext caches or databases, whereas others, such as Gemini, aggregate conversation, browser data and images through cloud-based exports. In response, agencies are moving towards hybrid handset-plus-provider acquisition strategies. However, these practices raise privacy and location data exposure concerns even when location services have been disabled. Therefore, data-minimisation controls are fundamental implementation requirements, not just optional security measures (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability development) [222,231].

In SaaS-by-default operations, the strategic value derived from AI/LLM depends on the capacity to ingest, standardise and correlate log data at enterprise scale. Architectures based on the principle of ‘collect once, reuse many times’ have become more attractive. However, such architectures create additional risk exposure around data integrity and change management. Implementing blockchain-anchored, publicly auditable systems demonstrate effective controls by rendering log-evidence tamper evident and selectively verifiable. These approaches prevent the exposure of sensitive information while also supporting later analytics processes (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance) [47,74,88,91,108,111,121,202].

Within the domains of IoT/OT and ephemeral telemetry, explainability and the establishment of performance baselines are recognised as key operational enablers. Current research in IoT focuses on integrating explainable AI with established information quality standards, while also ensuring the significance of precise timing data for imaging and system restoration. Further, the development and use of attribution-labelled datasets, specifically structured for federated learning approaches, support benchmarking and comparative evaluation across diverse operational locations within the requirements of real-world deployment (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Capacity Building) [83,112,127,146,177,[263], [264], [265]].

Multimedia authenticity

Multimedia authenticity testing is the evaluation of the provenance, integrity and reliability of audio-visual and image-based evidence, which is especially important in contexts where manipulated and synthetic media are not self-authenticating. It is noted that another review in this volume takes a deeper examination of this subject. (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Strategy; Governance) [177,236,[268], [269], [270], [271], [272], [273]].

There is a distinction in workflow between investigative screening and forensic examination of multimedia. The priorities for investigative screening are high recall rates, rapid processing and extensive media collections, whereas the priorities for forensic examination are defensible attribution and controlled error rates. For forensic analysis of source-camera determination, evaluation is based on investigation-oriented recall and runtime rather than verification-oriented error rates. Verification thresholds are then held for formal, court admissible evaluations (Policies/Actions: Capacity Building; Strategy; Capability Development) [257,274,275].

Clarifying the boundary between integrity and authenticity will prevent some avenues for potential overstatement in opinion evidence. Media authentication is the assessment of whether or not a given media item is an accurate representation according to established criteria. In contrast, integrity, as demonstrated by hashing, confirms that a file matches its source, however, it cannot provide assurance of the events depicted. Consequently, reporting language and decision logic should:

•separate file integrity and the continuity of handling from

separate file integrity and the continuity of handling from

•content authenticity.

content authenticity.

Further, it should also be acknowledged that files obtained directly from a camera may capture scenes that have been staged or reenacted (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Capacity Building) [35,36].

Contemporary approaches for deepfake detection are focused on explainability and temporal modelling which recognise their importance in establishing evidence reliability. Some examples include:

•TAENet, a two-branch autoencoder that produces explanatory maps for both authentic and manipulated content and

Submissions with an uncertain chain of custody, for example those submitted by the public, should be evaluated for originality before more detailed analysis is conducted. Acquisition and triage procedures should reflect the practical limitations of authenticating individual items. A single image can be subjected to manipulations that are undetectable to trained experts. Therefore ideally, a series of images depicting the same subject or video should be tested for authentication, although this is not always feasible. Articulating minimum quality and quantity criteria and recording escalation decisions will support defensibility. Taking this approach, when presented with circumstances in which insufficient questioned materials are available, the risk of evidentiary overreach is reduced (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Capability Development; Governance) [35,36].

Both the structure and context, not just metadata, of the questioned image are features of authenticity examinations. Therefore, image authentication examinations consider both the scene content and the non-scene image attributes such as file type, compression techniques, metadata, container or hexadecimal structure and noise signatures such as PRNU for example. It should also be acknowledged that metadata can be incomplete, absent or subject to manipulation (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance; Capacity Building) [35,[276], [277], [278]].

Anti-forensics techniques challenge both scalability and defensibility. The ExS-GAN model targets multiple manipulation techniques by seeking to remove forensic identifiers while preserving the apparent content. This deliberate deception technique endeavours to deceive both automated detection systems and human experts. Therefore, as anti-forensic techniques are adversary-adaptive, continuous adversarial testing, drift monitoring and controlled model updates are needed (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance) [269,270,273,279].

A recurring theme of this review is the importance of system-level assurance in determining the authenticity of digital evidence. Blockchain-based public auditing supports verification for shared-cloud operation logs without requiring complete downloading, which mitigates the risk of collusion between CSPs and users. When combined with transparent and accountable oversight, authenticity determinations are substantiated with auditable integrity controls (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Leadership and Management; Governance) [81,86,91,98].

Adversarial modification attacks targeted to detector attention regions can degrade the efficacy of detectors. Moreover, these techniques are transferable across a range of detectors while remaining imperceptible. Therefore, adversarial testing protocols that address modification attacks should be integrated into quality assurance and validation (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Strategy) [280].

Verifying multimedia authenticity relies on human judgement amid uncertainty. The contemporary concept of “imposter bias” refers to when, due to the prevalence of generative AI, a forensic examiner systematically doubts the authenticity of multimedia content assuming that it has been generated or manipulated using AI tools. This bias introduces significant consequential risks, such as the potential for erroneous judgments and false accusations or the misclassification of real context as fake. Objective, standardised assessment procedures and comprehensive staff training, underpinned by ethical and legal guidelines are effective mitigation strategies (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Leadership and Management; Governance) [23,36,236,281].

Cognitive bias mitigation should be embedded as an operational requirement within authentication processes, rather than being a supplementary compliance function. Best practices recognise that authentication involves some subjectivity in evaluating scene content. Therefore, organisational controls should be in place such as:

•maintaining contemporaneous records

•use of standardised forms and checklists and

use of standardised forms and checklists and

•independent review by personnel with comparable expertise.

Effective implementation will also manage the workload and examiner wellbeing as components of error prevention and quality assurance, as examiner fatigue and contextual contamination present risks to reliability (Policies/Actions: Leadership and Management; Capacity Building; Governance; Capability Development) [18,23,35].

Cloud/SaaS and log digital forensic science

During the period spanning 2023-2025, the adoption of cloud/SaaS and log digital forensic science represented a paradigm shift from traditional end recovery towards evidence management facilitated by services providers. This transition was well represented in the published literature with the prevailing trend in favour of SaaS by default evidence models. This is not an upgrade in technology, it represents an organisational and governance shift (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capability Development; Leadership and Management) [51,52,91,108,121].

The main technical advancement in cloud/SaaS and log digital forensic science is that the forensic analysis has moved beyond the analysis of simple retained logs. The consensus of the literature is that conventional logs are insufficient for answering forensic questions and correlating events across disparate systems. Conventional logs generally record the observable event, “what happened?“, such as a login, a file accessed, process started etc. But they do not reliably reveal the underlying cause, “why did it happen?“, "what triggered the event?", "which process or user caused it?", "whether it was automated or consequent to another action?". To overcome the limitations of traditional logs, systems need structures, such as provenance tracking, causal graphs and event-dependency chains, that track causal relationships between events to improve event reconstruction. Causality-aware tracking records the relationships between events, such as which event triggered another and how the events are connected in a sequence. It includes features such as correlation/trace identification using unique identifiers that tie related events together and the ability to follow a trace across processes, systems or service and linking logs from different sources into a coherent storyline; parent-child event relationships (the hierarchical relationship between events); and consistent contextual metadata such as metadata, user or process identifiers, system state, environment variables in addition to source and destination information. However, the main challenge in this area is one of scalability, i.e. managing the size of the log corpus. Therefore, the capability development priority is to design efficient logging requirements rather than to focus solely on subsequent processes such as log parsing and SIEM integration (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance; Capacity Building) [51,91,108,121,282].

Independent auditing of logging data integrity improves the reliability of cloud-based evidence. Log-less verification in parallel with privacy protection and ensuring tamper resistance is one approach that supports this process. These measures will mitigate the risk of evidence inadmissibility which might occur when reliant on cloud service provider logs alone. Therefore, prior to analysing any log data, the logs must be independently verified to ensure they are authentic and unaltered. They cannot be simply trusted as provided by the cloud service provider (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Strategy) [51,91,108,121].

Several articles note that vehicle-cloud evidence is not acquired from a single, centralised source. It is obtained from many different applications and manufacturers, each of which collects and stores its own version of vehicle-cloud information. In situations where telemetry or account-related data in a device is inaccessible or compromised on the device, that information can still be obtained directly from the cloud using API-based acquisition tools. However, the solutions are not straight forward as operational challenges persist including lawful access, the reliance on credentials or tokens, API limitations and the diversity of provider systems. As certain forensic processes rely on external systems, providers or cloud-based data sources, SOPs are required to manage those dependencies (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Leadership and Management; Governance) [52,180,[283], [284], [285]].

Forensic readiness for cloud/SaaS environments requires not only lawful access SOPs, but also the ability to investigate compromises that affect cloud applications, including threats through add-ons and extensions. Reliable cloud-digital forensic science frameworks feature logging depth, provenance of digital traces and a defensible incident reconstruction model. Therefore, cloud/SaaS digital forensic science processes should be supported by governance documentation for SOPs and validated investigative workflows (Policies/Actions: Capability development; Governance) [51,91,286].

Cloud and SaaS data acquisition increasingly relies on session states, cookies and authentication tokens stored in trusted devices rather than relying on static account credentials alone. This means that browser-resident artefacts can facilitate access to cloud-based evidence. This is possible even when traditional security measures are in place, such as multi-factor authentication, device trust and re-authentication controls. Normally, these controls would prevent lawful data collection when only usernames and passwords were being used. Therefore, credential migration can be effective across a broad range of browsers and can support access to web services. It should be noted that the acquisition pathway and credential management are inseparable, therefore, specific legal authority will be required prior to commencement of acquisition when employing these techniques. The acquisition procedures will need to be narrowly defined and auditable (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Leadership and Management) [51,170,171].

Alterations to the browser version, provider authentication protocols or API updates can disrupt the reliability of cloud/SaaS evidence acquisition. If browser versions diverge, credentials can fail when migrated across platforms due to data structure and credential security measures changes. Therefore, ensuring repeatability will require a controlled version alignment and compatibility assessment. As has been shown, CSPs continually update their platforms, therefore they present a predictable integrity and operational risk that requires monitoring and regression testing of acquisition tools. These measures will preserve defensibility and avoid the reliance on reactive trouble shooting (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Strategy) [170,171].

When examiners work within cloud systems, they log into cloud dashboards, navigate provider portals, use web interfaces to retrieve logs or data, trigger API-based exports and interact with live, operational systems. These actions introduce human factors’ risks, such as altering timestamps, trigger sync events, change account states, modify logs or metadata and more, which become a threat to evidential integrity. Acquisition and preservation models require SOPs for image acquisition, data transfer, hashing and preservation controls. In addition, as legal and personal data protections impose significant limitations beyond those in physical evidence collection standards, they also must be documented in the SOPs. These procedures should also be accessible and easily explainable in judicial proceedings (Capability Development; Capacity Building; Governance) [51,122,239].

When transitioning to the cloud/SaaS and log digital forensic science capability, the adoption of cloud forensic readiness is a core organisational operating model. The persistent challenges include multi-jurisdictional data locations, dependence on the trustworthiness of CSPs, latency in formal communication channels and insufficient transparency. In order to meet the challenges of cloud-based evidence, such as the complex dependencies, access controls and provider-mediated processes, a formal request management architecture is advisable. An example of such a system is CLERMS, which offers a transparent workflow supporting forensic readiness through coordination, documentation and automation of how evidence is requested, accessed and preserved. In turn, this supports the principle of system-level assurance by connecting points of congestion in the legal process with technical decisions regarding queueing, monitoring and case management (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capacity Building; Governance) [51,108,121].

Enterprise collaboration platforms are now used for the distribution of evidential data across multiple endpoints including local caches, cloud accounts and provider logs. When analysing collaboration platforms such as Cisco Webex, no single source of evidence is complete on its own. Achieving event completeness is dependent on correlating evidence from multiple platforms and ensuring that the acquisition and retention practices align with the service provider structures and its data. Consequently, due to this complexity and variability, ad hoc methods and case by case improvisation is unreliable. Digital forensic science service providers must adopt standardised protocols and training that formalise how cloud, API and multi-platform evidence is acquired [170,287].

The most implementation ready framework for cloud/SaaS and log digital forensic science described during the period of the review is the lifecycle assurance discipline. This approach specifies:

•preservation requests

preservation requests

•management of legal scope, acquisition options for providers, custodians or APIs

management of legal scope, acquisition options for providers, custodians or APIs

•provenance checks and verification from independent sources

provenance checks and verification from independent sources

•contemporaneous documentation

contemporaneous documentation

•completeness verification

completeness verification

•cryptographic verification and

cryptographic verification and

•cloud-specific timestamp interpretation.

cloud-specific timestamp interpretation.

The most valuable insights and deepest understanding are gained from the cloud/SaaS and log digital forensic science process when the raw technical components are synthesised with their associated metadata and aggregated with findings from multiple sources. However, scalable implementation of cloud-based acquisition cannot be achieved just by adding tools or automating steps. Scalable defensible practice requires formal SOPs and appropriate training that consistently and safely guides examiners through cloud-acquisition methods that withstands scrutiny [51,239,240].

Blockchain and cryptocurrency evidence

Blockchain and cryptocurrency represents a substantial proportion of the published digital forensic science literature during the review period. It is regarded as a substantive technical domain and, obviously, feeds heavily into narrative 5, but also into narratives 4 and 3. In respect of policies and actions, the literature focus is dominated by capability development accompanied by contributions from other categories. Consequently, planning for transition should consider both technical and organisational aspects.

Blockchain digital forensic science is conceptualised as serving two principal functions:

1.investigative platform for tracking transactions, reconstructing provenance and analysing behavioural patterns and

investigative platform for tracking transactions, reconstructing provenance and analysing behavioural patterns and

2.integrity assurance service in which it facilitates the maintenance of audit logs and evidence registries.

integrity assurance service in which it facilitates the maintenance of audit logs and evidence registries.

Accordingly, implementation of blockchain digital forensic science capabilities should distinguish the validation of analytical methodologies from the design and governance of registry systems. This is particularly important in environments involving cloud-based logs and IoT ecosystems (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capability Development) [84,86,91,138,[288], [289], [290]].

Contemporary investigative practice is becoming more science-intensive which is characterised by the application of scientific methodologies and, also, hypothesis driven approaches. Advanced analytical techniques, such as wallet clustering, peeling-chain pattern analysis and interpretation of mixer activity, improve the generation of traces and the prioritisation of transactional traces. However, these methodologies are heuristic in nature, probabilistic and remain subject to challenge. Therefore, investigative reports need to differentiate objective observations of ledgers from attributional assertions, then to clearly articulate uncertainty in language appropriate to judicial contexts (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capacity Building; Capability Development) [137,138,158,254,266].

The dynamic nature of blockchain investigative methods is susceptible to lifecycle risk due to the evolution of transaction patterns, address usage, wallet behaviours and privacy features. Therefore, continuous monitoring, systematic regression testing and scheduled revalidation becomes a requirement. Version control is to be maintained that links every case output to the specific analytic method and parameter set used. Conservative reporting conventions should be adopted when uncertainty cannot be clarified (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development) [137,138,158,266].

Proprietary analytics platforms and vendor developed heuristics are the backbone infrastructure for blockchain investigations. This reliance presents a defensibility risk if investigators are unable to explain the assumptions, error characteristics, version histories and reproducibility of results. Therefore, chain analytics should be subject to governance arrangements in the same way as other procedures which impact investigation outcomes. These SOPs will cover controlled configuration, methodological limitations, independent verification and procurement processes that support auditability and long-term reproducibility (Policies/Actions: Governance; Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capability Development) [137,158,254].

The benefits of blockchain investigation findings to the justice system are realised when they are substantiated by validated methods that have been subjected to independent corroboration in combination with practices that include jurisdiction-specific requirements for recording and examination. Relying on cryptographic assertions as self-authenticating facts alone is insufficient attestation for evidence reliability, whereas adhering to the validated standards supports the reliability of evidence, its defensibility and judicial confidence. This is becoming increasingly important especially with the growing frequency of cross-chain activity in addition to the greater complexity and ambiguity resulting from privacy tools (Policies/Actions: Governance; Strategy; Capability Development) [137,138,158,203,254].

Blockchain-based preservation does not substantiate the authenticity or legality of the original data generated before capture. Accordingly, blockchain-preserved evidence should be considered as part of a broader admissibility chain that requires documentation of the context, maintenance of platform neutrality, reproducible acquisition workflows and delineation of the scope and limitations of blockchain-based verification. This approach is especially relevant when third-party evidence platforms are used (Policies/Actions: Governance; Strategy) [91,203,254].

Blockchain investigations realise a greater value when the analysis is conducted within an integrated framework that connects ledger data with relevant off-chain evidence. This additional evidence can include KYC (know your customer) identity data, IP or access logs, communications, financial intelligence reporting and device artefacts such as wallet application data and records of seed management. This level of integration requires pre-negotiated, lawful access arrangements, jurisdiction specific request templates and standardised documentation of provenance for off-chain information and the applied analytical processes. In the absence of this integration, there is an increased risk of “address-level” or “cluster-level” findings being overstated as definitive evidence of an individual's identity (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Leadership and Management; Capability Development; Capacity Building) [158,254].

Operational scalability is facilitated by ecosystem architectures that integrate blockchain technology with cloud computing, IoT systems and storage systems. These architectures encompass functions such as log-integrity auditing, IoT evidence preservation and programmable data management approaches. However, despite these advances, enduring limitations are apparent including insufficient depth of validation, challenges with interoperability, insufficient cross-platform coverage and lack of governance clarity. If expansion is pursued prior to maturation of these methodologies, significant risks are likely to compromise system-level assurance (Capability Development; Governance) [84,86,91,179,202,289,290].

Blockchain-based registries and integrity services should be designed for interoperability with established evidence management systems, case management procedures and multi-domain log pipelines, such as those used in cloud computing, IoT and telecommunications. Access control, auditability and cross-organisational evidence exchange should be specified early as the design choices underpin independent verifiability (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Governance; Capability Development; Capacity Building) [86,91,179,288,290].

The processes of implementation and transition are governance heavy. Agencies will need to establish specialised teams that work closely with financial intelligence operations and evidence custodians. A phased pilot strategy should be adopted that incorporates fail-safe fallback custody processes. The chain-of-custody architectures should be documented and cover preservation frameworks, self-sovereign identity protocols and privacy preserving verification methods. The systems will also need to be interoperable with defined migration strategies to underpin evidence admissibility (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capacity Building; Governance; Capability Development) [84,86,91,179,202,203,254,289].

Evidence handling is inseparable from operational security when seizing, freezing or controlling cryptocurrency assets. Irreversible loss, unauthorised transfer or compromised evidential integrity can result from mishandling asset credentials, seed phrases or signing devices. Therefore, the most effective custody models feature separation of duties, auditable signing protocols and SOPs for evidence chain-of-custody and continuity when under asset control. Effective SOPs will feature contingency plans for compromised keys and records of any asset movement under legal authority (Policies/Actions: Governance, Leadership and Management; Capability Development) [86,254].

Mobile/app centric digital forensic science

The mobile device and app centric domain is one of the most heavily researched and published areas of digital forensic science. The dominant emphasis is advancing forensic capabilities. However, persistent challenges remain in strategic implementation, interoperability and governance. Further, the discipline extends well beyond handset-specific issues with overlaps into cloud/SaaS, vehicular systems, blockchain technologies and automation.

The technical specifications within the mobile device and app-centric domain are characterised by evidential fragmentation arising from variations in device hardware, operating system versioning, application architectures, update cycles, encryption states and, as a reflection of continued technological development, cloud-associated artefacts. As a consequence, the acquisition strategy should be determined and recorded as a hypothesis-driven, source-prioritised process instead of the traditional, singular comprehensive acquisition approach. This contemporary methodology has a direct impact on the reproducibility of results and the ability to clearly explain findings during judicial proceedings (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Capacity Building; Leadership and Management; Strategy; Governance) [143,[291], [292], [293], [294], [295], [296], [297]].

The foremost technical advancement in the field is not simply an increase in the quantity of recovered data. The primary innovation lies in the systematic structuring of evidence, where specific application and platform methodologies transform complex mobile digital traces into timelines that are both transparent and reproducible, while also resolving peer and account relationships in addition to establishing provenance linkages. Further, these methodologies also reveal negative findings, such as the absence of specific content types or environment-dependent decryption prerequisites which, consequently, strengthens both the reliability and defensibility of the evidentiary process (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance; Strategy) [[298], [299], [300]].

Contemporary investigative practice is undergoing a transformation from exclusively device seizure to the adoption of a more risk-aware, context-led interpretation of mobile evidence. The literature demonstrates that excessive dependence on mobile phone data, without integrated analytical frameworks, increases the likelihood of failures related to disclosure, interpretation and quality. The risks become more pronounced when first responders interact with devices and have the potential to inadvertently alter evidence or to prompt alerts to perpetrators, with particularly serious potential consequences in cases involving vulnerable persons. In order to mitigate these risks, mobile and application capabilities are systematically integrated into investigative doctrine, supervision and operational protocols (Policies/Actions: Governance; Leadership and Management; Capacity Building; Strategy; Capability Development) [78,[292], [293], [294],[301], [302], [303], [304], [305], [306]].

The scalability of operations in mobile device and application-centric forensic science is primarily facilitated by integrated automation, systematic triage and adaptive parsing. However, the preponderance of the literature in this domain indicates that the extent of scalability is conditional of the depth of validation processes and the test data's currency and relevance. The collective findings converge on improvements in repeatability and interoperability are achievable in contemporary practice, whereas reliance on generic, commercial tools may not keep pace with necessary adaptations to emerging applications and file structures. Therefore, flexible detection logic, continuous updating to data sets and the controlled use of ML/LLM-assisted artefact discovery is key to maintaining an effective capability (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Strategy; Capacity Building; Governance) [14,15,22,141,257,258,291].

System-level assurance in the justice system depends on effective governance frameworks for evidence acquisition through both application and API integrations, not just focusing on technical efficacy. The motor vehicle/API element of this domain demonstrates dual advantages for investigations:

1.expanded access to remote data and improved logging transparency and

expanded access to remote data and improved logging transparency and

2.explicit legal authority with clear provenance of credentials, proportionality in data collection and validation prior to attributing evidential weight.

explicit legal authority with clear provenance of credentials, proportionality in data collection and validation prior to attributing evidential weight.

The governance requirements are directly analogous to mobile cloud-connected application ecosystems (Policies/Actions: Governance; Strategy; Capability Development; Leadership and Management; Strategy) [52,255,283,307].

The advantages conferred by technical advancement are optimised when agencies integrate mobile and application-based solutions with integrity controls and well-defined legal framing. This approach supports the use of cryptographically secured and automated acquisition records, ensures explicit consideration of admissibility and evidentiary standards for application-based communications in addition to implementing policy controls for the management of privacy sensitive mobile application digital traces, including location and cloud account data. Collectively, these features provide confidence to the judicial system while limiting the potential for misuse or overreach (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance; Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capacity Building) [45,86,91,115,222,252,298,299,[308], [309], [310], [311]].

When implementing a new mobile and application-centric digital forensic science capability or upgrading an existing capability, the literature collectively prescribes a strategic portfolio approach that encompasses the five policy and action domains. The strategic framework should outline the prioritisation of evidence sources and delineate the legal pathways for the acquisition of data from devices, applications and cloud platforms. Leadership and management are responsible for aligning investigative demand with established laboratory quality standards. In parallel, capability development and capacity building initiatives are focused on maintaining currency with evolving methods, training and documentation. Finally, governance activities will institutionalise validation processes and communication of uncertainty while ensuring that auditability forms the foundation for scalable and defensible operational practices [14,15,81,86,91,121,146,312].

Automation, data reduction and triage

As a proportion of the literature pertaining to digital forensic science that was published during the review period, automation, scalability and triage is the smallest of the defined technical domains. Capability development was the most dominant focus of the research suggesting a prevailing transition that was driven by engineering priorities.

Automation is conceptualised as an integrated, end-to-end pipeline attribute that enhances process repeatability and promotes interoperability while ensuring reliable and consistent workflow transfer when under operational demand. This approach reduces or replaces the requirement for customised integration by easing the incorporation of new analytics solutions with minimal disruption to ongoing processes (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Capacity Building) [55,313,314].

Model-assisted triage is conceptualised as an effective tool to improve the efficiency of initial screening and prioritisation of digital traces. The consensus view promoted in the literature is that the processes are to be treated as formal methods and, therefore, are subject to validation procedures, systematic documentation of decisions and continual human oversight to ensure accountability (Policies/Actions: Governance; Accountability) [100,257,258,275,315,316].

The process of data reduction involves strengthened heterogenous digital traces standardised into a cohesive, searchable trace model. This approach enables:

•large-scale querying without the need for repeated acquisition and

large-scale querying without the need for repeated acquisition and

•selective, explainable analytical strategies.

selective, explainable analytical strategies.

One such operational example is Hansken which performs large-scale digital trace searching supported by AI transcription and recognition. Standardisation is achieved using CASE which facilitates interoperability [55,148,257,312,314,317].

Techniques for detecting content similarity and efficient hash-based retrieval are positioned as fundamental infrastructure for de-duplication, clustering and prioritisation within large malware or media collections. However, assertions of scalability and error rates must be substantiated by reproducible empirical validation to withstand challenge (Policies/Actions: Capability Development; Governance) [[318], [319], [320], [321], [322], [323]].

Edge and fog triage approaches are designed to reduce the time-to-insight and minimise the laboratory workload by conducting data processing closer to the original source. On the other hand, these approaches place greater demands on admissibility processes as results obtained from expeditious filtering must also be assured that data integrity has been both preserved and justified through documented criteria. These decisions are subject to later review (Capability Building; Governance) [144,252,257,258,275,315].

Effective automation, data reduction and triage systems confer significant advantage to the justice system and its member constituents, the most significant being timeliness and proportionality. Scalable triage processes alleviate case backlogs and narrow analysis to probative material. The risk, however, is that these same efficiency measures can obscure exculpatory digital traces, propagate bias, hallucinate evidence or provide incomplete summaries. To mitigate these risks, limitations and uncertainties should be transparent and communicated, especially to actors who may lack technical expertise (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Governance) [100,257,275,324].

The effectiveness of this capability is initially dependent on the foundational elements, ie. the strategic selections of the infrastructure and the operating model. Key considerations are processes for centralised intake for data normalisation, such as forensic warehouse frameworks consistent with SIEM better practices. Further considerations include cross-functional coordination with legal and privacy teams to ensure that automation processes are designed to support lawful scope definition, data minimisation and reproducible disclosure (Policies/Actions: Strategy; Leadership and Management; Capability Development) [55,100,257,312,313,315].

Several papers note a transition in the workforce. Where there was previously a reliance on individualised digital trace by digital trace focused artisan practice, it has been replaced by a movement to oversight of automated processing pipelines. This evolution is supported by a workforce with new competencies and workplace dynamics including:

•establish appropriate performance metrics and

•adoption of standardised test datasets to ensure that outputs remain interpretable and comparable.

As automation increases abstraction from primary raw data sources, scalable education strategies based on curated artefacts and practical exercises are presented as an effective way to maintain competence and currency (Policies/Actions: Capacity Building; Leadership and Management; Capability Development) [134,313,315,325,326].

In summary, the movement to automation, data reduction and triage must converge with assurance controls that are, in turn, scalable to meet the requirements as these capabilities develop. In order to continue developing these capabilities effectively and safely, the technological progression will necessarily be accompanied by change control, version control, systematic regression testing and continuous drift monitoring for the automated pipelines. Integrated integrity services, such as scalable timestamping, will serve to strengthen chain-of-custody narratives, especially when selective acquisition is applied (Policies/Actions: Governance; Capability Development; Capacity Building) [100,134,252,257,258,275,315].

Emerging threats and challenges

Threat overview

A very brief overview of emerging threats and challenges is provided reflecting how they are described in the literature. An indication of threat maturity (emerging, accelerating or entrenched) is estimated commensurate with the date that the reference(s) were published.

Synthetic/AI-generated evidence contamination (accelerating)

Digital falsification is no longer primarily about post-hoc tampering with real evidence. Accessibility to rapidly advancing technology enables the creation of convincing fake evidence at the point of origin. Conventional methods for assessing authenticity, such as metadata, visual artefacts, inconsistencies, plausibility and apparent realism no longer provide reliable assurance due to advances in generative AI. Consequently, the “liar's dividend” creates plausible deniability as a risk to reliable forensic examination [235,236,270,273,279,327].

Model-mediated analysis and automation bias (accelerating)

The increasing reliance on AI/LLM-enabled models as the default assistive tools for evidence triage, prioritisation and interpretation is introducing new risks of systematic bias, hallucination, opacity and more. The influence of model-generated recommendations impacts investigative processes and direction, the allocation of workloads and decisions made under time pressures. The bias present in these systems may lead to unintended consequences [4,5,135,142,267,328].

Defensibility gaps in validation and uncertainty reporting (entrenched and intensifying)

Digital forensic science conclusions remain vulnerable and open to challenge when the:

•validation of methods is inadequate where tool testing is insufficient, reliability is undemonstrated and limitations are unknown

validation of methods is inadequate where tool testing is insufficient, reliability is undemonstrated and limitations are unknown

•performance characterisation and evaluation, such as accuracy and error rates, specificity and operating conditions, are insufficient and/or undocumented and

performance characterisation and evaluation, such as accuracy and error rates, specificity and operating conditions, are insufficient and/or undocumented and

•statements of uncertainty are absent or lack transparency where conclusions are presented as absolute and assumptions and probabilistic limits are not disclosed [22,26,114,115,244,259].

statements of uncertainty are absent or lack transparency where conclusions are presented as absolute and assumptions and probabilistic limits are not disclosed [22,26,114,115,244,259].

SaaS/cloud-native evidence pathways (accelerating)

Provider-mediated acquisition relies on evidence collected through CSPs and is, therefore, governed by provider APIs. The cloud platforms automatically generate logging and define the digital trace lifecycles. These practices introduce persistent risks related to jurisdictional authority as data may be stored across multiple jurisdictions. Additional risks also arise from insufficient or lack of change control as platforms evolve. Maintaining SaaS/cloud evidence integrity depends on auditable logging of every access, transaction and transformation. The logs must be tamper-evident and reviewable, then collected following defined, lawful, repeatable and documented workflows [51,74,91,121,329].

Ephemeral telemetry and volatile state loss (accelerating)

Data produced by IoT/OT systems and live memory are essential for post-incident analysis and reconstruction. The digital traces are ephemeral and present significant operational challenges to collect. The resultant impacts are shorter timeframes for effective triage and increasing the cost of any delay in response [108,109,264,265,330,331].

Ransomware-driven investigative compression (entrenched)

The exigent circumstances of ransomware incidents necessitate rapid and high-priority evidence acquisition processes, while evasive behaviours, such as masquerading and other obscuration, intensify the need for rapid response. Compression also arises from dependence on the volatile state during live response as key ransomware material may only be available during the code's execution phase. Under these conditions, evidence loss, chain-of-custody compromise and excessive reliance on automation while under time pressure present heightened risk [142,330,[332], [333], [334], [335]].

Blockchain investigations and blockchain-backed custody (emerging - > accelerating)

Blockchain is a technology that underpins both a platform for conducting investigations and a system for maintaining evidence integrity. Caution is noted that trust in these processes is highly dependent on transparent procedural standards with associated explanation and governance structures that support scalability across jurisdictions [10,84,86,110,111,201,202,204].

Threat 1: synthetic evidence and adversarial AI contamination

AI can now generate deepfakes by mimicking official formats, language and metadata to create fake documents such as contracts, receipts or official communications. Manipulation was traditionally a post-production exercise where a real document was later altered which could be forensically determined. Manipulation is now fabricated at the point of creation with no “original” for comparison and is virtually indistinguishable from authentic records. Conventional integrity controls only come into effect after the logs are exported or files are copied or data is disclosed by the provider. Generally, they are considered to be insufficient as they do not confirm authenticity of evidence when the evidence was generated by the claimed system. The inability to verify provenance at the document's origin creates a risk to the reliability and integrity of documentary evidence.

Now the evidence is contestable. The possibility of deepfakes creates plausible deniability is an example of the “liar's dividend”. It allows authentic evidence to be dismissed as fake simply by alleging they are synthetic. Whereas, synthetic artefacts can be subjected to sound forensic processes, such as hashing and chain-of-custody, to meet procedural requirements and appear to be authentic. That is, unless their origins, based on source verification, creation-time authenticity, generation method and context, are examined. Meeting this challenge involves directing the focus towards provenance at creation. The measures include recording hashes during file creation, applying institutional watermarking to the files and preserving native files with their metadata for auditability. Governance emerges as the primary mitigation strategy and requires:

•established, sound SOPs for evidentiary formats, metadata and verification tasks

established, sound SOPs for evidentiary formats, metadata and verification tasks

•continuous training programmes to keep abreast of new deepfake techniques and to maintain currency of expertise and

continuous training programmes to keep abreast of new deepfake techniques and to maintain currency of expertise and

•policies placing technical authenticity as the authoritative analytical method over visual plausibility.

policies placing technical authenticity as the authoritative analytical method over visual plausibility.

In summary, building authenticity at scale depends on developing detection capabilities and establishing provenance infrastructure. Employing transparent, explainable forensic tools and validation practices will ensure that the principle of defensibility first is met [28,235,236,336,337].

Threat 2: model-mediated analysis and automation bias

AI is increasingly viewed as a productivity layer. AI-enabled forensic tools rapidly filter large volumes of heterogenous digital traces, rank their relevance, enable initial triage, all under tight operational timeframes. While early-stage decision-making can be streamlined and enhanced, adoption of AI-enabled tools depends on establishing practitioner trust, alignment with existing workflows and understanding the internal functioning of the closed-box nature of many models. Recent trials of LLM-enabled methods have been focused on extracting entities and relationships from digital traces, then constructing evidence networks. Shaped by the model prompting suggestions, this recent repositioning increases the risk that the investigation starts to follow the model's logic instead of the examiner's reasoning.

The reliability and integrity of AI-enabled workflows are only as reliable and trustworthy as the quality and representativeness of the training data. In other words, bad or unrepresentative training data leads to unreliable AI outputs. Delegating decision-making to models trained on limited or biased datasets increases the risk of mis-prioritisation at the initial investigative stage. This can also lead to false negatives early in the process where important digital traces may be excluded from further examination. Consequently, later conclusions may be influenced by earlier, model-driven decisions. In the forensic context, explainability and transparency are requirements, they are not optional. The opaque logic of closed box models undermines the traceability of reasoning and also prevents comparability across cases. The examiner's ability to identify bias or errors is hindered and, as a result, the defensibility of findings is weakened.

Digital forensic scientists are becoming increasingly concerned that AI-enabled tools are producing outputs that are not sufficiently validated, transparent, explainable or clearly presented. In the absence of transparent protocols that specify where human oversight occurs, the management of errors and the basis for model outputs, AI-enabled tool output is becoming inseparable from expert opinion. As automated tools make it easier to perform complex testing and assign attribution, new risks are introduced. Examiners may rely too heavily on automated results, such as suggested links between entities or automated attributions, without sufficient verification to ensure that they are actually correct.

At the system level, the main risks introduced by automated LLM-enabled analysis fall within the authenticity at scale and defensibility first narratives. The specific risks are associated with admissibility of the AI generated outputs and the probative value or weight that the court or decision-maker assigns to the evidence. The risk treatments are effective validation, transparency and explainability controls which are managed through workplace design, supervision and capability assurance, i.e. within the remit of leadership and management. Leadership responsibilities do not end there as they also need to formally set expectations for when model outputs may inform prioritisation, documented verification is required for time-critical triage decisions and to ensure staff are trained to recognise model limitations and bias [253,257,258,338,339].

Threat 3: defensibility gaps in validation and uncertainty reporting

The legal and scientific defensibility of digital forensic science depends on validated methods that are integrated within a quality management framework. That ensures all procedures are repeatable, reproducible and empirically tested through a documented, context-specific test plan. Consistent with all forensic science, credible digital forensic science results require transparent, empirically tested and reproducible methods that work in real-world environments. Transparency across the disciplines of science, engineering, legal professionals and the courts allow the courts to assess reliability, opposing experts to evaluate the methods and allow the decision-makers to understand limitations.

The multi-layered procedural approach is a persistent challenge to the reliability and integrity of digital evidence analysis. Each abstraction layer can potentially introduce errors, which are often systematic as they are embedded in the tool or method. As the evidence is managed through the forensic process, errors will accumulate, possibly even compound. The vulnerability to these errors is exacerbated when employing proprietary or closed box tools, limiting an examiner's insight into underlying assumptions. Although integrity checks provide some limited assurance, they do not provide certainty. Therefore, defensibility requires the review of error and output logs accompanied by documentation that details the tool's specifications, version and failures.

Meeting admissibility standards and producing defensible testimony requires the disclosure of all calibrations and validation data that are then made available for independent review and judicial examination. As in all forensic science, error in digital forensic science procedures is inevitable. Currently, it is generally accepted that forensic tools are not trustworthy by default (see previous Interpol Digital Evidence Reviews) [12]. Identifying and mitigating errors and potential errors are integral to quality assurance frameworks and should be reflected in examiner reporting and testimony. Defensibility requirements, including controls for managing variability, are intensified due to the growing penetration of AI-enabled digital forensic science pipelines. The system outputs may exhibit non-deterministic characteristics that require validation and repeated testing to detect inaccuracies and hallucinations.

Strategically, a ‘defensibility first’ will avoid the pitfalls of using unvalidated tools and opaque processes that can erode the reproducibility and contestability of findings. These risks are amplified when the digital forensic science capacity is being scaled up, therefore the task of tool characterisation and behavioural documentation in each layer of the toolchain becomes an obligation. Governance frameworks should mandate the creation and maintenance of validation evidence, including, but not limited to, controlled test plans, repeatability criteria and acquisition records. The evidence of these studies supports independent review processes and defensibility during proceedings [5,15,26,115,142,239,260].

Investigating authorities are presented with new dependencies and challenges. Threat 5: ephemeral telemetry and volatile state loss

The loss of ephemeral telemetry and volatile state pose significant technical and operational challenges. As digital traces are usually lost when systems are powered down, live acquisition is the only option for their reconstruction. Volatile evidence is also time sensitive, that is, the evidence remains while it is being used in computing operations, but overwritten or even actively removed when it is no longer required. In OT systems, like SCADA and HMI systems, data held in volatile memory does not persist uniformly, is fragile and degrades rapidly once critical processes stop running. Consequently, effective evidence acquisition must be collected while the system has active processes and threads running, it is performing system calls and kernel interactions, while data is memory resident.

In all forensic science and digital forensic science procedures, reliability and integrity are non-negotiable otherwise they are valueless, perhaps destructive. Yet, when conducting live data acquisition, the processes can inadvertently alter system states which may lead to contestability over authenticity and reliability. Mitigating these risks requires validated live acquisition methods and robust integrity controls, such as hashing and memory dump verification. These are practical measures that demonstrate non-tampering and, therefore, satisfy investigative and admissibility requirements.

Fog and edge computing environments expand the number of evidence locations, such as edge devices, gateways, local controllers, fog nodes and cloud backends. They comprise a vast array of devices that differ in hardware, operating systems, storage capacity, logging behaviour and retention policies. The distributed nature and volatility of digital traces in these environments heighten the risk of evidence disappearing before the digital forensic process can be fully engaged. Similarly, ephemeral messaging platforms also heighten admissibility and disclosure risks. Many older platforms have upgraded to offer disappearing messages, often ranging from as little as 24 h or even less and up to 90 days or more. Therefore, operational readiness and rapid response capabilities are paramount organisational strategies.

The threat posed by ephemeral telemetry and volatile state loss is directly aligned to the ephemeral telemetry narrative and secondarily to defensibility first. The need for live capture, volatile-memory dependence and cloud/container access draw the focus to the policies and actions domain of capability development. Threat 6: ransomware-driven investigative

Ransomware has received considerable public attention due to the impact that it has had on organisations and on services. Underneath the public awareness, ransomware incidents impose significant pressure on the available timeframe for investigative response. They compel a heavy reliance on automated triage solutions including LLM-enabled tools for memory and process analysis. The automation quickly reduces the number and volume of candidate artefacts. However, LLM-enabled triage processes introduce systematic errors including hallucination where false reasoning leads to false positives (such as the erroneous suspicion of legitimate core processes) and false negatives, often resulting from gaps in automation rules and/or knowledge. At the same time, in the eternal competition, adversaries are developing and using evasion techniques, such as process masquerading, to complicate the rapid discrimination of threats. Therefore, defensibility is based on:

•decision frameworks with documented predefined logic

decision frameworks with documented predefined logic

•traceable prompts that have been accurately documented

traceable prompts that have been accurately documented

•model versions, settings and configurations are documented and preserved for review and

model versions, settings and configurations are documented and preserved for review and

•measured, systematic and documented human oversight of hallucination and false negative behaviours.

measured, systematic and documented human oversight of hallucination and false negative behaviours.

To maintain reliability and integrity throughout the investigative process, incident leadership ensures that governance structures prioritise investigative tasks and determine the acceptance criteria for automation outputs. A human decision point or investigator judgement checkpoint is a fundamental part of the process as early phase capability or coordination gaps at first response can result in evidence loss and require later additional work.

These challenges are best met through aligning strategic priorities including the:

•management of volatile ransomware-related telemetry including memory resident digital traces

•scaling authenticity through automation and

•prioritising defensibility through validation and transparent articulation of LLM error modes and human oversight.

Ultimately, organisational leadership is responsible for formally defining and documenting authorities for decision making and predefined escalation pathways. Residing within these defined responsibilities are the documented risk acceptance protocols and the known limitations [2,109,142,257,333,347].

Threat 7: blockchain investigative compression

Blockchain technology is increasingly being promoted for evidence preservation. The proposals centre around the technical tamper-evident strengths which are achieved through cryptographic hashing, the immutable nature of distributed ledgers and chronological tracking. A common architectural pattern is a hybrid design where only metadata is stored on the blockchain while the digital traces are stored off chain, for example in IPFS. Smart contracts are used to manage how the various stakeholders interact with that data. Storing hashes on the blockchain enables tamper detection and actions to be audited. Successful implementation is dependent on controlled access and the data lifecycle events are formally governed. The overall operational effectiveness of this solution depends on the scalability and procedural integration into the justice system workflows.

The literature converges on the notion that, despite the promise of “blockchain-enabled evidence”, a significant gap remains in the standardised protocols and assurance mechanisms for identifying and mitigating manipulation and forgery risks. If these risks remain unresolved, they will lead to uncertainty in meeting judicial expectations for validity, relevance and authenticity. The effectiveness of these systems for strengthening provenance relies on governance over access controls and lifecycle events, i.e. who can create, transfer, retrieve and update evidence records, underpinned by how those permissions are enforced.

Strategically, the threats posed by blockchain investigations and blockchain evidence preservation align with Narrative 5 (Blockchain Investigations - crypto tracing and blockchain evidence management) and interact with Defensibility First. The admissibility of blockchain-preserved evidence introduces interpretive risks. The value of blockchain records depends on transparency based on documented accessible and understandable underlying assumptions, validated integrity and provenance accompanied by articulation of system limits and governance controls. Meeting these challenges requires a phased capability development programme. Initial implementation should begin with small, controlled pilot projects that test whether access controls, custody transfer logic and integrity verification work as planned and expected, then what adjustments might be needed, if any. These pilot studies should be supported through the alignment of procedures and standards that anticipate a range of judicial examination and disclosure requirements [83,87,111,203].

Overarching implications

Digital evidence is quickly growing in both volume and complexity to the extent that the capabilities of traditional forensic laboratory models are being outpaced. As a result, organisations are increasingly relying on automation and cloud-based platforms. This reliance introduces new dependencies, risks and vulnerabilities where defensibility:

•depends on provider updates and retention controls such as provider change management, retention policies, regression testing following updates and auditable log integrity and

depends on provider updates and retention controls such as provider change management, retention policies, regression testing following updates and auditable log integrity and

•vulnerabilities such as hallucinations or misclassification, opaque decision-making and susceptibility to manipulation or adversarial attacks must be addressed.

vulnerabilities such as hallucinations or misclassification, opaque decision-making and susceptibility to manipulation or adversarial attacks must be addressed.

Protection from these risks require stronger validation, governance and defensibility controls.

Key evidence, in the form of volatile telemetry and transient messaging, now exists only briefly and disappears quickly soon after creation if it is no longer needed. Investigative timelines are compressed and investigations must be ready to launch immediately, highlighting the importance of readiness and first response competence. Be prepared to act before incidents occur and maintain evidential integrity. Digital forensic science is moving away from analysing individual artefacts, i.e. one device at a time, after the fact and checking integrity later after collection, towards systems that automatically record, protect and prove integrity from the moment data is created. To put it another way, integrity is no longer reconstructed after an incident, it is built into the system. Finally, multi-disciplinary collaboration across the investigating organisation becomes de rigueur as fragmentation across organisational units (laboratories, incident response teams and legal teams) can lead to divergent practices, conflicting assumptions and inconsistent evidence handling. If the organisation is not joined up, it can lead to inter-jurisdictional challenges where different legal standards apply across jurisdictions, inconsistent practices are exposed under scrutiny that can undermine defensibility at scale [15,91,108,121,142,235,331].

Quality and standards

Digital forensic workflows are evolving faster than traditional validation and accreditation cycles, placing quality frameworks under strain. Standards are struggling to reflect current practice and assurance systems cannot keep up with operational reality. A consistent theme across the five strategic narratives reveals a disparity between:

1.traditional, document-based quality systems that rely on fixed procedures, static validations and periodic audits and

traditional, document-based quality systems that rely on fixed procedures, static validations and periodic audits and

2.continuously evolving and updating, platform-dependent sources of evidence, automated and AI-driven tools and analytic methods, that all update frequently, change behaviour without notice and depend on third-party providers.

continuously evolving and updating, platform-dependent sources of evidence, automated and AI-driven tools and analytic methods, that all update frequently, change behaviour without notice and depend on third-party providers.

This misalignment presents the practical risk of ‘defensibility drift’ in which organisations maintain formal compliance, yet casework practices gradually deviate from established validations [5,14,82,86,114,115,233,[348], [349], [350]].

Where assurance is breaking

Frequent software change is undermining the ability to reproduce forensic results consistently. Frequent software changes, including regular tool updates, changing operating systems, shifting libraries and APIs, all on continuously updating platforms, is compromising repeatability, a core principle of digital forensic science. Updates from cloud service providers, mobile operating systems, firmware releases and IoT/OT ecosystems can alter digital traces, logging conventions and data acquisition procedures with little notice. Repeatability is the expectation that the same forensic method produces the same result, different examiners can reproduce findings and results can be independently verified.

Best practice frameworks, such as those produced by standards bodies, recognise that tools and platforms frequently change and acknowledge that this instability is not exceptional. They recommend continuous monitoring using ongoing checks that tools behave as expected and regression testing after updates or configuration changes. However, many organisations persist with scheduled tool revalidation cycles on an annual or periodic basis and rely on fixed audit schedules which cannot keep pace with the rate of technical change. Software updates occur faster than revalidation cycles, tools may change many times between validations, therefore validations quickly become out of date. When change control is weak, as demonstrated by the inability to respond to the change in the operational environment, the audit trails are incomplete, therefore undermine the ability to trace results to a specific, governed method version.

The integration of automation and AI into forensic workflows has fundamentally altered the quality risk. Quality is now about whether the overall method, including how automation and AI are employed, governed, validated and overseen, is properly controlled. It is no longer about whether a tool works correctly. LLM-enabled triage and analysis introduce new risks including:

•non-reproducibility of tool outputs across different model versions as model updates can change behaviour and results cannot always be recreated

non-reproducibility of tool outputs across different model versions as model updates can change behaviour and results cannot always be recreated

•undocumented changes to prompts or parameters, therefore outside of validation controls and without a clear audit trail and

undocumented changes to prompts or parameters, therefore outside of validation controls and without a clear audit trail and

•ambiguous distinctions between model-generated recommendations and examiner judgement where responsibility for conclusions becomes blurred.

ambiguous distinctions between model-generated recommendations and examiner judgement where responsibility for conclusions becomes blurred.

The consensus is that AI models must be validated as methods, not treated as generic tools. Therefore, forensic documentation is being designed to comply with ISO/IEC 17025 requirements, with human oversight ensured through detailed decision logging that record how and why conclusions were reached.

At the time of writing, managing uncertainty and interpretation remains inconsistent in emerging digital forensic science domains. Analytical techniques, such as location traces, voice comparison and cryptocurrency tracing, will often rely on probabilistic or heuristic inference methods. The primary deficiency is not a lack of awareness of uncertainty, but that methods and reporting language are inconsistent in their communication of method limitations, error and decision thresholds, particularly where formats are to meet the needs of the judicial process. Disparate responsibilities between laboratory, incident response and investigative teams further exacerbate inconsistencies in the validation evidence and reporting practices.

Tool validation versus method validation

Throughout the review, it has become clear that traditional tool validation no longer assures quality (methods behave as intended), reliability (results are consistent and repeatable) and trustworthiness (courts and stakeholders can rely on analytical results). The traditional validation quickly becomes outdated and tool behaviour changes between validation cycles.

Tool validation focuses on understanding how the software behaves, confirming it performs as expected in specific configurations. Tool validation answers the question “does this tool do what it claims to do?” Conversely, method validation looks at the full analytical workflow which incorporates how tools are combined, how human judgement is applied, how interpretations are made and whether conclusions are justified by the process. Method validation answers the question “is this entire process defensible for the claims we are making?”

Vendor's claims about how a forensic tool works can help inform evaluation as their documentation can provide insight and claimed functionality can guide testing. However, vendor claims do not absolve the laboratory's obligation to independently validate how the tool is used in practice, using representative data and understanding controlled conditions. This distinction becomes decisive as, in AI-enabled forensic workflows, the method is not just the software itself. It is also how the model is configured, guided, reviewed and reported. Therefore, elements of use, such as prompts, thresholds, review rules and reporting language, are treated as parts of the validated method.

Governance versus accreditation

Accreditation demonstrates compliance with recognised standards, such as ISO 17025. It confirms the scope of activities, management systems and documented process which are periodically assessed for continued compliance through a systematic audit process. Accreditation answers the question, “does the organisation meet the standard?” Governance, through auditability and accountability, determines whether those standards continue to be meaningfully upheld in practice as methods change and scale increases. It refers to who owns methods and decisions, how changes are approved and tracked, then how is accountability maintained as workflows evolve. Governance answers the question, “can the organisation remain defensible as things change?” The prevailing view is that governance represents the principle point of failure as operations increase in scale as more tools, data and automation are introduced and change accelerates. As organisations and operations grow, informal controls break down. Effective governance implements and maintains mitigation strategies, including clear ownership of forensic methods, controlled management of datasets, monitoring for drift in tools or outputs and formal change processes. Without effective governance, accreditation risks becoming a retrospective metric that reflects past performance and does not present current or future failures. Consequently, reliability erodes between audits.

Many digital evidence standards and best practice guides were published during the review period, primarily by the Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence. The ones cited here informed the synthesised discussion above [17,35,36,51,116,117,161,229,230,239,240,[351], [352], [353], [354]].

With the rapid change that has occurred over the review period, significant impacts have been felt in the quality and standards domain. The approach to quality and standards in digital forensic science has been forced to change accordingly. Consequently, as can be seen, there is considerable scope for many recommendations for organisational responses to address these challenges. The most important of these are outlined in next section in which a strategic framework is presented for organisations to use for guidance.

Conclusion and Strategic Framework for Action

Executive synthesis of key insights

Digital forensic science has moved from a device-centred, craft-based practice in which the primary evidence was the physical device where examiners relied heavily on personal expertise and methods were often bespoke (tool-specific and case-specific). Evidence was largely static and under the examiner's control. It is now science-intensive requiring validated methods, empirical testing, measurement of uncertainty, reproducibility, formalised SOPs and governance and produce findings that must withstand scrutiny. Further, digital forensic science is transformed by platform-mediated evidence management in which evidence is created, stored and controlled by cloud platforms, SaaS providers and distributed systems. It is now accessed through APIs, dashboards and provider-controlled interfaces; shaped by provider data models, retention policies and logging architectures; and dependent on authentication, access pathways and platform governance. The central leadership issue is no longer only how to collect more data as in cloud-scale environments, data volume is no longer a limiting factor. It is now how to preserve the qualities of legality, provenance, reproducibility and trust, which are more important than raw data volume, as scale, speed and complexity increase, which will cause traditional methods to fail.

They are redefining what organisations must be capable of in order to conduct modern digital forensic science. These advances increase reach and speed allowing access to more data sources, acquire evidence faster, automate tasks that were previously performed manually and to scale beyond device-centric limits. But they also increase admissibility risk from challenge in court with questions over reliability, provenance, chain of custody and whether or not the process employed meets scientific or legal standards. The risks are managed through sound governance, validation and accountability.

Traditional (static) quality assurance approaches are no longer adequate. They are based on the assumptions of stable tools, predictable workflows, slow rates of change, clear ownership of each process and infrequent updates. Static quality systems were not built to meet the challenges presented by dynamic platform-driven forensic environments. Consequently, strategic failure now occurs for several reasons:

Strategic implications of the five narratives

The review was structured around the five narratives that emerged from the literature during the period 2023-2025. As the narratives are revisited as a retrospective from the end of the review, it can be seen that each one needs to be considered carefully in the context of:

•the changes in the field and

the changes in the field and

•how individual organisations are positioned to meet the challenges of those changes.

how individual organisations are positioned to meet the challenges of those changes.

Authenticity at scale: AI/LLM models can extend review capacity with faster triage, summarisation of large datasets, pattern detection and prioritisation of relevant material. However, the effective use of models in digital forensic science is dependent on:

•humans with responsibility and accountability remaining as the decision-makers as AI cannot be the final arbiter of evidential meaning

humans with responsibility and accountability remaining as the decision-makers as AI cannot be the final arbiter of evidential meaning

•explainability of the reasoning behind AI outputs must be understood to ensure that decisions are not opaque or unchallengeable and reviewers can justify why an AI-assisted conclusion was accepted or rejected and

explainability of the reasoning behind AI outputs must be understood to ensure that decisions are not opaque or unchallengeable and reviewers can justify why an AI-assisted conclusion was accepted or rejected and

•decision logging provides transparency which is essential for reproducibility, auditability and legal defensibility.

decision logging provides transparency which is essential for reproducibility, auditability and legal defensibility.

Defensibility first: signals a priority shift away from the primary design goals of speed, convenience and tool availability towards defensibility where the method can be justified, the results are reproducible, the process withstands scrutiny and the evidence is admissible. Validation, reproducibility and accreditation readiness are designed into workflows from the start, not added as an afterthought following deployment.

SaaS-by-default evidence: evidence is created and stored by cloud platforms, SaaS applications and provider managed services. Cloud and SaaS telemetry are primary evidence sources comprising activity logs, audit trails, API events, authentication records, configuration changes and collaboration artefacts. Therefore, lawful access pathways are required including warrants, legal requests, API-based acquisition authorised by credentials and provider-approved export mechanisms. The processes must be aligned to the provider's data model, policies and limitations. The evidence acquisition (or export) process must be auditable.

Ephemeral telemetry: volatile evidence demands pre-positioned readiness where the tools are deployed before they are needed, access pathways are already configured, the credentials, APIs and permissions are ready to activate, while the staff are trained and authorised to act immediately once notified. As the telemetry expires quickly, it must be captured in or near real time with volatile digital traces prioritised first. First-response governance must be disciplined in its control, documentation, legal authorisation, procedural consistency and the employment of validated methods.

Blockchain investigations: attribution, the linking of addresses, clusters, behaviours and transactions must develop in tandem with evidential integrity, demonstrating that the blockchain is authentic, correctly extracted, reproducible and defensible. Strong attribution without integrity is inadmissible and strong integrity without attribution is incomplete. However, there must be a clear separation between analytic inference and proof as blockchain investigations often blend probabilistic reasoning, intelligence led-hypotheses, deterministic cryptographic facts, provider-mediated artefacts and reconstructed timelines.

These narratives are interdependent as each one fails if the supporting governance is missing. If any one of them is weak, the whole system becomes fragile. AI without defensibility produces errors faster and with more confidence. Cloud without legal governance weakens admissibility and may be excluded. Volatile evidence without readiness is lost. Blockchain without standards creates false assurance, i.e. confidence without defensibility.
