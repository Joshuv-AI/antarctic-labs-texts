A soft diet attenuates exercise-induced increases in cortical bone density and formation in long bones in growing mice
Authors: Yasuhiro Shimizu, Fatma Rashed, Huan Cao, Yuki Arai, Takashi Ono, Kazuhiro Aoki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone and muscle are closely linked through functional interdependence. Training and regular exercise can improve and maintain optimal bone mass and strength. Here, we investigated the effects of exercise combined with soft versus hard diet (pellet) on long bones in mice. Four groups of male C57BL/6J mice (n = 5 per group) were studied: hard diet (pellet) with exercise, soft diet (powdered) with exercise, hard diet (pellet) without exercise, and soft diet (powdered) without exercise. Femur and tibia were analyzed using radiographic, micro-CT, and histomorphometric techniques. Exercise combined with a hard diet significantly increased cortical bone density, mineral apposition rate, and bone formation rate in long bones. In contrast, exercise with a soft diet attenuated these osteogenic responses, while bone resorption activity remained comparable across groups. Serum corticosterone levels were elevated in soft diet groups, whereas serum sclerostin levels were higher in hard diet exercise groups. Additionally, serum insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) was reduced in the soft diet exercise group compared with the hard diet exercise group. These results suggest that a soft diet compromises exercise-induced gains in bone mineral density and bone formation, highlighting the importance of masticatory stimulation in optimizing skeletal adaptation in the growth stage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-52803-1.

Introduction

Bone and skeletal muscle are functionally and biologically interconnected tissues that adapt to mechanical stimuli. Exercise and habitual mechanical loading promote bone modeling by stimulating bone formation and suppressing resorption, whereas unloading leads to bone loss1–6. This adaptive relationship represents a fundamental principle of skeletal physiology.

Masticatory function constitutes a specialized form of mechanical loading. In the craniofacial skeleton, reduced occlusal loading induced by a soft diet results in alveolar bone atrophy, mandibular growth retardation, and cartilage thinning, particularly during development7–10. Restoration of mechanical loading can partially recover jawbone turnover in growing animals. These findings establish that masticatory forces regulate local skeletal structures through mechanical and biological mechanisms.

Beyond local craniofacial effects, emerging evidence suggests that mastication may influence systemic musculoskeletal regulation. Increased masticatory activity has been associated with alterations in osteocyte-derived signaling molecules, including insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and sclerostin, which are known regulators of bone metabolism11–13. Occlusal disharmony has also been linked to systemic endocrine responses, such as elevated glucocorticoid levels, that negatively affect long-bone strength14. Neural mechanisms have additionally been proposed, as occlusal conditions can influence appendicular muscle performance and neuromuscular excitability15,16. Together, these findings raise the possibility that masticatory function may interact with whole-body skeletal adaptation.

However, whether altered masticatory loading modulates exercise-induced adaptation of long bones remains unclear. This represents an important gap in understanding the integration between craniofacial function and systemic skeletal regulation. The primary hypothesis of the present study is that reduced masticatory stimulation attenuates long-bone adaptation to exercise. Neural and endocrine pathways are considered secondary, exploratory mechanisms that may contribute to this interaction but were not directly evaluated in the present study.

Because growing mice were used, this model reflects skeletal development. Bone adaptation during growth involves both cortical and trabecular compartments, as well as growth plate–associated remodeling. The present study focused primarily on cortical bone structural adaptation of long bones, as cortical bone is highly responsive to mechanical loading and provides a direct morphological readout of functional adaptation. Trabecular bone and growth plate remodeling were evaluated but were not the primary endpoints in order to maintain a focused assessment of cortical mechanical responsiveness during growth.

Based on these considerations, this study was undertaken to determine how masticatory loading influences exercise-induced structural adaptation of long bones by comparing soft-diet and hard-diet conditions in exercising growing mice.

Results

Exercise and diet consistency effects on muscle mass

As shown in Fig. 1a–d, gastrocnemius, masseter, soleus, and temporalis muscle weights were evaluated. Gastrocnemius, masseter, and soleus muscles showed a similar pattern, with exercise-associated increases under the hard-diet condition but not under the soft-diet condition; a similar tendency was also observed for the temporalis muscle. Two-way ANOVA identified a significant diet × exercise interaction for gastrocnemius (F(1,16) = 6.579, p = 0.0208), while no significant interaction was detected for the other muscles. In addition, the two-way ANOVA model detected significant overall differences between diet conditions for gastrocnemius and masseter; these differences should be interpreted as averages across exercise conditions. Exercise and hard diet increase bone density in long bones

Peripheral quantitative computed tomography (pQCT) revealed that exercise combined with a hard diet improved long-bone density parameters, whereas this effect was attenuated under the soft-diet condition (Fig. 2; Supplementary Figs. S1, S2). In the tibia, a significant diet × exercise interaction was observed for cortical density at the midshaft (F(1,16) = 10.43, p = 0.0052). Similar interaction patterns were also found for tibial total bone density and femoral total bone density (Table S1). In contrast, no significant main effect of diet was detected for major pQCT-derived density indices, while exercise showed significant effects in selected parameters. Soft diet attenuates exercise-induced bone formation

Fluorescent labeling with calcein and alizarin demonstrated a reduced distance between labels in soft-diet mice compared with hard-diet mice (Fig. 3a). Two-way ANOVA identified a significant main effect of exercise and a significant diet × exercise interaction for mineral apposition rate (MAR; interaction: F(1,16) = 12.96, p = 0.0024). Bone formation rate (BFR) also showed a significant diet × exercise interaction (F(1,16) = 15.42, p = 0.0012), indicating an attenuated response to exercise under the soft-diet condition. In contrast, mineralizing surface per bone surface (MS/BS) showed significant main effects of diet and exercise, with no significant interaction. Detailed F-values, degrees of freedom, and p-values for all analyses are provided in Table S1.

Soft diet increases corticosterone and decreases IGF-1

Serum corticosterone showed a significant main effect of diet, with higher levels in the soft-diet condition (Fig. 5a, Table S1). Serum sclerostin levels showed significant main effects of diet and exercise, with higher overall levels in the hard-diet and exercise conditions (Fig. 5b, Table S1). Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that climbing exercise increased cortical bone mineral density (BMD) and bone formation parameters in the hindlimbs of growing mice fed a hard diet, whereas these osteogenic responses were markedly attenuated under soft-diet conditions. Using the climbing model we previously reported, the exercise-induced increases in hindlimb BMD were similarly reproduced17,18. The administration protocols for the standard- and soft-diet groups were based on published models of reduced occlusal stimulation8,19. Muscle mass changes showed a pattern consistent with the skeletal findings, with increases observed under hard-diet exercise conditions but attenuated under soft-diet feeding. These findings indicate that adequate masticatory stimulation is required to achieve the full skeletal benefits of exercise during growth.

The primary objective of this study was not to examine general skeletal development, but to determine whether masticatory loading modulates exercise-induced mechanical adaptation of long bones. Mechanical loading is a key regulator of cortical bone modeling, particularly in load-bearing regions. Therefore, cortical structural parameters were selected as the primary endpoints because they directly reflect mechanical responsiveness and functional adaptation. Although growth plate remodeling and trabecular turnover are essential aspects of skeletal development during growth, they reflect broader developmental processes rather than localized mechanical adaptation. Trabecular parameters were evaluated and are presented in the Supplementary Figures, showing trends consistent with cortical findings. In addition, no significant differences in femoral length were observed among groups, suggesting that longitudinal growth per se was not substantially altered. Thus, the observed differences primarily reflect altered mechanical adaptation rather than generalized growth impairment. Nevertheless, exclusion of detailed growth plate analyses represents a limitation and warrants further investigation.

Serum bone turnover markers (e.g., P1NP and CTX-I) reflect global skeletal metabolism. Since our objective was to characterize localized cortical bone structural changes, we prioritized direct morphological assessment. Nevertheless, integrating systemic biochemical markers in future work could help link local structural adaptations with whole-body bone metabolism.

One potential mechanism underlying the attenuated response under soft-diet conditions involves functional interactions between masticatory and locomotor systems. Reduced masticatory activity may influence neuromuscular regulation in distant muscles, thereby diminishing the effective mechanical stimulus generated during exercise. Previous studies have shown that occlusal conditions can affect appendicular muscle performance and neuromuscular excitability20–23. The H-reflex represents a neural pathway through which voluntary muscle activation can modulate spinal reflex excitability in remote muscles24,25. However, because neurophysiological parameters and locomotor output were not directly measured in this study, this pathway should be interpreted as a biologically plausible hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

Endocrine modulation may also contribute. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of diet on corticosterone levels, with soft-diet feeding associated with elevated circulating corticosterone26,27. A trend toward a diet × exercise interaction was observed for corticosterone levels, with exercise reducing corticosterone under hard-diet conditions but not under soft-diet conditions. Corticosterone concentrations exceeding approximately 200 ng/mL are generally considered indicative of stress-related endocrine activation in rodents28,29, and elevated glucocorticoids are known to suppress bone formation30–32. Importantly, food intake and actual exercise volume were not directly quantified. Therefore, while these findings are consistent with a role of masticatory conditions in modulating stress responses, this effect may depend, at least in part, on adequate masticatory stimulation, and other contributing factors cannot be excluded.

Serum insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels were lower in the soft-diet exercise group compared with the hard-diet exercise group. IGF-1 is a well-established anabolic regulator of bone formation and positively correlates with BMD in both experimental and clinical studies33–35. Reduced IGF-1 signaling may therefore have contributed to the diminished exercise-induced bone formation observed under soft-diet conditions.

Serum sclerostin levels were elevated in the hard-diet exercise group. Although sclerostin is widely recognized as a negative regulator of bone formation via inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, circulating sclerostin levels have been reported to positively correlate with BMD in clinical studies36,37. Emerging evidence indicates that serum sclerostin reflects dynamic osteocyte responses to mechanical loading, including transient protein release, rather than solely local transcriptional suppression. Mechanical stimulation can induce both regulatory changes in SOST expression and protein release from osteocytes38–41. Accordingly, the elevated circulating sclerostin observed in the hard-diet exercise group may reflect adaptive osteocyte-mediated responses to combined climbing exercise and masticatory loading, rather than suppression of bone formation. These findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly in growing animals exposed to multiple mechanical stimuli.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Neurophysiological parameters, detailed growth plate morphology, food intake, and actual exercise volume were not directly assessed. Future studies integrating biomechanical, endocrine, and developmental analyses will be necessary to fully elucidate the mechanisms linking masticatory function and systemic skeletal adaptation.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that masticatory loading modulates exercise-induced cortical bone adaptation during growth. These results highlight the integration of craniofacial function and systemic skeletal responsiveness and suggest that adequate masticatory stimulation is an important determinant of the skeletal benefits of physical activity.

Materials and methods

Animals

Four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Sankyo Labo Service Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Upon arrival, mice were acclimatized for 4 days under standard housing conditions at 21 ± 1 °C under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Dietary modification and exercise interventions were initiated when the animals reached four weeks of age. The intervention period lasted for two weeks, and all mice were sacrificed 15 days after the start of the intervention, corresponding to six weeks of age.

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tokyo Medical and Dental University (019130279 A) and performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. After a 4-day acclimation period, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group):

The hard diet was replaced with a powdered diet (Nippon Clare Co., RI sterile feed CE-2, 30 kGy, 2.0 kg × 5 bags/box) for the soft diet groups. Both groups were fed the same commercially available sterilized rodent chow (CE-2, CLEA Japan, 30 kGy).

Exercise protocol

Exercise groups were housed in cages equipped with a 100-cm steel mesh tower (mesh spacing ~ 5 mm). Mice voluntarily climbed the tower to access water bottles placed at the top. Climbing activity was monitored weekly for 24 h using a CCD video camera (DCR-TRV30; Sony, Tokyo, Japan), and daily climbing distance and duration were recorded. Preliminary experiments confirmed that all mice could climb full height soon after introduction to the tower.

Radiological analyses

The mice were anesthetized with medetomidine hydrochloride (1.0 mg/kg, Domitor; Zenoaq, Fukushima, Japan), and euthanasia was then performed by cervical dislocation by trained personnel. This procedure was conducted in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals: 2020 Edition. Femur and midshaft tibia were excised then scanned using peripheral quantitative computed tomography (pQCT; XCT Research SA+, Stratec Medizintechnik GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany). Cortical bone area, cortical thickness, and cortical bone density were measured from an average of three slices per sample.

Histological assessments and bone histomorphometry

Tibiae were embedded in Super Cryoembedding Medium (SCEM; Section-Lab Co., Ltd., Hiroshima, Japan) and frozen at − 100 °C. Micro-CT images guided sectioning of the embedded bones. Undecalcified blocks were cut into 5 μm-thick sections using a microtome (CM3050sIV; Leica Microsystems) with adhesive film (Cryofilm type 2 C; Section-Lab). Fluorescently labeled sections were observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (FSX100; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and histomorphometric analyses were performed with ImageJ (v1.53i, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Bone formation parameters included mineral apposition rate (MAR), mineralizing surface per bone surface (MS/BS), and bone formation rate (BFR). Bone resorption was assessed by osteoclast number per bone surface (N. Oc/BS) using tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining with methyl green counterstain. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells on trabecular bone surfaces were identified as osteoclasts.

Serum biochemical analysis

Whole blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 20 min. Serum was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. Serum corticosterone; Corticosterone HS EIA kit (MBL Co., Ltd., Nagoya, Japan; DS-AC15F1), sclerostin; Mouse Sclerostin ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA; MSST00), and IGF-1 levels; Mouse IGF-1 ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Inc.; MG100) as well as bone resorption marker; MouseTRAP™ Assay kit (MBL Co., Ltd.; DS-SBTR103) were quantified according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were performed by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Sample size was determined a priori based on variability and effect size observed in our previous climbing-exercise study14. Power analysis (α = 0.05, 80% power) indicated that a minimum of four animals per group was required. To account for biological variability and the multi-group design, five animals per group were included.
