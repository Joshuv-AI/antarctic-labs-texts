Hypoglycaemia in a patient with undiagnosed Doege–Potter syndrome
Authors: Aman Vora, Simon Buelens, Chantal Mathieu, Marc Van de Velde, Dirk Van Raemdonck, Steffen Rex, Layth Al Tmimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Doege–Potter syndrome (DPS) is a rare paraneoplastic disorder characterised by non-islet cell tumour hypoglycaemia (NICTH), resulting from ectopic secretion of insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-II) by solitary fibrous tumours (SFTs). We describe the case of an 85-year-old woman, presenting in October 2024, with a chronic subdural haematoma and profound intraoperative hypoglycaemia. Further investigation revealed a large pleural mass; the pathophysiological findings were consistent with DPS, and histopathology confirmed a malignant SFT. Hypoglycaemia resolved after complete tumour resection. The current case highlights the importance of considering NICTH in older patients with unexplained, treatment-resistant hypoglycaemia, especially when comorbidities (like subdural hematoma) mask the clinical picture. A focused narrative review accompanies this report, providing clinicians with practical insights into the spectrum of clinical presentations, diagnostic work-up, therapeutic modalities, and anaesthetic considerations in DPS. Early recognition is crucial to avoid serious complications. Complete surgical removal remains the cornerstone of effective management, while alternative therapies may be needed in inoperable cases.

Background

Doege–Potter syndrome (DPS) is a rare paraneoplastic syndrome caused by solitary fibrous tumours (SFTs),1 which are uncommon mesenchymal neoplasms originating from spindle cells. While most SFTs are histologically benign with low mitotic activity, a subset demonstrates malignant behaviour and diverse clinical presentations.2 These tumours can arise in various anatomical locations, including the abdomen, pelvis and liver,3,4 with the pleura amongst the most common sites.3

DPS is characterised by non-islet cell tumour hypoglycaemia (NICTH), due to ectopic secretion of insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-II) or its incompletely processed precursor, pro-IGF-II.5 The abnormal production of these factors leads to severe and refractory hypoinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia, a key feature of the syndrome.6 The high-molecular-weight ‘big’ IGF-II activates insulin receptors, promoting glucose uptake in peripheral tissues and inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis.7 The result is profound and refractory hypoinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia, often presenting with symptoms varying from sweating, tremors and seizures to altered mental status and coma.1,8,9 Although hypoglycaemia occurs in less than 5% of SFTs,6 it is typically linked to large, metabolically active, or malignant tumours.7,10 Diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion alongside Whipple's triad (defined by documented low plasma glucose, hypoglycaemic symptoms and resolution of symptoms after glucose level correction), biochemical (including suppressed insulin and C-peptide with disproportionate IGF-II blood levels) and histopathological confirmation.7,8,11,12 Notably, normal IGF-II levels do not exclude the diagnosis.

Early recognition is essential, as DPS can be masked by other concurrent pathologies such as intracranial bleeding, infection, or other illnesses. In this manuscript, in compliance with the CARE Guidelines (see Supplemental Data 1, CARE checklist),13 we describe a patient with a chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) and profound hypoglycaemia, finally diagnosed with DPS. In addition, we present a literature review to support clinical decision-making, focusing on the clinical spectrum, diagnostic challenges, treatment options and anaesthetic considerations associated with DPS.

Case report

An 85-year-old woman on dual antiplatelet therapy for a recent coronary stent placement presented at the emergency department in October 2024 with headaches following a recent fall. Cranial computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed a chronic subdural haematoma (cSDH) with mass effect. On admission, the neurological examination was unremarkable; however, due to the mass effect, she was admitted for close observation and monitoring. In addition, her dual antiplatelet therapy was temporarily put on hold. After a decline in consciousness and a generalised seizure, an urgent burr hole evacuation was performed the next day. During the procedure, profound hypoglycaemia (21 mg dl−1) was detected and treated with intravenous glucose boluses. Postoperatively, the patient remained hypoglycaemic and required a continuous infusion of glucose.

Further clinical examination revealed systemic symptoms, including fevers, night sweats, dizziness and unintentional weight loss of 7 kg over a short period. Auscultation revealed bronchial breath sounds on the right lung base. A chest X-ray revealed a moderate amount of right-sided pleural effusion. Tumour markers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and neuron-specific enolase (NSE), serum cortisol level and adrenocorticotropic hormone were all within normal limits. However, further laboratory tests revealed a low serum insulin level of 2.8 pmol l−1 and a C-peptide level of 0.01 nmol l−1, ruling out endogenous hyperinsulinism (Table 1). IGF-II was elevated (538 ng ml−1), and IGF-I was low (<25 μg l−1), confirming NICTH (Table 1).

A thoracic positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan showed a large, heterogeneous, slightly to moderately hypermetabolic mass in the right lung (Fig. 1), highly suspicious for malignancy. A few days later, a thoracotomy was performed, and a 1552 g encapsulated pleural mass was excised (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative management included peripheral intravenous access, central venous and arterial lines, and an epidural catheter placement for postoperative analgesia. Glycaemic control was maintained with continuous 5% glucose infusion. Anaesthesia was provided with total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) using propofol and remifentanil. Estimated blood loss was 500 ml. Postoperatively, hypoglycaemia resolved, and the patient was discharged to a rehabilitation centre on postoperative day 8. There were no signs of local recurrence or metastatic disease at 1-year follow-up.

Macroscopically, the tumour appeared white and nodular, with minor yellow discolouration, limited necrosis and small cystic structures. Histological analysis showed a multinodular spindle cell neoplasm with moderate mitotic activity (up to 4/10 HPF), minimal necrosis (<10%) and no significant pleomorphism. Immunohistochemistry confirmed the diagnosis of SFT, revealing positive staining for cluster of differentiation 34 (CD34) and nuclear expression of signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6), with negative S-100 protein.

Discussion and narrative literature review

To understand the clinical presentation better, diagnosis and management options of DPS, we conducted a focused narrative literature review. This review included all relevant records available up to January 2025. The search was systematically carried out across MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase and the Cochrane Library.

From a total of 1772 records identified through the search, 152 publications met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (see Figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, PRISMA 2020 Flow Diagram). Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria:(1)case reports or case series describing patients with DPS;(2)published in English or Dutch;(3)full-text available.

Clinical manifestations

DPS typically presents with severe, treatment-resistant hypoglycaemia due to ectopic secretion of IGF-II from SFTs.11,14 The clinical symptoms are often dominated by neuroglycopenic manifestations, including dizziness, lethargy and confusion.3,15 In severe cases, hypoglycaemia can lead to life-threatening events such as seizures or coma, which underscores the need for prompt recognition and immediate management.9,16

Moreover, hypoglycaemia in DPS can lead to generalised weakness and fatigue, which are common complaints among affected individuals.17,18 Recurrent episodes of hypoglycaemia exacerbate these systemic effects, contributing to a chronic state of physical debilitation. The interplay between neurological and systemic symptoms is particularly evident during acute hypoglycaemic episodes. For instance, recurrent syncopal events caused by severe hypoglycaemia may result in secondary injuries such as falls or head trauma.11,19,20 Such incidents not only highlight the immediate dangers posed by untreated hypoglycaemia but also emphasise its broader impact on patient safety and mobility. Notably, most of the signs and symptoms of DPS were also mirrored in our case.

Clinical manifestations vary depending on the tumour location. While intrathoracic SFTs are more common and may remain asymptomatic until large enough to cause compressive symptoms, extrapleural tumours might be detected earlier due to their impact on surrounding structures.5,21

Notably, the advanced age of many patients diagnosed with DPS adds complexity to symptom presentation. Older patients may exhibit atypical or muted signs of hypoglycaemia due to age-related physiological changes or medical treatment for comorbidities (e.g. beta-blockade) that mask classic adrenergic or neuroglycopenic symptoms. The variability emphasises the need for heightened clinical awareness and comprehensive biochemical assessment to ensure timely diagnosis and management.

Diagnostic challenges

The rarity of DPS often results in delayed diagnosis, making a structured diagnostic approach essential. Evaluation begins by ruling out more common causes of hypoglycaemia. In patients presenting with severe hypoglycaemia but lacking a history of diabetes, an initial biochemical workup including plasma glucose, insulin, C-peptide and proinsulin is required to exclude exogenous insulin administration or insulinoma9,22 When these causes are ruled out, and laboratory findings show suppressed insulin and C-peptide levels, NICTH should be considered in the differential diagnosis.9,22 Whipple's triad, is a cornerstone in diagnosing true hypoglycaemia and is also applicable in cases of DPS.14,23

In suspected NICTH, further analysis of IGF levels is warranted.16 DPS typically shows low insulin, proinsulin, C-peptide and beta-hydroxybutyrate levels.9,24 While direct assays for the high-molecular-weight IGF-II are limited, a significantly elevated IGF-II level, along with an IGF-II to IGF-I ratio above 10, is diagnostic for NICTH.19,25,26

Imaging is crucial for identifying SFTs. Notably, CT and MRI can detect well defined, vascular tumours, often showing necrosis or calcifications.27 PET scans, especially those using gallium-68 tracers, offer added sensitivity for tumour characterisation; however, their role remains limited by the generally low metabolic activity of these tumours.28 Ultrasound guidance is often used for biopsies.29

Histopathology confirms the diagnosis of SFTs, which are composed of spindle cells arranged within a highly vascular stroma.30 According to the United Kingdom's pathologic criteria, SFTs are classified as benign or malignant based on factors such as hypercellularity, >4 mitotic figures/10 high-power fields (HPFs), pleomorphism/atypia, infiltrative growth pattern, necrosis and haemorrhage.3

Immunohistochemical markers are key: CD34 is present in over 90 to 95% of cases, and STAT6 nuclear expression, due to NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion, is highly specific for SFTs.10,30,31

Lastly, diagnosing DPS requires a multidisciplinary approach combining clinical assessment, biochemical testing, imaging techniques and histopathological analysis.

Treatment modalities

Complete surgical resection is the definitive treatment for DPS, as it removes the source of IGF-II overproduction and resolves hypoglycaemia.3,32 Even subtotal resection in a case with multiple tumours has effectively eliminated hypoglycaemic symptoms.33 However, alternative therapies must be considered if surgery is not feasible.34

Radiation therapy is sometimes employed postoperatively to target microscopic residual disease and reduce the likelihood of local recurrence. However, its effectiveness is inconsistent and lacks standardised protocols.3,11 Chemotherapy has generally shown poor results compared to surgical resection combined with adjuvant therapies such as radiation.3,35 In contrast, immunotherapy targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which is overexpressed in SFTs via the STAT6 pathway, appears more promising.35 Anti-VEGF agents have shown potential in controlling tumour growth and related hypoglycaemia, though their use remains experimental.35

For patients awaiting surgery, glucose control is essential. Intravenous dextrose provides immediate relief but may require frequent or high-volume administration, which increases the risk of fluid overload and electrolyte imbalances.36,37 Among nonsurgical options, glucocorticoids appear to be the most effective for long-term management by suppressing IGF-II production.3,38 Their effectiveness appears dose-dependent, with doses below 20 mg day−1 prednisolone often proving inadequate.3 When glucocorticoids are insufficient or not tolerated, adjunctive therapies such as recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) or continuous glucagon infusion have also been explored.3 rhGH enhances IGF-binding protein levels, reducing free IGF-II, though its use is limited by high doses, cost and potential tumour stimulation.3 At daily doses of 4 to 12 U, rhGH was able to alleviate hypoglycaemia in patients with NICTH.8,22,39

Selective arterial embolisation offers a therapeutic alternative for unresectable SFTs or as a palliative measure for paraneoplastic hypoglycaemia by reducing tumour vascularity and IGF-II secretion.4,40 A novel technique, targeting intratumoural lactic acidosis transcatheter arterial chemoembolisation (TILA-TACE), has succeeded in metastatic liver SFT, achieving glycaemic control and tumour stability.4 In TILA-TACE, bicarbonate is administered in the embolised feeding artery to neutralise the tumour bed and convert intratumoral postembolisation lactic acidosis to lactosis, which effectively prevents tumour cells from using lactates instead of glucose and accelerates cell necrosis.4 Moreover, such approaches may serve as bridges to eventual surgical intervention.4

Anaesthetic management

Preoperative hypoglycaemia in DPS is often severe and resistant to conventional glucose management.20,36 This creates a challenging scenario where fasting protocols typically employed before surgery may exacerbate hypoglycaemia. Therefore, preoperative nutritional support becomes critical. Combined i.v. with oral glucose intake has been shown to stabilise blood glucose levels in such cases, although frequent episodes of hypoglycaemia may still occur despite these measures.36 Intrathoracic SFTs can reach 30 cm in diameter and cause tracheal and mediastinal shifts, making intubation and ventilation challenging.3,41 A thorough preoperative airway evaluation must be performed to develop an appropriate strategy.

Hypoglycaemia reduces cerebral glucose availability, increasing the brain's vulnerability to sedatives and hypnotics.20,42,43 It also contributes to cardiovascular instability. Of note, propofol-based TIVA causes minimal impact on glucose homeostasis and may thus be preferred over inhalational anaesthetics.20,42,43 Blood glucose levels should be closely monitored throughout surgery, as fluctuations can occur due to tumour-related IGF-II or stress hormones. Glucose infusions (e.g. 10 to 50% dextrose) may be needed continuously.44,45 Furthermore, a closed-loop artificial endocrine pancreas system (STG-55, Nikkiso Co., Japan), capable of continuously measuring blood glucose levels while automatically adjusting the insulin or glucose infusion rate, was effectively utilised to maintain a blood glucose level of 150 mg dl−1 during surgery.20

Haemodynamic instability is another concern, as SFTs are highly vascular, and manipulation can cause significant bleeding. Anaesthetic protocols should anticipate significant blood loss and include strategies for fluid resuscitation and transfusion. Preoperative embolisation has been attempted to minimise bleeding risk, but its effectiveness remains uncertain.3

Conclusion

DPS is a rare, but potentially life-threatening condition caused by IGF-II-secreting SFTs, leading to severe, often refractory hypoglycaemia. Diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion, after ruling out other more frequent endogenous or exogenous causes of hypoglycaemia. Confirmation is supported by Whipple's triad, a stepwise biochemical work-up, and advanced imaging and immunohistochemistry. Although surgical resection remains the definitive treatment, alternative or bridging strategies such as glucocorticoids, embolisation and TILA-TACE therapies may be necessary when surgery is not feasible or delayed. Anaesthetic management is particularly challenging because of glucose-level instability, airway difficulties and bleeding risks. Early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach are essential to ensure appropriate and timely intervention and optimal patient outcomes.
