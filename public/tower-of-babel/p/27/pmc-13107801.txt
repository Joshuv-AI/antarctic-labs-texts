Introduction

Climate change is rapidly altering global environments, posing substantial and potentially catastrophic threats to species persistence and ecosystem functioning within biological communities [1–5]. The accelerated rate of these changes exceeds the capacity of numerous populations to formulate effective adaptive responses, consequently accelerating demographic contractions and increasing extinction probabilities [6, 7]. In response to these challenges, species primarily rely on three adaptive strategies: shifting their geographic ranges to more suitable habitats, persisting in situ through phenotypic plasticity, or undergoing adaptive evolution via genetic change [8–11]. However, significant challenges persist in ascertaining the timing of such eco-evolutionary responses, differentiating between these strategies, and identifying potential evolutionary ‘winners’ and ‘losers’ [12, 13].

Alpine ecosystems manifest a distinct duality in their response to climate change. They function not merely as sensitive ‘early warning systems’ reflecting global patterns, but also furnish localized refugia via microclimatic heterogeneity engendered by their intricate topography [14, 15]. The global treeline has been ascending at an average rate of 0.4 m/yr throughout the past century [16]. Previous research has established that species can prolong their adaptive capacity through altitudinal microhabitat shifts to higher elevations [17]. This ‘microclimatic buffering’ mechanism, predicated on spatial redistribution, effectively mitigates extinction risks. However, in contrast to such external buffering achieved via spatial migration, it remains uncertain whether species constrained by geographical barriers can develop a temporal dimension of adaptive buffering through intrinsic genetic and epigenetic modifications while remaining stationary. Specifically, in the absence of geographical displacement, can populations attain effective adaptation through dynamic reorganization of genomic and epigenomic architectures across multiannual timescales? Addressing this question is essential for comprehensively elucidating the multidimensional adaptive mechanisms of alpine species.

Characterizing genetic and epigenetic diversity is crucial for assessing the adaptive potential of threatened populations and species in the face of climate change [18, 19]. Population genetics establishes a research framework linking genetic variation to adaptive potential and ultimately to environmental responses, building a bridge between genomic data and ecological performance [20–22]. Meanwhile, DNA methylation serves as a molecular basis for phenotypic plasticity, regulating gene expression without altering the DNA sequence and enabling rapid responses to environmental stress [23, 24]. Certain methylation marks can be inherited transgenerationally, thereby enabling offspring to acquire adaptive advantages preemptively [25, 26]. This mechanism effectively compensates for the time lag inherent in genetic adaptation, providing essential evolutionary resilience in the face of rapid environmental change [27, 28]. This capacity proves particularly vital in highly vulnerable ecosystems such as alpine regions.

Pikas are cold-loving alpine species, regarded as keystone species of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and sentinels for global warming. This study delves into the Gansu pika (Ochotona cansus), a species uniquely native to China and nestled within the Hengduan Mountains Region. Gansu pikas have a remarkable high-altitude adaptability and sensitivity to climatic variations, making them particularly valuable as an indicator species [29–32]. Its habitat in the alpine treeline ecotone is undergoing significant transformations, as schematically illustrated in the Graphical Abstract, including timberline upward shift at an average rate of 0.4 m per year [16], tree growth advancement, and meadow shrubification, providing a unique setting for studying short-term climate responses. Current research primarily focuses on phylogenetic relationships and the long-term evolutionary history of pikas, which limits the ability to capture contemporary climate-driven short-term adaptive evolution [33–36]. In contrast, population genomic studies based on temporal sampling can directly detect and quantify dynamic changes in genetic diversity, population structure, and epigenetic modifications during the sampling period, providing direct evidence for distinguishing between ‘genetic adaptation’ and ‘plastic responses’ [37–39].

The samples for this study were obtained from Wolong National Nature Reserve in Wenchuan County, Sichuan Province, China (30.75°-31.42°N, 102.85°-103.40° E). Established in 1963, this protected area spans approximately 54 km east–west and 62 km north–south, encompassing a total area of 2.0 × 10⁵ hectares. It is dedicated to conserving giant pandas, other rare wildlife, and alpine forest ecosystems. Its distinct geographical setting and ecological composition render it a sensitive and vulnerable region for climate change response, exhibiting irreplaceable value for biodiversity conservation and climate impact research.

Building upon this contextual foundation, we integrated whole-genome sequencing data and genome-wide DNA methylation profiles from the same geographic population of Gansu pikas collected over a six-year interval between 2015/2016 and 2022, with samples collected in 2015 grouped into the 2016 cohort. By comparing genetic diversity, methylation levels, and highly differentiated genomic regions across two temporal intervals, we investigate two principal questions: (1) whether climate change elicits discernible genomic and epigenomic responses within a six-year timeframe; and (2) whether rapid adaptation predominantly arises from directional allele frequency shifts, phenotypic plasticity mediated by epigenetic mechanisms, or synergistic interactions between genetic and epigenetic processes. Our findings offer empirical insights into in situ adaptation mechanisms of non-migratory treeline species, yield molecular evidence for assessing their persistence potential under climate change, and provide novel temporal perspectives for biodiversity conservation strategies.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 17 samples were collected during wildlife diversity surveys using a line-transect method. These included 16 O. cansus individuals collected in 2015 (n = 3), 2016 (n = 6), and 2022 (n = 7) at Huayanzi Tunnel (30.88°N, 102.97°E) within the Wolong National Nature Reserve, Sichuan, China (Fig. 1A). This population is geographically isolated by rivers at lower elevations and unsuitable alpine meadows at the summits, which precludes long-distance migration. Additionally, one Ochotona thibetana individual (n = 1) was included as an outgroup for ancestral allele inference in the genetic load analysis. All animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation, which is consistent with animal welfare standards. Muscle tissue (hind leg) was collected from each individual, immediately preserved in 95% ethanol, and stored at -80 °C for long-term storage. All specimens are deposited at the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IOZCAS). The detailed information and accession numbers of samples in this study are available in Supplementary Table S3. The animal handling and sampling protocol followed the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committee. This field investigation was conducted in accordance with Permit No. YW2022080901 issued by the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences for animal experimentation.

Sample sequencing

From the samples collected, 15 individuals were selected for whole-genome sequencing. Additionally, whole-genome data for two samples collected from the same location in 2015 were obtained from Ge et al. [34]. Muscle tissues were sent to Wuhan BGI Technology Service Co., Ltd. for whole-genome sequencing. For DNA extraction, approximately 50–100 mg of muscle tissue was processed using a modified phenol-chloroform protocol. Tissues were lysed in a preheated solution containing nuclear lysis buffer, proteinase K (20 mg/mL), and SDS (20%) at 56 °C for 1–2 h. After centrifugation, nucleic acids were purified by extraction with an equal volume of phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1), followed by precipitation with isopropanol. The DNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in TE buffer. Following extraction, the DNA was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, and its integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Library preparation was performed using the Watchmaker Enzyme-based Fragmentation DNA Library Prep Kit (CAS: 7K0019-096) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each sample, 200 ng of DNA was used for library construction and enzymatically fragmented into an average insert size of 350 bp. After library construction, preliminary quantification was conducted using Qubit 3.0, and the size distribution of library fragments was assessed using a fragment analyzer. Once the library size and peak profile met the expected criteria, qPCR quantification was performed on an ABI Quant Studio 12 K Flex to ensure library quality. Finally, sequencing was carried out on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform using the DNBSEQ-T7RS High-Throughput Sequencing Reagent Set (FCL PE150) V3.0, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were processed with fastp v0.20.1 [40] to trim low-quality bases (Phred score < 30) from the 5′ to 3′ ends and remove adapter sequences, low-quality reads, and duplicates. Quality assessment of the processed data for each sample was performed using FastQC v0.11.9 [41]. The mean sequencing depth was 16.17× (range 13.75–21.02×). DNA sequencing metrics are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) was performed by Annoroad Gene Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd on muscle tissues from 14 samples collected in 2015 (n = 1), 2016 (n = 6), and 2022 (n = 7). Genomic DNA was extracted using the phenol-chloroform protocol as described above. Starting with 500 ng of gDNA, libraries were constructed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Lightning™ Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the EpiArt® DNA Methylation Library Prep Kit for Illumina V3 following the standard protocol. The procedure included DNA fragmentation by ultrasonication, bisulfite conversion, DNA denaturation, 3ʹ-adapter ligation, single-strand extension, 5ʹ-adapter ligation, and PCR amplification to generate single-strand WGBS libraries. Following library preparation, samples were initially quantified with Qubit® 2.0, insert size distribution was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and effective concentration (> 10 nM) was verified with a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System to ensure quality standards. Qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina high-throughput platform to obtain genome-wide methylation profiles. Raw reads were processed with fastp v0.20.1 [40] for quality control and filtering, and the quality of processed data for each sample was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9 [41]. Detailed WGBS metrics and accession numbers of samples are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

SNP calling and filtering

Due to the absence of a well-annotated reference genome for O. cansus, the genome of the North American pika (GCA_030435755.1_mOchPri1.hap1, NCBI) was selected as the reference based on its superior assembly quality. The analytical pipeline was executed as follows: first, 150 bp paired-end reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA v0.7.17 [42] with the parameter -M to mark shorter split hits as secondary; the resulting SAM files were then converted to BAM format using SAMtools v1.12 [43] (view -bS), sorted, and indexed. Subsequently, alignments were filtered to retain only high-quality mappings (MAPQ ≥ 30) using samtools view -q 30, and PCR duplicates were removed with samtools rmdup. Finally, variant calling was conducted per chromosome in a parallelized workflow using BCFtools v1.12 [44]. The bcftools mpileup step was run with the --annotate option to specifically add key annotation fields (FORMAT/AD, FORMAT/ADF, FORMAT/ADR, FORMAT/DP, FORMAT/SP, INFO/AD, INFO/ADF, INFO/ADR). Variants were then called with bcftools call -mv to output multiallelic and variant sites. Individual chromosomal VCFs were subsequently merged into a genome-wide raw SNP dataset using bcftools concat. VCFtools v0.1.16 [45] was employed to filter the raw SNPs using the following criteria: only biallelic sites were retained (--max-alleles 2 --min-alleles 2), sequencing depth was limited to 10–30× (--minDP 10 --maxDP 30), minimum quality was set to 30 (--minQ 30), and sites with more than 10% missing data were excluded (--max-missing 0.9). Indels were removed (--remove-indels), and a filtered VCF file was generated (--recode --recode-INFO-all) for downstream analyses. To minimize potential confounding effects, all sex chromosome sequences and scaffolds not anchored to chromosomes were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, given their temporal proximity and high genetic continuity with the 2016 cohort, samples collected in 2015 were grouped with the 2016 samples in subsequent analyses. This filtering process ultimately yielded 32,179,369 high-quality SNP sites for analysis.

Kinship inference

To verify the geographical consistency of sample origins and to exclude the influence of closely related individuals from the same region, this study used KING v2.1.3 [46] to calculate pairwise kinship coefficients. This approach served to validate sampling effectiveness while ensuring relative genetic independence among samples. To maintain genetic information integrity, the analysis was based on VCF files containing sex chromosomes. All sample pairs were evaluated for kinship strength. According to the KING classification criteria, pairs with kinship coefficients greater than 0.177 were defined as closely related (twins and first‑degree relatives).

Genetic diversity analysis

To systematically investigate the dynamics of genetic diversity in the O. cansus population under short-term climate change, we conducted a series of analyses based on whole-genome SNP data from two periods (2016 and 2022). First, we applied PLINK v1.90 [47] to perform linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning on the VCF file (parameters: --indep-pairwise 50 10 0.1), followed by principal component analysis (PCA) to reveal the population genetic structure. Subsequently, the original VCF was subjected to quality control using PLINK v1.90 [47] with parameters --maf 0.05 and --geno 0.01 to obtain a high-quality SNP set for downstream analyses. Using this filtered dataset, we calculated minor allele frequency (MAF) with PLINK and categorized the SNPs into five MAF intervals (0–0.1, 0.1–0.2, 0.2–0.3, 0.3–0.4, 0.4–0.5). A chi-square test was then applied to assess whether the allele frequency distribution had been reshaped between the two time points. In parallel, we used VCFtools v0.1.16 [45] to calculate three population genetic metrics: individual observed heterozygosity (Hₒ, using --het), and nucleotide diversity (π) and Tajima’s D in 100 kb non-overlapping windows (using --window-pi 100000 and --TajimaD 100000, respectively). Differences between temporal groups in Hₒ were assessed with an independent samples t-test, while differences in π and Tajima’s D were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Finally, based on the initial quality-controlled VCF, we employed PopLDdecay [48] with its default parameters to analyze and compare the genome-wide linkage disequilibrium decay patterns between the two time points.

Genomic inbreeding and genetic load

Runs of Homozygosity (ROH) serve as direct genetic markers for assessing the extent of inbreeding in individuals, with their total length showing a significant positive correlation with the individual inbreeding coefficient [49]. By quantifying the distribution characteristics of ROH within a population, it is possible to systematically evaluate the level of inbreeding and the degree of genetic diversity loss [50]. Using SNP data from the filtering pipeline (detailed in ‘SNP Calling and Filtering’), we identified ROHs with PLINK v1.90 [47] using the following parameters: --homozyg --homozyg-density 10 --homozyg-gap 100 --homozyg-kb 100 --homozyg-snp 10 --homozyg-window-het 1 --homozyg-window-snp 50 --homozyg-window-threshold 0.05 --homozyg-window-missing 5 --homozyg-het 1. Regions with ROH lengths greater than 100 kb were selected, and the individual inbreeding coefficient (FROH) was calculated as FROH = LROH / Lauto, where LROH is the total length of ROHs per individual and Lauto is the total length of the autosomes [51]. Differences in FROH between the two temporal groups were compared using an independent samples t-test. Concurrently, the CMplot package was used to divide the genome into 1 Mb non-overlapping windows and generate ROH density plots to visualize the distribution of homozygous segments [52].

Accelerated climate change and intensified human activities are generally recognized as external drivers of natural population decline [53], whereas the accumulation of genetic load within populations is a core genomic factor leading to their rapid deterioration [34, 54, 55]. Notably, inbreeding can significantly increase the expressed burden of genetic load by elevating the homozygous exposure of deleterious alleles, a process defined as the genomic mechanism of inbreeding depression [56, 57]. To compare the dynamics of genetic load between different years, we annotated all biallelic SNP sites for all individuals using the standard annotation protocol of SnpEff v4.3 [56, 57] with default parameters. Prior to annotation, all sites containing missing genotypes were removed to mitigate the influence of differences in sequencing coverage. Based on their functional impact, the genetic load was categorized into three types: loss-of-function, missense, and synonymous. Using the annotation results, we extracted genotype information for sites belonging to each functional category to achieve precise quantification of the genetic load. The specific calculation procedure involved: first, inferring the ancestral allele for each site based on the outgroup genotype; subsequently, counting the number of homozygous derived sites for each individual within the three functional categories, thereby obtaining their absolute genetic load values; finally, employing an independent samples t-test to analyze differences in each category of genetic load between the 2016 and 2022 populations.

Reconstruction of recent effective population size

We used GONE [58] to estimate recent changes in effective population size (Nₑ) for the whole population. This method quantifies linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNP pairs across different intervals of recombination rate and identifies the series of Nₑ values that best explains the observed LD spectrum. Due to the lack of a species-specific genetic map, we employed the software’s default recombination rate parameter (1 cM/Mb). Using SNP data from the filtering pipeline, we first converted the files to PLINK-compatible PED and MAP formats, which were then reformatted to meet GONE’s input requirements. To evaluate variation in Nₑ estimates across multiple runs, we performed 10 replicate experiments for each analysis group, each involving random resampling of 500,000 SNPs (similar to bootstrapping) to quantify the stability of the estimates.

Identification of cross-temporal genomic divergence regions

The combined use of Fixation Index (FST) and nucleotide diversity (π) has proven to be an effective approach for detecting regions under selective sweep, particularly in identifying functional genes closely associated with environmental adaptation [59, 60]. We calculated FST between the 2016 and 2022 populations and estimated nucleotide diversity (π) for each group with VCFtools v0.1.16 [45], applying a 100 kb window size and 50 kb step size. We further computed the π ratio (2022/2016) to assess temporal changes in genetic diversity. To improve reliability, all windows containing fewer than 20 SNPs were excluded. Results were visualized using the R package ggplot2 [61]. We identified candidate selection regions by extracting the top 1% of FST windows and the extreme 1% of π ratio windows (both high and low). Functional annotation of variants within these regions was performed using the standard annotation protocol of SnpEff v4.3 [62] with default parameters. Given the limited annotation resources for the target species, we further performed enrichment analysis using ShinyGO v0.85 [63] with its default settings, using Rattus norvegicus as the reference background. Following the identification of significantly enriched terms (FDR < 0.05, minimum of 2 genes per term), the corresponding pathways underwent biological interpretation.

Comparative analysis of WGBS data

For DNA methylation analysis, we aligned paired-end sequencing reads from O. cansus to the North American pika reference genome (GCA_030435755.1_mOchPri1.hap1, NCBI) using Bismark v0.24.2 [64] with the alignment score threshold set to --score-min L,0,-0.6. PCR duplicates were subsequently removed using deduplicate_bismark, and genome-wide methylation information was extracted via bismark_methylation_extractor. To ensure data quality, we performed a binomial test for each methylation site in every sample, setting the expected conversion efficiency to 99%. We retained only high-confidence methylation sites that met the following criteria: coverage > 10× and an FDR-corrected Q-value < 0.01.

Differential methylation analysis was performed using methylKit v1.32.1 [65]. To mitigate PCR amplification bias, we excluded sites with extreme coverage (> 99.9th percentile) and normalized read counts using the sample median coverage. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were called in the genome using 1000 bp non-overlapping windows, requiring a mean methylation difference > 10% and a Q-value < 0.01.

A differentially methylated gene (DMG) is defined as a gene that overlaps with a differentially methylated region (DMR) within its gene body or flanking regions (± 2 kb). Given the limited annotation resources for the target species, we further performed enrichment analysis using ShinyGO v0.85 [63] with its default settings, using Rattus norvegicus as the reference background. Following the identification of significantly enriched terms (FDR < 0.05, minimum of 2 genes per term), the corresponding pathways underwent biological interpretation.

Results

Data quality and kinship among samples

The final genome datasets comprised 766.8 Gb of high-quality sequences, with an average Q30 ratio of 93.31%, ensuring high base-calling reliability (Supplementary Table S4). Kinship analysis with KING v2.1.3 [46] showed that all sample pairs had kinship coefficients ranging from 0.0933 to 0.1476 (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S6). This result indicates that all sampled individuals originated from the same small geographic population, consistent with the ‘local small population’ framework established for this study. Furthermore, since no first-degree relatives or twins requiring exclusion were detected in the samples, all individuals were retained for subsequent analysis. This outcome ensures relative genetic independence among the samples and provides a reliable basis for investigating short-term genetic and epigenetic variation in the population.

Temporal dynamics in genetic diversity

Principal Component Analysis (PCA, Supplementary Fig. 1) of the SNP data showed that the 2016 and 2022 samples overlapped in the genetic space without forming distinct temporal clusters, suggesting the genetic structure remained stable over the six-year period. Neither the observed heterozygosity (Hₒ, Fig. 2A) nor nucleotide diversity (π, Fig. 2B) showed significant differences between the two temporal groups (Hₒ: independent samples t-test, p > 0.05; π: Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p > 0.05), indicating no marked fluctuation in the overall level of genetic variation within the population. However, Tajima’s D values (Fig. 2C) showed a highly significant decreasing trend (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.001) in samples of 2022, and the proportion of windows with positive values significantly decreased.

Analysis of the Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) distribution (Fig. 2D) further validated this genetic reshuffling trend. The chi-square test revealed a highly significant change in the allele frequency distribution of the population between 2016 and 2022 (χ² = 75,510, df = 4, p < 0.001). The proportion of low-frequency alleles (0-0.1) increased significantly from 18.6% to 27.6%.

Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) decay analysis (Fig. 2E) showed that the LD decayed faster in the 2016 group than in the 2022 group, reflecting a lower level of linkage disequilibrium within the 2016 genomes. Moreover, the ROH density distribution plots (Fig. 3A and B) visually present the distribution characteristics of ROH across the genome, with density differences among chromosomes reflecting the temporal heterogeneity of inbreeding events. The inbreeding coefficient (FROH) calculated based on ROH (Fig. 3C) revealed that the inbreeding level was significantly lower in the 2022 group compared to the 2016 group (independent samples t-test, p < 0.05), suggesting a mitigation of inbreeding pressure within the population between 2016 and 2022.

Moreover, we found no significant differences between the two groups for loss-of-function (LOF, Fig. 3D), missense (Fig. 3E), or synonymous genetic load (Fig. 3F) (independent samples t-test, p > 0.05). This indicates that despite the allele frequency reconfiguration, the accumulation of deleterious mutations did not change significantly.

Recent effective population size dynamics

We estimated recent effective population size (Nₑ) dynamics of O. cansus using GONE [58]. Based on 10 subsampling replicates (each with 500,000 randomly selected SNPs), results revealed consistent fluctuation patterns in Nₑ across individual replicates (orange lines) and their mean (black line) (Fig. 3G). Across a broad temporal scale encompassing 200 generations, Nₑ maintained relatively elevated levels between approximately generations 50 and 140, prior to a gradual decline. However, detailed analysis of the most recent 30 generations revealed a recovery in Nₑ commencing approximately 15 generations ago.

Identification of genomic differentiation regions across time periods

To investigate the genetic response of the O. cansus to short-term climate change, we conducted a genome-wide scan using a sliding window approach (100 kb windows, 50 kb step size) that integrated the Fixation Index (FST) and the nucleotide diversity ratio (π ratio, 2022/2016). The analysis revealed minimal genetic differentiation between the 2016 and 2022 populations (mean FST = 0.0021) and no significant difference in nucleotide diversity (mean π ratio = 1.024), consistent with expectations for a single geographic population over a short timeframe.

Through combined analysis of FST and π ratio (Fig. 4A), we identified 45 candidate genes (Supplementary Table S7) that showed outlier signals in both metrics, with their differentiation patterns visualized in Manhattan plots for FST (Fig. 4B) and π ratio (Fig. 4C). Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that the candidate genes were significantly associated with carbohydrate metabolism and glycosylation processes (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table S8). Specifically, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted key pathways in glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, including mannose-type O-glycan biosynthesis and galactose metabolism. In parallel, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed strong enrichment for carbohydrate kinase activities, such as glucokinase and hexokinase.

Genome-wide DNA methylation patterns and dynamic changes across time

To reveal the epigenetic response of O. cansus to short-term climate change, this study performed Whole-Genome Bisulfite Sequencing (WGBS) on samples from 2016 to 2022. A total of approximately 1643.8 Gb of high-quality data was obtained from 14 samples, with the Q30 base percentage ranging from 85.7% to 90.7% (Supplementary Table S5). The average mapping rate for O. cansus samples was 35.6% (Supplementary Table S5), which aligns with expectations when using a reference genome from a closely related species and meets the requirements for subsequent analysis.

The average methylation level at CG sites across the population was 62.2%–69.3% (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table S5), consistent with typical methylation levels in mammals [66–68]. Distribution analysis across gene bodies and flanking ± 2 kb regions showed that methylated sites were predominantly located in intronic regions, followed by exonic regions (Supplementary Table S9). Further analysis of methylation trends across gene bodies and their flanking ± 2 kb regions revealed that O. cansus exhibits the classic vertebrate methylation pattern of ‘genome-wide high methylation with low methylation near the Transcription Start Site (TSS)’ (Fig. 5B), confirming the biological validity of the data [66].

Comparative analysis identified 188 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between the two time points, comprising 17 hyper-methylated and 171 hypo-methylated regions (Supplementary Table S10). These DMRs were associated with 107 differentially methylated genes (DMGs), including GLIS3, FZD4, and STYXL2 (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table S11). The notable predominance of hypo-methylated DMRs suggests that demethylation at these loci is a predominant feature of the local epigenetic response in O. cansus to sustained climate change.

Functional enrichment analysis of DMGs revealed that the significantly enriched Gene Ontology Biological Process terms were primarily involved in development, proliferation, and stress response. Key representative terms included organ development, regulation of cell proliferation, and response to stimulus, among other related processes (Supplementary Table S12, Supplementary Fig. 2).

Genetic and epigenetic co-response of key candidate genes

To systematically decipher the molecular adaptation of O. cansus to environmental change, this study integrated population genomic and epigenomic analyses to identify core genes exhibiting coordinated responses. Cross-omics analysis identified only four genes (TRNAL-CAG, FZD4, MAMDC2, and GLIS3) at the intersection of the top 1% of FST, extreme nucleotide diversity (π ratio), and differential methylation, highlighting them as key adaptive candidates (Fig. 5D).

Further analysis focused on GLIS3 and FZD4, which exhibited the most extreme methylation changes. Both genes demonstrated characteristic co-response patterns across genomic and epigenomic levels (Fig. 6). Genomically, both genes were located near regions overlapping local FST peaks and extreme troughs of log₂ (π ratio) (Fig. 6A and B), providing strong evidence for recent natural selection. Epigenetically, DMRs were identified within the gene body regions of both genes, with the DMR in FZD4 overlapping part of its coding sequence (CDS) (Fig. 6C and D). Compared with 2016, the gene body DMRs of both GLIS3 and FZD4 showed significant demethylation in 2022 (Fig. 6E and F). These synchronous directional changes at both genetic and epigenetic levels collectively point to a central role for GLIS3 and FZD4 in the short-term climate adaptation of this species. The feature of the FZD4 DMR directly overlapping part of its CDS further suggests that methylation participates in the adaptive process by modulating the epigenetic state of coding regions.

Discussion

Genetic dynamics on a short timescale: homeostasis maintenance and adaptive reconfiguration

Genetic diversity serves as the foundation for species adaptation to environmental change, and its temporal dynamics can reveal climate adaptation strategies and predict population persistence potential [69]. By comparing genomic data over a six-year interval from a pika population living at the alpine-treeline ecotone, we uncovered a genetic response strategy that combines resilience and flexibility in the face of climate change. While caution is warranted in interpreting ‘recovery’ from limited temporal snapshots, we found that, while maintaining overall genetic diversity and structural stability, Gansu pikas exhibit effective management of potential genetic load and a capacity for adaptive reconfiguration across the genome over this short temporal scale.

Overall, we detected a high degree of overlap in the genetic space without temporal clusters, and neither nucleotide diversity (π) nor observed heterozygosity (Hₒ) differed significantly between groups (Fig. 2). While stable π and Hₒ do not alone constitute definitive evidence of ‘genetic recovery,’ they indicate that the standing reservoir of genetic variation was not substantially eroded over this period, providing a crucial foundation for potential future adaptation [69, 70]. However, beneath this apparent stability, active adaptive changes were occurring. The significant decrease in Tajima’s D, coupled with a marked reshuffling of the MAF distribution, collectively points to a rapid restructuring of allele frequencies, characterized by an increase in low-frequency alleles. This pattern is compatible with an adaptation mode resembling a ‘soft sweep,’ where selection may act on multiple low-frequency variants, allowing rapid genetic adjustment while preserving overall diversity—a feature postulated in short-term adaptive evolution [71–74].

Admittedly, the observed shift in Tajima’s D could also arise from other processes, including demographic changes (e.g., population expansion), background selection, or their interplay with positive selection [75, 76]. Disentangling the relative contributions of these forces remains challenging over such a short timescale. Furthermore, the limited sample size may reduce the statistical power to detect subtle changes and increase the variance in population genetic estimates. Therefore, while the ‘soft sweep’ model provides a coherent explanation for the concurrent genomic signals, the conclusions should be considered alongside these alternative possibilities and methodological constraints.

The Gansu pika population also showed positive signals in managing its genetic health. Analysis based on ROH revealed that the inbreeding coefficient was significantly lower in the 2022 group compared to the 2016 group, indicating a mitigation of inbreeding pressure over this approximately 12-generation period. This directly reduces the risk of homozygous exposure of deleterious recessive alleles [77]. Furthermore, the reconstruction of recent effective population size (Nₑ) dynamics using GONE [58] showed a recovery beginning approximately 15 generations ago (Fig. 3). This recovery in effective population size likely contributed to the observed mitigation of inbreeding, as a larger pool of potential mates reduces the probability of mating between related individuals. When inbreeding is effectively suppressed, deleterious mutations are more likely to remain ‘latent’ in the genome in a heterozygous state [78]. Consequently, despite the genetic reshuffling, no significant changes were found in loss-of-function, missense, or synonymous genetic load between the two temporal groups. The observed ‘stability’ of genetic load in this study should be interpreted as a ‘healthy steady state’ or a ‘favorable balance of load,’ which is crucial for the population to maintain its long-term adaptive potential [55].

While the six-year interval of this study (approximately 12 generations) is evolutionarily brief, it provides a valuable snapshot of genomic dynamics in a population facing rapid environmental change. We acknowledge that this timeframe is insufficient to observe long-term shifts in allele frequencies or the full accumulation of genetic load that might occur over centuries. The stability of genetic load we observed should therefore be interpreted as a short -term equilibrium rather than a permanent state. However, in the context of the rapidly transforming alpine treeline ecotone [71, 72], where selective pressures are intensified, even a few generations can be critical for adaptive responses. The significant reduction in genomic inbreeding we detected underscores that meaningful genetic changes can occur within this period. In light of these findings, we propose that the pika population is exhibiting a state of ‘dynamic resilience’—actively mitigating inbreeding while maintaining a stable load profile amidst genomic reshuffling. This strategy may be essential for persistence in a volatile environment. Future studies spanning decades or utilizing temporal genomic series will be crucial to validate and extend these observations over longer evolutionary timescales.

Viewing this ‘dynamic resilience’ through the evolutionary context of alpine species underscores its significant value. The alpine treeline ecotone is characterized by high environmental heterogeneity and is undergoing rapid change [79, 80]. Species that have persisted long-term in such environments may have evolved a suite of unique “load resistance reserves” mechanisms, including enhanced purging of deleterious alleles through strong local selection driven by gene flow barriers in mountainous terrain, and the maintenance of rich genetic diversity “reserves” via historical processes such as glacial microrefugia [81–84]. Thereby, these adaptive mechanisms confer resilience in the face of environmental variability. This dynamic balancing strategy highlights the complex evolutionary resilience of alpine treeline species in a changing environment.

Cross-temporal genetic divergence and epigenetic regulation in concerted adaptation

Through functional analysis of highly differentiated genomic regions and differentially methylated genes, we revealed the molecular regulatory strategies employed by Gansu pikas in response to alpine environmental changes at both genetic and epigenetic levels. This provides direct evidence for understanding the rapid adaptation mechanisms of treeline species.

At the genetic level, a combined analysis based on the population differentiation index (FST) and nucleotide diversity ratio (π ratio) successfully identified 45 candidate genes (Supplementary Table S7). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism and glycosylation biosynthesis (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Table S8), including Galactose metabolism and Mannose type O-glycan biosynthesis. This finding closely corresponds to the characteristic environmental changes in the alpine treeline ecotone. There, climate warming drives habitat alterations that may reshape the structure and availability of food resources. Specifically, between 2016 and 2022, the study area underwent pronounced local transformations characterized by an upward treeline shift, increased shrubification of meadows, and notable changes in plant community structure [85, 86]. Given that sugars are not only core energy substrates but their derived glycosylation modifications also extensively participate in protein function regulation and cellular signal transduction [87], the selection signals detected in these pathways may indicate that the population is fine-tuning fundamental physiological processes like energy metabolism and nutrient utilization. This optimization likely aims to enhance its energy budget and maintain physiological homeostasis in the changing environment [88, 89].

At the epigenetic level, the regulatory response demonstrated a broader range of functional involvement. Although the genome-wide average methylation level remained stable, we detected strong localized functional signals across 107 Differentially Methylated Genes (DMGs) (Supplementary Table S11). It is particularly important to note that while the WGBS data were aligned to a reference genome of a congeneric species, the observed classic pattern of genome-wide high methylation with low methylation near Transcription Start Sites (TSS) (Fig. 5B) strongly confirms the biological validity of the data [66, 90], providing a reliable foundation for subsequent analysis. Functional enrichment analysis of these DMGs showed significant concentration in multiple biological processes related to development, proliferation, and stress response (Supplementary Table S12, Supplementary Fig. 2). These included regulation of cell proliferation, organ development, response to stimulus, and more specific terms such as sensory organ development and circulatory system development. These diverse functions are directly relevant to the multifaceted stressors of the alpine environment. ‘Sensory organ development’ might be linked to habitat structural complexity induced by shrubification, potentially driving adaptive adjustments in perceptual capabilities [91, 92]. ‘Circulatory system development’ could be associated with the reinforcement of the cardiovascular system in response to temperature fluctuations and environmental stress [93–96]. This may include a potential for rapid acclimatization to oxygen levels at higher altitudes, as evidenced by studies showing swift cardiovascular adjustments in other species at high elevations [97–99]. This extensive reprogramming at the epigenetic level enables the population to rapidly adjust gene expression networks related to morphological, physiological, and behavioral plasticity without altering the DNA sequence. Thereby, it provides a capacity for rapid response to complex environmental changes, effectively compensating for the temporal lag inherent in genetic adaptation [100, 101].

Coordinated genetic and epigenetic response of key genes to short-term environmental change

Through the integration of population genetics and epigenomics analyses, this study identified candidate genes potentially playing critical roles in responding to short-term environmental changes. Cross-omics analysis revealed that both GLIS3 and FZD4 exhibited significant genetic differentiation signals and differential methylation in their gene body regions when comparing the 2016 and 2022 time periods (Figs. 5D and 6). This synchronous change at both genetic and epigenetic levels suggests these two genes likely possess important functions in the adaptive process of O. cansus to rapid changes in the alpine environment.

The FZD4 gene (Frizzled class receptor 4) is a key receptor in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [102]. The encoded Frizzled-4 protein belongs to the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family and is characterized by multiple transmembrane domains. In mammals, FZD4, by mediating the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, plays a central role in crucial biological processes including cell fate determination, tissue homeostasis maintenance, and vasculature development [103–105]. This study not only detected signatures of selection at the FZD4 locus but also observed significant demethylation in its gene body region, which partially overlapped with its coding sequence (CDS) (Fig. 6A, C and E). Studies have shown that altered methylation status in gene bodies is associated with transcription elongation efficiency, mRNA stability, or co-transcriptional regulation processes [106–108]. In the alpine treeline ecosystem, climate warming-driven meadow shrubification may pose multidimensional challenges to the pika’s physiological homeostasis by altering temperature and humidity conditions within burrows, affecting their ventilation, and modifying the surrounding vegetation that serves as foraging grounds [109, 110]. Therefore, the demethylation in the CDS region of the FZD4 gene might indicate its involvement, through fine-tuning at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level, in adaptive mechanisms related to tissue repair and homeostasis maintenance in response to environmental fluctuations.

The GLIS3 gene (GLIS family zinc finger 3) is an important member of the GLIS family transcription factors. As a DNA-binding transcription regulator, it can perform dual functions of transcriptional activation or repression depending on the physiological context [102, 111]. The encoded protein contains five C2H2-type zinc finger domains, localizes to the nucleus, and is a core transcription factor regulating insulin secretion, glucose metabolic homeostasis, and pancreatic beta cell development [112–114]. Our analysis revealed that GLIS3 underwent significant demethylation in its gene body, concurrent with genetic selection signals (Fig. 6B, D and F). Similar to FZD4, the epigenetic alteration in the gene body region of GLIS3 might facilitate fine-tuning of the energy metabolism network by influencing its transcriptional activity or post-transcriptional regulation [106, 108]. The processes of alpine meadow shrubification and treeline ascent, accompanied by changes in plant community structure, may reshape the composition and availability of food resources [85, 86]. These changes would necessitate more flexible metabolic regulatory capacity to adapt to altered patterns of energy intake and nutritional structure. This flexible regulation, achieved through epigenetic remodeling of GLIS3, may enable the population to maintain metabolic homeostasis in an environment with fluctuating energy resources.

Study limitations and future perspectives

While this study provides novel insights into short-term genetic and epigenetic adaptation, several limitations should be acknowledged. The six-year period, though capturing rapid dynamics, is brief on an evolutionary timescale; our interpretations regarding genetic load stability thus reflect a short-term equilibrium within the proposed state of ‘dynamic resilience.’ Furthermore, while genomic and epigenomic patterns offer strong inference, the current design cannot delineate ultimate causal mechanisms. Specifically, tracking the same individuals through time and incorporating more rapidly responsive traits (e.g., tissue-specific gene expression) would be required to establish direct mechanistic links between environmental change and molecular responses. Methodologically, our inferences are constrained by the use of a congeneric reference genome, the absence of procedural blank controls (e.g., no-template controls) during DNA extraction and library preparation, and a moderate bisulfite sequencing mapping rate that may limit epigenomic resolution.

These limitations chart clear pathways for future research. Extending the evolutionary timeframe through long-term longitudinal monitoring and paleogenomic analyses of historical specimens would bridge our short-term observations with centennial-scale trajectories. To enhance mechanistic resolution, building a chromosome-level genome for O. cansus and functionally validating candidate genes and epigenetic markers—via gene editing or controlled transcriptomic studies—are crucial next steps. Finally, integrative ecological genomics that couples temporal genomic data with concurrent measurements of microhabitat, individual fitness, and demography would directly quantify selection pressures and their consequences. Addressing these areas will solidify our understanding of how alpine endemics navigate rapid climate change.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals that swift adaptive evolution can unfold over surprisingly brief periods through the coordinated interplay of genetic and epigenetic mechanisms within a wild alpine mammal species. Our sampled Gansu pika population from the Wolong National Nature Reserve maintained genomic stability while achieving adaptive fine-tuning through allele frequency shifts and localized gene body hypomethylation. This coupled genetic-epigenetic strategy provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how alpine treeline species persist in rapidly changing environments.
