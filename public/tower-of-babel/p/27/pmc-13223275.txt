Modeling and optimizing mileage homogeneity in public transportation using Gini index
Authors: Burak Sevim, Berk Ayvaz, Fahrettin Eldemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFleets of city buses experience uneven allocation of miles, which causes uneven wear, elevated maintenance expenditures, and inefficiencies when providing service. This research proposes the application of the Gini index as a base-objective function for attaining a higher level of homogeneity of miles traveled by fleets. Using 756 days of operating data from 160 articulated public buses serving Makkah, Saudi Arabia, the research initially assesses the scope of each day’s mileage inequalities using descriptives and Lorenz curve interpretation. The results suggest unevenness during the early operations significantly, whereby the Gini attains a maximum of 0.38, diminishing as allocation tactics by heuristics were undertaken. To further reduce the level of unevenness, an integer model employing the Gurobi optimizer was formulated for Python. Used for a subset of 25 buses, the model reduced the Gini from a level of 0.1648 (mean) down to a level of 0.0462, exhibiting a significant improvement regarding fairness of work. Beyond the numeric contribution, the research results call attention further to operational merits, such as level maintenance scheduling, expanded lifetimes for vehicles, and increased sustainability. While the analysis focuses on a single large-scale urban bus network, the proposed framework can be extended to multi-route and multi-modal systems, incorporating demand variability and additional equity dimensions in future research. By integrating the Gini index as a transport planning objective into the vehicle assignment problem naturally, the research connects transport justice with the efficiency of operations, providing transport agencies with a functional model for sustainable and equitable bus operations.Keywords: Gini Index, Optimization, Equity, Homogeneity, Public transportation, MaintenanceSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Public transportation systems are the backbone infrastructure components in the centers of contemporary cities, which provide environmentally sustainable mobility options. Due to growing urban population and urbanization, the efficiency and effectiveness of public systems have become more significant. By serving multiple types of users with multiple daily trips, public transportation offers various advantages including decreasing traffic congestion, decreasing air pollution, and maximizing the use of energy.

The efficient operations of public transport networks rely on the smooth interface of various parts. The bus fleet is one of the most fundamental parts of such networks. Comprising the heart of the urban transport network, the city’s bus fleet supports the daily travel hundreds of thousands of people. Managing such a fleet is inherently complex and multi-dimensional. Passenger demand, routing, maintenance requirements, and driver management, among other variables, need to be carefully considered in order to keep the operations efficient and sustainable.

Thus, among the major issues in bus fleet operations is the inconsistency in the release of daily mileage. On any fleet of vehicles, some vehicles cover long distances in the day while others cover relatively small routes. The inconsistency results in disproportionate wear and tear of vehicles as well as rising maintenance costs. More frequent maintenance with vehicles because of high use doesn’t only add the cost of operations but also extends the vehicles’ downtime. Such inconsistency is harmful in disrupting the fleet’s overall efficiency as well as the efficiency of public services.

Achievement of homogeneity in the miles traveled by bus fleets - that is, ensuring as much as possible that all the buses run the same number of miles - is one significant goal of efficient fleet management. Achievement of the goal is maximally advantageous in terms of vehicles wear and tear, maintenance costs, and uniformity in services due to minimized downtime. In addition to that, fair and effective utilization of available resources is also vital in the attainment of sustainable public transport networks.

The study recommends the use of the Gini Index as a way of facilitating more equitable mileage redistribution across bus fleets. Whilst the Gini Index may be most famously associated with the measuring of income inequality, it is no less applicable when exploring how well distributed are the assets. By measuring inequalities in a dataset, it gives an clear numerical insight into inequality–the contrast in kilometers travelled across the many different buses. The use of this index across public transport allows possible detection of imbalances whilst also informs the development of certain measures aimed at more equitable division of daily travel. Through this, it thus fosters more efficient working as well as more equitable use of assets.

The paper mentions the use of the Gini index as an aid in the achievement of consistency in mileage in bus fleets. Though in general used as an index of income inequality, the Gini Index is just as accurate in the measurement of the equitability in the distribution of the resources. Being used in the measurement of the differences in the data set, the Gini index provides us with the numeric value of the asymmetry of the kilometers traveled distribution of the vehicles. Through the use of the index in public networks, there is the ability in the design of efficient measures that would provide maximum daily distribution of the mileage, the end result improving the efficiency in the operations as well as the equitability in the available resources.

The purpose of this research is to offer an innovative way of improving the homogeneity of bus fleet mileage using the Gini Index. The analysis in this context will be focused on the examination of the current homogeneity of vehicle mileage distribution, the estimation of the Gini index, and the use of optimization models. The study will use this method with the objective of helping in the more effective and environmentally sustainable management of bus fleets.

Even as the literature on equity in transit continues to expand, significant areas for contribution exist in the operationalization of fairness in fleet administration, especially in the allocation of mileage among vehicles.

While equity was reviewed in a comprehensive manner in the case of accessibility, travel time, and cost, the issue of mileage equity in bus fleets was not considered much. Previous studies overwhelmingly use the Gini index descriptively, although use as an objective of optimization has been sparingly studied. Moreover, most such studies are small in scale or theoretical in nature, with limited exploration on practical transit networks. Filling in the blanks, the current study embeds the Gini index as a primary objective in a block–vehicle task assignment problem for articulated buses. Using data from Makkah for a duration of over two years, the problem establishes through the model that mileage equity improvement not only maximizes fairness but also benefits maintenance scheduling, fleets longevity, and sustainability in transit operation. While thus filling the gap between transport justice on the one hand and operation optimization on the other hand, the study provides practical insights for resource allocators who are seeking balanced resource distribution.

This study starts with a literature review on transportation equity and corresponding measurement approaches and introduces a Gini index-oriented system for measuring and optimizing homogeneity in bus mileage distribution. Data collection processes are made a part of methodology and extend the usability of Gini index through empirical data from Makkah including the computation of 756 Gini values for a duration of two years in an attempt to understand temporal distribution patterns. An integer program is then defined, comprising well-established objective function, constraints, and decision variables. Formulation is then applied on a selected bus route of 25 vehicles, programmed in Python via Gurobi solver, and optimized via Branch-and-Bound algorithm. Annual optimal assignment plan identifies a significant minimization of Gini index, favoring equity in distribution of fleets.

Research problem and objectives

Achieving balance in the assignment of buses to service operations in public transportation systems is essential. When such balance is not maintained, maintenance planning becomes more challenging and may result in additional costs for operating companies. In an environment where cost reduction is a priority and competition is increasing, the efficient and balanced utilization of resources is of critical importance. Even though equity and sustainability are becoming more and more important in public transportation planning, operational equity in bus fleet management–specifically, the fair distribution of vehicle mileage–is still not given enough attention. Therefore, the main research issue that this study attempts to solve is: How can equity in bus mileage allocation be explicitly operationalized and optimized through a rigorous mathematical framework in public transportation systems?

Literature gap

An examination of the existing literature on the Gini index indicates that prior studies have predominantly focused on the expansion of public transportation services or on the evaluation of service levels. However, there is a notable lack of research examining resource efficiency and balance in public transportation planning. The majority of studies treat the Gini index as a descriptive indicator rather than directly incorporating it into optimization models, despite the substantial expansion of the literature on transport equity. Furthermore, in large-scale, actual public transportation systems, equity in vehicle-level operational workload–particularly mileage distribution among buses–remains largely unexplored.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has used the Gini index as the main objective function for optimizing the mileage of a large bus fleet under actual operational constraints.

Nonetheless, most equity metrics, like the Gini index, are mostly used to describe or evaluate things, not to make decisions that are focused on optimization. This is especially true in large, policy-driven public transportation systems.

Although numerous equity indicators have been suggested in transportation literature, including entropy-based measures like the Theil index, need-based approaches such as alpha-fairness, and variance-oriented distribution metrics, their direct incorporation into extensive operational optimization models is still constrained. Entropy-based indices frequently necessitate logarithmic adjustments and display susceptibility to outliers, complicating their integration into mixed-integer formulas. Likewise, alpha-fairness methodologies rely on inequality aversion parameters, necessitating further calibration and subjective assumptions. Variance-based measures, while computationally less complex, predominantly measure absolute dispersion and may not adequately represent relative disparity in diverse fleet contexts. Conversely, the Gini index offers an advantageous equilibrium among interpretability, responsiveness to distributional inequality, and mathematical manageability. The formulation, grounded in pairwise absolute deviations, facilitates explicit linearization via auxiliary variables, permitting its direct use as an objective function in a mixed-integer linear programming context. The Gini index is particularly appropriate for equity-focused optimization in public transportation operations, where transparency, scalability, and repeatability are critical.

In addition to its computational feasibility, the Gini index offers a theoretically unique viewpoint in contrast to dispersion metrics based on variance or entropy. Variance quantifies absolute dispersion from the mean, but the Gini index assesses relative inequality throughout the distribution, aligning it more closely with equity-focused interpretations. In fleet operations, the Gini index indicates proportionate workload disparities rather than solely numerical discrepancies. Thus, reducing the Gini index directly addresses structural inequality in workload distribution, providing a more explicit managerial understanding of equity and balanced resource usage.

In this study, Gini index is utilized for measuring homogeneity in bus kilometer distribution using a new equity-oriented objective function. Contrary to the common optimization methods where minimization of cost or maximization of efficiency is focused, the present paper seeks to strike a balance in operational load in harmony with spatial discrepancy principles.

Methodology

Methodological framework

This study employs a systematic and transparent methodological approach to implement fairness in bus fleet management via mileage uniformity. The framework has four consecutive stages: (i) measurement of baseline inequality, (ii) formulation of the optimization model, (iii) derivation of the exact solution using a branch-and-bound algorithm, and (iv) comparative assessment of optimized and observed results.

Initially, actual operational data are utilized to calculate daily and cumulative Gini index values, establishing a baseline evaluation of mileage disparity throughout the fleet. This step determines the magnitude and temporal progression of imbalance before any optimization measures are implemented.

In the second phase, an optimization model is developed that incorporates mileage homogeneity, quantified by the Gini index, directly into the objective function. The proposed formulation diverges from traditional fleet optimization models that focus on cost reduction or efficiency enhancement by explicitly minimizing disparities in vehicle workload while adhering to operational feasibility constraints, such as comprehensive service block coverage, single-task assignment per vehicle per day, and annual mileage planning for each vehicle. To facilitate a precise solution, the intrinsically nonlinear Gini index formulation is converted into a corresponding set of linear constraints, yielding a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model.

During the third stage, the MILP model is resolved via a precise branch-and-bound technique executed via the Gurobi Optimizer. Ultimately, optimal outcomes are methodically contrasted with actual mileage distributions to assess the efficacy of the equity-focused methodology.

GINI index

The GINI index is a mathematical index developed first for the purpose of assessing income distribution differences. It is a number that expresses inequality within a population as a quantity ranging from 0 (perfect equality) up to 1 (perfect inequality). In the context of public transport and fleet management, the GINI index is an excellent instrument for assessing inequality within the allocation of everyday mileage for buses. In such an example, the index is computed and provides interesting data for the purpose of attaining a more balanced utilization of buses and a more just allocation of resources.

In bus fleets for public transport, homogenization of mileage allocation is foremost, particularly for the purpose of the optimization of operational and maintenance spending, and for the enhancement of the quality of the service provided. Equalization of mileage utilization across buses forestalls over-utilization on individual buses and rebalances the repair and maintenance requirements across the fleet. The GINI index is an indicator of the extent of equalization of such allocation. The lower the GINI index, the more homogenously allocated the mileage is. A higher GINI index, however, is indicative of a very high degree of heterogeneity or inequality.

GINI index based MILP

Public transit system management can be made more efficient by minimizing mileage usage differences among bus fleets. The GINI index can be an effective tool for evaluating mileage distribution differences among buses, and the index minimization is the key for more even mileage allocation. In this section, we deal with the possibility of incorporating the GINI index as a mathematical constraint and minimizing it afterward.

Though initially created as a statistical tool measuring the extent of income inequality, the GINI index can further be used for determining the homogeneity of the distribution of resources. The index varies from 0 up to the value of 1, and where 0 signifies complete equivalence and 1 signifies total inequality. In public transport systems, the GINI index can be used for evaluating and minimizing differences within the usage of mileage among buses.

Reducing the GINI index for public transport operations tries to make the mileage distribution for the entire fleet uniform, thus causing uniform vehicle wear and tear and evening out maintenance expenditure. The mathematical formulation for reducing the GINI index can be stated as below:

By putting the Gini index into the optimization model’s objective function, the proposed formulation makes it clear how equity issues are related to operational decision-making. Using binary bus-assignment decision variables makes sure that each operational unit is assigned in a way that is possible and doesn’t overlap with any others. Algorithmic framework and solution procedure

Integration of decision variables and Gini index linearization

The optimization model includes binary option variables that assign each bus to a feasible vehicle task, as well as continuous variables representing annual mileage accumulation. Incorporating the Gini index into the optimization framework necessitates the calculation of pairwise absolute differences between total annual bus mileages. Auxiliary continuous variables are utilized to linearize the nonlinearity of absolute value formulas.

This linearization transforms the original nonlinear Gini formulation into a reasonable mixed-integer linear framework, enabling the direct minimization of inequality during the optimization process rather than as a subsequent evaluation. Thus, concepts of equality are intrinsically linked to operational decision variables, ensuring that justice is maintained during optimization rather than subsequently.

Branch-and-bound solution approach

The optimization problem is addressed using a branch-and-bound approach because of the binary assignment decisions involved. Branch-and-bound methodically investigates the solution space by recursively dividing it into smaller subproblems. At every node of the search tree, a linear programming relaxation of the MILP is resolved to provide a lower bound on the objective function.

When the solution at a certain node contravenes integrality criteria, branching is executed on one or more binary decision variables. Bounding criteria are then implemented to discard subproblems with lower bounds that surpass the best-known feasible solution. This procedure persists until the global optimum is discerned within a specified optimality tolerance.

Within the framework of this study, branch-and-bound is crucial as the integration of binary bus-to-task assignments and auxiliary variables implemented for Gini linearization cannot be addressed only by continuous optimization methods. The algorithm ensures global optimality while maintaining operational feasibility and equity goals.

Implementation via the Gurobi optimizer

The branch-and-bound method is executed via the Gurobi Optimizer, which is tailored for addressing extensive mixed-integer linear programming challenges. Gurobi’s presolve routines diminish model size by removing superfluous constraints and refining variable bounds, while cutting-plane techniques enhance the linear relaxation and expedite convergence.

Parallel node exploration significantly improves computational efficiency, allowing the solution to manage the combinatorial complexity posed by equity-based constraints. Solver performance metrics, such as solution duration and the number of nodes examined, are tracked to guarantee transparency and reproducibility of outcomes.

The proposed methodology integrates the branch-and-bound algorithm with the Gurobi optimization environment, establishing a clear and replicable connection among decision variables, equity measurement, and operational constraints, thus addressing issues of methodological transparency and reproducibility.

The computational difficulty escalates with fleet size because to the quadratic increase of pairwise Gini linearization variables; yet, solver efficiency can be optimized by decomposition methodologies, parallelization, and tailored pretreatment techniques.

All computational tests were performed utilizing Gurobi Optimizer version 12.0.1 under an academic Web License Service (WLS) license. The solution was run with the following non-default settings: MIPFocus = 1, Heuristics = 0.5, Presolve = 2, Cuts = 2, and Threads = 0 (which lets it use up to 32 logical processors automatically). The limitations for default feasibility and optimality were kept the same (MIPGap = ). All of the tests were done on a Windows 11 workstation with an Intel Core i9-14900HX CPU. These parameters make sure that the computational framework is clear and can be repeated.

Robustness and sensitivity analysis

To further assess the stability of the proposed optimization framework, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted with respect to planning horizon assumptions and extreme initial mileage values.

First, the planning horizon was cut down from 365 days to 300 and 180 days, but the starting mileage levels stayed the same. The optimal Gini coefficient went up from 0.0462 (365-day horizon) to 0.0506 (300-day horizon) and then to 0.0616 (180-day horizon). These results show that larger planning horizons make the model better at making mileage distribution more even. As the planning horizon shortens, the relative impact of pre-existing cumulative mileage levels intensifies, constraining the extent of feasible equalization within a reduced timeframe. Still, the optimization methodology is structurally sound and works well across all evaluated horizons.

Second, sensitivity tests were done by changing the very high initial mileage values while keeping the 365-day planning horizon. When the lowest accumulated mileage was cut by 10% and the maximum was raised by 10%, the Gini coefficient came out to be 0.0498. With a stronger change (−20% for the lowest and +20% for the greatest initial mileage), the Gini coefficient went up to 0.0535. These findings illustrate that, even in scenarios of heightened starting disparity, the suggested MILP structure preserves significant workload homogeneity capability.

The sensitivity analysis shows that the model is structurally strong even when the planning horizons and beginning mileage distributions change a little. The results also indicate that mileage homogeneity is fundamentally a medium- to long-term planning goal, wherein adequate temporal scope improves the efficacy of equity-driven optimization.

Operational efficiency implications

The main goal of the proposed model is to reduce mileage inequality, however it is important to see if this goal of fairness influences how well the model works. Total service production is kept the same in this formulation since each allocation technique must cover all operational blocks exactly once. So, the overall annual mileage for the system stays the same for all three solutions: random, rotation, and optimized.

The Gini index becomes better not by driving less miles, but by spreading the work out across the vehicles. In other words, the optimization framework doesn’t cut down on service or vehicle use; it just changes the assignments so that the total number of kilometers driven by all the buses is the same each year.

From an efficiency point of view, the average annual mileage per vehicle stays the same as the total operational demand divided by the number of vehicles in the fleet. The MILP method, on the other hand, greatly reduces the spread of mileage across vehicles. This shows that equity can be improved without hurting operational output.

So, the results imply that in this case, mileage homogeneity and operational efficiency are not goals that are at odds with each other. In fact, balancing the workloads of vehicles may improve long-term operational stability by allowing maintenance cycles to be coordinated and cutting down on extreme vehicle abuse.

For the selected subset, the total annual mileage remained constant at 1,731,557.08 km across all allocation methods, while the standard deviation of annual vehicle mileage decreased from 29,674.64 km (baseline) to 16,449.99 km (MILP solution).

Discussion

The results of the present work illustrate the merits of considering equity-oriented objective functions for the optimization of public transport systems. Involving the Gini index for mileage distribution among buses and minimizing its value through optimization, the work reveals that equity can be concretely and numerically operationalized within optimization models. In distinction from the classical models where the main focus is on minimizing the cost or increasing the efficiency of the service, the Gini-based model accounts for the fairness of the vehicle usage, such that the operational loads are more evenly allocated throughout the fleet.

The comparison with baseline allocation schemes underscores the enhanced usefulness of incorporating the Gini index directly into the objective function. Both random allocation and basic rotation rules resulted in moderate workload balance (Gini 0.062), although neither method achieved the significantly lower inequality level attained by optimization (Gini = 0.0462). This suggests that structured equity-driven optimization offers quantifiable benefits over intuitive or heuristic balancing methods.

The optimization solutions demonstrate that for very complex fleet configurations, significant homogeneity improvements can be achieved within computationally manageable frames. Reduction of the Gini index to as low as 0.0462 reaffirms that differences in workload can be decreased substantially by careful block-to-vehicle task allocation strategies. The outcome, in turn, has critical managerial implications: through leveling annual mileage, the optimization not only increases fairness but also enables aligned maintenance scheduling, extends vehicle life, and reduces uncertainty of operations because of anomalous wear and tear.

Moreover, the proposed methodology is compatible with future equity-oriented models of transportation planning. Previous studies emphasized the hard task of balancing efficiency and fairness; however, the current analysis manifests operational efficiency and equity as not contradicting but complementing one another. In fact, mileage homogenization increases system sustainability by preventing the overuse of specific vehicles and underuse of others. Importantly, the reduction in mileage inequality does not come at the expense of operational efficiency, as total service output remains unchanged under the proposed framework. At the same time, the methodology provides an operational framework that can be enriched along other transit equity dimensions such as accessibility, frequency allocation, or environmental impact.

The suggested framework can be put into place in existing transit agency workflows in a progressive way. The optimization model can be used at the yearly or semi-annual planning stage, when the current block schedules are already set. At this point, the model is a tool that helps make decisions by changing the vehicle-task combinations to make mileage more uniform without changing service coverage.

Second, the balanced annual mileage estimates can be sent directly to maintenance departments so that preventative maintenance cycles can be coordinated and the chances of parts wearing out too soon can be lowered. The framework doesn’t replace existing operational planning; it only adds to it because the total service output stays the same.

Third, the model can be run again every month or every three months, for example, utilizing new cumulative mileage data from AVL or fleet management systems. This kind of rolling-horizon deployment would let agencies fix new imbalances slowly while still being able to change how they do things.

The suggested equity-oriented optimization methodology can be integrated into existing planning, assignment, and maintenance coordination processes, serving as an analytical improvement to extant decision-making routines.

In real-world transportation operations, the optimum schedule may not work out as planned if there are unanticipated problems, like a vehicle breaking down, being unavailable for a short time, or having to be moved to a different location. The suggested framework works best when operational conditions are steady over the planning horizon. However, the model structure is flexible enough to handle such changes through periodic re-optimization. In real-world situations, the optimization process can happen on a regular basis (like once a month or once a quarter), which lets new information about vehicle availability and mileage be used in the decision-making process. So, even if unforeseen operational shocks can momentarily throw off the balance of effort, the suggested equity-oriented framework can use adaptive re-planning to systematically restore homogeneity.

However, there are certain limitations to be considered. The model was tested for one route with 25 buses that, although representative enough, could not deliver the complete complexity of the entire operational network. In addition, the study utilized the day mileage data as the primary equity measure regardless of passenger demands and revenue emphases, both of which can arguably affect fairness observations. Future research can expand the scope by introducing multi-objective formulations where equity is paired with cost, quality of service, and environmental concerns and therefore having a more complete perspective.

The optimization was deliberately showcased on a representative sample of 25 busses to furnish a transparent and computationally feasible proof of concept. The proposed MILP formulation is not intrinsically constrained to this scale. The quantity of binary decision variables escalates in direct correlation with the number of buses and viable vehicle duties, while the count of auxiliary variables for Gini linearization expands quadratically with fleet size. Despite the rise in computational complexity, contemporary MILP solvers like Gurobi can manage far bigger problem instances, especially when utilizing presolve reductions and parallel processing. Consequently, the framework can be expanded to accommodate larger fleets or multi-route setups, contingent upon the availability of suitable computational resources.

From a methodological perspective, this study differs from prior equity-focused transportation research by embedding the Gini index directly into the optimization process rather than using it solely as an evaluative metric. Utilizing the Gini index as an objective function redirects the analytical emphasis from minimizing absolute mileage disparities to minimizing proportionate disparity among vehicles. This distinction is operationally significant, as it prevents scenarios in which a limited number of vehicles consistently bear disproportionately large workloads, despite an overall moderate dispersion. By including relative inequality into the decision-making process, the framework ensures that fairness is fundamentally integrated into fleet planning rather than considered a secondary concern. While existing studies predominantly assess inequality ex post, the proposed framework integrates equity into decision-making, enabling the systematic optimization of mileage homogeneity under realistic operational constraints. Although the present study adopts a vehicle-level perspective on equity, this operational viewpoint is closely connected to broader service- and user-level equity considerations. More homogeneous mileage allocation contributes to balanced vehicle condition, more predictable maintenance cycles, and reduced risk of unexpected service disruptions. In turn, this operational stability supports service reliability, which is a critical component of user-level equity. While the proposed framework does not directly optimize passenger accessibility or demographic fairness, it establishes an operational foundation upon which higher-level equity objectives can be systematically integrated in future multi-objective formulations.

The robustness analysis further indicates that the proposed framework maintains stable performance under alternative planning horizons and variations in initial mileage distributions, reinforcing the reliability of the model structure.

The findings of this research improve upon prior equity-focused transportation evaluations in several important ways compared to existing studies. While previous studies predominantly employed equity measurements such as the Gini index, Theil index, or accessibility measures as descriptive or evaluative tools, the present study incorporates equity directly into the optimization framework. The proposed paradigm, unlike earlier models that focused on cost minimization or efficiency maximization, emphasizes workload balance across vehicles under realistic operating constraints. This distinction enables a more prompt implementation of equality principles in routine operational choices, so augmenting and broadening the equity assessments outlined in previous transportation planning and sustainability studies.

Conclusions

This study suggested an equity-focused optimization framework that directly includes the Gini index in the objective function of a bus fleet optimization model to make mileage more even under real-world operational constraints. The results show that minimizing mileage inequality can be systematically included in decision-making without making operations less feasible, using large-scale operational data from an urban bus network in Makkah. The optimized solutions show that the mileage spread between vehicles has clearly decreased, showing that treating equity as an operational goal rather than a post hoc evaluation metric is useful in practice. Sensitivity analyses conducted under varying planning horizons and modified initial mileage conditions further confirmed the structural stability of the proposed model.

From a planning and operational point of view, the results show that mileage homogeneity can help with both fairness between vehicles and bigger sustainability goals, like better use of assets and more predictable maintenance cycles. The study moves forward with public transportation planning methods that usually only focus on efficiency or cost minimization by putting equity into a mixed-integer optimization framework. So, the results give transportation authorities useful information that they can use to make sure that equity is taken into account when planning day-to-day operations.

The comparative assessment against random and rotation-based baselines demonstrates that the proposed approach significantly reduces workload disparity, underscoring its practical significance for transit fleet planning.

Even though the results are promising, this study has some problems that need to be pointed out.

First, the study only looks at one big city bus network, so the results may only apply to that network’s specific operational characteristics. Second, the optimization framework only uses static historical mileage data and doesn’t take into account changes in demand, real-time disruptions, or behavioral responses. Third, the Gini index is the only measure of equity. It works well for measuring mileage homogeneity, but it doesn’t cover all aspects of transport equity, like accessibility, service reliability, or fairness at the user level.

There are a number of ways that future research can deal with these problems. To see if the proposed framework can be used in other cities or modes of transportation, it could be applied to multi-city or multi-modal transport systems. Adding dynamic demand patterns, real-time operational changes, or random elements could make the model even more realistic. In the future, studies may also look at policy-driven constraints or use more than one equity indicator along with the Gini index to better reflect social, spatial, and regulatory goals in public transport planning. Future studies may further investigate full-fleet implementations and explore decomposition-based or multi-stage optimization strategies to enhance scalability.
