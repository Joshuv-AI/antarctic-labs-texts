Kaposi sarcoma in the era of immunodeficiency: challenges and opportunities for treatment and prevention
Authors: Pranjal Kumar Singh, Muhammad Shahid Mehmood, Fariha Javaid, Huda Adnan, Mohammad Aitzaz Hassan, Eisha Kashif, Muhammad Nabeel Saddique, Naseeb Danaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a multifocal angioproliferative disorder closely linked to human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8) infection and profound immunodeficiency. Despite landmark advances in combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) that have lowered KS incidence, this malignancy persists as a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and among marginalized populations. Here, we review the current understanding of KS pathogenesis, emphasizing the interplay between viral latency, lytic reactivation, and host immune suppression. We summarize epidemiological trends before and after cART implementation, highlighting regional disparities in seroprevalence and cancer burden. Key challenges in KS management include delayed diagnosis due to lesion heterogeneity, difficulty distinguishing KS from mimickers such as bacillary angiomatosis, and barriers to histopathological confirmation in resource-limited settings. We evaluate therapeutic approaches, from early antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation and supportive chemotherapy to novel targeted agents like pomalidomide and immune checkpoint inhibitors, and discuss their limitations and emerging prospects. Future directions focus on HHV-8 vaccine development, implementation of point-of-care diagnostics enhanced by artificial intelligence, and integration of KS surveillance into existing HIV frameworks. Comprehensive prevention strategies encompassing routine HHV-8 screening of high-risk groups, robust adherence support for ART, and interdisciplinary care models are essential to curb KS’s global impact. Bridging research, clinical practice, and public health efforts will be critical to achieving equitable access to early detection and innovative treatments, thereby transforming KS from a relentless complication of HIV to a manageable condition.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is a chronic, progressive disease characterized by a gradual decline in immune function, primarily due to the depletion of CD4⁺ T lymphocytes, inversion of the CD4:CD8 ratio, and concurrent dysfunction of other immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, accompanied by increasing viral replication[1]. As HIV advances, sustained viremia and immune activation impair both innate and adaptive responses across multiple cell types, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and NK cells[2]. This acquired immunodeficiency renders infected individuals vulnerable to opportunistic infections and malignancies, particularly where antiretroviral treatment remains inadequate or inaccessible[3]. As a consequence of this progressive immunodeficiency, infected individuals become highly susceptible to a wide range of opportunistic infections and malignancies, among which Kaposi sarcoma (KS) represents one of the most significant AIDS-defining conditions.

KS is one of the most prevalent AIDS-defining malignancies and emerged as an early indicator of the HIV epidemic. In 1981, KS was among the first clinical manifestations reported in young men who have sex with men (MSM) in New York and California, signaling the onset of the AIDS epidemic[4,5]. First described in 1872 by Moritz Kaposi as a slow-growing angioproliferative tumor predominantly affecting elderly men of Mediterranean origin[6], KS was subsequently diagnosed in more than 20% of AIDS patients across Europe[7]. Four distinct epidemiological subtypes are recognized: classical (predominantly Mediterranean and Eastern European men), endemic African [affecting younger adults and children in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)], iatrogenic (transplant-associated), and epidemic HIV-associated[8,9]. The classic form affected elderly men rarely, while the HIV-associated form transformed the epidemiological landscape of this malignancy entirely[5]. In 1994, herpesvirus-like DNA sequences identified in KS tissue from AIDS patients[10] led to the characterization of human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8/KSHV) as the causative agent. This discovery raised interest in antiviral therapies targeting herpesviruses as potential KS treatment modalities[11,12]. Before the antiretroviral era, AIDS-related KS affected approximately 20–30% of individuals diagnosed with AIDS[13,14].

Between 1975 and 2005, 12 006 KS cases were recorded across nine geographical areas in the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results tumor registries, of which 11 135 (93%) were AIDS-related, underscoring the profound epidemiological impact of the AIDS epidemic[15]. HHV-8 seroprevalence varies markedly by region: reaching 50% in Uganda[16] versus below 15% in early adulthood in South Africa and Zimbabwe, with rates escalating with age[17]. A global meta-analysis estimated HHV-8 seroprevalence among gay and bisexual men at 33.0% (95% CI), with high-risk sexual behavior as a significant contributing factor[18]. Globally, approximately 42 000 new KS cases and 20 000 deaths occurred in 2018[19], with a pronounced male predominance, particularly among HIV-positive individuals[20].

KS persists as a critical AIDS-associated malignancy disproportionately affecting marginalized populations despite widespread antiretroviral therapy (ART) availability. Aggressive and recurrent lesions, worsened by structural inequalities in healthcare access, late HIV diagnosis, and socioeconomic barriers, drive poor outcomes in immunocompromised individuals. Current chemotherapy approaches falter in advanced cases due to toxicity and incomplete understanding of HIV-KS pathophysiology, while prevention strategies have narrowly focused on ART adherence, overlooking early HHV-8 monitoring and tailored screening. Innovations, including host-directed therapies, biomarker-based detection, and decentralized care models, offer promise. Integrating KS prevention into HIV programs through proactive oncological monitoring, risk reduction education, and HHV-8 vaccination research is essential to addressing this persistent global health challenge.

Pathogenesis and impact of immunodeficiency on KS

Immunosuppression, notably low CD4⁺ T cell counts and impaired cellular immunity, is the principal driver of KS development. Importantly, the decline in CD4⁺ T cells during HIV progression is accompanied by persistent activation of other immune cells, particularly macrophages, which continuously produce pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to chronic infection. These cytokines play a central role in carcinogenesis by promoting angiogenesis, cellular proliferation, and genomic instability[21,22].

Before antiretroviral therapy, more than 50% of HIV-infected individuals developed KS[23,24]. High HIV-1 viral load, elevated beta-2 microglobulin, and neopterin have also been independently associated with KS risk[25]. Notably, KS can occur even in individuals with high CD4[+] counts or controlled HIV viremia, implicating chronic inflammation, increasing age, and duration of HIV infection as additional risk factors[26]. Chronically activated macrophages and innate immune cells in HIV infection persistently produce pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic cytokines, creating a tumor-permissive microenvironment for HHV-8-driven oncogenesis[22]. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a key mediator of inflammation and vascular remodeling, has been strongly implicated in angiogenesis and tumor progression[27]. Furthermore, TNF-α signaling through TNFR2 promotes proliferation of lymphoid and tumor cells rather than apoptosis, contributing to lymphoproliferative and malignant processes[28].

Microbial toxins activate oncogenic signaling pathways such as STAT3, reinforcing inflammation-driven tumorigenesis in this context[29]. Chronic interleukin-6 (IL-6) exposure has been shown to induce genotoxic effects and contribute to de novo tumorigenesis even in the absence of active inflammation[22]. Persistent TNF-alpha signaling promotes tissue remodeling and angiogenic responses via TNFR2, which mediates lymphoid and tumor cell proliferation rather than apoptosis, generating a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory microenvironment[27]. Only after these immunological and cytokine-driven mechanisms are established does KSHV-driven oncogenesis further amplify these pathways through molecular signaling cascades such as STAT3 and mTOR activation. KSHV activates the mTOR pathway, transforming vascular endothelial cells toward a lymphatic endothelial phenotype; in immunosuppressed individuals, including HIV-infected patients and solid organ transplant recipients, HHV-8-infected cells propagate with reduced immune surveillance[30].

The pathophysiology of KS is incompletely understood[31], and not all HHV-8-infected individuals develop the malignancy[32]. KSHV (HHV-8) is a double-stranded DNA gammaherpesvirus with an enveloped icosahedral capsid of 150–200 nm, bearing multiple envelope glycoproteins (gB, gH, gL, gM, gN, and gpK8.1A) required for host cell binding and entry[33]. The virus binds to surface receptors, including the ephrin receptor EPHA2, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, integrins (alpha3beta1, alphaVbeta3, and alphaVbeta5), and the cystine-glutamate transporter xCT, entering via endocytosis[34,35]. LANA (latency-associated nuclear antigen), encoded by ORF73, is central to episomal maintenance and host cell reprogramming[36]. Additional molecular mediators include HMGB1, VEGF, IL-17A, G-CSF, CXCL5, EMMPRIN, and PDGF-AA[37], as well as FGF2, TGF-beta, angiopoietins, COX-2, and cell cycle regulators Rb and p53[38]. KSHV maintains latency through expression of a limited gene set (miRNAs, kaposin, LANA, v-cyclin, vIRF3, and vFLIP), while lytic reactivation, triggered by the RTA protein encoded by ORF50, results in viral progeny release and cell death[39] (Fig. 1). Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) represents an important complication in patients initiating combination antiretroviral therapy (cART)[40], manifesting either as paradoxical worsening of previously diagnosed opportunistic conditions or unmasking of previously subclinical infections. Both forms arise during successful immune reconstitution shortly after treatment initiation[41]. A European cohort study demonstrated a 3.94-fold higher risk of KS development in the first 3 months after starting cART versus untreated controls; a US prospective study found a KS incidence of 1342 per 100 000 person-years in the first 6 months after cART initiation[41]. KS patients with pulmonary involvement at diagnosis face particularly elevated risk of KS-IRIS, and the development of thrombocytopenia at 12-week follow-up is associated with high mortality[42]. Importantly, KS-IRIS does not indicate ART failure or necessitate a change in the ART regimen and should not prompt treatment discontinuation[40].

Multiple clinical trials confirm the protective role of cART against KS. The SMART trial demonstrated a higher KS incidence when cART was interrupted versus maintained, highlighting the importance of continuous viral suppression[43]. The START trial, enrolling 4685 HIV-positive patients with CD4 counts above 500/μL, showed significantly reduced KS incidence with immediate versus deferred cART initiation[44]. However, an evolving epidemiological pattern is evident: KS increasingly occurs at higher CD4[+] counts. Data from eight cohort studies (1996–2011) showed KS development in patients with low viral loads and preserved CD4 counts[45]; between 2007 and 2011, 15% of KS cases occurred with CD4[+] counts above 500/mm³[46]. This rising trend among virologically suppressed individuals reflects the growing cART-treated population and underscores the need for continued KS surveillance even in well-controlled HIV infection[46].

Epidemiology and changing trends in the ART era

The seroprevalence of KSHV infection demonstrates striking regional heterogeneity. Rates are below 10% in northern Europe, Asia, and the United States; intermediate (10–30%) in the Mediterranean region, including Italy, Sicily, and Sardinia; and above 40% across most of SSA[47]. Between 2008 and 2015, an estimated 5306 new KS cases occurred in HIV-infected patients in the United States, declining from 719 in 2008 to 624 in 2015. Non-Hispanic Blacks comprised 37% (1981) and MSM 89% (4711) of these cases, reflecting substantial health disparities[48]. Among intravenous drug users, KSHV prevalence is estimated at 14.71%, reflecting heightened susceptibility compared to the general population[49]. In high-income countries, highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) has reduced opportunistic infections by 43–97%, attributable to earlier and more extensive treatment access[50]. In contrast, in low- and middle-income countries such as Malawi, KS remains the most prevalent cancer, with some of the highest incidence rates globally in SSA[51]. A critical observation is that 49–77% of children with KS in SSA were already receiving ART at diagnosis, indicating that ART alone may be insufficient to prevent KS in children in high-burden regions[52]. Among patients initiating cART, the risk of IRIS varies by pre-existing opportunistic infection; KS has a comparatively lower IRIS rate (6.4%) than cytomegalovirus retinitis (37.7%), cryptococcal meningitis (19.5%), or tuberculosis (15.7%)[53]. In Mexico City, a 12-year study documented significant reductions in HIV-related oral lesions following HAART introduction, with the lowest lesion prevalence in HAART-treated patients, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and comprehensive HIV care[54]. To prevent ART failure, HIV programs must address comorbidities, including hepatitis, renal disease, and diabetes, to optimize long-term survival[55]. CD4 count remains the most clinically useful marker for assessing HIV-related illness risk, though its fluctuation with acute infections, smoking, exercise, and stress limits precision[56]. In resource-limited settings where CD4 testing is difficult, total lymphocyte count serves as a practical surrogate marker[57].

Challenges in managing HIV-associated KS

KS presents formidable clinical challenges for both medical professionals and patients. Its asymptomatic early stages frequently delay diagnosis, resulting in more advanced disease and reduced treatment compliance.

Barriers to early diagnosis

Early detection of HIV-associated KS remains challenging due to its diverse clinical presentations, significant overlap with other dermatologic and systemic conditions, and limited access to confirmatory diagnostic tools in resource-constrained settings. As HIV progresses and immunosuppression worsens, the likelihood of KS development increases, but distinguishing KS from other HIV-associated skin and systemic diseases is crucial for ensuring timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment initiation.

Differentiation from bacillary angiomatosis and other mimickers

KS must be differentiated from vascular and infectious conditions, particularly bacillary angiomatosis (BA), a recently described multisystem bacterial infection caused by Bartonella henselae or Bartonella quintana, predominantly affecting immunocompromised individuals. Like KS, BA presents with red-to-purple angiomatous skin lesions. A key distinguishing feature is the presence of osteolytic bone lesions, which favors BA over KS in the differential diagnosis[58] (Fig. 2). BA lesions regress with antibiotic therapy, whereas AIDS-associated KS requires HAART, which achieves complete remission in 46% and partial remission in 16% of patients[59]; advanced cases additionally require cytotoxic chemotherapy[60]. KS can produce atypical internal organ involvement that complicates diagnosis. In one reported case, a 30-year-old man with advanced HIV initially presented with clinical features indistinguishable from COVID-19; further investigation revealed extensive pulmonary and skeletal KS involvement confirmed by bone marrow biopsy demonstrating CD34[+]/HHV-8[+] spindle cells[61]. Simultaneous occurrence of Hodgkin disease and KS in the same lymph node has also been documented in HIV-positive patients, suggesting that severe immunosuppression may predispose individuals to multiple concurrent malignancies via shared oncogenic mechanisms[62].

Challenges in resource-limited settings

In low-income settings, KS diagnosis is severely impeded by limited access to histopathology, HHV-8 immunohistochemistry, and molecular diagnostics, forcing reliance on clinical judgment and increasing misdiagnosis rates. Health-seeking behavior is further delayed by a lack of symptom awareness, HIV-related stigma, poor rural healthcare access, and inadequate infrastructure[63]. The AIDS Malignancy Consortium (AMC) has initiated global clinical trials in SSA to address the rising cancer burden in HIV-infected populations, though these efforts face substantial obstacles, including workforce shortages, data management limitations, regulatory delays, and logistical challenges[64].

Treatment challenges

HIV-associated KS management in SSA faces significant barriers, including limited oncology infrastructure and resource constraints[65]. KS patients on ART are vulnerable to opportunistic bacterial infections, and MDR gram-negatives are a growing concern[66]. While HAART alone benefits some patients, combining it with chemotherapy using doxorubicin, bleomycin, and vincristine improves tumor regression and quality of life[67]. Alitretinoin 0.1% gel demonstrated superior efficacy over vehicle gel (35% vs. 18% response rate) in randomized trials for AIDS-related cutaneous KS[68]. Microbiome modulation has emerged as a complementary strategy for improving chemotherapy tolerability[69]. Despite these advances, AIDS-related KS remains incurable, emphasizing the urgent need for evidence-based treatment guidelines optimized for resource-limited settings[64,67].

Management in advanced and refractory cases

ART has substantially reduced the KS burden in developed nations, yet significant disease persists across resource-limited settings where treatment infrastructure is inadequate. For ART-resistant cases, localized radiotherapy has demonstrated benefits in improving symptoms and functional status. cART provides a protective effect against KS development even in patients with relatively preserved immune function[24]. Most patients receiving cART achieve lesion stabilization or resolution without specific anti-neoplastic therapy; however, advanced or rapidly progressive cases require combined cART and cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens[24]. Strengthening healthcare outreach in high-risk communities, improving access to diagnostic equipment, and enhancing clinician education are all necessary to reduce barriers to early KS detection and improve treatment outcomes[24].

Optimizing prevention strategies

Effective KS prevention in the era of immunodeficiency depends primarily on managing HIV-related immunosuppression and limiting the transmission of KSHV. HIV infection accounts for the vast majority of KS cases worldwide and poses more than a 100-fold excess risk in SSA, making HIV prevention and ART coverage the central levers for KS control[70,71]. Despite expanded ART programs across SSA, reductions in KS incidence have been incomplete and heterogeneous, reflecting persistent gaps in HIV diagnosis, treatment coverage, and sustained viral suppression[72].

Early ART as a primary prevention tool

Early and continuous ART constitutes the primary prevention strategy against KS. On the individual level, early ART initiation significantly reduces KS risk by preventing severe CD4 depletion and suppressing HIV replication. In a South African cohort of over 18 000 HIV-infected adults, the KS incidence without ART was 1682 per 100 000 person-years, compared to 138 per 100 000 person-years with ART, an approximately 90% reduction[73]. An interrupted time-series analysis using the South African National Cancer Registry confirmed that national ART rollout reduced age-standardized KS incidence by 58.3% in women and 50.3% in men over the 9-year post-rollout period[74].

Despite these impressive population-level gains, ART alone does not eliminate KS risk entirely. A multicohort study spanning ART programs in Botswana, South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe reported a residual KS incidence of 164 cases per 100 000 person-years in ART-treated patients, with risk increasing significantly at lower CD4 counts. The authors highlighted the need for earlier HIV diagnosis and maintenance of high CD4 counts to further reduce KS morbidity and mortality[75].

Randomized trial evidence provides strong support for immediate ART. In the START trial, early combination ART at CD4 counts above 500 cells/μL reduced infection-related cancers (mainly KS and non-Hodgkin lymphoma) by 74% compared to deferred initiation (hazard ratio 0.26, 95% CI 0.11–0.64)[44]. In patients with established KS, observational data indicate that viral suppression is the most important predictor of KS regression, independent of specific treatment components[76]. Together, these data strongly justify international guidelines advocating universal, same-day ART initiation following HIV diagnosis as the primary pillar of KS prevention[44,70].

Addressing persistent KS risk in a high-burden environment

An estimated 34 000 KS cases occurred globally in 2020, with approximately 80% attributable to HIV infection in SSA, and Southern and Eastern Africa bearing the highest HIV-attributable KS incidences globally[71]. Regional cancer registry data from SSA indicate that KS incidence increased sharply during the HIV epidemic, followed by an unweighted average 27% decrease between 2000–2010 and 2011–2016, tracking ART coverage and annual HIV incidence trends, though this decrease was not statistically significant across all registries[71].

Pediatric data underscore that partial HIV control is insufficient for KS prevention. In contemporary cohorts from Malawi, Tanzania, and South Africa, 49–77% of children with KS were already receiving long-term ART at diagnosis, with only a minority of long-term survivors on ART alone[52]. These findings indicate that comprehensive prevention in high-burden settings requires not only ART availability but also earlier pediatric HIV diagnosis, improved viral control, nutritional support, and prompt evaluation of persistent lymphadenopathy[52,70,72].

HHV-8-targeted biomedical prevention

Since KSHV infection is a prerequisite for KS, HHV-8 replication inhibitors provide complementary prevention for high-risk populations. Herpesviral DNA polymerase inhibitors, such as ganciclovir, foscarnet, and cidofovir, reduce HHV-8 lytic replication and were associated in observational studies with approximately 5- to 10-fold KS risk reductions among HIV-positive individuals receiving cytomegalovirus disease therapy[77]. A randomized, placebo-controlled crossover trial of once-daily valganciclovir in HHV-8-seropositive men reduced oral HHV-8 shedding by 46% with acceptable tolerability (−0.44 log copies/mL)[78]. While these findings demonstrate the biological plausibility of antiviral prophylaxis for KS prevention, no established prophylactic indication currently exists, and further trials are warranted[77,78].

In solid organ transplantation, where iatrogenic immunosuppression elevates HHV-8-related cancer risk, targeted screening has emerged as a preventive measure. A multicenter prospective study using combined serology and quantitative PCR detected HHV-8 infection in 4% of donors and 18% of recipients; of seropositive donor/seronegative recipient mismatches, 25% developed primary HHV-8 infection, one patient developed fatal non-malignant HHV-8 disease, and KS occurred in 1.9% of recipients[79]. A global survey of 51 transplant centers found that only one-third performed pre-transplant HHV-8 serology and 41% conducted post-transplant nucleic acid monitoring, with no standardized protocols[80]. HHV-8 screening and intensified virologic surveillance should be considered routine measures in high-risk transplant programs.

KSHV vaccination prospects

KSHV vaccination represents a promising medium-term prevention strategy. An NIH-funded workshop identified KSHV as a neglected oncogenic pathogen among oncogenic viruses responsible for at least 15% of the global cancer burden and established target product profiles for both prophylactic and therapeutic vaccines[81]. In SSA, where KSHV transmission occurs predominantly in early childhood through saliva, integrating a pediatric KSHV vaccine into existing immunization schedules could prevent primary infection and substantially reduce future KS incidence[81]. Advances in mRNA vaccine platforms developed for cancer immunotherapy provide a conceptual framework for KSHV vaccine development[82]. Despite the current absence of clinical KSHV vaccine trials, progress in multivalent envelope glycoprotein formulations and mRNA technology makes prophylactic vaccination a realistic near-term goal, particularly targeting high-risk groups such as MSM and transplant candidates[81].

Fortifying public health and ART adherence

Sustained viral suppression at the population level is a defining determinant of cART’s effectiveness in reducing the KS burden. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 45 studies in SSA found that adherence counseling, peer support, community-based ART provision, SMS reminders, and food provision each had statistically significant effects on ART adherence improvement (pooled odds ratio: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.35–1.80) compared to standard care[83]. Integrating multi-component adherence strategies into HIV programs, alongside stigma reduction, service decentralization, and cancer awareness campaigns, will be necessary to translate pharmacologic efficacy into sustained KS incidence reductions in high-burden African settings[72,74,83].

Environmental and socioeconomic factors play a critical role in the epidemiology and clinical progression of HIV-associated KS. In many developing regions, limited healthcare access, inadequate education, and persistent stigma surrounding HIV infection delay early diagnosis and treatment initiation. These barriers contribute not only to increased transmission rates but also to presentation at advanced disease stages, where KS is more aggressive and difficult to manage. Addressing these structural determinants through public health education, stigma reduction, and improved healthcare accessibility is essential for effective KS prevention and control.

Future directions and opportunities

Since the introduction of cART in 1996, AIDS-defining cancer incidence has declined substantially, with improvements in immune function, survival, and quality of life for people living with HIV[84–86]. Nonetheless, KS persists as a significant clinical concern. Among emerging targeted therapies, thalidomide analogs have shown the most promising results. Pomalidomide and lenalidomide are orally available, immunomodulatory, anti-angiogenic, and anti-proliferative thalidomide derivatives[87] that inhibit VEGF production and suppress VEGF-induced PI3K-Akt pathway activation, limiting the aberrant angiogenesis that is a hallmark of KS[88]. In clinical trials, pomalidomide achieved an overall response rate of 71%, leading to Food and Drug Administration approval in May 2020 for KS treatment in both HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients[89]. Limitations include significant neutropenia and teratogenicity risk, requiring specific risk evaluation and mitigation strategies[89]. Further trials evaluating pomalidomide for HIV-associated KS in SSA are underway, though cost and availability of novel agents substantially constrain research in these settings[90]. Imatinib, targeting platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and tyrosine kinase receptors, has demonstrated favorable outcomes in early studies[91]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly anti-PD-1 agents nivolumab and pembrolizumab, represent another promising avenue; these agents enhance HIV-specific CD8[+] T cell survival and cytokine production in vitro and have a favorable toxicity profile with limited drug interactions[92].

Artificial intelligence has an emerging role in enhancing early KS detection, reducing dependence on conventional histopathology in settings with limited trained pathologists. Automated frameworks for identifying and quantifying anti-LANA positivity in immunohistochemical slides can enable precise, timely pathological diagnosis; weakly supervised deep learning approaches using multiple instance learning are being advanced to facilitate localization and classification of LANA-positive cells[93,94]. Novel synthetic biology approaches, including engineered probiotics delivering CRISPR-based antimicrobials against oncogenic pathogens, represent transformative therapeutic paradigms for controlling viral-driven malignancies and antimicrobial-resistant infections in immunocompromised populations[95].

Integrating KS management into existing HIV/AIDS control programs offers a critical opportunity to improve outcomes in endemic regions. Multidisciplinary collaboration among oncologists, infectious disease specialists, and public health experts is indispensable for advancing KS care within global HIV/AIDS frameworks. No KSHV vaccine currently exists, though mRNA-lipid nanoparticle platforms targeting key viral entry proteins, particularly glycoprotein B (gB) and gpK8.1, are under active investigation[96]. These approaches aim to generate stronger neutralizing antibody responses than previous vaccine platforms and prevent the establishment of lifelong latency in B cells and endothelial cells[97]. This is particularly important in SSA, where KSHV transmission commonly occurs during childhood, typically preceding HIV infection[98]. Adapting and extending existing HIV prevention frameworks to include KSHV transmission risk reduction represents a pragmatic and high-impact path forward[96].

Equitable access to KS treatment in resource-limited settings remains constrained by HIV stigma, systemic healthcare inequities, and barriers to clinical trial participation[99]. To address these gaps, the US National Cancer Institute established the AMC to evaluate novel treatments, preventive strategies, and clinical guidelines for people living with HIV through both domestic and international research programs[99]. The AMC prioritizes building durable research infrastructure in resource-limited settings through the training of international researchers, standardization of cancer response assessment methodologies, adverse event reporting frameworks, and community engagement strategies[99]. Conclusion

KS remains a persistent challenge in the HIV era, driven by immunosuppression, socio-economic disparities, and limited access to early diagnosis and tailored therapies. While antiretroviral therapy has dramatically reduced incidence and improved outcomes, gaps in prevention, resource-limited treatment infrastructure, and emerging resistance underscore the need for integrated strategies. Strengthening healthcare systems to enable prompt KS detection, expanding access to novel targeted and immunomodulatory agents, and fostering global collaborations will be pivotal in closing the equity gap and ultimately reducing the burden of this AIDS-associated malignancy.
