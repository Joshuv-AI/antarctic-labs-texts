The relative immunogenicity of 20-valent and 15-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines: A systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Ilaria Valentini, Erika Alessandra Strangi, Gaia Baccarini, Claudia Martini, Monica Paoloni, Alice Mannocci, Chiara de Waure
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigher-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV) may reduce adult pneumococcal disease, but no head-to-head trials compare PCV20 vs. PCV15 in adults. We synthesized serotype-specific immunogenicity from randomized controlled trials using a network meta-analysis via the common comparator PCV13. PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched for trials enrolling adults (≥18 y) and evaluating PCV20 or PCV15. The primary endpoint was opsonophagocytic activity (OPA) geometric mean titers (GMTs) at 30 d, summarized as ratios of means on the log scale. Heterogeneity/inconsistency were assessed and prespecified age-stratified analyses were performed where possible. Seventeen trials met inclusion criteria but nine contributed OPA data for 15 serotypes. Across most shared serotypes, PCV20 elicited comparable or lower OPA responses than PCV15, with significantly higher responses for PCV15 for several serotypes, including 3. PCV20 exceeded PCV15 only for serotype 4. Age-stratified results suggested greater relative advantages for PCV15 in older adults. Direct comparative and real-world effectiveness studies are needed.KEYWORDS: Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, PCV20, PCV15, adults, network meta-analysis, immunogenicity, opsonophagocytic activity

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, manifesting as invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD; e.g., meningitis, bacteremia, bacteremic pneumonia) or noninvasive disease (e.g., otitis media, nonbacteremic pneumonia).1–3 In 2016 alone, pneumococcal infections accounted for an estimated 197 million episodes of lower respiratory tract infections and 1.2 million deaths.4 The highest burden falls on vulnerable populations, including young children, older adults, and individuals with comorbidities.1,5

The introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) has substantially reduced the burden of pneumococcal diseases through robust individual protection and herd effects that limit transmission.5,6 Indeed, the global burden of IPD and lower respiratory infections due to S. pneumoniae has declined substantially, from approximately 1.03 million deaths in 1990 to 0.51 million in 2021, with the age-standardized mortality rate falling from 19.28 to 6.40 per 100,000. Recent global surveillance analyses indicate that long-term PCV use reduced IPD especially in children but to a lesser extent in older adults.7 This keeps the burden of pneumococcal disease high, especially in older adults because of the lower vaccination coverage in this target group, and the serotype replacement phenomenon.

More than 100 serotypes have been identified, but only a subset is strongly associated with invasive disease, and their distribution varies widely by region in relation to local epidemiology, vaccination strategies, and population structure.8,9 Non-vaccine serotypes continue to cause substantial morbidity and mortality.5,10

Currently, two vaccine classes are approved for pneumococcal disease prevention: the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) and PCVs.11 PPSV23, licensed in 1983, contains 23 purified capsular polysaccharides but induces primarily B-cell–dependent responses that are less durable and do not generate immunological memory.12,13 To overcome these limitations, PCVs were developed by conjugating polysaccharides to carrier proteins such as CRM197, thereby inducing both B- and T-cell responses, long-lasting immunity, mucosal protection, and reductions in nasopharyngeal carriage.12,13

The progressive introduction of higher-valency PCVs has been driven by serotype replacement, whereby non-vaccine serotypes emerged as dominant causes of IPD following PCV introduction.14 Consequently PCV10 and PCV13 were developed after PCV7 and both were rapidly incorporated into childhood immunization programs; PCV13 was later approved for adults as well.15 Nonetheless, important non-PCV13 serotypes, including 8, 12F, and 22F, have persisted as prominent causes of IPD16,17 leading to the authorization of PCV15, which includes serotypes 22F and 33F, and PCV20, which extends coverage to serotypes 8, 10A, 11A, 12F, 15B, 22F, 33F.18,19 Both PCV15 and PCV20 have demonstrated strong immunogenicity in clinical trials,5,20 positioning them as key candidates to further reduce residual pneumococcal disease. However, direct comparative evidence on their relative immunogenicity remains limited.21,22 The scientific rationale for evaluating relative immunogenicity stems from the immunological trade-off inherent to higher-valency PCVs: including more antigens on a fixed carrier protein may attenuate antibody responses to individual serotypes.21,22 The focus on adults is justified by the adoption of both vaccines in this age category, as recommended by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP).23 Whether PCV15 or PCV20 provides superior serotype-specific immunogenicity in adults, and whether this varies by age, remains unresolved, as no direct head-to-head trial has been conducted.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and network meta-analysis is to evaluate and compare the immunogenicity of PCV15 and PCV20 in adults, using indirect comparison methodology. By synthesizing available data, this study seeks to clarify their relative immunogenicity and provide critical insights to inform vaccine policy and optimize strategies for reducing the burden of pneumococcal disease in adult populations.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025117786124). Reporting follows PRISMA 2020 and the PRISMA extension for network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA)25 (see Table S1). As no individual patient data were collected and no interventions on human participants were carried out, Institutional Review Board approval was not required.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was specified using the PICOS framework (Table 1). We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted on adults (≥18 y) that evaluated pneumococcal conjugate vaccination with PCV15 or PCV20 (against each other or PCV13, or PCV13 in combination with PPSV23) with follow-up sufficient to assess immunogenicity (30 d post-vaccination).

Inclusion criteria.

The treatment network included PCV15, PCV20 and PCV13 as distinct nodes to preserve differences in valency and serotype coverage, enabling indirect comparisons between PCV15 and PCV20 via shared comparators.

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The last search was conducted on September 24th 2025. Full strategies are reported in Supplementary Table S2. Reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies were hand-searched for additional records. Only peer-reviewed, English-language articles were considered eligible; conference abstracts and preprints were excluded.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible records. Studies were included if they met all eligibility criteria. Only peer-reviewed articles were considered; conference abstracts, preprints, and non-English publications were excluded. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data were extracted in duplicate using an Excel sheet. Extracted variables included: author, year, study country, trial phase, masking and randomization methods, number and characteristics of participants, intervention and comparator, number of participants per arm, OPA GMTs with confidence intervals (CIs). For trials with incomplete information in the published articles, supplemental materials and, when available, previously published reports were reviewed to obtain missing data. Studies without sufficient data for NMA were retained for qualitative synthesis.

Risk of bias within individual studies

The quality of individual studies was evaluated using Risk of Bias (RoB) 226 across five domains: randomization process; deviations from intended interventions; missing outcome data; outcome measurement; and selection of the reported result.

Data synthesis and analysis

Aggregate serotype-specific OPA GMTs and CIs were used for the quantitative synthesis. The primary effect measure was the ratio of means (ROM)27 defined as the log-transformed ratio of OPA GMTs between experimental (numerator: PCV15 or PCV20) and control arms (denominator: PCV13), estimated on the log scale as the difference in log-transformed GMTs. GMTs and CIs were log-transformed, and standard deviations (SDs) and standard errors (SEs) were derived from the width of the reported 95% CIs on the log scale, following Cochrane Handbook methods for extracting SEs from CIs.28 Standard deviations were subsequently obtained by multiplying the SE by the square root of the corresponding sample size.

Under this convention, ROM < 1 indicates higher OPA responses with the experimental vaccine (e.g., PCV20 superior to PCV13), while ROM > 1 indicates higher responses with the comparator. For the indirect PCV20-versus-PCV15 comparison, ROM < 1 indicates higher responses with PCV20 and ROM > 1 indicates higher responses with PCV15. It should be noted that the five serotypes unique to PCV20 (8, 10A, 11A, 12F, and 15B) were not included in the NMA, as no comparator data from PCV13 or PCV15 trials were available for these serotypes, rendering indirect comparisons unfeasible.

NMAs were conducted for each serotype using the netmeta package in R Studio version 4.5.0 (2025), applying a fixed-effect model with PCV13 as the reference as primary approach. As a sensitivity analysis, all NMA models were re-fitted under a random-effects model to assess the robustness of the results.

A formal inconsistency test was not applicable due to the star-shaped network structure (no closed loops). Therefore, inconsistency has been assessed only qualitatively.

Multi-arm trials were handled appropriately. Results are presented as ROMs with 95% CIs and displayed in forest plots. Heterogeneity and precision were assessed via SEs and internal consistency checks. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup NMAs stratified by population age (e.g., 18–49, ≥50, ≥60 y). These age categories were determined post-hoc based on the age strata of the cohorts enrolled in the included trials, which consistently stratified participants according to these thresholds. Evidence synthesis was feasible for all PCV15 serotypes.

Results

Study selection

As shown in Figure 1, the search identified a total of 390 records: 61 from Cochrane, 190 from PubMed, 104 from Scopus, and 35 from ClinicalTrials. After removal of 100 duplicates, 290 articles remained for title and abstract screening, of which 263 were excluded. Of the 27 articles retrieved for full-text assessment, 8 could not be accessed in full, leaving 19 articles to be evaluated according to the prespecified eligibility criteria.

PRISMA flow chart.

Among these, 2 studies were further excluded based on the PICO(S) framework. Ultimately, 17 articles were included in the systematic review. Of these, all 17 provided quantitative data suitable for extraction (i.e., OPA GMTs); however, only 9 met the additional requirement for inclusion in the NMA, namely the availability of SDs or SEs or CIs for the OPA GMTs. The remaining 8 studies reported only point estimates without variance data, precluding their inclusion in the NMA while retaining them for qualitative analysis.

Risk of bias results

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, as previously described in the Materials and methods section. The detailed distribution of judgments across the different domains is presented in Figure 2.

Risk of bias assessment of the included studies.

Overall, the methodological quality of the included RCTs was good. Most studies were judged at low risk of bias across RoB 2 domains. Some minor concerns arose mainly in D2 (deviations from intended interventions), largely related to incomplete reporting of blinding or adherence. D1 (randomization process), D3 (missing outcome data), and D4 (measurement of the outcome) were predominantly at low risk with no consistent signals of concern. All studies were judged to be at low risk in D5, as the available protocols and trial registrations allowed adequate verification of outcome selection.

Study characteristics

The 17 RCTs were published between 2018 and 2024 (see Supplementary Table S3). Most trials were conducted in the United States29–38 while several were multicenter and involved European countries, Canada, Israel, and Asia.39–42 Two studies were conducted exclusively in Japan or East Asia.43,44 The majority were phase 2 or phase 3 randomized, double-blind trials, with two phase 1/1b studies33,45 and one trial without explicit phase designation.35

Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from 91 participants33 to more than 4,000.36 Six study included generally immunocompetent, pneumococcal vaccine-naïve adults stratified by age groups (18–49, 50–59, ≥60 or 65 y).30–32,36–38 Some trials included participants with stable underlying conditions,34,40,41,43 and previous PPSV23 vaccination.35

The interventions assessed included the newer pneumococcal conjugate vaccines PCV1530,33,35,37,39–43 and PCV20,34,36,38,44,45 compared against PCV13, or PCV13 in combination with PPSV23.29,31,32 Randomization was most often 1:1, although different allocation ratios were used depending on the study design, such as 3:131,32 or 2:2:2:1.36

The transitivity assumption was assessed qualitatively (column “Characteristics of the participants” and “Type of intervention” in Supplementary Table S2). Both PCV15 and PCV20 RCTs enrolled vaccine-naïve adults or with a previous history of PPSV23 vaccination, and reported OPA GMTs at 30 d post-vaccination, supporting comparability on these key dimensions. However, some imbalances were noted: PCV20 RCTs tended to enrich older age strata (≥60 and ≥65 y), whereas PCV15 RCTs more frequently enrolled younger adults (18–49 y); one PCV15 RCT administered PCV15 followed by PPSV23, which may influence OPA responses; and one PCV20 RCT was conducted in East Asia, where baseline serotype epidemiology differs from Western settings.

Immunogenicity

OPA responses with corresponding variance data were reported in 9 articles.29,31,32,34–36,39,40,45 Based on these data, a NMA was performed to compare OPA GMTs across PCVs, using the ROM as the outcome measure (see Supplementary Figure S1). The analysis focused on head-to-head comparisons of PCV20 vs. PCV13 and PCV15 vs. PCV13, thereby enabling indirect comparisons between PCV20 and PCV15. Data were available for the following serotypes: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6A, 6B, 7F, 9 V, 14, 18C, 19A, 19F, 22F, 23F, and 33F.

The resulting evidence network included PCV13, PCV15, and PCV20. PCV13 served as the central comparator, being directly connected with both PCV15 and PCV20. Specifically, four studies compared PCV13 with PCV20,29,34,36,45 while five compared PCV13 with PCV15.31,32,35,39,40 Although these direct comparisons involved PCV13, the primary role of the network was to support indirect estimation of the relative immunogenicity of PCV15 vs. PCV20 across shared serotypes, in the absence of head-to-head comparative trials.

Across the 15 serotypes assessed, the NMA provided indirect estimates of the relative immunogenicity of PCV20 vs. PCV15, using PCV13 as the common network comparator. Results are reported as ROMs of OPA GMTs, with values <1 indicating higher responses in favor of PCV20 and values >1 indicating higher responses in favor of PCV15 (Figure 3).

Forest plots of immunogenicity comparisons (OPA GMTs, expressed as ROM with 95% CI) between PCV20 and comparator vaccines (PCV13 and PCV15) across serotypes included in the analysis (fixed effect model). ROM values <1 indicate higher antibody responses in favor of PCV20, whereas values >1 indicate stronger responses for the comparator. Horizontal lines represent 95% CIs.

OPA responses were broadly comparable between PCV20 and PCV15 for serotypes 7F, 9 V, 14 and 19A, as indicated by non-significant ROM estimates.

For most other shared serotypes, PCV20 showed significantly lower OPA responses than PCV15. Specifically, ROMs favored PCV15 for serotypes 1 (ROM = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.06–1.56; p = .0113), 3 (1.64; 1.43–1.89; p <.0001), 6A (1.22; 1.01–1.48; p = .0379), 6B (1.52; 1.27–1.83; p < .0001), 18C (1.87; 1.56–2.24; p <.0001), 19F (1.45; 1.19–1.76; p = .0002), 22F (2.82; 2.12–3.76; p < .0001), 23F (1.37; 1.10–1.71; p = .0047), and 33F (1.73; 1.36–2.20; p < .0001). Conversely, PCV20 showed significantly higher OPA responses only for serotype 4 (ROM = 0.75; 0.62–0.90; p = .0017).

Under the fixed-effect model, significant between-study heterogeneity (see Supplementary Table S4) was detected for 22F (Q = 261.288; p < .001), 33F (Q = 63.616; p < .001), and 18C (Q = 32.150; p < .001). For the remaining serotypes, there was no statistical evidence of heterogeneity.

As a sensitivity analysis, all indirect comparisons were re-run using a random-effect model (see Supplementary Figure S2). For 12 of the 15 serotypes analyzed, point estimates and CIs were virtually identical between the two models, supporting the robustness of the main findings. The main exceptions were serotypes 4, 22F, and 33F. For serotype 4, the advantage of PCV20 over PCV15 observed under the fixed-effect model was attenuated under random-effect assumptions (ROM = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.61–1.00; p = .0524), suggesting that this result should be interpreted with caution. For serotypes 22F and 33F, which exhibited the highest heterogeneity, random-effect estimates yielded substantially wider CIs crossing the null, with both serotypes showing a non-significant result (22F ROM = 1.87; 95% CI: 0.26–13.75; p = .5372; 33F ROM = 1.47; 95% CI: 0.70–3.06; p = .3090).

In the stratified NMA, patterns differed by serotype and age group (see Supplementary Figures S3–S17).

For serotype 1, no differences emerged in participants ≥50 y; evidence of superiority was observed for PCV15 vs. PCV20 in the 18–49 and ≥60 y groups. For serotype 3, the comparison remains in favor of PCV15 for each age group. For serotype 4, PCV20 outperformed PCV15 only in adults 18–49 y. For 6B, PCV15 was superior in the 18–49 y group. For serotype 9 V, superiority was observed for PCV15 in the ≥60 y group. For serotype 14, superiority was observed for PCV20 in the ≥50 y group. For serotype 18C, ROMs favored PCV15 in the 18–49 and ≥60 y groups. For serotype 19F, PCV15 was superior in the 18–49 y group. For serotype 22F, estimates indicated better performance of PCV15 relative to PCV20 in the ≥60 y group but not in the ≥50 y groups. For serotype 23F, the comparison favored PCV15 in adults 18–49 y. Finally, for serotype 33F, the comparison indicated an advantage for PCV15 vs. PCV20 in the ≥60 y group. On the contrary, no significant differences were observed in any age group for serotype 5, 6A, 7F, 19A. Full forest plots for each serotype and stratum are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Discussion

This NMA provides an indirect comparative assessment of the immunogenicity of PCV20 vs. PCV15 across pneumococcal serotypes and age groups. Overall, the results highlight a heterogeneous, serotype-dependent pattern.

Across the evaluated serotypes, the NMA suggests that PCV20 may generally elicit comparable or lower OPA responses than PCV15. For several serotypes (1, 3, 6A, 6B, 18C, 19F, 22F, 23F and 33F), point estimates favored PCV15 and significant differences were observed, indicating higher OPA GMTs with PCV15. In contrast, evidence supporting superior responses with PCV20 was limited to serotype 4. Results were robust for most serotypes, except for serotypes 22F, 33F, and 4 whose results were sensitive to the modeling approach and should be treated as exploratory.

These findings are consistent with the expected immunological trade-off associated with higher-valency conjugate vaccines, whereby the inclusion of additional serotypes may attenuate responses to shared antigens. Within the inherent constraints of indirect comparison, these data suggest that PCV15 may elicit equal or higher functional antibody responses than PCV20 for most shared serotypes, though direct head-to-head trials are required to confirm this inference. However, the robustness of the comparative conclusions between PCV15 and PCV20 warrants explicit consideration. The loss of statistical significance for serotypes 4, 22F and 33F under the random-effect model indicates that results for these serotypes are sensitive to modeling assumptions, reflecting the substantial between-study heterogeneity of results. Consequently, the relative advantage of PCV15 over PCV20 and vice versa in respect to these serotypes should be regarded as affected by high uncertainty. For the remaining serotypes in which fixed-effect and random-effect estimates were concordant, the comparative conclusions are more robust; nevertheless, all findings remain subject to the inherent uncertainty of indirect inference in the absence of direct head-to-head trials.

The interpretation of these findings should be contextualized against serotypes epidemiology. According to recent ECDC surveillance reports,46 serotypes 3, 8, 19A, and 22F remain among the most frequent causes of IPD in Europe. Notably, serotypes 3 and 19A account for a substantial and increasing proportion of IPD in young children, representing approximately 39% of cases in children under five years of age, with serotype 3 remaining a leading cause of severe disease and mortality despite longstanding inclusion in conjugate vaccines.46 In this NMA, the observation that PCV15 demonstrates consistently higher immunogenicity than PCV20 for serotype 3 across age groups is of relevance. Given the clinical importance and persistence of this serotype, differences in functional antibody responses may have implications for protection.

Age-stratified analyses showed that the relative immunogenicity of PCV20 and PCV15 varies by age group. In younger adults (18–49 y), immunogenicity differences were generally attenuated, with occasional advantages for PCV20 observed for individual serotypes. Conversely, in adults aged ≥ 60 y, comparisons more frequently favored PCV15, suggesting that age-related immune responses may accentuate differences between the two vaccines in older populations. These findings align with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis in older adults reporting superior immunogenicity of PCV15 vs. PCV13 for shared serotypes at one month, and non-inferiority of PCV20 vs. PCV13 despite lower average responses in older age.47 Findings from pediatric indirect treatment comparisons further support the adult NMA results. In healthy children vaccinated according to a 2 + 1 schedule, which is widely adopted across Europe, PCV15 demonstrated superior immunogenicity compared with PCV20 for several shared serotypes, including 1, 3, 4, 5, 6B, 9 V, and 23F after the primary series, as well as sustained advantages for serotype 3 after the booster dose. For the additional serotypes 22F and 33F, PCV15 showed non-inferior immunogenicity relative to PCV20 at both post-primary and post-toddler timepoints.48 These pediatric data reinforce the notion that higher valency does not necessarily translate into better protection if individual serotypes are considered.

Taken together, our data are in favor of international guidelines that suggest that PCV20 provides greatest value in settings where the added serotypes substantially contribute to local disease burden, whereas PCV15 remains competitive, and in some circumstances preferable, across many shared serotypes, with effect modification by age.49,50 Therefore, the local serotype epidemiology is of utmost importance in vaccine choice.49,50 Economic evaluations similarly highlight the context-dependent nature of comparative assessment of the two vaccines. While several analyses suggest favorable cost-effectiveness profile for PCV20 when broader serotype coverage and population-level impact are assumed,51,52 others show that conclusions are highly sensitive to epidemiological assumptions, including baseline disease incidence, circulating serotypes distribution, and vaccine effectiveness.53–55 Accordingly, as with immunogenicity outcomes, economic conclusions regarding PCV20 vs. PCV15 are not universal but depend critically on the underlying epidemiological context.

Ongoing surveillance of serotypes dynamics and direct comparative evidence between PCV15 and PCV20 remain priorities, as highlighted by recent commentaries56 and calls for post-marketing studies, real-world evidence, and surveillance following vaccine introduction to quantify real-world effectiveness.57,58

This synthesis carries limitations inherent to the available data and to NMA methodology.

Although 17 trials were included, only 9 reported serotype-specific OPA estimates with variance data (SDs, SEs or CIs), showing reporting limitation of the primary studies and limiting the extent of quantitative analysis.

The age-stratified analyses are exploratory, affected by multiple comparisons and sparse data in several strata, and with low statistical power due to small sample sizes within each age stratum, warranting cautious interpretation. Furthermore, the restriction to English-language publications may have introduced a language bias, potentially excluding relevant data from non-English-language trials. Moreover, our approach relies on indirect comparisons and aggregate-level data; differences across studies in sampling timepoints, laboratory platforms, vaccination schedules, co-administrations, and participant characteristics may leave residual heterogeneity not fully captured by the model. The transitivity assumption, which requires that studies are sufficiently homogeneous to enable indirect comparison, was not formally evaluated. As discussed above, the slight heterogeneity in age groups representation in study populations and vaccination strategies (e.g., PCV alone vs. PCV+PPSV23) represents a potential threat to this assumption; therefore, findings should be interpreted with this uncertainty in mind. A further structural limitation concerns network inconsistency. Because the evidence network is triangle-shaped, with PCV13 as the sole central comparator directly connected to both PCV15 and PCV20, and no closed loops, formal statistical assessment of inconsistency (e.g., loop-specific inconsistency tests or node-splitting) was not feasible. As a result, the entire comparative inference between PCV15 and PCV20 rests exclusively on indirect evidence channeled through PCV13. This means that any violation of the consistency assumption cannot be detected empirically within this network, and the plausibility of the indirect comparison depends entirely on the validity of the transitivity assumption. Readers should therefore interpret the PCV15-versus-PCV20 estimates with an awareness that they cannot be cross-validated against direct evidence, which represents an inherent limitation of the current evidence base. Overall, this analysis is a step toward clarifying comparative immunogenicity between PCV20 and PCV15 and underscores the need for head-to-head trials and individual-participant-data analyses to determine whether one product consistently outperforms the other across age groups and epidemiological contexts.

Conclusion

Based on evidence from our indirect comparison, PCV15 may elicit equal or higher functional antibody responses than PCV20 across most shared serotypes. This is also verified in older adults (≥60 y), where age-stratified analyses more consistently favored PCV15, including for the clinically important serotype 3. In younger adults (18–49 y), differences between vaccines were generally attenuated. Nevertheless, these results must be interpreted with caution as constrained by the reliance on indirect comparisons through a triangle-shaped network without the possibility of formally checking the consistency assumption. Furthermore, the comparison between the two vaccines was sensitive to modeling assumptions for a few serotypes and it must be recognized that PCV20 broadens serotype coverage and may yield greater public-health value in settings where the additional serotypes substantially contribute to local disease burden. Hence, health policy decisions should be tailored to local sero-epidemiology, population age structure, and context-specific issues. Continued surveillance of serotypes dynamics and the generation of direct head-to-head comparative evidence are essential to refine future vaccine recommendations.
