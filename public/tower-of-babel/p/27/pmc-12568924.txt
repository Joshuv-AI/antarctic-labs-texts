Genomic characterization and epidemiology of Mycobacterium tuberculosis lineage 2 isolates from Kazakhstan
Authors: Dana Auganova, Sabina Atavliyeva, Nour Gharbi, Elena Zholdybayeva, Yuri Skiba, Akmaral Akisheva, Anna Tsepke, Arike Alenova, Christophe Guyeux, Thierry Wirth, Christophe Sola, Pavel Tarlykov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKazakhstan has one of the highest incidence rates of MDR-TB in the WHO European Region. However, data on the genomic diversity of circulating Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains in Kazakhstan remains limited. We performed whole-genome sequencing on 177 clinical M. tuberculosis isolates belonging to the L2/Beijing lineage, sourced from 15 regions across Kazakhstan. WGS data were analyzed using the computational genomics pipelines TB-Profiler, TB-Annotator, and MTBseq. We then carried out Bayesian analyses using the BEAST algorithm to predict the effective population size dynamics of M. tuberculosis in Kazakhstan over the last 50 years. Phenotypic drug susceptibility testing revealed a significant proportion of MDR (46.9%) and pre-XDR (14.7%) isolates. Phylogenetic analysis showed that 67.2% of M. tuberculosis L2/Beijing isolates belonged to the Central Asian outbreak (CAO) sublineage. Putative compensatory variants were detected in 89.6% of RIF-resistant CAO isolates, with rpoABC variants showing strong sublineage specificity. Furthermore, phylogenetic analysis contributed to the identification of six novel historical clusters, each characterized by distinct SNP signatures. Phylogenetic reconstruction indicated recent transmission and clonal expansion of CAO isolates. Meanwhile, Bayesian skyline analysis revealed a marked increase in the effective population size of Mtb. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the world’s most pressing health threats due to the widespread impact of the disease and the resilience of the pathogen Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). The disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic significantly disrupted TB control efforts worldwide, leading to reduced case detection and a surge in undiagnosed cases1,2. The global TB incidence rate has increased by 4.6% between 2020 and 2023, while the estimated annual number of people developing multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) or rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB) remained unchanged3. However, there is a list of 30 high-burden countries for MDR/RR-TB, including Kazakhstan, which was recognized in 2016 as a high-burden MDR/RR-TB country by the WHO and reaffirmed again in 20214. The majority of undiagnosed or ineffectively treated MDR-TB patients continue to transmit their infection5. This alarming trend emphasizes the need for country-specific approaches and targeted interventions.

The Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) has been classified into eleven distinct lineages and sister lineages6,7. Lineage 2 (L2) is widespread in Central Asia and is further divided into three major phylogenetic sublineages: proto-Beijing (L2.1), Ancestral Beijing, and Modern Beijing8–10. Lineage 2 is of significant concern due to its high virulence, increased drug resistance, and potential for rapid dissemination11,12. Overall, lineage 2 accounts for 60% of cases in the East Asia region. Studies indicate that L2 strains dominate MDR-TB outbreaks in many countries with a high burden of MDR/RR-TB, including Russia, China, and Central Asian countries such as Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan13,14. In countries of the former Soviet Union, the spread of drug-resistant Mtb clones is thought to be driven by drug supply disruptions, incorrect implementation of regimens, and insufficient infection control measures, particularly during the socio-economic crisis of the 1990s15.

The phylogenetic classification of L2 strains is of significant clinical and epidemiological relevance, as these strains are strongly associated with MDR-TB outbreaks16–18 and are characterized by an elevated mutagenic capacity, compensatory mechanisms, increased transmission potential, and hypervirulence19. Two major sublineages have been identified among recent MDR-TB outbreaks in Russia and Central Asia: B0/W148 and the Central Asia outbreak (CAO)8,19. According to the latest classification by Thawornwattana et al., B0/W148 and CAO strains fall within the L2.2.M4 lineage, designated as sublineages L2.2.M4.5 and L2.2.M4.9.1, respectively9.

Here, we examined 177 isolates of the L2/Beijing family of M.tuberculosis (Mtb) from 15 regions of Kazakhstan, sampled between 2010 and 2022. Our aim was to study the population structure and effective population size of M. tuberculosis (Mtb) using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and various computational genomics pipelines, including TB-Profiler, TB-Annotator, and MTBseq, as well as Bayesian statistical approaches.

Materials and methods

Study population

A convenience sample of 204 cultured Mtb isolates was collected from newly diagnosed patients with clinically suspected TB and previously treated TB patients across 15 regions of Kazakhstan between 2010 and 2022. The study was approved by the National Centre for Biotechnology Institutional Review Ethics Committee (NCB-IREC 04 − 03/07082020). All principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for Biomedical Research Involving Human Participants were adhered to during the sample collection, based on good medical and laboratory practices. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Drug susceptibility testing (DST) of Mtb cultures was performed using a Bactec MGIT 960 culture system (Becton Dickinson, Cockeysville, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The sample comprised susceptible (sensitive), mono-resistant (not RIF-resistant), poly-resistant, MDR, pre-XDR, and XDR isolates.

DNA extraction and genotyping

For culture, the selected strains were inoculated onto solid Löwenstein–Jensen (LJ) medium and incubated at 37 °C for approximately 10 weeks. DNA extraction was performed with the traditional cetyltrimethylammonium (CTAB) procedure20. All DNA samples were genotyped using a real-time PCR assay that targeted the dnaA-dnaN::IS6110 region, which is specific to L2 isolates21. Additionally, 126 out of 177 samples had previously been analyzed using two high-throughput hybridization methods (TB-SPRINT and TB-SNPID) as described by Klotoe et al.22. Based on genotyping results, 177 L2 isolates were retained for further analysis (Table S1) after the exclusion of 27 non-L2 Mtb isolates (data not shown). A QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to clean up DNA and improve its quality before whole-genome sequencing. DNA quality was assessed using the Qubit dsDNA Quantification Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, USA) and a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer23 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, USA).

Whole genome sequencing and genotypic drug resistance prediction

A total of 177 L2 isolates were sequenced using a MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Libraries with an average fragment size of 600 bp were prepared for MiSeq sequencing using a DNA Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting FASTQ files were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (see Table S1 for accession numbers). The reads from all Mtb genomes were mapped to the M. tuberculosis H37Rv genome (GenBank ID: NC_000962.3) using the MTBseq pipeline v.1.0.36,24,25, using TB-Profiler26, and TB-Annotator27,28. The TB-Profiler and PhyResSe online tools were then used to check the obtained WGS data for genotypic antibiotic resistance and heteroresistance29,30. Heatmap analysis was performed using the Pheatmap package (v1.0.12) in RStudio v.2024.12.1.

Computational genomics pipeline

We used the TB-Annotator platform (Senelle et al., 2023; DOI: 10.1101/2023.06.12.526393v1) to combine whole genome sequencing data from Kazakhstan with publicly available sequence read archives (SRA) data from 125,000 MTBC isolates held by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The platform can identify genetic variations and structural variants, including single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), regions of difference (RDs - both known and novel short or long indels), insertion sequences (ISs), and the orientation of IS6110 insertions relative to the H37Rv reference genome (NC_000962.3). TB-Annotator also performs raw CRISPR reconstruction based on BLAST and provides a coverage quality index for spacers, facilitating expert-based visual interpretation of the spoligotype. The platform supports the export of alignments for further analysis with phylogenetic software, such as RAxML, to construct phylogenetic trees. These trees can then be re-imported into the graphical/numerical interface of TB-Annotator, enabling interactive exploration of specific criteria linked to branch selection within the tree. Users can manually or through filtering menus examine the presence or absence of studied polymorphic markers (e.g., signature SNPs, RDs, and IS6110) within the tree and its subbranches, thereby defining both globally and locally the specificity and sensitivity of these markers. The TB-Annotator database also includes metadata on read quality (coverage and read length), genotypic drug resistance, and the geographical location of variant isolation.

Phylogenetic and demogenetic analyses

A dataset of 173 Mtb isolates collected in Kazakhstan was used for this analysis, after excluding four isolates due to low sequencing quality. For the phylogenetic analysis, a concatenated SNP alignment was created based on variant positions, excluding insertions and deletions, SNPs located within 12 bp or in repetitive regions, or resistance-associated genes. This was done using the MTBseq pipeline v.1.0.3, as described elsewhere24. The pipeline employs the widely used, open-source programs BWA, SAMtools, Picard Tools, and the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK)31–33. The phylogenetic tree was reconstructed with 3,939 SNPs using the ML method34 and the GTR + F + G4 substitution model. Circular strips on the phylogenetic tree indicate the following metadata (inside out): (1) geographic regions of Kazakhstan; (2-3) Sublineage classification; (4) drug susceptibility output based on genotyping; and (5) the outer circle represents drug susceptibility output based on phenotyping.

For the coalescent-based analyses, evolutionary rates and tree topologies were analyzed using the general time-reversible (GTR) and Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano (HKY) substitution models with gamma distributed among-site rate variation with four rate categories (Γ4). The substitution rate was estimated under different demographic and clock models using BEAST v2.3.235, taking advantage of a sampling timeframe from 2010 to 2022. We tested both a strict molecular clock (which assumes the same evolutionary rates for all branches in the tree) and a relaxed clock, which allows for different rates among branches. Constant-size and Bayesian skyline plot models were used, based on a general, non-parametric prior that does not impose any particular demographic history. For each model, three independent chains were run for 100 or 300 million generations, and convergence was assessed by checking ESS values for key parameters using TRACER V1.6.

Geographic information systems and map construction

Geospatial mapping and visualization were performed using the Geographic Information System (GIS) QGIS v3.40 (http://www.qgis.org). Free shapefiles for Kazakhstan were obtained from GADM (version 2.8, May 2025; http://www.gadm.org). The available published data and results from this study were compiled into CSV files, formatted for use with QGIS. The geographic coordinates of major cities in Kazakhstan were retrieved via a Google search and incorporated into the CSV files. Pie charts illustrating sublineage distributions by region were generated according to the QGIS user manual.

Results

Sample selection and sequencing

This study included Mtb isolates collected from TB patients during routine diagnostic procedures in Kazakhstan between 2010 and 2022. A total of 177 isolates were classified as L2 based on RT-PCR genotyping21 and/or high-throughput hybridization methods (TB-SPRINT and TB-SNPID) as part of a previous study by Klotoe et al. (2019), and selected for whole-genome sequencing22. The regional distribution of the isolates is illustrated in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 1. The gender distribution of the patients was as follows: 121 men, 51 women, and 5 with unknown gender. The patients ranged in age from 16 to 81 years (Table S1). The majority of patients were diagnosed with infiltrative pulmonary TB (n = 110; 62.2%). Other TB forms included fibrocavernous tuberculosis (n = 15; 8.5%), focal tuberculosis (n = 3; 1.7%), infiltrative pulmonary tuberculosis with cavitation (n = 3; 1.7%), disseminated tuberculosis (n = 2; 1.1%), primary tuberculosis complex (n = 2; 1.1%), miliary tuberculosis (n = 2; 1.1%), and tuberculous pleurisy (n = 2; 1.1%). Information on the form of TB was unavailable for 38 patients (21.5%). The selected sample represents approximately 0.2% of the total TB cases reported nationwide during the study period, based on available epidemiological data.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis population structure

The Mtb isolates were obtained from 15 regions, including the former and current capital cities of Almaty and Astana (Table 1). The majority of the L2 isolates belonged to sublineage CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) (119/177, 67.2%), followed by sublineage L2.2.M4.9 (44/177, 24.9%), the Europe/Russia B0/W148 outbreak (L2.2.M4.5) (11/177, 6.2%), Clade A (L2.2.M4.9.2) (2/177, 1.1%), and sublineage L2.2.M4 (1/177, 0.6%). The distribution of L2 sublineages across the 15 regions of Kazakhstan shows that CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) is the most prevalent sublineage, consistently dominating the majority of areas, particularly in the south and west. This includes the Atyrau region (92.3%), the Almaty region (80%), the Kyzylorda region (83.3%), and the West Kazakhstan region (84.6%). The parental sublineage L2.2.M4.9 is also widespread, but appears at lower frequencies. It is notably prevalent in northern and central Kazakhstan, specifically in the Pavlodar (50%), Jambyl, and Akmola (50%) regions. The B0/W148 sublineage (L2.2.M4.5) is less prevalent, appearing in sporadic small outbreaks in the north and east, but not in the south of Kazakhstan.

Genotypic drug resistance

Genotypic prediction of drug resistance was performed for the Mtb isolates. According to phenotypic drug susceptibility (PDS) testing (Table S1) and WGS data, multidrug-resistant and pre-extensively drug-resistant isolates were prevalent in the sample. PDS testing revealed a high proportion of multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolates (n = 83; 46.9%) and pre-extensively drug-resistant (pre-XDR) isolates (n = 26; 14.7%). Additionally, one extensively drug-resistant (XDR) isolate (n = 1; 0.6%) was identified in the study population. The genotypic prediction characterized most of the L2 isolates studied as RIF-resistant (n = 128; 72,3%) (Table S3). The most prevalent mutations in the RR-determining region (RR-DR) of rpoB (codons 426–452) were S450L (114/128 isolates) and H445Y (5/128 isolates). Amino acid changes at codons 450 and 445 in the rpoB gene correspond to codons 531 and 526 in the Escherichia coli nomenclature. The remaining rpoB mutations associated with RR were represented by L430P/R, D435Y/V, N437S, H445Y/L, K446Q, and L452P. Notably, phenotypic DST reported five isolates with RR-DR mutations as RIF sensitive (‘s’). Two of the five isolates (468 and 4511-19) carried borderline mutations (H445L and D435V), which have been associated with low-level resistance to RIF. In addition, clonal heteroresistance was detected in four isolates with mixed calls (less than 95% read depth) in alleles associated with drug resistance (Table S3). Associated resistance to bedaquiline (BDQ) and clofazimine (CFZ) was exclusively identified in Mtb isolates from Astana, driven by mutations in the Rv0678 gene (n = 4; 2.3%). These included the missense mutations G66E and S68G, as well as a frameshift deletion 193delG. Two of the four BDQ-resistant isolates (CUS0000737 and CUS0000746) were genetically clustered and shared the same frameshift deletion 193delG. Further investigation revealed that both isolates originated from the same male patient, collected six months apart.

Compensatory mutations

The CAO sublineage (L2.2.M4.9.1) exhibited the highest prevalence of RIF resistance among Mtb isolates (96/119, 80.7%), surpassing L2.2.M4.9 (11/44, 25%) and B0/W148 isolates (8/11, 72.7%). Moreover, 89.6% of RR CAO isolates (86/96) carried putative compensatory mutations. For this study, a variant in rpoA, rpoB, or rpoC was considered a putative compensatory mutation if it occurred alongside a RIF resistance (RR)-conferring mutation. Phylogenetic variants such as rpoC E1092D, silent mutations, and mutations in the RR-determining region (rpoB codons 425–452) were excluded. Other Mtb sublineages with RR-conferring mutations were L2.2.M4.9 and B0/W148, which had putative compensatory mutations in 54.6% (6/11) and 75.0% (6/8) of RR Mtb isolates, respectively.

As a result, 63 putative compensatory mutations were detected in the studied L2 isolates, including 33 variants in the rpoC gene, 26 in rpoB, and 4 in rpoA (Tables S3-S4). Most of these variants were associated with the rifampicin resistance-conferring rpoB S450L mutation (60 out of 63 isolates). Additionally, the RR-conferring mutations H445Y (2/63) and L452P (1/63) were each accompanied by non-synonymous variants in the rpoA, rpoB, or rpoC genes. Of the 63 identified compensatory variants, 30.2% resulted in a change in amino acid polarity (from polar to non-polar or vice versa), while the majority (69.8%) retained their original polarity (Table S4).

A heatmap was constructed to visualize the distribution of the putative compensatory variants among the sublineages (Fig. 2). The variants included in the heatmap met the following criteria: (1) the variant was absent from all RIF-susceptible isolates; and (2) it was present in at least one RIF-resistant isolate lacking a high-confidence compensatory mutation, as described by Comas et al., 201236. It is noteworthy that the studied L2 isolates exhibited strong sublineage specificity in the distribution of the acquired putative compensatory variants. The rpoA high-likelihood compensatory variants T187A and G31S were exclusively found in the CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) isolates15,36 (Table S3-S4). Furthermore, the most prevalent rpoC compensatory variants, V1039A, V483G/A, and L449V, were also solely identified in CAO isolates. We conducted a Fisher’s exact test to assess the association between the RR-conferring mutation rpoB S450L and the four most frequently observed putative compensatory mutations in our dataset. We observed statistically significant associations between RR-conferring mutation rpoB S450L and rpoA T187A (P = 6.5 × 10−6, Fisher’s exact test), rpoC V1039A (P = 1.2 × 10−3, Fisher’s exact test), and V483A/G (P = 4.6 × 10−3, Fisher’s exact test), rpoB R552H (P = 2.7 × 10−2, Fisher’s exact test), respectively. Two of these mutations (rpoA T187A and rpoC V483A/G) were previously confirmed as high-confidence mutations, while rpoC V1039A was classified as a likely compensatory mutation (Table S4). Other putative compensatory variants were observed at low frequency in our dataset and were classified as being of uncertain significance, unless they had been previously reported as high-confidence compensatory mutations.

To assess whether the co-occurrence of RR-conferring and compensatory mutations could be attributed to clonal expansion or represented independent events, we performed homoplasy analysis using TreeTime v0.7.4. Homoplasy analysis revealed that the rpoA T187A mutation, detected in 25 isolates, arose independently only once, suggesting clonal expansion within a genomic cluster. Similarly, the rpoB R552H mutation, present in 9 isolates, also appeared to have a single origin and clonal expansion. In contrast, homoplastic occurrences of rpoC V483A/G and V1039A were observed in 6 out of 13 and 2 out of 15 isolates, respectively, indicating multiple independent acquisition events and potential convergent evolution.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic relationships and genetic diversity of 173 Mtb isolates collected in Kazakhstan are represented as a circular phylogenetic tree, which was reconstructed using the ML method with the GTR + F + G4 substitution model (Fig. 3). Interestingly, the CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) sublineage is the major monophyletic clade, comprising isolates from all regions, and indicating historical and recent ongoing transmission and clonal expansion across the country. Furthermore, several subclusters within L2.2.M4.9.1 were also observed, each differing by only a few SNPs (< 12 SNPs), suggesting recent transmission events. The L2.2.M4.9.2 and L2.2.M4.5 sublineages appeared to be less prevalent and exhibited region-specific clustering (i.e., from Turkistan and Kostanay provinces).

The mean intra-cluster distance computed on the CAO cluster was 47 ± 25 SNPs, suggesting a contemporary history of TB emergence for the CAO L2 sublineage. As CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) was the predominant sublineage, a substantial genomic database (TB-Annotator v2.5, comprising 125,000 SRA) was employed to compare the recently obtained WGS data with previously published sequences originating from Kazakhstan. In TB-Annotator, we selected a total of 705 SRAs based on a combination of two filters: country name (Kazakhstan) and the presence of SPDI NC_000962.3:2939371:AG: GC characteristic of L2.2.M4.9.1. A new tree based on the GTR + G model was built using RaxML-NG37 and reimported into TB-Annotator. The results show that the samples in this study feed into previously identified epidemic clusters, allowing the discovery of six new clusters (L2.2.M4.9.3 to L2.2.M4.9.8), which have specific SNP markers and higher intra-cluster distances than those described in the L2.2.M4.9.1 phylogenetic tree (Fig.S1, Tables S5-S6). One of these clusters (L2.2.M4.9.7 concerning two SRAs) shares a few SNP markers with L2.2.M4.9.1; this result merits further investigation. Notably, a basal clade, designated L2.2.M4.5 A, was proposed for L2.2.M4.5 isolates lacking the allele 1,364,705:G: A. The ancestral L2.2.M4.5 A sublineage occupied a basal position in the phylogenetic tree (Fig.S1H).

Effective population size of Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Next, we aimed to assess the signature of evolving populations within the L2 dataset. However, this analysis did not yield a significant signal. This lack of signal is likely due to the narrow time frame of the sample collection, limiting the probability of capturing a temporal signal. We therefore implemented a molecular clock of 1.12 × 10− 7 substitutions/nucleotide/year, in line with two of our previous studies14,38. Bayesian analyses were carried out using the BEAST algorithm on the collection of 173 Mtb WGS sequences. The Bayesian skyline model proved to be the most robust, displaying the highest likelihood scores. The effective population size of the Beijing strains in Kazakhstan shows clear fluctuations. The most striking feature, which is highlighted in Fig. 4, is the order of magnitude increase in the effective population size of the M. tuberculosis population in the late 1990s and early 2000s. This sudden surge followed a period of historically low GDP in Kazakhstan, coinciding with the 1997 energy crisis and the 1998 financial crisis. It should be noted that the observations and results reported here relating to L2 population dynamics do not rule out the possibility of an unreported epidemic in Kazakhstan prior to our sampling period, or that massive bottlenecks or clade replacements might have obscured such signals.

Discussion

Kazakhstan has one of the highest incidence rates of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) in the WHO European Region. In 2023, Kazakhstan reported 642 TB deaths, reflecting a 9.2% decrease from 707 deaths in 2022 and a TB mortality rate of 3.2 per 100,000 population, representing an 18% reduction compared to 2015 levels. Treatment success rates remain high, with 90% of patients who began treatment for drug-susceptible TB in 2022 and 82% of patients with drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) who started treatment in 2021 completing therapy successfully. The most recent national efforts include the implementation of WGS-based surveillance, which is currently underway.

However, genomic data on the population structure of Mtb circulating across different regions of Kazakhstan remains limited39–42. Klotoe et al., 2019 characterized MTBC genetic diversity in Kazakhstan using high-throughput hybridization methods (TB-SPRINT and TB-SNPID)22. The 700 Mtb isolates studied belonged to the L2 and L4/Euro-American families. The L2 family accounted for nearly 80% of the isolates, with drug-resistant cases predominantly linked to the Central Asian/Russian cluster of this lineage. However, the Central Asian/Russian cluster was not differentiated into sublineages. Consequently, the Central Asia Outbreak (CAO), Central Asia (CA), B0/W148, and Clade A sublineages, despite their epidemiological importance, were not thoroughly studied.

To address this knowledge gap, the Mtb population from 15 regions of Kazakhstan was analyzed using whole-genome sequencing. WGS-based genotyping revealed a strong geographic dominance of the CAO sublineage, particularly in the southern and western regions, while clades L2.2.M4.9 and B0/W148 were present to a lesser extent. The high prevalence of the CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) sublineage indicates successful clonal transmission and adaptation in the region.

Putative compensatory variants were detected in 80.7% of RIF-resistant CAO isolates, with rpoA, rpoB, and rpoC mutations displaying strong sublineage specificity. The observed compensatory mutations may have arisen in response to the fitness costs associated with primary resistance mutations by affecting the growth and fitness of Mtb. Several compensatory mutations exhibited strong sublineage-specific enrichment. Notably, rpoA T187A and rpoC V1039A were the most frequent and were predominantly associated with the dominant CAO sublineage (L2.2.M4.9.1). Other recurrent mutations included rpoC L449V, rpoC V483A/G, and rpoB R552H, which suggests that they play a role in restoring fitness among rifampicin-resistant strains. Due to the low frequency of the variants in the studied population, statistical correlations for rare compensatory mutations were not feasible. Instead, we focused on the most frequently observed mutations and detected significant associations between the RR-conferring mutation rpoB S450L and several compensatory variants (e.g., rpoA T187A, rpoC V483A/G, V1039A, and rpoB R552H). Homoplasy analysis supports the notion of non-random co-occurrence between resistance-conferring and compensatory mutations, particularly among rpoC mutations (V483A/G and V1039A).

Importantly, BDQ has been considered a core drug for the treatment of MDR-TB in Kazakhstan since 201843. In this study, however, only 50 out of 177 Mtb isolates were collected after 2018, primarily from the capital city of Astana. As expected, mutations in the Rv0678 gene were detected exclusively in Mtb isolates from Astana (n = 4). These findings imply that there was a low level of intrinsic or transmitted BDQ-resistance in the local Mtb population in Kazakhstan before 2018.

The current study elucidated the evolutionary history of the Mtb population using whole-genome sequencing in conjunction with Bayesian and phylogenetic analyses. The results revealed an increase in the effective population size of the Mtb population in the 1990s and at the turn of the century. The first sudden surge occurred after the dissolution of the Soviet Union in late 1991, whereas the second one coincided with Kazakhstan’s lowest GDP level, which occurred during the 1997 energy crisis and the 1998 financial crisis. Merker et al., (2018)19 suggested another possible factor in the increase in CAO effective population size in neighboring Uzbekistan. Indeed, both Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan introduced the WHO-recommended DOTS strategy in 1998. If a significant proportion of CAO strains were first-line drug-resistant, patients without proper DST would have been prescribed standard first-line DOTS therapy, which is ineffective against these strains. This selective pressure may have contributed to the expansion of the CAO population, favoring CAO MDR-TB strains in both countries. Both studies identified the CAO sublineage as the dominant clone circulating in the region. In the studied cohort, which was collected between 2010 and 2022, CAO accounted for 67.2% of L2 isolates. Meanwhile, Merker et al. observed that this clade represented 62.5% of MDR-TB cases in Karakalpakstan (Uzbekistan) between 2001 and 2006. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed the monophyletic origin of CAO isolates, supporting their widespread dissemination and ongoing interregional expansion. Moreover, whole-genome SNP analysis revealed that over 50% of the isolates classified within the CAO (L2.2.M4.9.1) sublineage belong to a single large genomic cluster, with pairwise distances below 12 SNPs (Fig.S2-S3, Table S7). The finding is particularly striking considering that the Mtb isolates were sampled from 15 geographically diverse regions across Kazakhstan.

Importantly, the effects of impoverishment on a population and its consequences for health are not instantaneous. The same pattern of evolutionary dynamics was observed for the B0/W148 clone in Russia following the collapse of the Soviet Union, thereby highlighting the major impact of a socioeconomic crisis on the healthcare system14. The persistence and spread of the B0/W148 clone were facilitated by compensatory mutations in the rpoABC genes, which alleviate the fitness cost of rifampicin resistance and enhance transmissibility44,45.

Thawornwattana et al. originally proposed the existence of distinct subclades within several Mtb genotypes, including L2.2.M4.9, which are potentially associated with local outbreaks9. To confirm this, a large-scale comparative phylogenetic analysis was conducted using publicly available WGS data on the major representative CAO sublineage, L2.2.M4.9.1. In-depth phylogenetic analysis revealed six new historical clusters (L2.2.M4.9.3 to L2.2.M4.9.8), which are defined by specific SNPs and exhibited higher intra-cluster distances than those described within the L2.2.M4.9.1 phylogenetic tree. Although our study proposes refinements to the nomenclature of L2/Beijing subclades and highlights the presence of compensatory mutations in the rpoA, rpoB, and rpoC genes in RIF-resistant Mtb isolates, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Both a new genotyping scheme and the identification of compensatory mutations are based on a relatively limited dataset of isolates from Kazakhstan. As such, these results remain preliminary and require validation in larger, more geographically diverse collections of Mtb isolates.

In conclusion, genomic characterization revealed that the CAO sublineage has been highly successful during the spread of MDR-TB in Kazakhstan, which has coincided with a period of major economic fluctuations. The observed genomic clustering pattern indicates an ongoing epidemic, characterized by the expansion of a single CAO clone across multiple regions of Kazakhstan. We hypothesize that continued transmission of CAO strains without timely DST and individualized treatment could result in an increase in XDR-TB cases.

Finally, this study has several limitations. Investigating the epidemiological clustering relationships of all L2.2.M4.9.1 is difficult given its recent history, and genomic epidemiology would benefit from the use of conventional contact tracing methods alongside it. To validate and increase the significance of these findings, it is recommended that a larger sample size of Mtb isolates from Kazakhstan and neighboring countries be utilized in combination with WGS-based and social network analysis over a longer timeframe.
