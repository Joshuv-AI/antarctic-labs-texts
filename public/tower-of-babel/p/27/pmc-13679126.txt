Clinical spectrum and risk factors for severe arrhythmias in non-surgical pediatric patients: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Liuqing Yang, Ce Deng, Bailing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyze the clinical spectrum and identify risk factors for severe arrhythmias in children.MethodsA single-center retrospective study was conducted on 428 consecutive children (0–18 years) diagnosed with arrhythmia between January 2021 and October 2025. Patients were classified as having severe arrhythmia-related clinical presentation (severe group, n = 107) or non-severe presentation (non-severe group, n = 321) according to the presence of hemodynamic instability, syncope, heart failure, or malignant arrhythmia features. Clinical spectrum characteristics were collected. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for severe arrhythmias. Electrocardiographic (ECG), echocardiographic, and serological parameters were compared.ResultsThe severe group had higher proportions of infants/toddlers (0–3 years), underlying cardiac disease, syncope history, and class III-IV heart function (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified these as independent risk factors (P < 0.05): infant/toddler age [2.30 (1.08–4.89)], underlying cardiac disease [12.66 (6.01–26.68)], syncope history [45.84 (12.74-164.91)], and class III-IV heart function [32.97 (13.39–81.17)]. The prediction model showed good fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.171) and discrimination (AUC = 0.925, 95% CI: 0.893–0.958). The severe group also exhibited longer QTc, wider QRS, more third-degree atrioventricular block, higher ventricular premature burden, larger cardiac dimensions (LVEDD/LAD Z-score), higher pulmonary artery systolic pressure and myocardial injury/heart failure markers (CK-MB, cTnI, BNP, NT-proBNP), but lower heart rate variability (SDNN), left ventricular ejection fraction, and serum potassium (P < 0.05).ConclusionInfant/toddler age, underlying cardiac disease, syncope history, and advanced heart failure are independent clinical risk factors for severe pediatric arrhythmias. A model incorporating these factors demonstrates strong predictive ability for early high-risk identification.

Introduction

Pediatric arrhythmias represent a common yet highly heterogeneous group of disorders within childhood cardiovascular medicine. Clinical presentations vary widely, from benign symptoms like occasional palpitations to life-threatening conditions such as syncope, hemodynamic compromise, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death [1, 2]. Moreover, the natural history and prognosis of a given rhythm disturbance can differ substantially based on the child’s age and underlying health status [3]. This complexity makes risk stratification and diagnosis particularly challenging in children compared with adults. Young children often cannot articulate symptoms clearly, leading to early signs being misinterpreted as irritability, fatigue, or respiratory illness [4]. Furthermore, key electrophysiological parameters, including QT interval, heart rate variability, and conduction times, exhibit pronounced age-dependent variation, complicating the distinction between normal variants and pathological abnormalities [5]. Pediatric arrhythmias also frequently occur in association with structural heart disease, cardiomyopathies, inherited channelopathies, perinatal insults, or systemic disorders. Thus, electrocardiographic (ECG) classification alone is inadequate to predict which patients will progress to severe events or when intensified monitoring or early intervention is required.

Timely recognition of severe arrhythmias is critical in clinical practice. Once syncope, shock, acute heart failure, or malignant rhythms occur, children may rapidly deteriorate into circulatory collapse, worsening myocardial injury, or multi-organ hypoperfusion. The narrow therapeutic window heightens the demands on clinical decision-making, resource allocation, and patient transfer [6, 7]. Conversely, certain rhythm disturbances, such as low-burden premature beats or brief supraventricular tachycardia, are often self-limiting or improve with trigger management. Unnecessary testing or aggressive treatment in these cases can impose undue physical, emotional, and economic burdens on patients and families [8, 9]. Hence, a practical risk-stratification tool that uses readily available clinical data at initial presentation is needed. Such a tool could improve triage in emergency and outpatient pediatric settings, guide monitoring intensity for high-risk cases, and help avoid unnecessary investigations and hospitalizations.

Current evidence on pediatric arrhythmia risk remains fragmented. Many studies focus on specific populations, such as postoperative congenital heart disease patients or children with inherited arrhythmia syndromes, or evaluate singular diagnostic or therapeutic techniques. Consequently, there is limited information on the clinical spectrum and integrated risk factors for severe arrhythmias in non-surgical pediatric populations. Importantly, the generalizability of existing risk models may be restricted by local factors including patient age distribution, prevalence of underlying cardiac disease, referral pathways, and diagnostic resource availability [10]. Postoperative arrhythmias following cardiac surgery often have distinct mechanisms and risk profiles. To focus on the natural clinical spectrum of pediatric arrhythmias and avoid confounding by surgical interventions, children with prior cardiac surgery were excluded from this study. Therefore, a systematic assessment of the clinical characteristics of children with arrhythmias in a consecutive, real-world cohort is warranted. Identifying independent risk factors, drawn from symptoms, underlying cardiac conditions, and functional status, and combining them with ECG, echocardiographic, and biomarker profiles could support the development of a clinically interpretable and actionable prediction model.

Accordingly, this study aims to compare the clinical spectrum between children with severe arrhythmia-related clinical presentations and those with non-severe presentations within a non-surgical cohort, perform multivariate logistic regression to identify independent risk factors, and evaluate the resulting model’s discriminative performance. Differences in ECG parameters, echocardiographic measures, and serum biomarkers will also be analyzed. This work seeks to provide actionable evidence to aid early identification and management of children at high risk for severe arrhythmias.

Materials and methods

Patient data

A single-center retrospective cohort study design was employed. This study focused exclusively on non-surgical pediatric patients; children with prior cardiac surgery or other cardiac interventions were deliberately excluded to avoid confounding effects on arrhythmia patterns and cardiac structure/function. Children presenting to the pediatric department of our hospital and diagnosed with arrhythmia for the first time based on clinical evaluation and auxiliary examinations between January 2021 and October 2025 were consecutively enrolled as study subjects (n = 428). Inclusion criteria were: ① age of 0–18 years; ② clear diagnosis of arrhythmias confirmed by standard 12-lead ECG and/or 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring; ③ complete clinical records including demographic data, detailed medical history, and physical examination documentation; ④ assessable cardiac function classification data [Ross or New York Heart Association Functional Classification (NYHA) classification based on age]; ⑤ available echocardiography report; ⑥ available results for relevant serological biomarkers collected at admission/initial visit; and ⑦ signed informed consent from guardians for the use of clinical data in research. Exclusion criteria included: ① unclear diagnosis of arrhythmia, or only transient arrhythmia (e.g., stress-induced premature beats, arrhythmia resolved immediately after electrolyte correction) without need for clinical follow-up; ② concurrent severe systemic diseases (e.g., hepatic/renal failure, coagulation disorders, autoimmune diseases, malignancies) that could interfere with serological markers or cardiac function assessment; ③ acute phase of infectious diseases (e.g., acute myocarditis, sepsis, severe pneumonia); ④ prior cardiac interventions/surgeries (e.g., cardiac surgery, radiofrequency ablation, pacemaker implantation) that might alter arrhythmia patterns or cardiac structure/function; ⑤ long-term use of antiarrhythmics, digitalis, or corticosteroids with unclear duration and dosage, which could confound parameter assessment; ⑥ concurrent participation in other clinical studies that might affect cardiac rhythm. Based on the presence of hemodynamic instability, syncope, heart failure, or features of malignant arrhythmia [11], children were divided into a severe arrhythmia-related clinical presentation group (severe group, n = 107) and a non-severe presentation group (non-severe group, n = 321). This classification was used as a research-based definition to identify children with severe arrhythmia-related clinical presentations. In this study, “severe” refers to the severity of clinical presentation and risk of adverse events associated with arrhythmia, rather than the arrhythmia subtype itself or overall disease severity. Accordingly, the classification was intended for risk stratification purposes and may not directly correspond to specific treatment decisions.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data for all enrolled children were retrospectively collected through our hospital’s electronic medical record system, laboratory information system, and picture archiving and communication system. To ensure accuracy, two uniformly trained researchers independently extracted and entered data. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior pediatric cardiologist. Specific collection content and methods are as follows:

(1) Clinical spectrum characteristics: Gender, age (stratified per Chinese pediatric standards: infant/toddler 0–3 years, preschool 4–6 years, school-age 7–12 years, adolescent 13–18 years), weight (stratified per Chinese pediatric body mass index standards [12, 13]: underweight, normal weight, overweight/obese), birth details (gestational age, weight), perinatal abnormality history (e.g., asphyxia, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy), maternal medication history during pregnancy, non-cardiac comorbidities (e.g., asthma, epilepsy, thyroid disease), family history of cardiac disease (within three generations of direct relatives), allergy history, underlying cardiac disease (structural or myocardial disease), specific arrhythmia classification, and primary symptoms at presentation were recorded. Cardiac function assessment was based on age: Ross heart failure classification for age < 6 years, NYHA functional classification for age ≥ 6 years [14], derived from physical examination and activity tolerance records in the medical notes.

(2) ECG parameters: From the first resting 12-lead ECG, corrected QT interval and QRS duration were measured. Presence of pre-excitation wave, ST-T segment abnormalities, and atrioventricular (AV) block classification were recorded. From 24-hour ambulatory ECG reports, 24-hour burden (percentage of total beats) for ventricular premature complexes (VPCs) and atrial premature complexes (APCs), and the heart rate variability index standard deviation of normal-to-normal RR intervals (SDNN) were obtained.

(3) Echocardiographic indices: Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) Z-score, left atrial anterior-posterior diameter (LAD) Z-score, and estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) were extracted from echocardiography reports.

(4) Serological indicators: Levels of creatine kinase (CK)-MB, cardiac troponin I (cTnI), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), C-reactive protein (CRP), serum potassium (K+), magnesium (Mg2+), and calcium (Ca2+) measured at admission or initial presentation were collected.

Statistical analysis

This single-center retrospective cohort employed a consecutive enrollment/full sample inclusion strategy: 428 children aged 0–18 years with arrhythmia were enrolled between January 2021 and October 2025, comprising 107 in the severe group and 321 in the non-severe group (severe event rate ~ 25%). For the primary outcome (severe arrhythmia), four independent variables (infant/toddler age 0–3 years, underlying cardiac disease, syncope history, cardiac function class III-IV) entered the final multivariate logistic regression model. The events per variable (EPV) ratio was 107/4 ≈ 26.8, well above the commonly recommended EPV ≥ 10 (or even ≥ 20) for predictive modeling studies, indicating sufficient sample size and event numbers to support stable estimation and reduce overfitting risk for this multivariate model.

SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., USA) was used for statistical analysis. Categorical data were presented as n (%) and compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact test. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as M (P25, P75) and compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Normally distributed data were presented as x ± s, with homogeneity of variance verified by Levene’s test. Two-group comparisons for normally distributed data with equal variance used independent samples t-test; unequal variance used Welch’s t-test. Univariate analysis was used for preliminary variable screening. After confirming no multicollinearity (variance inflation factor, VIF < 5), multivariate logistic regression was performed to build the model. Model fit was assessed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Discriminative ability was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to estimate optimism and calculate optimism-corrected area under the curve (AUC). Additionally, 10-fold cross-validation was conducted to assess the model’s stability, with the mean AUC and standard deviation (SD) reported. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Clinical spectrum characteristic variables showing statistical significance in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. Collinearity diagnostics indicated no multicollinearity among variables (all VIF < 2). The Omnibus test of model coefficients confirmed that the overall model was statistically significant (χ² = 260.001, P < 0.001). The results identified the following as independent risk factors for severe arrhythmias (P < 0.05): infant/toddler age (0–3 years) [2.299 (1.081–4.887)], presence of underlying cardiac disease [12.660 (6.007–26.683)], history of syncope [45.844 (12.744-164.913)], and cardiac function class III-IV [32.966 (13.389–81.169)]. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated good model fit (χ² = 6.399, P = 0.171). The ROC curve derived from this model for predicting severe arrhythmias yielded an AUC of 0.925 (95% CI: 0.893–0.958). At the maximum Youden’s index (0.683), sensitivity was 90.70% and specificity was 77.60%. To evaluate the robustness of the model in this cohort, internal validation of the AUC was conducted: After optimism correction using bootstrap resampling (approximately 1,000 iterations), the mean optimism was 0.0015 and the optimism-corrected AUC was approximately 0.924, which was very close to the apparent AUC. Further 10-fold cross-validation yielded a mean AUC of 0.926 with a SD of 0.042. These findings suggest a low risk of overfitting and indicate good internal reproducibility and robustness of the model (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study, based on 428 consecutively enrolled children with arrhythmias and stratified into severe and non-severe groups according to the severity of arrhythmia-related clinical presentation and adverse event risk, systematically delineated the characteristics of the presenting pediatric arrhythmia population and the risk profile for severe events in a real-world single-center cohort. The key finding is that infant/toddler age (0–3 years), underlying cardiac disease, history (or current episode) of syncope, and cardiac function class III-IV are independent risk factors for severe arrhythmias. The model constructed from these variables demonstrated good fit and strong discriminatory ability, suggesting that effective high-risk screening can be achieved at admission/initial presentation relying primarily on medical history and physical/functional assessment.

First, the independent association between infant/toddler age and severe event risk has a clear clinical rationale. Infants and toddlers have limited self-expression abilities, and their symptoms often manifest as feeding difficulties, irritability, pallor, or recurrent respiratory-like presentations, potentially leading to later presentation or recognition only when the condition is more severe [15, 16]. Additionally, cardiac reserve is relatively limited in this age group, and heart rate and cardiac output are more sensitive to rhythm stability. Once sustained tachycardia, significant bradycardia, or high-grade AV block occurs, progression to low perfusion and heart failure can be rapid [17, 18]. The persistence of infant/toddler age (0–3 years) as an independent factor in the multivariate model suggests that clinical triage and monitoring strategies should maintain a lower threshold for this age group. Even if initial vital signs appear stable, more proactive completion of ambulatory ECG monitoring, assessment of myocardial injury and heart failure markers, and close observation for perfusion/consciousness changes are advisable.

Second, underlying cardiac disease is one of the most weighted structural background factors. It can not only predispose to AV conduction abnormalities, atrial/ventricular remodeling, and electrophysiological instability through structural anomalies and pressure/volume load changes, but may also be accompanied by pulmonary hypertension, chamber dilation, and systolic dysfunction, thereby amplifying the circulatory impact of arrhythmias [19, 20]. The observed lower LVEF, higher LVEDD and LAD Z-scores, and higher PASP in the severe group on echocardiography support the cascade of structural/functional impairment, decreased electrical stability, and increased severe event risk. Therefore, for children with concurrent structural or myocardial disease, viewing arrhythmia merely as an electrical problem may underestimate risk. A more prudent strategy involves integrating chamber remodeling, systolic function, and pulmonary circulation pressure reflected by echocardiography into overall risk assessment, and initiating collaboration with pediatric cardiology specialists early if needed to formulate more proactive heart failure and rhythm control plans.

Third, syncope emerged as one of the strongest clinical warning signs in this study. In our cohort, all children had documented arrhythmias, and syncope was significantly more prevalent in the severe group. This finding underscores syncope as a key clinical indicator of high-risk arrhythmias specifically within children already diagnosed with arrhythmias, rather than in the general pediatric population where most cases are non-cardiac in origin. Syncope in this population reflects transient cerebral hypoperfusion, potentially due to brief ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation-like events, long RR intervals from high-grade AV block, or extreme tachycardia reducing cardiac output [21, 22]. While vasovagal syncope is common in pediatrics, its occurrence alongside a confirmed arrhythmia substantially increases the risk of adverse events. The markedly higher proportion of syncope in the severe group indicates that it should not be considered merely a concomitant symptom, but a critical trigger for intensified evaluation and management. In clinical practice, children with a history of unexplained syncope, atypical or absent prodromal symptoms, exertion- or sleep-related episodes, combined conduction block, significant QTc prolongation, or high VPC burden should be prioritized for hospitalization, extended Holter/event recorder evaluation, and necessary genetic or electrophysiological assessment [23, 24].

Fourth, cardiac function class III-IV also showed a strong independent association and was significantly more prevalent in the severe group on univariate analysis. This finding indicates that within the pediatric arrhythmia population, severe events depend not only on the rhythm type itself but also critically on cardiac pump reserve. Cardiac dysfunction may be either a consequence of arrhythmia (e.g., tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy) or a manifestation of underlying cardiomyopathy/structural disease. Regardless of causality, poorer cardiac function implies lower tolerance to rhythm fluctuations and a greater likelihood of circulatory instability under similar rhythm burden [25, 26]. Therefore, age-stratified Ross/NYHA functional assessment is both practical and immediate for risk stratification, particularly suitable for resource-limited or emergency decision-making settings.

Beyond clinical risk factors, this study further delineated the high-risk phenotype of the severe group across ECG, echocardiographic, and serological domains. The severe group exhibited longer QTc, wider QRS, higher third-degree AV block prevalence, greater VPC burden, and lower SDNN, suggesting combined involvement of conduction system impairment, ventricular repolarization abnormalities, diminished autonomic regulation, and increased ventricular ectopic activity in the occurrence and sustenance of severe events [2]. Echocardiographic findings of chamber dilation, elevated pulmonary pressure, and reduced systolic function provide structural and functional evidence for electro-mechanical coupling imbalance [27]. Serologically, elevated CK-MB, cTnI, BNP, and NT-proBNP alongside lower serum K+ point to increased myocardial injury and heart failure burden, suggesting that electrolyte imbalance may be a modifiable risk amplifier [28–30]. Clinically, these differences support adopting a more systematic bundled assessment for suspected high-risk cases: not only determining rhythm type but also concurrently evaluating myocardial injury, heart failure severity, and electrolyte status to promptly correct reversible triggers and mitigate deterioration risk.

It is worth emphasizing that the multivariate model constructed here achieved an AUC of 0.925, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated good fit, suggesting strong discriminatory ability and reasonable calibration within this cohort. From an application perspective, all model variables are readily obtainable clinically (age stratification, underlying cardiac disease, syncope history, cardiac function class), without reliance on costly or time-consuming tests, making it suitable for early stratification in emergency/outpatient settings. Once high risk is indicated, priority can be given to monitored beds, shortening Holter wait times, early bedside echocardiography and heart failure/myocardial injury marker testing, and initiating multidisciplinary consultation or transfer plans if needed.

Concurrent with these strengths, the generalizability of the model should be interpreted cautiously. This study excluded children with prior cardiac surgery or other cardiac interventions to focus on non-surgical pediatric arrhythmias; therefore, the findings may not directly apply to postoperative populations where arrhythmia mechanisms and risk profiles differ. Although we stratified patients by severe vs. non-severe arrhythmia based on hemodynamic and clinical criteria, we acknowledge that alternative endpoints, including initiation of antiarrhythmic therapy, hospitalization, or long-term treatment requirements, could provide complementary insights into clinical severity and management decisions. The single-center retrospective design may be subject to selection and information bias. Although severe event classification was based on clear clinical criteria, variations in physician documentation and management strategies could influence grouping. In addition, detailed electrophysiological subclassification of arrhythmias was not consistently available in this retrospective dataset. Therefore, specific arrhythmia subtypes were analyzed using broader categories rather than more granular classifications, which may have limited a more comprehensive characterization of the clinical spectrum and subtype-specific risk profiles. Moreover, the model lacks external cohort validation and prospective application assessment. Future studies should test its stability in multi-center, more finely stratified cohorts and explore whether incorporating key ECG/echocardiographic/biomarker variables as incremental predictors can further enhance performance while maintaining simplicity. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized data collection and detailed arrhythmia subtype characterization are also warranted to further evaluate subtype-specific clinical features and risk patterns.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides systematic evidence for severe event risk identification in pediatric arrhythmias across four dimensions: clinical spectrum, independent risk factors, multimodal phenotypic differences, and actionable prediction model. Infant/toddler age, underlying cardiac disease, syncope history, and cardiac function class III-IV are the priority high-risk signals. These are corroborated by objective indicators including conduction block, QTc prolongation, increased ventricular ectopic burden, chamber dilation, and elevated heart failure/myocardial injury markers. Integrating this information into initial decision-making workflows has the potential to improve early identification efficiency and management timeliness for high-risk children, reducing missed diagnoses and delays for severe events, and laying the groundwork for subsequent multi-center validation and standardized management pathway development.
