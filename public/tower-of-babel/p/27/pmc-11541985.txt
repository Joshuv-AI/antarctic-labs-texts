Navigating the Landscape of Plasma Biomarkers in Alzheimer's Disease: Focus on Past, Present, and Future Clinical Applications
Authors: Sarrah E. Ankeny, Julia R. Bacci, Boris Decourt, Marwan N. Sabbagh, Michelle M. Mielke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As the prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and its impact on healthcare systems increase, developing tools for accurate diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression is a priority. Recent technological advancements have allowed for the development of blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) to aid in the diagnosis of AD, but many questions remain regarding the clinical implementation of these BBMs. This review outlines the historical timeline of AD BBM development. It highlights key breakthroughs that have transformed the perspective of AD BBMs from theoretically ideal but unattainable markers, to clinically valid and reliable BBMs with potential for implementation in healthcare settings. Technological advancements like single-molecule detection and mass spectrometry methods have significantly improved assay sensitivity and accuracy. High-throughput, fully automated platforms have potential for clinical use. Despite these advancements, however, significant work is needed before AD BBMs can be implemented in widespread clinical practice. Cutpoints must be established, the influence of chronic conditions and medications on BBM levels must be better understood, and guidelines must be created for healthcare providers related to interpreting and communicating information obtained from AD BBMs. Additionally, the development of BBMs for synaptic dysfunction, inflammation, and cerebrovascular disease may provide better precision medicine approaches to treating AD and related dementia. Future research and collaboration between scientists and physicians are essential to addressing these challenges and further advancing AD BBMs, with the goal of integration in clinical practice.

Key Summary Points

Blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) offer a less invasive and more accessible alternative to cerebrospinal fluid analysis via lumbar puncture and positron emission tomography imaging for in vivo assessment of AD pathology.Advancements in assay technology including single-molecule detection and mass spectrometry methods have improved sensitivity, allowing BBMs to be reliably detected and creating the potential for their implementation in both primary and secondary healthcare settings to aid in the diagnosis of AD.Before application in clinical practice, standardized cutpoints and guidelines for physicians need to be established, and the influence of chronic conditions on BBM levels must be understood to avoid misdiagnosis.Beyond amyloid-beta, phosphorylated tau, and neurofilament light, additional BBMs associated with synaptic dysfunction, inflammation, and cerebrovascular disease offer potential avenues for enhancing diagnostic precision and advancing personalized medicine approaches.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) dementia is a progressive, neurodegenerative disease currently affecting nearly 6.7 million individuals living in the United States, and it is predicted that this number will double by 2060 [1]. Historically, the definite diagnosis of AD could only be obtained post mortem with confirmation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau pathologies in the brain; the diagnosis of possible or probable AD was based on clinical symptoms alone [2]. This resulted in inaccurate diagnoses because AD clinical symptoms are heterogenous and overlap with the clinical presentations of other neurodegenerative diseases, such as vascular dementia and Lewy body dementia [3]. For example, studies have shown that up to 30% of individuals clinically diagnosed with AD by neurologists did not have brain AD pathology at autopsy [4–7]. However, methodological advancements in the field over the past decade have enabled the development of biomarkers for the in vivo assessment of the key pathological hallmarks of AD to aid in the clinical diagnosis. Evidence of Aβ and tau pathologies can be clinically assessed for the diagnosis of AD using cerebrospinal fluid (CSF; i.e., low Aβ42/40 ratio and high phosphorylated tau [P-tau] levels) or positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (i.e., amyloid PET and tau PET). Markers of neurodegeneration can be measured via structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or via changes in CSF protein levels [e.g., elevated neurofilament light (NfL)]. With the development of these in vivo biomarkers, the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association guidelines were updated in 2018 to include both a biological and a clinical definition of AD, defining the disease by its primary pathological hallmarks, including extracellular Aβ plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tau tangles, and neurodegeneration [abbreviated as AT(N)] as well as clinical symptoms [3]. Indeed, studies have shown that the addition of CSF or amyloid PET data provides a more accurate diagnosis of AD dementia in dementia specialty clinics [8].

Although the use of amyloid PET, CSF Aβ, and CSF P-tau biomarkers contribute to a more accurate diagnosis of AD, PET and a lumbar puncture for CSF have not been widely adopted in general clinical practice due to their invasiveness, high cost, and limited accessibility, especially in more rural areas. Notably, the AT(N) framework left open the consideration of new biomarkers as the field progressed [3], for example the inclusion of blood-based biomarkers (BBMs) and the addition of vascular and inflammation biomarkers. Historically, the development of BBMs was difficult due to the lower concentrations of AD proteins in the blood versus CSF, and possible mixed peripheral and brain sources of Aβ and P-tau in the blood compared to CSF. However, with the accelerated technological advancements of the past 5 years, there are now clinically available BBMs to aid in the diagnosis of AD. BBMs offer many advantages over PET and CSF, as they are less invasive, less costly, and more accessible in primary care settings. These qualities, in addition to the feasibility of serially measuring BBMs, support the utility of BBMs for providing an earlier diagnosis of AD. Despite the promise of AD BBMs, however, several questions remain regarding their use in the clinical diagnosis of AD. In this review, we first describe the history of the development of BBMs and some platforms and assays currently clinically available in this rapidly evolving field. We then discuss the potential use of BBMs in clinical practice in the United States and provide an overview of potential new BBMs that are under development but not yet clinically available. This narrative review is intended to provide clinicians with an overview of BBMs clinically available in the United States to aid in the diagnosis of AD.

Methods

The content of this narrative review was based on the epidemiological knowledge and clinical experience of the authors in addition to published literature. The scope of this article was limited to BBMs that are currently clinically available in the United States. Relevant articles were identified using PubMed and the author's knowledge of the field, with citations from these articles being reviewed and included when applicable. Additional information was included from resources available through relevant government agencies and health organizations to offer a comprehensive overview of the landscape of clinically available BBMs for Alzheimer’s disease. Formal inclusion and exclusion criteria for articles were not applied.

This review is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Historical Timeline of Developing AD BBMs and Measurement Platforms

Lack of Sensitivity of Early Assays. A major challenge in measuring Aβ and tau pathology using AD BBMs has been that the concentrations of Aβ42 and P-tau are approximately 30- and 100-fold lower than in the CSF, respectively [9, 10]. There are likely several reasons for the lower levels in blood, including (i) the high blood-to-CSF volume ratio; (ii) lower free Aβ levels in the blood because the composition of blood is much more complex than CSF [11] and Aβ can bind to blood erythrocytes [12]; (iii) the selectivity of the blood–brain barrier in the brain parenchyma allows only a fraction of brain proteins to enter the blood via active, protein-driven transport; (iv) drainage from CSF into peripheral blood via arachnoid villi and granulations in the walls of major venous sinuses; and (v) the glymphatic system (see [13] for a summary of these mechanisms). Therefore, although the value and importance of identifying BBMs for AD has been recognized for more than two decades, initial attempts at incorporating these peripheral biomarkers to predict cognitive decline and risk of AD yielded conflicting results due to the lack of sensitivity of the assays [14–16].

Plasma Aβ biomarkers (e.g., Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels and the Aβ42/40 ratio) were the first BBMs to be widely studied in the late 1990s and early 2000s. Although some initial studies found no differences in the plasma Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels or the Aβ42/40 ratio between cognitively unimpaired individuals and patients with AD [17–19], other studies reported either elevated plasma Aβ40 or Aβ42 levels or lower Aβ42 levels for patients with AD [20, 21]. These early studies typically used the sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which lacked the sensitivity needed to detect Aβ40 and Aβ42 in plasma at levels that correlated with more reliable CSF levels [22]. The absence of correlation between blood and CSF Aβ levels led to an initial hypothesis that blood Aβ levels were largely peripherally derived, and thus not representative of the central nervous system [23]. In addition to the lack of sensitive assays, there was also a lack of standard practices for collecting and processing blood samples for analysis, which further contributed to the substantial variability in results.

SiMoA and Mass Spectrometry Methods

The single-molecule enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (SiMoA; Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) was developed in 2010, which allowed for the detection of individual enzyme-labeled proteins by reducing the matrix effects of the assay via large dilution of plasma and serum samples [24]. This method enhanced the sensitivity for detecting low-concentration proteins, such as Aβ42 and P-tau, compared to conventional ELISA methods [25]. In particular, plasma Aβ42 was measurable to levels as low as 0.032 pg/mL with almost 100% recovery using SiMoA [26], demonstrating a key breakthrough towards a more accurate quantification of blood Aβ.

Several additional techniques have since been developed. For example, immunoprecipitation–liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (IP-LC/MS) and immunoprecipitation–matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (IP-MALDI) were introduced in 2014 as high-precision techniques for measuring plasma Aβ levels [27, 28], and subsequently shown to be highly accurate for detecting brain amyloid pathology compared to amyloid PET [29, 30]. In the immunoprecipitation step, the target analyte binds to antibody-coated beads and then is eluted. Once isolated, mass spectrometry is used for quantification. IP-MS methods are highly specific for their target analyte and outperform SiMoA and other immunoassays for Aβ [31]. Companies with available mass spectrometry assays include C2N Diagnostics and Quest Diagnostics . However, despite the high accuracy of IP-MS for measuring AD BBMs, limitations of this method include the higher cost and lower throughput compared to immunoassays, which make translation to general clinical settings difficult.

MSD Platform

Initial papers that attempted to quantify blood P-tau181 and 217 utilized the Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) platform [32–34]. The MSD platform is plate-based and structured similarly to an ELISA. However, instead of employing typical enzyme-linked colorimetric methods, MSD utilizes electrochemiluminescence, which increases measurement sensitivity and dynamic ranges. Electrodes on the bottom of the MSD plate create a reaction resulting in emission of light following application of electricity with the MSD reader; the resulting light intensity is used for quantification. In addition to increased sensitivity, the MSD system’s multiplex technology allows assays for up to 10 analytes to be measured within a single well. However, matrix effects in plasma and serum prevent the accurate measurement of blood Aβ levels using MSD. Further, despite the reliable measurement of total and P-tau levels in research settings, this platform is unlikely to have the throughput clinically necessary for population-based measurement because it is plate-based and must be run manually.

Development of Fully Automated Platforms

Most recently, two fully automated platforms, Elecsys (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA) and Lumipulse (Fujirebio, Ghent, Belgium), were developed for the measurement of AD BBMs. Both platforms use chemiluminescence for quantification. Studies have shown that the measurement of Aβ42/40 and P-tau181 using these platforms is highly accurate for detecting brain amyloid compared to amyloid PET [35, 36]. These fully automated assays are promising for clinical implementation in primary and secondary care because they are sensitive, high-throughput approaches that eliminate manual labor and reduce the opportunity for human error. For all platforms described above, antibodies recognizing Aβ and P-tau isoforms are utilized.

Importance of Sample Processing and Storage

In addition to the above-described technological developments, standards for the collection and processing of blood samples to measure AD BBMs have also been under development and refinement. Initial studies focused on the identification of pre-analytical variables including fasting status, needle gauge, order of the blood draw tubes, sample pre-processing, and storage size of aliquots [37, 38]. A joint effort by the Standardization of Alzheimer’s Blood Biomarkers workgroup and the Global Biomarker Standardization Consortium more recently developed a standard operating procedure for blood sample handling based on empirical evidence [39]. Current Status of Clinically Available AD BBMs

Although many of the initial AD BBM assays, antibodies, and platforms are still only available for research use, some AD BBMs have now become available for clinical use. At present, in the United States, all clinically available tests are considered laboratory-developed tests (LDTs), meaning that none are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as in vitro diagnostics (IVDs). LDTs are designed, manufactured, and used in a single laboratory or laboratory network and undergo limited analytical and clinical validation or regulatory review [40, 41]. In contrast, FDA-approved IVDs are tests that have undergone regulatory review, demonstrated reliable performance, and been validated for analytical and clinical performance in the intended-use population. BBMs represent a rapidly moving field, and FDA approval of some BBMs will likely occur in the next 2 years.

The determination of which AD BBM LDTs are most useful for clinical implementation in primary and/or secondary care is ongoing. There are multiple contexts of use for which BBMs could be deployed, and the authors refer readers to Blennow et al. (2023) for a more comprehensive review of this topic [42]. Briefly, BBMs in clinical settings can potentially be used to rule out AD pathologies as a contributing cause of cognitive impairment, as a diagnostic test for AD, as a prognostic test to predict disease progression, or to assess the efficacy of disease-modifying therapies (DMTs). Head-to-head comparisons of different assays measuring Aβ and P-tau have been conducted and show differences in accuracy and performance across platforms, assays, and antibodies [31, 43]. Oftentimes, the choice of BBM is dependent on the availability of assays and platforms at the clinical laboratory accessible to a specific health system. However, it is important that healthcare providers are aware of these differences.

Clinical Use of Plasma Biomarkers

The Alzheimer’s Association released appropriate use recommendations in 2022 that cautiously advised the utilization of BBMs in specialty care, with additional confirmatory CSF or PET biomarker measurements when possible [44]. Since those recommendations were published, there has been increasing use of the BBMs in both specialty and primary care. BBMs should not be used as a stand-alone test to diagnose AD, but rather alongside other examinations as part of a diagnostic workup for patients with cognitive impairment that is suspected to result from AD pathology. Importantly, BBMs should not be utilized in clinical settings as the sole source of information to identify preclinical AD in cognitively unimpaired patients until a DMT is available for this stage of the disease. As such, timely detection and diagnosis of AD in clinical settings relies upon identifying both cognitive impairment and pathology, necessitating parallel advances in screening tests for cognition and AD pathology. A brief description of the AD BBMs (Aβ42/40 ratio, P-tau181, P-tau217, and NfL) and their potential clinical application is provided below.

Aβ42/40 Ratio

Differences in Aβ42/40 ratios between individuals with and without elevated brain amyloid pathology, as determined by PET, are less pronounced in plasma (approximately a 15% reduction) than in CSF (approximately a 50% reduction) [29]. Despite a lower blood concentration, studies have consistently demonstrated that the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio has excellent diagnostic accuracy to detect elevated brain amyloid across the AD cognitive spectrum [29, 30, 45]. However, the diagnostic performance of the Aβ42/40 ratio as a stand-alone test of elevated brain amyloid varies depending upon the assay used. For example, the plasma Aβ42/40 ratios measured using the Lumipulse and IP-MS have higher diagnostic accuracy for elevated amyloid PET (area under the curve [AUC] of 0.81 and 0.84) compared to the antibodies developed for the SiMoA platform (AUC of 0.57) [46]. The longitudinal trajectory of Aβ42/40 ratios has been shown to be largely stable in individuals with no cognitive impairment and with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), indicating that this BBM will not be informative with regard to tracking disease progression [47].

In specialty care, the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio has begun to be included as part of an AD diagnostic workup by clinicians who are dementia specialists and have experience interpreting biomarkers [48, 49]. At present, these clinicians are focused on determining the interpretability of the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio and understanding the utilization of the ratio as a stand-alone BBM or in conjunction with additional measures. It is likely that Aβ42/40 ratios should be considered in the context of P-tau levels.

P-tau. Plasma P-tau181 and P-tau217 are biomarkers specific to AD brain pathology and are not increased in other tauopathies such as frontotemporal dementia [34]. Plasma P-tau181 and P-tau217 correlate with both amyloid PET and tau PET across the clinical spectrum of AD and have been found to predict progression from MCI to AD [32, 33, 50, 51]. Although both P-tau181 and P-tau217 are clinically available, several studies now suggest that P-tau217 is more accurate than P-tau181 for detecting elevated brain amyloid [43, 52, 53]. Plasma P-tau217 has demonstrated dynamic longitudinal trajectories, with steeper increases in P-tau217 in more advanced stages of AD [47, 54] and decreases in P-tau217 in patients treated with donanemab [55]. This evidence suggests a potential role for plasma P-tau217 as a BBM to monitor disease progression and DMT efficacy.

Plasma P-tau181 or P-tau217 measurements have begun to be incorporated as an AD diagnostic tool in the evaluation of patients presenting with cognitive impairment or decline in specialty care. Although the context of utilization will evolve in clinical practice as more research becomes available, the tests will be informative for their negative predictive value (i.e., they will indicate that there is a low likelihood that the cognitive decline stems from AD pathology). Contrarily, if the results are positive, further evaluation of Aβ and tau pathologies using CSF or PET will be warranted.

NfL

Plasma and serum neurofilament light (NfL) are BBMs of large-caliber axonal injury and neurodegeneration that are robustly correlated with CSF NfL levels [56, 57]. Importantly, changes in blood NfL correlate more strongly with cognitive symptoms and brain atrophy than blood biomarkers of Aβ or P-tau [57–61]. However, NfL is nonspecific to AD pathology, and levels are increased in other neurodegenerative diseases, such as frontotemporal dementia [62] and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [63]. As such, NfL should not be used in isolation from other pathological markers for the diagnosis of AD, but rather in combination with biological and cognitive markers of AD.

Current Challenges in Incorporating AD BBMs for Clinical Use

The use and implementation of AD BBMs to enhance the clinical diagnosis of AD is in the early stages. Currently, there are several challenges to the implementation of the BBMs for both primary and specialty care, including the lack of established cutpoints for use in primary care settings, impact of multiple chronic conditions and their treatment on the accurate interpretation of the BBMs, and incorporation of AD BBMs into patient care pathways that minimize burden but appropriately first objectively measure cognition. Lack of Established Cutpoints

Although multiple studies suggest the clinical utility of AD BBMs for a more accurate diagnosis of AD, as described above, most studies to date have been conducted in specialty clinic populations. Compared to the general population, these specialty clinics tend to consist of patients and/or research participants who are more likely to be White, of higher socioeconomic status, healthier than the general population with fewer comorbid conditions, and on average younger than the typical patient presenting with cognitive concerns. Thus, the cutpoints for the AD BBMs established in these studies, and their incorporation in clinic use, may not be as accurate in the real world when used in primary care. Even less research has examined the utility of AD BBMs for patients with autosomal-dominant AD (ADAD) or Down syndrome AD (DSAD). It is not currently understood whether cutpoints for the AD BBMs would be similar for patients with ADAD or DSAD compared to cutpoints for patients with late-onset AD.

The recent approvals of DMTs for AD highlight the importance of establishing standardized BBM cutpoints of equivalence across assays and platforms. The label for lecanemab and donanemab requires evidence of amyloid pathology, and this can be obtained via BBMs [64, 65].

Impact of Multiple Chronic Conditions

Compared to CSF AD biomarkers, BBMs are more influenced by peripheral physiological mechanisms that can influence the levels of the BBMs and potentially result in a false positive or negative diagnosis. Several studies have now demonstrated that certain chronic conditions and medications may impact the accuracy and interpretation of the AD BBMs for the diagnosis of AD. For example, chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been shown to cause elevated plasma Aβ42 and Aβ40, P-tau181, P-tau217, and NfL levels due to reduced renal function and clearance. As a result, patients with CKD may be falsely diagnosed with AD when, in fact, they do not have AD brain pathology [52, 66, 67]. The use of biomarker ratios may mitigate some of the effects of CKD on the biomarker levels [67, 68]. In addition to CKD, obesity has also been shown to result in lower levels of the AD BBMs due to greater blood volume [52, 67–69].

The use of the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio alone for detecting amyloid pathology may also not be appropriate in patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) or who use some cardiovascular medications. Aβ peptides are degraded by nearly 20 enzymes in the brain and in the periphery, including neprilysin (NEP), insulysin (insulin-degrading enzyme; IDE), angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), and cathepsin B [70]. A recent study reported that the medication combining sacubitril/valsartan, which is used to manage chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and hypertension, significantly decreases the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio, but at the same time increases absolute levels of blood Aβ40 and Aβ42 [71]. This effect is likely the result of NEP inhibition by sacubitril, because the angiotensin type I receptor (AT1) antagonist valsartan alone had no effects on plasma Aβ levels and the Aβ ratio (to note, valsartan acts on AT1 receptors, not on ACE, and does not affect Aβ degradation). Thus, plasma Aβ42/40 ratio should not be included in the clinical diagnosis of AD among patients treated with therapeutic interventions that affect the activity and availability of Aβ-degrading enzymes (e.g., NEP and ACE inhibitors used in the management of CVD). Notably, the authors reported that plasma P-tau181 and P-tau217 were not significantly affected by sacubitril/valsartan and may therefore be more accurate biomarkers of brain amyloid pathology among patients with CVD.

Integration and Implementation of BBMs into Primary Care

Primary care physicians (PCPs) are inundated with the overflow of information for every disease. Most PCPs do not feel comfortable making a diagnosis of AD given the lack of training, the long-held belief that AD can only be diagnosed with an autopsy, and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) doubling down on not including cognitive screening at the annual Medicare visit. As a result, the expectation that PCPs will be able to diagnose AD with more confidence is incongruous with the reality. The alternative solution is to teach PCPs to use the BBMs for symptomatic patients, because PCPs are comfortable managing patient care by blood values including prostate-specific antigen (PSA), HbA1C, and cholesterol panels. One possible solution, among others, would be to expand guidelines to support the use of BBMs in primary care for patients with cognitive impairment and have them endorsed by the American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP) and the American Geriatrics Society (AGS). However, such a shift in clinical practice will occur only after FDA approval of IVDs.

The development of patient care pathways for incorporation of AD BBM biomarkers, as well as CSF and PET biomarkers, across primary and secondary care is ongoing. Please see the following references for in-depth summaries and proposed patient care pathways [72–75]. Ultimately, the patient care pathways may differ depending on the healthcare system and access to CSF and PET biomarkers. For example, PET tracers may not be available in remote geographical areas (e.g., rural areas), and primary care providers in these areas are rarely trained to carry out lumbar punctures. In addition to the challenges described above in implementing the AD BBMs, ethical considerations are warranted, because a positive AD blood test in a medical record may prevent access to long-term care insurance, life insurance, and disability insurance. This potential consequence needs to be discussed with the patient prior to performing the blood test. Further, although AD BBMs measured using immunoassays are typically reimbursed, other blood tests using mass spectrometry have higher out-of-pocket costs. This needs to be remedied before BBMs will be available for widespread use in primary care [72].

Exploration of Additional Blood Biomarkers for Improved Personalized Medicine Approaches

Based on the challenges indicated above, to better discriminate dementia types, and to identify precision-medicine-based treatment regimens, it will be necessary to integrate additional BBMs in the diagnostic process in specialty settings. Some biomarkers, such as new P-tau isoforms, may help better identify the stage and pathological burden of AD. Other biomarkers may help to identify beyond AT(N) to assess potential co-pathologies (e.g., vascular pathology, Lewy body pathology) or fully describe disease heterogeneity and mechanisms both upstream and downstream of AD pathology. As a result, the AT(N) framework has been recently expanded to ATX(N) [76]. Below we briefly present an update on new P-tau isoforms in addition to what are currently clinically available. We also provide some studies and examples of promising future biomarkers of “X” that, in our opinion, appear promising as BBMs to aid in the accurate diagnosis and prognosis of AD and related dementias.

Additional Plasma P-tau Isoforms

In addition to plasma P-tau181 and P-tau217, other P-tau isoforms including P-tau231 and P-tau212 have been shown to robustly detect AD pathology across the clinical spectrum [77–79]. Multiple studies suggest that plasma P-tau217 has a greater fold increase than P-tau181 or P-tau231 for participants with versus without elevated brain amyloid assessed via CSF or amyloid PET [80]. However, plasma P-tau231 levels may increase earlier than P-tau217 and P-tau181 [47, 81]. It is not currently known if or when plasma P-tau231 may become clinically available.

Markers of Synaptic Dysfunction

Neurogranin (Ng) is a postsynaptic, dendritic protein associated with protein kinase C, mainly found in cortical and hippocampal neurons [82]. CSF Ng is increased in AD [83]. While, overall, plasma Ng levels were not found to be different between AD and cognitively unimpaired subjects [84], the examination of plasma neuronal-derived exosomes (NDEs) showed that Ng is decreased in both frontotemporal dementia and AD [85]. The latter was confirmed in a separate study, which also demonstrated intermediate levels of Ng in blood NDEs in MCI [86]. Similar results were observed, with increased CSF levels but decreased NDE levels, of the synaptic proteins neuromodulin/growth associated protein 43 (GAP43), synaptosome associated protein 25 (SNAP25), and synaptotagmin 1 (SYT1) [86–89]. Additional studies are warranted to validate these results in larger cohorts.

Markers of Inflammatory Processes

This category includes markers of reactive glial cells, neuroimmunity, and neuroinflammation. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is overexpressed by reactive astrocytes during brain inflammatory events, including traumatic brain injury and neurodegeneration, and CSF levels are increased in several neurodegenerative disorders (NDD) [90]. Elevated plasma GFAP levels have been associated with both AD and cerebrovascular pathologies [91].

Another astrocytic protein expressed during CNS inflammatory events and altered in NDDs is chitinase-3-like protein 1 (also termed YKL-40) [92, 93]. As reported in a recent meta-analysis, CSF levels of YKL-40 are significantly higher in AD than in MCI and cognitively unimpaired individuals [94]. Independent studies reported that plasma [95] and serum [96] YKL-40 levels are increased in patients with AD relative to cognitively unimpaired patients.

A marker associated with phagocytic cells is triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells-2 (TREM2), which binds oligomeric Aβ [97]. CSF soluble TREM2 (sTREM2) levels are elevated in the prodromal and early stages of AD [98] and in multiple sclerosis [99]. Some studies have reported elevated serum sTREM2 levels in patients with all-cause dementia, AD, and vascular dementia [100, 101]. In addition, as suggested by the transient increase in CSF sTREM2 levels, another study observed that high plasma sTREM2 levels in patients with MCI was associated with a greater risk of progression to AD [101].

Markers of Cerebrovascular Disease

Cerebral small vessel pathologies, as measured by MRI [e.g., white matter hyperintensity (WMH)] often co-occur with AD pathology. Abnormal vascular BBMs could be integrated into algorithms comprising AD- and NDD-specific markers to inform clinicians of vascular problems that could be addressed in a treatment plan. Here, we mention a few vascular markers that have been shown to be altered in the blood of patients with MCI and AD.

Vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) and intracellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) are glycoproteins expressed on the surface of endothelial and immune cells [102, 103]. During inflammatory events, both in the periphery and CNS, endothelial cells become activated and overexpress VCAM-1 and ICAM-1, which results in the disruption of the blood–brain barrier and increases leukocyte recruitment, adhesion, and infiltration into the brain parenchyma. CSF ICAM-1 levels were found to increase in prodromal, MCI, and early AD stages [104]. Another small study observed that serum soluble ICAM-1 levels were higher in patients with AD than in those with noninflammatory neurological disease [105]. Similarly, plasma soluble VCAM-1 levels were reported to be higher in patients with AD, vascular dementia, and cerebrovascular disease without dementia [106].

Epidermal growth factor (EGF) is a trophic factor involved in the growth and survival of many cell types, including brain cells. It has been hypothesized that EGF levels decrease in the brain and periphery during NDDs. In Parkinson’s disease, low levels of serum EGF were associated with cognitive decline [107–109]. The levels of plasma EGF were also found to be lower in a cross-sectional Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) sample of patients with MCI or AD compared to patients who were considered cognitively unimpaired. Furthermore, lower baseline levels in patients with MCI predicted faster cognitive decline [109, 110].

Placental growth factor (PlGF) is a trophic factor involved in angiogenesis [111]. PlGF is mainly expressed in the placenta, but can be overexpressed in pathological conditions, including inflammatory events. Elevated CSF PlGF levels have been observed in several NDDs, including patients with MCI or AD [112, 113]. Serum PlGF levels were also found to be higher in patients with AD than cognitively impaired patients with no dementia and cognitively unimpaired patients in an Asian memory clinic cohort [114]. A second, multisite study showed that higher plasma PlGF levels were associated with risk for vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) due to a high burden of WMH [115].

Conclusion

The development of new technologies over the past 5 years has led to clinically available BBMs to aid in the diagnosis of AD in both primary and secondary care. However, many unknowns remain regarding how best to utilize and implement the BBMs in general clinical practice. More diverse cohorts of older adults with multiple chronic conditions and cognitive impairment are needed to further assess the accuracy of the AD BBMs for predicting brain amyloid in the real world, as well as to better understand the impact of medications that degrade Aβ or the impact of chronic conditions that physiologically raise or lower BBM levels. These diverse cohorts will be helpful for establishing more accurate real-world cutoff values for the AD BBMs. The FDA approval of AD BBM IVDs will be critical for increasing clinical use of these biomarkers. In addition, the development of guidelines to support the use of BBMs in primary care for patients with cognitive impairment will be essential. Finally, exploring BBMs of synaptic dysfunction, inflammation, or cerebrovascular disease could address limitations of the AT(N) framework and improve the diagnostic process in specialty settings.
