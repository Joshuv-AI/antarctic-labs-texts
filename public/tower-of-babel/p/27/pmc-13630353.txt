Comparative evaluation of chorion allograft and sub-epithelial connective tissue autograft for the treatment of isolated mandibular recession with laterally closed tunnel (LCT) technique— A pilot study
Authors: Shalini Mundra, Neetha J. Shetty, R. Vani Lakshmi, Dhruv Ahuja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Mandibular recession remains challenging because of anatomical limitations, including frenulum attachment, tooth position, and vestibular depth. The laterally closed tunnel (LCT) technique offers a minimally invasive approach for root coverage. This pilot study compared the effectiveness of LCT with chorion membrane (CM) allograft and subepithelial connective tissue graft (CTG) in isolated mandibular Miller’s Class I, II, and III recessions.Materials and Methods:Six healthy patients with isolated mandibular recession defects ≥3 mm were treated with LCT combined with either CM (test group) or CTG (control group). Three patients received CTG and three received CM. The primary outcome was complete root coverage (CRC) at 12 months.Results:No significant postoperative complications were observed. At 12 months, CRC was achieved in 4 of 6 defects (75%), including 3 in the control group and 1 in the test group. The remaining defects exhibited 75% and 66% root coverage. Mean recession depth decreased from 4.00 mm to 0.00 mm in the control group and from 3.67 mm to 0.67 mm in the test group. Keratinized gingival width increased similarly in both groups. Mean root coverage was 100% in the control group and 75% in the test group (P = 0.193).Conclusion:LCT with CM appears to be a promising alternative to CTG for isolated mandibular recession treatment. Larger randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate potential advantages, including reduced donor-site morbidity.

INTRODUCTION

Gingival recession (GR) presents a challenge to the modern dentist in the 21st century. The primary objective is to achieve a balance between esthetic and functional outcomes while favoring minimally invasive treatment approaches. When GR is left untreated in individuals with good oral hygiene, the depth of the recession is likely to increase over time during long-term follow-up.[1] In addition, GR may result in esthetic impairment, dentin hypersensitivity, root caries, and cervical abrasion.[2]

Various surgical interventions have been suggested for the treatment of mandibular recession, including free gingival graft (FGG), subepithelial connective tissue graft (SCTG) in combination with coronally advanced flap (CAF),[3] laterally advanced flap,[4] and tunneling or modified coronally advanced tunnel (MCAT) techniques.[5] However, most of these procedures have certain limitations. First, the split-flap approach required for flap preparation is difficult to perform in the mandibular anterior region and increases the risk of flap perforation and graft necrosis. Second, achieving tension-free coronal displacement of the flap is challenging and may result in flap dehiscence due to excessive tension. Third, the use of connective tissue grafts (CTGs) may cause patient discomfort because of the need for a second surgical site. Fourth, vertical releasing incisions used in CAF may delay wound healing. Therefore, there is a need for an innovative clinical technique that can reliably manage deep isolated mandibular recession with fewer postoperative complications.

Recent evidence has suggested the use of placental-derived chorion membrane (CM), which has gained popularity in dentistry as a guided tissue regeneration material. Gingival tissue has been compared with fetal tissue in terms of composition, fibroblast characteristics, and its ability to heal without scarring. The presence of stem cell reservoirs, various growth factors, and extracellular proteins such as fibronectin and laminin in the chorion may accelerate healing. Because the chorion is an immune-privileged tissue, it has antimicrobial properties, low immunogenicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability. It is also readily available, cost-effective, and free from major ethical concerns.[67] Based on these properties, we hypothesized that CM would be an excellent material for the management of GR. The aim of this study was to comparatively evaluate the LCT technique with CM allograft and SCTG in the management of isolated mandibular Miller’s Class I, II, and III recessions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The institutional Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocol. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, 1975, as revised in 2000.

In total, six healthy patients, four females and two males aged 20–50 years, were recruited from the Department of Periodontology. The inclusion criteria were age 20–50 years, isolated mandibular Miller’s Class I, II, and III recessions with a depth of ≥3 mm [Figure 1], systemic and periodontal health, and nonsmoking status. A total of six isolated mandibular recession defects were treated. LCT was performed in all six participants on the isolated mandibular recession defect, either in conjunction with CTG (control group) or with CM (test group). Randomization was performed using the coin-toss method. The investigator (NS), who was different from the surgeon (SM), examined and recorded the baseline clinical measurements and allocated the surgical procedure. A masked investigator (VK), blinded to all surgical treatment, assessed all patients postoperatively, tabulated the results, and applied the statistics. The clinical parameters evaluated were probing depth, GR, keratinized gingival width (KGW), and mean root coverage (MRC).

Surgical procedure

After administration of local anesthesia, root planing was performed with curettes. Slightly beveled intrasulcular incisions were made using a microsurgical blade, and a mucoperiosteal tunnel was meticulously created using tunneling instruments. No specific effort was made to eliminate the epithelium around the pouch margins, as this was addressed through the use of beveled intrasulcular incisions. The pouch was further mobilized with the help of tunneling instruments in the apical direction beyond the mucogingival junction and extended in the mesial and distal directions. Any fibrous or muscular attachment inside the prepared pouch was released by blunt dissection to obtain tension-free mobilization of the flap. Care was taken to avoid displacement of the interdental papillae or flap perforation. This minimally invasive approach is called the laterally closed tunnel (LCT) technique. Subsequently, in the test group [Figure 2], cryopreserved CM was inserted and adapted to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ). In the control group [Figure 3], a 2-mm-thick CTG harvested from the palate was carefully inserted and adapted at the CEJ. Finally, the pouch margins were approximated and sutured with interrupted sutures.

Postoperatively, participants were given analgesics (diclofenac and serratiopeptidase [tablet Lyser-D]) twice daily for 2–3 days and an antibiotic (amoxicillin and clavulanic acid 1000 mg [capsule Augmentin]) for 7 days to prevent infection. Participants were instructed to refrain from brushing for 14 days and were advised to use 0.2% chlorhexidine digluconate twice daily for 1 min. Suture removal was performed 14 days after surgery. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using jamovi (version 2.6), an open-source statistical software (The jamovi project, 2025, https://www.jamovi.org). The primary outcome variable was CRC, and MRC was also assessed. Intragroup comparisons between baseline and 12 months were performed using a paired t-test. Differences were considered statistically significant when the P < 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of six patients, four females and two males, who fulfilled the inclusion criteria, were treated with LCT + CTG or LCT + CM. Of these, three recessions were classified as Miller’s Class I, one as Miller’s Class II, and two as Miller’s Class III. All six teeth were mandibular central incisors. No postoperative complications, such as bleeding, infection, or abscess formation, or graft necrosis, were reported.

At 12 months, CRC was achieved in four of the six defects, representing 75% of the treated sites. In the remaining two defects, recession coverage was 75% in one defect and 66% in one defect [Table 1]. As shown in Table 2, mean recession depth (RD) in the control group changed from 4.00 mm at baseline to 0.00 mm at 12 months, whereas in the test group it changed from 3.67 mm at baseline to 0.67 mm at 12 months. MRC gain was 100% in the control group and 75% in the test group (P = 0.193). Mean KGW increased in the control group from 0.67 mm at baseline to 2.33 mm at 12 months and in the test group from 1.00 mm at baseline to 2.33 mm at 12 months.

DISCUSSION

Mandibular recession defects are often observed in association with orthodontic therapy.[8] Several studies have explored the relationship between orthodontic treatment and the occurrence of GR.[8910] Retrospective findings indicate that among orthodontically treated patients, mandibular incisors are particularly prone to GR. Wennström and Lindhe (1994) demonstrated a higher prevalence of recession in orthodontically treated individuals, particularly those with a thin gingival biotype and limited alveolar bone support.[2] A systematic review[11] identified factors such as appliance type, treatment duration, and oral hygiene as determinants of recession development. Collectively, studies[12131415] underscore the complex relationship between orthodontic treatment and GR. These findings emphasize the need for clinician awareness of risk factors and the use of newer strategies such as the minimally invasive LCT technique,[16] to optimize treatment outcomes and shorten treatment time. Other techniques, such as partially epithelialized FGG,[17] have also reported similar outcomes. At 12 months, the single-recession group showed 0.3 ± 0.5 mm of residual recession, corresponding to 94% ±11% root coverage. A similar study using MCAT or LCT in conjunction with hyaluronic acid and SCTG[18] reported an overall MRC of 96.09%, with CRC achieved in 50% of sites. Another study demonstrated that for isolated recessions ≥3 mm, the best outcome was obtained with tunneling plus a laterally positioned pedicle and CTG.

The literature identifies SCTG with CAF, either alone or in combination with another biomaterial, as the gold standard for the treatment of single or multiple recessions.[19] Evidence supports better stability of the gingival margin and creeping attachment with SCTG + CAF compared with other techniques.[19] However, it has certain disadvantages, including being time-consuming, traumatic, and dependent on obtaining connective tissue of uniform thickness. Therefore, a new allograft, human amnion and CM, has gained attention as an alternative to autogenous grafts. It is a readily available, biocompatible membrane enriched with growth factors, cytokines, hyaluronic acid, collagens, laminin, fibronectin, and proteoglycans.[7] Chorion allograft is also a suitable alternative to CTG for the treatment of GR. A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of CM and concluded that it is an ideal membrane with several beneficial properties for the coverage of GR.[20] The membrane has shown promising results in terms of root coverage and improvement in gingival biotype thickness.[21]

Several factors may explain the excellent results obtained in the present study. First, case selection was appropriate, as none of the patients were smokers and only defects with RD of ≥3 mm were included. Second, the minimally invasive nature of the LCT technique provides predictability and facilitates adequate flap mobilization in the mesiodistal and apical directions. Third, the use of CM demonstrated excellent esthetic results without any adverse reactions. Fourth, microsurgical instrumentation enhances illumination and magnification and increases the precision of surgical procedures.

CONCLUSION

Both the placental-derived membrane and CTG showed improved clinical outcomes when used in conjunction with LCT. The disadvantages of CTG and the biological benefits of CM support the use of the membrane. However, the present pilot study has several limitations. First, the technique was evaluated only in isolated mandibular defects, limiting the ability to draw conclusions regarding its effectiveness in multiple recessions. Second, the sample size was small, making the study underpowered; therefore, the reported P value (P = 0.193) should be interpreted with caution, as lack of statistical significance does not imply equivalence between treatment groups. In addition, the coin-toss randomization method is prone to bias, and future studies should use computer-generated sequences or block randomization to improve validity. Third, future studies should include patient-centered outcomes such as pain, esthetics, satisfaction, and morbidity to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment efficacy. The primary advantage of using CM is the avoidance of a second surgical site. Finally, long-term follow-up is necessary to assess the stability and durability of outcomes, which is critical in periodontal regenerative studies. These limitations indicate the need for larger, well-designed randomized controlled trials to validate the present findings.
