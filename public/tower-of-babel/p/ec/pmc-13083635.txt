T cell-intrinsic integrated stress response: friend, foe, or frill?✰
Authors: Si Lu, Chenghang Zhao, Yuxiong Feng
Published: 2026

The integrated stress response (ISR) is a conserved pathway activated by diverse stresses to maintain protein homeostasis, primarily through eIF2α phosphorylation and the transcription factor ATF4.1 Within the complex ecosystem of a tumor, the function of the ISR is sharply compartmentalized. In cancer cells, it is a well-characterized adaptation mechanism that promotes survival under microenvironmental pressure.1 Yet, its roles within tumor-infiltrating immune cells, particularly T lymphocytes, remain a critical and unresolved area of investigation. A recent study by Pauneto et al. significantly advanced this field by identifying T cell–intrinsic ATF4 as a driver of exhaustion, thereby positioning the ISR as a key checkpoint in anti-tumor immunity.2 When considered alongside the established literature, however, this model presents an opportunity for discussion and refinement, as inconsistencies emerge with other studies. In this commentary, we examine these inconsistencies to address important questions that remain: What is the precise role of the T cell ISR in tumor progression, and how definitively is it linked to immunotherapy response?

The “Goldilocks” ISR: a paradox of duality in T cell biology

Pauneto et al. demonstrated that sustained ATF4 activation promotes terminal exhaustion in CD8+ T cells.2 While compelling, this model captures only one facet of a more complex regulatory landscape. The ISR emerges as a double-edged sword in T cell biology: although hyperactivity of ATF4 drives dysfunction, its complete loss also impairs T cell survival and anti-tumor capacity.3 This paradox—wherein both excessive and insufficient signaling are detrimental—suggests a "Goldilocks" principle for ATF4, wherein only a precisely calibrated level of activation permits optimal T cell function. Notably, this duality may not extend uniformly across the ISR pathway. In contrast to ATF4, several studies indicate that loss of the upstream kinase PERK can enhance CD8+ T cell survival and efficacy in multiple disease contexts.4,5 However, the effect of PERK manipulation appears to be T cell subtype-dependent, as its ablation may be detrimental to CD4+ T cells while beneficial to CD8+ T cells.4 This differential sensitivity is likely driven by the distinct metabolic and functional demands of the two lineages: CD4+ T cells, which rely on robust proliferative expansion, lineage differentiation (e.g., toward Th1/Th17 subsets), and helper cytokine networks, appear more dependent on PERK-mediated stress adaptation and translational control for effective responses. In contrast, excessive or sustained PERK activation in CD8+ T cells may impose chronic ISR signaling (e.g., via ATF4/CHOP), leading to metabolic dysregulation, mitochondrial oxidative stress, or restrained effector functions, such that its attenuation enhances cytotoxicity and antitumor efficacy. This cell-type-specificity introduces an additional layer of regulation, suggesting that a distinct "Goldilocks" optimum may exist for PERK expression—one that must balance the survival and function of different T cell subsets within the tumor microenvironment (TME).

To delineate the physiological relevance of the ISR in T cell biology, two approaches are critical. First, inducible genetic systems (e.g., Cre-ERT2 or tetracycline-controlled systems) that enable gene knockout or knock-in with temporal precision are needed to dissect the stage-specific functions of ISR components during T cell activation and exhaustion. Second, the pathway’s relevance must be examined in human settings. Interestingly, a recent study reported a global increase in protein synthesis during human T cell exhaustion without showing a concomitant rise in ATF4 or PERK,6 challenging the presumption of ISR activation in terminal exhausted CD8+ T cells. This is further supported by multiple independent datasets showing no consistent positive correlation between ISR component expression and CD8+T cell exhaustion7,8 (Fig. 1A). From T cell biology to immunotherapies: a translational uncertainty

Building on the foundational biology, a critical question is how these mechanistic insights translate to immunotherapy. Preclinical evidence from the Pauneto et al.’s work suggests T cell–intrinsic ATF4 drives resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).2 Their study also reports that ATF4 expression in peripheral T cells of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) patients is higher in responders to ICI.2 However, since the functional data primarily implicate intra-tumoral T cells, the clinical relevance would be more directly supported by assessing ATF4 in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Analysis of publicly available transcriptomic datasets from ICB-treated patients offers insights into this question. Surprisingly, in cohorts of hepatocellular carcinoma7 and melanoma,9 no significant correlation is observed between CD8+ T cell-intrinsic ATF4 expression and patient response to therapy (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, in a lung cancer cohort, higher ATF4 expression is associated with an improved clinical response—a trend opposite to the prediction derived from the murine model2 (Fig. 1B). While validation in additional cohorts is warranted, these findings from independent human datasets highlight a potential discrepancy between the murine model and clinical observations, suggesting the relationship may be more complex in human cancers.

This discussion also warrants a careful re-examination of the bulk tumor data presented by Pauneto et al., which showed a strong association between high tumor ATF4 levels and ICI resistance.2 Although the correlation is valid, attributing it specifically to CD8+ T cells is confounded by the overwhelming ATF4 signal from malignant cells, as spatial mapping shows < 20 % co-localization with T cells.2 Independent transcriptomic data consistently confirm tumor cells as the predominant source10, 11, 12, 13, 14 (Fig. 1C). Thus, the correlation more accurately identifies ATF4 overexpression in cancer cells—not in CD8+ T cells—as the key predictor of poor response, decisively redirecting therapeutic strategies toward tumor-intrinsic ISR inhibition. A critical next step is to elucidate the precise mechanisms through which ISR activity in cancer cells, T cells, or both drives resistance to immunotherapy.15 Such investigations should determine in which TME compartment ISR exerts a decisive functional role (Friend or Foe) and whether, relative to the more pronounced ISR response observed in tumor cells, its activity in T cells constitutes merely an accessory, non-essential feature (Frill).

The true target of ISR inhibition: a cell-type-specific dilemma

The clinical inconsistencies and cellular source ambiguity naturally lead to a re-examination of the proposed therapeutic mechanism in Pauneto et al.’s work. The exciting finding that the ISR inhibitor ISRIB can sensitize tumors to ICI has been attributed to its action on CD8+ T cells.2 However, this T cell–centric model is difficult to reconcile with the established biochemistry of ISRIB.16,17 ISRIB functions as an activator of eIF2B, which is only inhibited—and thus amenable to rescue by ISRIB—in environments with high levels of phosphorylated eIF2α (p-eIF2α). The reported low p-eIF2α levels in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells suggest that eIF2B is not substantially inhibited in these cells, thereby questioning the mechanistic basis for ISRIB’s action specifically in this compartment.2 This mechanistic paradox suggests that the primary target of ISRIB may lie outside the T cell compartment. Nevertheless, because p-eIF2α is labile and highly sensitive to tissue processing and ex vivo stress, this snapshot observation alone should be interpreted cautiously and does not definitively exclude transient or compartmentalized ISR activation in CD8+ TILs. Future experiments, such as testing ISRIB in models with T cell–specific ATF4 manipulation, could clarify the true cellular target, thereby reframing our therapeutic strategy toward the most relevant cell type in the TME.

Concluding remarks

In summary, the role of the ISR in anti-tumor immunity transcends a linear model of T cell dysfunction. Much like the unfolded protein response,18 another prototypical stress pathway, the ISR exerts exquisitely fine-tuned control over T cell function. This complexity is reflected in its context-dependent duality within T cells, its ambiguous clinical relevance in this compartment, and the emerging possibility that its primary therapeutic effects may be mediated through malignant cells. Although current evidence does not yet allow precise delineation of the conditions under which tumor-intrinsic versus T cell–intrinsic ISR predominantly drives resistance to ICI, we propose that such context dependency may be influenced by tumor histology (e.g., solid versus hematologic malignancies) and immune infiltration status (e.g., “hot” versus “cold” tumors). Therefore, a holistic, cell-type-specific dissection of the ISR across the TME will be essential to unlock the full potential of ISR-modulating immunotherapies.
