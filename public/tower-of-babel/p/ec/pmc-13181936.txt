Clinical efficacy of a modified Ilizarov external mini-fixation technique for boxer’s fractures: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Chen Xie, Yanchen Dong, Dawei Wang, Hongyue Men, Zongyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of a modified application of Ilizarov external mini-fixation (IEP) in comparison with conventional surgical.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 45 patients with boxer’s fractures who underwent surgical intervention between January 2018 and December 2023. Thirteen patients were treated with the modified IEF. Baseline characteristics, intraoperative parameters, and postoperative outcomes—including fracture healing time, residual angulation, metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint range of motion (ROM), return-to-work time, and complication rates—were assessed and compared among the IEF, KW, and LP groups.ResultsComplete fracture healing was achieved in all cases, with no significant differences in mean healing time among the three groups. However, residual angulation was significantly greater in the KW group than in the IEF and LP groups. At 4 weeks postoperatively, the IEF group demonstrated excellent MP joint ROM compared to the KW and LP groups. At the final follow-up, the ROM in the IEF group was comparable to the KW group and slightly better than that observed in the LP group. Patients in the IEF group returned to work significantly earlier than those in the KW. No significant differences in the overall complication rates were observed among the groups. ConclusionsThe modified IEF technique demonstrated early functional advantages, including improved early ROM and expedited return to work, while maintaining long-term efficacy and complication rates comparable to established fixation methods. Despite its inherent limitations, this method may be particularly beneficial for patients with high functional demands or those prioritizing early rehabilitation.Keywords: Boxer's fractures, Metacarpal neck fractures, Ilizarov external mini-fixator, Modified application

Background

Metacarpal neck fractures are among the most common hand injuries, accounting for approximately 20% of all hand fractures [1, 2]. Among these fractures, fifth metacarpal neck fractures are the most prevalent, primarily affecting young males and often resulting from direct trauma, such as punching or ground impact, hence the term “boxer’s fracture.” Failure to properly reduce and stabilize these fractures can lead to significant functional impairment and aesthetic deformities of the hand.

Most boxer’s fractures can be effectively managed with non-surgical interventions, such as manual reduction followed by rigid fixation with plaster casts or splints, or soft fixation using buddy taping or functional bandages [3–7]. However, fractures characterized by severe angulation, shortening deformities, or significant rotational displacement require surgical intervention. Common surgical techniques for treating these fractures include retrograde crossed Kirschner wires (KW) fixation, antegrade intramedullary K-wire fixation, intramedullary screw fixation, plate and screw fixation, and external fixation, with each offering distinct advantages and disadvantages. For instance, retrograde crossed K-wire fixation is simple and minimally invasive but offers limited stability. Plate fixation provides solid support but requires open reduction and capsule stripping, which potentially results in significant tissue trauma [8, 9]. Intramedullary K-wire or screw fixation offers a balance between minimal invasiveness and stability but may lead to complications such as skin and tendon irritation [10].Linear external fixation devices are challenging to apply in boxer’s fractures, primarily due to the small distal fracture fragment and the relatively large diameter of the screws.

The Ilizarov external mini-fixator (Fig. 1) has been widely adopted for managing fractures and bone defects of the hand and foot, demonstrating promising clinical outcomes [11–13]. The standard technique involves inserting three to four K-wires perpendicular to the bone’s long axis, positioned at both the proximal and distal segment of the fracture and secured to an external frame. However, in boxer’s fractures, the distal segment of the fracture is the metacarpal head, limiting sufficient space for inserting the three to four parallel K-wires. To address this limitation, we modified the conventional Ilizarov external mini-fixator by altering the position and orientation of the K-wires. The closed reduction and external fixation technique, which was previously challenging to apply in boxer’s fractures, was successfully implemented for the treatment of this fracture type, yielding favorable outcomes.

This study comprehensively analyzed clinical outcomes in patients with boxer’s fractures treated with the modified Ilizarov external mini-fixation (IEF) compared to conventional surgical methods, specifically crossed K-wires and mini locking plates (LP). The analysis aimed to provide a nuanced and objective assessment of the clinical efficacy and applicability of the modified IEF method in boxer’s fractures.

Methods

General data

This retrospective study, approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the 960th Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army (Approval No. 2023-032), included 45 patients with boxer’s fractures treated surgically at the institution between January 2018 and December 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants and legal guardians of minors. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: ① patients with isolated closed boxer’s fractures; ② angulation deformity greater than 30°, shortening exceeding 5 mm, or presence of rotational deformity; ③ fresh fractures that occurred within 7 days after injury; and ④ absence of other underlying health conditions. Exclusion criteria included: ① multiple metacarpal or phalangeal fractures; ② fractures associated with open skin or soft tissue injuries; ③ old fractures; and ④ comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or coronary heart disease. Patients were categorized into three groups based on the surgical method employed: the IEF group, comprising 13 patients (12 males and 1 female) treated with the modified Ilizarov technique; the KW group, consisting of 21 patients who underwent percutaneous crossed KW fixation; and the LP group, comprising of 11 patients treated with open reduction and internal fixation using the mini locking plate. Modified application of IEF

The standard approach for treating metacarpal and phalangeal fractures with an Ilizarov external mini-fixator involves inserting 3–4 K-wires perpendicular to the bone’s long axis at both the proximal and distal segment of the fracture. The wire tails are bent dorsally along the transverse plane of the limb and secured to the external fixator. However, in boxer’s fractures, the relatively small distal segment of the fracture limits the placement of the K-wires. Even if successfully inserted, the wires are often positioned too closely, preventing proper alignment with the fixator’s grooves. To address these challenges, we modified the conventional Ilizarov external mini-fixator to optimize its application in boxer’s fractures. This modification involved adjusting the position and orientation of the K-wires, which yielded favorable outcomes. In this revised technique, two K-wires were first inserted in a retrograde, crossed orientation at the metacarpal head, with the tips traversing the fracture line to penetrate the proximal cortical bone on the opposite side, similar to the standard retrograde crossed K-wire fixation technique. Subsequently, 1–2 additional K-wires were inserted perpendicular to the metacarpal long axis (If there is sufficient space for the distal fracture fragment, two K-wires are inserted; otherwise, only one is inserted) between the first two crossed K-wires and the fracture line, ensuring the placement of at least three K-wires distal to the fracture line. The proximal K-wire insertion was performed according to standard. To secure the external fixator, the tails of the crossed K-wires were bent proximally along the coronal plane until they aligned parallel with the other K-wires. The K-wires are then bent dorsally and secured to the fixator frame (Fig. 2). This modified approach allows for effective stabilization and alignment of the boxer’s fracture while overcoming the spatial limitation of the distal segment.

Surgical procedure

IEF group

Surgical procedures (Fig. 3)were performed under brachial plexus anesthesia without the use of a tourniquet after standard sterilization of the affected upper limb. Traction was applied to the proximal phalanx of the little finger while flexing the metacarpophalangeal (MP) joint to 90°. To reduce fracture, dorsal pressure was applied to the metacarpal head from the palmar side and counterpressured using the thumb from the dorsal to the palmar side. Two 1.5 mm K-wires were percutaneously inserted in a retrograde, crossed orientation at the distal metacarpal head while maintaining the reduction. The wire tips traversed the fracture line and penetrated the contralateral proximal cortical bone. Following fluoroscopic confirmation of proper wire placement, 1–2 vertical 1.5 mm K-wires were inserted into the metacarpal head distal to the fracture line along the metacarpal axis. Three K-wires were then inserted into the proximal metacarpal base, alternating between both sides. Precautions were observed during wire insertion to avoid injury to the extensor tendons of the little finger. After fluoroscopic confirmation of proper wire placement, the two crossed K-wires were bent proximally in the coronal plane to align with the other wires. All wires were bent dorsally to fit into the grooves of the external fixator ring and secured in place. Excess wire ends were trimmed, and the insertion sites were dressed with gauze strips, completing the procedure.

Surgical procedure. A Manual reduction. B-D Kirschner wires inserted using the modified method. E-F Post-surgery appearance. G-J Radiographs of each procedural step

KW group

In the KW group, closed reduction was performed under brachial plexus anesthesia, also without the use of a tourniquet. Following successful reduction, two 1.5 mm KW- were percutaneously inserted in a retrograde, crossed configuration through the metacarpal head, using the same technique described in the IEF group. Optimal wire positioning and fracture alignment were confirmed fluoroscopically. The protruding ends of the KW were trimmed to approximately 0.5 cm above the skin surface, and sterile dressings were applied to the insertion sites.

LP group

In the LP group, a pneumatic tourniquet was applied to the upper arm following brachial plexus block. The limb was disinfected and draped. A longitudinal dorsal incision of 3–4 cm was made over the fifth metacarpal, extending proximally from the metacarpal head. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were dissected carefully to preserve the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve. The extensor tendon and joint capsule were incised longitudinally along the dorsoulnar side and retracted to expose the fracture site. Under direct visualization, the fracture fragments were reduced and temporarily stabilized with 1–2 K-wires. A T- or L-shaped mini LP was positioned on the dorsoulnar surface of the metacarpal bone, ensuring two screw holes were distal and three were proximal to the fracture line. Two locking screws were inserted into the distal metacarpal head and three into the proximal metacarpal shaft to ensure rigid fixation. Fluoroscopic imaging was used intraoperatively to confirm proper alignment and implant positioning. The surgical site was irrigated, and the incision was closed in layers.

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, patients in the IEF and LP groups did not require continuous immobilization with braces or plaster casts. However, these devices were recommended during specific situations, such as nighttime rest or crowded environments, to prevent accidental trauma to the operated hand.

In the IEF group, passive range-of-motion (ROM) exercises of the little finger, including flexion and extension, were initiated on the second postoperative day, progressing to active exercises after 1 week. Patients were advised to protect the affected limb and the external fixator from trauma and to prevent snagging of the fixator screws and K-wire ends on clothing. Daily pin-site disinfection was performed to minimize the risk of infection.

In the LP group, passive flexion and extension exercises of the little finger were initiated 3–5 days after the surgery, depending on the resolution of incision-related pain. Active ROM exercises commenced approximately 10 days postoperatively.

Patients in the KW group were instructed to wear protective braces continuously for at least 2 weeks postoperatively. After this period, braces were temporarily removed to allow passive flexion and extension exercises and reapplied immediately after exercise completion. At 4 weeks postoperatively, if clinical examination revealed no tenderness or percussion pain at the fracture site, the braces were permanently discontinued and active ROM exercises were gradually initiated. Pin-site disinfection was performed daily to reduce the risk of infections.

Radiographic follow-up, including anteroposterior and oblique X-ray of the affected hand, was performed every 2 weeks to assess fracture alignment and healing progress. Implants, including the external fixators in the IEF group and percutaneous K-wires in the KW group, were removed in the outpatient clinic upon radiographic confirmation of complete fracture union—defined by obliteration of the fracture line and absence of localized tenderness or percussion pain at the fracture site. Following device removal, patients continued with ROM and muscle-strengthening exercises. However, strenuous activities and heavy lifting were contraindicated for at least one month after implant removal. Subsequent follow-ups were conducted every 3 months to assess long-term functional recovery.

Efficacy evaluation and statistical analysis

The clinical efficacy of the three surgical methods was evaluated based on the following parameters: operative duration, MP joint ROM and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand(QDASH) score at 4 weeks postoperatively, fracture healing time, degree of residual angulation and shortening deformity at the time of fracture union, final MP joint ROM, time for patients to return to work, final QDASH score and postoperative complication rate. These data were obtained from patients’ medical records, follow-up imaging studies, and physical examination findings.

Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied if variables were normally distributed across all groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied if any group deviated from a normal distribution. For categorical variables, intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were conducted if significant differences were identified among groups. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics—including age, gender, mechanism of injury, and dominant hand involvement—showed no significant differences among the three groups. All patients completed the follow-up evaluations ranging from 12 to 24 months, with no significant differences in follow-up duration observed among groups. Preoperative angulation averaged 40.1° in the IEF group, 39.6° in the KW group, and 38.2° in the LP group. Preoperative shortening averaged 5.4 mm, 5.1 mm, and 4.8 mm in the IEF, KW, and LP groups, respectively. There were no significant differences in either angulation or shortening among the three groups (Table 1).

The KW group exhibited the shortest mean operative time (25.3 ± 1.0 min), which was significantly lesser than that of the IEF group (35.4 ± 3.1 min, P = 0.016) and the LP group (44.6 ± 2.3 min, P = 0.001).

At 4 weeks postoperatively, the IEF group achieved significantly greater MP joint ROM (70.4 ± 1.9°) than the KW group (51.2 ± 1.9°, P < 0.001) and the LP group (60.0 ± 2.2°, P = 0.044). At this time, the QDASH score of the IEF group was 10.1 ± 7.4, which was significantly lower than that of the KW group (23.1 ± 7.5, P < 0.001) and comparable to that of the LP group (11.9 ± 6.9, P = 1.000).

Return-to-work time was significantly shorter in the IEF group (4.5 ± 0.3 weeks) than in the KW group (7.1 ± 0.3 weeks, P < 0.001), and comparable to the LP group (5.6 ± 0.5 weeks, P = 0.326) (Table 2).

The above series of findings demonstrate that the IEF group exhibits a significant advantage in terms of early functional recovery.

All patients achieved complete fracture healing by the final follow-up, with an average healing time of approximately 6 weeks across all groups and no significant intergroup differences. Residual shortening was minimal (approximately 1 mm in all groups), with no significant intergroup differences. However, residual angular deformity was significantly greater in the KW group than in the IEF (P = 0.031) and LP (P = 0.048) groups, indicating superior maintenance of anatomical alignment with IEF and LP techniques.

At the final follow-up, the IEF group demonstrated the highest mean MP joint ROM (87.3 ± 2.3°)(Figs. 4 and 5), followed by the KW group (85.0 ± 1.6°) and the LP group (79.1 ± 2.5°). The IEF group showed significantly better ROM than the LP group (P = 0.002), while no significant difference was observed between the IEF and KW groups (P = 0.471). At this time, the mean QDASH scores of the respective groups were as follows: 2.3 in the IEF group, 3.2 in the KW group, and 2.9 in the LP group. All fractures healed completely without non-union(Figs. 4 and 5). In the IEF group, one patient developed a localized pin-site infection characterized by erythema and swelling, which resolved with local anti-infective treatment and improved pin-site care. Another patient experienced transient dorsoulnar sensory disturbances (paresthesia over the dorsoulnar palm and little finger), which gradually resolved after external fixator removal. Discussion

The optimal treatment strategy for boxer’s fractures remains a subject of ongoing debate, with various approaches tailored to the severity and characteristics of injury. While conservative management is generally suitable for minimally displaced fractures, surgical intervention is often required for cases with significant angular, shortening, or rotational deformities. Common surgical fixation techniques include K-wire fixation, intramedullary screw fixation, plate and screw fixation, and external fixator stabilization. Among these techniques, K-wire fixation is the most frequently employed for hand fractures, encompassing techniques such as retrograde crossed pinning, crisscross pinning, antegrade intramedullary pinning, and transverse pinning [14]. Crossed and transverse pinning methods are straightforward and widely applicable but provide limited fixation stability. Antegrade intramedullary pinning offers greater stability but has a steeper learning curve and carries risks such as articular surface damage and dorsal ulnar nerve branch injury [15]. Plate and screw system offers excellent stability but requires extensive soft tissue dissection, which increases the risk of joint stiffness and tendon injury [9, 16]. Consequently, with the development of minimally invasive techniques, the use of plates has gradually declined. In 2010, Boulton first introduced intramedullary headless compression screws for treating subcapital metacarpal fractures [17]. Subsequently, studies have reported favorable outcomes, including fracture healing and functional recovery with the use of these screws in the metacarpal neck and shaft fractures [18, 19]. However, this technique poses risks of articular cartilage and joint capsule injuries during screw placement.

External fixators have a long history of managing hand fractures [20, 21]. Initially indicated for open fractures or cases with bone defects, their application has expanded to include closed metacarpal and phalangeal fractures, with several studies reporting successful outcomes [22–24]. External fixators offer several advantages, including ease of application, minimal soft tissue damage, high stability, and facilitation of early functional rehabilitation. The fundamental principle involves inserting K-wires or screws into the proximal and distal fracture segments following reduction. Variations among external fixation techniques arise from differences in K-wire or screw connection and stabilization [24, 25].

In the context of boxer’s fractures, the small size of the distal bone fragment, combined with the relatively large diameter of conventional external fixator screws, render the safe placement of two or more screws infeasible. This limitation significantly restricts the clinical applicability of traditional straight external fixators for this specific fracture type.

The Ilizarov external mini-fixator is a specialized device designed for hand fractures. The standard technique involves inserting three K-wires perpendicular to the bone’s long axis at the proximal and distal segments of the fracture. These wires are bent dorsally in the horizontal plane to cross on the dorsal side of the hand and secured to the external fixator rings using slotted washers.

In boxer’s fractures, the conventional Ilizarov external mini-fixation technique presents two main challenges. First, fracture lines often occur at the metacarpal head-neck junction, where the distal bone segment is short (approximately 1 cm) and composed of cancellous bone, thus limiting the accommodation of 3–4 parallel K-wires. Even if successfully inserted, the wires are often too closely spaced and may not align with the slots on the external fixator’s washers, reducing holding power and compromising stability. Second, the proximity of the metacarpal head to the metacarpophalangeal joint makes it difficult to maintain reduction during distal wire placement. To address these challenges, we modified the external fixation technique. The first modification involved inserting two retrograde crossed K-wires into the distal fragment immediately after fracture reduction(Figs. 2A and 3B and G). This approach provided initial stabilization, maintained reduction during subsequent wire placement, and enhanced overall fixation stability. The entry points were positioned more distally to create more space at the metacarpal head for the insertion of additional wires. The second modification involved bending the tails of the crossed K-wires proximally in the coronal plane and dorsally in the horizontal plane(Fig. 2D and E). This adjustment ensured proper alignment and secure attachment of the wires to the fixator washer slots. The flexibility in wire positioning improved overall stability and addressed the spatial constraints in the distal segment.

The primary significance of this modification is to address the previously existing challenge of applying the external fixator in the management of boxer’s fractures. This improvement enables the smooth implementation of the Ilizarov external mini-fixator for the treatment of this fracture type, thereby fully leveraging its minimally invasive benefits while also providing sufficient stability. From a biomechanical perspective, the improved configuration first fixes the fracture ends with crossed K-wires, then inserts parallel K-wires, and finally fixes the crossed and parallel K-wires together on the external fixator. This is equivalent to double fixation of the fracture ends, and its inhibitory effect on the angular deformity of the distal bone fragment is stronger than that of the traditional Ilizarov external fixation configuration and simple K-wire fixation. Our previous, biomechanical studies demonstrated that the modified IEF provides greater fixation stability than both crossed and intramedullary KW fixation and is comparable to LP fixation [26].

In this study, the clinical efficacy of the modified IEF was assessed in comparison with conventional fixation methods, specifically KW and LP fixation.

Operative time for IEF was longer than that for KW fixation but comparable to LP fixation. This extended duration can be attributed to the technical complexity of the IEF procedure, which involves precise insertion of 6–7 KW and assembly of the external fixator frame. Nonetheless, the operative time remained within a clinically acceptable range of 30–60 min. While prolonged operative duration may be a concern in patients with severe trauma, in cases of isolated metacarpal fractures, this increased duration did not adversely affect clinical outcomes. On the contrary, the improved mechanical stability and early mobilization offered by IEF appear to justify the modest increase in operative time, particularly in patients with high functional and aesthetic demands. However, in cases of concomitant severe injuries, such as vascular or nerve damage, where time efficiency is critical, the benefits of IEF must be weighed against the potential risks associated with prolonged operative time.

Fracture healing time did not significantly differ among the three fixation methods. This finding aligns with existing evidence highlighting the high osteogenic potential of the metacarpal neck region, a richly vascularized metaphyseal area associated with low rates of non-union. Even without robust stabilization, most cases achieve bony union. Therefore, while IEF does not confer a distinct advantage in accelerating fracture union, its benefits become evident in preventing malunion. Compared to KW fixation, the modified IEF demonstrated outstanding performance in maintaining angular alignment and was comparable to LP fixation. The KW construct, due to its limited stability, is susceptible to secondary displacement during early mobilization, which can lead to residual angular deformities. In contrast, the enhanced stability of the IEP and LP fixation minimizes this complication, maintaining anatomical alignment throughout the healing process. These findings are consistent with previous biomechanical studies [27, 28].

For postoperative functional recovery, MP joint ROM at 4 weeks postoperatively averaged 70° in the IEF group, which was superior to that of the KW group (51.2°) and the LP group (60.0°). Patients undergoing K-wire fixation required post-operative brace immobilization and were unable to initiate early mobilization, resulting in suboptimal early functional recovery outcomes. In contrast, patients in the LP and IEF groups exhibited superior fixation stability, enabling early mobilization and functional rehabilitation, which contributed to more favorable overall recovery. However, the ROM in the LP group was slightly lower than that in the IEF group. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to the need for joint capsule incision or dissection during plate placement—within the first 4 weeks, the injured joint capsule had not fully healed, and while early mobilization was feasible, vigorous exercises could induce pain, leading to reduced exercise amplitude and consequently compromising recovery efficacy. In addition, scarring at the incision site and within the subcutaneous tissue, as well as potential tendon adhesions, may also contribute to suboptimal recovery of joint ROM.

While the joint ROM in the LP group was slightly lower at this time point, the QDASH scores of the LP group were comparable to those of the IEF group, with both groups demonstrating significantly superior outcomes to the KW group. Additionally, the time to return to work was comparable between the IEF and LP groups, and both were shorter than that in the KW group. These two findings collectively indicate that the IEF and LP groups exhibit advantages in early functional recovery. Two potential explanations may account for this observation: First, IEF fixation enables patients to initiate functional rehabilitation at an earlier stage, facilitating earlier recovery of the affected limb’s basic functions. Second, the superior stability provided by IEF fixation allows patients to engage in low-intensity work prior to complete fracture union—both of which are not feasible with K-wire fixation.

At the final follow-up, most patients in the IEF group achieved a fully normal MP joint ROM (87.3°) comparable to the KW group (85.0°) and superior to the LP group (79.1°). This favorable long-term outcome observed with IEF and KW fixation methods may be attributed to the minimally invasive (closed reduction) and percutaneous nature of both procedures, which minimizes damage to surrounding tendons and joint capsules. In contrast, plate placement necessitates an open reduction, involving dissection and retraction of tendons and joint capsules, increasing the risk of postoperative tendon adhesion and joint stiffness, and adversely affecting long-term joint function recovery [8, 29]. At the final follow-up, no significant difference was observed in QDASH scores across the three groups, suggesting that the three fixation modalities yield comparable long-term functional recovery outcomes.

The incidence of postoperative complications showed no significant differences among the three groups. However, the KP group exhibited significantly higher levels of postoperative residual deformity compared to the other two groups, with one case developing malunion. These findings suggest that the stability provided by KW fixation is significantly inferior to that of the other two fixation modalities. In the IEF group, the primary complications included pin tract infections and potential injury to adjacent tendons and nerves, largely due to K-wire placement. To mitigate these risks, careful palpation of the extensor tendons, such as the extensor digiti minimi and proper extensor of the little finger, is crucial to prevent damage during surgery. Furthermore, fluoroscopic guidance and pre-marked entry points during proximal metacarpal pin placement are vital to reduce repeated punctures and minimize the risk of dorsal ulnar nerve branch injury. While only one case of pin-tract infection was observed in the IEF group in our study, the potential risk of this complication warrants substantial attention. Should pin-tract infection remain inadequately controlled, it may compromise functional recovery outcomes and progress to severe complications such as osteomyelitis. Postoperative care should prioritize diligent pin-site hygiene to prevent infections. Another inherent limitation of the IEF method is the protrusion of the external fixator frame, which may interfere with daily activities and require patient education to avoid inadvertent impacts or friction with clothing.

This study has several limitations. First, this study is a retrospective one, merely summarizing the clinical application effects and lacking prospective controls, thus the level of clinical evidence is relatively low. Second, in a retrospective study, there are no clear standards for the selection of surgical methods, which may lead to certain selection biases. We have tried to minimize this bias by strictly and precisely defining the inclusion criteria for cases, but it cannot be completely eliminated. Third, the total sample size and the sample size of each group included in the study are relatively small, which reduces the statistical power to detect differences between groups. Fourth, while IEF was compared with conventional fixation methods—KW and LP—newer fixation modalities such as headless intramedullary compression screws or bioabsorbable implants were not included, as they were not in use at our institution during the study period, thus limiting the scope of the comparative analysis. These limitations underscore the need for future, larger-scale prospective studies to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of IEF over a broad spectrum of contemporary orthopedic interventions for boxer’s fractures.
