In vivo brain macromolecules in schizophrenia spectrum disorders
Authors: Joshua Chiappelli, Hongji Chen, Stephanie A. Korenic, Frank E. Gaston, Zhenyao Ye, Hwiyoung Lee, Shuo Chen, S. Andrea Wijtenburg, Laura M. Rowland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain macromolecule signals detected with proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) are often regarded as a nuisance, but may be potential markers of pathophysiological processes, such as inflammation, impaired glymphatic clearance, and damage to cellular membranes. To date, there is little published data on macromolecule levels in schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD). In this study, we used metabolite-nulled proton MRS to assess macromolecule signals in four brain regions of interest, the anterior cingulate cortex, striatum, hippocampus, and white matter, in 24 people with SSD and 22 healthy controls. Participants were also assessed for cognitive performance and blood levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), an inflammatory marker. There were significant differences in macromolecule levels between people with SSD and controls in the left hippocampus. There were no significant associations of macromolecule levels with cognition, symptom severity, or CRP levels. There were significant associations of macromolecule levels in the striatum with current antipsychotic dose in the SSD group, and macromolecule levels in the anterior cingulate with subjective sleep quality. Except for the slight reductions in hippocampus, these results suggest that macromolecules may not be notably different in SSD and may not significantly impact MRS metabolite measures collected with short TE sequences, although larger studies are needed to confirm.

Introduction

Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) has been a useful tool in identifying neurochemical clues towards the pathophysiology of schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD), including abnormalities in glutamate, glutamine, and GABA. However, a challenge for the interpretation of MRS data collected with <150 ms echo time (TE) sequences at 3 T1 is the presence of mobile macromolecules (MM), large molecular weight molecules with shorter transverse relaxation times (T2). MMs create broad signals that underlie and overlap with metabolite resonances, which complicate the reliable and accurate quantification of some metabolites. The characteristic pattern of MMs has been shown in animal and human work2–5, and the seven main peaks are commonly labeled by location along the ppm scale (e.g., MM09, MM12, MM14, and MM17) or designated M1–M7 (but see ref. 6 for MM peaks detected at a very high MR field strength). There is still uncertainty regarding the specific constituents of MMs, but preclinical and human brain tissue research identified macromolecules as being mobile cytosolic amino acid proteins (and possibly lipids) such as isoleucine, leucine, valine (M1), threonine, alanine (M2–M3), lysine, arginine (M4 and M7), glutamate, glutamine (M5–M6)2 (See supplemental Table 1).

MM signals are commonly regarded as nuisance signals, but they may be functionally significant. MM levels are higher in multiple sclerosis7, stroke8, tumors4,9, and aging10 in humans (but see11). One 4 T MRS study of drug naïve schizophrenia reported higher MM levels around 3.0 ppm corresponding to M7 in the left anterior cingulate assessed with a standard metabolite sequence and not a metabolite-nulled MM sequence12. Interestingly, administration of peripheral lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce inflammation causes an increase in M1 that corresponds with microglia activation/changes in healthy mice but not in a transgenic mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease13.

Changes in MM levels have also been hypothesized to reflect abnormal functioning of the glymphatic system, a system activated during sleep and thought to be responsible for removing waste products from the brain. One study reported that M3 (MM14) and M4 (MM17) were significantly elevated in individuals with normal pressure hydrocephalus, possibly reflecting poor clearance from the brain14. Another study reported no differences in M1 (MM09) and M5 (MM20) in the anterior cingulate cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and dorsal caudate in people with schizophrenia and healthy controls15. However, both studies used a standard metabolite sequence and not a metabolite-nulled MM sequence. A standard metabolite sequence detects metabolites that MMs overlaps with, whereas a metabolite-nulled MM sequence applies an inversion pulse that nulls the metabolite signals and results in an MM spectrum.

The purpose of this study was to examine MM levels in people with SSD and healthy controls in four regions known to be involved in the pathophysiology of SSD: the anterior cingulate cortex (AC), left hippocampus (LHP), striatum (STR), and white matter (WM), using a very short TE, metabolite-nulled sequence. We tested the hypotheses that MM levels are higher in people with SSD versus healthy controls and related to clinical and cognitive measures. These hypotheses were based on research showing possible neuroinflammation in schizophrenia16,17. In addition, we explored potential associations of MM levels with a peripheral inflammatory marker, C-reactive protein (CRP), and subjective sleep quality.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the University of Maryland, Baltimore, Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to study enrollment. Patients with SSD (including schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder) were recruited from the outpatient clinics of the Maryland Psychiatric Research Center and nearby mental health clinics and were clinically stable outpatients with no evidence of symptom exacerbation or change in medication within the last month prior to participating in the study. Healthy control participants were recruited through local media advertisements. Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 was used to confirm or exclude psychiatric diagnoses. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological conditions, head trauma with cognitive sequelae, active and uncontrolled medical conditions, or taking anti-inflammatory drugs on a regular basis (more than twice a week in the last month). Participants with substance abuse and dependence other than nicotine in the three months prior to the study were excluded. Participants who smoked refrained from smoking one hour prior to study participation to minimize acute nicotine effects. Females were scanned during the first half of the menstrual cycle when applicable. Chlorpromazine dose equivalent (CPZ) was calculated for participants with SSD antipsychotic medication regimen at the time of the study18.

Clinical and cognitive measures

Overall psychiatric symptoms and negative symptoms were assessed with the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)19 and the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS)20. General cognitive function was assessed using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB)21. Functional capacity was measured using the UCSD Performance-Based Skills Assessment (UPSA-2)22. Subjective sleep quality was assessed with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)23, with higher total composite scores indicating poorer subjective sleep quality. Blood was drawn to assess high-sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (CRP) levels through a standard medical laboratory.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy

MR scanning was completed using a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner equipped with a 64-channel head/neck coil. Structural MP-RAGE images aligned along the AC-PC line were used for prescribing the spectroscopic voxel in four regions: anterior cingulate (AC), centrum semiovale white matter (WM), striatum (STR), and left hippocampus (LHP). MRS data were acquired using a very short TE metabolite nulled STEAM sequence: TR/TM/TE = 2000/10/6.5 ms, AC VOI ~ 9 cm3 (3 × 2 × 1.5 cm), WM VOI ~ 7 cm3 (3 × 1.5 × 1.5 cm), STR VOI ~ 8 cm3 (2 × 2 × 2 cm), and LHP VOI ~ 4.5 cm3 (2.5 × 1.5 × 1.2 cm), AC NEX = 128, WM NEX = 192, STR NEX = 256, LHP NEX = 320, 2.5 kHz spectral width, 2048 complex points, and RF phases: φ1 = 135°, φ2 = 22.5°, φ13 = 112.5°, φADC = 0°24. The RF phases were alternated in this phase rotation scheme in order to remove signals from unwanted coherence pathways from contaminating the final spectrum. A water reference (NEX = 16) was acquired for phase and eddy current correction. FASTESTMAP was used to shim. A TR of 2000ms was selected, similar to ref. 25 to minimize the time that a participant was in the scanner while delivering sufficient SNR in regionally specific voxels. First, spectra were acquired in 4 volunteers using the very short TE metabolite nulled STEAM sequence described previously with NEX = 64 across a range of inversion pulse delay times (TIs) from 500 to 800 ms in 100 ms intervals, similar to prior published 3 T macromolecule studies25,26. Each spectrum was visually inspected to determine at which TI the metabolite peaks transition from inverted to erect. This transition appeared to occur between 500 and 700 ms, so additional data were acquired in 25 ms intervals from 500 ms to 700 ms, and the NEX remained the same. This same strategy was used for all four regions. The order in which the metabolite null spectra were acquired varied between participants, and the total scan time for each participant was approximately one hour and 45 min. Overall, results indicated that the optimal TI was 600 ms in all four regions. Each macromolecule spectrum from each region was processed using TARQUIN 4.3.1027. TARQUIN analyzes data in the time domain using a non-linear least-squares Levenberg-Marquardt approach. There were no soft constraints on the 7 peaks contained in the basis set. Soft constraints on the amplitude were used in fitting to avoid over-parameterization of fitting27. Preprocessing steps included water removal, phasing, and 2 Hz Gaussian line broadening28. A basis set consisting of Gaussian peaks corresponding to macromolecule resonances at 0.9 (M1), 1.2 (M2), 1.4 (M3), 1.7 (M4), 2.0 (M5), 2.3 (M6), and 3.0 (M7) ppm was imported into TARQUIN for fitting. All spectra and MM fits were visually inspected, and those of poor quality were excluded. In addition, spectra were excluded if FWHM > 0.15 ppm for water, or fit quality, Q, was <1 or >3 as determined by TARQUIN. Q is the standard deviation of the fit residual (between 0.5 and 4 ppm) divided by the standard deviation of the noise region as determined by TARQUIN. In-house code based on Gasparovic et al.29 was used to calculate the MM levels using water as a reference corrected for the proportion of gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within each spectroscopic voxel using SPM12 and relaxation effects with MM T1 and T2 selected from Behar et al.2. See supplement for more details on MM quantification. MM levels are reported as M1–M7 similar to refs. 2,25 and in institutional units similar to ref. 25.

Statistics

Group differences on demographic variables were tested with t-tests or chi-square tests. We used co-expression network multivariate regression (CoReg)30 to assess group differences in MMs and applied the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). This method accounts for dependencies among high-throughput outcome variables as traditional linear mixed-effects models for repeated measures in lower dimension cases, given the clear block-wise correlation pattern among the MMs across the four regions. Missing MM values were imputed within each region to preserve this correlation structure and improve statistical power. We applied the high-dimensional multiple imputation (HIMA31) approach to perform multiple imputation (m = 5) and then averaged the results across the imputed datasets. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted between MMs and the MCCB total composite scores, BPRS (total, positive, and negative scores), SANS, PSQI, CPZ, and C-reactive protein. For each measure within each region, p-values across the 7 MM levels were corrected using the BH procedure to control the FDR.

Results

Participant characteristics

Twenty-four people with SSD (including schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder) and 22 healthy controls aged 20–57 years old participated in the project. There were no significant differences between groups for age (t = 1.02, p = 0.32), number of smokers (χ2 = 0.92, p = 0.33), or sex (χ2 = 1.34, p = 0.25). Healthy controls had greater number of years of education (t = 2.6, p = 0.014), higher general cognitive function, MCCB total scores (t = 3.4, p < 0.001), functional capacity, UPSA total scores (t = 3.1, p = 0.002), and better subjective sleep quality (PSQI total score; t = 2.9, p = 0.003) compared to people with SSD. Participant characteristics.

In the SSD cohort, there were 2 people not taking antipsychotics at the time of the study, 4 people taking a first-generation antipsychotic, and 17 people taking a second-generation antipsychotic. One person could not provide information about the type of medication they were taking, and 5 people could not recall the dose of their antipsychotic medications; therefore, CPZ units were not calculated for these people. Nine people with SSD were taking an antidepressant (SSRI or NDRI). The illness duration, based on the age of first psychosis symptoms, ranged from 3.5 to 37.5 years with an average of 15.1 years. Illness duration could not be determined for 3 people with SSD because they could not remember the age at which they first experienced psychotic symptoms.

Macromolecule differences

AC MM spectra from one healthy control and LHP MM spectra from another healthy control were of poor quality and excluded from the analysis. There were no significant differences between groups for FWHM of water, Q, or voxel tissue proportions (see supplemental Table 2). For the AC region, 99.1% of MM fits were good; M2, M3, and M7 from one control had poor fits and were excluded. For the WM region, 94.7% of the MM fits were good; M1 from one SSD, M2 from three controls and two SSD, M3 from three controls and two SSD, M4 from two controls and one SSD, M6 from one SSD, and M7 from two SSD were excluded. For the STR region, 93.2% of MM fits were good; M2 from three controls and one SSD, M3 from three controls and three SSD, M4 from three controls and two SSD, M5 from one control, and M7 from one control and five SSD were excluded. For the LHP, 91.8% of MM were good; M2 from seven SSD, M3 from two controls and two SSD, M4 from one control, M6 from one control, and M7 from five controls and eight SSD were excluded.

CoReg results showed significant differences for the LHP M1 (MM09) (t = -4.88, p = 0.0005), LHP M5 (MM20) (t = −3.51, p = 0.0166), and LHP M6 (MM23) (t = −3.02, p = 0.0425) such that healthy controls had higher levels compared to SSD. The other LHP MMs were higher in healthy controls (all p’s < 0.05, uncorrected) but did not pass the BH FDR threshold. No other significant differences in MMs for other regions were found. Macromolecules and clinical correlations

Cognition, sleep, and CRP

There were no significant correlations between MMs and cognitive and functional measures after BH FDR correction. Subjective sleep quality assessed with the PSQI total composite score was related to M1 (r = −0.43, p = 0.009), M2 (r = −0.46, p = 0.009), M4 (r = −0.43, p = 0.009), M5 (r = −0.42, p = 0.009), M6 (r = −0.34, p = 0.033), and M7 (r = −0.43, p = 0.009) in the AC.

There were no significant associations between CRP and MM levels for any region after correction for multiple comparisons.

Psychiatric symptom severity and CPZ units

There were no significant correlations between MMs and psychiatric total and positive symptom severity (BPRS) or negative symptom severity (SANS), after BH FDR correction. CPZ units were related to M5 (r = -0.57, p = 0.049) and M7 (r = −0.64, p = 0.049) in the STR.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first project investigating brain MM levels in SSD compared to controls with a metabolite-nulled spectroscopic technique. This study found that MM levels in SSD compared to healthy controls were comparable in the anterior cingulate, striatum, and white matter brain regions. In contrast, there was an overall decrease in MM levels in the left hippocampus in SSD compared to healthy controls, and this was most pronounced in M1, M5, and M6. M1 is thought to be composed of branched-chain amino acids, leucine, isoleucine, and valine2 and a small amount of lipids. While the exact meaning of lower left hippocampal M1 levels is unclear, leucine, isoleucine, and valine are involved in metabolism and neurotransmitter cycling, both of which are likely altered in SSD32. M5 and M6 are noted in the literature as reflecting protein-bound glutamate and glutamine, and lower levels could reflect a reduction in cytosolic, structural proteins consistent with post-mortem hippocampus studies of SSD33,34. The pattern of overall lower MMs in the hippocampus in SSD may reflect lower cytosolic protein levels6 which may correspond to microstructural alterations like fewer synapses, lower cellular density, or number observed in the illness35,36.

The implications of the M1 finding for spectroscopic studies of the hippocampus in SSD are negligible since there are no major metabolites that resonate at 0.9 ppm. The other left hippocampal MMs (M2–M7) were lower in SSD but to a lesser degree, and given that the MM signal is composed of short T2 species, this may have some implications for <150 ms TE26 3 T spectroscopic studies of the hippocampus in SSD because M5-M6 underlie glutamate and glutamine and M7 underlies GABA. This hypothetically could be a confound in studies reporting lower levels of these metabolites in chronic SSD37,38; although, it is likely to be minor given the small effects noted in this study. Nevertheless, special attention is necessary to account for MM resonances in either acquisition or metabolite fitting methods.

Our AC and STR results are consistent with a prior report that found MM levels were not significantly abnormal in the AC or STR in people with SSD compared to controls15, but not a study of first episode, drug naïve schizophrenia that reported higher AC M712. It is important to note that there are significant methodological differences between the two studies that include different spectroscopic sequences to quantify MM levels (conventional 20 ms TE STEAM versus 6.5 ms TE metabolite nulled sequence), voxel size and location (left AC 1.5 cm3 voxel versus bilateral AC 9 cm3 voxel), and quantification software (Levenberg-Marquardt minimization algorithm versus TARQUIN). Theberge et al.12 also studied a different patient population, first episode, antipsychotic drug naïve, in contrast to the current study, which largely included people with chronic illness and antipsychotic medication exposure.

There were significant correlations observed between MMs and some measures. The more robust patterns were observed with higher AC M1, M2, M4, M5, M6 levels related to better subjective sleep quality and higher STR M5, M7 levels related to lower antipsychotic medication levels. Since higher MMs in both regions were associated with better outcomes, it is reasonable that higher MMs may reflect higher protein levels corresponding to higher microstructural tissue integrity in this sample. The brain regions that were specifically related to sleep quality and antipsychotic dose are supported by research showing a relationship between sleep disruption and anterior cingulate structural and functional alterations39,40 and the direct relationship between antipsychotic dose, dopamine D2 blockade, and striatal tissue changes41,42. There were no significant correlations that emerged with LHP MM levels despite the differences in LHP MM levels between people with SSD and healthy controls. Contrary to our hypothesis, CRP, psychiatric symptoms severity, and cognitive performance were not significantly related to any MM levels in any of the four brain regions, suggesting that MM may have limited utility as a clinical biomarker in schizophrenia.

There are limitations and considerations regarding this project. The modest sample size limits statistical power to detect small differences, and therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Also, most of the patients included in this study were taking psychotropic medications, which could have impacted results. As this was a cross-sectional study, we are also not able to comment on whether MM levels change across the illness course. Larger and longitudinal studies are needed to determine how MM levels change with illness duration and medication exposure. Metabolite residual, although minimal, was not removed and therefore must be regarded as a limitation. Future macromolecule studies could benefit from estimating T1s for individual macromolecules, computing the average T1 across macromolecules, and then determining the optimal TI and TR for metabolite nulling. The exact biological significance of the macromolecule background signal remains unclear; thus, the results of the study should be interpreted with caution, and more studies are warranted.

In conclusion, this study did not find notable differences between people with SSD and healthy controls in levels of MM in the AC, WM, and STR. Thus, macromolecule signals are unlikely to be a confounding factor in other MRS studies of these brain regions in chronic schizophrenia, although larger studies are needed to confirm these findings. Nevertheless, slight reductions in LHP MM levels suggest that researchers should be cautious when interpreting hippocampal spectra from sequences with TEs shorter than 150 ms at 3 T26 without accounting for MM in fitting.
