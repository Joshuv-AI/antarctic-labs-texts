Trientine Tetrahydrochloride, From Bench to Bedside: A Narrative Review
Authors: C. Omar F. Kamlin, Timothy M. Jenkins, Jamie L Heise, Naseem S. Amin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Trientine tetrahydrochloride (TETA-4HCl, Cuvrior®) is a copper chelating agent with the active moiety triethylenetetramine (trientine), developed by Orphalan, Inc. to address the unmet needs in the treatment of Wilson disease. The journey from bench to bedside builds upon the documented safety profile of trientine hydrochloride capsules developed initially to meet the needs of individuals intolerant to d-penicillamine (DPA). Trientine hydrochloride capsules are inherently unstable requiring strict cold chain storage conditions from production, transportation, and use at home by the patient. Trientine tetrahydrochloride has a distinctive, patent-protected unique polymorphic form, which permits the production at scale of film-coated scored tablets deemed room temperature stable for 36 months. Trientine tetrahydrochloride is supported by a well-characterized pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic, and metabolic profile demonstrating reliable and predictable dose linearity and dose proportionality kinetics. Trientine tetrahydrochloride is the only trientine formulation that has been compared with DPA in a prospective randomized clinical trial, demonstrating non-inferiority to DPA in adults with stable Wilson disease. On 28 April, 2022, the US Food and Drug Administration approved TETA-4HCl for use in adult patients with Wilson disease who are de-coppered and tolerant to DPA. Health authorities in multiple countries worldwide have approved TETA-4HCl for the treatment of adults and children aged 5 years or more who are intolerant to DPA including the European Union, UK, Saudi Arabia, Switzerland, Colombia, Australia, New Zealand, and China. This article aims to provide a comprehensive narrative review of the key milestones in the development of TETA-4HCl.

Introduction

Wilson disease (WD) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of copper transport and metabolism caused by defects in the ATP7B gene, which encodes for a copper-transporting ATPase [1]. With an estimated prevalence of fewer than 15,000 cases annually in the USA, WD is classified as an orphan disease. The progressive nature of the disorder underscores the importance of early diagnosis and minimizing delays in initiating treatment. Copper accumulation in the hepatocytes impacts not only hepatic function but it can also result in free (non-ceruloplasmin bound) copper overspilling into the circulation from the liver and affecting other tissues and organs [2]. Excessive intracellular hepatic copper produces reactive oxygen species initiating the injury cascade mediated via inflammatory pathways, iron accumulation, cuproptosis, and ferroptosis [3]. As the disease has an asymptomatic phase, and early symptoms are often non-specific, timely diagnosis may be challenging. Without therapeutic intervention, WD can lead to complications, including liver disease, central nervous system dysfunction, disability, and premature death [2, 4, 5]. Appropriate timing of diagnosis and management of WD are thus critical to mitigate the impact of the disease on affected individuals.

In 1951, Denny-Brown and Porter described the successful treatment of the chelating agent British anti-Lewisite (2,3-dimercaptopropanol) in the treatment of WD. These early results transformed the treatment of the disorder and catalyzed a fundamental shift in the field of neurology, from a descriptive to an interventional discipline, shaping the way we understand and manage neurological disorders to this day [6].

Prior to the approval of trientine tetrahydrochloride (TETA-4HCl) in the USA, approved therapies for WD included d-penicillamine (DPA), trientine hydrochloride (trientine-2HCl), and zinc salts. d-penicillamine, a copper chelator, was the first oral therapy approved (1956) for WD and has been considered the predominant initial therapy for symptomatic patients [1, 4]. The frequency and severity of both short-term and long-term adverse events with DPA exposure can lead to treatment discontinuation in approximately 30% of patients [7–10]. Trientine dihydrochloride (trientine-2HCl) was approved in 1969 and is widely used in the treatment of WD. Trientine hydrochloride is a copper chelator indicated in the treatment of WD in individuals who are intolerant to penicillamine. The storage requirements of many trientine-2HCl formulations are derived from formulation instability. This chemical instability is driven by moisture, exposure to oxygen, and ambient temperature and contribute to product degradation, including conversion to a less soluble trientine dihydrochloride dihydrate form [11], necessitating refrigeration and the need for storage in airtight glass containers, all of which can limit portability and reduce convenience and adherence to therapy [12, 13].

Zinc acetate was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1997 for the treatment of WD. Zinc acetate and other zinc salts act differently than chelators, by inducing metallothionein and blocking the absorption of copper in the intestinal tract. Zinc preparations may have a slower onset of action than chelators, frequent (three times daily) dosing schedules, and limited systemic chelation effects [14]. The amount of elemental zinc varies between prescription and over-the-counter products and may lead to dosing challenges. While zinc products are generally well tolerated in adults, gastrointestinal side effects may prompt the discontinuation of therapy [15].

Despite the availability of effective treatments for this rare condition, life-long treatment adherence for those living with WD is challenging with one in three patients exhibiting low adherence, especially in asymptomatic individuals [13]. This low adherence is associated with signs of disease progression (i.e., elevated liver enzymes and higher free copper levels, compared with patients who display moderate or high levels of adherence) [13]. The impact of non-adherence can potentially lead to long-term suboptimal efficacy, risking irreversible disease progression, lower quality of life, and increased healthcare utilization [2, 12, 16]. The challenges with current therapies described above underscore the need for improved therapeutic options to address the progressive nature of WD and enhance patient outcomes.

In the search for a more stable crystalline structure of triethylenetetramine, TETA-4HCl was developed to address room temperature stability and adherence challenges associated with trientine dihydrochloride [13]. The molecular structure of trientine has four amine groups that can bind hydrochloride. The stability challenges associated with trientine-2HCl result from the susceptibility of its two unbound amine groups to oxidative reactions. These amine groups are sensitive to oxygen, water, temperature, and humidity, which can lead to product degradation over time. Moreover, interactions between these reactive amine groups and excipients may trigger the Maillard reaction, further exacerbating stability issues. To overcome these stability challenges, the development of TETA-4HCl included the addition of two further hydrochloride groups to the molecule, so that all four amines are bonded to the hydrochloride moiety (Fig. 1). This modification effectively neutralized all the reactive amine groups present in trientine and trientine-2HCl, resulting in a more stable molecule. Using Raman spectroscopy, it was possible to ascertain the most thermodynamically stable crystal structure of the active ingredient [17]. This thermodynamically stable polymorphic form (B) further enhances its stability under various storage conditions [17]. Trientine tetrahydrochloride represents a significant advancement in the treatment landscape for WD. The development of a twice-daily galenic formulation is a major contribution to patient care. Trientine tetrahydrochloride was approved by the European Medicines Agency in 2017 with supporting clinical data of switching DPA-intolerant patients from trientine-2HCl to TETA-4HCl and pharmacokinetic data demonstrating greater bioavailability compared with a European reference trientine-2HCl [18]. The approval in Europe/UK is indicated for the treatment of WD in adults, adolescents, and children aged ≥5 years intolerant to DPA therapy. In the USA, the first prospective trial comparing two chelators for the treatment of WD, the CHELATE trial, demonstrated that TETA-4HCl is non-inferior to DPA. In a population not previously studied, adults who were de-coppered and tolerant to DPA were included. The two different populations were approved in the European Union/Europe and the USA.

Pharmacodynamics

Trientine tetrahydrochloride is the first drug in its class with an extensive and well-characterized pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic, and metabolism profile. Trientine is a selective copper chelator with a dual mechanism of action [20]. As a systemic copper chelator, trientine eliminates absorbed copper from the body by forming a stable complex that is then eliminated through urinary excretion. In addition to its systemic effects, trientine has a direct effect within the intestinal tract through the inhibition of copper absorption, thereby reducing copper transport in the portal vein and uptake in the liver [21]. Trientine inhibits intestinal uptake of 64Cu by at least 50% while DPA has little to no effect on the intestinal uptake of 64Cu [20].

The primary pharmacodynamic action of trientine has been demonstrated in patients with WD by measuring free serum copper and urinary excretion of copper [22]. Excretion of copper in the urine is highest following initiation of chelation therapy in drug-naïve patients who require removal of the build-up of copper that has occurred in their organs and tissues and decreases as the patients move into the maintenance phase of disease control.

Pharmacodynamic profiles and modeling of trientine are available from two studies in healthy volunteers reported in the literature. Copper was excreted at the highest rate in the 0-h to 2-h interval following dosing, and the excretion decreased in each subsequent 2-h interval [23]. The mean 0-h to 4-h cumulative urine copper excretion accounted for approximately 72% of the mean total 24-h urine copper excretion. A pharmacodynamic analysis showed no significant differences in copper excretion in the urine between the fast and slow acetylation phenotypes [24].

In the phase III CHELATE study, serum non-ceruloplasmin-bound copper (NCC) and 24-h urinary copper excretion (UCE) were measured in adult patients with WD who were de-coppered and who were randomly switched from DPA to TETA-4HCl. The mean change in serum NCC in patients switched to TETA-4HCl was comparable to the mean serum NCC in patients who remained on DPA. After 24 weeks, the mean difference in the serum NCC by speciation (NCC-Sp) assay (see below for a description of the NCC-Sp assay) between the penicillamine group and TETA-4HCl group was −9.1 μg/L (95% confidence interval [CI] −24.2, 6.1), with the lower limit of the 95% CI within the pre-defined non-inferiority margin [18]. The mean UCE was significantly lower with TETA-4HCl than with penicillamine at 24 weeks from baseline [237.5 μg/24 h (99% CI 115.6, 359.4)]. Importantly, the within-subject variability of UCE was lower with TETA-4HCl than DPA at both 24 and 48 weeks. The differences in UCE between the two chelators reflect the differences in bioavailability and the inhibition of copper in the intestine by TETA-4HCl [20].

Pharmacokinetics

Absorption and Distribution

The phase I TRIUMPH study (EudraCT 2015-002199-25) [18] and TRIUMPH-2 study (NCT06128954) [25] together demonstrated dose linearity and dose proportionality kinetics for TETA-4HCl across three dose ranges of trientine base (450, 600, and 750 mg) in healthy volunteers compared to the listed drug. These findings confer predictability during dose adjustments or during dose titration. Trientine tetrahydrochloride contains the same active moiety as trientine-2HCl, but in a different salt form. The pharmacokinetics of the crystalline form B of TETA-4HCl are driven by two main factors: the actual galenic formulation affecting the dissolution profile and the absorption and bioavailability properties. Trientine formulations are Biopharmaceutical Classification System Class III drugs that exhibit high solubility and poor absorption. Lu [26] reported that trientine is poorly absorbed with a bioavailability of 8–30% and can be improved by faster dissolution rates. Dissolution tests at pH 1.2, pH 4.0, and pH 6.8 demonstrated that galenic TETA-4HCl tablets dissolved more rapidly than trientine-2HCl capsules [25]. Trientine tetrahydrochloride tablets dissolved in approximately 15 min versus 120 min for trientine-2HCl capsules, or approximately eight times faster. Additionally, these data support a lower oral dose of TETA-4HCl on a mg/mg basis (by a factor of 0.6) to achieve similar systemic trientine exposure [18]. Trientine is widely distributed in tissues with relatively high concentrations measured in the liver, heart, and kidney.

Clinical and non-clinical reports on the pharmacokinetics of trientine consistently show it has a low oral bioavailability with a fast first-pass metabolism and high inter-individual variability. Major differences can be seen in the pharmacokinetic profile of identical forms of trientine-2HCl with a 60% difference in bioavailability [18].

The phase I TRIUMPH study conducted in healthy subjects demonstrated that the median time to reach maximum plasma concentration was 2 h for TETA-4HCl and 3 h for trientine-2HCl following oral administration. The rate (maximum plasma concentration) and extent (area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity) of absorption of the active moiety, trientine, were greater for TETA-4HCl than for trientine-2HCl by approximately 68% and 56%, respectively. Compared with an identical dose of a trientine-2HCl formulation, TETA-4HCl provided more rapid absorption of trientine and greater systemic exposure in healthy subjects [18].

A population pharmacokinetic analysis indicated that following a single oral dose of 900 mg of TETA-4HCl, healthy male volunteers demonstrated higher systemic exposure of TETA-4HCl, but lower mono-acetylated and di-acetylated metabolite levels compared with female volunteers. Male volunteers appeared to have higher systemic exposures of trientine (approximately 30% and 50% higher for maximum plasma concentration and area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity, respectively) following a single oral dose of 1500 mg. This difference is not considered clinically meaningful. No sex differences in the terminal half-life were observed [18]. Trientine tetrahydrochloride is not metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes; therefore, drug–drug interactions between drugs that are substrates, inducers, or inhibitors of cytochrome P450 enzymes are unlikely.

Metabolism and Excretion

Trientine structurally resembles the naturally occurring polyamines (spermidine and spermine), in contrast to DPA, which is chemically similar to the amino acid cysteine [18]. Because of the close structural similarity to spermidine, trientine is likely to have a predictable profile of metabolism and excretion. The mean terminal half-life of trientine ranged from 13.8 to 16.5 h. Trientine is metabolized via acetylation, and two major acetylated metabolites exist in human serum and urine: N(1)-acetyl triethylenetetramine and N(1),N(10)-diacetyl triethylenetetramine. Mean t1/2 was 17.1 ± 9.22 and 14.7 ± 8.39 h for N(1)-acetyl triethylenetetramine and N(1),N(10)-diacetyl triethylenetetramine, respectively. Within 24 h, trientine is rapidly cleared from the kidney, with no accumulation. Trientine is mainly excreted in urine as the unchanged parent drug and the two acetylated metabolites [26].

Therapeutic Efficacy

Phase III CHELATE Study

For decades, the prevailing opinion held by physicians has been that DPA was more potent than trientine-2HCl in the treatment of WD, a perception based on higher levels of UCE in patients previously exposed to DPA [22]. These observations were largely unchallenged because of the difficulties in measuring free copper and fecal copper [27]. However, DPA has a conditional boxed warning label approval in the USA because of potential serious adverse reactions and clinical reports of up to 30% of patients experiencing adverse reactions necessitating discontinuation. In contrast, trientine has a better safety profile and is non-immunogenic. A non-inferiority trial comparing TETA-4HCl with DPA, if confirmed, would provide physicians and patients with an evidence-based alternative to DPA as continuation of first-line treatment, post de-coppering, and prior to the development of adverse events secondary to DPA therapy.

Trientine tetrahydrochloride was approved by the European Medicines Agency in 2017 for the indication of “Wilson disease patients intolerant to d-penicillamine”. For US approval, the sponsor sought approval for a new indication; patients who have been de-coppered, are clinically stable, and tolerant to DPA. The primary intention of this indication was to address an unmet need, providing an alternative to DPA before clinical intolerance was recognized, as some known toxicities of the skin (i.e., elastosis perforans) may develop insidiously. By demonstrating non-inferiority for this population, TETA-4HCl provides an approved alternative therapy.

The CHELATE trial (NCT03539952), a multicenter, randomized, open-label clinical trial, compared TETA-4HCl with DPA in stable patients with WD. The primary hypothesis and endpoint were that the efficacy of TETA-4HCl was non-inferior to penicillamine, as measured using the NCC-Sp assay developed by Orphalan Inc. at 24 weeks post-randomization. The assay was developed and validated [28] at the request of the FDA after limitations of an exchangeable copper assay were identified during the study [28, 29]. The secondary composite efficacy endpoint was NCC and 24-h UCE.

The study enrolled 53 adult subjects (aged 18–75 years) with clinically stable WD, with a Leipzig score ≥4 at diagnosis. The study population was treated with DPA monotherapy for at least 1 year with no dose adjustments in the 6 months prior to screening. In addition, specific inclusion criteria, including laboratory measures of serum NCC levels ≥ 25 and ≤ 150 mcg/L, 24-h UCE between ≥ 100 and ≤ 900 mcg/24 h, and liver function tests (alanine aminotransferase < 2 times the upper limit of normal) defined clinical stability alongside assessments made by an independent adjudication committee of three WD experts who would later be blinded to treatment allocation post-randomization. After 24 weeks, the mean difference in the serum NCC-Sp assay between the penicillamine group and the TETA-4HCl group was −9.1 μg/L (95% CI −24.2, 6.1), with the lower limit of the 95% CI within the defined non-inferiority margin, meeting the primary endpoint of the study (Fig. 3). At the end of the extension period (48 weeks), TETA-4HCl remained non-inferior to penicillamine (mean difference NCC −15.5 μg/L [95% CI –34.5, 3.6]). Urinary copper excretion at 48 weeks remained in the expected range for well-treated patients in both study groups, and the mean difference (124.8 μg/24 h [99% CI –37.6, 287.1]) was not significantly different [30].

The CHELATE study is the first prospective head-to-head trial comparing any trientine formulation with DPA. Results demonstrated that TETA-4HCl is non-inferior to DPA as measured by the NCC using a newly developed methodology [28] of copper protein speciation using high-performance liquid chromatography inductively coupled mass spectroscopy assay (NCC-Sp). Trientine tetrahydrochloride was well tolerated and during treatment, more patients achieved the pre-specified composite endpoint of NCC and 24-h UCE within therapeutic target ranges, compared with patients treated with DPA, 50% vs 24%, respectively. Neurological, cognitive, and hepatic assessments confirmed clinical stability over a 48-week period. The independent adjudication committee, blinded to site and treatment allocation, confirmed all enrolled subjects remained clinically stable throughout the 48-week post randomization period.

In summary, in a first of its type of a direct head-to head comparison of two chelators for the treatment of WD, the CHELATE trial demonstrated that TETA-4HCl is non-inferior to DPA. Although trientine-2HCl was first approved in 1985, the breakthrough development program for TETA-4HCl produced the first scored tablet form that could be split, and in contrast to trientine-2HCl, was the first room temperature-stable trientine. Trientine tetrahydrochloride addressed one of the biggest challenges in supply and stability, recognized as important factors affecting patient adherence to therapy. Most trientine-2HCl formulations require storage at 4 °C to mitigate product degradation. In contrast to room temperature stability for TETA-4HCl, trientine-2HCl rapidly converts to a dihydrate form at room temperature, with normal humidity (e.g., relative humidity; 40%). The dihydrate form reduces solubility by 50% compared with the original trientine-2HCl, which may negatively impact the absorption and efficacy of trientine-2HCl in patients [11].

Development of the NCC-Sp Assay

During the early conduct of the phase III CHELATE trial, the NCC exchangeable assay resulted in an underestimation of NCC lacking robust precision and accuracy to be used for assessment of copper load as a primary efficacy endpoint. Recognizing this limitation, Orphalan Inc., in consultation with the FDA, developed an alternative two-step method to determine NCC.

The NCC-Sp assay is a validated, liquid chromatography inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy method [28]. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy is used to quantify total serum copper. The second part utilizes liquid chromatography to separate all the copper-bound proteins; the largest peak is ceruloplasmin-bound copper, followed by albumin and alpha-2 macroglobulin peaks. The area under each peak quantifies the proportion of bound copper to each protein. Non-ceruloplasmin-bound copper by speciation is then calculated by subtracting ceruloplasmin bound copper from total serum copper [28].

The accurate and precise measurement of NCC has the potential to be an important biomarker for WD; assessing copper toxicity and monitoring the efficacy of treatment in WD. Traditional diagnostic assays for NCC have been unreliable, leading to challenges in accurate measurements of copper load. It is estimated that 20% of patients with WD will have a negative estimated NCC because of ceruloplasmin overestimation by an immunoassay [31]. Importantly, the acceptance of the NCC-Sp assay by the FDA as the primary endpoint in the CHELATE trial underscores the reliability, precision, and accuracy of the assay. In the accompanying editorial to CHELATE trial, Medici and Heffern state, “This method [NCC-Sp] has promise to provide reliable and reproducible quantification due to its direct read-out of copper components excluding those bound to ceruloplasmin” [32]. This tool holds promise for providing clinicians in the future with a reliable assay for assessing treatment response and guiding therapeutic decisions [30].

Tolerability of TETA-4HCl

The CHELATE trial provides robust evidence supporting the safe switch from DPA to TETA-4HCl without compromising efficacy in the treatment of WD.

Adverse Events

TETA-4HCl builds upon the trusted safety profile of prior trientine formulations. In the TRIUMPH study, a single identical oral dose of trientine-2HCl and TETA-4HCl demonstrated that both trientine formulations were safe and well tolerated. Nineteen subjects (73.1%) [11 subjects during trientine-2HCl exposure and 12 subjects during TETA-4HCl exposure] reported at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE). Overall, gastrointestinal events were reported in nine (34.6%) subjects (four subjects during trientine-2HCl exposure and five subjects during TETA-4HCl exposure) and headache reported in five (15.4%) subjects (four subjects during trientine-2HCl exposure and one subject during TETA-4HCl exposure). All TEAEs were mild and resolved during the study. No subjects discontinued the study because of a TEAE, and no serious adverse events occurred during the study [18].

The CHELATE trial also reported TETA-4HCl as safe and well tolerated with no serious adverse events compared to DPA, which allows clinicians to safely switch patients from DPA without compromising efficacy. Treatment-emergent adverse events temporally related to the study treatments were comparable in both groups and were graded as either mild or moderate and transitory in nature. Treatment-emergent adverse events, as judged by the treating physician in the post-randomization period, included 17/27 (63%) in the DPA group and 14/26 (54%) in the TETA-4HCl group. The most common TEAEs were headache (19%) and abdominal pain (3.7%) with DPA versus 8% and 15% with TETA-4HCl. One patient developed a rash with TETA-4HCl that resolved on discontinuation of therapy [30]. Similarly, findings from the phase I TRIUMPH-2 trial reported ten healthy subjects (43%) reported at least one TEAE considered to be related to TETA-4HCl by the study investigator. The most common TEAE reported was headache. All TEAEs were mild and resolved during the study. No subjects discontinued the study because of a TEAE, and no serious AEs occurred during the study. No clinically significant findings for laboratory safety values or vital sign data were reported [25]. The safety and effectiveness of TETA-4HCl in pediatric patients have not been established.

Dosage and Administration of TETA-4HCl

Trientine tetrahydrochloride is formulated as a film-coated scored tablet, containing 300 mg of TETA-4HCl (150 mg of trientine base) for oral use and dispensed in a convenient, double-aluminum foil blister pack. Trientine is not a fixed-dose therapy. The recommended starting total daily dosage of TETA-4HCl in adult stable patients with WD is 300 mg (150 mg of trientine base) up to 3000 mg (1500 mg of trientine base) taken orally in divided doses (two times daily). Trientine tetrahydrochloride is not substitutable on a milligram-per-milligram basis with other trientine products because of the different content of trientine base in the unit dose. The TRIUMPH-1 pharmacokinetic study demonstrated considerable inter-subject variability in absorption (time to reach maximum plasma concentration, maximum plasma concentration, half-life, and area under the plasma concentration–time curve). Starting dose and optimization by titration will thus need to be personalized and likely to differ between subjects. When compared to the European reference product, data from TRIUMPH-1 demonstrated that TETA-4HCl was not bioequivalent and, at a population level, the mean equivalent starting dose of TETA-4HCl would approximate to 0.6 of the reference trientine-2HCl formulation. However, dosing decisions are ultimately the decision of the treating physician and should align with the marketing authorization holder’s package insert.

It is important to avoid concomitant use of mineral supplements (e.g., iron, zinc, calcium, magnesium) as these metal cations may chelate trientine in the gut and diminish its absorption and systemic effect. There is also a significant food effect, and patients should be counseled to administer TETA-4HCl on an empty stomach, at least 1 h before meals or 2 h after meals and at least 1 h apart from any other food or milk. Patients should be instructed not to remove tablets from the blister pack until just before dosing and to swallow tablets without crushing, chewing, or dissolving. For patients who have difficulty swallowing the tablet whole, the scored tablet can be divided into two equal halves. Trientine tetrahydrochloride is stable for 36 months at room temperature.

Ongoing Clinical Trials

The iWilson Registry (NCT05239858) is active and recruiting individuals, aged 12 years and older with WD, to describe the natural history of treated WD and to explore the clinical phenotype, geographic, ethnic, and gender influences and the choice of treatment on the course of the condition. As of July 2024, 385 patients from Europe, UK, and Saudi Arabia have been enrolled into this prospective real-world study.

Monitoring treatment and disease progression is challenging because of methodological limitations in the measurement of free or labile copper that is not bound to ceruloplasmin (NCC). Indirect estimation using ceruloplasmin measurements is inaccurate and unreliable [31]. The NCC-Sp assay developed by Orphalan Inc. was used to determine the primary endpoint of the CHELATE trial [30]. The clinical utility of this assay is being evaluated in a real-world evidence study recruiting 50 subjects aged ≥ 18 years with WD, who are assessed as unstable and requiring a change in therapy from their current treatment (e.g., DPA, any trientine, or zinc salt). The primary objective is to determine the clinical utility of this new NCC-Sp assay in the identification and monitoring of an unstable WD population. The REASON study (NCT05783687) has an estimated expected completion date of December 2025. Data will be collected over an approximate 12-month period.

Prospectively collected data from therapeutic trials in the pediatric population in WD are lacking. Orphalan Inc. is in consultation with the FDA to explore the use of TETA-4HCl in pediatric WD. Conclusions

Wilson disease is a rare disorder of copper transport and metabolism. Despite the availability of effective treatments for this rare condition, life-long adherence for WD is challenging and significant unmet needs prompted the development of TETA-4HCl to provide a stable trientine formulation. The unique development and crystallization process protects all amine groups on the trientine structure allowing a coated tablet formulation that can be stored at ambient temperature and does not require an airtight container. Randomized clinical trial evidence confirms that TETA-4HCl provides effective well-tolerated therapy with twice-daily dosing. Trientine tetrahydrochloride is the first new trientine formulation approval for this rare disorder in a quarter century from the FDA on 28 April, 2022, for adult patients with WD who are de-coppered and tolerant to DPA. Furthermore, orphan drug exclusivity has been granted in the USA. The NCC-Sp assay is currently only available as a research-based tool.
