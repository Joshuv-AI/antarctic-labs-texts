Factors Associated With Maternal Health Care Facilities Utilization in the Slum Communities of Kathmandu Valley, Nepal: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Bijaya Mani Devkota, Tantrika Raj Khanal, Bhakti Prasad Subedi, Indira Pant, Samjhana Subedi, Mohan Kumar Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMaternal health remains a serious concern in urban low‐resource settings where healthcare disparities persist. Women residing in slums in Nepal face multiple obstacles that delay access to health facility‐based delivery services, contributing to high maternal mortality. This study aimed to assess socioeconomic and demographic factors influencing the utilization of health facilities for delivery among women residing in slum communities within the Kathmandu Valley, guided by Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Utilization.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted from November 27, 2023, to November 27, 2024. Data were collected using a two‐stage cluster sampling technique, selecting 580 households across 29 slum sites. The study population included 1042 married women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who had given birth within the last 5 years and were interviewed using a structured questionnaire. Descriptive analysis, χ 2 tests, and binary logistic regression were performed; an a priori significance level was set at α = 0.05 for two‐sided tests.ResultsAmong the 1042 participants, 77.0% (n = 802) delivered in Health facilities. Younger women aged 15–34 years had higher health delivery rates (79.7%–83.1%) than older women aged 35–49 years, among whom 52.1% delivered at home. In terms of education, 86.8% of women with higher education were delivered in health facilities compared with 66.1% of women with no formal education (p = 0.06). Caste/ethnicity showed significant disparities: Brahmin/Chhetri women had the highest delivery rate at health facilities (93.3%), followed by Madhesi women (91.7%), while Dalit women had the lowest rate (49.2%, p < 0.001). In the multivariate logistic regression, Dalit women had significantly lower odds of health facility delivery (odds ratio [OR] = 0.051, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.012–0.218, p < 0.001), while home ownership (OR = 3.401, 95% CI 1.525–7.584, p = 0.003) and residence in Kathmandu district (OR = 2.700, 95% CI 1.138–6.409, p = 0.024) were associated with higher odds of health facility delivery.ConclusionMaternal health disparities in slum communities require culturally sensitive interventions, financial incentives, and improvements to healthcare infrastructure. Addressing caste‐based discrimination, improving housing security, and implementing district‐specific strategies are essential to reduce maternal and neonatal health risks in Nepal's urban slums. Multisectoral collaborations involving health, housing, and social welfare sectors will be most effective.

Introduction

Maternal health is essential for evaluating overall health and national development. To lower maternal and neonatal mortality rates, as documented [1], women must use safe delivery services. In rapidly urbanizing areas like the Kathmandu Valley in Nepal, slum areas, also known as “informal settlements,” have created unique challenges for healthcare delivery [2]. Informal settlement areas often lack basic infrastructure, including health facilities, making it essential to assess the factors that determine utilization of Health Facility (HF) delivery services in such areas [3].

HF delivery service utilization refers to a pregnant woman's ability to access and use institutional healthcare settings—hospitals, clinics, or maternity centers—to deliver her baby [4]. Institutional delivery has the beneficial effect of improving maternal and neonatal outcomes through the presence of skilled birth attendants and emergency obstetric care [5]. However, in slums, various socioeconomic, cultural, and systemic factors may act as barriers to the use of such facilities [6].

This study is guided by Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Utilization [7], which conceptualizes healthcare‐seeking behavior as a function of three domains: (1) predisposing factors (age, education, caste/ethnicity, and religion), (2) enabling factors (home ownership, household income, and district of residence), and (3) need factors (perceived and evaluated need for maternal care). This framework provides a structured approach to understanding why some women in slum communities utilize health facility delivery services while others do not, despite similar needs.

Although global efforts are promoting institutional deliveries, many women in slum areas continue giving birth at home without skilled assistance [8]. In the Kathmandu Valley, rapid urbanization has expanded slum communities, which face inadequate healthcare infrastructure, financial constraints, and various sociocultural barriers [9]. These conditions lead to highly compromised maternal health and high mortality risks [10]. Understanding the factors that impede and promote the utilization of HF delivery services in these settings is essential for designing targeted interventions [11].

Compared with the relatively well‐documented maternal health literature for rural Nepal, evidence on urban health among slum populations, especially regarding service utilization, remains limited. For maternal health in rural Nepal, information on urban health among slum populations, especially regarding service utilization, remains limited [3]. The existing literature shows that slum residents face various challenges, including insecure housing, denial of legal recognition, and social exclusion [12]. Health policies and programs aimed at benefiting poor urban communities should focus on this population [2]. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the current utilization rate of HF delivery services among women in the slums of the Kathmandu Valley and to identify predisposing and enabling factors associated with utilization, as framed by Andersen's Behavioral Model.

Urban slums are characterized by overcrowding, poor sanitation, and limited access to health services, particularly maternal health services [5]. Women in these settings face high risks during pregnancy and childbirth. A study conducted in informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya, found that maternal mortality rates were significantly higher compared with nonslum urban areas, with the main contributing factors including hemorrhage, sepsis, and eclampsia [13]. Major determinants of such outcomes include poverty, limited access to quality health services, and sociocultural practices [11].

There has been a significant improvement in maternal health status in Nepal over the past few decades. However, disparities continue, especially for the most marginalized populations [1]. Nepal's National Health Mission has increased institutional delivery through initiatives like the Maternity Incentive Scheme, which provides financial incentives to pregnant women [12]. Programs like these have improved institutional delivery rates, but in urban slum settings, they still encounter significant resistance from residents due to a lack of awareness or difficulties in accessing these arrangements [2].

Barriers to using HF delivery services in slum areas include direct costs (such as fees and transportation) and opportunity costs, which deter women from seeking institutional care [6]. Women in slum areas have traditionally believed that giving birth in HF is time‐consuming and even financially draining. Moreover, the positive influence of family members and trust in Traditional Birth Attendants (TBA) play a significant role [5]. Poor perception of quality of care, unfriendly attitudes of healthcare service providers, and lack of culturally sensitive services are barriers that prevent vulnerable populations from accessing these services [4]. Lack of transportation networks and limited information about available health services are additional factors that deter utilization [3].

Conversely, factors that enhance utilization of HF delivery services include higher maternal education and awareness of the benefits of delivering in a HF, which encourage women to use these services [2]. The involvement of community health workers and local organizations has demonstrated positive effects on maternal healthcare [1]. Cash conditional transfer programs, such as the Maternity Incentive Scheme in Nepal, have been effective tools for ensuring institutional delivery [12]. Urban health resource centers in other countries have implemented community‐determined programs focusing on proactive health outreach, behavior change communication, and infrastructure improvements in slum areas [9]. Similarly, the Abiye project in Nigeria adopted a comprehensive approach to enhance maternal health, resulting in a significant reduction in the maternal mortality rates [8]. These case studies tend to underscore the importance of context‐specific, community‐based interventions to improve maternal health outcomes in poor urban settings [3].

In Nepal, various programs within the National Health Mission (NHM) are working to improve maternal health status. These programs aim to protect mothers and children from maternal and newborn mortality and morbidity through active referral mechanisms, particularly for pregnant women from low‐income households requiring institutional delivery [10]. However, further assessment is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of such programs in the urban slum areas of the Kathmandu Valley [2].

Materials and Methods

The study period was from November 27, 2023, to November 27, 2024, focusing on targeted slum areas of the Kathmandu Valley for female respondents of reproductive age (15–49 years) who had given birth within the last 5 years. A two‐stage cluster sampling design was employed to ensure representativeness, minimize sampling error, and support statistical inference [14].

Research Design

An explanatory cross‐sectional design was applied to identify factors affecting the use of maternal healthcare facilities in slum settlements of the Kathmandu Valley, following contrast guidelines for cross‐sectional studies. The study population comprised married women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who had given birth within the 5 years preceding the survey. The two‐stage cluster sampling method helped achieve adequate representation. Specifically, 29 wards were selected using Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) based on household counts, followed by systematic random sampling of 20 households per ward for a target sample of 580 households; however, all eligible women within each household were interviewed, resulting in 1042 participants.

Sample Size Determination

Sample size was calculated using the standard formula for cross‐sectional studies. n≥z2α/2p(1−p)d2, where Zα/2 is the standard normal variate,

p is the proportion of the event in the population, and

d is the desired levle of precision.

Using a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), an assumed population proportion (p) of 0.050 (to maximize sample size and account for the lack of prior slum‐specific estimates), and a desired precision (d) of 0.05, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 384 households. To account for nonsampling errors and ensure a minimum response rate of 90%, we increased the target to 580 households. Using PPS, 29 settlements were selected, yielding 580 households. The unit of analysis was married women of reproductive age, and all eligible women (n = 1042) within sampled households were included to ensure representative data on maternal healthcare practices [7].

Variables

Outcome variables: HF delivery was defined as delivery occurring in a hospital, clinic, or maternity center attended by skilled health personnel (doctor, nurse, or midwife). The variable was coded as a binary variable (1 = health facility delivery, 0 = home delivery). Explanatory variables: based on Andersen's Behavior Model, the following predisposing and enabling factors were included: (1) predisposing factors; Age (categorized as 15–24, 25–34, and 35–49 years), level of education (no education, basic [grades 1–5], lower secondary [grades 6–8], secondary [grades 9–10], higher [grades 11+]), religion (Hindu, Buddhist, and other), and caste/ethnicity (Brahmin/Chhetri, Janajati, Dalit, Madhesi, and Muslim) and (2) Enabling factors; Home ownership (yes/no) and district of residence (Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur).

Data Collection and Analysis

A structured questionnaire was developed based on validated instruments from the Nepal Demographic and Health Surveys and previous slum health studies. The questionnaire was translated into Nepali, back translated to ensure accuracy, and pretested in a nonsampled slum area (n = 30) to assess clarity and cultural appropriateness. Trained enumerators conducted face‐to‐face interviews in private settings within participants' homes. Each interview lasted approximately 35–45 min.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were computed for all variables [15]. Bivariate associations between each explanatory variable and health facility delivery were assessed using Pearson's χ 2 tests. A p < 0.05 (two‐sided) was considered statistically significant in bivariate analyses. Binary logistic regression was used for multivariate analysis because the outcome variable (health facility delivery) is binary. This approach estimates the probability of health facility delivery while simultaneously adjusting for multiple predictors and produces odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as interpretable effect sizes. All variables with p < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. The final model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test (p = 0.32, indicating adequate fit) [16]. An a priori significance level of α = 0.05 (two‐sided) was set for the multivariate analysis.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Board (ERB) of Tribhuvan University, Nepal (ERBTU‐080‐21). Verbal informed consent was obtained from each participant aged 16 years or older. For participants under 16 years of age (none were enrolled, as the study focused on married women of reproductive age, all of whom were ≥ 16 years), guardians' assent would have been obtained. Consent was documented by research team members in written records immediately following each interview, including the date, time, participant code, and the researcher's name. A second research team member was present during each consent process as a witness, and their name and confirmation of verbal consent were recorded. This procedure for obtaining and documenting verbal consent was reviewed and approved by the ERB, which determined that verbal consent was appropriate given the minimal risk nature of the research and the cultural context in which written consent might have posed a barrier to participation. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Results

The result section is presented in two subthemes: The first theme explains the distribution of health facility delivery by participants' characteristics. In which the overall health facility delivery rate was 77.0%. The second theme illustrates binary logistic regression, which identifies factors independently associated with health facility delivery while adjusting for potential confounders. The overall model was statistically significant (likelihood‐ratio χ 2 = 83.88, df = 10, p < 0.001), indicating the independent variables collectively predicted health facility delivery. The pseudo‐R 2 value was 0.275, suggesting that the model explained approximately 27.5% of the variation in health facility delivery. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test yielded p = 0.32, indicating adequate model fit.

Discussion

This study examined factors associated with health facility delivery among women in slum communities of the Kathmandu Valley, guided by Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Utilization. The findings reveal that health facility delivery utilization (77.7%) varies significantly across sociodemographic, economic, and structural factors that affect women's healthcare‐seeking behavior in marginalized urban settings. Consistent with Andersen's framework, both predisposing factors (age and caste/ethnicity) and enabling factors (home ownership and geographic location) were significant determinants of health facility delivery.

Age proved to be a significant predisposing factor. Women aged 35–49 years were significantly less likely to give birth in a HF than younger women (OR = 0.159, p = 0.001). This finding aligns with Andersen's model, which posits that age influences healthcare utilization through generational differences in health beliefs and exposure to health promotion messages. This observation aligns with the global literature: younger women are more likely to opt for institutional childbirth because they have greater exposure to health promotion and antenatal care services than older women [17]. Older women may prefer traditional home birth practices that have historically been the norm, preventing HF utilization [18]. This trend suggests the need for age‐targeted interventions to promote HF delivery services for women of all ages, particularly older women who may have lower health literacy.

Caste and ethnicity, as predisposing factors, showed stark disparities. Dalit women had very low odds of facility‐based delivery compared with upper‐caste women (OR = 0.052, p < 0.001), while Janajati women also had lower odds (OR = 0.270, p = 0.076). Within Andersen's framework, caste operates as a predisposing factor that shapes health beliefs, social networks, and exposure to discrimination. These findings align with evidence from South Asia, where marginalized communities consistently face barriers to accessing institutional maternal health services due to deep‐rooted social exclusion and discrimination [19, 20]. Addressing these inequalities requires targeted policy interventions, including caste‐sensitive service delivery and active community mobilization.

Home ownership emerged as one of the most significant enabling factors in this study. Women who owned their homes had 3.4 times the odds of delivering at a health facility compared with nonhomeowners (OR = 3.401, p = 0.003). In Andersen's framework, home ownership represents an enabling resource that facilitates access to care through economic stability and social capital. This finding indicates that home ownership confers a level of socioeconomic stability essential for seeking institutional care. Previous international studies have demonstrated a close relationship between housing security and healthcare access [21, 22]. In slum areas, home ownership often correlates with higher income and stability, thereby reducing financial and psychological barriers to accessing formal healthcare.

Education did not significantly predict institutional delivery in the multivariate analysis, although higher education showed a trend toward increased odds. While education is typically seen as a crucial enabling factor [23], its relative lack of significance here may stem from the compounded barriers slum women face, including affordability, mobility constraints, and distrust of public health systems. This finding suggests that education alone is insufficient to overcome infrastructural and socioeconomic barriers. It requires integration with other initiatives, such as making maternal care accessible, culturally sensitive, and coupled with economic support [24].

Religion was not significantly associated with delivery location in this study. While some global studies have shown that religious norms affect maternal healthcare utilization [25], the absence of a significant finding here may be because poverty and locality conditions largely override any influence of religious preference on decision‐making in slum areas, where socioeconomic survival takes precedence.

Geographic location (district of residence) also functioned as an enabling factor. Women in Kathmandu had significantly higher odds of delivering at a health facility than those in Bhaktapur (OR = 2.700, p = 0.024). This reflects geographic variation in the availability of enabling resources, including health infrastructure, transport networks, and outreach services, which are concentrated in the capital city. Studies have documented urban–rural and intraurban disparities in maternal health access, whereby those in major cities often benefit from concentrated resources [26]. However, this urban advantage is not evenly distributed, as slum areas within Kathmandu remain underserved despite their proximity to health institutions.

Conclusion

This study aimed to identify factors associated with HF delivery among women in the urban slums of Kathmandu Valley, Nepal, guided by Andersen's Behavioral Model of Health Services Utilization. These results show that institutional delivery rates are moderate (77.0%), and marginalized groups, particularly Dalit and Janajati women, face serious sociocultural and economic barriers to accessing these services. Consistent with Andersen's framework, predisposing factors (younger age, higher caste status) and enabling factors (home ownership, residence in Kathmandu district) were associated with higher odds of HF delivery, while education and religion were not significant predictors in the multivariate model. The findings highlight entrenched sociocultural inequalities that perpetuate unequal access to maternal health services. Policies should advocate for community‐oriented strategies that address caste discrimination and socioeconomic barriers, along with health education targeting older women who may prefer traditional birthing practices.

Home ownership was identified as a strong enabling factor for health facility utilization, reflecting the importance of economic stability and household resources in facilitating institutional childbirth. Geographic factors also played a role, with women in Kathmandu having better access to HF than those from Bhaktapur. These findings underscore the need for integrated maternal health strategies that extend beyond the health sector to include education, housing, and infrastructure reforms. A multisector response is needed to reduce maternal mortality and ensure that no woman is denied safe delivery services due to age, caste, education, or socioeconomic status.
