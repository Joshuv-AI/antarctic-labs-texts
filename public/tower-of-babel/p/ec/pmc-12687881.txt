DistRMI: a deep distance-aware neural network for explainable RNA loop motif-small molecule interaction prediction
Authors: Zhaoxiang Liu, Qiqi Zhu, Qingyan Tian, Lei Zhong, Yingxiang Deng, Dengguo Wei, Haitao Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA participates in the occurrence and development of various diseases by regulating gene expression. Owing to its potential to circumvent the limitations of traditional “undruggable” protein targets, it is regarded as a core direction for next-generation precision therapy. Against this backdrop, the accurate and interpretable prediction of RNA–small molecule interactions has become a key link in accelerating the discovery of RNA-targeted drugs. However, existing methods suffer from insufficient prediction accuracy and interpretability, failing to effectively guide lead compound screening or elucidate the mechanism of action. This study presents DistRMI, which integrates Transformers and graph neural networks to capture, respectively, the sequence information of RNA loop motifs and the chemical topological features of small molecules while introducing distance priors between them and leveraging a distance-aware attention mechanism to capture their interaction information. The results show that DistRMI outperforms baseline models, and its performance remains robust even when confronted with unknown RNA loop motifs and small molecules. Visualization of attention weights reveals that bases near the paired bases of RNA loop motifs contribute significantly. Furthermore, retrospective case studies validate the model’s reliability. Predicting the binding preferences between RNA loop motifs and small molecules while providing interpretability facilitates an in-depth understanding of RNA–small molecule interactions, promotes in-depth research on RNA and related drugs, and opens up new avenues for disease treatment.

Introduction

RNA, as a core molecule in cellular life activities, serves both as a faithful transmitter of genetic information and a precise regulator of gene expression [1–3]. It dynamically modulates protein abundance through its involvement in transcription and translation while forming complex interaction networks with DNA, other RNAs, and proteins, thereby exerting a profound impact on life processes [4, 5]. In the context of diseases, the role of RNA is particularly prominent. For instance, miR-21 and miR-155 are highly expressed in various cancers, driving tumor proliferation and metastasis by inhibiting tumor suppressor genes [6]. In breast cancer, the long noncoding RNA HOTAIR accelerates tumor spread through regulating gene expression and chromatin modification [7]. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, dysfunction of the TDP-43 protein disrupts the stability of RNA splicing, leading to neuronal death [8]. In fragile X syndrome, abnormal silencing of the FMR1 gene perturbs micro RNA regulation, resulting in neurodevelopmental defects and autistic phenotypes [9]. RNA viruses such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) hijack host RNA via reverse transcription or RdRp for efficient infection [10]. Furthermore, studies have shown that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim $\end{document}70%–90% of the human genome is transcribed into RNA, with <2% of this RNA encoding proteins [11, 12]. Compared with protein targets, RNA presents a richer landscape of therapeutic targets for disease intervention.

In recent years, RNA has rapidly emerged as a frontier target in small-molecule drug development. Owing to its unique ability to circumvent the bottlenecks associated with traditionally ”undruggable” proteins, RNA has come to the forefront in areas such as antiviral and anticancer research [13–15]. In the antiviral field, the Nekhai team was the first to identify the benzoxazole compound T0516-4834, which, through virtual screening and activity validation, precisely blocks the Tat–TAR RNA interaction of HIV-1, thereby inhibiting the source of viral transcription [16]. The Hargrove team, on the other hand, introduced amiloride derivatives into anti-SARS-CoV-2 research, confirming that targeting the viral 5’UTR region alone can exert broad-spectrum antiviral activity [17]. In the field of oncology, the Duca laboratory has focused on Dicer-mediated pre-miR-372, discovering that the small molecule PA-1 can occupy the cleavage site of Dicer, block the downregulation of the tumor suppressor gene LATS2, and thereby significantly inhibit cancer cell proliferation [18]. The Disney team went a step further, using RIBOTAC technology to conjugate 2’-5’ oligoadenylate with the miR-96 inhibitor TGP-96, achieving the programmed degradation of oncogenic pre-miR-96 and thereby inducing cancer cell apoptosis [19]. Furthermore, statistics from the Rbind database indicate that 98% of nucleotides at binding sites are located in highly flexible loop regions, such as hairpin, bulge, internal, and multibranch loops, as well as their adjacent areas, which, as ”trap” sites with open conformations and exposed bases, are key to the precise binding between small molecules and RNA [20]. In summary, RNA-targeting small molecules are reshaping the landscape of drug development with unprecedented breadth and depth, providing a new tool for overcoming intractable diseases.

The core bottleneck in RNA-targeted drug development lies in wet experiments, whose high costs and long cycles severely hinder the progress of this field [21]. To date, the FDA has approved only one small molecule drug, risdiplam, whose mechanism of action involves binding to the hairpin loop of SMN2 mRNA to regulate splicing, thereby treating spinal muscular atrophy [22, 23]. Traditional approaches for RNA–small molecule interaction prediction, such as molecular docking, have been widely used to estimate binding poses and affinities. However, recent benchmarking studies have revealed that the performance of current docking tools remains limited. For instance, Agarwal et al. [24] evaluated AutoDock4, AutoDock Vina, and rDock on 173 RNA–ligand complexes, reporting modest success rates even under favorable conditions. Similarly, Jiang et al. [25] systematically assessed eight docking programs on the largest RNA–ligand dataset to date and found that pose-prediction accuracy (27%–36%) and affinity correlations remained low. These results highlight the intrinsic challenges of RNA docking, including structural flexibility and the lack of accurate scoring functions.

To address this challenge, significant efforts have been made in developing data-driven computational tools, with existing research generally categorized into two directions: the prediction of RNA–small molecule interactions and the prediction of binding sites. In interaction prediction, the RNALigands [26] screens small molecules based on secondary structure motifs. The team led by Professor Disney developed Inforna [27, 28], which combines RNA secondary structure design with high-throughput screening to quickly identify motifs in target RNAs that are most prone to bind to small molecules and reversely infer potential drug structures. RNAmigos [29] and RNAmigos2 [30] construct an augmented base-pairing network using 3D RNA structures to predict small molecules that can bind. ZHMol-RLinter [31] employs a two-layer stacked random forest model to process interactions between RNA loop motifs and small molecules. In binding site prediction, Rsite [32] and Rsite2 [33] predict binding sites by calculating the Euclidean distance between nucleotides. RNAsite [34] integrates sequence conservation and spatial topological features, combining graph neural networks (GNNs) and convolutional networks to identify druggable sites in noncoding RNAs. RLBind [35] utilizes deep learning to mine hidden interaction patterns in RNA sequences and structures, locating small molecule binding hotspots. RNet [36] introduces a self-attention and reinforcement learning framework to dynamically capture long-range RNA interactions that affect small molecule binding. Recent studies have further incorporated machine learning and explainable artificial intelligence techniques into RNA–small molecule interaction research. Tools such as fingeRNAt and the experimentally derived ROBIN library utilize structural fingerprints and molecular descriptors to predict RNA-binding propensity and interpret model decisions, highlighting key nucleotide interactions and ligand features [37, 38]. In parallel, deep learning methods, including RLaffinity, exploit 3D structural information together with contrastive pretraining and 3D convolutional networks to capture both global pocket characteristics and local nucleotide environments, thereby improving binding affinity predictions [39]. Nevertheless, challenges remain, including dataset biases, normalization issues, and limited generalization to unseen RNA–ligand complexes [40]. Although these methods have achieved certain results, their effectiveness remains limited, with accuracy still needing improvement and a lack of transparent explanations for the “how” and “why” of decision-making.

This study presents DistRMI, a method for predicting RNA loop motif-small molecule interactions based on Transformers and GNNs, which not only predicts the binding between RNA loop motifs and small molecules with high accuracy and screens potential RNA-targeted small molecules, but also interprets and visualizes the model’s decision-making process. First, information on RNA loop motifs and small molecules is extracted from the PDB database to construct a dataset for their interactions, followed by learning sequence-based features of RNA loop motifs and features of small molecules, a process that captures their interaction information via an attention mechanism while introducing distance priors between them. Results demonstrate that DistRMI exhibits superior predictive performance compared with baseline models, even for challenging unknown RNA loop motifs and small molecules. The visualization of attention weights further reveals the key decision-making features on which the model relies when predicting RNA loop motif-small molecule interactions, providing intuitive and in-depth insights into understanding its underlying mechanism. We hope that DistRMI can accurately and effectively assist researchers in advancing the development of RNA-targeted drugs.

Materials and methods

Datasets

We curated data from the PDB database [41] to train DistRMI. Manual curation ensures that each entry contains both RNA and small molecule structures and that all entries are unique. Specifically, entries without associated small molecules were removed, and duplicate RNA–small molecule complexes were eliminated. We first screened 185 representative independent RNA chains from the PDB (Table S1), including various functional RNA types such as riboswitches, aptamers, viral RNAs, G-quadruplexes (G4s), ribozymes, and others (Figure S1). We then employed the MXfold2 algorithm [42] to predict their secondary structures and extract four types of loop motifs. We defined the interaction criterion as the shortest distance between any base in the motif and the small molecule being <4 Å [31, 43], which was regarded as a positive interaction, while pairs not satisfying this condition are treated as negative samples. We finally collected 479 RNA loop motifs, 8916 nucleotides, 148 small molecules, and 870 “RNA loop motif-small molecule” pairs, forming the RNA loop motif-small molecule interaction database (Table 1). This database contains 75 bulge loops, 190 hairpin loops, 143 internal loops, and 71 multibranch loops (Fig. 1A), where bulge loops are generally short in length with almost all being within 10 nt, hairpin loops are the most numerous with lengths concentrated in the 6–10 nt range, internal loops also mostly fall within this interval, and multibranch loops are the longest with almost all exceeding 10 nt. While the small molecules exhibit diverse chemotypes (Figure S2), their molecular weights (MWs) are predominantly concentrated in the range of 0–800 g/mol (Fig. 1B).

Overview of DistRMI

In this study, we propose a method for predicting the interactions between RNA loop motifs and small molecules. The task is formulated as a binary classification problem, determining whether a given RNA loop motif–small molecule pair interacts, rather than predicting the binding pose or binding pocket. This approach provides an effective computational framework for studying RNA–small molecule interactions. As illustrated in Fig. 2, DistRMI is designed to predict the interactions between RNA loop motifs and small molecules by integrating a Transformer-based sequence encoder with a graph convolutional network (GCN)-based molecular encoder. To achieve accurate and interpretable predictions, the framework is organized into four modules: (i) the loop motif encoder, which employs a Transformer to capture sequential dependencies and contextual representations of RNA loop motifs; (ii) the small molecule encoder, which utilizes a GCN to extract topological and chemical features of small molecules; (iii) the distance-aware fusion module, which aligns RNA and molecular representations by constraining the attention scores with nucleotide–molecule distances through a mean squared error (MSE) auxiliary loss, thereby enhancing biologically meaningful feature fusion; and (iv) the prediction module, which integrates the fused features and feeds them into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to estimate association scores between RNA loop motifs and small molecules.

Overview of the DistRMI framework, which consists of (1) a Transformer-based loop motifencoder, (2) a GCN-based small-molecule encoder, (3) a distance-aware fusion module integrating RNA and molecule features, (4) an MLP-based predictor trained with main and auxiliary distance losses, and (5) the overall workflow for RNA loop motif–small molecule interaction prediction.

Experiments and results

Experiment settings

To ensure consistent data distribution, more robust model evaluation, and more reliable generalization [56, 57], we employed a stratified sampling method where 20% of the instances are first selected as an independent test set and 80% as the training set, with 10% of the training set further designated as the validation set. Following the previous work [58–60], we perform five-fold cross-validation (5-CV) using stratified sampling for all evaluation scenarios to ensure balanced distributions of RNA loop motifs and small molecules across folds. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of DistRMI, we conduct four practical scenarios:

S1: Warm start. The training set and test set share the same distributions of RNA loop motifs and small molecules.

S1: Warm start. The training set and test set share the same distributions of RNA loop motifs and small molecules.

S2: Unseen RNA loop motifs. The test set contains only RNA loop motifs not seen in the training set.

S2: Unseen RNA loop motifs. The test set contains only RNA loop motifs not seen in the training set.

S3: Unseen small molecules. The test set contains only small molecules not present in the training set.

S3: Unseen small molecules. The test set contains only small molecules not present in the training set.

S4: All unseen. The test set contains RNA loop motifs and small molecules not present in the training set.

S4: All unseen. The test set contains RNA loop motifs and small molecules not present in the training set.

Performance evaluation

Following the previous work [61, 62], we employ AUC, AUPR, Precision, Accuracy, Recall, F1-Score, and Specificity to evaluate model performance. All metrics are calculated on a separate 20% test set held out during training to ensure an unbiased assessment. Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) and Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPR) are calculated using standard protocols in binary classification tasks. The evaluation metrics are calculated as follows:

Here, TP represents the number of samples that are actually binding and are correctly predicted as binding, reflecting the model’s correct identification ability for the positive class. FP represents the number of samples that are actually nonbinding but are incorrectly predicted as binding (i.e. false positives), measuring the model’s risk of misjudging the negative class. TN represents the number of samples that are actually nonbinding and are correctly predicted as nonbinding, reflecting the model’s correct identification ability for the negative class. FN represents the number of samples that are actually binding but are incorrectly predicted as nonbinding (i.e. false negatives), measuring the model’s risk of missing the positive class. Precision is the proportion of samples predicted as binding that are actually binding, reflecting the reliability of the prediction results (high precision = low false positive rate). Accuracy is the proportion of all samples that are correctly predicted, measuring the model’s overall classification ability. Recall is the proportion of truly binding samples that are correctly predicted, reflecting the model’s coverage ability for the positive class (high recall = low false negative rate). The F1-Score comprehensively evaluates precision and recall, balancing both performances (F1-Score is high only when both are high).

High interpretability of DistRMI

The attention mechanism of DistRMI is biologically interpretable and provides meaningful insights into the model’s decision-making process.

This section reveals the binding preferences between RNA loop motifs and small molecules through attention weight visualization: the bulge loop and internal loop each contain two pairs of paired bases, forming two high-weight trajectories in the heatmap respectively. The tail of the hairpin loop shows a single high-weight band. Although the multibranch loop contains three or more pairs of paired bases, it still concentrates on two main trajectories (Fig. 4A). Overall, all loop types exhibit significant weight aggregation at the end of the sequence (Figure S4A). In summary, paired bases and their adjacent regions, especially the tails of loop motifs, may play a decisive role in RNA loop motif–small molecule interactions.

RNA loop motif–small molecule attention scores for all correctly predicted samples, shown by loop type (bulge, hairpin, internal, and multibranch), with (A) contributions of nucleotide positions across motif lengths and (B) contributions of both nucleotide positions and types to prediction performance.

Next, we analyze the contribution of base types at different positions to RNA loop motif-small molecule binding. For the bulge loop, A bases exhibit significant contributions across various positions, with G and U bases predominantly concentrated at the sequence tail. In hairpin loops, A, G, and U bases show high contributions, where A and G bases are mainly concentrated in the sequence tail, while U bases are primarily concentrated in the middle region. Within internal loops, all four base types display high weight distributions at the sequence tail. For multibranch loops, A, C, and U bases contribute substantially at the sequence tail, with U bases mainly focused in the middle of the sequence (Fig. 4B). Overall, base contributions across all loop types are consistent with those of multibranch loops (Figure S4B). The result indicates that the binding preference between RNA loop motifs and small molecules is influenced by the different types of RNA loop motifs.

To further investigate model explainability from a different perspective, we evaluate the effect of random mutations in RNA sequences on prediction performance. Random mutations are introduced at varying levels (mutation rates from 0.1 to 0.9), and the resulting prediction accuracy, confidence changes, and flip rates are recorded. We also assess the robustness of the model to random mutations in RNA sequences. As shown in Table S11, the model remains largely robust to minor perturbations, with only slight decreases in accuracy at low mutation rates. Higher levels of mutation lead to substantial drops in prediction accuracy, indicating that the model is sensitive to significant sequence changes.

These findings not only deepen our understanding of the interaction mechanism between RNA loop motifs and small molecules but also provide interpretable structure-function clues for vaccine epitope optimization and the screening of RNA-targeted small molecules.

Utility of DistRMI demonstrated by case studies

DistRMI not only predicts molecular interactions but also provides interpretability of interaction mechanisms, which is not available in previous methods. We use MXfold2 to predict the secondary structure of RNA sequences and then extract the corresponding loop motif sequences to predict interactions with small molecules. To illustrate the effectiveness of our method, we test two RNA–ligand complexes from the test set, demonstrating its capability to predict and interpret interaction mechanisms. First, we conducted a case study using the co-crystal structure of a methyltransferase ribozyme with the small molecule GUN (PDB ID: 7V9E_A)(Fig. 5A). The predicted secondary structure of 7V9E_A contains two hairpin loops and one multibranch loop (Figure S5A). Prediction results indicate that multibranch loop motifs bind to GUN, while the other two loop motifs do not, which is consistent with experimental results. The presence of four binding sites among the highest 40% of base weights in the prediction results further validates the interpretability of the model.

Case studies for (A) the methyltransferase ribozyme (PDB ID: 7V9E A) and (B) the RNA aptamer (PDB ID: 5BJO Y) display the highlighted loop motifs with bound small molecules shown in stick representation, where specific nucleotides correctly identified as predicting loop motif–small molecule interactions are indicated as individually marked bases.

Another example is the co-crystal structure of an RNA aptamer with the small molecule 747 (PDB ID: 5BJO_Y) (Fig. 5B). In the predicted secondary structure, we identify a hairpin loop and two internal loops (Figure S5B). The model successfully predicts that the hairpin loop and the long internal loop bind to 747, while the short internal loop does not, which is consistent with experimental results. Three binding sites are found within the top 40% of base weights predicted by the model. We propose that the distribution of high base weights is derived from two aspects, one being the direct interaction with the molecules and the other being the maintenance of the structural conformation.

Conclusions

In summary, we propose an interpretable deep learning model that combines Transformers with GCN, enabling not only the prediction of RNA loop motif–small molecule interactions but also the exploration of interaction and decision-making mechanisms, thereby addressing the Lack of transparency in decision-making. Our method is evaluated on four datasets and demonstrates higher prediction accuracy compared with other models. This approach can be used for predicting molecular interactions without relying on crystal structures and provides insights into interaction mechanisms. It will contribute to the design of RNA-targeted inhibitors and play an important role in RNA engineering and medical applications.

Our future work will focus on developing a comprehensive computational framework for RNA-targeted drug discovery, where we plan to employ advanced multimodal deep learning architectures to investigate binding affinity landscapes between RNA sequences and small molecules. Furthermore, beyond predictive modeling, a key innovation will involve integrating a conditional generation module to design novel RNA-binding ligands under affinity constraints. By combining Transformer-based RNA representations that capture sequence and structural features with 3D-aware graph neural networks for small molecules, the system will predict interactions while generating chemically feasible compounds optimized for specific RNA targets.
