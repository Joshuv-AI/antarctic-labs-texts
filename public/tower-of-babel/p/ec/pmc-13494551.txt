DeepFaceMouse enables scalable prediction of large-scale brain activity from facial dynamics in mice
Authors: Kemal Ozdemirli, Tenesha Connor, Kaleb Kim, Ersan Alp Unlu, Macit Emre Lacin, Miguel Maldonado, Frederick Bell, Thiago Peixoto Leal, Caglar Oksuz, Anthony Sloan, Nicholas Sarn, Anthony Chomyk, Bruce Trapp, Justin D Lathia, Ignacio Mata, Charis Eng, Murat Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDeepFaceMouse is a high-precision, scalable deep learning framework for analyzing orofacial behavior in mice and predicting large-scale brain activity from facial dynamics. Built on a pose-estimation pipeline with optimized training, inference, and data-handling workflows, it enables robust tracking across diverse behavioral conditions and neurological disease models. Systematic benchmarking demonstrates improved tracking precision compared with existing approaches, along with substantial gains in computational efficiency, particularly on GPU-enabled systems. Importantly, these improvements translate into more accurate prediction of distributed cortical activity across multiple brain regions. By linking behavioral measurement quality to downstream neural signal reconstruction, DeepFaceMouse provides a reproducible and scalable framework for studying brain-behavior relationships. This approach establishes a functionally grounded benchmark for evaluating behavioral modeling methods and supports applications in both basic and disease-focused neuroscience research.Keywords: orofacial behavior, facial dynamics, pose estimation, SLEAP, deep learning, brain-behavior coupling, neural activity prediction, widefield calcium imaging, mesoscale imaging, systems neuroscience

Introduction

Facial dynamics in both mice and humans are tightly coupled to large-scale brain activity. Spontaneous facial movements are embedded within widespread cortical signals, often reflecting internal behavioral states more strongly than responses to structured cognitive tasks.1,2 High-dimensional neural activity across distributed brain regions captures these dynamics, reframing trial-to-trial variability as behaviorally meaningful fluctuations rather than noise.1 Consistent with this view, behavioral state reorganizes functional connectivity across the cortex, supporting a close link between facial behavior and brain-wide neural dynamics.3

In humans, facial expressions are encoded within specialized cortical regions such as the posterior superior temporal sulcus,4 while perturbation of sensory regions can evoke facial movements,5 highlighting the integrative nature of sensorimotor networks. Accordingly, facial analysis has emerged as a powerful, non-invasive modality for probing neural state and disease. Recent studies have demonstrated that facial features can be used to detect genetic syndromes6 and identify Parkinson’s disease.7 In preclinical models, deep learning-based facial analysis enables classification of behavioral states8 and detection of motor impairments such as facial paralysis.9

Despite these advances, extracting reliable and scalable facial features from large and heterogeneous behavioral datasets remains a major challenge. Existing methods such as Facemap10 and DeepLabCut11 provide powerful pose-estimation capabilities but often require extensive manual refinement, per-video retraining, or task-specific customization, limiting their scalability across large datasets, longitudinal experiments, and multimodal studies. Furthermore, current approaches primarily evaluate performance based on tracking accuracy or latent representations, without directly assessing how behavioral feature quality impacts downstream neural inference.

To address these challenges, we developed DeepFaceMouse, a high-precision and scalable orofacial tracking framework built on the SLEAP pose-estimation ecosystem.12 In contrast to existing approaches, DeepFaceMouse is designed as a retrainable, unified framework that enables consistent cross-animal modeling without per-video refinement and supports efficient batch processing of large datasets. Importantly, beyond improving tracking accuracy, DeepFaceMouse formulates brain-behavior analysis as a direct neural signal reconstruction problem, enabling end-to-end evaluation of how behavioral measurement quality propagates to the prediction of distributed cortical activity.

DeepFaceMouse integrates optimized training and inference strategies with user-friendly graphical interfaces and flexible deployment across CPU and graphics processing unit (GPU) environments, supporting both local and high-performance computing (HPC) workflows. While we demonstrate its application in widefield calcium imaging across multiple transgenic mouse lines, behavioral paradigms, and disease models, the method is specifically designed for large-scale behavioral-neural datasets where scalability, reproducibility, and cross-condition robustness are essential. Together, these features establish DeepFaceMouse as a unified and scalable framework for linking facial behavior to large-scale neural dynamics in systems and translational neuroscience.

Results

DeepFaceMouse improves tracking precision and enables scalable orofacial analysis across mouse lines

We developed DeepFaceMouse, a standardized and scalable pipeline for high-precision orofacial tracking across multiple transgenic mouse lines (Figure 1). The framework enables robust feature extraction from large-scale behavioral video datasets acquired during brain imaging and behavioral monitoring (Figure 1A; see STAR Methods for details). From each raw video, uniformly sampled frames were concatenated into composite training videos, allowing efficient labeling and consistent coverage of behavioral variability across animals and recording days. A subset of labeled frames was then used to train SLEAP-based pose estimation models, which were subsequently deployed for automated inference on the original videos (Figure S1; see STAR Methods for details).

High-precision and ultrafast orofacial tracking using DeepFaceMouse across multiple transgenic mouse lines for large-scale orofacial video analysis

(A) DeepFaceMouse analysis pipeline. To achieve robust and generalizable tracking of orofacial features across different calcium indicator mouse lines (GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m), a standardized and scalable workflow was developed. From each video (n = 90), 330 frames were uniformly extracted and concatenated into a single training video using a custom-built GUI (shown in Figures 4A and 4B). A total of 1,800 frames were labeled with 12 key points (eye, nose, mouth, and whiskers) and used to train models using SLEAP (U-Net backbone). The trained model was then applied back to individual original videos for inference and benchmarking using a custom-built analysis GUI in the SLEAP environment (shown in Figure 4A).

(B) DeepFaceMouse model selection. To identify the most accurate model architecture, ten different SLEAP models were trained and evaluated using GCaMP6s M1 videos (n = 6). Model performance was quantified by precision performance (mean ± SEM), and results demonstrated that training DeepFaceMouse with a U-Net backbone yielded the highest precision across test videos. Detailed precision values for each experimental day of M1 across all tested models are shown in Figure S2.

(C) DeepFaceMouse UNet training parameter selection. After identifying the U-Net backbone as the optimal model architecture, training parameter optimization was performed by varying the training patch size from the default value of 32 × 32 to 64 × 64. The models were evaluated on the lowest-precision videos across datasets (GCaMP6s: M5, day 2; GCaMP6f: M3, day 3; and jGCaMP8m: M3, day 4). The results confirmed that a patch size of 64 × 64 produced superior performance and was selected for all subsequent analyses.

(D) DeepFaceMouse analysis parameter selection. Following optimization of training parameters, inference parameters within the SLEAP analysis pipeline were evaluated. Two primary analysis modes (simple and optical flow) were tested, each with six built-in parameter combinations (12 in total) as shown. Performance was assessed using the same challenging videos described in (C), revealing that the flow + centroid + greedy combination provided the best overall accuracy for large-scale data analysis. DeepLabCut (black), Facemap Basemodel (purple), and Facemap Refined (green) results are shown for comparison.

To identify the most accurate model architecture, we systematically compared multiple SLEAP backbones, using a common benchmark dataset (Figure 1B). This analysis revealed that the U-Net architecture consistently achieved the highest precision across test videos, motivating its use in all subsequent DeepFaceMouse models (Figure S2). After selecting the optimal architecture, we further optimized training parameters by varying the input patch size (Figure 1C). Increasing the patch sizing from 32 × 32 to 64 × 64 frames significantly improved performance on the most challenging datasets, indicating that enhanced temporal context improves tracking robustness under low contrast or rapid motion conditions.

We next optimized inference-time parameters within the SLEAP analysis pipeline (Figure 1D), comparing simple and optical flow-based tracking modes across multiple built-in parameter combinations. This analysis demonstrated that the optical flow-based approach combined with centroid and greedy linking strategies yielded the highest accuracy across mouse lines and experimental conditions. Importantly, DeepFaceMouse consistently outperformed alternative approaches, including DeepLabCut and both “baseline” and “refined” Facemap models, highlighting its robustness for large-scale orofacial analysis (Figure 1D).

DeepFaceMouse improves tracking precision compared to existing orofacial tracking frameworks

We benchmarked DeepFaceMouse against two widely used orofacial tracking approaches, DeepLabCut and Facemap, under matched, large-scale analysis conditions (Figure 2). To ensure a fair comparison, we implemented DeepLabCut using the same data preparation, frame extraction, and labeling strategy as DeepFaceMouse (Figure 2A). We used identical numbers of frames and key points across pipelines, with DeepLabCut models trained using a standard ResNet-50 backbone and evaluated on the same set of original videos. In parallel, we evaluated the Facemap pipeline by using both its base configuration (since it is a pretrained model) and a refined workflow in which a small number of frames per video were manually annotated and used to retrain a Mini-U-Net model (Figure 2B), reflecting common best practices for adaptive refinement.

DeepFaceMouse model comparison to DeepLabCut and Facemap for large-scale orofacial video analysis

(A) DeepLabCut analysis pipeline. To directly compare DeepFaceMouse with DeepLabCut for large-scale animal data analysis, DeepLabCut was implemented using an equivalent data preparation and labeling strategy. DeepLabCut used the same number of images for frame extraction, concatenation, and labeling as in the DeepFaceMouse pipeline. From each video (n = 90), 330 frames were uniformly extracted and concatenated into a single training video using a custom-built GUI (shown in Figures 4A and 4B). A total of 1,800 frames were labeled with 12 key points (eye, nose, mouth, and whiskers) and used to train models using a ResNet-50 backbone. The trained model was then applied back to individual original videos for inference and benchmarking.

(B) Facemap refinement workflow. In parallel, the performance of the existing Facemap pipeline was evaluated. Each video was first processed using the Facemap Basemodel as it is their design by default. A minimal set of 15 frames per video were then manually labeled with 11 key points, and the model was retrained using the Facemap Mini-U-Net. The refinement loop was repeated for each video within a transgenic line, enabling adaptive fine-tuning across datasets.

(C) Model precision across transgenic lines. Precision performance (mean ± SEM, n = 6) was benchmarked for each of the five mice (M1–M5) across six experimental days and three transgenic lines (GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m). DeepFaceMouse (blue) consistently outperformed the Facemap Basemodel (purple), Facemap Refined (red), and DeepLabCut (black) in terms of precision across GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m lines. The final image aggregates performance across all transgenic lines, highlighting DeepFaceMouse’s superior and generalizable accuracy (GCaMP6s: p < 0.05 vs. refined, p < 0.0001 vs. DeepLabCut and base model; GCaMP6f: p < 0.001 vs. refined, p < 0.0001 vs. DeepLabCut and base model; jGCaMP8m: p < 0.001 vs. refined and base model, p < 0.0001 vs. DeepLabCut; paired t test).

(D) Runtime efficiency of analysis pipelines. Total processing time (in min) was quantified for each model across all videos and transgenic lines. DeepFaceMouse achieved high precision with minimal runtime overhead, significantly outperforming DeepLabCut and Facemap Refined pipelines. Deployment of DeepFaceMouse on a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster further reduced runtime, demonstrating scalability for large-scale datasets.

We systematically quantified model performance across five wild-type mice, multiple experimental days, and three transgenic mouse lines (GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m; Figures 2C; Videos S1, S2, and S3). Across all datasets, DeepFaceMouse consistently achieved higher tracking precision than DeepLabCut, the Facemap Basemodel, and the Facemap Refined approach (Figure S3). Importantly, this performance advantage was preserved across mouse lines that differ in baseline behavior, head posture, facial kinematics, and movement statistics, rather than being optimized for a single animal or recording condition. This robustness is notable given prior reports that pose-estimation models trained on limited mouse cohorts or single lines often fail to generalize across animals with distinct behavioral and postural dynamics.13 Aggregated analyses across transgenic lines further confirmed the statistical significance of these improvements, demonstrating that DeepFaceMouse generalizes reliably across animals and experimental sessions.

In addition to accuracy, we evaluated computational efficiency by quantifying the total runtime required to process the full dataset for each method (Figure 2D). DeepFaceMouse achieved superior precision with substantially lower runtime than DeepLabCut and Facemap Refined pipelines, which incurred large computational overhead due to per-video retraining and post-processing steps. The results indicate that DeepLabCut does not scale efficiently for large-scale orofacial data analysis, and Facemap Refined relies on video-by-video manual refinement, rendering the workflow tedious and impractical for high-throughput analysis. Analyzing 90 videos required approximately 13 h using Facemap with manual refinement, whereas DeepFaceMouse completed the same analysis in 2.5 h when deployed on an HPC system and 4 h when using the local computer. This efficiency advantage scales dramatically with dataset size: processing 1,000 videos with DeepFaceMouse can be completed within a single day in a fully automated manner, while the same analysis using Facemap would require nearly a month of manual intervention. Together, these results demonstrate that DeepFaceMouse provides a favorable balance of accuracy, speed, and scalability for large-scale orofacial video analysis in neuroscience experiments.

Behavioral tracking precision determines accuracy of brain-wide neural decoding

We show that high-precision orofacial tracking with DeepFaceMouse enables accurate decoding of large-scale cortical activity in awake, head-fixed mice (Figure 3). We combined high-speed facial videography with widefield calcium imaging to simultaneously record fine-grained orofacial dynamics and brain activity across 11 cortical regions spanning motor, retrosplenial, somatosensory, and visual cortices for GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m mouse lines (Figure 3A; Videos S4, S5, and S6). We extracted facial features using DeepFaceMouse, Facemap Refined, and the Facemap Basemodel, allowing direct comparison of how tracking precision influences the downstream neural decoding performance.

Decoding brain activity from orofacial dynamics using DeepFaceMouse in head-fixed mice

(A) Experimental setup and data collection. A high-resolution behavioral and neural data acquisition system was developed, combining 120 Hz facial videography with widefield one-photon calcium imaging to record activity from 11 cortical brain regions across both hemispheres. Three transgenic mouse lines (GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m) underwent six-day experimental protocols, with each session lasting 5 min (300 s). A total of 300 s of data were collected per experiment, with the first 40 s shown for visualization purposes. Brain imaging was performed at 40 Hz for GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f mice and at 100 Hz for jGCaMP8m mice to accommodate the faster kinetics of the jGCaMP8m calcium indicator (Figure S6). Following each session, facial videos were processed using three analysis pipelines (DeepFaceMouse, Facemap Refined, and Facemap Basemodel), extracting 12 facial features corresponding to the x/y positions of the eyelids, nose, mouth, and whiskers. Brain signals were segmented into 4 main anatomically defined regions (motor [MO], retrosplenial [RSP], somatosensory [SSp], and visual [VIS]) and further separated into 11 specific brain regions for downstream analysis.

(B) Deep learning model for brain activity prediction. To evaluate whether orofacial dynamics encode brain-wide neural activity, a feedforward neural network was trained using 24 input features (x/y coordinates) derived from face tracking. A preprocessing module was applied to enhance the temporal and spatial continuity of these signals before input into multi-layer neural networks consisting of either five or ten hidden layers. The network output consisted of predicted calcium activity traces for each of the 11 brain regions. Model training and testing were performed using GCaMP6s animal data, and the first 40 s of the recorded and predicted signals are displayed for visualization purposes.

(C) Representative predictions from different models. DeepFaceMouse-enabled predictions show strong alignment with recorded brain signals across cortical regions, substantially outperforming predictions generated using the Facemap Refined and Facemap Basemodel pipelines. For visualization clarity, a 30-s segment of the recorded (green) and predicted (red) signals is shown for GCaMP6s M1. Data from a representative GCaMP6s mouse illustrate the model’s fidelity across motor, sensory, and visual cortices, underscoring DeepFaceMouse’s robustness and precision in decoding brain dynamics from orofacial behavior.

(D) Zoomed-in comparison for primary motor cortex (MOp). A short temporal window (5 s) highlights the alignment between recorded and predicted MOp activity. DeepFaceMouse captured both slow fluctuations and rapid transients with high fidelity, whereas Facemap Refined showed reduced temporal precision and amplitude mismatch, and the Facemap Basemodel failed to reproduce meaningful neural dynamics.

(E) Zoomed-in comparison for retrosplenial cortex (RSPv). Similar zoomed-in analysis demonstrated that DeepFaceMouse could accurately reconstruct retrosplenial activity patterns, including dynamic oscillatory components, while Facemap Refined exhibited degraded prediction quality, and the Facemap Basemodel showed minimal correspondence to recorded signals.

(F) DeepFaceMouse brain activity prediction. (Left) To determine the optimal number of training images for predicting brain activity, DeepFaceMouse models were trained using 180, 900, or 1,800 labeled images. In the GCaMP6s dataset, five out of six experimental videos per animal were used for training (n = 25 total videos), while the remaining video from each mouse was reserved for testing (model construction details are shown in B). Brain activity prediction was evaluated for each brain region (n = 11) across animals (n = 5), yielding 55 total prediction values using coefficient of determination (R2). Statistical comparison (t test) revealed a significant increase (p < 0.0001) in prediction accuracy when using 1,800 images compared to 180 or 900 images, confirming that 1,800 images (20 images per video) provide optimal performance for training the DeepFaceMouse model. (Right) The four main brain regions (defined in A) were clustered and analyzed to directly compare DeepFaceMouse and Facemap Refined (n = 5 animals). For each brain region, predictions from the same tested video are represented as paired points connected by a line, illustrating the difference between DeepFaceMouse and Facemap Refined in their ability to predict region-specific brain activity. DeepFaceMouse showed significantly higher predictive performance in the motor and RSP regions (p < 0.0001) and moderately higher performance in the SSp and VIS regions (p < 0.001) compared to Facemap Refined.

(G) Cross-line model evaluation. Coefficient of determination (R2) was used to quantify brain activity prediction accuracy across all models and transgenic lines. DeepFaceMouse consistently outperformed competing models (Facemap Refined and Facemap Base) across all five animals in each genotype. The bottom-right image aggregates prediction accuracy across all conditions and demonstrates DeepFaceMouse’s superior generalization and neural decoding capacity (GCaMP6s: p < 0.0001 vs. refined and base models; GCaMP6f: p < 0.05 vs. refined, p < 0.0001 vs. base model; jGCaMP8m: p < 0.001 vs. refined, p < 0.0001 vs. base model; paired t test).

To evaluate how orofacial tracking precision influences neural decoding analyses, we trained feedforward neural networks by using x–y trajectories of facial features as inputs to predict calcium signals from individual brain regions (Figure 3B; Figures S4 and S5; Table S1). Rather than testing whether orofacial dynamics encode neural activity per se, we designed this analysis to assess whether conclusions drawn from face-to-brain encoding are sensitive to the choice and accuracy of the underlying face-tracking model. Network architecture incorporated preprocessing steps to enhance temporal continuity and was trained and evaluated using a consistent cross-validation strategy across methods. Predicted activity traces derived from DeepFaceMouse features more closely matched recorded neural signals, capturing both slow fluctuations and rapid transients across multiple cortical areas, indicating that higher-precision orofacial tracking yields substantially more faithful neural predictions (Figure 3C).

Representative examples highlight the fidelity of DeepFaceMouse-enabled predictions across motor (MOp and MOs), somatosensory (SSpll, SSpul, and SSptr), retrosplenial (RSPagl, RSPd, and RSPv), and visual (VISa, VISam, and VISpm) regions (Figure 3C). In contrast, predictions generated from Facemap Refined and Facemap Basemodel features showed reduced temporal alignment and lower explanatory power, indicating that small errors in facial feature tracking substantially degrade neural decoding accuracy. We consistently observed these differences across animals and experimental sessions.

To further interrogate model performance at fine temporal scales, we performed zoomed-in analyses of predicted vs. recorded neural activity for representative brain regions. More specifically, we focused on short (5 s) temporal windows from the primary motor cortex (MOp) and retrosplenial cortex (RSPv), respectively, two regions with distinct functional roles and dynamical profiles (Figures 3D and 3E). In the motor cortex (Figure 3D), DeepFaceMouse predictions closely tracked the recorded calcium signal, accurately capturing both low-frequency fluctuations and higher-frequency transients. In contrast, predictions generated using Facemap Refined showed attenuated amplitudes and temporal misalignment, while the Facemap Basemodel failed to reproduce structured neural dynamics, yielding near-zero predictive power. This highlights the importance of precise, temporally stable orofacial feature extraction for decoding motor-related cortical activity. A similar pattern was observed in the RSPv (Figure 3E), a region associated with internal state, spatial processing, and arousal-related dynamics. DeepFaceMouse successfully reconstructed complex RSPv activity patterns, including oscillatory components and rapid transitions, whereas Facemap Refined exhibited markedly reduced fidelity and the Basemodel showed minimal correspondence to recorded signals. Together, these zoomed-in examples demonstrate that DeepFaceMouse not only improves overall prediction accuracy but also preserves fine-scale temporal structure essential for decoding region-specific cortical dynamics from orofacial behavior.

Next, we evaluated how the number of labeled training images influences downstream applications, specifically the prediction of brain activity from orofacial dynamics (Figure 3F, left). Increasing the number of labeled frames led to a significant improvement in brain activity prediction accuracy, with 1,800 labeled images providing optimal performance. These results establish a practical trade-off between labeling effort and predictive accuracy and demonstrate that DeepFaceMouse enables reliable behavioral feature extraction sufficient to support quantitative modeling of large-scale neural activity. Quantitative analysis using the coefficient of determination (R2) revealed that DeepFaceMouse significantly outperformed both Facemap-based approaches in predicting brain activity measured with GCaMP6s mice within anatomically defined cortical regions (Figure 3F, right). Region-specific analyses demonstrated particularly strong improvements in the MOp and RSPv, with more moderate but still significant gains in somatosensory and visual areas, suggesting that orofacial dynamics are tightly coupled to distributed cortical states.

Finally, we evaluated model generalization across calcium indicator lines with distinct kinetics and signal-to-noise characteristics (Figure 3G). DeepFaceMouse consistently achieved the highest prediction accuracy across GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m datasets, outperforming both Facemap Refined and Basemodels in every animal tested. During these experiments, widefield calcium imaging was initially performed at 20 Hz across all indicator lines; while this sampling rate supported reliable brain activity prediction for GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f mice, it was insufficient for jGCaMP8m mice due to its faster kinetics. Increasing the imaging rate to 100 Hz for jGCaMP8m mice restored robust prediction performance, indicating that appropriate temporal sampling is critical for capturing indicator-specific neural dynamics (Figure S6). The relationship between orofacial dynamics and cortical activity likely reflects a combination of motor efference, global internal state variables such as arousal, and distributed neuromodulatory influences. Facial movements are tightly coupled to brain-wide fluctuations in cortical excitability, yet our region-specific decoding analyses demonstrate that distinct cortical areas exhibit differential encoding strength and temporal structure. DeepFaceMouse provides a quantitative framework to disentangle shared global signals from region-specific neural dynamics.

DeepFaceMouse enables scalable deployment across local and HPC environments

We designed DeepFaceMouse to support both local desktop analysis and large-scale deployment on HPC systems (Figure 4). To facilitate broad adoption by users with varying computational resources, the framework operates as a modular pipeline that integrates seamlessly with the SLEAP environment while providing custom graphical interfaces for efficient data handling and analysis.

DeepFaceMouse workflow for local and high-performance computing environments

(A) DeepFaceMouse detailed analysis flow chart for local computer. All of the experimental face videos (either in mp4 or avi video format) were inserted into the concatenation GUI for creating a concatenated video (mp4 or avi format). The GUI asked the user to specify the number of frames that were extracted evenly per video, as well as the concatenated video frame rate and output location. After the video was concatenated, it was uploaded into the SLEAP default GUI for labeling and training the model. After training the model, the custom DeepFaceMouse analysis GUI was opened inside the SLEAP Anaconda environment for analyzing the videos. Once the GUI opened, it required the user to first input the model location, then insert the desired videos into the video path location. After the videos were placed into the video path location, the GUI automatically generated the SLEAP analysis file (.slp), feature coordinates csv file, and labeled video inside the same file path as the inserted raw video. Finally, clicking the “run all green” button helped in analyzing all of the inserted video data.

(B) DeepFaceMouse detailed analysis flow chart for high-performance computing (HPC). The DeepFaceMouse analysis process was exactly the same as in the local computer until after training the DeepFaceMouse model. Once the model was trained, a SLURM batch script was created to analyze the video data. Inside the script, the number of nodes and GPUs are specified, as well as the SLEAP commands to analyze all the videos. Then, the SLURM batch script was sent to the HPC system for video analysis, and the output was the SLEAP analysis file.

For local analysis, we imported raw facial videos into a custom concatenation graphical user interface (GUI) that evenly samples a user-defined number of frames from each video and generates a single concatenated training video at a specified frame rate (Figure 4A). This approach standardizes data preparation across experiments and minimizes manual preprocessing. The concatenated video is then labeled and used to train a DeepFaceMouse model within the default SLEAP graphical interface. Following model training, a custom DeepFaceMouse analysis GUI is launched within the SLEAP environment, where users specify the trained model and select videos for inference. With a single execution step, the pipeline automatically generates SLEAP analysis files, labeled output videos, and feature coordinate files, enabling rapid and reproducible analysis of behavioral datasets.

To support large-scale studies involving hundreds to thousands of videos, the same workflow can be deployed on HPC systems without modification to the underlying analysis logic (Figure 4B). After model training, users generate a SLURM batch script specifying the desired number of compute nodes and GPUs, along with the corresponding SLEAP analysis commands. This script is submitted to the HPC scheduler, enabling parallelized processing of large video datasets. The output files produced by the HPC workflow are identical to those generated locally, ensuring consistency across computational environments. Together, these workflows demonstrate that DeepFaceMouse combines ease of use with scalability, allowing both individual laboratories and large consortia to perform high-throughput orofacial analysis efficiently and reproducibly.

DeepFaceMouse maintains high-precision tracking across diverse disease models and behavioral conditions

We evaluated the robustness and generalizability of DeepFaceMouse across diverse neurological disease models and behavioral paradigms (Figure 5). We first assessed tracking precision in three distinct pathological contexts—glioblastoma (GBM, using the syngeneic mouse model SB28),14,15 phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)-associated autism spectrum disorder (ASD),16 and cuprizone-induced multiple sclerosis (MS)17,18—each of which introduces unique challenges such as altered facial morphology, atypical motor patterns, or disease-related behavioral variability (Figures 5A–5C). Across all disease cohorts, DeepFaceMouse consistently achieved high precision (>90%) at an individual animal level, demonstrating that the model maintains accuracy despite substantial inter-animal and disease-specific differences18 (Figures S7 and S8).

We next evaluated DeepFaceMouse performance across a range of behavioral conditions in wild-type mice, including spontaneous behavior, virtual reality-guided tasks, and social interaction assays (Figures 5D–5G, Videos S7 and S8; Figures S7 and S8). Precision remained high and stable during spontaneous and virtual reality behaviors, demonstrating robustness to changes in the task structure, sensory input, and movement dynamics. Having established reliable performance across distinct behavioral paradigms, we next challenged the model using social interaction experiments, which combine complex, non-stereotyped facial movements with close physical proximity between animals (Figure S8). To further increase task complexity, we examined social interactions involving not only wild-type visitor mice (Figures 5F and S8A) but also neurologically mutant PTEN visitor mice (Figures 5G and S8B), which introduced altered behavioral dynamics and facial motion patterns. In addition, repeated exposure to the same visitor was used to induce familiarity, providing a longitudinal perturbation of social behavior. DeepFaceMouse maintained consistent precision across novel and familiar encounters, as well as across interactions involving mutant animals, indicating stable performance in settings that combine complex behavioral structure with disease-associated variability. Together, these results demonstrate that DeepFaceMouse generalizes across increasing levels of behavioral and experimental complexity, supporting its use in multi-animal, longitudinal studies of behavior-brain interactions in both healthy and disease-relevant contexts. We note that this generalization reflects robustness across diverse conditions within a unified experimental framework, rather than across independently collected datasets from different laboratories.

Discussion

Facial behavior is increasingly being recognized as a rich and informative readout of internal brain state, reflecting neural dynamics distributed across sensory, motor, and associative cortical networks.10,19,20,21,22 In this study, we introduce DeepFaceMouse, a scalable and high-precision framework for orofacial tracking in mice and show that improvements in behavioral feature extraction substantially strengthen the reliability of downstream brain-behavior analyses. Rather than re-establishing whether facial dynamics relate to neural activity, our results demonstrate that the accuracy and stability of facial tracking itself critically shape the conclusions drawn from such analyses, particularly in complex experimental settings. Notably, DeepFaceMouse maintains high precision not only across standard behavioral paradigms but also in challenging conditions that couple neurological perturbations with naturalistic behavior, such as social interactions involving PTEN-mutant animals. Rather than prescribing a fixed parameter configuration, DeepFaceMouse provides a transparent and reproducible framework for systematic parameter selection. Together, these findings position high-fidelity facial tracking as an essential methodological foundation for robust, scalable brain-behavior modeling across increasingly complex and disease-relevant experimental contexts.

A central contribution of DeepFaceMouse lies not in introducing a fundamentally new neural network architecture but in establishing a structured and scalable framework that links high-precision orofacial tracking for large scale data analysis to direct reconstruction of region-level neural activity. This shift, from latent behavioral representation to direct neural activity reconstruction, provides a more biologically interpretable evaluation of behavioral tracking performance. While existing tools such as DeepLabCut and FaceMap have enabled transformative advances in pose estimation, our systematic benchmarking reveals limitations that become pronounced in large, heterogeneous datasets, including sensitivity to video quality, reliance on per-video or per-animal refinement, and substantial computational overhead. Across all evaluated datasets, DeepFaceMouse consistently achieved higher tracking precision, yielding absolute improvements of approximately 10%–25% relative to DeepLabCut and both base and Facemap Refined workflows while maintaining stable performance across transgenic mouse lines, neurological disease models, and behavioral contexts. Importantly, comparisons with Facemap reflect differences in framework design, specifically, retrainable vs. refinement-based workflows, rather than strictly matched training regimes. Accordingly, these comparisons also reflect differences in practical usage and design philosophy rather than fully controlled training equivalence. These gains reflect lower pixel-wise error and reduced frame-to-frame variability across facial and orofacial features, enabling reliable cross-animal generalization within the evaluated experimental conditions without task-specific retraining. DeepFaceMouse achieves this performance through an optimized SLEAP-based U-Net architecture, systematic selection of training and inference parameters, and fully automated workflows that minimize manual intervention. In addition to improved accuracy, DeepFaceMouse substantially reduces analysis time by up to an order of magnitude compared with existing approaches, particularly when deployed on GPU-enabled HPC systems.

Importantly, the gains in tracking accuracy provided by DeepFaceMouse translated directly into improved neural decoding performance. Using only orofacial dynamics, we were able to predict activity across distributed cortical regions, including motor, retrosplenial, somatosensory, and visual cortices (>90% accuracy). Together, these results not only reinforce the growing view that spontaneous and task-related facial movements reflect latent brain states22 at the mesoscale but also demonstrate that the precision of facial tracking critically shapes the conclusions drawn about brain-behavior relationships. Notably, even modest differences in tracking fidelity between the methods produced disproportionately large differences in downstream brain activity prediction, underscoring that behavioral precision is not merely a technical consideration but a fundamental limiting factor for systems-level neuroscience analyses.10 Importantly, the predictive modeling approach used in this study builds on existing feedforward neural network frameworks, rather than introducing novel architecture. The key distinction lies in the formulation of the problem as direct reconstruction of region-resolved neural activity, rather than prediction of low-dimensional latent representations. This formulation enables a more direct assessment of how improvements in behavioral tracking propagate to downstream neural decoding.

A key strength of DeepFaceMouse is its robustness across neurological disease models and behavioral contexts. We demonstrate stable performance in GBM, PTEN-associated ASD, and cuprizone-induced MS models—conditions that alter movement patterns, arousal, and facial expressivity.23,24,25 DeepFaceMouse also maintained high precision across spontaneous behavior, virtual reality tasks, and social interaction assays involving both wild-type and mutant animals, which introduce familiarity effects, dynamic whisker and facial movements, and context-dependent behavioral shifts. Together, these results indicate that DeepFaceMouse demonstrates robustness across diverse conditions within a unified experimental framework and is well suited for longitudinal and disease-focused studies in which behavioral variability is intrinsic rather than a confound.

Beyond performance, DeepFaceMouse was designed with accessibility and reproducibility as core principles. By integrating custom graphical interfaces for data concatenation and analysis with standardized SLEAP workflows, DeepFaceMouse lowers the barrier to large-scale behavioral analysis for users without extensive computational expertise. At the same time, full compatibility with HPC environments enables rapid processing of hundreds to thousands of videos, ensuring that increases in dataset size do not come at the cost of turnaround time or reproducibility. The HPC workflows provided here should be viewed as reference implementations rather than stand-alone methodological contributions. This dual-mode design allows DeepFaceMouse to scale from individual laboratories to large collaborative efforts.

The relationship between orofacial dynamics and cortical activity likely reflects a combination of motor efference, internal state variables such as arousal and vigilance, and distributed neuromodulatory influences that shape large-scale cortical dynamics.26 Although widefield calcium signals exhibit prominent global components, our region-resolved decoding analyses demonstrate meaningful heterogeneity across cortical areas, with distinct encoding strength and temporal structure observed in motor, retrosplenial, somatosensory, and visual regions. Moreover, the mouse models examined in this study—GBM, PTEN-associated ASD, and cuprizone-induced demyelination—differ substantially in pathology, behavior, and network organization, yet DeepFaceMouse robustly captures facial dynamics that remain predictive of brain-wide activity across conditions. This suggests that disease states may alter the structure and weighting of face-brain coupling, instead of abolishing it. While the present study focuses on establishing methodological robustness rather than defining disease-specific facial or neural signatures, DeepFaceMouse provides a scalable framework for future work aimed at quantifying pathological differences in facial dynamics, network activity, and their interaction over time or in response to therapeutic intervention.

Limitations of the study

First, all experiments were performed in head-fixed mice, using a side-view facial imaging configuration, enabling stable, high-resolution videography and simultaneous widefield calcium imaging. However, facial movements in freely moving animals may differ in kinematics, occlusions, and viewpoints. As with other pose-estimation approaches, DeepFaceMouse models are not expected to generalize across arbitrary geometries.27 Instead, the primary contribution is a standardized and reproducible training framework. Direct comparison with Facemap under fully matched training conditions is not feasible due to differences in framework design and training paradigms, and comparisons should be interpreted as reflecting practical usage scenarios, rather than strictly controlled benchmarking. Extending to freely moving,28 multi-camera,29 or 3D tracking12 will require retraining with representative data and expanded labeling schemes.10

Second, DeepFaceMouse was developed using grayscale, high-frame-rate videos acquired under controlled conditions within a single experimental framework. While performance was robust across disease models and behaviors, cross-laboratory generalization remains to be established. Differences in imaging systems and setups may require dataset-specific retraining, as is typical in pose-estimation workflows.27,30 To facilitate adaptation, we provide documentation, GUIs, and optimized parameters.

Third, training requires GPU resources for practical runtimes. While preprocessing and inference can be performed on standard systems, large-scale training benefits from HPC environments, which introduce additional technical requirements such as command-line workflows and cluster-specific configurations. Provided HPC pipelines serve as reference implementations and require adaptation to institutional infrastructure.

Fourth, brain activity prediction relied on feedforward neural networks prioritizing robustness and interpretability. While these models explain substantial variance, they do not capture latent states or long-range temporal dependencies.10 More complex models, such as recurrent or state-space approaches, may further improve performance.

Fifth, neural decoding was limited to mesoscale cortical activity using widefield calcium imaging. This approach does not address prediction at single-cell or subcortical levels, where neural activity may relate differently to behavior.1,10 Future work integrating multiphoton imaging31,32 or electrophysiology33 will be needed to assess generalization across spatial scales.

Sixth, DeepFaceMouse provides optimized parameters derived from systematic evaluation, but these are not universally applicable. Instead, the framework should be viewed as a reproducible workflow for parameter optimization, with adaptation expected across experimental conditions.

Finally, this study focused on a defined set of orofacial key points. Other applications including whole-body tracking,34 species-specific features,35 or fine-scale muscle dynamics,36 may require expanded labeling schemes. The modular design supports such extensions, but they were beyond the scope of this work. Despite these limitations, DeepFaceMouse provides a scalable and adaptable framework for large-scale orofacial tracking and brain-behavior modeling, designed to evolve with the emerging experimental paradigms.

Experimental model and study participant details

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mouse genotyping was performed on ear punches by TransnetYX (Cordova, TN) using their automated real-time PCR platform. Mice were maintained on a 14:10 light: dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The room temperature was regulated between 18°C and 26°C. Male and female littermates were separated by sex after weaning and housed with same-sex littermates. Reporter (transgenic) mice were generated by crossing either Ai162 (TIT2L-GC6s-ICL-tTA2)-D (stock no. 031562, Jackson Laboratory), Ai148 (TIT2L-GC6f-ICL-tTA2)-D (stock no. 030328, Jackson Laboratory), or TIGRE2-jGCaMP8m-IRES-tTA2-WPRE (stock no. 037718, Jackson Laboratory) with B6.Cg-Tg(Camk2a-cre)T29-1Stl/J mice (CaMKIIα-Cre, stock no. 005359, Jackson Laboratory). The ages of mouse lines (both male and female) were 36–40 weeks for GCaMP6s (n = 5 male), 28–32 weeks for GCaMP6f (n = 5, 2 male and 3 female), 12–16 weeks for jGCaMP8m (n = 5, 2 male and 3 female, see Figures S3 and S6).

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)–related phenotypes were studied using the cytoplasmic-predominant PtenY68H/+ mouse model, originally described previously.16 This line was backcrossed for >10 generations onto a C57BL/6J background (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME). For experimental cohorts, male PtenY68H/+ mice on the C57BL/6J background were bred with wild-type female mice (C57BL/6J; The Jackson Laboratory) to generate the mice used in this study. The ages of this mouse line (both male and female) were 28–32 weeks for both wildtype (n = 6, 4 male and 2 female) and mutant (n = 6 female) conditions (see Figure S8).

Multiple sclerosis (MS)–like demyelination was induced by administering cuprizone at a concentration of 0.3% (w/w) to wildtype mice in their diet. Cuprizone-containing food pellets were obtained from Envigo and provided ad libitum for 8 weeks to induce demyelination. Food pellets were replenished by the experimenter three times per week throughout the treatment period. Following the demyelination phase, mice were returned to a standard chow diet for a 6-week remyelination period. The ages of this mouse line (n = 6, 3 male and 3 female) were 32–36 weeks (see Figure S7B).

Glioblastoma (GBM) mice were generated by stereotaxic implantation of the SB28 tumor cell line (NRasV12/Pt2/Shp53/mPDGF) acquired from Dr. Hideho Okada at UCSF to wildtype mice. A volume of 3–5 μL of tumor cell suspension was pressure-injected unilaterally into the left dorsolateral striatum (A.P. +1.2 mm, M.L. −1.6 mm, D.V. −3.8 mm relative to bregma) using a 31-gauge needle mounted on a stereotaxic frame (RWD, #68535). Following injection, the craniotomy was sealed with bone wax, and animals were allowed to recover under standard postoperative care. The ages of this mouse line (n = 6, 4 male and 2 female) were 20–36 weeks (see Figure S7A).

Method details

Surgical procedures

Surgical procedures for GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, jGCaMP8m, ASD and MS mice closely followed the procedure previously used for whole-cortex widefield imaging.37 Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction 2.5%; maintenance 1–1.5%). Buprenorphine (3.25 mg/kg), Meloxicam (5 mg/kg), and sterile saline (0.05 mL/g) were administered at the start of surgery. Anesthesia depth was confirmed by toe pinch. Hair was removed from the dorsal scalp (Nair, Vetiva Mini Hair Trimmer) and the area was disinfected with 3 alternating applications of betadine and 70% isopropanol. Bupivacaine (5 mg/kg) was then injected under the skin for local anesthetic before the scalp was removed. The skull was then exposed, cleaned, and dried. The remaining outer skin was affixed in position with tissue adhesive (Vetbond, 3M) for clean surgical margins. We created an outer wall using dental cement (C&B Metabond, Parkell) while leaving as much of the skull exposed as possible. A custom circular headbar (eMachineShop) was secured in place using dental cement (C&B Metabond, Parkell). A layer of optical glue (Norland Optical Adhesive NOA 81, Norland Products) was then applied to the exposed skull and cured with a UV flashlight (LIGHTFE, UV301Plus-365nm). The mice were allowed to fully recover in a warmed chamber and then returned to their home cages. Post-operational care consisted of three daily injections of Meloxicam (5 mg/kg) following the surgery.

In addition to the surgical procedures described above, glioblastoma (GBM) mice underwent stereotaxic implantation of the SB28 tumor cell line during the same surgical session. Following skull exposure, a small craniotomy was made above the left dorsolateral striatum, and 3–5 μL of SB28 tumor cell suspension was pressure-injected unilaterally at A.P. +1.2 mm, M.L. −1.6 mm, D.V. −3.8 mm relative to bregma using a 31-gauge needle mounted on a stereotaxic frame (RWD, #68535). The craniotomy was sealed with bone wax, after which the procedures for skull preparation, headbar fixation, optical clearing, and wound closure were completed as described above. Postoperative recovery and analgesic care were identical to those described for other experimental groups.

Data acquisition, triggering, and synchronization

Animal locomotion was measured using a custom air-ball system that allowed free planar movement. The airball system was composed of a 20-cm polystyrene foam ball and a hemispherical 3D printed PLA bowl with an internal diameter that fitted with the ball modified from previous studies.38,39 The bowl had eight holes for pressured air at the bottom. The head of the mouse was fixed to a rigid head mount bar and posts via the head plate and positioned ∼1 cm above the top of the ball. Ball motion was tracked using two orthogonally mounted USB optical mice (Gaming Mouse G302, Logitech), capturing forward–backward, lateral, and rotational components of movement. Raw motion signals were read by a Raspberry Pi, converted into three analog voltages via a digital-to-analog converter, and streamed to a data acquisition (DAQ) system (National Instruments PCI-6321 and BNC-2110). These signals were used in real time to control the animal’s movement within the virtual reality (VR) environment (Blender).

Widefield brain imaging and orofacial video acquisition were performed using cameras operating in hardware-triggered mode. Each camera (Thorlabs, CS505MU1, Kiralux) was controlled by an Arduino microcontroller that initiated frame acquisition only when receiving a synchronization signal from the DAQ system. This ensured precise temporal alignment across imaging modalities. Brain imaging used alternating blue and violet illumination, implemented by the Arduino at 40 Hz, with successive frames captured under alternating wavelengths to enable spectrally distinct measurements.

The VR system (implemented in Blender) served as the master controller for experiment timing and synchronization. At experiment onset, the VR engine initiated a DAQ trigger that simultaneously started all imaging systems and locomotion recording. During the experiment, animal movement data were continuously read by the VR engine to update the virtual environment in real time. At experiment termination, the VR system stopped the DAQ trigger, halted all camera acquisition, and saved synchronized locomotion and imaging data to disk.

Blender (Python-based) implementations followed the same four-stage control logic: system initialization, synchronized experiment start, real-time maintenance during task execution, and synchronized shutdown with data saving. This architecture ensured that neural imaging, behavioral video, facial dynamics, and locomotion data were temporally aligned across all trials.

Widefield imaging of brain activity

For wide-field imaging, cortical excitation was performed sequentially using blue (M470L5, Thorlabs) and violet (M405LP1, Thorlabs) light emitting diodes (LEDs). Blue illumination (470 nm) targeted genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECI) (GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m), while violet (405 nm) illumination immediately followed to provide a reference measurement used to correct hemodynamic artifacts. The two excitation wavelengths were combined through a dichroic beamsplitter (87-063, Edmund Optics) and sent onto the cortical surface via an objective lens (MVL50M23, Navitar). Emitted light was collected through an emission dichroic beamsplitter (T495LPXR, Chroma), and filtered using a band-pass filter (86–963, Edmund Optics). The filtered fluorescence signal was then focused on a CMOS camera (CS505MU1, Kiralux, Thorlabs) through a second objective lens (MVL16M23, Navitar). Data was acquired at a resolution of 1200 × 1200 pixels with 4 × 4 pixels binning, resulting in an effective 300 × 300 pixels covering a cortical area of 9.85 × 9.85 mm2. This configuration enabled an acquisition rate of 40 frames per second (fps), sufficient for resolving the kinetics of the GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f calcium indicators. Experiments were carried out at 40 Hz frame rate for blue and violet light combined (effective frame rate of 20 Hz). Average laser powers were maintained below 10mW for the blue LED and below 50 mW for violet LED. Beam diameters were measured by the knife-edge test method, (1/e2 criterion) were 9.2 for blue LED and 11.7mm for violet LED. For functional imaging with jGCaMP8m mice, we used another objective (MVL100M23, Navitar) and utilized 1200 × 1200 pixels with binning 2 × 2 pixels (effectively 600 × 600 pixels) which cover 9.85 × 9.85 mm2 surface area on the mouse brain. With this configuration, we can achieve 100 frames per second (fps) acquisition rate, which is fast enough to record jGCaMP8m calcium indicator. We used <10 mW average power for blue LED. By performing the knife-edge test, we measured 1/e2 lateral spot sizes of the blue beam as 9.2 mm.

Allen brain atlas registration

We used the alignment script provided by the locaNMF toolbox40 to rigidly align the skull surface’s anatomical landmarks to a standard template atlas. For each animal, we selected seven key points: the left, center, and right intersections between the anterior cortex and olfactory bulb, the location of the bregma, and the base of the retrosplenial cortex on the midline skull suture.

Hemodynamic subtraction

For hemodynamic correction, we first computed the blue and violet ΔF/F for each brain region by subtracting and dividing by the median average brain activity during the whole session (5 min). We used linear regression to regress the violet time series on the blue time series, i.e., find coefficients m and b of the regression b = mv + b where b is the blue and v is the violet ΔF/F signal. The subtracted signal was given by b – (mb + b). This gives a single time series per region, which was used for subsequent analyses.

Widefield imaging: Spontaneous behavior

Mice underwent three days of training to acclimate to head-fixation and test environment. On the first day, the animal was head-fixed and was allowed to move freely on the top of the foam ball while pressurized air was off. On the second and third days, the pressurized air gradually increased to quarter and half of the final pressure level (80psi).

Widefield imaging: Virtual reality (VR)

An interactive VR landscape rendered from a first-person perspective was projected by two compact liquid crystal display (LCD) projectors (M110, Dell) onto the back of a custom-made 40 cm-diameter translucent acrylic semi-domal screen that was positioned 20 cm in front of the mouse and covered 240° of the mouse’s visual field. The VR arena mimicked a realistic open-field enclosure and consisted of a three-dimensional circular arena surrounded by colored objects including a red car, a brown chair, and a tree. The arena was bounded with brown fences. When the mice hit the fence in the arena, they adhered to it visually, but no physical barrier prevented them from continuing their locomotion. They were therefore allowed to move until they spontaneously changed direction.

Widefield imaging: Social interaction

For social interaction test the visitor animals were placed in polycarbonate tubes. The test and visitor animals faced one another, approximately eye-to-eye along the anterior-posterior axis. The inter-snout distance between the test and visitor animals was 5-7mm allowing whisker-to-whisker touch but prevent adverse physical interactions. The social interaction test consisted of three consecutive 5-min long sessions during which the test animal remained in the behavioral set-up. The test mouse was first introduced to the first visitor animal (visitor#1) and then a move second visitor animal (visitor#2). On the last session, the familiar visitor#1 was brought back. Between visitor animal replacements, the visitor animal stage and tube were thoroughly wiped with Clorox wipes and dried. Visitor animals were 6–8 weeks younger non-litter mate, Pten+/+ or PtenY68H/+males.

Imaging of orofacial features

We recorded the facial features of the animal in all mouse lines. To illuminate the left side of the face we used an infrared light source (LIU780A, Thorlabs). The face images were collected with a separate camera (CS505MU1, Thorlabs) and infinity corrected objective lens (MVL25M23, Navitar) with a frame rate of 120Hz. We acquired orofacial images at 120 Hz with an effective resolution of 400 × 400 pixels (0.0588 mm/pixel) after 3 × 3 hardware binning, providing sufficient temporal resolution to capture fast facial movements while maintaining full coverage of the facial region of interest.

DeepFaceMouse pipeline architecture and availability

To facilitate reproducible and accessible analysis of orofacial behavioral data, we developed the DeepFaceMouse pipeline as a modular, open-source framework composed of two complementary graphical user interface (GUI) tools: mediaGUI for video preprocessing and sleapGUI for large-scale model inference and analysis. These tools are designed to be used sequentially within a unified workflow.

To simplify adoption of the pipeline, we created a central GitHub repository, DeepFace-Mouse (https://github.com/yyildirimlab/DeepFaceMouse), which serves as the main entry point for users and provides direct links to the mediaGUI and sleapGUI repositories for installation. This repository includes clear installation instructions for both tools, along with tutorial videos demonstrating how to build and deploy custom DeepFaceMouse models tailored to different experimental paradigms. The tutorials present a step-by-step “recipe” guiding users from raw video collection, through preprocessing and model training, to efficient large-scale inference and downstream analysis. This structure enables new users to adopt the pipeline with minimal technical expertise while allowing advanced users to adapt the workflow to their own datasets.

The two GitHub repositories correspond to distinct components of the DeepFaceMouse pipeline that address different computational bottlenecks. mediaGUI standardizes and stabilizes training data preparation, while sleapGUI enables efficient batch analysis and model deployment at scale. Together, they form an integrated pipeline for DeepFaceMouse-based orofacial tracking. If users encounter issues, unexpected behavior, or have suggestions for improvement, they are encouraged to report them through the issue tracker on the DeepFaceMouse GitHub page. Reported concerns will be reviewed, investigated, and addressed to ensure continued usability and long-term maintainability of the pipeline.

mediaGUI methodology

mediaGUI was developed to efficiently preprocess facial video data by extracting and concatenating frames across multiple experimental sessions (Figure 4). Users upload raw videos, specify the number of frames to be evenly extracted from each video, and generate a single concatenated output video for model training. Through systematic testing, we found that training stability is optimal when concatenated videos contain ≤30,000 frames at 400 × 400 pixel resolution. At frame counts ≥50,000, model training becomes unstable and memory requirements exceed what is feasible on standard desktop or laptop computers. By standardizing preprocessing, mediaGUI ensures consistent and stable inputs for models trained in SLEAP and can also be used with related frameworks such as DeepLabCut. The tool supports output in both MP4 and AVI formats and minimizes manual effort through a streamlined, minimalistic interface.

mediaGUI is implemented in Python and uses PyQt6 (v6.6.0) for GUI development. Video processing operations are powered by OpenCV-Python (v4.8.1) and NumPy (v1.26.0). Where available, the CUDA Toolkit is used to offload frame reading and resizing to the GPU, significantly accelerating preprocessing. On Windows systems, the openh264 library is integrated to improve MP4 rendering speed relative to AVI output. The software is cross-platform and has been tested on Windows 10/11, macOS 14/15, and Ubuntu 22.04. The software is open-source and available under the MIT License in the main DeepFaceMouse Github Page. Installation is recommended via a standalone executable but is also supported through pip, with detailed usage instructions provided in the repository.

sleapGUI face methodology: Large scale data inference

sleapGUI was developed within the SLEAP environment (Figure 4A) to automate DeepFaceMouse model deployment and overcome limitations of the default SLEAP graphical user interface. In the default SLEAP GUI, users are required to manually specify analysis parameters for each individual video, a process that must be repeated for every analysis run and becomes increasingly time-consuming when processing large datasets. sleapGUI automates this workflow, enabling batch analysis of multiple videos with a single user action. Optimized analysis parameters for large-scale mouse orofacial tracking are embedded directly within the sleapGUI backend commands. Specifically, inference is performed using a predefined combination of Flow, Centroid, and Greedy tracking methods, which we empirically determined to be optimal for DeepFaceMouse-based facial analysis. Because these parameters are hard-coded into the analysis backend, users do not need to manually select or tune inference settings, eliminating repetitive parameter entry and reducing the risk of inconsistent analysis configurations across experiments.

Two sleapGUI variants were developed to support different experimental paradigms. The original sleapGUI Face is designed for single-animal facial tracking using a 12-keypoint model and was used to analyze all experimental data in this study except for the social interaction experiments. For social interaction experiments, we developed sleapGUI Face Social, which supports multi-animal tracking using an 18-keypoint model and enables simultaneous analysis of facial features from two interacting animals. Both sleapGUI variants are designed to mimic HPC batch workflows on local machines, allowing multiple videos to be analyzed sequentially without user supervision. Output files include.slp tracking files, .csv data tables, and labeled visualization videos, which are immediately suitable for downstream analyses such as brain activity prediction.

The applications are implemented in Python and leverage QtPy (v2.4.2), which is pre-installed within the SLEAP environment, for GUI development. Core functionality relies on DeepFaceMouse command-line interfaces, with the GUI acting as an abstraction layer. Threading is used to maintain interface responsiveness during computationally intensive tasks, and platform-specific optimizations ensure reliable execution on Windows, macOS, and Linux systems. sleapGUI is compatible with SLEAP versions up to v1.4.1. The sleapGUI requires an NVIDIA GPU to analyze the videos in a fast manner. The main DeepFaceMouse GitHub page provides open-source access to sleapGUI along with detailed installation instructions and usage documentation, including resources to download and install SLEAP v1.4.1, enabling users to run the custom sleapGUI within a supported SLEAP environment.

DeepFaceMouse optimization for large scale orofacial analysis

DeepFaceMouse analyses were performed using SLEAP version 1.3.3 as the pose estimation backbone. Training videos were generated using mediaGUI, a Python-based graphical user interface, by evenly extracting 330 frames from each behavioral video. A total of 90 videos were used, spanning GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m mouse lines, with brain imaging performed at 20 Hz for GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f animals and at 100 Hz for jGCaMP8m animals. These videos represented 15 animals across three transgenic lines, with each animal undergoing six experimental sessions. Each experimental video contained approximately 36,000 frames. The extracted frames were concatenated into a single training video (Figures 1A and S1A). This concatenated video was then imported into the SLEAP environment, from which 1,800 frames were evenly sampled for manual annotation (Figure S1A). A total of 12 facial keypoints were labeled, including four eyelid points, two nose points, two mouth points, and four whisker points (Figures 1A and S1A). Prior to training, anatomical relationships between keypoints were defined, including connections between the top and bottom eyelids, left and right eyelids, top and bottom mouth points, top left and top right whiskers, and bottom left and bottom right whiskers (Figure 1A). Following annotation, we trained a total of 10 candidate DeepFaceMouse models using different backbone architectures within the SLEAP environment. The evaluated backbones included UNet, DenseNet169, DenseNet201, EfficientNetB5, InceptionNetV2, InceptionV3, MobileNetV2, ResNet101, ResNet152, and ResNeXt101. Each model was applied to six recording days from a representative GCaMP6s animal (M1). Model performance was evaluated by computing overall precision for each backbone (Figure 1B) as well as day-by-day precision across all six recording sessions (Figure S2). This systematic comparison revealed that the UNet backbone consistently achieved the highest precision across recording days and was therefore selected as the empirically optimal model architecture within this dataset for all subsequent DeepFaceMouse analyses.

After identifying U-Net as the optimal backbone, we optimized training parameters by varying the training frame patch size from the default value of 32 × 32 to 64 × 64 (Figure 1C). Models were evaluated on the lowest-precision videos across datasets to assess robustness under challenging conditions (GCaMP6s: M5, Day 2; GCaMP6f: M3, Day 3; jGCaMP8m: M3, Day 4). This analysis confirmed that a frame patch size of 64 × 64 yielded superior performance across all tested conditions and was selected for all downstream DeepFaceMouse models.

Following optimization of training parameters, inference parameters within the SLEAP analysis pipeline were systematically evaluated (Figure 1D). Two primary analysis modes were tested: simple tracking and optical flow–based tracking. For each mode, six built-in association strategies were evaluated, consisting of all combinations of three instance-matching metrics—intersection over union (IOU), centroid matching, and instance matching—paired with two frame-to-frame assignment algorithms, greedy and Hungarian, resulting in a total of twelve inference configurations. IOU, commonly used in object detection, measures the overlap between predicted and ground-truth shapes. Centroid matching computes the Euclidean distance between the centroids of predicted and actual labels, while instance matching calculates normalized distances between sets of labels across frames. For assignment, the greedy method selects the nearest prediction on a frame-by-frame basis, whereas the Hungarian algorithm optimizes global matching efficiency across frames. Performance was assessed using the same low-precision videos described in Figure 1C, and tracking precision was recorded for all twelve parameter combinations. Across all tested configurations and animal types, Flow tracking with Centroid matching and Greedy assignment (Flow + Centroid + Greedy) consistently yielded the highest tracking precision and robustness for large-scale data analysis and was therefore selected for all subsequent DeepFaceMouse analyses. Results from DeepLabCut, Facemap Basemodel, and Facemap Refined Model are shown for comparison (Figure 1D).

To determine the optimal number of training images required for accurate brain activity prediction, DeepFaceMouse models were trained using 180, 900, or 1,800 labeled images (Figure 3F). In the GCaMP6s dataset, five out of six experimental videos per animal were used for training (n = 25 total videos), while the remaining video from each mouse was reserved for testing (model construction details shown in Figure 3B). Brain activity prediction performance was evaluated for each brain region (n = 11) across animals (n = 5), yielding a total of 55 prediction values quantified using the coefficient of determination (R2). Statistical comparisons using a t test revealed a significant improvement in prediction accuracy when using 1,800 images compared to 180 or 900 images (p < 0.0001), confirming that approximately 20 labeled images per video provide optimal performance for training the DeepFaceMouse model. For DeepFaceMouse, pose estimation results for each experimental video were exported as CSV files and used for downstream post-processing and precision analysis (Figure 2C), as well as brain activity prediction (Figure 3).

DeepLabCut methodology

DeepLabCut (DLC) analyses were performed using version 2.3.10. The same concatenated training video generated via mediaGUI was used to ensure a fair comparison between methods (Figures 2A and S1A). Manual annotations were identical to those used for DeepFaceMouse (1800 labeled images), consisting of the same 12 facial keypoints. The DLC model was trained using the ResNet-50 architecture with the following parameters: a shuffle value of 1, image augmentation enabled via the imgaug framework, a maximum of 1,000 training iterations, checkpoints saved every 500 iterations, training progress displayed every 50 iterations, and a total of five recorded model snapshots. Upon completion of training, each original experimental video was analyzed using the trained DLC model. For DeepLabCut, pose estimation results for each experimental video were exported as CSV files and used for downstream post-processing and precision analysis (Figure 2C).

Facemap Refined methodology

Unlike SLEAP-based pipelines, Facemap is not designed to be trained from scratch on arbitrary datasets with user-defined labeling sizes. Instead, it relies on a fixed pretrained model followed by dataset-specific refinement using a limited number of manually labeled frames. Accordingly, we followed the recommended Facemap refinement protocol as described in the original publication and online Github page. A flowchart was created to illustrate the workflow and demonstrate how Facemap was applied in the analysis (Figures 2B and S1B). The basemodel in the Facemap code package was the pretrained model with their own dataset therefore it requires retrained with any new data. To improve accuracy of the basemodel (2400 images), we refined the Facemap Basemodel for each video (n = 90 videos) using the default graphical user interface (GUI) parameters: 15 images, 50% random frames, and a 95% difficulty threshold. A total of 11 key points were selected for labeling in Facemap per frame, including four eye locations, two nose points, two mouth points, and three whisker points. Four additional points (nose (R), nose (tip), nose (bridge), and paw) were excluded from the refinement process to maintain consistency with the DeepFaceMouse and DLC models. Once the model was refined and retrained, the updated Facemape Refined version was reapplied to the video, and the output h5 file was converted into CSV format for post-processing analysis using a MATLAB script (Figure S1B). The refinements were saved for each input model, allowing for progressive improvements in accuracy over time. This process was repeated for each video to enhance the model’s precision and reliability, which led to a total of 3750 images labeled for Facemap Refined. A custom MATLAB code was developed to convert the output.h5 file into.csv format, enabling the extraction of precision values, and x,y coordinates for each tracked point. The csv format files for Facemap Refined and Facemap Basemodel were used for brain activity prediction (Figure 3).

High performance computing (HPC) methodology

HPC was utilized for DeepFaceMouse analysis to process all videos in a high-throughput manner. This was achieved by running a single SLURM job, which leveraged parallel computing to accelerate inference speed (Figure 4B). A batch SLURM script was created and submitted to the HPC system to analyze all videos using SLEAP’s command-line interface. The analysis was conducted on NVIDIA A100 GPUs with the following configuration: two nodes, three GPUs per node, and 128 GB of memory per GPU.

The SLURM batch scripts used for HPC analysis are provided in the DeepFaceMouse GitHub repository (https://github.com/yyildirimlab/DeepFaceMouse) for reference and transparency. These scripts reflect the job submission workflow and computing environment used at the Cleveland Clinic Research. Because HPC infrastructures, scheduling systems, and resource management policies vary across institutions, HPC analysis workflows are not standardized. As such, the provided scripts are intended as reference implementations and may require modification to accommodate different institutional HPC systems, including adjustments to resource allocation, module loading, and environment configuration. The inclusion of these scripts is intended to illustrate how DeepFaceMouse analyses can be efficiently scaled in an HPC environment and to assist users in adapting the pipeline to their own computational resources.

Brain activity prediction using custom Deep Learning Model

We developed a MATLAB based GUI application to build and evaluate a neural network model that predicts brain signal at time (t) from mouse facial movement features. The primary analytical steps involve data acquisition, preprocessing and feature extraction, temporal alignment of modalities, neural network training, and comprehensive evaluation using multiple performance metrics.

Facial movements were recorded at 120 Hz using a custom-built imaging system and analyzed with either DeepFaceMouse or FaceMap software, producing CSV files containing the raw two-dimensional (x, y) coordinates of facial landmarks, including the eyes, nose, mouth, and whiskers. Concurrently, neural activity was recorded using hemodynamic-subtracted widefield calcium imaging from 11 cortical regions in both hemispheres. For GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f transgenic mouse lines, neural activity was sampled at 20 Hz. We initially applied the same 20 Hz sampling rate to jGCaMP8m mice; however, this temporal resolution proved insufficient to accurately capture the faster calcium dynamics characteristic of the jGCaMP8m indicator (Figure S6). To address this limitation, we implemented 100 Hz single-color blue imaging for jGCaMP8m mice, enabling accurate tracking of rapid brain activity changes. Because facial movements were recorded from the left side of the animal, we included activity from the 11 cortical regions in the right hemisphere for analysis. Each facial recording (CSV file) was systematically paired with its corresponding brain activity recording (.mat file) from the same experimental session, yielding matched face–brain data pairs that were used for subsequent model training and evaluation.

The raw facial coordinate data underwent multiple stages of preprocessing to extract meaningful spatial and dynamic features from each recorded frame (Figure S4). Spatial features were computed as follows: eye features were derived by calculating the centroid from the four points of the eyes; nose and mouth features were obtained by calculating the midpoint coordinates between their respective top and bottom landmarks. Whisker features were similarly computed as the centroid of four points for DeepFaceMouse or three points for Facemap recordings, calculated as the mean position across whisker coordinates. Following spatial feature extraction, velocity features were derived by computing the frame-to-frame spatial differences multiplied by the sampling rate (120 Hz), thereby capturing the dynamic aspects of facial movement over time. The resulting 12 spatial and 12 velocity features (11 velocity features in Facemap) were combined into a 24-dimensional raw feature vector per frame. Missing or invalid data points were subsequently removed and replaced using linear interpolation with end-extrapolation. For temporal dynamics, a sliding-window aggregation of five consecutive frames was performed. This reshaped each sequence of 5 consecutive 24-dimensional vectors into one flattened 120-dimentional vector.

Neural activity signals were extracted from hemodynamic-subtracted fluorescence signals for GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f and raw fluorescence signals for jGCaMP8m. These signals were band-passed filtered using a second-order Butterworth filter with cutoff frequencies at 0.1 and 9.99Hz for 20Hz imaging and 0.1 to 49.99 for 100Hz imaging to isolate physiologically relevant signal frequencies and to remove drifts. Filtering was performed in both forward and backward directions using MATLAB’s “filtfilt” function to ensure zero-phase distortion. Sequentially, signals were normalized by computing the ΔF/F. To reduce high-frequency fluctuations, ΔF/F signals were smoothed using a 1-s moving-average filter.

To enhance predictive power, we also added principal component (PCA) analysis across the 11 brain regions, and the first three principal components were retained. Derived brain features were linearly interpolated to exactly match the duration of the processed brain activity data. The first 5 s of data from both modalities were removed to eliminate initial recording transients and filter artifacts. Subsequentially, facial features (originally at 120Hz) were interpolated to match the brain activity timestamps (20 Hz or 100 Hz, depending on mouse strain). Derived PCA-based brain features were also interpolated to the brain timestamps. DeepFaceMouse model creation for PTEN and spontaneous data

A new DeepFaceMouse model was created to analyze the PTEN animals, as these data were collected within a similar time window and experimental context. Thirteen videos from thirteen animals were used for training, including six PTEN disease animals and seven wild-type controls (Spontaneous animals). The same methodology used to construct previous DeepFaceMouse models was applied to this dataset. Specifically, 2,300 frames were extracted per video using mediaGUI, resulting in a concatenated dataset of approximately 30,000 frames. Twenty images per video were manually labeled, and a U-Net model was trained using the default SLEAP GUI. Once training was completed, all 13 videos were analyzed using the custom-built sleapGUI Face pipeline. Precision values from the six PTEN animals were reported in Figure 5B, while precision values from the seven wild-type animals were reported in Figure 5D. The DeepFaceMouse local workflow shown in Figure 1A was used as a guide for model creation and data analysis.

DeepFaceMouse model creation for cuprizone data

A separate DeepFaceMouse model was created to analyze the cuprizone animals. This dataset consisted of nine animals, each undergoing three experimental weeks. After data collection, a new DeepFaceMouse model was developed following the local DeepFaceMouse workflow outlined in Figure 1A. For training, 1,110 images were concatenated per video to achieve a total of approximately 30,000 frames. Twenty images per video were manually labeled, and a U-Net model was trained within the SLEAP environment. Once training was complete, all 27 videos were analyzed using the custom SLEAP GUI Face pipeline. Precision results were plotted for each experiment (Figure S7B) as well as an overall average for each animal’s experiment (Figure 5C). The DeepFaceMouse local workflow shown in Figure 1A was also used as a guide for model creation and data analysis.

DeepFaceMouse model creation for social interaction experiments

A completely new DeepFaceMouse model was developed for social interaction experiments because two animals interacted simultaneously, requiring the face features of both animals to be tracked. To accommodate this, six additional keypoints were added to the visitor mouse (four whisker points and two nose points), while the same twelve keypoints were retained for tracking the resident mouse. Facemap could not be used for this experiment, as it is unable to reliably track key facial feature movements from two interacting animals simultaneously. 13 animals were used in the training set, with each animal undergoing three experimental days. A total of 39 videos were used to construct the social interaction model by concatenating 770 frames per video. 20 images per video were manually labeled, and the model was trained within the SLEAP environment. A custom SLEAP GUI Social Interaction environment was developed to enable analysis using the 18-keypoint model. Precision results were plotted for each experiment involving wildtype visitors (Figure S8A) and mutant visitors (Figure S8B), as well as an overall average for each animal’s experiment with mutant and wild-type visitors (Figures 5F and 5G, respectively).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Model precision across transgenic lines was evaluated by benchmarking precision values for each of five mice (M1–M5) across six experimental days and three transgenic lines (GCaMP6s, GCaMP6f, and jGCaMP8m) (Figure 2C). Precision values were first averaged within each animal, yielding five independent precision values per model for each transgenic line. Statistical comparisons were then performed across animals using paired two-tailed t-tests, comparing DeepFaceMouse against the Facemap Basemodel, Facemap Refined, and DeepLabCut within each genotype. DeepFaceMouse consistently achieved significantly higher precision than competing models, outperforming Facemap Refined, DeepLabCut, and the Basemodel in GCaMP6s mice (p < 0.05 vs. Refined; p < 0.0001 vs. DeepLabCut and Base), GCaMP6f mice (p < 0.001 vs. Refined; p < 0.0001 vs. DeepLabCut and Base), and jGCaMP8m mice (p < 0.001 vs. Refined and Base; p < 0.0001 vs. DeepLabCut). Aggregated analysis across all transgenic lines further demonstrated the robustness and generalizability of DeepFaceMouse.

To determine the effect of training dataset size on brain activity prediction performance (Figure 3F, left), prediction accuracy was quantified using the coefficient of determination (R2) across 11 brain regions and 5 animals, yielding 55 matched prediction values per condition. DeepFaceMouse models trained on 1,800 images were compared against models trained on 180 and 900 images using paired two-tailed t-tests, as predictions were generated from identical animals, brain regions, and testing videos across conditions. This analysis revealed a highly significant improvement in prediction accuracy when using 1,800 training images relative to either 180 or 900 images (p < 0.0001 for both comparisons), demonstrating that 1,800 images (20 images per video) provide optimal training performance for the DeepFaceMouse model.

For the region-specific comparison (Figure 3F, right), a two-way ANOVA was performed to directly assess differences in predictive performance between DeepFaceMouse and Facemap Refined across grouped brain regions. Each data point represents the average R2 value per animal (n = 5) for a grouped cortical region: Motor cortex (average of MOp and MOs), retrosplenial cortex (average of RSPagl, RSPd, and RSPv), primary somatosensory cortex (average of SSP_ll, SSP_tr, and SSP_ul), and visual cortex (average of VISa, VISm, and VISpm). This analysis revealed significantly higher predictive performance for DeepFaceMouse in the Motor and retrosplenial regions (p < 0.0001), as well as moderately higher performance in the somatosensory and visual regions (p < 0.001), compared to Facemap Refined.

Cross-line model generalization (Figure 3G) was assessed by comparing brain activity prediction accuracy across all transgenic lines using R2 values averaged across five experiments for each brain region, yielding 11 data points per condition. Paired two-tailed t-tests were used to compare DeepFaceMouse with Facemap Refined and Facemap Basemodel within each genotype. DeepFaceMouse consistently outperformed competing models across all conditions, demonstrating significantly higher prediction accuracy in GCaMP6s mice (p < 0.0001 vs. Refined and Base), GCaMP6f mice (p < 0.05 vs. Refined; p < 0.0001 vs. Base), and jGCaMP8m mice (p < 0.001 vs. Refined; p < 0.0001 vs. Base). These results demonstrate the strong generalization capacity and robustness of DeepFaceMouse across genetically distinct datasets.

Additional resources

The DeepFaceMouse GitHub repository provides a comprehensive, end-to-end framework for high-precision tracking of orofacial dynamics in head-fixed mice and for linking these behaviors to large-scale brain activity. The repository includes optimized pipelines for data preprocessing, model training, and high-throughput inference built on pose-estimation backbones (e.g., SLEAP), alongside custom wrappers that enable scalable analysis across thousands of videos using HPC resources. It also contains scripts for extracting and organizing facial keypoints (e.g., whiskers, nose, mouth, eyelids), integrating temporal features, and performing downstream analyses such as predicting cortex-wide neural activity from behavior. In addition, the repository provides example datasets, configuration files, and reproducible workflows for running DeepFaceMouse across diverse behavioral paradigms (spontaneous, virtual reality, and social interaction) and disease models (e.g., GBM, ASD, MS). Together, these resources make the platform a robust and extensible tool for studying brain–behavior relationships with high temporal precision and scalability.
