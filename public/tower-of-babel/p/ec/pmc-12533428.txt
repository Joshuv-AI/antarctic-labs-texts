Integrated vector and parasite management for malaria control in high-endemic areas: pummel and pin strategy to prevent malaria setbacks
Authors: Mansour Ranjbar, Jimmy Opigo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRebounding to a higher malaria endemicity level or remaining at the same level despite implementation of malaria control interventions has been reported in high-endemic areas of many sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) countries. Based on current trends, the 2030 targets of the World Health Organization, which aim for at least a 90% reduction in malaria mortality and morbidity, are out of reach. Moreover, the experiences of many countries in SSA show that the implementation of various packages of malaria control interventions in areas of low to moderate transmission can achieve a significant reduction in the disease burden but ultimately fail to interrupt malaria transmission. Multiple factors may contribute to the stagnation or even reversal of the declining trend of malaria endemicity intensity in endemic countries across SSA. Notably, high levels of receptivity and vulnerability can hinder the sustainability of achieved reductions in malaria transmission. To address these challenges, based on evidence from the literature focusing on Uganda, this article presents a novel approach- the Integrated Vector and Parasite Management (IVPM)- for endemicity reduction and maintaining hard-earned gains following reduction of malaria endemicity intensity in high-endemic areas. In line with IVPM, the article also proposes the “Pummel and Pin” strategy, outlining an implementation framework for IVPM. The IVPM Pummel-and-Pin model emphasizes the need to reduce both the parasite reservoir and vectorial capacity, highlighting the often-neglected Pin strategy as essential to sustaining achieved reductions in endemicity without any lag. It may offer a practical solution to prevent setbacks and accelerate progress towards achieving the national malaria targets on burden reduction in Uganda and other SSA countries. However, assessing its feasibility and cost-effectiveness across different endemicity levels remains a priority for operational research, particularly since IVPM is resource-driven and malaria control programmes worldwide have faced growing financial deficits. Operational research aimed at reducing malaria endemicity in high-transmission areas of sub-Saharan African countries needs to cover two key questions: how to reduce transmission intensity and how to sustain those reductions.Keywords: IVPM, IVM, aEIR, High endemic, Rebound malaria, Malaria outbreak, Receptivity, Vulnerability, Malaria transmission

Background

In 2023, Uganda was the third-largest contributor to global malaria burden, accounting for approximately 5% of malaria cases and 3% of malaria deaths worldwide[1]. Since 2009, Uganda has made considerable progress in the fight against malaria. Malaria prevalence declined by 80%, from 42% in 2009 to 9% in 2019 [2]; however, analysis of data from the Uganda Health Information Management System (HMIS) reveals that major outbreaks occurred in 2015/2016, 2019, and 2022 [3]. This demonstrates that the path to elimination is not linear, and rebounds to previous or even higher levels of endemicity are common in the country. Moreover, in Uganda, some districts, mainly in the southeast or highland regions, have been classified as low or very low malaria endemic areas for over a decade; however, malaria transmission has never been fully interrupted.

The phenomena of rebound to higher malaria endemicity levels or remaining at the same level despite malaria control efforts has also been reported in other African countries [2]. Since 2015, the rate of progress in decreasing prevalence of both cases and deaths has stopped in several countries with moderate or high malaria transmission in African region of the World Health Organization (WHO) [4, 5]. Globally, a 40% reduction in malaria case incidence by 2020 compared to 2015 was targeted; however, only a 3% reduction was achieved, falling 37% short of the goal [5]. This is not limited to high malaria endemic areas; the experiences of many countries in in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) show that implementation of various packages of malaria control interventions in areas of low to moderate transmission can achieve a significant reduction in the disease burden but ultimately fail to interrupt malaria transmission [6]. Some studies claimed that using current tools, malaria elimination in SSA will not be achievable by 2050 [7, 8]. Based on current trends, the 2030 WHO global targets for malaria mortality and morbidity and the interim milestones are out of reach [5].

Many reasons may contribute to stepping back or no progress towards malaria elimination in Uganda and other endemic countries in SSA, such as growing human population, climate change, humanitarian emergencies, the emergence of biological threats, changes in vector biology, and inadequate coverage of the interventions, mainly because of insufficient financial resources [5]. However, an important question is how the effectiveness of the current package of interventions can be enhanced to accelerate reduction of malaria endemicity in high-endemic areas to support progress towards a Malaria Free Uganda. This article aims to review the available evidence in the literature on the drivers of malaria transmission, with a focus on the concepts of receptivity and vulnerability, as well as the impact of different packages of interventions across various endemicity settings. Based on the review, the article introduces a practical new model for achieving sustainable reduction of malaria endemicity in high-endemic areas of Uganda.

Epidemiology of high transmission areas in Uganda

High levels of receptivity and vulnerability in Uganda, among other reasons, can be considered as contributing factors to the unsustainability of achievements and stopping progress toward interruption of transmission. The epidemiology of malaria in Uganda, varies widely from highlands with low prevalence to areas with the highest recorded malaria transmission intensities in the world, where elimination and even advances in control will be most challenging [9].

The Annual entomological inoculation rate (aEIR) is often considered the ‘gold standard’ for measuring malaria transmission intensity and defined as the number of infective bites per person per year [10, 11]. The authors believe that aEIR can serve as a criterion indicating the likelihood of sustained malaria transmission and the potential risk of resurgence following the cessation of malaria control interventions. As presented in Table 1 reported aEIR in Uganda ranges from 4 in the southwestern part of the country to > 1500 in a swampy area near the Nile River [12]. It can be concluded that people living in high-endemic areas may receive an average of one to two infective mosquito bites per person per day, which is very high.

aEIR depends on the interaction of receptivity and vulnerability. Receptivity means the extent to which an ecosystem allows the transmission of malaria from one human to another human. Vulnerability means, the likelihood of a population being infected with malaria, either proximity to a malarious area or frequent influx of infected individuals or groups and/or infective anophelines [15, 16]. This article explores the concepts of receptivity and vulnerability as the foundation for analysis.

In Uganda, the level of receptivity to malaria transmission is high across nearly all regions throughout the year, due to favorable environmental conditions such as abundant breeding sites, suitable temperature and humidity, and a high density and variability of efficient vectors. Additionally, the country exhibits a high level of vulnerability—including in low endemic areas—due to significant human and vector malaria parasite reservoirs.

Human parasite reservoirs can be viewed as the main portion of vulnerability, given the human population serves as the primary and larger reservoir of malaria parasites [17]. Malaria infections comprise both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases, with parasitaemia that may be microscopic or submicroscopic. This reservoir is often likened to an iceberg—where the visible tip represents symptomatic cases detectable by microscopy, who seek care and are reported in the health system. The larger, hidden portion includes asymptomatic individuals and those with submicroscopic parasitaemia, who often remain untreated and overlooked (Fig. 1).

Distinct groups of human malaria parasite reservoirs. a Symptomatic cases who seek treatment and are detectable by RDT or microscopy have a high likelihood of receiving effective treatment. Once treated successfully, they no longer contribute to the parasite reservoir. b Symptomatic cases who seek treatment but are not detectable by RDT or microscopy due to low parasitaemia may receive a false-negative result. As a result, they might not receive appropriate treatment and could continue to contribute to transmission over an extended period. c and (d) Asymptomatic cases do not seek treatment and can remain infectious for a long time, serving as a persistent source of transmission. Group (d), in particular, is undetectable by RDT or microscopy due to low parasitaemia

Research shows that all infected individuals—including those with submicroscopic, asymptomatic infections—can carry mature gametocytes and contribute to transmission [18], with some studies suggesting asymptomatic cases play a greater role than symptomatic ones in malaria transmission [17, 19, 20].

Literature review in many countries indicates that approximately 20% of infections are submicroscopic in areas of high transmission intensity, but submicroscopic rate may increase to 70–80% in low transmission settings. Additionally, over 75% of infections detected during community surveys mainly in high endemic areas are asymptomatic, with many persisting for several months at varying parasite densities while remaining without symptoms [17]. Moreover, a known fact has been observed in some highly endemic countries, indicating that infections are more likely to be submicroscopic in older children and adults compared to young children [21].

In Uganda, literature consistently shows high prevalence of malaria infections and confirms that asymptomatic and submicroscopic cases constitute the majority of malaria infections, although reported figures vary depending on the endemicity of the study area, transmission seasonality, sample size, and the sensitivity and specificity of diagnostic methods used. In one study, asymptomatic infection prevalence has been reported around 34.7%, with the highest rates among children aged 5–10 years (45.9%) [22]. Additionally, a high rate of asymptomatic malaria among school children has been identified as a potential reservoir contributing to ongoing transmission [20, 23].

Studies in Uganda have also highlighted a significant reservoir of malaria parasites that often go undetected by routine diagnostic tools such as rapid diagnostic rests (RDTs) and microscopy, due to their limited ability to detect low parasite densities. One study reported that both tests failed to detect over 40% of infections that were identified using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) [23]. The combined prevalence of microscopic and submicroscopic parasitaemia has been found to be more than five times higher than microscopic parasitaemia alone [2] and submicroscopic parasitaemia have been detected in 25.8% of children and 39.2% of adults in Uganda [24]. Additionally, in one study in Uganda the proportion of low, moderate, and high parasitaemia among malaria infections has been reported at 46.8%, 28.0%, and 25.2%, respectively [25]. This means that diagnosing approximately 46.8% of malaria cases with low parasitemia using microscopy and conventional RDTs will be challenging. It should be noted that submicroscopic parasitaemia is particularly common in Plasmodium malariae, and to some extent in Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium ovale spp. [26]. Mono-infections of non-falciparum malaria species are often underdiagnosed and untreated for two key reasons: the widespread use of RDTs, which only detect Plasmodium falciparum, and the low sensitivity of microscopy in identifying mono-infections of non-falciparum species [26].Therefore we can conclude that non falciparum malaria species can contribute to increasing trend of submicroscopic parasite reservoirs in Uganda. These findings underscore the need to address submicroscopic parasitaemia and asymptomatic cases in high-transmission settings to achieve meaningful progress in malaria control and eventual elimination [27].

Impact of routine malaria control interventions on reducing endemicity intensity in Uganda

Current malaria control interventions in Uganda have not significantly reduced receptivity and vulnerability to a level that could interrupt transmission or maintain the transition of high-endemic areas to low endemicity, once achieved. The focus of current malaria control interventions is on receptivity reduction rather than vulnerability reduction. Vector control interventions, preventive chemotherapy supported by social mobilization and behavior change communication (BCC), are key interventions to prevent malaria in Uganda [28]. In 2025, to reduce malaria mortality, the malaria vaccine for children 6–18 months was introduced in 105 districts classified as moderate, high, very high.

Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) and long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) are the primary interventions aimed at reducing receptivity to malaria transmission in the country. Numerous studies in many countries have confirmed the positive impact of IRS on reducing vector density, particularly when coverage reaches at least 80% of the population [29]. Similarly, the effectiveness of LLINs in reducing malaria burden has been demonstrated in multiple studies [30]. The impact of IRS on reducing receptivity was reported to be significantly greater than that of LLINs [31].

However, in high-endemic areas, the impact of vector control intervention either IRS or LLINs on receptivity remains limited, has not resulted in transmission interruption [32], and the duration of IRS effectiveness on receptivity is also short-lived. These interventions primarily target indoor biting and resting mosquitoes, leaving outdoor vectors largely unaddressed. Additionally, insecticide resistance across multiple classes has been reported in various parts of the country [33]. Insecticide resistance may reduce the effectiveness of LLINs and IRS, highlighting the need to shift to new products that are resource intensive. Some studies have shown that the effectiveness of LLINs and IRS on primary malaria vectors (Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto, Anopheles. arabiensis, and Anopheles funestus) may be influenced negatively by new behavioural traits of these mosquito populations [34]. In many areas of Uganda, an unusually early evening biting peak has been observed, which negatively impacts the protective effectiveness of LLINs [35]. Furthermore, low LLIN usage rates [36], and negative community attitudes toward IRS that can result in low coverage may undermine the effectiveness of these interventions.

Studies indicated that climatic factors, especially temperature, rainfall, and humidity, are critical drivers of malaria transmission and increased rainfall and humidity are positively associated with the incidence of malaria in Uganda [37]. Therefore, changes in environmental conditions—such as humidity, rainfall patterns, or temperature—as well as shifts in vector bionomics and species composition that favor transmission, may lead to decreasing impacts of vector control interventions. As a result, vectorial capacity—the key indicator of receptivity—may remain at a significant level and even after routine vector control and malaria transmission dose not reach zero [38]. Similar phenomena have been reported in many areas of Africa [39]. Switching in malaria vectors’ biting behaviour after the implementation of LLIN at universal coverage has been reported in the literature [40].

Besides, current transmission control measures focus only on vector control and fail to significantly reduce parasite reservoirs—particularly asymptomatic cases—therefore vulnerability levels remain largely unchanged providing a potential reservoir for malaria transmission (Fig. 2). Consequently, while vector control interventions can temporarily lower receptivity, endemicity is likely to rebound to previous or even higher levels once vector control efficacy declines or withdraws. This is because Uganda's environment is highly conducive to mosquito breeding, therefore the mosquito density grows up quickly and considering the available parasite reservoirs newly emerged mosquitoes become infected in short period.

This scenario has happened frequently in Uganda and in high-endemic areas of many SSA countries [2]. Therefore, while relying on vector control plus passive case management services may temporarily reduce malaria burden, it does not have a sustainable impact on transmission dynamics or the level of endemicity. To significantly reduce endemicity levels in these settings, many studies and published literature have emphasized the need for parasite reservoir reduction measures as complementary interventions to vector control [31, 35, 41–44].

The key question is: what the impact would be if the programme in high-endemic areas focused on reducing vulnerability to lower transmission intensity? The authors reviewed literature to explore experiences of other countries on parasite reservoir reduction.

Literature evidence on impact of interventions focusing on vulnerability level reduction

Mass drug administration (MDA), mass testing and treatment (MTaT), targeted testing and treatment (TTaT), reactive case detection and treatment (RACDT) [10, 45] are among common interventions that actively target human parasite reservoir reduction Table 2. MDA has been used for malaria control and elimination for over a century [46]. The interventions specifically targeting the reduction of vulnerability—are currently not part of Uganda’s national malaria control strategy.

MDA is the most effective intervention targeting the parasite reservoir among the items listed in Table 2, as it covers both asymptomatic and submicroscopic cases that other methods cannot. Besides, fMDA does not cover the entire population within the targeted focus, resulting in a lower impact compared to MDA [39, 49, 51]. Therefore, the authors in the literature review focused on the impact of MDA in reducing, interrupting, and sustaining reductions in endemicity in high-endemic areas.

Evidence in the literature revealed that the duration of the impact of MDA depends on local malaria epidemiology [52]. Many studies targeting transmission interruption have reported significant reductions in parasite reservoirs following MDA implementation [53], however MDA alone is insufficient to interrupt transmission in many settings. In the absence of concurrent vector control, conducting MDA in high-transmission settings may lead only to short-term reductions in endemicity, with a rapid rebound expected once the chemoprophylactic effect of the drug diminishes [54]. According to WHO malaria guidelines, MDA can rapidly reduce clinical malaria incidence in areas with moderate to high P. falciparum transmission; however, this effect typically wanes within 1–3 months [45]. WHO recommends that MDA in areas of moderate to high transmission of P. falciparum may be applicable to provide short-term reductions in disease burden but to reduce transmission MDA is not recommended. In areas with very low to low levels of P. falciparum transmission, MDA can be given to reduce transmission [45].

Focusing solely on reducing vulnerability—without effective complementary vector control—leaves receptivity high, ultimately leading to unsustainable outcomes (Fig. 3). Vectors already infected prior to MDA can continue transmission once the prophylactic effect of the drug wanes. Furthermore, a large vector population remains in the environment, some of which may become infected through existing reservoirs, such as untreated individuals not covered by MDA or imported malaria cases.

Integrated vector and parasite management (IVPM)

Given complexity of malaria transmission dynamics in Uganda; high level of receptivity and vulnerability levels in high endemic areas, and considering evidence from the literature, to reduce significantly malaria transmission intensity and maintaining hard-earned gains, the authors propose, malaria control interventions should be designed and implemented based on Integrated Vector and Parasite Management (IVPM) approach in which both receptivity and vulnerability levels should be targeted simultaneously. Accordingly Pummel and Pin Model based on comprehensive, simultaneous, continuous, smart principles (CSCS) has been proposed (Fig. 4).

IVPM, Pummel and Pin model

In the first phase called “Pummel”, the IVPM approach aims to reduce rapidly malaria transmission intensity to below a critical tipping point by simultaneously targeting both receptivity and vulnerability through a comprehensive set of population-wide interventions. Although a combination of MDA plus IRS seems a good choice for “Pummel” phase, set of the package of interventions should be selected based on robust evidence, their feasibility and their pros and cons. To reduce receptivity levels as much as possible —where applicable and feasible based on local conditions and considering WHO guidelines—a combination of vector control interventions should be implemented (Fig. 5).

Impact of IVPM, Pummel and Pin model on endemicity reduction and prevention of rebound. This figure illustrates the impact of the Pummel and Pin interventions on receptivity and vulnerability levels. The Pummel phase targets both receptivity and vulnerability through intensive vector control and parasite reservoirs reduction (e.g. MDA). In contrast, the Pin phase focuses primarily on reducing vulnerability, mainly through prompt treatment of malaria cases and targeted vector control. As a result, we observe a slight increase in receptivity during the Pin phase compared to the Pummel phase. However, sustained reduction in vulnerability during the Pin phase helps maintain the gains achieved, preventing a rebound in transmission

In the next phase called Pin, the IVPM approach mainly aims to maintain the achieved lower level of endemicity and to further reduce it gradually through targeted continuous, smart interventions. Targeted vulnerability reduction plus focused endemicity reduction interventions (if needed) are components of set of Pin phase. Early detection and initiation of effective treatment within 24 h of fever onset is one of the key interventions in the Pin phase, aimed at reducing vulnerability. Therefore, strengthening the health system to effectively deliver vulnerability reduction interventions through expanding free of charge community-based malaria case management for all age groups using RDTs and enhancing primary health care services are prerequisite of Pummel and Pin model. The set of interventions in both phases of Pummel and Pin should be evidence-based, cost-effective and tailored to local, albeit dynamic, conditions.

Time is crucial in the Pummel and Pin model; malaria must not be given any opportunity to resurge. Rapidly reducing the EIR as early as possible in Pummel phase, followed immediately by the implementation of Pin interventions to actively preserve these gains, is essential.

Based on endemicity level and impact of interventions, more than one round of Pummel phase may be needed to deduct transmission intensity at low or very low levels. In this case Pin set of interventions should be conducted between cycles of Pummel phases. Pummel phase is short term, but Pin phase is long term. Pin set of interventions will be regularly modified based on endemicity level and risk of rebound; however, in the context of Uganda, it may need to be continued for a long period of time (Fig. 6).

Timeline of Pummel and Pin phases. The figure shows the sequential arrangement of the Pummel and Pin phases. In the same year, Pin interventions begin immediately after the conclusion of the Pummel phase

High coverage of interventions planned in “Pummel” and “Pin” phases is essential to achieve and maintain transmission intensity reduction. Therefore, community engagement is crucial to achieve high coverage as well as to address a lack of knowledge among target populations as a major reason for low uptake of malaria protective tools [55–57].

When endemicity level is reduced to below API < 250/1000, foci classification can be used as a basis for identifying hot spots and high-risk populations for targeted intervention during Pin phase [58].

Evidence from the literature supporting IVPM approach and Pummel and Pin model

Necessity of targeting both receptivity and vulnerability in the literature

Comprehensive interventions to cover both receptivity and vulnerability have been supported by literature.

IVPM approach has been built based on synergy philosophy. Synergy in malaria control refers to the interaction of two or more interventions that together produce a greater impact than the sum of their individual effects. Such synergies have the potential to enhance health outcomes per dollar spent, making malaria control strategies more cost-effective and helping programmes save resources [31]. A systematic review in malaria control interventions in low- and middle-income countries showed that use of multiple malaria prevention methods was effective in reducing malaria infection and mosquito density in comparison with single methods [59].

Many studies suggested that combination of interventions targeting both receptivity and vulnerability is needed in high-endemic areas [2] and combination of interventions targeting both vector and parasite reservoirs can achieve large reductions in disease burden which is a necessary step in the pathway towards elimination [6, 31, 60]. A study conducted in Uganda showed that MDA plus IRS provided significant additional protection from malaria infection over IRS alone [60]. In Uganda, a combination of MDA and IRS was implemented during the 1950s and 1960s, which resulted in interruption of transmission in highlands [31]. Many studies have focused on the combination of IRS and MDA; however, the combination of MDA and LLINs has also been piloted, although much less commonly [31], Both scenarios showed a positive impact on reducing transmission intensity, but the combination of IRS and MDA was found to be more effective.

Importance of combination of vector control interventions in the literature

Combination of vector control interventions based on synergy principle to reduce receptivity has been suggested in the literature [59]. Additionally, the WHO recommends an integrated vector management (IVM) approach for designing and implementing vector control interventions [61]. Larval source management (LSM) may serve as a useful supplement to core interventions to get better results on endemicity reduction. Based on WHO guidelines it is applicable only in specific settings where breeding sites are few, fixed, and findable, and where the density of the population to be protected is high enough to justify the required resources [62]. WHO also doesn’t recommend co-deployment of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and IRS for the prevention and control of malaria in children and adults in areas with ongoing transmission. [45].

Importance of the treatment of asymptomatic cases in the literature

The necessity of treating asymptomatic malaria cases has been highlighted in the literature. From an individual health perspective, although asymptomatic and submicroscopic infections may not present with fever, they can still cause subtle chronic effects—such as anaemia—that require treatment [27, 63]. From the public health perspective, accurately identifying and targeting the human reservoir of malaria parasitaemia is recognized as a critical step in reducing malaria burden in both low- and high-transmission settings [27].

MDA pros and cons in the literature

There are ongoing controversies regarding the pros and cons of MDA. While some studies have raised concerns about its potential role in accelerating parasite resistance [44], other research suggests no direct link between MDA and drug resistance [64]. Still, some experts argue that, if implemented with high coverage (over 80%) and using effective medication capable of eliminating the parasite, MDA could help prevent or slow the spread of resistance [64]. Some argue that in highly infected communities already under chemical pressure from frequent case management, MDA or MSAT may not significantly increase overall Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT) use but rather reorganize its distribution across time and population groups, instead of long-term deployment [31]. Some argue that in highly infected communities already under antimalarial drug pressure due to frequent treatment, MDA or MSAT may not significantly increase overall Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT) use or intensify drug pressure but may instead redistribute its use across time and population groups [31]. The literature suggests that MDA improves access to treatment for symptomatic malaria cases and helps clear infections across the broader population [39]. Besides MDA treat infected cases and offers all those covered a limited prophylactic period of perhaps 2–4 weeks depending on the anti-malarial used [31].

Importance of tailor-made interventions based on local conditions in the literature

Based on the literature, the set of interventions should be adjusted based on the local situation and feasibility. An appropriate and cost-effective parasite reservoir reduction strategy should be selected based on the programme goal (control or elimination), endemicity level, and feasibility, including community acceptance [10].

Importance of concurrent interventions in the literature

Synchronous deployment of interventions targeting both parasite reservoir and vectorial capacity has been highlighted in the literature [31]. As both the IRS and MDA campaigns exert control over transmission differently, and with different durations, the synergy of their combination is dependent on their relative times of deployment [31].

Importance of early detection and treatment strategy in the literature

The importance of having good access to case management and a strong surveillance system—as prerequisites for combining MDA with vector control interventions and as key strategies for sustaining progress toward elimination—has been highlighted in several studies [46]. One study found that after MDA, the degree of access to prompt diagnosis and treatment and to effective vector control in the area, can determine rebound to previous levels of incidence and prevalence [44].

Literature highlighted the importance of early detection and prompt effective treatment as a fundamental strategy of malaria control/elimination programmes in all endemicity settings- including in high-endemic areas. This approach not only contributes to the reduction of the EIR but also directly and indirectly reduces malaria mortality [26]. Even in areas with low levels of immunity, prompt treatment of malaria cases has been shown to decrease the incidence of severe disease and mortality [2, 26]. Moreover, a study indicated that increasing treatment coverage in low and moderate-transmission settings leads to significant reductions in both malaria incidence and mortality rates [65]. Early detection and treatment also play a crucial role in lowering malaria endemicity, particularly in regions affected by P. falciparum, as it typically takes approximately two weeks for new P. falciparum infections to develop mature gametocytes capable of sustaining transmission [17].

The WHO established that timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment of malaria should occur within one day of the onset of malaria symptoms [66]. Forty one percent of the children under age five years delayed receiving prompt malaria treatment beyond the recommended 24 h [67].

Importance of community-based case management for all age groups (CMCM) in the literature

In 2010, the Ugandan Ministry of Health launched the community health worker (CHW)-delivered the integrated community case management (iCCM) programme to tackle malaria, pneumonia and diarrhea among the under-five (U5) children [68], however many studies revealed that the burden of malaria from has been shifted from younger to older individuals [69]. This supports community-based case management for all age groups. Involving community-based malaria workers is ever more critical to ensure the elimination efforts engage hard-to-reach populations in remote areas [70].

Pummel and Pin in the literature

Literature review showed that the Pummel approach has been examined in high endemic areas for rapid reduction of endemicity however, Pin approach to sustain achievement is a big defect of current studies. The authors found only one study covering both Pummel and Pin approaches; however, there is no information regarding coverage and effective coverage of case management services [6] (Table 3).

IVPM was partially examined in a study conducted in Katakwi District, Uganda, during 2016–2018. In that study, one round of MDA was implemented alongside IRS [60]. IVPM approach suggests that if both MDA and IRS are applicable, two rounds of MDA should be conducted in conjunction with IRS: the first round concurrently with IRS, and the second round 6–8 weeks later. This timing is intended to cover the full human-to-human transmission cycle—encompassing the incubation interval and pre-patent period of secondary cases [71]. For P. falciparum malaria, the incubation interval is estimated at 36 days (comprising 12 days for the extrinsic cycle in mosquitoes, 10 days for the pre-patent period in humans, and an additional 14 days for the development of infectivity). Thus, 36 + 10 = 46 days (approximately 6 weeks), and including the further development of gametocytes in secondary cases yields 46 + 14 = 60 days (around 8 weeks).

Another key limitation of this study, as well as the studies listed in Table 3, was the absence of the Pin phase and its key interventions including prompt treatment strategy following the Pummel phase. These two components are often missing in studies aiming to reduce endemicity in high-transmission areas, despite being critical elements of the IVPM model. Additionally, some studies relied on LLINs combined with MDA under “Pummel phase” without monitoring LLIN utilization, which is essential. Moreover, while LLINs may be suitable for the Pin phase, IRS is preferable—where applicable—for reducing transmission during the Pummel phase.

Furthermore, the Pummel phase seems similar to the “attack” phase, and the Pin phase like “consolidation” phase of the malaria eradication programme conducted in the 1950s–60s. However, as elaborated in Fig. 6, the IVPM model stresses the implementation of the Pin strategy in the same year as the Pummel interventions. In contrast, the historical eradication programmes maintained the attack phase for up to four years or more, only transitioning to the consolidation phase after incidence had dropped below 0.1 per 1,000. Notably, the attack phase focused on vector control and reduction of the human parasite reservoir, though the latter was not mandatory. Chemotherapy was recommended as an essential supplementary intervention, especially if transmission persisted despite vector control [72]. However, in IVPM model parasite reservoir reduction is an essential element.

Additionally, Pummel phase of the model shows conceptual overlap with the “Scaling-Up for Impact” (SUFI) approach advocated by the Roll Back Malaria (RBM) Partnership. SUFI emphasizes rapidly scaling up both preventive and therapeutic interventions and sustaining their impact over time. Its goal is to achieve universal coverage of all at-risk populations with appropriate interventions (e.g., LLINs, IRS, IPTp, antimalarial drugs, and diagnostics), supported by strong health systems [73–75]. While SUFI aligns with the Pummel strategy in terms of scaling up interventions, it does not fully encompass the IVPM approach. Specifically, it does not simultaneously address both endemicity and vulnerability reduction. Moreover, the Pin strategy, including the emphasis on prompt treatment of malaria cases within 24 h, is absent in the SUFI framework. In Zambia, the SUFI approach contributed to a 63% reduction in P. falciparum prevalence. However, the findings also emphasized that malaria elimination requires interrupting both mosquito-to-human and human-to-mosquito transmission [41, 76].

Conclusion

High-endemic areas in Uganda and other SSA countries have struggled to maintain reduced endemicity levels and reach pre-elimination levels using current interventions. The current set of interventions is effective in temporarily reducing the disease burden; however, due to high levels of receptivity and vulnerability, achieving a sustained reduction in endemicity remains unattainable.

Drawing on evidence from the literature, this article presents the Integrated Vector and Parasite Management (IVPM) approach as a foundational strategy for reducing malaria endemicity in high-transmission tropical areas and preserving hard-earned gains. The IVPM Pummel-and-Pin model emphasizes the rapid reduction of both the parasite reservoir—including symptomatic, asymptomatic, and submicroscopic cases—and vectorial capacity. It also highlights the often-overlooked Pin strategy as essential for sustaining reductions in endemicity that should be implemented without delay after Pummel phase.

A key focus of the Pin phase is the prompt treatment of malaria cases, which not only reduces malaria mortality and the incidence of severe disease but also contributes significantly to further reducing endemicity and maintaining achieved gains.

The IVPM, Pummel, and Pin model is informed by studies conducted in Uganda and other high-endemic countries, and it builds on lessons learned from past eradication programme and the SUFI initiative.

IVPM may offer a practical solution to prevent setbacks and accelerate progress towards achieving the global malaria targets in Uganda and other SSA countries. However, assessing its feasibility and cost-effectiveness across different endemicity levels remains a priority for operational research, particularly since it is resource-driven and malaria control programmes worldwide have faced growing financial deficits. Piloting the IVPM, Pummel and Pin model—using different sets of interventions in districts with varying endemicity levels in Uganda and other SSA countries is recommended.

Best practices from the pilot sites can be scaled up, guided by feasibility and cost-effectiveness analysis results. Operational research aimed at reducing malaria endemicity in high-transmission areas of SSA countries should also address the ‘Pin’ phase, focusing on two key questions: how to reduce transmission intensity and how to sustain those reductions.

Annual entomological inoculation rate (aIER)

Annual Parasite Incidence (API)

Receptivity

The extent to which an ecosystem allows the transmission of malaria from one human to another human

Vulnerability

The likelihood of a population being infected with malaria. Either proximity to a malarious area or frequent influx of infected individuals or groups and/or infective anophelines.
