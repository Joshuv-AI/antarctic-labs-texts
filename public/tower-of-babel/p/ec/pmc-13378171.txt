Genomic characterization of an extensively drug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae co-harboring mcr-3.11, blaNDM-5 and blaCTX-M-27 isolated from pelvic effusion in a colon cancer patient
Authors: Kaixuan Zhao, Wei Wang, Ming Ma, Junhua Feng, Tianyu Qi, Jiajie Wang, Jing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize the genomic features and possible transmission mechanisms of an extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP2024). The isolate was recovered from pelvic effusion of a postoperative colon cancer patient in Hebei, China, with a focus on the rare mcr-3.11 gene as well as blaNDM-5 and blaCTX-M-27.ResultsGenetic analysis of key resistance determinants identified three epidemiologically important plasmids: an IncFIB plasmid carrying blaCTX-M-27 (pKP2024-1), an IncFII plasmid carrying mcr-3.11 (pKP2024-3), and an IncX3 plasmid carrying blaNDM-5 (pKP2024-4). Comparative genomic analysis indicated that blaCTX-M-27 was located within a highly conserved transposition unit mediated by ISEcp1. Additionally, mcr-3.11 and diacylglycerol kinase (dgkA) formed a conserved mobile genetic element, while blaNDM-5 was located within a typical Tn3-IS3000-IS5-blaNDM-5-bleMBL-trpF-IS26-ISKox3 structure on the IncX3 plasmid. All these plasmids harbored complete conjugative transfer systems or mobile genetic elements, indicating a high potential for horizontal gene transfer.ConclusionThis study reports an XDR K. pneumoniae co-harboring mcr-3.11, blaNDM-5, and blaCTX-M-27, isolated from the postoperative pelvic effusion of a colon cancer patient. Multiple key resistance genes are distributed on different types of plasmids, conferring resistance to “last-line” clinical agents such as carbapenems and colistin. The co-existence of multiple plasmids and the co-evolution of resistance genes may further increase the risk of resistance transmission, highlighting the importance of enhancing clinical surveillance for such highly resistant clones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05193-3.Keywords: Klebsiella pneumoniae, mcr-3.11, blaNDM-5, blaCTX-M-27, plasmid

Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) has shown a continuous upward trend, with resistance rates reaching 25% in some regions of China [1]. Among the resistance mechanisms, blaNDM-5 is a key gene mediating resistance to carbapenems, encoding a metallo-β-lactamase that can hydrolyze almost all β-lactam antibiotics [2]. blaCTX-M-27 belongs to the blaCTX-M family of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) genes, mediating resistance to cephalosporins [3]. Consequently, colistin has long been regarded as the “last line of defense” for treating infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria such as CRKP.

However, the emergence and dissemination of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes (e.g., mcr) have substantially reduced the efficacy of this defense line [4]. Since the first identification of mcr-1 in swine-derived Escherichia coli SHP45 (Zhejiang, China, 2015) [5], this gene family has spread rapidly globally. Subsequently, mcr-3 was identified in another swine-origin E. coli pWJ1 (Zhejiang, China, 2017) [6], and has since been reported in various hosts [7, 8]. Of particular concern is the rare subtype mcr-3.11, which was first isolated from Enterobacteriaceae in a Chinese chicken farm. This study describes the identification of the mcr-3.11 genotype in a human-derived K. pneumoniae isolate. To date, no study has reported this genotype in human-derived isolates; nevertheless, its prevalence in clinical settings cannot be ruled out. If a single strain harbors mcr genes, carbapenemase-encoding blaNDM, and ESBL-encoding blaCTX-M simultaneously, it may develop an XDR phenotype.

In this study, K. pneumoniae was isolated from the pelvic effusion culture of a patient with a postoperative pelvic infection following colon cancer surgery. Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics confirmed that it was an XDR strain co-harboring mcr-3.11, blaNDM-5, and blaCTX-M-27. This study aims to systematically characterize the strain’s resistance phenotype, genomic features, and the genetic context of key resistance genes, including mobile genetic elements associated with resistance gene mobilization. These findings may provide baseline data that could inform molecular epidemiological surveillance and infection control strategies for XDR K. pneumoniae.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of colistin-resistant bacteria

The test strain (KP2024) was isolated in 2024 from the pelvic effusion of a patient who had undergone radical resection for colon cancer at a tertiary hospital in Hebei Province. The specimen was streaked onto blood agar (Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China) and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h, after which single colonies were selected for further analysis. Strain identification was performed using the Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry system (MALDI-TOF MS, bioMérieux, France) with an identification confidence level of ≥ 99.9%. E. coli ATCC 8739 was used as the reference strain.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The MICs of the isolate against common antimicrobial agents were determined using the VITEK 2 Compact automated microbiology system (bioMérieux, France). The tested drugs included 16 clinically relevant antibiotics (Table 1), Results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100-Ed34 [9]. E. coli ATCC 25,922 and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 were used for quality control.

To further verify colistin resistance, the Colistin Broth Disk Elution (CBDE) was employed [10]. Following CLSI M100-Ed34, colistin disks were eluted in cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (CAMHB) to prepare broths with final concentrations of 0, 1, 2, and 4 µg/mL. The bacterial suspension turbidity was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland, and inoculated into the medicated tubes to a final concentration of 7.5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. After incubation at 35 °C for 16–20 h, broth turbidity was visually examined. Visible growth in the tube containing 4 µg/mL colistin was interpreted as resistance, while no visible growth at this concentration indicated susceptibility. P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 was used for quality control.

Modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) and EDTA-modified carbapenem inactivation method (eCIM)

To detect carbapenemase production and differentiate metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs) from serine carbapenemases, the mCIM and eCIM were performed according to CLSI M100-Ed34 [11]. A 1-µL loopful of the test strain (KP2024) was inoculated into 2 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) and TSB containing 5 mmol/L EDTA, respectively. After vortex mixing, a 10-µg meropenem disk was added to each tube and incubated at 35 °C for 4 h ± 15 min. Subsequently, the disks were removed and placed onto Mueller-Hinton Agar (MHA) plates that had been pre-inoculated with a 0.5 McFarland suspension of E. coli ATCC 25,922. After incubation at 35 °C for 18–24 h, the inhibition zone diameters were measured. K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-2146 and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA-1706 were used for quality control.

Whole genome sequencing and assembly

The genomic DNA was extracted by using the Cetyltrimethyl Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) method with minor modification [12]. DNA concentration and purity were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) and a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), respectively. Illumina short‑read sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). For long-read sequencing, Nanopore libraries were constructed using the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK109, ONT, UK). Nanopore basecalling was performed using a recurrent neural network (RNN) model based on current signal intensity and variation. Sequencing was performed using a hybrid strategy combining Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (short-read) and Nanopore PromethION 48 (long-read) platforms, with a sequencing depth of approximately 219× for Illumina short reads and 219× for Nanopore long reads. Raw data were processed using Fastp v0.23.1 [13] to remove adapters and low-quality reads, with a quality threshold of Q ≥ 20 and read length ≥ 50 bp. The high-accuracy Illumina short reads and long Nanopore reads were assembled using the Unicycler v0.5.0 [14] hybrid assembly pipeline under default parameters, with sequence correction performed using Pilon v1.24 [15] to generate a high-quality circular genome.

Bioinformatics analysis

The quality and completeness of the assembled genome were evaluated using CheckM v1 [16]. Protein-coding genes were predicted using GeneMarkS v4.32 [17]. Non-coding RNAs were annotated as follows: tRNAs were predicted using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.10 [18], and other ncRNAs were identified using Rfam v12.2 [19]. PlasmidFinder v2.1 [20] was used to identify plasmid replicon types, and ISEScan v1.7.2.3 [21] was used to identify full-length or partial insertion sequences (IS elements). Virulence genes were annotated using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB, Release 2024) [22], with the following parameters: E-value ≤ 1e-5, coverage ≥ 70%, and identity ≥ 60%. Resistance genes were screened using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD v2024.03) [23] (E-value ≤ 1e-5, coverage ≥ 70%, identity ≥ 45%) and ResFinder v4.5.0 [24] (coverage ≥ 60%, identity ≥ 80%), focusing on key resistance genes such as mcr-3.11 and blaNDM-5. Circular genome maps were visualized using CGView v2020 [25].

Comparative genomic analysis

To perform comparative analysis, reference plasmids for blaNDM-5 or blaCTX-M-27 were identified via NCBI BLAST searches, applying thresholds of > 99% identity and > 70% coverage. Only complete circular plasmids from K. pneumoniae were retained. To ensure broad representativeness, references were given to isolates from diverse geographic regions and host species. Given the limited availability of mcr-3.11-carrying plasmids, all available complete circular K. pneumoniae plasmids in GenBank were included regardless of the initial thresholds to ensure a sufficient sample size for analysis.

Plasmid comparative genomic analysis was performed using BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG) v0.95 [26]. The full-length nucleotide sequences of plasmids pKP2024-1, pKP2024-3, and pKP2024-4 isolated in this study were used as references. BLASTn alignments were performed against the selected representative plasmid sequences with an E-value threshold of 1e-5. The generated circular maps included GC content, GC skew, and sequence homology alignment information, with annotations for key functional genes and resistance genes. The genetic context and sequence homology were further visualized using Easyfig 2.2.5 [27].

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and core genome phylogenetic analysis

The sequence type (ST) of K. pneumoniae KP2024 was determined using the K. pneumoniae MLST scheme based on seven housekeeping genes (gapA, infB, mdh, pgi, phoE, rpoB, tonB) [28]. MLST was performed using the MLST 2.0 service (version 2.0.9) available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/). To further determine the phylogenetic position of the strain, representative genome sequences were downloaded from NCBI, including those of epidemic clones ST11-KL64, ST11-KL47, as well as non‑epidemic ST11 strains. The genome of strain HS11286 (GenBank assembly accession: GCA_000240185.2) was used as a reference, The core-genome phylogenetic tree was constructed with Parsnp v2.0.4 [29], Putative recombination regions were identified and removed using Gubbins v.2.1.0 [30] to mitigate the impact of homologous recombination on phylogenetic inference. A maximum‑likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML v.8.2.9 with 500 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated using tvBOT [31]. Additionally, Kleborate v3.2.4 [32] was used to predict K antigens (capsule types) and colistin resistance determinants for all included strains.

Results

Isolation of K. pneumoniae KP2024 and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The clinical isolate was identified as K. pneumoniae by MALDI‑TOF MS and designated KP2024. It was recovered from a pelvic effusion sample of a patient who developed a postoperative infection after radical resection of sigmoid colon. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) revealed that KP2024 exhibited an XDR phenotype, showing resistance to multiple clinically important antimicrobial agents.

The strain was only susceptible to tigecycline and amikacin (Table 1). According to the international consensus definition (Magiorakos et al., 2012), this resistance profile meets the criteria for extensively drug‑resistant (XDR), defined as non‑susceptibility to at least one agent in all but two or fewer antimicrobial categories. Based on these AST results, the patient was treated with tigecycline, and the clinical symptoms improved after treatment. The CBDE confirmed colistin resistance, with an MIC ≥ 4 µg/mL (Fig. 1). The mCIM/eCIM assay further confirmed that KP2024 produces a metallo-β-lactamase. In this assay, EDTA is added in the eCIM test to inhibit metallo-β-lactamase activity, and restoration of carbapenem activity in the presence of EDTA indicates a positive result (Fig. S1).

Genomic characteristics of K. pneumoniae KP2024

The complete genome sequence of KP2024 was determined by whole-genome sequencing (WGS). The genome comprises a single chromosome (5,352,465 bp) and eight intact plasmids. The sequences have been deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers JBTLRR010000001–JBTLRR010000009.

A total of 21 antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, conferring resistance to 10 classes of antimicrobial agents, were identified across the entire genome (Table 2). Specifically, the chromosome harbors four resistance genes: blaSHV-182, blaSHV-159, blaSHV-158, and oqxB.

KP2024 harbors a complex plasmid repertoire, including multiple IncF plasmids and an IncX3 plasmid. IncF and IncX3 plasmids are both predominantly found in Enterobacterales yet differ fundamentally in their transmission biology. IncF plasmids are low‑copy‑number, highly stable vectors that serve as long‑term reservoirs for ESBL genes [33]. In contrast, IncX3 plasmids are characterized by high conjugation efficiency, negligible fitness cost, and the ability to enhance biofilm formation, which collectively make them one of the primary vehicles for blaNDM dissemination [34]. As they belong to distinct incompatibility groups, IncF and IncX3 plasmids can stably coexist within the same bacterial cell without competing for replication mechanisms. Bioinformatics analysis of plasmids

pKP2024-1 is a 214,839 bp IncFIB-type plasmid harboring blaCTX-M-27, with an overall GC content of 50.84%. Besides this ESBL gene, the plasmid also carries multiple resistance genes, including floR, tet(D), and catAII. A complete conjugative transfer (tra) operon was identified. Sequence analysis revealed the presence of abundant insertion sequence (IS) elements, such as IS26, IS3, and ISKpn24. Notably, the blaCTX-M-27 gene is directly associated with the insertion sequence ISEcp1 (Fig. 2A).

pKP2024‑2 is a circular molecule of 69,711 bp with a GC content of 53.36%. The plasmid harbors a mosaic multidrug resistance (MDR) region carrying multiple antimicrobial resistance determinants. These include resistance genes for β‑lactams (blaTEM‑1), aminoglycosides (aac(6’)-Ib-cr, aac(3’)-IId, aph(3’)-Ia), sulfonamides (sul1), trimethoprim (dfrA12, dfrA27), macrolides (mph(A)), and the quaternary ammonium compound resistance gene qacEΔ1. The MDR region also contains multiple mobile genetic elements, including insertion sequences IS26, IS1, IS6100, IS110, and the class 1 integron integrase gene intI1 (Fig. 2B).

pKP2024-3 is an IncFII-type plasmid carrying mcr-3.11, with a length of 67,341 bp and a GC content of 52.76%. A key finding is that this plasmid harbors the colistin resistance gene mcr-3.11 in a human-derived Enterobacteriaceae strain. It also carries the quaternary ammonium compound resistance gene qacEΔ1. In addition, the plasmid contains multiple insertion sequence elements (ISKpn19, IS26, IS3, IS5075, IS91). Furthermore, it carries a complete conjugative transfer operon, including traH, traK, traX, traP, and traQ (Fig. 2C).

pKP2024-4 is an IncX3-type plasmid harboring blaNDM-5, with a length of 46,152 bp. The core resistance element blaNDM-5 is flanked by IS5 and bleMBL, forming a conserved IS5-blaNDM-5-bleMBL-trpF gene cluster [35]. It is also associated with a recombination system in its downstream region, consisting of ISKpn3, IS26, IS3000, and Tn3. Meanwhile, the plasmid harbors a complete virB gene cluster that encodes the Type IV Secretion System (T4SS) [36], which is the core machinery mediating conjugative transfer of IncX3-type plasmids (Fig. 2D).

Comparative genomic analysis of plasmids

While the co-occurrence of common mcr variants with blaNDM and blaCTX-M genes in Enterobacteriaceae has been increasingly reported globally [37], the specific combination of mcr-3.11, blaNDM-5, and blaCTX-M-27 in a single strain has not been previously described. To characterize the genetic features and transmission potential of these plasmids, this study performed a comparative genomic analysis of pKP2024-1, pKP2024-3, and pKP2024-4.

pKP2024‑1 was compared with four reference plasmids from distinct temporal and geographic origins: CP023943 (USA, 2017), CP047337 (Yangzhou, China, 2020), CP068691 (Shanghai, China, 2021), and CP178612 (Changchun, China, 2024). BLASTn-based comparative genomic analysis revealed that pKP2024-1 shared high sequence coverage (≥ 70%) and a nucleotide identity of 99.91% with reference plasmids (CP023943, CP047337, CP068691, and CP178612) (Fig. 3A). Extensive sequence synteny was observed in regions involved in replication and conjugative transfer. The multidrug‑resistant (MDR) region is located between 100 kb and 125 kb and carries key resistance genes including blaCTX‑M‑27. This region is flanked by multiple mobile genetic elements. Furthermore, while the MDR region is largely syntenic across these plasmids, distinct structural variations are observed in the 125 kb–140 kb region. Notably, all reference plasmids also carry the heavy metal resistance operons sil, pco, and ars.

pKP2024-3 harbors the rare colistin resistance gene mcr-3.11. The mcr-3.11 gene is integrated within a transposon structure. As shown in Fig. 3B, comparative genomic analysis revealed that pKP2024-3 shares ≥ 96% sequence coverage and ≥ 99.99% nucleotide identity with K. pneumoniae-derived reference plasmids MH341574 (Chengdu, China, 2018) and CP104575 (Shandong, China, 2022), but exhibits limited sequence identity with E. coli-derived plasmid MG552133 (Chengdu, China, 2017) (Fig. 3B). These high levels of sequence conservation suggest that this plasmid backbone is largely conserved across Klebsiella spp. To characterize the genetic context of mcr-3.11, pKP2024-3 was compared with reference plasmids CP104575 and MH341574. Notably, the Tn3-IS3-mcr-3.11-dgkA core module was nearly identical in pKP2024-3 (human-derived), CP104575 (broiler-derived) and MH341574. All three plasmids carry both ISKpn19 and IS26 elements, and these mobile elements are also present in the vicinity of the core region. However, some structural variations were observed in the flanking regions of this resistance cassette (Fig. 3D).

pKP2024-4 exhibits the typical chimeric architecture characteristic of IncX3 plasmids. It shows near-complete sequence identity and ≥ 94% sequence coverage with plasmids including CP076035 (Beijing, China, 2021), CP178567 (Jinhua, China, 2024), and MH107030 (Changzhou, China, 2018). Notably, while the nucleotide identity with CP196082 reached 100%, the sequence coverage was only 10%, indicating that the two plasmids share a highly conserved blaNDM-5-containing segment but possess distinct plasmid backbones. Functional annotation identified the carbapenemase gene blaNDM-5, along with multiple insertion sequences, transposons, and conjugative transfer-related genes (Fig. 3C). This pattern may indicate the acquisition of a carbapenem resistance module via horizontal gene transfer, rather than the dissemination of the entire plasmid backbone.

Core genome phylogenetic analysis

MLST typing classified KP2024 as ST11 (gapA-infB-mdh-pgi-phoE-rpoB-tonB: 3-3-1-1-1-1-4). Notably, Kleborate analysis identified its capsule type as KL170. To the best of our knowledge, KP2024 is the first reported ST11 K. pneumoniae isolate carrying the KL170 capsule type [38]. Consistent with the MLST results, phylogenetic reconstruction placed KP2024 firmly within the ST11 clade. It exhibited the closest genetic relatedness to strains EuSCAPE_SK009 (GCA_900510675.1), AUH-KIMP217 (GCA_002775405.1), and EuSCAPE_IT127 (GCA_900514915.1), which were isolated from Slovakia, Lebanon, and Italy, respectively (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Colon cancer patients undergoing radical resection are prone to postoperative pelvic infections. KP2024 was resistant to colistin and most β‑lactams, leaving tigecycline as one of the few effective options, which prompted genomic analysis of its multiple resistance plasmids. Structural analysis of pKP2024-1 indicated that blaCTX-M-27 resides within a conserved ISEcp1-mediated transposon unit. Consistent with previous findings [39, 40], this mechanism appears to be conserved across regions and species, likely playing a key role in the dissemination of blaCTX-M-27 among Enterobacteriaceae. Comparative genomics revealed a conserved backbone shared among strains from the US and various Chinese regions, hinting at possible cross-border transmission. Notably, the co-localization of multidrug resistance genes with heavy metal resistance operons (sil, pco, ars) suggests a strategy for clinical adaptation. Under hospital environmental pressures (e.g., disinfectants and heavy metals), co‑selection may enable these plasmids to persist and spread via genetic hitchhiking [41, 42].

Colistin resistance may emerge via chromosomal mutations or, increasingly, through plasmid-borne mcr genes that facilitate horizontal dissemination [43]. Here, we identified the rare mcr-3.11 variant on an IncFII plasmid (pKP2024-3). Compared with the prototype mcr-3, mcr-3.11 harbors two amino acid substitutions (G373V and T468Q) that may mediate lipid A modification and subsequent colistin resistance [44]. Although acquisition of mcr genes typically imposes a substantial fitness cost on the host, the tight linkage of mcr-3.11 to dgkA in pKP2024-3 might ameliorate this cost. Specifically, dgkA overexpression might help resolve MCR‑induced phospholipid dysfunction and restore outer membrane integrity [45]. Furthermore, the Tn3-IS3-mcr-3.11-dgkA module may provide a compensatory mechanism for stable maintenance, and could also theoretically allow the formation of circular intermediates favorable for potential horizontal transfer. The coupling of an mcr gene with a potentially fitness‑compensating element within a mobile genetic vehicle may represent an adaptive strategy that warrants close epidemiological surveillance.

In addition, the IncX3 plasmid pKP2024-4 carries blaNDM-5, further complicating the resistance profile of this strain. Although blaNDM-5 has been associated with dissemination via various plasmid types (IncX3, IncFII, IncN, IncF) [46, 47], IncX3 plasmids have become the predominant vehicle for its transmission, especially in China [48]. Comparative analysis revealed that pKP2024-4 contains a core structure (Tn3-IS3000-IS5-blaNDM-5-bleMBL-trpF-IS26-ISKox3) highly similar to that of an IncX3 plasmid from Escherichia fergusonii [49], and related conserved architectures have also been observed in other Enterobacteriaceae [50]. Such structural conservation likely contributes to plasmid stability and suggests a genetic framework capable of supporting resistance gene transfer. Notably, pKP2024-4 lacks some accessory genes (e.g., ISAba125, dsbC/dsbD) present in previously reported homologous plasmids, suggesting possible streamlining that removes non‑essential elements to facilitate horizontal gene transfer.

Phylogenetic analysis provided important insights into the evolutionary background and epidemiological significance of KP2024. To our knowledge, KP2024 represents the first reported ST11 K. pneumoniae isolate carrying the KL170 capsule type. This observation expands the known capsular diversity of the ST11 high-risk clone. Although the bootstrap support for the immediate clade containing KP2024 was moderate (< 70%), the isolate clustered within the well-supported major ST11 clade alongside several previously described international strains. While this placement confirms that KP2024 belongs to the broader ST11 lineage, the limited support for its specific subclade precludes robust inferences regarding its precise phylogenetic position or direct evolutionary relationships. Furthermore, due to the scarcity of closely related genomes, no definitive transmission routes or international dissemination events could be identified. Enhanced genomic surveillance and broader geographic sampling are warranted to determine whether ST11-KL170 variants are emerging or circulating more extensively.

From a clinical perspective, the susceptibility of KP2024 to tigecycline was pivotal for the patient’s recovery, owing to tigecycline’s excellent intra-abdominal distribution. With MBLs precluding the use of ceftazidime-avibactam [51], tigecycline monotherapy served as a viable, albeit restricted option. However, the global emergence of tigecycline resistance, driven by RND efflux pump overexpression and tet(X) variants, underscores the urgent need for alternatives [52]; for such MBL-producing carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) infections, aztreonam/avibactam is a promising option with favorable pharmacokinetics and low toxicity. Concurrently, mitigating the severe clinical impact of colistin resistance requires innovative strategies. Current promising approaches include combination therapies, phage therapy, and novel antibiotics, which collectively provide crucial directions for future CRE management [53].

The clinical isolate in this study carried mcr-3.11 on an IncFII plasmid (pKP2024‑3), whereas mcr-3.11 has been reported on IncI1 plasmids in broiler‑derived K. pneumoniae co‑carrying ESBL genes [54]. Moreover, a large‑scale genomic survey of commercial chicken farms in China identified IncX3 and IncFII as the most common blaNDM-5 plasmid types and documented the co‑occurrence of blaNDM-5 with mcr genes [55]. Strikingly, our clinical isolate harbored the same two plasmid types, IncFII for mcr-3.11 and IncX3 for blaNDM-5, demonstrating a convergence of plasmid replicon types between agricultural and clinical settings. These findings suggest that mcr-3.11 and blaNDM-5 possess the genetic basis for mobilization across ecological niches via multiple broad‑host‑range plasmids. From a One Health perspective [56], the overlap in plasmid types between farms and clinical settings points to a plausible transmission route: resistance genes originate in animals and enter clinical settings via plasmid capture and horizontal gene transfer. This highlights the need for cross-sectoral surveillance to track high-risk plasmid-gene combinations and systematic monitoring of animal, environmental, and clinical samples to validate cross-domain transmission.

This study has certain limitations: first, we did not conduct conjugation experiments or other biological assays to verify plasmid transfer frequencies [57], the actual functional transferability of these plasmids under clinical conditions remains to be experimentally established; second, we have not yet experimentally evaluated the fitness impact of harboring three distinct resistance plasmids [58, 59]; third, the prevalence, function, and clinical significance of this rare gene require further investigation using larger sample sizes and more diverse strains. Future research will focus on investigating the stability and transmissibility of multidrug-resistant plasmids under different antibiotic pressures.

Conclusion

This study characterizes the genomic landscape of the XDR K. pneumoniae strain KP2024, which harbors four distinct plasmids (IncFIB, IncFIA/IncR, IncFII, and IncX3). We identified a rare mcr-3.11 variant on the IncFII plasmid, co-localized with the dgkA gene, which likely mitigates fitness costs. Additionally, a streamlined blaNDM-5-bearing IncX3 plasmid was identified, potentially enhancing its horizontal transfer potential. The blaCTX-M-27 gene was situated within a conserved ISEcp1-mediated transposon on the IncFIB plasmid. Notably, KP2024 represents the first ST11 strain reported with the KL170 capsule type, revealing an expansion in the capsular diversity of this high-risk clone. Phylogenetic analysis highlights its integration into globally disseminated ST11 lineages. Clinically, resistance to both colistin and carbapenems left tigecycline monotherapy as the only viable option, underscoring the critical need for enhanced genomic surveillance, stricter stewardship, and the development of novel therapeutic strategies against such XDR pathogens.
