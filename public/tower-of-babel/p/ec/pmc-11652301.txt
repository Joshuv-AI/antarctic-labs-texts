Digital Papillary Adenocarcinoma: A Case Presentation and Literature Review
Authors: Haley F. Pate, David B. Fulton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Digital papillary adenocarcinoma is a rare cutaneous tumor of eccrine origin that often presents as a papule or nodule on the dorsal aspect of a finger. It is challenging to diagnose because of its rarity, its benign appearance, and the lack of specific clinical or histological features. Excision is recommended, given the malignant nature and variable recurrence rates with metastases well documented in the literature. We present a case of a 49-year-old man with a digital papillary adenocarcinoma on his right middle finger. We discuss this patient’s treatment course and the lack of standardized treatment guidelines. Further research is needed to establish evidence-based management strategies for digital papillary adenocarcinoma.

Digital papillary adenocarcinoma (DPA) is a rare cutaneous tumor of eccrine origin. It is unusual in that no clinical or histological features are predictive of biologic behavior.1 The relatively benign appearance and indolent course of the mass often lead to misdiagnosis.1 Given that nearly 80% of DPA’s occur on the fingers, ganglion cysts and glomus tumors are often high on the differential diagnosis list.1 However, despite their benign appearance, excision is imperative as up to 50% of patients can experience local recurrence with rates of metastasis reported to be between 14% and 41%.1 Unfortunately, because these tumors are so rare, there are no clinical guidelines published for how to best treat patients with a diagnosis of DPA. Here, we report a case of a male patient with a DPA and discuss his clinical presentation and clinical course.

Case Report

A 49-year-old man presented to the dermatology clinic with a mass of the right middle finger that had been enlarging for about 6 months. He denied any known trauma preceding the appearance of the lesion. He endorsed increased pain with minor trauma and with finger movement. The increasing discomfort and growth of the mass triggered his evaluation. His medical history included gastroesophageal reflux disease, hyperlipidemia, and gout. He had never had gout flares involving the upper extremities, and his initial trial of medical therapy for gout did not improve his symptoms regarding the finger mass.

Physical examination revealed a 1.0 cm × 1.7 cm mass involving the distal volar aspect of the right middle finger. The mass was tender to palpation and had a central area of ulceration with an overlying scab (Fig. 1). The digit had full range of motion and was neurovascularly intact. Differential diagnoses included glomus tumor, pyogenic granuloma, ganglion, or other neoplasm of uncertain origin. Initial histopathology showed a well-circumscribed multinodular tumor within the dermis and subcutis, with ulceration of the epidermis. There were focal papillary structures and evidence of lymphovascular invasion. Histology was concerning for DPA, and slides were sent to a larger academic center for further evaluation. After confirmation of the diagnosis, the patient was referred to hand surgery for surgical treatment.

Given the rarity of his condition, the patient was referred to orthopedic oncology for subspecialty evaluation. A decision was made to obtain magnetic resonance imaging with gadolinium of the finger, which confirmed that the lesion was contained within the soft tissues of the distal pulp. Right axillary lymph node ultrasound was performed, and no enlarged lymph nodes were noted. The patient then underwent right middle finger partial amputation through the distal interphalangeal joint with negative tissue margins. The patient was seen 2 weeks after surgery in follow-up and was doing well with full range of motion at the proximal interphalangeal joint (Figs. 2 and 3). Shared decision making for follow-up was made, as there are no current treatment guidelines for DPAs of this size. The patient opted for self-checks at both the surgical site and axillary lymph nodes. He will otherwise follow-up with his primary care provider and only seek orthopedic evaluation for any concerns. Discussion

Digital papillary adenocarcinoma is a rare, low-grade, sweat gland tumor, of which 85% occur on the hands. The volar aspect of the fingers is the primary site in which 79% of DPAs are present. The vague appearance of the lesion makes the initial differential diagnosis broad including pyogenic granuloma, paronychia, cyst, vascular malformation, giant cell tumor, glomus tumor, foreign body granuloma, and benign adnexal tumor.2 Digital papillary adenocarcinomas most often occur in men in their fifth to seventh decades of life.3 The most common presentation is a solitary cutaneous nodule that develops within weeks to years and can be either painful or painless.2 The average tumor size is between 1.7 and 2.0 cm but can range between .4 and 4.3 cm.1,2 Excisional biopsy or punch biopsy often leads to definitive diagnosis, which then guides treatment. Unfortunately, due to the low prevalence of this tumor, there are no recommended clinical guidelines for excision margins of DPAs. However, margins are routinely checked after removal to decrease the risk of recurrence and metastasis. There are few reports of Mohs micrographic surgery for treatment of DPA, but most commonly when involving the digit-wide local excision (WLE) or partial amputation is performed.2 Comparison of WLE versus partial amputation was studied by Hsu et al,4 and they found no significant difference in the prognosis of patients who underwent WLE compared with partial amputation and thus favored WLE with or without sentinel lymph node biopsy for patients with no bony involvement or metastasis.5 Patients diagnosed at the time of excisional biopsy without further excision have recurrence rates reported between 21% and 50%.1,4 Recurrence rates are minimized to 5% when re-excision or partial amputation is performed.1,2 Currently, there are no specific recommendations regarding indications for sentinel lymph node biopsy in DPA patients.2 Given that there is no known survival benefit, the morbidity associated with sentinel lymph node biopsy seems like an unnecessary burden to the patient.5 On initial presentation, 3% to 5% of patients have regional lymph node metastasis.1,2,4 Staging at the onset of diagnosis including physical examination and imaging is critical, given the propensity of recurrence and metastatic disease. Metastatic disease has been reported as late as 20 years after definitive excision, making long-term follow-up important.1,4 Unfortunately, because there are only about 200 reported cases in the literature, there are no specific guidelines to follow.6 Some authors have suggested physical examination and yearly screening chest X-ray for 10 years after diagnosis, even in the setting of negative margins, given that 71% of metastasis are to the lung.3 However, these recommendations were created in 1987 and have not been re-evaluated for their efficacy.7 In patients who present with metastatic disease, there has not been promising evidence for the use of chemotherapy in treating DPA.3 Case reports to date have shown no benefit and minimal response to chemotherapy.7 Additionally, there is only one report of the use of radiation therapy as an adjuvant measure for tumor size reduction.8 Recent research highlighted the significant overexpression of FGFR2 in DPA with hopes that targeting the fibroblast growth factor receptor pathway may be a further therapeutic option.9 Fortunately, our patient presented with localized disease, had negative tumor margins, had no suspicious lymph nodes, and has maintained full function of his dominant hand. However, the lack of guidelines on how to work with patients with this tumor makes management difficult. The harm of putting patients through extensive imaging including computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging or positron emission tomography scans, lymph node biopsies, etc, must be weighed against the limited data available. In our case, shared decision making was used to develop a follow-up plan since the current literature does not give a plan that is evidence-based. There will likely continue to be ambiguity in treatment approaches, as the rarity of the disease limits the ability to conduct randomized controlled trials.
