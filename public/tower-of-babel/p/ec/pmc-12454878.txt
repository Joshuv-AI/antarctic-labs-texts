Rapid osteointegration with a strontium-containing hydroxyapatite coating on tantalum by activating Itga6-PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Xi Wang, Tiantian Chen, Peng Pan, Chundong Jiang, Wentao Liu, Xu Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Owing to its excellent biocompatibility, tantalum has the potential to replace titanium as a new mainstream bone-repair material. However, it was recognized as an inert metal. Therefore, it is necessary to improve the bioactivity of tantalum through surface modification to achieve more stable osseointegration. The key to designing bone repair materials is to imitate the physical structure and chemical composition of natural bone. In this study, a microscale strontium-containing hydroxyapatite (Sr + HA) coating was loaded onto a tantalum surface using a hydrothermal method, which improved the protein adsorption ability and hydrophilicity of tantalum. The incorporation of Sr also promoted the release of Sr2+ and Ca2+. Furthermore, the results of in vitro and in vivo experiments showed that Sr + HA had good biocompatibility and bone integration. The research and development of biomaterials needs to be based on an understanding of the mechanism of action between materials and cells. Combined with transcriptomics and label-free quantitative proteomics analyses, it was confirmed for the first time that Sr + HA could activate the PI3K/AKT pathway by up-regulating Itga6 to promote the osteogenic differentiation of cells, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the development of tantalum-based implants with excellent osseointegration properties.

Introduction

Recently, the incidence of bone defects caused by tumors and trauma, which are common clinical diseases, has increased annually [1]. Ideal bone repair materials must have excellent mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and osseointegration abilities [2]. In 1802, Swedish chemist Anders Gustav Ekberg (A.G., 1767–1813) first discovered tantalum, which has excellent biocompatibility [3] and has been used in clinical fields such as orthodontics since the 1940s [4]. Studies have shown that tantalum, without any modification, has a better osteoinductive effect on rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells than titanium. The excellent osteoinductive effect of tantalum may be due to its more effective activation of BMP2/Smad1 pathway [5]. However, the elastic modulus of tantalum is much higher than that of the human bones, and an excessively high elastic modulus would mitigate the mechanical load that the human bone is supposed to bear, thereby generating a stress shielding effect [3,6]. Although tantalum is a metal with good biocompatibility, it also has biological inertness [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. However, the high elastic modulus and bioinertness of tantalum limit its wide clinical application. With the development of additive manufacturing, a porous tantalum with a reduced elastic modulus and an elastic modulus close to that of human bone was constructed. Generally, after a material is implanted in the body, a series of interactions occur between the surface of the material and the biological environment [14,15]. Therefore, improving the biological activity of tantalum through surface modification is essential to achieve faster, earlier, and more stable osseointegration.

Bone tissue is composed of a variety of bone tissue cells and a bone matrix, among which hydroxyapatite(HA) in the crystalline form of [Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2] is the main component of the bone matrix [16]. The key to designing of bone repair materials is to imitate the physical structure and chemical composition of the natural bone. HA is a bioceramic material with a chemical composition highly similar to that of natural mammalian bones [17]. HA exhibits stable chemical properties, excellent biocompatibility, and osteoinduction. However, because of its low mechanical strength, HA alone cannot be applied alone to bone defect sites that require load-bearing [18,19]. Therefore, it can be combined with a substrate as a coating to improve the osseointegration of the implant material by integrating the mechanical properties of the substrate and the biological properties of HA. Additionally, HA has high crystallinity and is not easily dissolved [20], and ions released after dissolution of the material are crucial for the osteogenesis process. Therefore, improving the biological properties of HA is the key to improving its suitability for bone tissues.

In addition to HA, the bone matrix is also rich in a variety of trace elements that play an important roles in maintaining bone homeostasis [21]. Among these elements, strontium enhances the adhesion ability of macrophages and promotes their transformation into the M2 phenotype, thereby promoting osteogenesis by up-regulating transforming growth factors and bone morphogenetic proteins [22]. Strontium can also significantly inhibit titanium particle-induced osteoclast activation and inflammatory bone loss by disrupting the NF-κB pathway [23]. The abovementioned studies have shown that strontium can enhance bone tissue density with its unique dual mode of action of promoting new bone formation and inhibiting bone resorption [[24], [25], [26]]. Furthermore, strontium and calcium ions are very similar in size and charge, based on the strong ion exchange ability of HA; therefore, strontium ions can replace some of the calcium ions in HA [[27], [28], [29]]. In conclusion, strontium can be used as a key complement to HA in improving its biological properties and inducing osteogenesis. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there are no reports on the combination of strontium-containing hydroxyapatite (Sr + HA) coating with tantalum-based materials and its mechanism of action.

In view of the abovementioned studies, breaking through the biological inertia of tantalum to promote osseointegration is the key to improving the success rate of implantation. In this study, the Sr + HA coating was loaded onto the tantalum using a hydrothermal method, and its physical and chemical properties were evaluated through a series of material characterizations. Subsequently, the materials were co-cultured with mouse pre-osteoblasts MC3T3-E1 cells to evaluate their biocompatibility and osteogenic differentiation in vitro, and their bone repair ability in vivo was evaluated using a rat femoral defect model. The development of implant materials must be based on an understanding of the mechanism of action between the material and the cell. Finally, the effects of Sr + HA on gene expression were analyzed using transcriptomics and label-free quantitative proteomics to explore the potential mechanism by which Sr + HA promotes osteogenic differentiation.

Results and discussion

Characterization of tantalum-based implant materials

Common surface modification methods include micro-arc oxidation (MAO), magnetron sputtering, and hydrothermal methods [30]. The stability of the MAO electrolyte is poor. High temperatures produced by magnetron sputtering technology can cause a certain degree of damage to the substrate. The hydrothermal method can load bioactive elements uniformly and stably onto substrates with complex shapes, and more importantly, the reaction temperature does not affect the biological activity of the elements [31,32]. In this study, a hydrothermal method was used to modify the surface of Ta to improve its biological inertness. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed (Fig. 1A) that the coating structures of HA and Sr + HA were uniform and uncracked, with interconnected micron-level pit structures, whereas the surface of Ta without a coating load was relatively flat. Energy dispersion spectrum (EDS) analysis (Fig. 1B) showed that the main elements of the HA coating were Ca and P, whereas the main elements of the Sr + HA coating were Sr, Ca, and P. The loading of HA and Sr + HA was also confirmed through X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 1C). Various elements were uniformly distributed in the coating (Fig. 1D). The elemental composition and distribution remained relatively stable after immersed in the simulated body fluid (Fig. 1E). HA has a strong adsorption capacity and can be combined with metallic elements to customize composite materials for diverse applications. Sr has an affinity for bones. In the newly formed bone tissue, although only a small amount of Sr can be incorporated into the HA crystal of the bone by replacing Ca, this small amount of Sr can significantly improve the compact arrangement of the bone structure [33]. Ni et al. tested the effect of a series of Sr incorporations into HA at different molar ratios on osteoblasts and found that 5 % and 10 % Sr substituted HA may enhance osteoblast differentiation [34]. Gu et al. demonstrated that strontium-doped calcium polyphosphate scaffolds with a Sr/Ca molar ratio of 8:92 promoted segmental bone regeneration by stimulating VEGF and bFGF secretion by osteoblasts [35]. A moderate concentration of strontium can promote the formation of new bone and inhibit bone resorption, whereas high concentrations of strontium may cause abnormal bone calcium metabolism in vivo [36]. In this study, the molar ratio of Sr/Ca was chosen to be 1:9 to load Sr + HA onto Ta, thereby achieving the local delivery of Sr and avoided the side effects of systemic administration. Cells are surrounded by a three-dimensional (3D) and dynamic extracellular matrix at the micro and nano scales. Therefore, biomaterials can be constructed at the micro and nano scales to simulate the in vivo environments [37]. The surface roughness (Ra) of Ta, HA, and Sr + HA was 0.32 ± 0.03 μm, 0.98 ± 0.27 μm, and 1.21 ± 0.34 μm, respectively (Fig. 2A). The loading of the coating improved the surface roughness of Ta, and no significant difference was observed between the Ra of HA and Sr + HA (Fig. 2B). Studies have confirmed that micro-scale topography can better promote the osteogenic differentiation of cells [38]. Roughness control in the range of 0.51–1.36 μm can polarize macrophages into M2 phenotype by down-regulating the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors and up-regulating the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines [39]. Both Sr + HA and HA coatings improved the hydropathy (Fig. 2C and D) and protein adsorption capacity (Fig. 2E) of Ta. Notably, no significant difference was observed in the hydropathy of the two coatings; however, the protein adsorption capacity of Sr + HA was better than that of HA at 24 h. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) showed that both HA and Sr + HA could stably release Ca2+, and Sr + HA could release Sr2+ as well. Notably, the incorporation of Sr increased the concentration of released Ca2+ (Fig. 2F). Although Sr2+ and Ca2+ are similar in structure, the ionic radius of Ca2+ (99 p.m.) is smaller than that of Sr2+ (113 p.m.). Therefore, the incorporation of Sr can cause crystal structure changes in HA, thereby reducing the crystallinity and increasing the solubility of Sr + HA [40]. The materials were immersed in the Tirs buffer solution. Both HA and Sr + HA showed a slight increase in pH, without disrupting the acid-base balance (Fig. 2I). Theoretically, the first reaction between a biomaterial and an organism involves protein adsorption. Electrostatic interactions occur between the protein via COO− and the cations released from the coating [41]. Therefore, it is suggested that the Sr2+ and Ca2+ released from Sr+HA cooperate to confer the superior protein adsorption ability of Sr + HA, and the difference in protein adsorption ability may lead to different cell behaviors. Valuable physical and chemical properties of bioceramics can also be obtained because of the presence of Sr in the apatite structure. Some studies have incorporated Sr/Cu into HA, and the doping of Sr/Cu enhanced the compressive strength and stability of HA, providing a 3D platform for cell–matrix interaction [42]. Herein, SEM and EDS analyses confirmed that the surface morphology (Fig. 2G) and elemental composition (Fig. 2H) of HA and Sr + HA did not change significantly before and after washing, indicating that the combination of the coatings with Ta was relatively stable. Furthermore, it was confirmed from several aspects that the Sr + HA coating at the micron scale, which can release Sr2+ and Ca2+, possessed excellent hydrophilicity and protein adsorption ability and could be stably loaded on Ta.

Evaluation of biocompatibility and osteoinductive properties of tantalum-based implant materials in vitro

Biocompatibility is a prerequisite for good osseointegration. Studies [43] have found that the proliferation rate of human osteosarcoma cell MG-63 on the surface of a titanium substrate gradually decreases with the increase of surface roughness (0, 1, 2, and 4 μm). However, some studies have indicated that there is no significant difference in the proliferation of human osteoblasts on Ti substrates with different surface roughness (0.08–0.1, 0.3–0.5, 1.2–1.4, and 3.3–3.7 μm) [44]. CCK-8 (Fig. 3A) showed that all cells exhibited good growth activity, and the OD value of Ta with lower roughness was higher than those of HA and Sr + HA. This may be attributed to three aspects: first, the dissolution of the coating may increase the PH value of the medium, and the increased PH value may inhibit the proliferation of cells [45]. Second, it can be hypothesized that the trend might be related to the competitive relationship between cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation [46,47]. Third, the cell types used in the aforementioned studies were different, and different cells exhibit different sensing and feedback to material roughness. The results of the live/dead cell double staining showed (Fig. 3B) that Ta, HA, and Sr + HA were not cytotoxic. Studies found that a strontium concentration of 360 mg/L could still promote the proliferation of cells [48]. Herein, the release amount of Sr2+ from the Sr + HA was 4.03 ± 0.06 mg/L, which was within the safe range. Fluorescence staining (Fig. 3C) revealed that the cells on the surface of the material were fusiform and polygonal. Cell adhesion is influenced by the cytoskeleton, and cells tend to form focal adhesions at locations that balance cytoskeletal forces [49]. The abundant protein adsorption layer on the surface of the material can promote the adhesion of cells to the surface of the material by interacting with the adhesion spots [50,51]. The cells in Sr + HA and HA with strong protein adsorption ability and relatively rough 3D morphology were observed to spread more, and a large number of filopodia were observed. Moreover, the cell shape was complementary to the surface contour to achieve the maximum contact area. Overall, the above results indicate that Ta, HA, and Sr + HA have good biocompatibility and provide a base for subsequent studies on osteogenic properties. The effect of Sr + HA on the osteogenic differentiation of cells in vitro was verified from multiple perspectives. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assay (Fig. 3D) showed that Sr + HA significantly increased ALP activity in osteoblasts. ALP staining (Fig. 3E) showed that the number of ALP-positive cells in the Sr + HA group was higher and the color was darker. Alizarin red staining further confirmed that Sr + HA induced collagen secretion and mineralization (Fig. 3F). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) showed (Fig. 3G) that Sr + HA promoted the expression of the osteogenic differentiation marker genes ALP, RUNX2, and OPG. Cellular immunofluorescence staining showed that the fluorescence intensity of the ALP protein in Sr + HA was the strongest (Fig. 3H). The results of western blotting (Fig. 3I and J) were consistent with those of qPCR in terms of protein expression levels. These results suggest that Sr + HA exhibits optimal osteogenesis in vitro. In addition, some studies have developed alginate gel containing strontium. The high concentration of Sr2+ released can promote the osteogenic differentiation of cells and the formation of blood vessels, but has no effect on cell proliferation [52]. Herein, the combination of Sr and HA released Sr2+ and Ca2+, wherein Ca2+ entered cells through the interaction with the Ca2+ sensing receptor (CaSR), activate Wnt5, and bound to G protein-coupled receptors to initiate the Ca2+ cascade in cells, and then regulated downstream genes, such as RUNX2 and ALP [53]. Sr2+ can also act on the CaSR, and Ca2+ sensitive pathways have been proposed as mediators of the Sr2+ induced effects [54]. Evaluation of bone repair ability of tantalum-based implant materials in vivo

After confirming that Sr + HA enhanced the osteogenic differentiation effect of tantalum in vitro, the bone-repair ability of tantalum in vivo was evaluated. The porous tantalum used for in vivo studies is the first and only innovative domestic porous tantalum product approved for marketing. Its unique 3D spatial structure, similar to the natural bone, can promote the growth of blood vessels and cells and improve the stability of bone repair materials through mechanical chimerization [56]. The SEM image showed that the internal coating of the porous tantalum was distributed relatively uniformly (Fig. 4G). Evidently, the tantalum based implant material was tightly and firmly integrated with the bone interface (Fig. 4A). The 3D reconstruction showed that the loading of Sr + HA and HA coatings significantly enhanced the amount of new bone formation in porous tantalum (Fig. 4B), and the POF value of the Sr + HA group was the highest (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, calcein and xylenol orange were used as fluorescent labels to analyze the deposition of new bone calcium salts in each material group (Fig. 4D). The fluorescence distribution area, fluorescence-labeling rate (Fig. 4E), and IOD value (Fig. 4F) of the Sr + HA group were the highest. The spacing between the fluorescent double labels gradually decreased over time (Fig. 5A), suggesting that the bone growth rate in the porous material decreased over time. However, the Sr + HA group exhibited the highest fluorescence double-label spacing (Fig. 5B), suggesting that the Sr + HA group had the highest rate of bone proliferation. Finally, as revealed by toluidine blue staining (Fig. 5C), the trabecular distribution of the newly formed bone was less regular than that of the host bone, and osteon units (Haversian system) and blood vessels were observed in the Sr + HA group. Calculations revealed (Fig. 5D) that Sr + HA significantly promoted bone in-growth in the porous tantalum-based material. These results indicate that Sr + HA has good bone repair ability in vivo. Medical metal materials have extensive clinical applications, including in bones, teeth, and cardiovascular tissues. Among them, the metal implants used in the oral cavity mainly rely on mechanical connections with alveolar bone to achieve fixation [57]. Unmodified tantalum and titanium-based implant materials may cause loosening after implantation due to their biological inertness, especially when applied to the elderly with diabetes and osteoporosis. This can directly affect the lifespan of the materials [58]. By enriching the topological structure on the implant surface or loading coatings containing bioactive substances, effects such as promoting bone integration, enhancing wear resistance and corrosion resistance can be achieved. Compared with traditional implants, implants with coatings can significantly reduce the risk of aseptic loosening. Surface modification has always been a hot topic in the field of biomedical engineering. The research on surface modification mainly includes surface nanocrystallization, surface functionalization involving bioactive components, and biomimetic coatings. Some researchers have combined anodization and atmospheric plasma spraying techniques to prepare Ta2O5 nanotube coatings with a diameter of approximately 15 nm and a length of approximately 800 nm. This nano-form reduces the release of metal ions and enhances the corrosion resistance of the material. In vitro experiments, it shows good cell compatibility and osteogenic activity [59]. The interaction between cells and the material depends on two aspects: surface morphology and chemical composition. Some researchers have also loaded bioactive substances such as bone morphogenetic protein onto the surface of the material to achieve functional modification [60]. However, the stability of organic active components such as bone morphogenetic protein is relatively poor, while the properties of metal elements such as strontium and silver are more stable and more conducive to preservation. Implants with silver hydroxyapatite coatings have been used in total hip replacement surgeries. Silver ions mainly enhance the antibacterial performance of the coating [61]. Our research has explored the design of coatings from two aspects: surface morphology and chemical composition. A micron-scale morphology and stable strontium-containing hydroxyapatite coating was loaded on the surface of tantalum-based materials, providing a new idea for further expanding the value-added utilization of tantalum. In addition, to optimize the bone integration efficiency, the development of new composite coatings with multifunctional characteristics by combining multiple technologies has become a research trend. However, the balance between these components remains a challenge. Some studies have developed multifunctional bioactive interfaces through metal-polyvinyl alcohol networks (MPN), providing a new method for designing surfaces with antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, angiogenesis, and osteogenic properties [62]. Study on the mechanism of Sr + HA promoting osteogenic differentiation of cells

After confirming that Sr + HA enhanced the osteogenic activity of Ta in vitro and in vivo, the molecular mechanism underlying its action on osteogenic differentiation was investigated. RNA-seq was used to determine the gene transcription profile (Lianchuan Bio, LC-P20240327011), and functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed. Compared to the HA group, 1829 differentially expressed genes were identified in the cells of the Sr + HA group, including 864 up-regulated genes and 965 down-regulated genes (Fig. 6A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes showed that they were enriched in three categories: molecular function, cellular component, and biological process (BP) (Fig. 6B). GO biological process analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in cell adhesion, skeletal system development, ossification, integrin-mediated signaling pathways, and cell-matrix adhesion (Fig. 6C). Integrins are a family of type I transmembrane glycoproteins ubiquitous in cells and are composed of α subfamily with 18 subunits and β subfamily with 8 subunits [63]. Ordered micro and disordered nano structures on a titanium surface can induce osteogenic differentiation of cells by promoting the expression of integrin α5 [64]. The more rigid extracellular matrix can promote the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells by up-regulating the expression of integrin-α2 [65]. Transcriptome sequencing and label-free quantitative proteomic analyses are the main methods used to study the dynamic expression of genes in BPs. The transcriptome represents an intermediate state of gene expression and reflects the mechanisms of transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation. Proteins are ultimate executors. To investigate the functional mechanism of Sr, label-less quantitative proteomics (Gikay Gene, M-GSGC0417669) was used to analyze the effect of Sr on protein expression levels. Protein quality was graded by Coomassie brilliant blue staining (Fig. 6G). Heat map analysis of differentially expressed genes by both transcriptomics (Fig. 6F) and label-less quantitative proteomics (Fig. 6I) showed that Itga6, a member of the integrin family, was highly expressed in the Sr + HA group. Itga6 is encoded by the Itga6 gene located on chromosome 2 (2q31.1), and its expression is regulated by multiple transcription factors including MYC and RUNX1. Transcriptome analysis revealed that RUNX1 was highly expressed in the Sr + HA group, and RUNX1 could bind to the promoter region of Itga6 and enhance its transcription [66,67]. Previous studies on Itga6 have focused on a variety of malignancies, such as breast cancer, cervical cancer, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Itga6 can bind to laminin substrates and activate Rac1, PKC, PI3K, and ERK signaling pathways, thereby affecting tumor invasion and prognosis [68]. Additionally, Itga6 can also promote angiogenesis by stimulating endothelial cell invasion and migration through ERK and NF-κB signaling pathways [69]. Notably, angiogenesis is an indispensable part of promoting bone repair, and miR-302b can regulate the expression of Itga6 by targeting the transcription factors E2F1 and YY1 to affect the drug sensitivity of triple-negative breast cancer cells to cisplatin [70]. These studies indicate that Itga6 affects cell adhesion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and drug resistance. However, the role of Itga6 in osteogenic differentiation of biomaterials has not yet been reported. Thus, this study is the first to show that Sr + HA could promote the expression of Itga6. Notably, there were no significant differences in the physical signals of the surface morphology between the Sr + HA and HA groups. The study also showed that the expression of integrin was not only affected by the physical and mechanical signals, but also by the chemical signals of the biomaterials. Previous studies have found that strontium-doped hydroxyapatite coatings improve graft-bone integration through macrophage-derived cytokine mediated integrin α3β1 and αvβ3 [71]; their finding is consistent with the findings of this study.

Through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 6D and H), the top 20 enriched differentially expressed genes were found to be mainly involved in focal adhesion, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed the regulatory roles of differentially expressed genes in focal adhesion and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 6E). The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway plays an important role in regulating cellular functions such as cell metabolism, autophagy, and differentiation [[72], [73], [74], [75], [76], [77]]. The protein–protein interaction network constructed by searching the STRING database revealed that Itga6 interacted with PI3K (Fig. 6J). Itga6 does not possess the pYMXM-binding domain for binding to PI3K, but Itga6 can enhance its binding to PI3K by promoting the tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) and IRS-2, thereby activating the PI3K signaling pathway [78]. The specific cytoplasmic region of Itga6 (amino acids 854–1183) cooperates with ErbB-2 to promote activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [79]. Itga6 can affect mouse nerve development via the PI3K/AKT pathway [80]. Itga6 can also inhibit tumor growth and angiogenesis by down-regulating the PLGF expression in gallbladder cancer via the PI3K/AKT pathway [81]. However, whether Sr + HA affects osteogenic differentiation through Itga6 regulating PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is unknown. Based on the sequencing results, PI3K/AKT was identified as a possible downstream pathway of Itga6-mediated Sr-induced osteogenic differentiation of cells to further explore the molecular mechanism of Itga6 in Sr + HA-induced osteogenic differentiation of cells.

Western blot analysis showed that the expression levels of Itga6 (Fig. 7A) and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway-related proteins (Fig. 7B) were significantly up-regulated in MC3T3-E1 cells in the Sr + HA group; this is consistent with the sequencing results. Furthermore, Itga6 expression was up-regulated and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was activated by Sr + HA. The effect of Itga6 silencing on osteogenic differentiation of cells induced by Sr + HA was observed. MC3T3-E1 cells in the control group were co-cultured with HA and Sr + HA, and MC3T3-E1 cells in the Itga6 shRNA-silenced group were co-cultured with Sr + HA and treated with the PI3K/AKT pathway agonist 740Y-P. The protein levels of Itga6, p-PI3K, p-AKT, ALP, and RUNX2 were significantly down-regulated in the Itga6 shRNA + (Sr + HA) group compared to those in the Con shRNA + (Sr + HA) group. Silencing of Itga6 significantly inhibited the up-regulation of PI3K/AKT pathway-related proteins and inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of cells treated with Sr + HA. Compared to the Itga6 shRNA + (Sr + HA) group, the Itga6 shRNA + (Sr + HA) + 740Y-P group partially restored the protein expression levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT, as well as ALP and RUNX2. The results of cellular immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 7D) showed that the silencing of Itga6 reduced the fluorescence intensity of OPG, whereas 740Y-P partially restored the fluorescence intensity of OPG. The same trend was observed after ALP staining (Fig. 7E). Silencing of Itga6 reduced the number of ALP-positive cells with the lightest color, whereas 740Y-P restored some color. These results indicate that silencing of Itga6 significantly down-regulated the osteogenic differentiation of cells induced by Sr + HA through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In conclusion, Sr + HA activated the PI3K/AKT pathway by up-regulating Itga6 to promote the osteogenic differentiation of cells. Thus, this study enriches the understanding of the mechanism by which Sr promotes the osteogenic differentiation of cells and provides theoretical support for the development of implant materials with excellent osseointegration properties. However, this study has a few limitations; first, the study only focused on cells as the research object of the molecular mechanism of action and needs to be improved in animal experiments. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that microscale Sr + HA coating loaded on Ta substrates significantly improves the roughness, hydrophilicity, and protein adsorption ability of Ta substrates, thereby enabling the continuous and stable release of Ca2+ and Sr2+. Sr+HA exhibited good biocompatibility and enhanced the osteogenic effects of tantalum-based materials in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, Sr + HA promoted osteogenic differentiation of cells by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through the up-regulation of Itga6, which enriched the mechanism by which Sr promotes osteogenic differentiation of cells. Thus, this study provides a theoretical basis for the development of implant materials with excellent osseointegration properties.

Sample preparation

Sr + HA coating and HA coating were loaded on tantalum based materials by hydrothermal method. Hydrothermal reaction solution of Sr + HA: Calcium chloride (CaCl2) (5.39g) and strontium chloride hexahydrate (SrCl2·6H2O) (1.44g) were dissolved in 50 ml deionized water under magnetic stirring to prepare a solution containing [Ca2+] + [Sr2+] (50 ml, total concentration of [Ca2+] + [Sr2+] 1.08 M). A phosphate solution (50 ml, 0.65 M) was prepared by dissolving disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4) (4.62g) in 50 ml deionized water. Disodium hydrogen phosphate solution was added slowly drop by drop to the solution containing [Ca2+] + [Sr2+]. After mixing, the molar ratio of [Ca2+]/[Sr2+] was 9:1, and the molar ratio of [Ca2++ Sr2+]/P was 1.67. Hydrothermal reaction solution of HA: calcium chloride (CaCl2) (5.99g) was dissolved in 50 ml deionized water under magnetic stirring to prepare a [Ca2+] solution (50 ml, total [Ca2+] concentration of 1.08 M). A phosphate solution (50 ml, 0.65 M) was prepared by dissolving disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4) (4.62g) in 50 ml deionized water. Disodium hydrogen phosphate solution was added slowly drop by drop to the solution containing [Ca2+], and the molar ratio of [Ca2+]/P of the mixed solution was 1.67. Then the PH was adjusted to 10 with ammonia water, and the two reaction solutions were transferred to a hydrothermal reactor at a pressure of 0.15 MPa for 24 h at 120 °C. Characterization

The surface and cross-sectional morphology of the material were observed by SEM (SEM, JSM-7100F, Japan), and the elemental composition of the coating was analyzed by an energy dispersion spectrometer (EDS, Oxford MaxN, UK). A laser confocal microscope (Olympus OLS4000, Japan) was used to test and calculate the roughness of the material surface. The chemical composition of the material was tested and analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Haoyuan DX-80BH, China). The surface contact Angle of the material was detected by the water drop shape method. BCA kit was used to detect the protein adsorption capacity of the material. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Thermo, America) was used to measure the ion concentrations of calcium and strontium in the material extract liquid. The coating was washed with deionized water in an ultrasonic cleaner with a frequency of 52 kHz for 60 min at room temperature. The changes of the coating before and after washing were observed by SEM and the elemental composition of the coating was analyzed. Immerse the material in the simulated body fluid for 7 days, test the elemental composition and distribution of the coating.

Cell experiments in vitro

Cell culture

In this study, mouse osteoblast precursor cells (MC3T3-E1) were selected, MC3T3-E1 cells in liquid nitrogen were resuscitated, 1 % double antibody was added to the medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum at room temperature, and cultured in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and the culture medium was changed every 2 days.

Biocompatibility testing

The material was placed in a 24-well plate, and 500 μL of MC3T3-E1 cells (2 × 104 cells/mL) suspension was added to each well on the surface of the material. CCK-8 was used to measure the cell viability at 1, 2, 4, 5 and 7 days, and the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was detected by microplate reader.

The material was placed in a 24-well plate and 2 × 104 cells were plugged into each well, co-incubated for 1 and 3d, and cell viability was assessed by Calcein/PI staining kit. The staining effect was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Calcein AM was green fluorescence, Ex/Em = 494/517 nm; PI, red fluorescence, Ex/Em = 535/617 nm).

Cell spreading morphology was observed by phalloidin staining

The material was placed in a 24-well plate, and 1 mL cell suspension (2 × 104 cells/well) was added to the material surface in a 37 °C, 5 % CO2 incubator. After 24 h of culture, phalloidin and DAPI were added, and the cell spreading morphology on the material surface was observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Determination of alkaline phosphatase activity in cells

The materials were placed in 6-well plates, and 2 mL MC3T3-E1 (2 × 105 cells/mL) cell suspension was connected to each well and cultured in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C for 3 days. The concentration of protein in the samples was determined by BCA kit. ALP reagent was added and the absorbance value at 405 nm was determined. According to the standard curve, the ALP activity on the surface of each material was calculated.

ALP and alizarin red staining

Cells (4 × 105 cells/well) were seeded on the surface of the material in the 6-well plate, co-incubated for 7 days, added BCIP/NBT staining solution, and photographed by optical microscope. After co-incubation for 21 days, 1 % alizarin red staining solution was added to cover the surface of the materials. After washing with distilled water, the materials were observed and photographed by optical microscope.

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

Cells (4 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with the material for 7 days and then incubated overnight (1:500) at 4 °C after the addition of primary antibody dilution. Fluorescent Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:200) was added, and rhodamine-labeled phalloidin was added for staining at room temperature in the dark. DAPI staining was added and observed under the microscope, and the average fluorescence density within the cell area was calculated using Image.

The transcription level of osteogenic differentiation marker genes was detected by real-time fluorescence quantitative real-time PCR

Cells (4 × 105 cells/well) were incubated with the material, and total RNA was isolated from the cells for differentiation analysis. Total RNA was isolated by Trizol reagent for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. Osteogenic markers containing Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and osteoprotegerin (OPG) were then analyzed by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT PCR) using the RT PCR System (Bio, USA). DAVID software was used to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of genes.

Cells were co-cultured with the material, scraped to the side of the dish with a cell scraper, transferred to a centrifuge tube, and centrifuged to remove the supernatant. Animal experiments in vivo

Experimental animals

Thirty healthy male SD rats weighing 300–330 g were used. The handling of experimental animals was carried out in strict accordance with the protocol of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of China Medical University (Welfare and Ethics Approval number CMUXN2023018). Thirty healthy SD rats were randomly divided into 6 groups (5 rats in each group). The sampling time was 4w and 8w.

Experimental methods

The rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital. A 1.5 cm longitudinal incision was made along the long axis of the femur at the lateral end of the femoral metaphyseal, and the distance from the femoral intercondylar fossa was 3 mm. After implantation of porous materials in each group, the wounds were closed by suture layer by layer. The rats were intraperitoneally injected with fluorescently labeled calcein 14 days before sacrifice. Fluorescently labeled xylenol orange was injected intraperitoneally 7 days before death. At 4 w and 8 w after implantation of the material, the rats were sacrificed by CO2 overdose inhalation. The material and about 0.5 cm of surrounding bone tissue were harvested and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde.

Microscopic computed tomography measurement

Micro-CT scanning and metrological analysis were performed. At 70 kV, the current intensity was 200 μA, and the scanning thickness was 20 μm. DateViwer, CTAn and CTvox software were used to scan and analyze the bone formation in the 0.5 mm radius around the implant. The pore occupancy fraction (POF) of the bone volume in the porous structure was calculated as POF=V bone/(V bone + V remaining pores) × 100 %.

Fluorescence microscope observation and metrological analysis

Hard tissue slices were prepared, and the slices were observed by inverted fluorescence microscope. Calcein was green light, and xylenol orange was red light. Images at both excitation wavelengths were collected, processed and merged for fluorescence images. The Integrated optical density (IOD), Fluorescence labeling rate (FLR) and fluorescence double label interval of each group at different time points were calculated to verify the osteogenic ability of each group at different time points.

Histomorphological evaluation

Tissue sections were stained with toluidine blue for histopathological evaluation. Image-Pro Plus software was used to calculate the bone area in the scaffold material (A bone) and the area of residual pores in the field of view (A residual hole). The percentage of bone formation Area (Area%) was calculated at different time points.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. First, the data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison between multiple groups of data that obeyed normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, and t-test was used for statistical analysis between two groups. P > 0.05 (ns) was not statistically significant, but P < 0.05 (∗) was statistically significant.
