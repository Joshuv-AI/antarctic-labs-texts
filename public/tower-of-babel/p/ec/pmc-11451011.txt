Insights and networks: methodological assessment and scientometric analysis of economic evaluations in dentistry
Authors: Nighat Naved, Abhishek Lal, Fahad Umer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAssessing the methodological quality of economic evaluations (EEs) is crucial for evidence-based decision-making. The study aimed to evaluate EEs in restorative dentistry and endodontics, while also analyzing the scientific landscape of researchers and publications through co-authorship and citation network analysis providing an insight into the distribution of scientific expertise.MethodologyA systematic search for relevant articles from 2012 to 2022 was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and EBSCO. Introduction

Economic evaluation (EE) is a systematic approach to assessing the value and efficiency of different healthcare programs [1]. It involves the evaluation of both the costs and the outcomes associated with a certain intervention to determine their cost-effectiveness (CE), cost-benefit (CB), or cost-utility (CU) [2]. A well-conducted EE provides trustworthy evidence contributing to effective decision-making and therefore must adhere to the set quality standards to ensure credibility, transparency, and validity [3, 4]. However, compliance with these standards may vary depending on the specific study design as well as the availability of resources [3].

High-quality EEs can influence decision-making allowing the stakeholders to prioritize interventions that offer the best value for money, thus optimizing the use of available resources and improving overall health outcomes [5]. There is some evidence in literature regarding the quality assessment of EEs in different healthcare specialties [4, 6]. However, it was noted that adherence to the methodologic criteria was deficient with compliance rates as low as 14% for economic analysis of radiological procedures [4]. Likewise, another study revealed compliance rates ranging from 9 to 45% for cost-effectiveness analysis (CEAs) of general surgical procedures published in various journals of medicine and surgery [6].

Dental healthcare resources are limited, and dental care costs tend to increase over time owing to inflation and the increased demand for dental services [7]. To cater to this, conducting research to study the methodological quality of EEs in dentistry is fundamental in ensuring that healthcare resources are allocated efficiently, and decision-making is evidence-based. Standards for methodologic assessment are generally uniform across disciplines but there are essential tenets to which any economic analysis should adhere [8]. Several task forces and panels have provided guidelines for conducting and reporting EEs in the past [9–11]. In this regard, the most widely used tool is the Drummond checklist which appraises the methodological quality of an economic analysis in a systematic manner [12].

To the best of our knowledge, there is deficient literature regarding the methodological quality assessment of EEs particularly in dentistry. Recently a study by Schwendicke et al. comprehensively reviewed the existing economic endodontic literature providing an insight into the available evidence on health economic analysis in endodontics [5]. However, they did not adopt any strict methodological approach to critically appraise the available literature in terms of quality assessment. Moreover, the focus was just on endodontic literature, and the economic evaluation studies in restorative dentistry were not accounted for. Therefore, to address this gap, the present study aimed to perform the methodological assessment of EEs in the field of restorative dentistry and endodontics using the Drummond checklist.

A network analysis in the context of dental health economics provides valuable insights into oral health research by analyzing connections and structure within systems and networks [13]. This analytical approach utilizing a science mapping tool helps to visualize key contributors and their relationships as well as trends in publication and citation in the form of co-authorship and citation network analysis respectively [14]. A co-authorship network analysis facilitates the identification of individuals in the context of specific healthcare settings working in collaboration with others in the same field within and across countries, thereby revealing a global research network [15]. This contextual understanding is essential for ensuring the relevance and applicability of economic assessments in dental care by mapping research trends, identifying influential studies, and highlighting collaboration networks. Likewise, citation analysis helps to identify the key journals involved in the publication of EEs, revealing connections and collaborations within the scholarly community [15]. Moreover, analyzing the citation patterns helps identify gaps for further research and provides insights into how economic evaluations can guide clinical decision-making and resource allocation in a specific dental healthcare setting.

Acquiring insight into the collaborative network of researchers contributing to dental health economics is paramount [13]. This information is valuable for understanding the distribution of scientific expertise, identifying potential research gaps, and fostering international collaborations to address common challenges in the field. Therefore, the secondary objective was to visualize the scientific network of researchers and EEs in the field of restorative dentistry and endodontics by performing the co-authorship and citation network analysis.

Materials and methods

The present analysis followed the preliminary guidelines for reporting bibliometric reviews of biomedical literature (BIBLIO).

Literature search

The authors conducted a comprehensive literature search in collaboration with a medical information specialist (Librarian) in June 2023 to identify the relevant literature in three electronic databases i.e., PubMed (NLM), Scopus, and EBSCO. Moreover, a manual search was performed using Google Scholar to identify the gray literature.

Search strategy

The following search terms were used to identify relevant literature from all electronic databases:

(economic evaluation OR cost-effectiveness analysis OR cost-benefit analysis OR cost-utility analysis OR cost-minimization analysis AND dental OR dentistry OR dental caries OR restorative dentistry OR dental restoration OR amalgam OR composite OR glass hybrids OR minimally invasive dentistry OR pulpitis OR dental abscess OR pulpal necrosis OR endodontic treatment OR root canal treatment OR endodontic retreatment OR endodontic microsurgery OR pulp cap OR pulpotomy OR vital pulp therapy)

(economic evaluation OR cost-effectiveness analysis OR cost-benefit analysis OR cost-utility analysis OR cost-minimization analysis AND dental OR dentistry OR dental caries OR restorative dentistry OR dental restoration OR amalgam OR composite OR glass hybrids OR minimally invasive dentistry OR pulpitis OR dental abscess OR pulpal necrosis OR endodontic treatment OR root canal treatment OR endodontic retreatment OR endodontic microsurgery OR pulp cap OR pulpotomy OR vital pulp therapy)

Screening process

The article citations were transferred to the endnote reference manager software and any duplicate references were removed. The titles, abstracts, and full texts of the articles were screened by two authors against a predetermined inclusion criteria and any disagreement was resolved through discussion amongst the authors.

Data extraction

The data was extracted by three authors on a customized proforma to critically analyze the methodologic quality of included studies using the ten-point Drummond checklist [12]. The Drummond checklist was used as it provides comprehensive insight regarding the key aspects of an EE by considering the robustness of the research question, description of the intervention, study design, identification, measurement and valuation of costs and consequences, discounting, incremental analysis, presentation of results with uncertainty and sensitivity analyses followed by discussion of results in the light of current evidence (further details of the Drummond checklist can be found in the Supplementary Appendix). A rating scale developed by Doran was employed to assign a score of 1 to each item in the checklist provided they fulfill the standard criteria and the aggregate result was rated as poor (1–3 points), average (4–7 points), and good (8–10 points) [16].

The journals in which the included EEs were published were further stratified into Scientific Journal Ranking (SJR), and compliance with methodologic criteria based on the journal’s quartile range was also reported. Moreover, the characteristics of the economic evaluation studies including details of authors, year of publication, journal, impact factor, study setting, type of analysis, chosen population, horizon, perspective, comparator groups, methodology, cost/health outcomes, and results were recorded in another customized proforma designed by the authors.

To fulfill the secondary objective, the bibliometric data of the included studies was derived from the Dimensions database (Dimensions, London, UK) using their digital object identifiers (DOIs). The data were imported as comma-separated values files (*.csv) to VOSviewer version 13.6.1 (http://www.vosviewer.com/, Centre for Science and Technology Studies, Leiden University, Leiden, the Netherlands) for visualizing the scientific landscape of included studies (co-authorship and citation network). The co-authorship network analysis was run with authors and countries being the unit of analysis and the citation analysis was run with sources being the unit of analysis to determine the connections within the network.

Results

The preliminary search yielded a total of 1221 articles. After removing duplicates, the publications were screened for relevance and were then sought for retrieval followed by eligibility assessment as per predetermined inclusion/exclusion criteria. After final screening, 37 full-text articles (restorative dentistry = 20, endodontics = 17) were included. The PRISMA flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Methodologic quality assessment of EEs in restorative dentistry and endodontics

In restorative dentistry, out of the 20 included studies, 16 scored good on the assessment rating scale, while the remaining 4 were of average quality, with none exhibiting poor methodological reporting. Similarly, in endodontics 14 out of the 17 included studies scored good on the assessment rating scale, 2 were of average quality, with only one study exhibiting poor methodological reporting.

While most studies received favorable ratings, a substantial portion of these faced methodological limitations, highlighting the prevalent challenges encountered in the execution of economic assessments for dental healthcare interventions.

Even among studies rated as good, there was a notable absence of reporting on indirect costs and micro-costing related to a specific healthcare intervention, along with incomplete details regarding probabilistic sensitivity analysis in a model-based cost-effectiveness analysis. Average-rated studies exhibited significant shortcomings, including the absence of key elements such as comprehensive presentation and discussion of results addressing user concerns, incremental analysis of costs and consequences, adjustment for differential timing in costs and consequences, credible valuation of costs and consequences, consideration of uncertainty in estimates, and the absence of a well-defined research question. The study with poor reporting only provided details on the comprehensive description of alternatives and the established effectiveness of the programs, while other crucial details were missing (Supplementary Appendix).

In restorative dentistry, most studies (90.4%) were published in Q1 journals based on SJR rankings. Only a single study was published in Q2 and Q3 journals respectively. Likewise, in endodontics, most studies (83.3%) were published in Q1 journals whereas the remaining 2 studies (16.6%) were published in Q2 journals (Table 1).

Co-authorship and citation network analysis

The co-authorship network analysis revealed an overall dense network of researchers with significant link strength in the field of dental health economics. Amongst the largest set of connected items within the network, Prof. Falk Schwendicke V. had a significant influence, with the highest link strength of 34 (total documents = 13; number of citations = 291), followed by Joachim Krois (link strength = 17; total documents = 4; number of citations = 46) and Mark Robertson (link strength = 15; total documents = 2; number of citations = 30). At the country level, Germany (link strength = 14; total documents = 18; citations = 378) had a significant link strength followed by Croatia (link strength = 8; total documents = 2; citations = 7) and Italy (link strength = 8; total documents = 2; citations = 7), whereas the developing countries had a negligible role. The citation analysis revealed that Journal of Dentistry had a considerable impact with the highest link strength of 27 (total documents = 9, citations = 136) followed by Journal of Endodontics (link strength = 22; total documents = 6; number of citations = 223) and BMC Oral Health (link strength = 12; total documents = 5; number of citations = 44) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The assessment of economic evaluations (EE) plays a crucial role in guiding resource allocation decisions, given the constraints on healthcare resources [5]. This study utilized the Drummond checklist to comprehensively analyze the methodological quality of EEs in restorative dentistry and endodontics. The primary objective was to contribute to the discourse on the efficiency and value of dental healthcare interventions, providing decision-makers with robust evidence. Moreover, the secondary aim was to identify patterns, strengths, and weaknesses in existing literature, enhancing our understanding of the methodological landscape in dental economic evaluations and guiding future research in this essential domain.

In the analysis, the distribution of studies in restorative dentistry and endodontics based on SJR quartiles revealed interesting patterns. Most of the included studies (89.18%) were published in Q1 journals with minimal representation in Q2 and Q3 journals, with just three studies and one study respectively. This suggests a concentration of research within high-impact journals, however, compliance with methodologic criteria showed variation. While studies in Q1 journals demonstrated a high overall compliance of 88.36%, Q2 journals exhibited 95% compliance, indicating a potential alignment of quality with impact. Notably, compliance dropped to 40% for Q3 journals, highlighting a discrepancy in methodological rigor among different quartiles. Nevertheless, this could be due to chance because of the limited number of studies in the Q3 range. These insights are vital as the concentration of research in high-impact journals may provide useful information for the researchers to target the submissions strategically however, the observed differences in compliance emphasize the need for a refined assessment of both impact and methodological quality in dental research.

Methodological quality assessment is beneficial as it helps to identify potential biases and limitations in EEs as some studies may overlook certain costs, use inappropriate data sources, or apply flawed analytical methods [6]. The findings of our analysis indicated that while most studies in restorative dentistry and endodontics received positive ratings, some still suffered from methodological shortcomings. Notably, some studies lacked reporting on indirect costs and micro-costing. Moreover, incomplete detailing of probabilistic sensitivity analysis in the model-based EEs was observed. Indirect costs, encompassing factors like productivity loss provide a holistic assessment that extends beyond direct medical expenses [55]. Simultaneously, micro-costing ensures precise analysis of intervention resources, aiding accurate evaluations [56]. Additionally, a probabilistic sensitivity analysis is imperative as it considers uncertainty in estimates, reflecting the real-world variability in costs and outcomes [57]. In the context of dental healthcare interventions, where uncertainties are inherent, the inclusion of probabilistic sensitivity analysis is paramount for ensuring the credibility and reliability of economic assessment results.

The included studies in restorative dentistry and endodontics showcased a notable disparity in the methodologies employed, predominantly favoring microsimulation modeling over data derived from randomized clinical trials. Trial-based analyses leverage empirical data from clinical trials, offering a direct assessment of the intervention’s effectiveness and costs [58]. On the other hand, model-based analyses, simulate complex scenarios through computational models, allowing for the exploration of long-term outcomes and potential variations in real-world conditions [59]. While trial-based analyses may be constrained by the limited timeframe and scope of the clinical trial, model-based analyses offer a more expansive and dynamic perspective, accommodating a broader range of parameters and uncertainties [60]. The preference for one over the other depends on the research question and available data. However, in the context of dental healthcare, where the complexities associated with long-term interventions are significant, microsimulation modeling, can be deemed more robust, providing a comprehensive understanding of the economic implications of interventions over time and under different conditions.

An interesting aspect of our assessment was the identification of distinct perspectives, including payer, societal/community-based, and mixed public/private perspectives across both fields. While payer perspectives emphasize economic aspects, the absence of societal viewpoints is notable. Including societal perspectives is crucial as it considers broader impacts including patient well-being, quality of life, and indirect costs beyond direct healthcare expenses, providing a more holistic understanding of the implications of dental interventions on individuals and communities [61]. Therefore, a balanced approach, integrating both economic and societal perspectives, is essential for informed decision-making and policy development.

The co-authorship network analysis revealed that Prof. Falk Schwendicke had a central connecting role exhibiting the highest level of interaction with other researchers, and co-authoring almost 43% of the included publications. This could be attributed to the fact that a high level of expertise is required for effective contribution to this relatively underexplored domain in dentistry owing to its complexity and interdisciplinary nature. At the same time, while recognizing the value of Prof. Schwendicke’s contributions to the economic considerations in dental care, it is important to note that the concentration of studies from one source may unintentionally narrow the diversity of perspectives in the broader research landscape. This reflection is not a critique of the quality or importance of his work but rather a call for a research environment that encourages a multitude of voices and viewpoints. Diverse participation is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of complex issues in dental health, ensuring that research outcomes reflect a broad range of experiences. Moreover, active contributions from a more varied cohort of researchers can enhance the inclusivity of scholarly discourse, maintaining a vibrant and diverse research community.

Finally, none of the included studies reported a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, indicating a significant gap in the current economic evaluations in dentistry. Establishing a WTP threshold is pivotal as it provides a standardized benchmark for decision-makers to assess the cost-effectiveness and societal acceptability of dental interventions [62]. It is important to note that WTP is not a fixed value but rather a context-specific parameter that is unique to each healthcare setting, depending on the disease burden, economic consideration, patient-related factors as well as available resources [62, 63]. In the US, generally, a WTP threshold of USD 50 000 per Life Year gained for an individual has been quoted [64, 65]. However, the authors are unaware of a predetermined WTP threshold in dentistry yet [66]. This absence hampers the interpretation of cost-effectiveness and limits informed resource allocation decisions. Therefore, future research should address this gap by incorporating well-defined WTP thresholds, enhancing the applicability of study findings, and guiding decisions in the allocation of resources within the context of a specific healthcare setting.

This study is the first of its kind using a strict methodological approach to assess the quality of included literature and science mapping to show co-authorship patterns, trends, and key contributors. However, because only medical and dental databases were included in the search, some dental-related health economic evaluation studies may have been missed. Lastly, owing to the lack of institutional access to the Web of Science database, it was not utilized to retrieve search results.

Conclusion

Most of the included economic evaluation studies received a good rating on the assessment scale indicating that a substantial portion of the research in the field maintains a high methodological standard. Whilst the quantity of good-quality studies is encouraging, the overall quality of evidence remains paramount for effective decision-making in healthcare policy and practice. Therefore, continuous efforts to improve methodological rigor and reporting practices are essential to contribute robust evidence for decision-makers. Furthermore, while the social network of researchers contributing to health economics in restorative dentistry and endodontics exhibits some diversity, participation from developing countries is limited. Hence, we recommend enhancing the inclusivity and diversity of the scientific network of researchers to promote equity and generalizability.
