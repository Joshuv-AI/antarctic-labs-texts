Postpartum Phone Call Intervention to Improve Postpartum Breastfeeding, Support, and Outcomes
Authors: Nikhita Ravikanti, Reece Burns, Jennifer Keomany, Hayrettin Okut, Jessica Kieffer, Holly Montgomery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFew studies have evaluated the utility of a postpartum phone call to assess patient status and improve outcomes. This study examined the effectiveness of a phone call intervention 1 week postpartum to evaluate and improve maternal support and outcomes, including breastfeeding, postpartum visit attendance, and intended contraception use.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients treated at a single obstetrical clinic. The intervention, a scripted, structured phone call performed 1 week postpartum by a nurse, asked about intended contraception use, postpartum depression risk factors, safe sleep practices, and gestational diabetes follow-up care, and also confirmed the postpartum visit. De-identified data were abstracted from electronic medical records to compare outcomes before and after the intervention was implemented.ResultsA total of 653 postpartum patients with a mean age of 27 (standard deviation = 5.3). Exclusive breastfeeding rates were 74.4% pre-intervention vs 62.9% postintervention, exclusive formula feeding was 19.8% vs 18.5%, and mixed feeding rates were 4.3% vs 18% (P = .005). Clinic postpartum follow-up attendance rose from 73.7% to 86.8% (P = .007).DiscussionMixed feeding rates were higher after the intervention was implemented, suggesting that patients who received the postpartum intervention were more likely to attempt mixed feeding.ConclusionsAfter implementing a structured postpartum phone call, mixed breastfeeding rates increased, in addition to postpartum visit attendance.Keywords: Breastfeeding, Formula Feeding, Postpartum

Introduction

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, the American Academy of Family Physicians, and the World Health Organization (WHO) have agreed that postpartum care is vital to the physical, psychological, and emotional health of patients. 1–5 During the first 6 weeks postpartum, maternal deaths, calculated from the onset of pregnancy to the first year postpartum, are at their highest. 2,3

The postpartum period can be defined as the 12 weeks following delivery, and more recent emphasis on its importance has earned this period the title of the “fourth trimester.” 2 There is a national, interdisciplinary initiative to make postpartum care an ongoing, patient-centered process rather than a single patient visit. 2–4 Maternal comorbidities, such as gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, further affirm the need for frequent monitoring in the weeks following delivery. However, up to 40% of women do not attend a postpartum visit, and of those who do have at least one postpartum visit, 43% have reported receiving insufficient information regarding contraception and half have reported poor guidance in the areas of emotional distress and postpartum sexuality. 4,5 Studies have shown that many women worry about discomfort, fatigue, feeding their newborn, and mood changes. In contrast, practitioners are more focused on risk factors for hemorrhage and infection, often leaving patients uncertain of who to ask regarding their concerns. 5,6 Earlier contact with patients during this critical time appears to reduce breastfeeding cessation prior to 6 weeks postpartum. 1,5,7,8

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends increasing follow-up care for these factors, yet implementation of evidence-based guidelines is often complex and variable. 1 Given the established postpartum risks and general lack of consistent methodology for decreasing postpartum issues, care strategies are needed to promote and maximize maternal participation in postpartum care.

Past studies have analyzed the efficacy of maternal education with various uses of phone call interventions to assess maternal concerns and to provide intervention for breastfeeding and depression symptoms. These have reported success in breastfeeding rates and have successfully identified maternal concerns early in the postpartum course. 4,9 One study in Malaysia implemented the use of twice-monthly lactation consults via telephone, which resulted in more exclusive breastfeeding at 1 month postpartum but no difference at 4 and 6 months postpartum. 10 Other studies have identified maternal problems in the postpartum period via a short-interval phone call, but those studies did not assess what interventions were administered or what impact the intervention had on maternal outcomes at their regularly scheduled follow-up visit. 11,12 One 2021 study evaluated medical students providing postpartum triaging at 72 hours after postpartum discharge via a phone call that resulted in several readmissions and medication prescriptions, suggesting the possible need for earlier contact. However, that study, conducted by Abraham et al, did not evaluate maternal outcomes associated with this intervention. 12 A review evaluating GDM postpartum screening found the rate of postpartum follow-up to be better when the practitioner gave reminders to follow-up for procedures, such as an oral glucose tolerance test, using methods such as telephone calls, emails, and short messaging services. 13 These were shown to increase the odds of a postpartum visit by up to 3 times more than when reminders were not sent.

The present study assessed the effect of a scripted, structured telephone call to patients performed 7–10 days postpartum on maternal health outcomes. Outcomes included postpartum feeding methods, contraception intention, postpartum depression, and appointment attendance, as well as screening at-risk patients for GDM. Patients’ concerns were identified and addressed.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study included patients treated at a single obstetrical clinic. The intervention in this study was a scripted, structured phone call performed 7–10 days postpartum by a nurse assessing breastfeeding practices and breastfeeding concerns, postpartum depression, safe sleep education and practices, and a GDM follow-up plan. During the phone call, the nurse also confirmed the patient’s postpartum visit (Appendix 1). De-identified data from the 6-week postpartum visit were abstracted from the medical record to compare outcomes before and after the intervention.

Participants

Eligible patients were postpartum patients seen at a single obstetrical clinic between August 12, 2019, and April 20, 2023. No exclusion criteria were identified for data abstraction.

Instrument

The primary outcome for this study was breastfeeding status, defined using American Academy of Pediatrics and WHO definitions. 14 These were extrapolated as either “exclusively breastfeeding,” “exclusively formula feeding,” or “mixed feeding,” a term that includes any use of breastfeeding in combination with any other feeding methods (ie, formula, water, or donor breast milk). The independent variable was the intervention, the scripted phone call (Appendix 1). This phone call had a structured algorithm used to ask specific questions and give specific responses based on statements made by the patient that were intended to optimize the support given. For example, patients reporting breastfeeding concerns were triaged to the practitioner and lactation services as needed.

Procedures

This study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of Kansas Medical Center. The data abstracted from each patient’s medical records included demographics, maternal history (gravity, history of depression, GDM), contraception intention at the time of the postpartum visit, and breastfeeding status.

Statistical analysis

Clinical data, sociodemographic characteristics, and 6-week postpartum outcomes were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Means and standard deviations (SDs), or medians and interquartile ranges, were reported for continuous variables; counts and percentages were reported for categorical variables. Likelihood–ratio, χ2, and Fisher’s exact tests were used to test the association and agreement for the comparisons of categorical demographic characteristics, clinical variables, and postpartum outcomes [including postpartum visit attendance rates, feeding practices, contraception types, and Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) screening results] between patients who received the phone call intervention and those who did not. Furthermore, Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel tests were used to reveal associations between categorical/nominal variables after controlling for the strata variables in a multiway table. The Mantel–Fleiss criterion was carried out to assess the validity of the χ2 approximation for the distribution of the Mantel–Haenszel statistic. For continuous variables, such as patient age, the Shapiro–Wilks test was employed to assess the assumption of normality. Subsequently, to compare the 2 intervention groups, independent samples t tests (or Welch’s t test in cases of unequal variances) were used for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U-tests were used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. SAS version 9.4 (SAS/STAT; SAS Inc, Cary, NC) was used for all analyses. A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

In total, 653 postpartum patient charts were evaluated. Of those, 561 (85.9%) patients were evaluated after receiving the phone call intervention, and 92 (14.1%) patients were in the cohort that did not receive the postpartum phone call. The ages of the patients from the chart abstraction ranged from 14 to 42 years, with a mean patient age of 27 years (SD = 5.3). Most of the population was not Hispanic and White (69.6%, n = 450; Table 1). More than a quarter (28.2%, n = 184) of the patients had a documented history of depression. Ten percent (n = 70) of all patients had a documented diagnosis of GDM. Of those, 9 patients had scheduled follow-up GDM labs at the time of the phone call. No significant differences in demographic or clinical characteristics before or after the implementation of the phone call intervention were observed. The postpartum visit attendance rate in the group that did not receive a phone call intervention was 73.7% compared to 86.8% in the group that received a phone call intervention (P = .007).

Differences in feeding practices

There was a significant difference in feeding practices between the groups who did or did not have a phone call intervention (P = .005; Table 2). Among patients who did not have a phone call intervention, 74.4% of them (n = 64) were exclusively breastfeeding, 19.8% (n = 17) were exclusively formula feeding, and 4.3% (n = 4) were doing a combination of both. In comparison, of patients who did have a phone call intervention, 62.9% of them (n = 346) were exclusively breastfeeding, 18.5% (n = 102) were exclusively formula feeding, and 18.0% (n = 99) were doing a combination of both.

Differences in contraception intention

Most patients (85.6%, n = 559) had documented contraception intention. Although there was no significant difference in the proportion of patients who noted that they did have plans for postpartum contraception during the phone call, there was a significant difference in the type of contraception intention documented at the postpartum visit (Table 2). Of note, in the group that did not have a phone call intervention, 20.5% (n = 15) intended to use a long-acting reversible contraception (LARC), 17.8% (n = 13) intended to use oral contraceptives, and 23.3% (n = 17) intended to use no contraception. Of the group that did have the phone call intervention, 18.9% (n = 92) intended to use a LARC, 27.2% (n = 132) intended to use oral contraceptives, and 18.5% (n = 90) intended to use no contraception. The difference in the proportions of contraception intention that postpartum patients chose between those who did and those who did not have a phone call intervention was significant (P = .005). When comparing any intention to use contraception at all compared to being undecided or not intending to use contraception, there was no meaningful difference (P = .408).

EPDS

The median EPDS score for the entire patient population was 4 (range: 0–29). Nineteen patients (5.5%) of the phone call intervention group were identified as having a phone call shortened EPDS survey. For the 10th question of the EPDS, regarding self-harm thoughts, 4.3% (n = 2) of the group without the phone call intervention stated that they had experienced self-harm thoughts, whereas 5.2% (n = 22) of those with the phone call intervention stated similarly (P = .8).

Discussion

This study examined the implementation of a scripted, structured postpartum phone call to promote maternal education and provide support for breastfeeding. The 7- to 10-day postpartum period is a critical time frame for intervention. This study is unique in that it used a structured postpartum phone call that offered breastfeeding support and triage for concerns to evaluate exclusive breastfeeding, mixed breastfeeding, and formula-only feeding. Prior studies have shown improvement with exclusive breastfeeding, which is not consistent with the present study, but existing studies do not have data on mixed breastfeeding and formula feeding after a directed phone call intervention with patients.

The present study found that pre-intervention and postintervention groups differed substantially in breastfeeding rates. Although it cannot be directly assessed based on the methods used in this study, the magnitude of difference was greatest in the mixed feeding group. As a result of this study’s phone call intervention, it appears that ~ 13% more patients in the postintervention group were more likely to report mixed feeding practices, whereas exclusive formula feeding remained relatively unchanged and a 12% decrease in exclusive breastfeeding was observed. It is possible that the phone call intervention not only offered some triaging for common issues but also gave encouragement and empowerment for patients to supplement with formula if necessary, which could explain the observed increased mixed feeding and decreased exclusive breastfeeding rates. Overall, the absolute number of patients doing exclusive or any breastfeeding was relatively unchanged between groups at the 6-week postpartum visit. It should be recognized that the barriers that patients face with breastfeeding cannot be alleviated with a postpartum phone call alone.

Although the WHO and the American Academy of Pediatrics recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of an infant’s life, most mothers report breastfeeding problems in the first month postpartum, before the traditional 6-week postpartum visit. 7 Maternal postpartum difficulties, such as postpartum depression, breastfeeding difficulties, and lack of health care practitioner support, are associated with earlier cessation of breastfeeding. In 2022, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported that at 1 month postpartum 78% of women were using any amount of breastmilk to feed their baby, whereas at 6 months postpartum this number dropped to 55%. They reported that only 24.9% of women were exclusively breastfeeding at 6 months. The authors' study group was on track to exceed these results, but the present study offers a viable outlet and intervention for continuing postpartum care that includes breastfeeding triage through a 6-month period. A longer study follow-up time to compare breastfeeding rates at this time frame would help to further validate this phone call intervention as a viable intervention. 15 Qualitative feedback regarding patient perception of the triaging of the phone call would also be a valuable addition to the results.

This study also found significantly different contraception intentions postpartum. The intent to use oral contraceptives at the postpartum visit was greater for the group with the phone call intervention, whereas the intent to use nothing for birth control was lower. Addressing birth control plans during the postpartum phone call may have influenced a greater number of women to choose a less invasive form of birth control rather than use nothing at all when it was addressed at the postpartum visit. It should be noted that LARCs were less often chosen by the group with a phone call intervention. A follow-up study confirming what contraception patients actually used would be enlightening.

The postpartum phone call did seem to increase overall postpartum attendance; although attendance rates initially (73.7%) were already higher than the national average (~ 40%), the attendance rate of postpartum follow-up visits increased to 86.8%. The phone call was easily integrated into the nursing workflow and was subsequently permanently implemented. It would be a viable intervention to apply into most day-to-day office practices.

Limitations

The practitioner in this study had started their practice and initiated the intervention a few months after seeing their first patients and, therefore, did not see as many patients in the period after initiating the intervention. To obtain more participants in this retrospective study, the authors evaluated a longer period after the intervention was implemented, and statistical analyses were done to ensure that this imbalance was accounted for. Utilizing more practitioners with established patient populations could allow for a more balanced comparison group. The scripted, structured postpartum phone call has been implemented as a permanent practice change; thus, an opportunity to repeat the study with a larger sample size could increase the power in future studies and allow for more analysis. Additionally, implementing this study design in the local residency clinics that have a greater population diversity and cultural considerations could improve the applicability to the general population. Additionally, longer-term postpartum follow-up could also be beneficial in evaluating longer-term changes in breastfeeding practices, as well as differences in 6-week contraception intention and ultimately received contraception. Although the phone call script was not validated prior to implementation, it was based on several breastfeeding tools that are widely available and commonly used in clinical practice. This was considered a preliminary, real-world, clinical intervention to determine if this could be integrated into the nursing workflow and refined in future studies and then used to increase postpartum visit attendance rates.

Additionally, interviewing patients to increase the amount of qualitative feedback on their feelings of support and asking if their feeding practice was congruent with their original desired feeding plan would provide valuable insight that would add to the impact of the intervention.

Conclusion

Implementation of a scripted, structured postpartum phone call intervention resulted in an increase in the proportion of patients using mixed feeding at the 6-week postpartum visit after a phone call intervention performed at 7–10 days postpartum. This early postpartum intervention has become a permanent step at the clinic evaluated in the present study and demonstrates the feasibility of using a postpartum phone call to improve maternal and fetal outcomes.
