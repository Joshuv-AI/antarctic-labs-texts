Diurnal temperature range and daily mean temperature in relation to multimorbidity among middle-aged or older Chinese adults: insights from the CHARLS
Authors: Peng Cao, Yanfang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultimorbidity is a major public health burden amid population ageing in China, where over 40% of adults aged ≥ 50 years are affected. While extreme temperatures and air pollution are established multimorbidity risk factors, the independent impacts of long-term routine diurnal temperature range (DTR) and daily mean temperature (DMT) remain poorly characterised. We aimed to investigate associations of long-term DTR and DMT with multimorbidity risk in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.MethodsWe conducted a dual cross-sectional and longitudinal cohort study using nationally representative data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. We included 16,930 adults aged ≥ 45 years for cross-sectional analysis, and 10,447 baseline multimorbidity-free participants for 7-year follow-up. Exposures were 3-year pre-baseline average DTR (mDTR) and average DMT (mDMT). Associations were assessed via fully adjusted hierarchical regression models, restricted cubic splines and prespecified subgroup interaction analyses.ResultsHigher mDTR was linearly associated with elevated incident multimorbidity (HR = 1.042 per 1℃ increase, 95%CI 1.032–1.051), with consistent effects across all subgroups. Lower mDMT had a nonlinear inverse association with multimorbidity (HR = 0.969 per 1℃ increase, 95%CI 0.960–0.978), with a critical 11.3℃ risk threshold and effect modification by age and geographic region. Findings remained robust across all prespecified sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsLong-term exposure to higher mDTR and lower mDMT was independently associated with increased multimorbidity risk among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Taken together, these findings suggest that routine temperature variability is a climate-sensitive environmental exposure with relevance to adaptation-oriented multimorbidity prevention and risk stratification. They also support climate-adaptation strategies, including urban thermal planning, housing protection, early-warning systems, and targeted chronic disease management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28514-7.

Background

Globally, multimorbidity is a major health burden amid population aging and rising chronic disease incidence [1]. Defined by the WHO as the coexistence of at least two chronic conditions, its global prevalence is 37.2% and exhibits substantial continental variations [2, 3]. In China, national cohort data demonstrate a 42.4% prevalence among adults ≥ 50 years and 61% among those ≥ 70 years, imposing substantial individual, caregiving, and socioeconomic burdens [4–6]. Environmental factors make a substantial contribution to multimorbidity, with ambient and household air pollution among the most widely investigated and potentially modifiable exposures [7, 8]. Long-term exposure to fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) has been linked to cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic, and neurological disorders, many of which frequently co-occur as components of multimorbidity [7, 9]. Household fuel type is also clinically important, because the use of solid fuels, such as coal, wood, and crop residues, for cooking or heating can markedly increase exposure to indoor particulate matter and gaseous pollutants, thereby adding to the burden of chronic respiratory and cardiovascular diseases [8]. However, despite extensive evidence on the health impacts of air pollution, the independent effects of meteorological factors—particularly routine variability in temperature—remain less clearly characterized [10].

Diurnal temperature range (DTR) and daily mean temperature (DMT) are established metrics for characterizing daily temperature variability. DTR is calculated as the difference between the maximum and minimum temperatures recorded within a 24-hour period, thereby capturing variation across both daytime and nighttime; DMT represents the mean temperature over the same period [11]. Prior studies have linked these metrics to adverse health outcomes. A systematic review from China reported that lower DMT was associated with higher mortality among older adults, whereas studies conducted in Japan, the UK, and South Korea found associations between extreme DTR and increased risks of mortality, acute arrhythmia, and respiratory infections [12–15]. Unlike sporadic extreme temperature events, DTR and DMT are ubiquitous in daily life, exerting long-term cumulative effects on physiological function and chronic disease progression. Biologically, repeated exposure to large diurnal temperature fluctuations may disturb circadian homeostasis and contribute to sustained low-grade systemic inflammation [16, 17]. In parallel, prolonged exposure to low ambient temperatures can induce persistent vasoconstriction, heighten sympathetic nervous system activity, and impair innate immune function [18, 19]. Collectively, these mechanisms may constitute shared pathological pathways underlying the chronic conditions that form multimorbidity [20]. Their associations with multimorbidity, however, remain unclear.

Although DTR and DMT exhibit distinct geographic patterns associated with latitude, altitude, humidity, cloud cover, vegetation, and regional circulation, they should not be interpreted solely as fixed geographic attributes [21]. Climate observations and modelling studies indicate that daytime and nighttime temperatures have changed asymmetrically under anthropogenic forcing, thereby generating temporal variation in DTR across periods and regions [22]. Recent station-based analyses have reported an increase in global terrestrial DTR during 1980–2021, whereas longer-term attribution studies suggest that greenhouse gases, aerosols, and land-use-related forcing contribute to DTR changes [23]. Moreover, vegetation, urban form, and land-surface processes may further dampen or amplify diurnal thermal oscillations [24]. Taken together, these findings suggest that routine temperature variability should be viewed as a climate-sensitive environmental exposure shaped by natural geographic conditions, anthropogenic climate forcing, and human-modifiable land-surface processes, rather than as an intrinsic attribute of place [21–24]. Accordingly, DTR and DMT should be interpreted not only as spatially patterned climatic conditions but also as temporally dynamic environmental exposures influenced by human activity and climate change. Temperature metrics and air pollution are linked through shared environmental processes. Ambient aerosols and particulate pollutants can modify surface radiation and cloud dynamics, thereby affecting daytime heating, nighttime cooling, and ultimately DTR and DMT [25]. At the same time, DTR and DMT may influence boundary-layer stability, atmospheric ventilation, pollutant dispersion, and secondary aerosol formation, with potential effects on PM₂.₅ concentrations [26]. This interdependence supports the need to account for air pollution when estimating the independent associations between temperature variability and multimorbidity. Accordingly, household fuel type and long-term PM₂.₅ exposure were included as environmental covariates in the fully adjusted models [27, 28].

Current research has critical limitations. Most studies prioritize extreme temperature events over routine DTR and DMT, failing to capture the cumulative health impacts of everyday temperature variability [5, 29]. Many also examine single temperature indicators in isolation, overlooking the joint effects of DTR and DMT [30, 31]. Evidence is further limited by a reliance on Western populations or regional Chinese studies, and few nationally representative data are available. In addition, limited evidence is available on whether these associations differ across key population characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, geographic region, residential location (rural or urban), sleep problems, and smoking status [5, 32, 33]. Clarifying these potential differences is therefore important for informing tailored public health interventions.

To address these limitations, we used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate associations between DTR, DMT, and multimorbidity risk in Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years. On the basis of prior epidemiological evidence and plausible biological mechanisms linking temperature variability to chronic disease pathogenesis, we prespecified three core hypotheses. First, long-term exposure to higher mDTR and lower mDMT would be independently associated with higher prevalence and incidence of multimorbidity. Second, mDTR would demonstrate a positive linear dose-response association with multimorbidity, whereas mDMT would demonstrate a nonlinear inverse association, potentially involving a risk threshold. Third, these associations would vary across key population characteristics, with older adults and residents of northern China expected to be more vulnerable. To test these hypotheses, We adopted an integrated cross-sectional and longitudinal design, with subgroup analyses examining effect modification by age, gender, BMI, alcohol consumption, geographic region, residential location, sleep problems, and smoking status. This study addresses key evidence gaps and informs public health strategies for multimorbidity prevention and management amid global climate change.

Methods

Study design and population

This study employed a combined cross-sectional and longitudinal design using data from the CHARLS [34]. CHARLS adopts a multistage stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling framework in which counties were initially stratified according to geographic region, urbanization level, and economic development. Subsequently, 150 counties or districts across 28 provinces were randomly selected, followed by random sampling of villages or urban communities and eligible households. Through this sampling framework, CHARLS established a nationally representative cohort of community-dwelling Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years [34]. The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), in line with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study used anonymized public secondary CHARLS data and received ethical exemption from the authors’ affiliated IRB.

Baseline data (2011) assessed temperature exposures, participant characteristics, and prevalent multimorbidity. Incident multimorbidity was tracked across three follow-up waves (2013, 2015, 2018). For cross-sectional analysis, we excluded participants < 45 years, those with incomplete meteorological data, and missing multimorbidity status, yielding 16,930 participants. For the longitudinal analysis, participants with incomplete follow-up data across the three consecutive survey waves (2013, 2015, and 2018) were initially excluded. However, all 16,930 participants included in the cross-sectional analysis completed multimorbidity assessments at each follow-up wave, and thus no participants were excluded because of incomplete follow-up. After further excluding 6,483 participants with prevalent multimorbidity at baseline, the final longitudinal analytical cohort comprised 10,447 participants with complete follow-up data. Outcome definition

Multimorbidity was defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions, consistent with the WHO definition [2]. Chronic conditions were identified using self-reported physician diagnoses obtained from the Health Status and Functioning module of the structured CHARLS questionnaire [35]. In this module, participants were asked whether a physician had ever diagnosed them with any of the following 14 conditions: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, cancer or malignant tumor, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive disease, emotional, nervous, or psychiatric problems, memory-related disease, arthritis or rheumatism, and asthma [35]. The primary cross-sectional outcome was prevalent multimorbidity at the 2011 baseline survey, whereas the longitudinal outcome was incident multimorbidity, defined as the first occurrence of multimorbidity between 2011 and 2018 among participants without multimorbidity at baseline.

Exposure assessment

Daily maximum/minimum temperature data (2008–2010) were obtained from the Chinese Research Data Services Platform (https://www.cnrds.com; accessed 31 January 2026), covering 124 cities in 28 Chinese provinces. The meteorological dataset consisted of city- or provincial-level records rather than gridded raster data [36]. For the present analysis, the available meteorological variables were daily maximum and minimum temperatures, from which DTR and DMT were calculated [36, 37]. Before exposure linkage, records with incomplete temperature information were excluded; accordingly, no missing mDTR or mDMT values remained in the final analytic sample. DTR was defined as the difference between the daily maximum and minimum temperatures, whereas DMT was defined as the mean of the corresponding daily maximum and minimum temperatures. Exposure assignment was performed at the administrative level by linking participants’ geocoded residential locations to the corresponding city- or province-level meteorological records available in the CNRDS dataset. Accordingly, the temperature exposure variables represented long-term ambient thermal conditions at the finest administrative scale available in the analytic dataset, rather than individual-level, nearest-station, or microclimate-level exposures. The “region” variable used in subgroup analyses was not the spatial unit used to estimate the primary exposure–outcome associations. Instead, it was included solely as a broad north–south effect-modification variable, whereas the primary associations were estimated at the individual-participant level after residential locations had been linked to the available city- or province-level meteorological records. Nearest-station matching was not performed because the analytic CNRDS extract did not provide weather-station identifiers, station coordinates, raw station counts, source-level validation metrics, or other station-specific information. As a result, participant-to-station distances could not be calculated or visualized. The findings should therefore be interpreted as associations with administratively assigned long-term ambient temperature metrics, with the possibility of non-differential exposure misclassification due to the relatively broad spatial scale of the available meteorological data. Long-term temperature exposure was specified a priori as the 3-year average of DTR and DMT during 2008–2010, denoted as mDTR and mDMT, respectively. This interval represented the pre-baseline exposure window immediately preceding the 2011 CHARLS baseline survey. It was chosen to capture long-term temperature conditions before baseline assessment and subsequent ascertainment of incident multimorbidity, thereby avoiding temporal overlap with post-baseline outcome assessment. For consistency across environmental covariates, the same 3-year pre-baseline window was applied to PM₂.₅ exposure assessment. Individual-level data were spatially linked to city- or provincial-level meteorological data using geocoded residential addresses. Administrative base map data (Tianditu, https://cloudcenter.tianditu.gov.cn; map approval GS(2024)0650) were used without modifications to ensure spatial accuracy.

Covariates

Using validated baseline questionnaires, we collected covariates selected a priori based on established literature linking temperature variability, individual characteristics, lifestyle factors, and environmental exposures to multimorbidity risk [5, 27, 29, 33, 38–43]. Consistent with standard epidemiological practice, covariates were organized into three hierarchical categories: (1) demographic factors, including age, gender, marital status, education level, geographic region, and residential location (rural or urban). In this study, “geographic region” referred to a broad north–south classification of participants’ residential locations, rather than a province-level spatial unit. This variable was therefore used only in prespecified subgroup and effect-modification analyses to assess whether the associations between long-term temperature metrics and multimorbidity differed across broad climatic and socioeconomic contexts; (2) socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, including socioeconomic status (SES), smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, social score, and BMI. SES was defined on the basis of baseline annual per-capita household income reported in CHARLS [44]. Per-capita household income was calculated by dividing total household income by household size and was subsequently categorized into low-, middle-, and high-income tertiles [44].Social score, a standardized composite index derived from CHARLS questionnaires, quantifies the frequency of social interactions and the availability of instrumental and emotional social support [45, 46]—factors consistently linked to elevated risks of chronic disease accumulation and multimorbidity in older adult populations [47, 48]. (3)sleep and environmental factors, comprising sleep duration, self-reported sleep problems, household fuel type, and 3-year average ambient PM₂.₅. Sleep duration and self-reported sleep problems were included as sleep-related covariates because prior studies have linked ambient heat and diurnal temperature range to poorer sleep quality and shorter sleep duration. These covariates were further justified by evidence associating poor sleep quality, sleep problems, and short sleep duration with chronic disease accumulation and multimorbidity in middle-aged and older adults [39, 49]. Household fuel type was dichotomized as clean fuels (natural gas, liquefied petroleum gas, and electricity) versus solid fuels (coal, wood, and agricultural crop residues) [43]. Incomplete combustion of these solid fuels emits high concentrations of indoor particulate matter, carbon monoxide, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, all established risk factors for respiratory and cardiovascular diseases and their co-occurrence in multimorbidity [28, 50]. Long-term exposure to ambient PM₂.₅, a primary constituent of fine particulate air pollution, penetrates deep into the respiratory tract and systemic circulation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic, and neurological disorders that form the core components of multimorbidity [27, 51]. PM₂.₅ data, with a 1-km spatial resolution, were obtained from the China High Air Pollutants database, and the 3-year pre-baseline exposure window was matched to that used for temperature assessments [52]. PM₂.₅ exposure was derived from the ChinaHighPM₂.₅ product, a spatially continuous gridded dataset of ground-level PM₂.₅ concentrations [52, 53].The product provides daily, monthly, and annual PM₂.₅ estimates at a 1-km spatial resolution with complete spatial coverage [52]. Its reported daily cross-validated coefficient of determination (R²) ranges from 0.86 to 0.90, with RMSE values of 10.0–18.4 µg/m³ [52]. This dataset was generated by integrating multiple data sources, including ground-based measurements, satellite remote sensing products, atmospheric reanalysis data, and model simulations [52, 53]. After spatial linkage, no missing PM₂.₅ values remained in the final analytic sample. Station-level information was not directly used in the present analysis because individual PM₂.₅ exposure was assigned from the gridded ChinaHighPM₂.₅ product.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed data, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis H tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Missing covariate data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations implemented with the mice package in R. Twenty imputed datasets were generated using five iterations and analyzed separately, with estimates pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Predictive mean matching was used for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary categorical variables, and polytomous logistic regression for unordered multicategory variables. The imputation model included predictors from the main analytic models, including demographic factors, socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, sleep-related variables, household fuel type, regional category, PM₂.₅, and multimorbidity status across survey waves. The primary temperature exposure variables and PM₂.₅ were not imputed because no missing mDTR, mDMT, or PM₂.₅ values remained after exposure linkage. The imputation procedure assumed that data were missing at random conditional on the observed variables included in the imputation model.

Four hierarchical models were applied in both the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. Model 1 was crude and unadjusted; Model 2 included adjustment for demographic factors; Model 3 further incorporated socioeconomic and lifestyle factors; and Model 4 was fully adjusted, additionally accounting for sleep-related and environmental factors. This hierarchical modelling strategy allowed us to assess whether the associations between temperature metrics and multimorbidity persisted after sequential adjustment for increasingly comprehensive confounder domains, while also estimating the incremental contribution of each covariate block. Multicollinearity was assessed via generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF). This assessment was undertaken because the included demographic, lifestyle, sleep-related, and environmental covariates may be interrelated, which could compromise the stability of regression estimates and increase standard errors. GVIF values < 5 were considered to indicate no substantial multicollinearity. As all covariates met this criterion, no variables were excluded from the final models.

Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations of mDTR/mDMT with prevalent multimorbidity at baseline (reported as odds ratios [OR] and 95% confidence intervals [CI]). Model predictive performance was evaluated via ROC curves. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression quantified associations with incident multimorbidity during follow-up (reported as hazard ratios [HR] and 95% CI); proportional hazards (PH) assumption was validated via Schoenfeld residual tests. Collectively, these models evaluated two related but distinct research questions: whether long-term temperature exposure was associated with baseline multimorbidity burden and whether it predicted the subsequent onset of multimorbidity. For categorical exposure analyses, mDTR and mDMT were categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference category. Accordingly, these analyses were based on quartile-defined exposure groups and did not constitute formal quantile regression models.

To characterize dose-response relationships between temperature metrics and multimorbidity risk, we employed restricted cubic splines (RCS), with ANOVA used to test for statistically significant nonlinearity. RCS analyses were conducted to evaluate potential nonlinear exposure–response relationships and to identify possible temperature thresholds. Based on the mDMT threshold identified through RCS analysis, we generated Kaplan–Meier curves to estimate multimorbidity-free survival and conducted log-rank tests to assess between-group differences. To explore potential effect modification, we conducted prespecified subgroup interaction analyses stratified by age, gender, BMI, alcohol consumption, geographic region, residential location, sleep problems, and smoking status. These subgroups were specified a priori based on epidemiological evidence indicating that these factors may influence thermoregulatory capacity, susceptibility to chronic disease, and exposure patterns related to temperature variability. This rationale was consistent with our prespecified hypothesis that older adults and residents of northern China would be more vulnerable. To further validate the robustness of our primary longitudinal findings, we performed three prespecified sensitivity analyses: (1) excluding incident cases identified in the 2013 first follow-up wave to mitigate potential reverse causation bias arising from undiagnosed multimorbidity at baseline; (2) restricting follow-up to the 2011–2015 period to assess the stability of effect estimates over shorter observation windows and rule out bias from long-term temporal changes in unmeasured confounders; (3) truncating extreme mDTR and mDMT values (defined as observations falling below the 1st or above the 99th percentiles) to quantify the impact of outlier measurements on our effect estimates.

All analyses were performed in R 4.5.0; forest plots and Kaplan–Meier curves were generated in GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Data quality assessment

We evaluated the data quality of key covariates to ensure analytical reliability, with missing rates ranging from 0.00% to 65.48% across indicators (Supplementary Table S1), reflecting heterogeneous data completeness. To mitigate missing data bias, we performed multiple imputation to generate 20 complete datasets, and all subsequent statistical analyses were pooled in accordance with Rubin’s rules.

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 16,930 middle-aged and older adults were included in the final analysis, with baseline characteristics summarized in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 59.11 ± 9.77 years, and 51.16% (n = 8,662) were female. At baseline, 38.29% (n = 6,483) of participants had multimorbidity, and 61.71% (n = 10,447) were free of the condition. For the primary temperature exposure metrics, the median (IQR) values were 12.90 (11.05, 16.18) °C for mDTR and 15.54 (12.23, 17.66) °C for mDMT. Across provinces, the median mDTR ranged from 8.48 °C in Shanghai to 26.17 °C in Xinjiang, whereas the median mDMT ranged from 3.19 °C in Inner Mongolia to 22.89 °C in Guangxi. Detailed province-specific median (IQR) values are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Population-level frequency distributions of mDTR and mDMT are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, and province-specific histograms are provided in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4. Baseline characteristics according to quartiles of mDTR and mDMT are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4, respectively, to summarize the distribution of demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, sleep-related, and environmental factors across temperature exposure levels. For ambient air pollution, the 3-year mean PM₂.₅ concentration was 55.35 ± 16.81 µg/m³.

Baseline characteristics of participants by prevalent multimorbidity status

Participants with multimorbidity had distinct baseline characteristics compared with those without. They were older (60.92 ± 9.63 vs. 57.98 ± 9.70 years), had a higher proportion of female participants (54.39% vs. 49.16%), and were more likely to have lower educational attainment, lower SES, and single marital status. This group also had higher rates of former smoking, never drinking, low-intensity physical activity, obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2), sleep problems, and short sleep duration. For environmental factors, participants with multimorbidity more frequently used solid fuels for heating and cooking, were predominantly resident in northern regions, and had a higher median mDTR (13.25 vs. 12.71 ℃) and lower median mDMT (15.12 vs. 15.63 ℃) than those without multimorbidity.

Geographic distribution of the study population and multimorbidity prevalence

The study population covered 28 provincial-level administrative units across China (Fig. 2A, with administrative division basemap in Supplementary Fig. 5), supporting the generalizability of our findings. At baseline in 2011, provincial multimorbidity prevalence exhibited a distinct north–south gradient (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S5), which aligned closely with the spatial distribution of mDTR (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S2) and was inversely associated with the latitudinal gradient of mDMT (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table S2). The provincial-level maps were used solely to characterize spatial patterns and were not treated as ecological analytical units for estimating the primary exposure–outcome associations. Instead, these associations were estimated using individual-level regression models after participants’ residential administrative locations were linked to the available city- or province-level meteorological records.

In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), each 1 ℃ increase in continuous mDTR was associated with a higher risk of prevalent multimorbidity (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.05–1.07; P < 0.001). Each 1 ℃ increase in continuous mDMT was associated with a lower risk of prevalent multimorbidity (OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.92–0.94; P < 0.001), with this association sustained across all four adjusted models.

Quartile analyses identified significant linear dose-response trends for both metrics across all models (all P for trend < 0.001), with multimorbidity risk increasing incrementally across mDTR quartiles and decreasing incrementally across mDMT quartiles. In Model 4, compared with the lowest quartile (Q1) as reference, the highest mDTR quartile (Q4) had the greatest multimorbidity risk (OR = 1.73, 95% CI 1.54–1.94; P < 0.001), while the highest mDMT quartile (Q4) had the lowest risk (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.47–0.64; P < 0.001). All Q2–Q4 subgroups for both metrics showed significant associations with prevalent multimorbidity in Model 4, consistent with continuous variable results.

RCS curves illustrated linear dose-response relationships of mDTR (Fig. 3A) and mDMT (Fig. 3B) with prevalent multimorbidity risk. ANOVA tests for RCS models confirmed no significant nonlinearity for either metric (both nonlinear P > 0.1), corroborating the linear positive association of mDTR and linear inverse association of mDMT with multimorbidity risk. GVIF analysis ruled out significant multicollinearity among all covariates (Supplementary Fig. 6), supporting regression model stability. Incorporating mDTR and mDMT into the predictive model for prevalent multimorbidity yielded a significant increase in AUC to 0.679 (95% CI 0.670–0.687), with a ΔAUC of 0.013 (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

We performed subgroup interaction analyses to assess effect modification of mDTR and mDMT on incident multimorbidity in middle-aged and older adults. For mDMT, significant interactions were observed with age(P for interaction = 0.035) and region (P for interaction = 0.023) (Fig. 6), indicating its association with multimorbidity risk differed by age strata and geographic region. No significant interactions were found for mDMT with gender, BMI, drinking status, residence type, sleep quality, or smoking status (all P for interaction > 0.05). For mDTR, no significant effect modification was detected across all evaluated subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 7), indicating its association with incident multimorbidity was consistent across all participant characteristics.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed three sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of the primary longitudinal associations (Table 4), including exclusion of incident cases identified in 2013, restriction of follow-up to 2011–2015, and removal of extreme mDTR and mDMT values (Supplementary Tables S5–S7). All sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results with the primary analysis: mDTR remained significantly positively associated with incident multimorbidity (all P < 0.001 in fully adjusted models), and mDMT remained significantly negatively associated (all P < 0.001 in fully adjusted models), with consistent dose-response trends across quartiles (all P for trend < 0.05).

Discussion

This nationally representative, dual cross-sectional and longitudinal cohort study identifies independent associations between long-term temperature variability and multimorbidity among Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. Higher 3-year average diurnal temperature range (mDTR) was linearly associated with elevated prevalent and incident multimorbidity, with consistent effects across all prespecified subgroups. Lower 3-year average diurnal mean temperature (mDMT) was associated with increased multimorbidity risk in a nonlinear pattern, with a critical risk threshold of 11.3 ℃ and effect modification by age and geographic region. These findings directly address key evidence gaps in climate and multimorbidity research, which has historically focused on extreme temperature events rather than routine, cumulative temperature exposure.

The linear, consistent association between mDTR and multimorbidity aligns with prior research linking diurnal temperature fluctuation to adverse acute health outcomes including mortality, cardiovascular events and respiratory infections [12, 13, 54–57]. This study extends beyond existing literature by demonstrating that long-term, everyday temperature fluctuation contributes to the development and progression of multiple coexisting chronic conditions, rather than isolated acute events or single chronic diseases. Robustness of this association is strengthened by consistent findings across both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, which supports a potential causal link between cumulative temperature-related physiological stress and multimorbidity onset. For mDMT, we observed a nonlinear inverse association with multimorbidity risk, alongside a previously unreported critical threshold of 11.3 ℃ in this population. While lower ambient temperatures have been linked to elevated mortality in older Chinese adults, few studies have explored nonlinear relationships or clinically relevant temperature thresholds for multimorbidity [58–60]. Notably, unadjusted Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in multimorbidity-free survival between groups stratified by the 11.3 ℃ threshold, but fully adjusted Cox regression identified a 15% risk reduction above this threshold. This discrepancy is explained by the strong confounding effects of demographic, socioeconomic and environmental factors, which mask the independent protective effect of moderate ambient temperatures in unadjusted analyses. These findings challenge the widespread use of linear dose-response assumptions in climate-health research, and highlight the need to account for nonlinear temperature effects in multimorbidity risk assessment. Subgroup analyses further clarified the population-specific effects of these temperature metrics. The effect of mDMT was more pronounced in adults aged ≥ 60 years and those residing in northern China, consistent with physiological and environmental realities. Older adults have impaired thermoregulatory capacity and a higher burden of pre-existing chronic conditions, increasing their vulnerability to temperature-related stress [61–63]. Northern China has persistently lower mDMT and wider mDTR, which may synergistically exacerbate multimorbidity risk, even after adjustment for individual-level socioeconomic and lifestyle factors. This regional pattern should be interpreted as evidence of effect modification by broader climatic context, rather than as indicating that province-level geography itself constituted the causal exposure. In contrast, mDTR showed consistent effects across all subgroups, indicating that routine temperature fluctuation is a universal, population-wide risk factor for multimorbidity. The lack of interaction for mDTR also explains the observed north-south gradient in multimorbidity prevalence, which is likely driven by the combined effects of regional temperature patterns and other area-level environmental and socioeconomic factors, rather than an isolated effect of mDTR.

The public health interpretation of long-term temperature metrics, particularly DTR, requires careful qualification [23]. Our findings do not indicate that individuals, clinicians, or local health agencies can directly modify outdoor diurnal temperature variability or daily mean temperature over short time scales [23]. Instead, these exposures are relevant to prevention because they capture thermal conditions that are partly shaped by climate forcing, land-surface characteristics, and urban form [23, 64]. At the upstream level, climate mitigation and land-use policies may affect long-term thermal patterns. At the community level, interventions such as urban greening, ventilation corridors, reflective or heat-buffering materials, and improved housing insulation may reduce local thermal fluctuations or limit indoor thermal exposure [64, 65]. Heating and air conditioning may influence the health effects of temperature variability by stabilizing indoor thermal environments. During periods of extreme heat or cold, adequate indoor climate control can mitigate thermal stress, particularly among older adults and individuals with chronic diseases [65]. Nevertheless, these adaptation measures may also entail unintended health and environmental trade-offs. Solid-fuel heating can elevate indoor concentrations of particulate matter and other combustion-related pollutants, whereas widespread reliance on energy-intensive air conditioning may indirectly exacerbate ambient air pollution through increased energy use, depending on the prevailing energy mix [50, 66]. Therefore, effective thermal adaptation strategies should prioritize clean heating technologies, energy-efficient cooling systems, adequate ventilation, and equitable access to indoor environments that provide protection from thermal extremes [65, 66]. Within the health system, temperature-based early-warning services and proactive care for older adults and patients with chronic diseases may help reduce susceptibility to adverse thermal conditions [67]. Accordingly, DTR and DMT should be interpreted as climate-sensitive environmental exposures relevant to multimorbidity prevention, rather than as exposures that are directly modifiable at the individual level [5, 23].

Multiple biological pathways may underpin these observed associations, independent of the sleep and environmental covariates adjusted for in our models. Repeated exposure to wide diurnal temperature fluctuations can disrupt circadian regulation and trigger sustained systemic inflammation, a core pathological driver of most chronic conditions included in our multimorbidity definition [68–71]. Lower long-term ambient temperatures can induce persistent vasoconstriction, elevate blood pressure, and impair immune function, all of which contribute to the onset and progression of cardiovascular, metabolic and infectious chronic diseases [72–75]. The 11.3 ℃ threshold for mDMT likely reflects a physiological tipping point, below which the body’s adaptive thermoregulatory mechanisms are overwhelmed, leading to accelerated chronic disease development.

Notable strengths of this study include its use of a nationally representative cohort with multistage stratified sampling, which addresses the limitations of prior regional Chinese studies and improves generalizability to the broader middle-aged and older Chinese population. The integrated cross-sectional and longitudinal design allows for both cross-sectional prevalence assessment and temporal evaluation of incident multimorbidity, thereby providing stronger temporal evidence than cross-sectional analyses alone, while causality cannot be established because of the observational design. We simultaneously assessed both mDTR and mDMT, avoiding the residual confounding that affects single-indicator temperature studies, and employed rigorous statistical approaches including hierarchical adjustment, restricted cubic splines and multiple sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our findings. Incorporation of mDTR and mDMT also significantly improved the predictive performance of the multimorbidity risk model, highlighting the potential clinical value of these temperature metrics for early risk stratification of chronic disease burden.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As an observational study, causal relationships cannot be established; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors including indoor temperature exposure and detailed individual physical activity patterns may affect our results, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of demographic, lifestyle and environmental covariates. Attrition bias due to loss to follow-up is unlikely to have materially influenced the longitudinal findings, as all 16,930 participants included in the cross-sectional analysis completed multimorbidity assessments at each follow-up wave, and no participant was excluded because of incomplete follow-up. Accordingly, comparing baseline characteristics between participants lost to follow-up and those retained in the longitudinal analysis was not applicable. Nevertheless, selection bias related to the initial eligibility criteria and complete-data requirements cannot be entirely excluded. Temperature exposure was estimated from city- or provincial-level meteorological records assigned according to participants’ geocoded residential administrative locations, rather than from individual-level, nearest-station, or high-resolution gridded temperature measurements. This broad spatial scale may have introduced non-differential exposure misclassification, particularly in provinces or cities with substantial within-area climatic heterogeneity. In addition, although geocoded residential addresses enabled administrative-level exposure assignment, the analytic CNRDS extract did not provide weather-station identifiers, station coordinates, raw station counts, source-specific validation metrics, or other station-level information for the meteorological dataset. As a result, participant-to-station distances could not be calculated or visualized, which limited our ability to quantify spatial uncertainty in the temperature exposure estimates. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as associations with long-term ambient temperature conditions assigned at the available administrative scale, rather than as estimates of personal-level or microclimate-level thermal exposure. Future studies incorporating station coordinates, high-resolution gridded meteorological products, indoor temperature data, or personal exposure measurements are needed to reduce spatial uncertainty and validate these associations at finer geographic scales. We did not incorporate other climatic factors such as humidity and precipitation, which may interact with temperature to affect multimorbidity risk. Finally, the median 7-year follow-up period limits our ability to assess very long-term effects of temperature exposure on multimorbidity progression over the lifespan.

Conclusion

This nationally representative, dual cross-sectional and longitudinal cohort study demonstrates that long-term higher mean diurnal temperature range (mDTR) and lower mean diurnal temperature (mDMT) are independently associated with increased multimorbidity risk among Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. We identified a linear dose-response relationship for mDTR with consistent population-wide effects, alongside a nonlinear inverse association for mDMT with a critical 11.3 ℃ risk threshold and effect modification by age and geographic region.

These findings indicate that routine temperature variability may serve as a climate-sensitive environmental correlate of multimorbidity, with relevance to chronic disease prevention, urban adaptation, and climate-informed health risk stratification. Future research should further elucidate the underlying causal pathways, assess indoor and personal-level thermal exposures, and determine whether comparable associations are evident across diverse climatic and socioeconomic contexts.
