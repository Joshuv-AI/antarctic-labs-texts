Colonization by Porphyromonas gingivalis in cervical squamous cell carcinomas promotes metastasis through FimA/CD151/ITGB1 signaling
Authors: Xiuting Huang, Yuan Zhuang, Rui Wang, Jing Qu, Yuanyang Tan, Liang Li, Wenjun Pan, Huilong Nie, Kefeng Li, Yujing Lin, Liutong Yi, Xiaoyu Feng, Huimin Lan, Guangxue Wang, Xiantao Zeng, Zhenqi Jiang, De-Yun Wang, Yongkang Qiao, Yan Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervicovaginal microbiota dysbiosis has been implicated in the progression of cervical cancer. Recent studies have reported an increased abundance of Porphyromonas species in the vaginal microbiota of cervical cancer patients. Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), a key periodontal pathogen, has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and bacterial vaginosis; however, its potential role in the progression of cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) remains largely unexplored.MethodsWe employed immunohistochemistry (IHC), 16S rRNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and immunofluorescence to detect P. gingivalis in CSCC tissues. The association between P. gingivalis colonization and CSCC patient survival outcomes was assessed. Transwell and wound healing assays were used to assess the migration and invasion abilities of CSCC cells. In vivo xenograft mouse models were established to evaluate the impact of P. gingivalis on tumor growth and metastasis. Pull-down assays were employed to investigate interactions between the P. gingivalis fimbrial protein FimA and host cell membrane proteins. RNA sequencing and Western blotting were utilized to identify signaling pathways activated by P. gingivalis in host cells.ResultsPorphyromonas gingivalis was detected immunohistochemically in 63% of CSCC tumor tissues, with significantly higher colonization in tumors compared to adjacent nontumor tissues (P < 0.0001). The presence of P. gingivalis was significantly associated with advanced tumor stage (P < 0.01), distant metastasis (P < 0.05), lymph node metastasis (P < 0.01), and poor survival outcomes (P = 0.0257, HR = 3.167) in CSCC patients. P. gingivalis preferentially adhered to CSCC cells and promoted cell migration and invasion. In this study, animal models revealed that P. gingivalis promoted lung and lymph node metastasis in CSCC without affecting tumor growth. Pull-down assays revealed that FimA interacts with CD151 and integrin β1 (ITGB1), which are highly expressed in CSCC cells. Knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1 significantly reduced P. gingivalis adhesion to CSCC cells (P < 0.01) and suppressed its effects on cell migration and invasion (P< 0.05). P. gingivalis treatment activated the JNK/paxillin pathway and triggered actin cytoskeleton reorganization.ConclusionThis study identifies P. gingivalis as a tumor-associated bacterium that promotes CSCC metastasis through direct interaction between its fimbrial adhesin FimA and the host CD151/ITGB1 complex. This interaction activates JNK/paxillin signaling, induces cytoskeletal reorganization, and enhances the metastatic capacity of CSCC cells. Targeting this microbial-host interaction may provide a novel therapeutic intervention for P. gingivalis-driven CSCC metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06928-y.Keywords: CD151, Cervical squamous cell carcinoma, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Metastasis, ITGB1

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women [1, 2], with CSCC accounting for approximately 70% of all cases [3, 4]. While high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a well-established etiological factor in cervical carcinogenesis, only 10–15% of HPV-infected women progress to cervical carcinoma, suggesting that additional factors in the cervicovaginal microenvironment contribute to tumor initiation and progression [5, 6]. Recent studies indicate that cervicovaginal microbiota dysbiosis is implicated in cervical carcinogenesis; however, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood [7, 8]. A characteristic shift in microbial composition, marked by a great reduction in Lactobacillus species and a concurrent increase in anaerobic bacteria, has been observed in cervical cancer patients [6]. While several studies have reported altered cervicovaginal microbiota in cervical cancer, specific bacterial species that actively contribute to tumor progression remain largely unidentified.

Increasing evidence has linked periodontitis-associated pathogens to systemic diseases and various cancers, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer [9–11]. A potential oral-female reproductive tract (FRT) axis has been proposed [12], supported by epidemiological associations between periodontitis and adverse pregnancy outcomes as well as bacterial vaginosis [13]. Porphyromonas species, which are members of the vaginal microbiota in healthy women, are found in greater abundance in conditions such as bacterial vaginosis, endometrial cancer, and cervical cancer [13–15]. Porphyromonas is a Gram-negative, strictly anaerobic, nonspore-forming, nonmotile genus that resides in the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and urogenital tract, and has been implicated in chronic oral infections and carcinogenesis [16]. Porphyromonas gingivalis, a pivotal pathogen in periodontitis, has been associated with bacterial vaginosis and adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preeclampsia, preterm delivery, spontaneous abortion, and intrauterine growth restriction [17–19]. Furthermore, P. gingivalis has been detected in the placenta, umbilical cord, and amniotic fluid of patients with poor pregnancy outcomes, suggesting its potential impact on reproductive health [20–22]. Despite these associations, the contribution of P. gingivalis to CSCC progression and its underlying molecular mechanisms remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we identified P. gingivalis as a microbial contributor to CSCC progression. Our findings demonstrate that P. gingivalis selectively colonizes CSCC tumor tissues and is significantly associated with poor patient survival. In vivo xenograft models further revealed that P. gingivalis promotes metastasis without affecting tumor growth. Mechanistically, P. gingivalis adheres to CSCC cells via its fimbrial adhesin FimA, which binds to the host CD151/ITGB1 complex, thereby activating the JNK/paxillin signaling pathway and enhancing cell migration and invasion. These findings establish a novel link between P. gingivalis colonization and CSCC progression, offering new insights into a previously unrecognized microbial mechanism that drives tumor metastasis, and identifying a potential therapeutic target for CSCC patients with P. gingivalis colonization.

Method

Clinical specimens

90 pairs of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) CSCC tissues and corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues were retrospectively collected from the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from September 2017 to June 2021. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathological confirmation of CSCC; (2) absence of genital tract infections; (3) no use of antibiotics, probiotics, or antifungal drug within 3 months; (4) no history of other malignancies; and (5) no prior anti-tumor therapy before surgery. Tumor staging was performed according to the FIGO (2009) classification system and verified by institutional pathologists. Ethical approval was granted by the hospital’s ethics committee (No. K72-1, 2024).

HPV status was not used as an inclusion/exclusion criterion during initial study design. Based on clinical records, HPV status was classified into three categories: positive (confirmed infection), negative (validated absence), and unknown (missing or inconclusive data).

To minimize sampling bias, a strict standardized tissue sampling protocol was implemented throughout the study. Tissues were aseptically dissected on ice and processed within 30 min after resection. All tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were collected by experienced pathologists following strict standardized protocols. Tumor specimens were obtained from central viable regions with avoidance of necrotic/hemorrhagic areas. Histopathological validation was performed for all cases by pathologists using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to confirm tumor type. Only tumor specimens with exceeding 30% neoplastic cells were included, ensuring that sufficient viable tumor regions were available for quantitative analysis [23]. Matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were sampled at a distance of ≥ 2 cm from the tumor margin.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded CSCC tissues were cut into 5-μm-thick sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were heated in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 ℃ 10 min for retrieve antigen, and treated with 3% H2O2 for endogenous peroxidase quenching. The sections were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Cat no. PH0424, Phygene, Fuzhou, China) and 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Cat no. V900933, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 0.2% Triton X-100 for 30 min. Primary antibodies (anti-Gingipain/P. gingivalis, Cat no. 61BG1.3, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa, USA; anti-LPS/P. gingivalis, Cat no. SAB4200832, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were applied on slides overnight at 4 ℃ and secondary antibody (Cat no. PV-6000, ZSBIO, Beijing, China) was added for 60 min at room temperature. The Rabbit IgG Isotype Control (Cat no. #3900, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA) was used as a negative control. After washing with PBS 3 times, sections were stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Cat no. ZLI-9017, ZSBIO, Beijing, China). After hematoxylin counterstaining, the sections were dehydrated and sealed with neutral balsam (Cat no. ZLI-9555, ZSBIO, Beijing, China).

Evaluation of IHC results

CSCC tissue sections were independently assessed by two experienced pathologists. For semi-quantitative evaluation, five high-power fields (HPFs; 400 × magnification) were randomly selected for each tissue section. In each field, tumor regions were manually delineated, and areas with positive immunostaining were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) through color thresholding [24]. The percentage of positive cells was calculated as: (Positive Area / Total Tumor Area) × 100%. The average percentage of positive cells was then calculated across the five fields and used for IHC scoring. Staining intensity was graded on a scale of 0 to 3: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The proportion of positive cells was scored as follows: 0 (0–5%), 1 (6–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The IHC score was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score by proportion score [25, 26]. The samples were classified into negative (IHC score < 2) or positive (IHC score ≥ 2) group.

Immunofluorescence assays

For paraffin-embedded tissues, after deparaffinization, rehydration and antigen retrieval steps, sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-X 100 in PBS for 10 min and blocked with 5% normal donkey serum (Cat no. 017-000-121, Jackson Immunoresearch, Philadelphia, PA, USA), 3% BSA and 0.05% Triton-X 100 for 30 min. Primary antibodies were applied on slides overnight at 4 ℃ and secondary antibody (Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488, Cat no. A21206, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing DAPI was added for 60 min at room temperature. Slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Cat no. P36930, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Images were acquired with a laser confocal microscopy (ZEISS LSM880, Jena, Germany).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay

The probe (sequence: 5′-CAATACTCGTATCGCCCGTTATTC-3′) specific to P. gingivalis 16S rRNA was conjugated with FITC and bacterial universal probe EUB338 (sequence: 5′-GCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3′) labelled with Cy3 was used as a positive control. FISH assay was used to detect P. gingivalis colonization in paraffin-embedded CSCC sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were treated with 0.2 N HCl for 15 min at room temperature and Proteinase K for 10 min at 37 °C. After incubation with blocking buffer on 55 °C for 2 h, specimens were incubated with probes in a dark and humidified chamber at 42 °C overnight. After 3 times washing, specimens were stained with DAPI and mounted with antifade mounting medium. Images were acquired with laser confocal microscopy (ZEISS LSM880, Jena, Germany).

Cell culture

The human CSCC cell lines, including SiHa, HCC94, CaSki, C33A and MS751, were provided by the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). SiHa, HCC94, CaSki, C33A and MS751 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Cat no. C11995500BT, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat no. 10270-106, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Human cervical epithelial cells (HCEC) were purchased from iCell company (Shanghai, China). HCEC cells were cultured in primary epithelial cell culture medium (Cat no. PriM-iCell-001 iCell, Shanghai, China). All CSCC cell lines and HCEC cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Bacterial culture

P. gingivalis strain 33277 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). P. gingivalis from − 80 °C glycerol stock was inoculated on blood agar plate (HuanKai Microbial, Cat no. 024070) at 37 °C in anaerobic jar with AnaeroPack (Cat no. C-1 and C-31, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical Company, Tokyo, Japan) for 5 days. For liquid culture, P. gingivalis was inoculated in brain heart infusion broth (BHI, Cat no. 024053, HuanKai Microbial, Guangzhou, China) supplemented with hemin (5 μg/mL) and vitamin K (1 μg/mL) for 24 h. Lactobacillus crispatus strain ATCC 33820 was grown in MRS broth or agar (Cat no. CM0359B, Cat no. CM1153, Oxoid, Hampshire, UK) at 37 °C under anaerobic condition for 1–2 days. Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC 33820, a vaginal commensal strain known to exhibit minimal adherence and no invasive potential toward cervicovaginal epithelial cells, was used as a negative control in adhesion assays [27–29].

CFSE-labeled P. gingivalis

After overnight cultivation, bacterial culture was harvested in mid-logarithmic phase and optical density (600 nm) was measured. 1 mL of bacterial culture was subjected to centrifugation at 6,000 × g for 5 min. The pellet was washed by PBS and then resuspend by cell culture medium. For heat-inactivation, the P. gingivalis suspension was incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. To obtain bacteria-free supernatant, the culture supernatant was filtrated through a 0.2 μm-pore-size filter. To obtain green-fluorescent-P. gingivalis, the pellet of P. gingivalis was resuspend by PBS and incubated with CFSE (Cat no. C34554, Thermo Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min; After incubation, the bacteria was subjected to 6000 × g for 5 min, was washed by PBS and then resuspended with cell culture medium prior to use.

Bacterial adhesion and invasion assays

For bacterial adhesion assays, CSCC cells were co-cultured with CFSE labeled bacteria at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10, 50 and 100 for 2 h or the indicated time points. After co-culture, to eliminate non-adhesive bacteria, cells were washed three times by PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS. For imaging, F-actin was stained with TRITC-phalloidin (Cat no. MX4405, MKBio, Shanghai, China) and nuclei were stained with DAPI. For bacterial invasion assays, cells were co-cultured with CFSE labeled bacteria at MOI of 10, 50 and 100 for 2 h or the indicated time points. To eliminate extracellular bacteria, cells were digested with trypsin and collected by centrifugation. The cell pellet was resuspended in cell culture medium with 50 μg/mL gentamicin and seeded into glass bottom dishes. After growth for 24 h, cells were fixed with 4% PFA and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS. For imaging, F-actin was stained with TRITC-phalloidin (Cat no. MX4405, MKBio, Shanghai, China) and nuclei were stained with DAPI. After staining, cells were observed by laser scanning confocal microscope (ZEISS LSM880, Jena, Germany).

Cell migration and invasion assay

Transwell assays were performed to assess the capacity of migration and invasion of CSCC cells. Transwell chambers (Cat no. 353097, Corning Falcon, NY, USA) equipped with 8.0 µm pore diameter inserts were used for cell migration and invasion assays. Inserts coated with Matrigel matrix (Corning Falcon, Cat no. 354234, NY, USA) were used for cell invasion assay, and uncoated inserts were used for cell migration assay. Briefly, cells were suspended with serum-free medium containing 5 × 105 cells/mL and 0.1 mL of cells suspension (5 × 104 cells) were seeded to each insert. Then, 0.7 mL of cell culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to the wells of 24-well plate. The plate with Transwell chambers was cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h (migration assay) or 36 h (invasion assay). After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and stained with crystal violets staining solution (Cat no. C0121, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. Non-migrating or non-invading cells into the top of insert were scrubbed gently with cotton swabs. Three random areas of each insert were imaged under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and counted by ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Experiments were repeated 3 times independently.

Cell proliferation assays

Cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded to 24-well plate and grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Cat no. C11875500BT, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. For bacterial co-culture, cells were exposed to P. gingivalis or heat-killed P. gingivalis with indicated MOI for 6 h in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere. After co-culture, medium containing the bacteria was removed and washed with PBS three times and the medium was replaced with RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 50 μg/mL gentamycin. The cells co-cultured with P. gingivalis was photographed by IncucyteS3 instrument (Sartorius, Goettingen, Germany) every 6 h and cell confluence analysis was performed by Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System. The co-culture was observed for four consecutive days.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8 assay)

For cell viability assay, cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were seeded to a 96-well plate and co-cultured with P. gingivalis indicated MOI (10, 50, 100) for 6 h. After co-culture, medium containing the bacteria was removed and cells were washed with PBS three times and the medium was replaced with RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 50 μg/mL gentamycin. The co-culture was performed for four consecutive days. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution was added to the cell culture (1:9 ratio). After 1 h incubation at 37 °C in dark, absorbance at 450 nm was measured on multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek Synergy LX, Winooski, Vermont, USA).

His pull-down assay

The His pull-down assay was performed using Pierce Pull-Down PolyHis Protein: Protein Interaction Kit (Cat no. 21277, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To generate the recombinant polyhistidine-tagged FimA protein (His-FimA), P. gingivalis fimA gene (GenBank: NC_010729.1) was cloned into the pET-28a ( +) vector and transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells. The transformed bacteria were then inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) broth containing 50 μg/mL Kanamycin and grown at 37 °C overnight. The bacterial culture was diluted into 1:20 ratio, when the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.4–0.6, Isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG) was added to get a final concentration of 0.1 mM to induce the expression of the recombinant proteins at 18 °C. After overnight expression, the bacterial pellets were collected, suspended in TBS and lysed with Pierce Lysis Buffer containing protease inhibitor by incubating the bacteria on ice for 30 min. The supernatant containing the His-FimA proteins was collected after centrifugation at 4 °C for 5 min at 12,000 × g. To perform the pull-down assay, His-FimA protein was incubated with HisPur Cobalt Resin at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by adding cell lysate from SiHa cells and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The resins were then washed 5 times and eluted by Elution Buffer. The eluted proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by silver staining or western blot, and protein bands were identified by mass spectrometry.

Silver staining

Silver staining was performed by Silver Staining Kit (Cat no. P0017S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After separating the proteins by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, the gel was fixed with Fixing Solution (50% ethanol, 10% acetic acid, and 40% ultrapure water) for at least 20 min. The gel was washed with Ethanol Wash Solution (30% ethanol) for 10 min and ultrapure water twice for 10 min each. After that, the gel was incubated with Sensitizer Working Solution for 2 min and ultrapure water twice for 1 min each. The gel was incubated with Silver Staining Solution for 10 min and quickly washed with ultrapure water for 1 min. Following that, the gel was incubated with Developer Working Solution until protein bands appeared (3–10 min). The gel was washed with Stop Solution for 10 min and ultrapure water for 5 min. The target protein bands were excised from the gel and subjected to mass spectrometry.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Proteins were reduced using 5 mM DTT in 50 mM ABC at 56 °C for 30 min, followed by alkylation with 15 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 30 min. Digestion was performed with trypsin at 37 °C for 20 h. The resulting peptides were desalted, dissolved in 0.1% FA, and stored at −20 °C until use. Each sample was separated using the nanoflow HPLC system nanoElute (Bruker), then analyzed by timsTOF Pro mass spectrometry (Bruker). Acquired MS data were searched against the SwissProt Human database using MaxQuant (1.6.14). Trypsin/P was selected, allowing up to 2 missed cleavages. Mass tolerance for precursor and fragment ions was set to 20 ppm and 0.1 Da, respectively. Protein FDR and peptide FDR were both set to 0.01.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cell lysates or recombinant proteins were incubated with 5 μg anti-ITGB1 or anti-CD151 antibodies (immunoglobulin G antibody was used as a control) at 4 °C overnight. 50 μL Protein G Magnetic Beads (Cat no. HY-K0204, MedChemExpress) was added to the mixture for 4 h incubation at 4 °C. The beads were washed with 0.05% PBST for 5 times and boiled with SDS-PAGE gel loading buffer for 5 min at 95 °C. Eluted proteins were detected by western blot.

Antibody neutralization assay

To assess whether the interaction between FimA and CD151/ITGB1 mediates P. gingivalis adhesion and subsequent tumor cell migration, antibody blocking assays were performed. SiHa cells were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Cells were serum-starved for 2 h, followed by pre-incubation with anti-CD151 antibody (10 µg/mL), and anti-ITGB1 antibody (10 µg/mL), or an isotype-matched control IgG for 1 h at 37 °C. Live P. gingivalis was then added and co-cultured for 2–4 h. For bacterial pre-blocking experiments, P. gingivalis was incubated with anti-FimA antibody (20 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h before being added to the cells. After incubation, cells were washed thoroughly with PBS to remove unbound bacteria or protein. Adhesion was quantified by fluorescence microscopy using CFSE-labeled P. gingivalis. Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed immediately after the blocking step to assess functional consequences.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells co-cultured with P. gingivalis at MOI of 50 for 6 h were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (pH 7.4) solution for 2 h. Samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) and fixed in 1% osmic acid solution at 4 °C for 2 h. Then the samples were gradient dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in Epon-Araldite resin for penetration and placed in a mold for polymerization. After the semi thin sections were used for positioning, the ultrathin sections were made and counterstained with solution containing 3% uranyl acetate and 2.7% lead citrate. The sections were observed and imaged with a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI HT7800, Tokyo, Japan).

RNA-seq

Cells were grown in a 6-well plate and cocultured with P. gingivalis at MOI of 50 for 4 h. After washing with sterile PBS. Total RNA was extracted from SiHa cells using the TRIzol reagent (Cat no.15596-018, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. RNA sequencing was performed with an Illumina HiSeq4000 sequencer (Illumina, USA) by GENEWIZ company (Suzhou, China).

Stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM)

For c-STORM imaging, SiHa cells were seeded in glass bottom dishes at a density of 5 × 104 per well in growth medium. After 1 day of incubation, the cells were co-cultured with P. gingivalis at MOI of 50 for 4 h and then were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, blocked with 10% normal donkey serum for 60 min, and incubated with primary antibodies (anti-CD151, Cat no. ab33315, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA; Anti-Integrin beta 1, Cat no. ab24693, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at 4 °C overnight. After washing 5 times, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488, Cat no. A21206, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA; anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 647, Cat no. A31571, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA) for 60 min at room temperature, washed with PBST 5 times, and post-fixed with 3.0% PFA/0.1% glutaraldehyde for 10 min. After washing with PBS 3 times, samples were kept in imaging buffer that contained 50 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, 0.5 mg/mL glucose oxidase (Sigma-Aldrich), 40 μg/mL catalase (Sigma-Aldrich), 10% (w/v) glucose and 143 mM β-mercaptoethanol. Then the samples were imaged on STORM microscope (Nikon CORPOR ATION, Tokyo, Japan) in c-STORM mode.

Structured illumination microscopy (SIM)

SiHa cells were seeded glass bottom dishes at a density of 1 × 105 per well in growth medium. After 1 day of incubation, the cells were co-cultured with P. gingivalis for indicated time. Cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and stained with TRITC- phalloidin (Cat no. MX4405, MKBio, Shanghai, China) and DAPI for 30 min. Images were acquired on a SIM microscope (Zeiss Elyra7, Jena, Germany). Raw SIM images were processed with ZEN3.0 SR Software (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Histopathological analysis

Mouse lung tissues and lymph node were fixed with 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin and cut in 5 μm sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Cat no. PH0516, Phygene, Fujian, China), dehydrated and sealed with neutral balsam (Cat no. ZLI-9555, ZSBIO, Beijing, China).

Gene knockdown and overexpression

For CD151 and Integrin β1 knockdown, non-target siRNA and siRNA targeting CD151 (siCD151) or integrin β1 (siITGB1) were purchased from GenePharma (Suzhou, China). siRNA was transfected into SiHa cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Cat no. 11668019, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 2 days of transfection, cells were used for bacterial co-culture assays. For CD151 and ITGB1 expression, the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of CD151 and ITGB1 were cloned into lentiviral vector pCDH-puro (IGEbio, Guangzhou, China). Lentiviruses were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, 293T cells were transfected with a mixture of viral plasmids and lentiviral packaging plasmids using the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent. After 24 h of transfection, medium was replaced by fresh medium and viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h. After filtration through 0.45 μm-pore size filters viral supernatants were concentrated using an ultracentrifuge (Beckman, Optima L-100XP, Miami, FL, USA). SiHa cells were infected with lentiviruses including CD151and ITGB1, and screened with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Cat no. P8833, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 2 weeks.

Western blot

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Cat no. 89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing 1 × protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor (Cat no. P1045, Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were measured by the bicinchoninic acid assay (Cat no. PH0326, Phygene, Fuzhou, China). 20 μg of protein from each sample was separated on 12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk in TBST buffer, membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, respectively. The membranes were incubated with enhanced chemofluorescent reagent (Cat no. 34095, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA) and imaged with a digital imager (iBright CL1500, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA).

Mouse xenograft models

For the xenograft experiments, four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology, Beijing, China) were housed in laminar flow cabinets under specific pathogen-free conditions. SiHa cells (5 × 106 cells) with or without of P. gingivalis infection (MOI 50 for 12 h) were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of each mouse to establish the subcutaneous xenograft model. Subcutaneous tumors volumes were measured every 5 days, and calculated by the formula Volume = (width)2 × length × 1/2 [30, 31]. Mice were euthanized and tumor tissues were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin, and sections were stained with H&E.

For the lung metastases model, a tail vein injection experiment was performed to evaluate the metastatic ability of CSCC cells in vivo. SiHa-luciferase cells (1 × 106 cells) with or without of P. gingivalis infection (MOI 50 for 12 h) were injected into the tail veins of nude mice. After 8 weeks of injection, bioluminescence signals of tumors were measure by in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Mice were sacrificed and lung tissues were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin, and 5 μm sections were stained with H&E.

The popliteal lymphatic metastasis model was established in nude mice by inoculating the footpad with SiHa-luciferase cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse). 8 weeks after implantation, mice were imaged by IVIS system (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III, Waltham, MA, USA) to evaluate lymphatic metastasis and tumor burdens. Mice were sacrificed, the primary tumors in footpads, inguinal lymph nodes and popliteal lymph nodes were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in paraffin, and sections were stained with H&E to analyze for lymph invasion.

Statistical analysis

Image J software was used for quantitative image analysis. The SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) were used for statistical analysis. The Student’s t-test was used for two groups comparison. One-way ANOVA was used for comparison of more than two groups. The contingency tables were analyzed by the chi-square test. Data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The survival analysis was calculated by the Kaplan–Meier method and analyzed by the log-rank test.

Results

Porphyromonas gingivalis preferentially colonizes the tumor tissues of CSCC patients

To investigate the presence of P. gingivalis in CSCC tissues, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) using antibodies specific to P. gingivalis gingipains on tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues. IHC analysis revealed that the staining intensity of P. gingivalis was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. The median IHC score in tumor tissues was 3.0 (IQR: 0.0–4.0), whereas it was 0.0 (IQR: 0.0–1.0) in adjacent tissues (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1A). The positive rate of P. gingivalis was significantly higher in tumor tissues (63.3%, 57/90) than in adjacent non-tumor tissues (23.3%, 21/90) (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1A). P. gingivalis exhibited a distinct and non-uniform spatial distribution within the tumor microenvironment, predominantly localizing to the tumor core and tumor-stroma interface, with sparse presence in stromal and immune cell-enriched regions (Fig. 1A). Both assays demonstrated a significantly higher bacterial load in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues, consistent with the IHC results. These findings suggest that P. gingivalis preferentially colonizes CSCC tumor tissues and may contribute to cervical cancer pathogenesis.

Porphyromonas gingivalis is associated with metastasis and poor survival in CSCC patients

To explore the clinical relevance of P. gingivalis colonization, we analyzed its association with various clinicopathologic parameters in CSCC patients. P. gingivalis colonization was significantly associated with advanced FIGO stage (P = 0.0036), lymph node metastasis (P = 0.0043), and distant metastasis (P = 0.0260; Table 1). In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between P. gingivalis colonization and HPV infection status, tumor differentiation, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), or parametrial invasion (P > 0.05, Table 1). These findings suggest that P. gingivalis colonization is independent of HPV infection in this cohort. Notably, P. gingivalis positivity was markedly increased in patients with lymph node metastasis, with 79.07% of metastatic cases testing positive compared to 20.93% in the non-metastatic group. Similarly, late-stage CSCC patients exhibited a higher frequency of P. gingivalis colonization (81.58%) than those with early-stage disease (18.42%, Table 1).

Porphyromonas gingivalis promotes CSCC cell migration and invasion in vitro

To elucidate the functional role of P. gingivalis in CSCC progression, we performed co-culture experiments using five CSCC cell lines (SiHa, HCC94, CaSki, C33A, and MS751) and one normal cervical epithelial cell line (human cervical epithelial cell, HCEC). Cells were exposed to P. gingivalis at varying multiplicities of infection (MOIs), heat-killed P. gingivalis, or P. gingivalis culture supernatant. Cell proliferation assays revealed that P. gingivalis had no significant effect on cell proliferation at low multiplicities of infection (MOIs of 10, 50, 100; P > 0.05; Fig. 2A), which was further supported by CCK-8 assays (P > 0.05; Fig. 2B). In contrast, Transwell assays demonstrated that P. gingivalis significantly enhanced the migration and invasion of SiHa and HCC94 cells in a dose-dependent manner (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2C and D). At a low multiplicity of infection (MOI of 10), no notable increase in cell motility was observed, whereas MOIs of 50 and 100 markedly promoted both migration and invasion. Specifically, at MOI 100, the migration and invasion capacities of SiHa cells increased by 72% (P < 0.0001) and 88% (P < 0.0001), respectively, and HCC94 cells increased by 54% (P < 0.001) and 63% (P < 0.001). No such effects were observed in normal HCECs (P > 0.05; Fig. 2E).

Porphyromonas gingivalis accelerates CSCC metastatic progression in vivo

To assess the metastatic potential of P. gingivalis in vivo, we established a xenograft model in nude mice. Compared with control treatment, subcutaneous injection of P. gingivalis-treated SiHa cells did not significantly affect the tumor size or growth rate (P > 0.05; Fig. 3A and B), which was consistent with the in vitro cell proliferation results. However, in a lung metastasis model generated by tail vein injection of P. gingivalis-treated SiHa cells, bioluminescence imaging and histological analysis revealed a significantly higher number of metastatic nodules in the lungs of mice injected with P. gingivalis-treated cells than in control mice injected with P. gingivalis-untreated cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 3C–E). On average, the number of lung metastases increased by 11.4 ± 3.5 nodules in the P. gingivalis-treated group. These findings indicate that P. gingivalis enhances the metastatic potential of CSCC cells, promoting colonization of distant organs.

Porphyromonas gingivalis preferentially adheres to CSCC cell lines

Given our previous findings that P. gingivalis preferentially colonizes CSCC tumor tissues and enhances metastatic progression, we next investigated its adhesion properties in CSCC cells. Bacterial adhesion assays were performed using five CSCC cell lines (SiHa, MS751, HCC94, CaSki, and C33A cells) and one normal cervical epithelial cell line (HCEC). The results revealed that P. gingivalis preferentially adhered to SiHa and HCC94 cells, with significantly higher numbers of bacteria bound per cell compared to HCECs and other CSCC cell lines (P < 0.01; Fig. 4A and B). The mean difference in bacterial counts between SiHa and HCEC cells was estimated at–4.3 (95% CI:–4.942 to–3.591), while the difference between HCC94 and HCEC cells was–0.68 (95% CI:–1.359 to–0.0078).This finding aligns with the pro-migratory effects of P. gingivalis in these two cell lines (Fig. 2C and D), suggesting that its ability to enhance cellular migration and invasion may rely on its capacity to invade host cells. In contrast, the non-pathogenic L. crispatus exhibited minimal adhesion to and invasion of SiHa cells (P < 0.0001; Fig. 4C and D). The number of adhered bacteria was significantly lower in the L. crispatus group compared to the P. gingivalis group, with a mean difference of 8.81 ± 0.39 bacteria per cell.

Porphyromonas gingivalis FimA selectively interacts with CD151/ITGB1 in CSCC cells

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which P. gingivalis recognizes and adheres to CSCC cells, we sought to identify host cell receptors that interact with the P. gingivalis fimbrial adhesin FimA, a key virulence factor responsible for initiating bacterial adhesion to host cells [32, 33]. Recombinant His-tagged FimA protein was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) and used for pull-down assays with SiHa cell lysates (Fig. 5A and B). Silver staining of the pull-down complexes revealed several distinct protein bands (Fig. 5C). The corresponding bands were excised and subjected to mass spectrometry analysis to identify potential host membrane proteins that interact with FimA-His. Among the candidate membrane proteins identified, CD151 and integrin β1 (ITGB1) showed the highest enrichment, while integrin α3 (ITGA3) and integrin α2 (ITGA2) were also detected (Fig. 5D, Table S1). Western blot analysis confirmed the interaction between FimA and both CD151 and ITGB1 (Fig. 5D), supporting the hypothesis that P. gingivalis adhesion to CSCC cells is mediated through the FimA–CD151/ITGB1 interaction. Notably, the tetraspanin CD9 showed no detectable binding to FimA, indicating the specificity of the FimA–CD151 interaction (Fig. 5D).

CD151, a member of the tetraspanin family, is known to promote tumor progression and metastasis by modulating the activities of laminin-binding integrins (such as α3β1, α6β1 and α6β4) [34, 35]. To further investigate the specificity of the FimA-CD151/ITGB1interaction, we expanded the pull-down assays to include additional members of the tetraspanin and integrin families. Among the tetraspanins tested, CD81 did not bind to FimA-His, and among the integrins, ITGA3 showed moderate binding, ITGA2 weak binding, while ITGB4, ITGA5, and ITGA6 exhibited no detectable interaction (Fig. 5E). Co-immunoprecipitation using anti-CD151 and anti-ITGB1 antibodies (Fig. 5F), along with biotin-labeled pull-down assay (Fig. 5G), further validated the formation of FimA/CD151/ITGB1 complex. These results confirm that FimA selectively binds the CD151/ITGB1 complex, most likely through the CD151-integrin α3β1 assembly, underscoring the specificity of this microbial–host interaction.

Interestingly, CD151 and ITGB1 were more highly expressed in CSCC cells (SiHa and HCC94) than in normal cervical epithelial cells (Fig. 5H), in line with the observed preferential adhesion of P. gingivalis to these two CSCC cell lines. This finding suggests that elevated expression of CD151/ITGB1 may facilitate P. gingivalis’ attachment to CSCC cells.

To visualize these interactions at the nanoscale level, we employed super-resolution stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) to examine the colocalization of P. gingivalis with CD151 and ITGB1 at adhesion sites on SiHa cells. STORM imaging revealed prominent colocalization between P. gingivalis and CD151/ITGB1 (Fig. 5I and J), and 3D-STORM analysis confirmed direct contact between P. gingivalis and CD151/ITGB1 (Fig. S1, and supplementary Movies 1 and 2).

Porphyromonas gingivalis adheres to CSCC cells through the FimA–CD151/ITGB1 interaction

Given that bacterial adhesion is a pivotal initial step in colonization and pathogenesis, we hypothesized that P. gingivalis adheres to CSCC cells through the interaction between its fimbrial adhesin FimA and the host CD151/ITGB1 complex. To test this hypothesis, bacterial adhesion assays were performed in SiHa cells with CD151 or ITGB1 knockdown or overexpression. Knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1 significantly reduced P. gingivalis adhesion to host cells, with an average decrease of 66% compared to the control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 6A–C). Conversely, the overexpression of CD151 and ITGB1 markedly enhanced bacterial adhesion, resulting in an approximate 96% increase (P < 0.01, Fig. 6D–F). These findings suggest that CD151 and ITGB1 are critical host factors mediating P. gingivalis attachment to cervical cancer cells. Furthermore, Transwell assays demonstrated that knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1 abolished the P. gingivalis-induced enhancement of cell migration and invasion (Fig. 6G and H), suggesting that these host receptors are essential for mediating the downstream pro-metastatic effects of bacterial colonization. To further confirm the role of the FimA-CD151/ITGB1 interaction in mediating bacterial adherence, we performed antibody-based neutralization assays targeting either the bacterial ligand or the host receptors. Pre-incubation of P. gingivalis with a FimA-neutralizing antibody or pretreatment of SiHa cells with anti-CD151 and anti-ITGB1 antibodies, significantly impaired P. gingivalis adhesion to SiHa cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 6I and J), resulting in a 67% and 62% reduction, respectively. Moreover, antibody blockade of FimA or CD151/ITGB1 effectively abolished the P. gingivalis-induced enhancement of cell migration and invasion (Fig. 6K–M). These results indicated that the FimA-CD151/ITGB1 interaction plays an important role in mediating P. gingivalis colonization and its downstream pro-metastatic effects.

CD151 and ITGB1 regulate P. gingivalis adhesion and its pro-migratory effects in normal cervical epithelial cells

To further investigate whether this mechanism is tumor-specific, we performed analogous loss- and gain-of-function experiments in normal cervical epithelial cells (HCECs), and the results are presented in Fig. S2. Unlike CSCC cells, P. gingivalis exhibited minimal adhesion and invasion in HCECs, and had no significant effect on cell migration or invasion (Fig. 2E). Knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1 in HCECs further reduced P. gingivalis adhesion by approximately 38% (P < 0.05, Fig. S2A–C), indicating that these receptors contribute to basal-level bacterial attachment even in non-tumor epithelial cells. Conversely, overexpression of CD151 and ITGB1 in HCECs significantly enhanced bacterial adhesion by approximately 40% (P < 0.05, Fig. S2D–F), and enabled P. gingivalis to enhance HCEC migration and invasion by approximately 30% (P < 0.01, Fig. S2G). These findings suggest that elevated expression of CD151 and ITGB1 is required for P. gingivalis to exert its pro-migratory effects and reveal a tumor-preferential response, wherein the extent of colonization and downstream signaling is determined by the expression level of host adhesion molecules rather than cell types.

Knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1 abolishes P. gingivalis-induced metastasis in vivo

To assess the requirement of CD151 and ITGB1 in mediating P. gingivalis-induced metastasis in vivo, we established a tail vein metastasis model using SiHa cells with stable knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient silencing of the target genes (Fig. 7A). In vivo bioluminescence imaging revealed that P. gingivalis treatment markedly enhanced pulmonary metastatic signals in mice injected with control cells, whereas this pro-metastatic effect was attenuated in mice injected with cells with simultaneous knockdown of CD151 and ITGB1 knockdown (Fig. 7B). Consistently, H&E staining of lung sections demonstrated a significant increase in the number of metastatic nodules in mice injected with P. gingivalis-treated cells, while this effect was abolished when CD151 and ITGB1 were simultaneously silenced (P < 0.05, Fig. 7C). These findings provide compelling in vivo evidence that the FimA-CD151/ITGB1 interaction is essential for P. gingivalis to promote cervical cancer metastasis, underscoring the critical role of this microbial–host interaction in tumor dissemination.

CD151 and ITGB1 are clinically associated with P. gingivalis colonization and poor prognosis in CSCC

To further investigate whether P. gingivalis colonization is clinically associated with host adhesion receptors, we performed IHC analysis of CD151 and ITGB1 expression in CSCC patient specimens. The expression of CD151 and ITGB1 in CSCC tumor tissues was higher than those in adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. S3A). Notably, P. gingivalis was predominantly localized in regions with high CD151 and ITGB1 expression, including high endothelial venules (HEVs) within metastatic lymph nodes (Fig. S3B), suggesting that P. gingivalis-bearing tumor cells may migrate to lymph nodes via HEVs. Correlation analysis confirmed a positive correlation between P. gingivalis colonization and increased CD151/ITGB1 expression levels (Fig. S3C). Moreover, survival analysis using data from the Human Protein Atlas database demonstrated that the high expression of CD151 or ITGB1 was significantly associated with worse overall survival in CSCC patients (CD151: HR 1.862, 95% CI: 1.046–3.313, P = 0.0122; ITGB1: HR 2.346, 95% CI: 1.472–3.738, P = 0.0005; Fig. S3D and E). These findings underscore the pivotal role of CD151/ITGB1 in P. gingivalis colonization and CSCC progression.

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms by which P. gingivalis promotes metastasis, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of SiHa cells cocultured with P. gingivalis for 4 h. Volcano plot analysis identified 204 differentially expressed genes (Fig. 8A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that several oncogenic pathways were upregulated, including the MAPK, Rap1, TGF-β, and actin cytoskeleton regulation pathways. Notably, the HPV infection pathway was also activated by P. gingivalis treatment (Fig. 8B).

Given the critical role of actin cytoskeleton remodeling in cell motility and metastasis, we further investigated the MAPK signaling cascade. Western blot analysis showed that P. gingivalis treatment led to increased expression of phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase (p-JNK) and phosphorylated-paxillin (p-paxillin), along with upregulation of CD151 (Fig. 8C), indicating the activation of the JNK/paxillin pathway. As JNK, a key component of the MAPK family, is essential for integrin-mediated cell migration and cytoskeletal regulation, these findings suggest that P. gingivalis promotes metastatic signaling via activation of the JNK/paxillin pathway.

F-actin reorganization facilitates P. gingivalis colonization

The F-actin cytoskeleton, a downstream target of integrin signaling, is a key regulator of cell migration and bacterial invasion. To assess whether P. gingivalis influences F-actin organization, we examined cytoskeletal changes and cell morphology following bacterial infection. Bacterial adhesion and invasion assays demonstrated a time-dependent increase in P. gingivalis colonization, accompanied by prominent cytoskeletal remodeling in CSCC cells (Fig. S4). Specifically, F-actin structures shifted from organized, parallel stress fibers to short, disorganized bundles (Fig. 8D). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a 14–19% reduction in maximum cell diameter (P < 0.01, Fig. 8E) and a 19–33% decrease in F-actin fluorescence intensity following P. gingivalis infection (P < 0.05, Fig. 8F), indicating substantial cytoskeletal disruption. Notably, P. gingivalis remained viable within host cells, and disruption of F-actin organization persisted even after multiple cell passages (Fig. S5). Despite the reduction in maximum diameter, the overall cell size remained unchanged (data not shown), suggesting a change in cell shape rather than volume.

To determine whether F-actin remodeling is essential for P. gingivalis colonization and metastasis, we treated cells with the F-actin polymerization inhibitor latrunculin A (LatA). P. gingivalis adhesion was significantly reduced upon LatA treatment (P < 0.01, Fig. 8G and H), and the P. gingivalis-induced enhancement of CSCC cell migration and invasion was abolished (Fig. 8I–K). These results indicate that P. gingivalis exploits F-actin reorganization to facilitate its colonization and promote migration and invasion of CSCC cells.

Discussion

While cervicovaginal microbiota dysbiosis and persistent HPV infection are well-established risk factors for cervical carcinogenesis, previous research has largely focused on microbial diversity and compositional changes, with less attention paid to specific pathogens and their functional roles [6, 36]. Our study addresses this gap by providing the first direct evidence that P. gingivalis colonizes CSCC tissues and promotes metastasis, thereby identifying it as a novel microbial driver in cervical cancer progression. We demonstrate that P. gingivalis colonization is associated with advanced FIGO stage, metastasis, and reduced survival in CSCC patients. Mechanistically, P. gingivalis adheres to and colonizes CSCC cells through binding of its fimbrial adhesin FimA to the CD151/ITGB1 receptor complex. This interaction activates the JNK/paxillin signaling pathway, triggering F-actin cytoskeleton remodeling and promoting cell migration and invasion. Importantly, blocking the FimA–CD151/ITGB1 interaction significantly reduced P. gingivalis colonization and suppressed lung metastases in vivo, highlighting this microbial–host interaction as a promising therapeutic target for preventing microbial-driven metastasis (Fig. 9).

Schematic diagram showing the mechanism of P. gingivalis colonizes CSCC cells and promotes metastasis. Porphyromonas gingivalis adheres to CSCC cells via its fimbrial adhesin FimA, which specifically interacts with the host membrane receptors CD151 and ITGB1. This binding facilitates bacterial colonization within tumor cells. Upon intracellular colonization, P. gingivalis activates the JNK/Paxillin signaling pathway, leading to F-actin cytoskeletal remodeling. These changes enhance the migratory and invasive properties of CSCC cells, ultimately promoting both lymphatic and distant metastases in vivo

P. gingivalis, a well-characterized oral pathogen, has previously been detected in the amniotic fluid [21], placenta [22], and umbilical cord [37] of women with adverse pregnancy outcomes, indicating its pathogenic role in reproductive health. Although increased abundance of Porphyromonas has been reported in the vaginal microbiota of women with bacterial vaginosis [38, 39], endometrial cancer [13, 15] or cervical cancer[14], these studies lacked species-level resolution. Thus, whether P. gingivalis is present in cervical tumors has not been previously identified. In this study, we confirmed the presence of P. gingivalis in CSCC tissues using IHC, immunofluorescence, and FISH, achieving spatially resolved and species-specific detection. Notably, P. gingivalis was not diffusely distributed but exhibited a distinct and selective localization pattern—enriched in the tumor core and tumor–stroma interface, with minimal colonization in adjacent non-tumor tissues or immune cell-rich regions. This tumor-specific colonization pattern suggests that P. gingivalis exhibits a particular affinity for malignant cells, which we have demonstrated to be mediated by the overexpression of CD151 and ITGB1 on CSCC cells. More importantly, the presence of P. gingivalis within CSCC tumors is significantly associated with advanced tumor stage, metastasis, and reduced survival, suggesting that intratumoral P. gingivalis may serve as a novel biomarker for CSCC diagnosis and prognosis. These findings also underscore the potential role of CD151/ITGB1 in microbial-driven tumor progression and highlight this receptor complex as a possible therapeutic target.

The presence of P. gingivalis in the reproductive tract system underscores the importance of the oral–reproductive tract microbial axis in disease development, For example, epidemiological data suggest an association between P. gingivalis and benign prostatic hyperplasia [40, 41], and a recent study demonstrated that P. gingivalis promotes benign prostatic hyperplasia via IL-6/IL-6R pathway [42]. In addition, Fusobacterium nucleatum, another well-known periodontal pathogen, has been epidemiologically linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes and a variety of gynecological disorders [43]. Its enrichment in patients with bacterial vaginosis suggests a potential role in vaginal microbiota dysbiosis [44], and emerging evidence indicates that F. nucleatum infection contributes to the development of endometritis [45]. These observations underscore the broader involvement of oral-derived bacteria in reproductive tract pathologies. Moreover, cooperative interactions between F. nucleatum and P. gingivalis have been well documented in the oral cavity, raising the possibility of a similar synergistic colonization mechanism in the cervicovaginal niche. Nonetheless, studies investigating the mechanistic roles of oral bacteria, particularly in the context of reproductive system malignancies, remain scarce. To our knowledge, this study provides the first mechanistic evidence linking an oral-derived pathogen to metastatic progression in cervical cancer, thereby expanding the pathological scope of the oral–reproductive tract microbial axis into gynecologic oncology [12].

P. gingivalis has been implicated in the progression of several gastrointestinal cancers, including oral [46], esophageal [47], colorectal [48], and pancreatic [49] cancers, through inflammation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune evasion [50, 51]. Specifically, in oral cancer, P. gingivalis virulence factors (gingipains and LPS) activate oncogenic NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways [52–54]. In colorectal and pancreatic cancers, P. gingivalis disrupts intestinal barrier integrity [50], activates NLRP3 inflammasome [55], and facilitates immune evasion [9, 56] and KRAS-driven oncogenesis [57]. However, these studies have primarily focused on downstream oncogenic signaling, with limited exploration of how bacterial colonization is initiated—a critical prerequisite for intratumoral microbial activity. In contrast, our study uncovers a colonization-dependent mechanism by which P. gingivalis promotes CSCC metastasis. We demonstrate that P. gingivalis adheres to tumor cells via FimA/CD151/ITGB1 binding, enabling colonization that specifically drives metastasis through F-actin cytoskeleton remodeling. Notably, unlike its role of promoting tumor growth in gastrointestinal cancers, P. gingivalis in CSCC does not accelerate primary tumor growth but specifically enhances metastatic dissemination, underscoring tumor-type–specific pathogenic mechanisms.

Our findings parallel recent studies showing that intratumoral bacteria drive metastasis. For instance, Fusobacterium nucleatum promoted colorectal cancer (CRC) metastasis by upregulating endothelial ICAM-1 (intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1) to facilitate cancer cell adhesion to the endothelium [58]. Bacteria residing in breast cancer tissues enhanced metastatic colonization through remodeling the actin cytoskeleton and conferring mechanical stress resistance in circulation [59]. Similarly, in our study, P. gingivalis specifically recognized and activated CD151/ITGB1 signaling in CSCC cells, which in turn induced F-actin cytoskeleton remodeling and promoted cell migration and invasion. These findings highlight a novel paradigm in which P. gingivalis exploits integrin-tetraspanin signaling to drive cytoskeletal remodeling and metastatic dissemination. Elucidating this microbial-host signaling axis may offer new avenues for therapeutic intervention in microbe-associated cancers.

As a downstream consequence of CD151/ITGB1 engagement, P. gingivalis activated the JNK/paxillin signaling pathway in CSCC cells. The JNK pathway, a critical branch of the MAPK signaling network, is a downstream effector of integrins and plays a pivotal role in tumor invasion and metastasis [60]. Paxillin, a scaffold protein within focal adhesions, interacts with integrin-associated proteins and F-actin microfilaments [61]. JNK-mediated phosphorylation of paxillin amplifies pro-migratory signaling and regulates cell adhesion and migration [62, 63]. The increased phosphorylation of JNK and paxillin in P. gingivalis-treated cells suggests that bacterial colonization may trigger intracellular signaling cascades that enhance metastatic potential. The observed F-actin reorganization following P. gingivalis infection further supports the role of cytoskeletal dynamics in bacterial-induced tumor cell migration. Future studies should investigate how P. gingivalis modulates intracellular signaling pathways involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell motility, and metastatic dissemination in CSCC, in order to fully clarify its pro-metastatic role.

Cervicovaginal microbiota dysbiosis and persistent HPV infection are key drivers of cervical carcinogenesis, and their complex interplay remains elusive [64]. Dysbiosis may compromise the epithelial barrier and increase host susceptibility to HPV, whereas HPV-induced alterations in the local microenvironment—such as inflammation and changes in cervical secretions—may, in turn, alter microbial composition [65]. Consistent with this concept, HPV infection has been associated with increased abundance of Porphyromonas species in the vaginal microbiota [14, 67, 68]. Although the causal mechanisms underlying these associations remain unclear, studies of other co-infection models may offer mechanistic insights. For example, influenza A virus (IAV) infection has been shown to upregulate the expression of integrin α5 and remodels the actin cytoskeleton via FAK/Akt signaling, thereby enhancing bacterial colonization [66]. Conversely, bacterial pathogens may also influence host susceptibility to viral infections. Studies have shown that P. gingivalis enhanced viral susceptibility by downregulating host interferon (IFN) signaling in the oral mucosa [69], and facilitated HIV entry into epithelial cells [70, 71]. These findings raise the possibility that P. gingivalis may similarly influence HPV persistence and pathogenesis in the cervicovaginal tract. In our study, although the association between HPV status and the colonization of P. gingivalis in clinical specimens was not significant, the KEGG pathway analysis revealed activation of HPV-related pathways after P. gingivalis infection in CSCC cells, suggesting potential synergistic interplay between bacterial infection and viral oncogenic signaling in promoting tumor progression. Nevertheless, the interaction between P. gingivalis and HPV infection remains poorly understood. Integrated mechanistic and epidemiological studies are warranted to clarify its clinical significance and potential role in cervical cancer development and progression.

CD151, a tetraspanin frequently overexpressed in tumors, acts as a critical regulator of integrin-mediated functions [72]. CD151 forms stable complexes with integrins (e.g., α3β1, α6β1 and α6β4) to enhance cell–matrix adhesion, activate pro-migratory signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K-Akt and Rac1), remodel F-actin cytoskeleton, and potently drive tumor cell migration and metastasis [34, 73–76]. Beyond its role in cancer, CD151 serves as a gateway for microbial colonization by forming tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (TEMs) exploited by pathogens for cellular endocytosis [77, 78]. It has been identified as a co-receptor for multiple viruses, including HPV [79, 80], HCMV [81], and IAV [82], suggesting a potential role of CD151 in HPV-driven cervical carcinogenesis. However, its involvement in bacterial colonization, and its functional significance in CSCC progression, remains poorly defined.

In this context, our findings provide novel mechanistic insight into CD151 function in cervical cancer. We showed that blocking CD151/ITGB1 significantly reduced bacterial colonization and lung metastasis, linking this receptor complex to a critical signaling axis that connects bacterial infection with tumor progression. Interestingly, we also found that P. gingivalis infection upregulated CD151 expression, suggesting the existence of a positive feedback loop that reinforces bacterial adhesion and pro-metastatic signaling. In this model, CD151 functions as a constitutive co-receptor (with ITGB1) essential for initial bacterial adhesion, while subsequent infection actively enhances CD151 expression. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle, wherein the elevated CD151 levels promote further bacterial attachment and activates pro-metastatic signaling pathways in tumor cells, while sustaining persistent bacterial infection. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify CD151 as a co-receptor mediating P. gingivalis adhesion and invasion in tumor cells, thus expanding the functional repertoire of CD151 in microbial-driven tumor progression. Blocking CD151-ITGB1 disrupts this loop at its origin, underscoring the critical role of this receptor complex in mediating P. gingivalis-induced tumor progression and highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target for preventing microbial-driven metastasis in cervical cancer.

These findings not only highlight the role of CD151 in mediating P. gingivalis-induced metastasis, but also raise the possibility that CD151 may serve as a common entry platform for both bacterial and viral pathogens in the cervical microenvironment. In particular, P. gingivalis-mediated CD151 upregulation may enhance HPV infection, potentially amplifying viral oncogenic signaling. Inhibition of CD151 function impairs both bacterial and viral infections, indicating that targeting CD151 may offer a therapeutic strategy for infectious diseases while reducing antibiotic use [78, 81, 83]. Collectively, these findings position CD151 as a shared receptor exploited by microbial pathogens—both viral and bacterial—making it a promising dual target for anti-microbial and anti-metastatic strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, the origin and transmission route of P. gingivalis in CSCC remain undefined. Although its presence in high endothelial venules suggests a potential for hematogenous dissemination, alternative pathways such as gastrointestinal translocation cannot be excluded and warrant further investigation. Second, while CD151 and ITGB1 have been implicated in FimA-mediated adhesion, the specific integrin α subunit partnering with ITGB1—most likely α3 based on pull-down results—has not been functionally validated, nor were the interaction mechanisms and binding motifs within the FimA/ITGB1/CD151 complex elucidated. A deeper understanding of these molecular interactions is critical for unraveling the host–microbe interface and advancing the development of precision therapies targeting microbial-driven metastasis. Finally, this study did not examine the interactions between P. gingivalis and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment. Elucidating the host-microbe immune crosstalk and evaluating the therapeutic potential of targeting the FimA-CD151/ITGB1 interaction should be a focus of future research.

Conclusion

Our study provides novel insights into the pathogenic role of P. gingivalis in CSCC. We demonstrate that P. gingivalis selectively colonizes CSCC tissues, activates JNK/Paxillin signaling pathways and enhances tumor metastasis via the FimA/CD151/ITGB1 interaction (Fig. 9). Given its strong association with metastasis and poor prognosis, P. gingivalis may serve as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in CSCC management. Further investigations are warranted to explore the translational potential of these findings and develop targeted interventions aimed at mitigating P. gingivalis-driven tumor progression.
