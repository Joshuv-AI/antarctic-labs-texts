BFLF1 gene polymorphisms are associated with increased mortality in patients with EBV-associated diseases
Authors: Xurong Li, Jing Wu, Shenglei Yu, Zhe Zhou, Wenhong Zhang, Lingyun Shao, Ning Jiang, Qingluan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpstein-Barr virus (EBV) -associated diseases comprise a spectrum of conditions, including infectious mononucleosis (IM) and chronic active EBV infections (CAEBV), as well as malignant lymphomas. The study aimed to compare genomic variations of Type 1 EBV across different subtypes of EBV-associated diseases to investigate the impact of EBV variants on disease progression and clinical outcomes.MethodsA total of 36 patients with EBV-associated diseases were enrolled. Targeted EBV genome sequencing on EBV genomic was performed with peripheral blood DNA samples (400µL) using Illumina sequencing technology. Following quality control with FastQC, Fastp, and TriTrimmomatic, 27 samples comprising 2 IM, 9 CAEBV and 16 NK/T-cell lymphoma cases meeting the critera of minimum effective coverage > 10 and coverage > 80% were retained. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) annotations were generated into VCF files, and variant profiles were visualized using heatmaps. Variant rates were compared using Fisher’s exact test, based on disease subtype, presence of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocystosis (HLH), and clinical outcomes. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using the Haldane-Anscombe correction.ResultsCompared with the reference stain B95.8/Raji, a total of 2182 variants were identified in 27 samples. The variants located in coding region included 523 missense variants, 1 insertion, and 2 deletion, all of which only involved single amino acid changes in the encoded proteins. Although a large number of variants were detected, no statistically significant differences were observed among the IM, CAEBV and lymphoma groups, or between patients with and without HLH. However, three variants in BFLF1 were significantly more frequent in deceased patients compared to survivors, which detailedly refers to Ile35Thr, Asp44Glu and Val246Ile [P = 0.0267, OR = 15.00, 95%CI= (0.7436,301.5)].ConclusionSingle amino acid changes in EBV proteins had no significant effect on the progression status of EBV-associated diseases, but the variants in BFLF1 were associated with an increased risk of mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41065-026-00708-4.Keywords: Epstein-Barr virus, Infectious mononucleosis, Chronic active EB virus infection, NK/T lymphoma, Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocystosis, Genomic variations

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), first identified by Epstein and Barr in 1964, is a widespread human herpesvirus. Its genome is a linear double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) approximately 172 kb in length, encoding 85 genes and separated by four internal repeat sequences (IRs) and terminal repeat sequences (TRs) at both ends [1]. Upon binding to complement receptors (CD21) through its membrane glycoproteins and entering target cells, EBV DNA remains in the cytoplasm, enabling long-term latency. The life cycle of EBV consists of two phases: latent infection and lytic replication. During latency, it mainly expresses EBV nuclear antigen (EBNA) and membrane proteins (LMP) while in the lytic phase, early antigen (EA) and capsid antigen (CA) are produced to utilize in viral assembly and cell lysis [2].

EBV primarily infects epithelial cells and B cells. Latent EBV infection may induce immortalization of target lymphocytes, which is associated with lymphoproliferative disorders. The main types of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated diseases include: infectious mononucleosis (IM), chronic active EBV infections (CAEBV), and carcinoma (lymphoma such as B-cell Burkitt lymphoma, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, extranasal NK/T-type lymphoma, aggressive NK cell leukemia, and non-specific peripheral T lymphoma and epithelial malignancies such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma and EBV-associated gastric adenocarcinoma) [3]. In some cases, acute primary infection triggers IM, characterized as benign lymphoid hyperplasia in B cells. The course usually lasts about one month, with most patients achieving complete remission. Only a small number of patients experience persistent EBV infection in hematopoietic stem or lymphoid progenitor cells in the bone marrow that later differentiate into T/NK cells. The infected lymphocytes subsequently undergo clonal expansion, immune evasion, and progressive accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations, giving rise to systemic CAEBV disease, with a poor prognosis of lymphoma progression or other severe complications [4]. Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocystosis (HLH) is one of the common complications characterized by excessive inflammatory responses. the feature of which involves abnormal proliferation of activated lymphocytes and histiocytes, triggering cytokine storm that induces systemic inflammation and often leading to life-threatening conditions [5].

Accumulating evidence has established that the genetic diversity and specific polymorphic lineaging of the EBV genome are heavily coupled with geographic distributions and tumor susceptibilities. However, while previous genomic sequencing efforts have primarily focused on epithelial malignancies, gene polymorphism governing the clinical transition across different lymphocyte-infiltrating diseases especially CAEBV, this rare pre-malignant lymphoproliferative disease, remains poorly characterized. Currently, robust viral genetic biomarkers capable of predicting this severe phenotypic evolution and fatal prognosis are critically lacking.

To investigate whether the EBV strain itself contributes to different disease manifestations and clinical outcomes, we developed an EBV-specific probe library and utilized Illumina technology to generate corresponding EBV DNA sequences and then compared them with the reference sequence B95.8/Raji (NC007605). This comparative analysis aimed to investigate how nucleotide polymorphisms in EBV DNA sequences influence disease progression and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patients

Our study enrolled 36 patients diagnosed with EBV-associated disease at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. The cohort comprised 5 cases of IM, 10 cases of CAEBV, and 21 cases of NK/T lymphoma. All participants were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) and excluded individuals with diabetes, autoimmune diseases, or other chronic infections (including HBV/HCV, CMV, HIV, and tuberculosis). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University.

Diagnostic Criteria for IM [6]: The patient must meet any three of the following clinical manifestations and one of laboratory evidences of primary EBV infection: (1) Clinical manifestations: ① fever; ② pharyngitis; ③ enlarged cervical lymph nodes; ④ hepatosplenomegaly; ⑤ rash; ⑥ eyelid edema; (2) Laboratory findings: ① positive anti-EBV-capsid antigen (CA)-IgM and anti-EBV-CA-IgG with negative anti-EBV-nuclear antigen (NA)-IgG; ② positive single anti-EBV-CA-IgG antibody with low-affinity binding to EBV-CA-IgG; (3) Exclusion of acute infection caused by other pathogens.

Diagnostic criteria for CAEBV [7]: (1) Persistent or recurrent infectious mononucleosis-like symptoms lasting over three months; (2) Increased EBV genomic load in peripheral blood and/or affected tissues (elevated EBV DNA or positive EBV EER); (3) EBV infection affecting T cells, NK cells, or multiple cell lineages (two or more types including T, B, or NK); (4) Exclusion of other chronic diseases that may cause similar symptoms. EBV-associated NK/T lymphoma can be diagnosed according to WHO-HAEM5 and increased EBV genome in peripheral blood and/or affected tissues (elevated EBV DNA or EBER positive) [8]. EBV-associated HLH can be diagnosed according HLH-2024 [9]: Increased EBV genomic load in peripheral blood and/or affected tissues (elevated EBV DNA or positive EBV DNA test).

EBV genomic sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from 400 µL of peripheral blood. DNA mass was quantified fluorometrically using Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The EBV DNA load was determined by absolute quantification qPCR targeting the BKRF1 gene (encoding EBNA1) using primers GCCGGTGTGTTCGTATATGG/CAAAACCTCAGCAAATATATGAG. To ensure the ability to capture diverse EBV strains, the capture probes were designed with 4x coverage tiling approach. Typically, 120-bp probes were arranged with a step size of approximately 30 bp, ensuring high coverage depth for uniform hybridization and comprehensive variant capture. The probe design was based on a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of isolates from different sub-strains of type I EBV genomic sequences. EBV reference genome (GenBank NC_007605) was used as the backbone for target sequence. Then, degenerate or parallel probes were synthesized to cover known single nucleotide polymorphisms and insertion/deletion sites. Probes are aligned against the host (human) reference genome using BLASTN, and sequences with high homology to the human genome are removed to reduce off-target capture. Threshold cycle (CT) values were compared to a standard curve generated from a linearized plasmid containing a 598-bp target sequence amplified from the EBV genome (primers: AGGCCATTTTTCCACCCTGT/CGTCAAAGCTGCACACAGTC) to calculate EBV genome copies per microliter. Samples exceeding 5.0 × 10³ copies/µg DNA were selected for sequencing.

Genomic DNA (1 µg) was fragmented to an average size of ~ 250 bp using the Covaris ME220 System (Covaris, Woburn, MA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Subsequent library preparation steps—including end repair, 3′-dA tailing, Illumina-compatible adapter ligation, and post-ligation cleanup—were performed according to the SureSelectXT Illumina Paired-End Sequencing Library protocol (Agilent Technologies). Target enrichment was performed using the SureSelectXT Target Enrichment Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) with hybridization at 65 °C, strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. Post-hybridization capture cleanup and enrichment PCR were conducted following the kit instructions. Final enriched libraries were quantified using the Kapa Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA) on an ABI 9700HT Fast Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (2 × 150 bp paired-end reads).

Quality control of raw data

The 36 FastQC files generated by the platform were processed through FastP and Trimmomatic in Java programs, with results shown in supplementary Fig. 1. We removed the adapter sequences and low-quality sequences with average Phred score less than 20 at read ends, and also checked the allele frequency of each polymorphic site, which was higher than 95%. It means only one dominant EBV strain existing in a patient. We observed that Per Base Sequence Content remained abnormal after processing by both FastP and Trimmomatic, indicating that the issue might not stem from low-quality sequences or adapter contamination, but rather from library construction or probe bias. Given the similar results from FastP and Trimmomatic, we selected FastP as the final fliter. We defined effective coverage as DP > 10 and valid samples as coverage > 80%, ultimately eliminating 9 invalid samples, with results shown in supplementary Table 1. We generated BAM files by alignment strategy to map the clean NGS reads to EBV reference genomic sequencing with Bowtie2 algorithm. The remaining 27 valid samples were further analyzed, including 2 cases of IM, 9 cases of CAEBV, and 16 cases of NK/T lymphoma.

Variant annotation

Using B95.8/Raji (NC007605) as the reference sequence, we performed variant detection on 27 valid samples through VCF analysis, covering single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions, and deletions. As EBV is haploid with only one allele per locus, the VCF annotation directly uses 0 (reference allele) and 1 (variant allele) markers. To minimize false-positive variant calls resulting from alignment ambiguities in highly repetitive sequences, variants located within the major repeat regions of the EBV reference genome (B95-8, NC_007605.1) were strictly excluded from downstream analyses. These excluded regions comprised the Terminal Repeats (TR; coords 170,000–171,823 bp), Internal Repeats 1 (IR1; coords 12,000–35,000 bp) and Internal Repeats 2 (IR2; coords 39,800–41,200 bp). We also utilized bioinformatics tools to generate phylogenetic trees for visualizing genetic variant patterns.

Structural prediction of mutant protein

We obtained relevant information of BFLF1 protein of B95.8/Raji from the UniProt database, including its amino acid sequence and spatial structure. Based on the amino acid changes caused by DNA variant, we generated the amino acid sequence of the mutant protein. Subsequently, both the wild-type and mutant protein sequences were respectively imported into AlphaFold3 to predict their quaternary structures. Finally, ChimeraX was used to visualize the structures.

Statistical analysis

The presence of variants at a certain site was expressed by 0/1 and displayed in the form of heat map. The comparison of variant rates between groups was expressed in the form of contingency table, and the statistical comparison was made by Fisher exact probability method. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the odds ratio (OR) was calculated using the Haldane-Anscombe correction. P < 0.05 was considered as statistical difference. GraphPad Prism9.5 was used to complete the analysis.

Results

Characteristic of enrolled patients

27 patients whose sequencing data meet the criteria of quality control showed no significant difference in any baseline characteristics except hemoglobin (P = 0.037) among IM, CAEBV and NK/T lymphoma. NK/T lymphoma patients had a lower number of hemoglobin than other 2 groups indicating that proliferating lymphoma cells would lead to hematopoietic disorder. Notably, EBV-DNA in whole blood from CAEBV patients was higher than that in patients with IM and lymphoma. This discrepancy may reflect the systemic massive expansion of EBV in infected cells in CAEBV. More detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Phylogenetic map

The 27 patients in the final cohort were from six provinces: Anhui, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Shanghai, Xinjiang, and Zhejiang, indicating considerable regional diversity. To assess whether disease type and geographic distribution were associated with viral genetic variation, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis to identify the EBV strain sources and their relationship. The results revealed that the submitted samples clustered across multiple evolutionary branches, with no significant pathological and geographical pattern observed among them (Fig. 6).

Variant between different groups

Landscape of variant sites in analyzed samples

The sequences of the 27 analyzed samples were compared with the B95.8/Raji sequence (GenBank accession: NC 007605) from the NCBI database and performed SNP annotation. 2182 single nucleotide variants (SNVs), 12 insertion, and 11 deletion were identified relative to the reference. Among these, only 523 were missense variants, and all identified insertions and deletions within the coding regions were strictly in-frame variations. Specifically, one insertion and two deletions resulted in single-amino-acid length changes (Serine duplication on 4 in BRRF1, Glutamine deletion on 61 in BKRF4, and Threoine deletion on 407 in BGLF1; seen in Table 2), while the remaining indels were located within non-coding regions. Because these indels maintained the triplet reading frames, they modified only individual codons rather than causing broader structural disruptions such as reading frameshifts or premature termination codons.

Variant site of EBV encoding proteins

Comparison of variant sites across EBV-associated disease subtypes

Patients were categorized into three groups based on their EBV-associated disease subtype: IM, CAEBV or EBV+NK/T lymphoma groups. Heatmaps were generated to visualize variant profiles across groups (Fig. 2), and comparative analysis of individual variant sites revealed no statistically significant differences. These results indicate that single-amino-acid changes in EBV proteins are not a major determinant of disease subtype classification.

The incidence of concurrent HLH varied among the disease subtypes. No HLH cases were observed in the IM group, while the incidence rate was 2/7 in the CAEBV group. Notably, lymphoma patients exhibited a significantly higher rate of 7/16 (Table 1). To examine whether EBV sequence variation is associated with HLH development, variant profiles were compared between patients with and without HLH (Fig. 3). No statistically significant variant sites were identified, indicating that single-amino-acid substitutions in EBV proteins are not linked to HLH occurrence in this cohort.

Comparison of variant sites with clinical outcomes

Disease severity and clinical outcomes vary considerably among the three EBV-associated diseases included in this study: IM, CAEBV and EBV-positive NK/T-cell lymphoma. While IM typically follows a mild course with most patients recovering spontaneously, CAEBV and EBV-positive NK/T-cell lymphoma frequently involve severe disease progression. These conditions are often characterized by widespread infiltration of proliferating lymphocytes or tumor cells into multiple organs, leading to life-threatening organ dysfunction. Based on hospital records and follow-up data, all IM patients in our cohort achieved remission or were completely cured. In contrast, the remission rate was 0% in the CAEBV group and 18.8% in the lymphoma group, respectively. Mortality was notably high, reaching 22.2% in CAEBV patients and 43.8% in lymphoma patients.

To evaluate whether EBV sequence variation influences clinical outcomes, we compared genomic polymorphisms across the study population (Fig. 4). After excluding two patients lost to follow-up, three specific variants in the BFLF1 gene were found to be significantly associated with mortality: nucleotide changes at positions 104, 132, and 736, resulting in amino acid substitutions at protein positions 35 (from Ile to Thr), 44 (from Asp to Glu), and 246 (from Val to Ile), respectively. These variants were significantly more frequent in deceased patients than in survivors (P = 0.0267) (Fig. 5). The corresponding odds ratio (OR) was as high as 15.00 [95% CI = 0.7436, 301.5].

The frequency of mutant amino acid in BFLF1 between dead and live patients

To exclude statistical bias caused by disease types, we tabulated the BFLF1 mutation frequencies across the distinct disease tracks (excluding 2 patients lost to follow-up). In the IM and CAEBV group, the BFLF1 variant rate was 66.7% (6 out of 9 patients) and in the Extranodal NK/T-cell Lymphoma group 75.0% (12 out of 16 patients). Statistically, there is no significant difference in the prevalence of these BFLF1 variants between the benign and malignant groups.

Structural anylysis of mutant BFLF1

Given that these three missense variant sites within the BFLF1 gene exhibited complete linkage disequilibrium—either co-occurring concurrently as a single haplotypic block or remaining entirely absent across all analyzed genomes—we hypothesized that they represent a highly conserved, distinct synchronized variant pattern associated with clinical outcomes. To evaluate the potential structural impact, we introduced all three single amino acid substitutions simultaneously into a mutant protein model. According to Uniprot, the native BFLF1 protein consists of two subunits, each assembled from five identical polypeptide chains. Due to the current limitation of AlphaFold3, we ultimately generated one of these two subunits. The structure was visualized using ChimeraX (Fig. 6), with the three substituted amino acid positions marked in red. Comparison between the wild type and mutant models revealed no apparent structural alterations, suggesting that these amino acid changes have a negligible effect on the overall conformation of the BFLF1 protein. At the sub-residue level, the three linked substitutions introduce distinct localized physicochemical shifts. The 44 (from Asp to Glu) and 246 (from Val to Ile) substitutions represent highly conservative changes, strictly maintaining the hydrophobic aliphatic and negatively charged properties of their respective wild-type residues. In contrast, the 35 (from Ile to Thr) substitution introduces a localized chemical transition, substituting a highly hydrophobic amino acid (Isoleucine) with a hydrophilic residue (Threonine) at position 35.

Discussion

EBV-associated diseases encompass a broad spectrum ranging from benign proliferative disorders to highly malignant tumors. Their heterogeneity poses significant challenges for clinical diagnosis, treatment selection, and prognosis assessment. Our research focuses on analyzing how genetic polymorphisms in EBV sequence influence the complex pathogenesis and clinical outcomes of EBV-associated diseases. Tiny disparity was observed among patients with different types of EBV-associated diseases. Only 3 sites on BFLF1 encoding gene were found to be more frequent in EBV from patients with poor outcomes.

The development of EBV-associated diseases involves the transformation and proliferation of EBV-infected lymphocytes, coupled with an imbalanced host immune surveillance against viral-infected cells. Various proteins encoded by EBV exhibit immunosuppressive effects. For instance, upon the establishment of long-term latency, the persistent EBNA1 functions to inhibit Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathways; conversely during late lytic replication, the virion-associated tegument protein BPLF1 acts swiftly to suppress retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) and interferon (IFN) signaling pathways. These mechanisms enable EBV to evade immune defense and surveillance, achieving prolonged latency and short-term lytic replication in infected cells [10]. Additionally, acquired immune deficiencies caused by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or immunosuppressive agents, along with inborn errors of immunity (IEIs), all increase susceptibility to EBV-associated diseases [11].

EBV can exist in host cells through multiple latent modes, expressing distinct latent genes such as EBNA1 (new BKRF1), EBNA2 (new BYRF1), LMP1 (new BNLF1), and LMP2A/B. These genes play crucial roles in maintaining viral latency, promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis and evading immune clearance. During the lytic activation and replication phase, EBV undergoes a highly regulated cascade of gene expression dividing into immediate-early (IE), early (E), and late (L) phases. This replication cascade initiates with the expression of IE transcription factors (such as BZLF1), which act as master molecular switches to transactivate E genes involved in the viral replication machinery, including the DNA polymerase (BALF5) and the viral protein kinase (BGLF4). This is followed chronologically by the expression of L genes encoding viral structural proteins required for virion assembly and maturation (such as BFLF1). This stringently coordinated kinetic cascade ensures successful lytic replication and propagation within host cells. The differences in EBV gene expression patterns, variations in host immune status, and diversity in affected lymphocyte types collectively shape the remarkable heterogeneity of EBV-associated diseases. By comparing EBV genomes derived from 27 patients with the reference sequence B95.8/Raji, we identified 2159 single nucleotide variants (SNVs), 12 insertion, and 11 deletion. Most (1636 SNVs, 11 insertions, and 9 deletions) were located in regulatory regions (e.g. upstream/downstream of coding frames or intergenic regions), with no direct amino acid alteration in protein. These findings indicate that EBV sequences in EBV-associated diseases patients remain relatively conserved, and genomic polymorphisms do not lead to protein structural diversity.

Despite the limited number of variants detected in the reading frame, the 523 missense variants, 1 insertion, and 2 deletion involve single amino acid alterations in 74 EBV structural proteins with multiple functions. For example, BNRF1, BPLF1 and BKRF4 are tegument proteins located between the capsid and envelope, playing crucial roles in nucleic acid transportation, virus assembly and immune evasion [12]. Notably, the rest 73 proteins except BARF0 with detected amino acid changes were expressed during the lytic phase, suggesting a close relationship between genomic changes in lytic phase proteins and EBV-associated diseases pathogenesis. This striking enrichment suggests a compelling coordination between genomic variations in lytic-phase proteins and the pathogenesis of specific EBV-associated diseases. Historically, researchers have frequently focused on classical latency genes, such as BNLF1 and BKRF1, as primary mutational hotspots in EBV-related malignancies. For instance, Borozan et al. [13], on the other hand, detected 13 novel sequences in the EBV genomes of patients with EBV-associated gastric adenocarcinoma. Their comparison with B95.8/Raji revealed that variant sites were predominantly concentrated in latency genes, highlighting the role of these genes in carcinogenesis. Khine et al. [14] also identified recurrent EBNA3B deletions as a tumor suppressor based on their global population cohort. However, in our cohort focusing on hematopoietic malignancies and lymphoproliferative disorders, variants within these classical latency loci were remarkably absent. This pathogenic profile highlights a potential “lytic-to-latency” driving mechanism unique to lymphocyte-infiltrating diseases. Muruta et al. [15] noted that genome deletion causes overexpression of lytic phase genes, while EBV’s failure to complete its lysis due to missing key genes leads to persistent infection in host cells, ultimately promoting the development of EBV-associated diseases such as CAEBV. Polymorphisms in lytic replication proteins may alter viral replication kinetics or induce low-level spontaneous reactivation, which continuously expands the reservoir of latently infected NK/T or B cells and sustains host microenvironmental inflammation. Variants in lytic genes can also, though slightly, influence other EBV-related diseases. Xu et al. [16] identified two BALF2 variant sites with higher risk of nasopharyngeal carcinoma through genomic sequencing of patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, other EBV-associated diseases and healthy individuals, providing a valuable tool for screening nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Due to insufficient sample size and limited disease spectrum, we failed to identify statistically significant sites from the discovered variants. Nevertheless, this finding suggests that EBV in EBV-associated disease patients, which promotes pathological proliferation of lymphocytes rather than epithelial cells, possesses distinct genetic sequences compared to other EBV-associated epithelial malignancies.

Although not finding any difference between disease types and the risk of HLH development, our comparison of patients with different clinical outcomes revealed that variants at three sites in the BFLF1 gene were significantly more prevalent in death patients than in survival patients. This suggests that changes in the BFLF1 protein structure may be positively related to the mortality risk in EBV-associated diseases. The BFLF1 protein, also known as the DNA packaging protein UL32, plays a crucial role in the herpesviral life cycle by participating in the cleavage and packaging of the viral genome. Its main function is to encapsulate newly replicated viral DNA into the capsid, thereby forming mature viral particles [17]. Pavlova et al. [18] successfully constructed an EBV mutant devoid of BFLF1 which produced virus-like particles (VLPs) free of EBV DNA that elicited a strong CD4+ T cell response. It highlights the essential role of BFLF1 in the assembly of EBV virion structure. While the role of UL32 has been extensively studied in other herpesviruses, there is a remarkable lack of research focusing on the specific function of BFLF1 in EBV-associated diseases. Our finding, therefore, assists to fill this gap in the literature.

Given the global prevalence of EBV, we aimed to investigate regional differences in EBV strains and their impact on EBV-associated diseases through phylogenetic analysis. EBV-associated tumors including nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer, and lymphoma exhibit significant regional variations. A 2020 epidemiological study revealed that between 1990 and 2017, East Asia accounted for 43% of EBV-related tumor cases and 40% of related deaths, because of the high prevalence of nasopharyngeal and gastric cancers in East Asia particularly China [19]. Palser et al. [20] analyzed EBV DNA sequences from 71 healthy individuals and EBV-associated tumor patients across multiple countries and regions, covering East Asia, Western Europe, Central Africa, North America and Oceania. They identified genetic variations concentrated in latency genes such as EBNA2 and LMP1, which may be crucial for distinguishing asymptomatic EBV infections from pathogenic cases. Our study enrolled patients from five eastern Chinese provinces (Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Jiangxi) and western Xinjiang. Through phylogenetic analysis, we found that the results did not demonstrate significant regional differences due to insufficient geographical diversity of the included patients. Lin et al. [21] detected EBV sequences in the peripheral blood of 8 Chinese patients with extranodal NK/T cell lymphoma (nasal type) (NKTCL). The phylogenetic tree also confirmed that their EBV sequences were similar to those of other Asian NKTCL patients, further indicating the relative conservation of EBV sequences in Asia.

Although this study failed to identify statistically significant variants in the EBV DNA sequences of EBV-associated diseases patients from the perspective of disease subtypes and HLH incidence, our findings from other EBV-associated diseases provide indirect evidence of both internal homogeneity within EBV-associated diseases and heterogeneity across different conditions. BFLF1 variants, which are associated with an increased mortality risk, may provide reliable prognostic markers for the clinical outcome of EBV-associated diseases. Another limitation of our study is the neglection of host variations in critical immune surveillance genes (such as ITK, XIAP, CD27, and CTPS1) as well as HLA polymorphisms, which may also contribute to ethnic heterogeneities. Our future research will focus on expanding the sample size, incorporating patients with other EBV-related diseases and populations from diverse regions to further investigate DNA sequence polymorphisms. Additionally, we will employ multi-omics approaches such as transcriptomics and proteomics to conduct comprehensive analysis of EBV-associated diseases.
