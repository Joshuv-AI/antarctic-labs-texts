The Effectiveness of Different Intracanal Medicaments in Treating Apical Periodontitis
Authors: Kamil Shahnawaz, Priyanka Sarangi, Sukanta Kumar Satapathy, Rohan Shinkre, Koti Raghavendra, Shadab Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Apical periodontitis is frequently the result of a pulpal infection, mainly brought on by bacterial entry and the body’s response to infection. A root canal system needs to be properly disinfected to achieve the success of endodontic therapy.Materials and Methods:Single-rooted human teeth replicating apical periodontitis were below the root end, and their surfaces were mechanically prepared by rotary NiTi instruments to size F3. We separated the samples into four teams of 12, with the control being saline. Group I received calcium hydroxide, Group II had triple antibiotic paste, Group III got 2% chlorhexidine (CHX) gel, and Group IV received saline. Once the tissue common to human and root canal system was infected with Enterococcus faecalis and incubated for 21 days, the intercanal area received medicaments for 7 days. Both pre- and post-medication microbial samples were gathered using sterile paper points and then spread on BHI agar plates. We enumerated colony-forming units (CFUs).Results:Every experimental group was shown to have fewer CFUs, on average, than the control group (P < 0.05). The greatest reduction in microbial counts was achieved by Group II (TAP), followed by Group III (CHX gel) and Group I (calcium hydroxide). Microbial reduction in the control group was only at 11.5% ±2.3. The usefulness of TAP greatly surpassed that of the other treatments (P < 0.01).Conclusion:Triple antibiotic paste was the top choice among all tested medicaments with strong antimicrobial activity against E. faecalis.

INTRODUCTION

Apical periodontitis results when infection in the root canal causes inflammation of the periradicular tissues. Enterococcus faecalis bacteria, in particular, which resist removal from the canal, are a key cause for failures in endodontic treatment.[1] When using conventional root canal treatment, the aim is to kill off the bacteria by mechanically cleaning, washing, and using drugs inside the tooth. Even with mechanical treatment, bacteria may not be totally eliminated from the challenging shape of the canal system.[2]

Many dentists recommend using intracanal medicaments often between visits to control microbes. Being very alkaline and able to dissolve tissues, calcium hydroxide has been used for generations, but E. faecalis bacteria prove difficult for it.[3] In recent times, TAP, with ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, and minocycline, has joined the group of effective antimicrobial agents used in endodontics, especially for polymicrobial infections.[4] It has also been found that using chlorhexidine (CHX) as a gel helps maintain its disinfectant effect in the disinfection of root canal systems.[5]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study used 48 fresh single-rooted human permanent teeth that were mature at their tips. Soft tissue inside the teeth was removed, and the teeth were kept in a saline solution before being used. Standard access cavities were prepared using a high-speed handpiece together with water cooling. A K-file size 10 was advanced until it could be visualized at the apex, and then 1 mm was removed from the resulting length.

We used ProTaper Universal rotary nickel-titanium files (Dentsply Maillefer) in all canals, up to a size F3, and installed lots of 3% sodium hypochlorite straight after each run. EDTA at a concentration of 17% was used for irrigation, and 1 minute elapsed before washes with sterile saline. Canals were dried by using sterile pieces of paper.

Sterilization was achieved by autoclaving the specimens, and then E. faecalis (ATCC 29212) was inoculated from suspension in BHI broth. All samples were incubated at 37°C for 3 weeks to represent chronic apical periodontitis, and bacteria were supplied every third day during the incubation.

After incubation, the teeth were randomly divided into four groups (n = 12 each) based on the intracanal medicament used:

Each medicament was introduced into the canal by using a lentulo spiral and sealed coronally with a temporary restorative material. The teeth were stored at 37°C with 100% humidity for 7 days.

Post-treatment, the medicament was removed by irrigating with saline and re-preparing the canals. Microbiological sampling was done using sterile paper points inserted into the canal for 60 seconds. The points were transferred into BHI broth, serially diluted, and plated onto BHI agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 48 hours, and the number of colony-forming units (CFUs) was counted manually.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

All experimental groups exhibited a significant reduction in bacterial counts compared to the control group. Among the tested medicaments, triple antibiotic paste demonstrated the highest antimicrobial efficacy against E. faecalis, followed by 2% CHX gel and calcium hydroxide. After 7 days of intracanal medication, Group II (TAP) showed the greatest reduction in CFU (mean reduction: 92.8% ±3.4), which was statistically significant compared to other groups (P < 0.01). Group III (CHX gel) resulted in an 87.6% ±4.1 reduction, while Group I (Ca(OH)2) showed a 74.3% ±5.2 reduction. Group IV (Control) demonstrated minimal antimicrobial activity with only an 11.5% ±2.3 reduction (P > 0.05). DISCUSSION

TAP works so effectively because all three medications are designed to interfere with multiple bacterial functions. Because of this strategy, more antimicrobials reach the dentin and target a wider range of oral infections.[1] Previously, studies have found that TAP greatly reduced both regular and resistant strains of bacteria, for example, E. faecalis.[23]

The antibacterial activity of CHX gel was greater than that of calcium hydroxide, which matches past findings that show CHX is more stable and acts against a wider range of microorganisms. After being placed in the canal, CHX remains attached to dentin, protecting the root surface after its removal from the treatment.[5] However, because it cannot dissolve organic tissues, it is not recommended as the main agent in canals.

The traditional choice, calcium hydroxide, does not effectively inhibit bacteria growth than newer materials. Its trouble with biofilms combined with E. faecalis’s ability to survive in basic conditions might be the cause, according to studies.[67] Nevertheless, its ability to break down tissue and support the growth of hard tissue makes it appropriate for use in some specific health care settings.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, while all medicaments tested demonstrated antibacterial efficacy, TAP remains the most effective in reducing E. faecalis load in infected canals. However, clinical judgment should guide the selection of medicament, considering patient-specific needs, infection severity, and possible adverse effects.
