MXene (Ti3C2Tx) mediated multiscale electrolyte regulation enables robust interphase for dendrite-free solid-state sodium metal batteries
Authors: Linfeng Zhao, Chenyi Cao, Tianxiao He, Penghui Cui, Kecheng Pan, Manman Wu, Bin Tang, Yong-Zheng Fang, Zhen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solid-state sodium metal batteries (SSMBs) have garnered significant attention for next-generation high-safety energy storage, but their practical application is severely hindered by sluggish ion transport kinetics in solid electrolytes and the unstable sodium metal/electrolyte interface. Herein, we employ Ti3C2Tx MXene with abundant surface polar terminations as a multiscale regulator to construct composite solid electrolytes (P-OGN@NZSP/MXene). At the micro-nano scale, MXene serves as a two-dimensional scaffold for uniform Na3Zr2Si2PO12 (NZSP) nanoparticle loading and continuous Na+ transport network construction. At the molecular scale, MXene modulates the Na+ solvation structure, weakening Na+-polymer coordination and promoting an anion-rich solvation sheath, which enables in situ formation of a dense inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase. This synergistic design yields over 1000 h of stable cycling in Na symmetric cells with ∼16 mV polarization, and 91.2% capacity retention after 1000 cycles at 0.3C in Na‖Na3V2(PO4)3 full cells. This work offers valuable insights into the multiscale regulation of composite solid electrolytes through the incorporation of two-dimensional polar nanosheets.

Introduction

Sodium metal batteries are regarded as a promising post-lithium-ion energy storage system due to the earth-abundant reserves and low cost of sodium, as well as the exceptionally high theoretical specific capacity (1166 mA h g−1) of sodium metal anodes.1,2 Among various configurations, solid-state sodium metal batteries (SSMBs) are widely considered a pivotal advancement toward next-generation high-safety electrochemical energy storage systems.3 Currently, solid electrolytes are broadly classified into three categories, including inorganic solid electrolytes, polymer electrolytes, and composite solid electrolytes (CSEs).4,5 Inorganic solid electrolytes, such as NASICON-type Na3Zr3Si2PO12 (NZSP), usually exhibit high ionic conductivity and excellent electrochemical stability, but are brittle and poorly contact electrodes.6 Polymer electrolytes, such as polyethylene oxide (PEO) and polyacrylonitrile (PAN), possess superior flexibility and excellent film-forming ability, yet suffer from limited ionic conductivity and insufficient mechanical robustness.7,8 In comparison, composite polymer electrolytes (CPEs) synergistically integrate the advantages of both components, delivering enhanced mechanical flexibility, improved electrode–electrolyte interfacial compatibility, and tunable compositional design, thus attracting extensive research attention.9,10

In CPEs, inorganic fillers can effectively suppress polymer crystallization and expand the amorphous regions, thereby creating additional transport pathways for Na+ migration.11 However, their tendency to agglomerate within high-viscosity polymer precursors impedes homogeneous dispersion, diminishing interfacial interactions with the polymer matrix and compromising overall electrolyte homogeneity.12,13 Employing coupling agents can improve filler dispersion and thus promote uniform ionic conductivity and mechanical modulus.14 Interfacial instability at the highly reactive sodium metal anode remains another critical bottleneck, leading to unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation and rapid capacity decay. Recent studies have demonstrated that regulating the Na+ solvation structure in polymer electrolytes is a critical approach to effectively improving the cycling stability and interfacial compatibility of SSMBs.15,16 Weakening strong coordination between Na+ and polymer chains and promoting anion participation in the solvation sheath synergistically enable fast bulk ion transport and stable SEI.17,18 Nevertheless, simultaneously achieving uniform dispersion of inorganic fillers and precise control of the solvation structure remains a key challenge in fabricating an ideal solid electrolyte.12,19

Two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides or nitrides known as MXenes, featuring polar surface terminations and large specific surface area,20,21 are expected to endow multiscale regulatory effects in CPEs. Herein, we employ Ti3C2Tx MXene as a multifunctional regulator to construct a CPE (P-OGN@NZSP/MXene) for stable SSMBs. As shown in Fig. 1, at the micro–nano scale, MXene can serve as a 2D dispersion scaffold that enables uniform loading of NZSP nanoparticles and facilitates the formation of a continuous Na+ transport network within the polymer matrix. At the molecular scale, its abundant surface functional groups modulate the local coordination environment of Na+, effectively weakening the strong interactions between Na+ and polymer segments and achieving an anion-rich solvation structure. Consequently, an ultrathin, dense, and inorganic-rich SEI is in situ formed at the sodium anode interface, significantly enhancing interfacial stability and Na+ transport kinetics. Benefiting from this synergistic multiscale design, the Na‖Na symmetric cells assembled with the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte demonstrate exceptional stability for over 1000 h and maintain an ultra-low polarization voltage of approximately 16 mV. Furthermore, the assembled Na‖Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP) full cells exhibit an initial discharge specific capacity of 110.1 mA h g−1 at 0.3C and retain 91.2% capacity after 1000 cycles, demonstrating excellent interfacial stability.

Results and discussion

First, few-layer Ti3C2Tx MXene nanosheets, surface-enriched with –F, –O, and –OH polar terminations (Fig. 2a and S1), were successfully prepared via selective etching of the Ti3AlC2 (MAX) precursor. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns confirm the successful synthesis, showing a significantly low-angle shift of the (002) peaks and complete disappearance of the MAX-phase characteristic peak (Fig. S2). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images verify an ultrathin 2D layered structure with a thickness of only two layers (Fig. 2b), while a clear Tyndall effect observed in the MXene colloidal dispersion (Fig. S3) demonstrates excellent solution dispersibility, laying a foundation for uniform NZSP anchoring. Subsequently, to address the NZSP agglomeration in the polymer matrix and construct a 2D rigid inorganic ion-conducting scaffold, the NZSP/MXene composite fillers were successfully synthesized by uniformly anchoring NZSP nanoparticles (∼50 nm; Fig. S4) onto the MXene surface (detailed procedures in SI). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses show characteristic peaks for Ti–F (685.5 eV) and Na–F bonds (684.4 eV) in the F 1s spectrum (Fig. 2c),22,23 confirming robust chemical interactions between NZSP and MXene mediated by surface F terminations, rather than simple physical mixing. TEM image and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping further validate that the Na, P (from NZSP) and Ti, F elements (from MXene) exhibit high spatial overlap (Fig. S5), directly verifying the uniform dispersion and stable anchoring of NZSP nanoparticles on MXene nanosheets.

Via a solution casting method, homogeneous P-OGN@NZSP/MXene composite solid electrolyte films were fabricated by incorporating the NZSP/MXene fillers, sodium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (NaTFSI) salt, and a blended ether-oxygen-containing polymer matrix (Fig. S6, detailed procedures in SI). To elucidate the underlying working mechanism of MXene, a MXene-free P-OGN@NZSP electrolyte was also prepared as a control, which exhibits severe NZSP particle agglomeration in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2d). In contrast, when using NZSP/MXene composite fillers, P-OGN@NZSP/MXene displays a uniform and dense surface morphology (Fig. 2e), with homogeneous elemental distribution across both the surface and cross-section (Fig. 2f and S7). This demonstrates that the MXene incorporation effectively eliminates NZSP agglomeration at the nanoscale and improves electrolyte surface flatness, which can promote uniform ion transport and reduce electrode–electrolyte contact impedance.

XRD results further confirm reduced crystallinity of the polymer matrix after MXene introduction, where the intensity of PEO-based characteristic crystalline peaks is significantly weakened (Fig. 2g).24 This trend indicates that 2D MXene nanosheets disrupt the ordered stacking of polymer chains and promote the formation of more amorphous regions, which is further verified by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC; Fig. S8). Compared with the P-OGN@NZSP electrolyte, the glass transition temperature (Tg) of P-OGN@NZSP/MXene decreases from −46.05 °C to −48.74 °C, while the melting point (Tm) drops from 47.73 °C to 45.41 °C. The reduced Tg reflects enhanced polymer segmental mobility in the amorphous regions, thereby providing more flexible and continuous transport pathways for Na+ migration.25 Tensile tests show the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte has an elastic modulus of 5.54 MPa and an elongation at break of 991.09%, which far exceed those of the MXene-free control (1.41 MPa and 510.8%, respectively; Fig. 2h). This significant improvement is mainly attributed to the uniform NZSP dispersion and the existence of a flexible MXene network, which not only improves film flexibility and integrity but also buffers volume changes and local stress concentrations during Na plating and stripping. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) uses 2 µA cm−2 as the oxidative decomposition threshold. The results show that MXene incorporation extends the electrolyte oxidative potential from 3.9 V to above 4.5 V (Fig. 2i), confirming its effectiveness in suppressing oxidative side reactions.

The above improvement in the physicochemical and electrochemical properties of the electrolyte stems not merely from the uniform dispersion of NZSP but predominantly from the incorporation of MXene, which modulates the coordination environment within the electrolyte. To further explore the working mechanism of MXene, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to reveal the coordination configuration of electrolytes. The corresponding molecular structure model is presented in Fig. S9, with detailed coordination information of Na+-TFSI− and Na+-EO chains provided in radial distribution function (RDF) analysis (Fig. 3a and b). P-OGN@NZSP exhibits higher Na+-EO coordination intensity and coordination number (CN) alongside lower Na+-TFSI− coordination, indicating that Na+ is strongly bound by EO segments. By contrast, when MXene is introduced, the Na+-EO coordination is effectively suppressed in the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte; more notably, the electrolyte shows significantly enhanced anionic coordination strength with two distinct coordination peaks. These findings indicate MXene facilitates the incorporation of numerous anions into the solvation sheath and the formation of a more anion-rich solvation structure (Fig. 3c), which can promote the preferential formation of an anion-dominated SEI.24 Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further employed to reveal the impact of MXene on the coordination structure in electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 3d, PEO chains exhibit a Na+ adsorption energy of −3.71 eV, while the presence of MXene markedly reduces this value to −2.61 eV, confirming that MXene can effectively weaken the strong coordination interaction between Na+ and ether oxygen segments and thereby promote the partial dissociation of Na+ from the polymer matrix, consistent with the RDF analysis results. Additionally, Fig. 3e reveals a substantially stronger adsorption of TFSI− on MXene (−3.24 eV) compared to its weak interaction with PEO (−0.48 eV). This indicates MXene can promote the dissociation of sodium salts and selectively anchor TFSI− anions, which significantly increases the Na+ carrier concentration and suppresses TFSI− anion migration, as corroborated by mean square displacement (MSD) analysis (Fig. 3f). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the MXene effectively modulates coordination interactions within the electrolyte, achieving two synergistic effects, including weakened coordination between Na+ and polymer and promoted sodium salt dissociation and anion anchoring. These effects jointly facilitate rapid sodium ion transport and the formation of anion-rich solvation structures.

Consistent with the calculations, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopies (Fig. S10 and S11) reveal significant red shift in the characteristic vibrational peaks of methylene (–CH2−) and ether (–C–O–C–) groups after MXene incorporation. This observation confirms that the MXene surface polar groups induce electron cloud polarization at PEO ether oxygen atoms,26,27 enabling directional electron shift from ether oxygen to MXene and thereby attenuating the Na+–O (ether) coordination strength. This creates favourable conditions for the dissociation and rapid migration of Na+. Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of 23Na further confirms this conclusion, where P-OGN@NZSP/MXene exhibits a distinct downfield shift of the Na+ resonance peak compared to the control (Fig. S12), reflecting reduced electron shielding around Na+.28 Furthermore, Raman spectroscopic analysis of the TFSI− symmetric stretching mode (∼740 cm−1; Fig. 3g and h),29 coupled with quantitative peak deconvolution, directly visualizes MXene's regulatory impact on ionic coordination structure. Relative to P-OGN@NZSP, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte exhibits a decrease in free TFSI− from 15.4% to 10.3%, accompanied by increases in contact ion pairs (CIPs) and aggregates (AGGs) from 73.3% to 76.3% and from 11.3% to 13.4%, respectively. These results experimentally verify that the anchoring effect of MXene on TFSI− anions effectively reduces free anion content and that the interaction between MXene and polymers promotes association between Na+ and TFSI−, forming an anion-rich solvation structure.

Ion transport performance of composite electrolytes was evaluated using stainless-steel symmetric cells. As shown in Fig. 4a, the addition of inorganic fillers effectively enhances ionic conductivity. When MXene is introduced, the conductivity of the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte exhibits a significant improvement, achieving a maximum ionic conductivity of 1.45 × 10−3 S cm−1 at an NZSP : MXene mass ratio of 10 : 1 (Fig. S13), which is more than double that of the MXene-free P-OGN@NZSP electrolyte (7.2 × 10−4 S cm−1). The accelerated ion transport kinetics are further verified by the Arrhenius analysis, where the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte displays a shallower slope in its Arrhenius plot, corresponding to a reduction in Na+ migration activation energy (Ea) from 0.451 eV to 0.361 eV (Fig. 4b and S14). This confirms that the uniform NZSP distribution and the coordination regulation effect of MXene effectively lower the energy barrier for Na+ migration, thereby facilitating faster ion conduction. Furthermore, the Na+ transference number (tNa+) increases substantially from 0.205 in P-OGN@NZSP to 0.557 in P-OGN@NZSP/MXene, as shown in Fig. 4c and S15, verifying that MXene selectively enhances the migration capability of Na+ by anchoring the TFSI− anion.

To further investigate the interface stability of the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene composite electrolyte, Na‖Na symmetric cells were assembled for a long-term cycling test. As shown in Fig. 4d, the cell with the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte exhibits exceptional cycling stability, maintaining a low and stable overpotential of approximately 16 mV for over 1000 h. In contrast, the MXene-free P-OGN@NZSP electrolyte delivers a rapid increase in polarization voltage to 125 mV within 113 h, terminating at a short circuit (Fig. 4e). This stark contrast confirms that MXene-mediated solvation structure regulation significantly enhances sodium metal anode compatibility. In situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed to reveal the interfacial dynamic evolution during the first 20 cycles (Fig. S16). Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analyses were further applied to decouple the interfacial reaction processes, which can be divided into three steps corresponding to different time constants (τ), including the SEI kinetics (Rsei), charge transfer (Rct), and diffusion process (Rdiff).30,31 For the P-OGN@NZSP (Fig. 4f), the DRT peaks corresponding to all time-constant intervals show significant fluctuations during cycling, especially Rsei, revealing rapid deterioration of the interfacial reaction processes. By contrast, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene exhibits highly consistent DRT peak positions and amplitudes across all cycles (Fig. 4h), reflecting remarkable interfacial robustness. In addition, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte also exhibits lower resistance values and faster relaxation times, reflecting improved reversibility and reduced polarization. Exchange current density (i0) analysis further quantifies the superior interface reaction kinetics, with P-OGN@NZSP/MXene exhibiting a higher i0 of 0.164 mA cm−2, exceeding that of P-OGN@NZSP (0.106 mA cm−2; Fig. S17). SEM observations of cycled sodium metal anodes further provide direct visual evidence for the performance differences. For the P-OGN@NZSP, numerous island-like dendrites form on the sodium metal surface after cycling (Fig. 4g), whereas the sodium metal surface with the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene remains a flat, dense, and dendrite-free morphology after cycling (Fig. 4i). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the uniform NZSP dispersion and regulated solvation structure jointly endow stable, reversible Na plating/stripping and suppress interfacial degradation.

To investigate the regulatory mechanism of solvation structure on the interfacial chemistry, XPS was performed for cycled Na metal anodes. Compared to P-OGN@NZSP, P-OGN@NZSP/MXene exhibits higher atomic percentages of Na, F, and N with lower C and O contents (Fig. 5d, e and S18), implying more inorganic components in the formed interphase. In high-resolution spectra, the key NaF (684.4 eV, Fig. 5a), Na2O (530.9 eV, Fig. 5b), and Na3N (397.8 eV, Fig. 5c) can be attributed to TFSI− decomposition,32–34 which possess high ionic conductivity and excellent mechanical stability. Upon MXene incorporation, the relative intensities of these inorganic species increase markedly, with 89% to 92% for NaF, 4% to 20% for Na2O, and 40% to 43% for Na3N (Fig. 5d and e). Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM) was further employed to characterize the morphology and nanoscale composition of the SEI. For the sodium metal anode cycled with the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte, an ultrathin and well-defined SEI architecture forms on the electrode surface, exhibiting an average thickness of 8 nm (Fig. 5f). High-resolution images further reveal that this SEI comprises an organic outer layer and a dense inorganic-rich inner layer with numerous NaF components. The formation of this SEI structure is attributed to the TFSI−-rich solvation environment, which drives the preferential adsorption and decomposition of the weakly coordinated TFSI− anions. Such a structured interphase not only suppresses continuous electrolyte decomposition but also enables uniform and rapid Na+ transport. In comparison, the P-OGN@NZSP electrolyte induces a much thicker (∼18 nm) and non-uniform SEI on the sodium metal surface, characterized by a mosaic-like structure with randomly interspersed organic and inorganic phases (Fig. 5g). These Cryo-TEM observations, together with XPS results, demonstrate that MXene-mediated anion enrichment in the solvation sheath directs preferential formation of an inorganic-dominated SEI, thereby enhancing Na metal anode stability and validating critical role of MXene in interfacial engineering through solvation structure regulation.

Given its rapid Na+ transport kinetics and robust inorganic-rich SEI, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte is expected to deliver excellent electrochemical performance in sodium metal batteries. Na‖Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP) full cells were assembled, and electrochemical floating tests were performed to further evaluate cathode compatibility for the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene and P-OGN@NZSP electrolytes (Fig. S19). Within the 4.0–4.7 V voltage window, the cell employing the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte exhibits lower and more stable leakage current, confirming the favourable oxidation stability of the composite electrolyte. Rate test shows that the cell with the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene electrolyte delivers outstanding rate capability, which presents an initial discharge capacity of 106.4 mA h g−1 at 0.1C and retains 98.6 mA h g−1 when the current density is increased to 1C (Fig. 6a). In contrast, the MXene-free P-OGN@NZSP delivers a capacity of 106.1 mA h g−1 at 0.1C, but suffers significant capacity decays to 87.2 mA h g−1 at 1C. Furthermore, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene cell exhibits markedly reduced charge/discharge voltage polarization (Fig. 6b and c), reflecting improved interfacial kinetics attributable to MXene-induced optimization of both the composite nanostructure and the Na+ solvation environment.

Long-term cycling performance tests further validate the interfacial stability at both anode and cathode. At 0.3C, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 110.1 mA h g−1 and achieves 91.2% capacity retention after 1000 cycles, with smaller increase in voltage polarization throughout the cycles (Fig. 6d and S20). In contrast, the P-OGN@NZSP cell delivers an initial capacity of 101 mA h g−1 but exhibits larger voltage polarization and fails due to short-circuiting after 501 cycles, reflecting rapid dendrite growth in the control. Furthermore, at a higher current density of 0.5C (Fig. 6f), the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene cell maintains stable cycling for over 500 cycles, achieving an average Coulombic efficiency of 99.5% and a capacity retention of 88.3% after cycling. The control cell, however, exhibits severe capacity decay and experiences short-circuiting after only 220 cycles. Compared with previously reported Na metal batteries using polymer-based electrolytes, the P-OGN@NZSP/MXene demonstrates superior cycling longevity and capacity retention (Fig. 6e, Table S1). These results confirm that the MXene-mediated uniform filler dispersion and anion-enriched solvation structure effectively enhance interfacial dynamics and stability, highlighting the strategic advantage of incorporating 2D polar nanosheets into polymer electrolytes. To assess its practical application potential, flexible pouch cells were fabricated and tested at 0.1C, delivering stable capacity output (Fig. S21). In addition, the pouch cell successfully powers an LED array even under extreme abuse conditions, including bending, folding, and shearing (Fig. 6g), demonstrating excellent mechanical robustness and intrinsic safety.

Conclusion

In summary, a P-OGN@NZSP/MXene composite solid electrolyte has been successfully developed using few-layer Ti3C2Tx MXene as a multifunctional additive, fulfilling multiscale regulation. At the microstructural level, MXene acts as a 2D structural scaffold that enables uniform dispersion of NZSP fillers. In the solvation environment, MXene modulates Na+ coordination by simultaneously interacting with polymer segments and TFSI− anions, thereby weakening Na+–polymer coordination and promoting anion-enriched solvation sheaths. These functions collectively yield a high Na+ transference number (0.557, 2.7-fold increase), ionic conductivity (1.45 × 10−3 S cm−1, 2-fold increase), and mechanical robustness (5.54 MPa, 3.9-fold increase) for the electrolyte. More importantly, the anion-enriched solvation structure directs the in situ formation of an inorganic-rich SEI, achieving long-term interfacial stability. Na‖Na symmetric cells employing this electrolyte stably cycle for over 1000 h at 0.1 mA cm−2 with an ultra-low polarization voltage of 16 mV. Na‖Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP) full cells deliver an initial discharge capacity of 110.1 mA h g−1 at 0.3C and retain 91.2% of this capacity after 1000 cycles, demonstrating exceptional electrochemical performance. These findings provide a paradigm for multiscale regulation of composite solid electrolytes via the incorporation of 2D polar nanosheets.
