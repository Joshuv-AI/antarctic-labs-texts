Social determinants of subjective well-being among Nigerian women
Authors: Ridwan Suleiman Adejumo, Babatunde Makinde Gbadebo, Joshua Odunayo Akinyemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubjective well-being (SWB) reflects individuals’ evaluations of life satisfaction and emotional experiences, influenced by economic, social, and psychological factors. Existing research on Nigerian women often suffers from narrow focus, small sample sizes, or limited methodological rigor, leaving gaps in understanding how diverse social contexts influence SWB. To address these gaps, this study employs nationally representative data from the 2021 Nigerian Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey.MethodsWe analysed data from the nationally representative 2021 Nigeria Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey, which included 40,326 women aged 15–49 years across all 36 states and the FCT. Subjective well-being (SWB) was measured using four indicators: overall happiness, life satisfaction, comparative life satisfaction, and future life satisfaction. Given the hierarchical structure of women nested within communities, a Bayesian multilevel cumulative logit model was applied to account for clustering and identify determinants of SWB.ResultsThe descriptive results show that 39% had poor, 21% average, and 40% good subjective well-being. We found that age was a significant factor, with women aged 35- 39 years having lower odds of reporting good well-being than younger women aged 15- 19 years (AOR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.37, 0.45). Educational attainment played a crucial role, as women with a tertiary education experienced better subjective well-being (AOR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.17, 1.47) compared to those with no education.ConclusionOur results underscore the need for policies enhancing education and reducing societal barriers to improve well-being among Nigerian women. The findings of this study emphasize the importance of gender-sensitive, context-specific policies, including expanded healthcare access, educational opportunities, and social protection for vulnerable groups such as elderly women and female-headed households. By accounting for Nigeria’s diversity, targeted interventions can more effectively enhance women’s well-being and overall quality of life.Keywords: Subjective well-being, Bayesian analysis, Nigeria, Multilevel model

Background

Subjective well-being is an important construct because it is closely linked to key indicators of life satisfaction and happiness and has significant implications for individuals’ daily functioning and long-term outcomes. Studies have shown that subjective health status and participation in social activities are critical determinants of happiness among older adults [13]. Research has shown that higher levels of subjective well-being are associated with better mental and physical health, stronger social relationships, higher productivity, and greater resilience to stress. Conversely, individuals who perceive their well-being as poor are more likely to experience lower life satisfaction, increased psychological distress, reduced motivation, and difficulties in social and occupational functioning.

Subjective well-being is defined as an individual's self-reported assessment of their life satisfaction and emotional experiences. Diener [7] emphasized that subjective well-being encompasses cognitive evaluations of life satisfaction and affective responses, including positive and negative emotions. The measurement of well-being can be categorized into objective and subjective approaches. Objective measures often rely on observable indicators such as income, education, and health, whereas subjective measures focus on individuals' feelings and experiences [5]. One of the most widely used subjective measures is the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), which assesses individuals’ global judgments about their overall life satisfaction. The SWLS consists of self-report items that ask respondents to evaluate the extent to which their life circumstances align with their personal standards and expectations, making it a reliable and valid tool for capturing the cognitive component of subjective well-being [5]. By focusing on individuals’ internal evaluations rather than external conditions alone, the scale reflects the multidimensional nature of well-being.

Although research has increasingly examined social determinants of subjective well-being (SWB) and health, there is a notable gap in comparative gender analysis that limits understanding of whether and how work and various social conditions differentially affect men and women. For instance, a comprehensive study on material living conditions and subjective health and well-being in Nigeria found that poor living conditions detrimentally influence well-being for both men and women, yet did not provide gender-specific mechanisms explaining how work or social status might impact health differently across genders [9]. Additionally, epidemiological research from the Ibadan Study of Ageing indicates gender differentials in how social and economic predictors relate to mental health outcomes, with occupational attainment and social contact showing distinct associations for men and women [17], underscoring the necessity of gender-comparative analysis. Taken together, these findings imply that while social influences on wellbeing are documented, existing research has not sufficiently clarified why women alone are studied without comparing men, nor articulated how specific work environments shape health outcomes differently by gender, leaving a significant empirical gap that future studies must address.

Research on subjective well-being (SWB) among Nigerian women reveals important but incomplete scholarly coverage that limits understanding of how diverse cultural and social contexts shape well-being. For example, studies focusing on specific subpopulations, such as pregnant women [23], provide valuable insights into life satisfaction and happiness during pregnancy, but do not account for the broader cultural and regional diversity within Nigeria’s population. Similarly, work on female teachers in Bayelsa State [22] and rural reproductive-age women in Southwest Nigeria highlights how social relationships and socio-demographic factors correlate with well-being, but it remains geographically and contextually limited. Broader quantitative studies using national data [9] examine material living conditions and well-being but still point to the need for more nuanced analysis of cultural, ethnic, and socioeconomic influences across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones. Furthermore, existing studies may rely heavily on self-report measures, which can introduce biases and limit the generalizability of findings [19]. Together, these studies imply that current literature frequently lacks comprehensive integration of the cultural heterogeneity and social complexity inherent to Nigerian women's lived experiences, limiting the generalizability and depth of findings. There is a need for more robust methodologies that account for these complexities and provide a clearer understanding of the factors influencing SWB among Nigerian women.

For instance, Abdullahi et al. [1] highlighted the role of age and gender in shaping subjective well-being among Nigerians, showing that cultural and social norms significantly influence women’s life satisfaction. However, many of these studies have limitations, such as small sample sizes, a lack of national representativeness, or a narrow focus on single dimensions of well-being, such as health or economic status. In contrast, our study addresses these gaps by using the 2021 Nigerian Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), which is nationally representative and includes diverse socioeconomic, cultural, and regional contexts. Additionally, we employed a Bayesian multilevel cumulative logit modelling approach, which accounts for the hierarchical structure of the data and reduces potential biases common in traditional regression methods. By integrating individual- and community-level factors, this study provides a more comprehensive and refined understanding of the social determinants of subjective well-being among Nigerian women. This robust approach offers evidence that can inform culturally relevant policies and targeted interventions to improve women’s quality of life across different regions of Nigeria.

Methods

Data source and study population

In our study, we utilized publicly available, de-identified data from the 2021 Nigeria Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) dataset, conducted by the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) in collaboration with the United Nations Children’s Fund. The data collection procedures adhered to ethical guidelines and received approval from relevant national authorities at the time of collection. Verbal consent for the MICS was obtained from each participating respondent, and for children aged 15–17 years, individual consent was obtained in advance of the child’s assent. As our study involves secondary analysis of anonymized data and does not involve direct interaction with human participants or the use of personally identifiable information, further ethical approval was not required. Our research complies with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The dataset encompasses the entire country of Nigeria, covering the 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT), Abuja, along with each state's respective local government areas (LGAs). The survey included both urban and rural areas across these states to ensure national representation and focuses on Nigerian women aged 15–49 years, comprising a sample of 40,326 women from 1,755 communities across the six geopolitical zones. The survey was conducted using a multistage, stratified random sampling technique, where stratification occurred based on urban and rural areas within each state. Clusters were selected from each stratum, with enumeration areas (EAs) chosen as the primary sampling units. Households were then randomly selected within these clusters for interviews. The Nigerian MICS 2021 women's dataset gathers information on various indicators related to women's health, education, fertility, childbearing, child health, and overall well-being, utilizing a cross-sectional study design. The data was collected through the administration of structured questionnaires via face-to-face interviews.

Data management

We merged the women’s and household datasets using their respective cluster and household numbers to obtain the household variables for each woman. We also recategorized certain variables, such as the age of the household head, and recoded all values into a numeric format to fit our analysis, while dropping variables that were not needed for our study. Each of the four items measuring subjective well-being had missing values ranging from 3.89%—4.51%. We conducted a chi-square test to analyse categorical variables and an independent sample t-test to compare individuals with complete information on subjective well-being items against those with missing data. Additionally, we performed an independent two-sample test for continuous variables. Our analysis revealed that the data were not missing completely at random (MCAR). Consequently, we applied multiple imputation via Bayesian ridge regression to address the missing data via the Python scikit-learn library [18].

Study variables

We measured the subjective well-being construct via four indicators (Table 1): overall happiness (five levels), life satisfaction (ten steps), comparative life satisfaction (three levels), and future life satisfaction (three levels). The inclusion of life satisfaction is because subjective well-being (SWB) reflects both future-oriented perceptions and structural living conditions [25]. Expectations about future life capture optimism and perceived life trajectories, which are recognized components of cognitive well-being and are widely used in large population surveys such as the Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) [16].

Using multiple correspondence analysis, we constructed composite indices for the subjective well-being construct on the basis of the first-dimensional factor scores, which captured the primary patterns of variation in the respective items. The subjective well-being index ranges from 0 (lowest possible score) to 100 (highest possible score), with higher scores indicating greater well-being. We then categorized these continuous indices in R by dividing them into percentiles on the basis of their ranks: values lower than 40 were classified as poor, values between 40 and 60 as average, and values above 60 as good.

Characteristics such as age, education, marital status, ethnicity, and health insurance coverage are established predictors of well-being as they reflect life-course position, human capital, social support, cultural context, and access to healthcare [9]. Household factors such as wealth index, household head’s education, sex, settlement type, and geographical location capture material resources and contextual inequalities that shape quality of life and health perceptions [15]. Prior research consistently shows that demographic and social conditions significantly influence life satisfaction and subjective health, supporting the inclusion of these variables in well-being models.

Statistical analysis

The MICS data have a hierarchical structure in which women are nested within communities. We presumed that women residing within the same community would exhibit more similar characteristics compared to women from different communities. This hierarchical structure violates the assumptions of independence of observations and equal variance required for a standard cumulative logit model. To address this, we used a two-level cumulative logit model to account for the nested nature of the data and obtain reliable estimates and standard errors. The analysis was weighted using the women’s weight variable to achieve nationally representative results.

Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) was employed in R via the Factor MineR R package to analyse the subjective well-being construct. Scree plot visualizations were generated via the ggplot2 package in R. The Bayesian regression model using the Stan (BRMS) package [3] was used to conduct the cumulative logit analysis via the Bayesian hierarchical statistical approach to identify factors associated with subjective well-being. The results were presented in the form of texts and tables, whereas the bayesplot R package [11] was used for diagnostic checks and result visualization. Since the outcome variable has three ordinal categories, a cumulative logit model was employed.

Discussion and conclusion

Individual factors associated with subjective well-being

Our study identified several individual factors that significantly influence subjective well-being, with age emerging as a key determinant. Specifically, the results corroborate findings from Abdullahi et al.[1], which indicated that age plays a substantial role in shaping women's subjective well-being, with patterns varying across different life stages. Additionally, the present study aligns with the conclusions of the English longitudinal study of ageing [14], which indicated that older cohorts report similar or even better levels of subjective well-being than younger cohorts do. However, when accounting for factors such as widowhood, retirement, and declining health, individual aging effects revealed that subjective well-being declines more sharply among older age groups. This suggests that although older women may initially report greater subjective well-being, they experience more pronounced declines as they age.

Furthermore, the study identified education level as another significant determinant of subjective well-being among women. This finding is consistent with the study conducted by Tran et al.[21], which utilized data from the Household, Income, and Labor Dynamics in Australia (HILDA) survey, where they reported that higher educational attainment is positively associated with both eudaimonic and hedonic well-being, increased positive affect, and reduced psychological distress among women.

Our study also supports the findings of Cao et al. [4], who examined 20-year trajectories of subjective well-being and revealed that married individuals generally report more positive appraisals of their health, emotional well-being, and social well-being than those who are divorced or single. Additionally, our study highlighted the role of health insurance in influencing women's subjective well-being, which aligns with the findings of Zhou et al. [24], who reported that health insurance significantly improves the subjective well-being of women in China.

In terms of ethnicity, numerous studies have demonstrated that subjective well-being varies across different ethnic groups. For example, Diaz and Bui [6] examined Mexican and Mexican American women and reported that acculturation, ethnic identity, gender roles, and perceived social support play crucial roles in shaping their subjective well-being. Similarly, Geldenhuys and Henn [12] reported that life satisfaction among women in South Africa varied significantly across ethnic groups, further supporting our finding that ethnicity is a key factor in determining subjective well-being.

In the Nigerian context, the intersection of cultural norms with socioeconomic factors adds complexity to these findings. For example, the disparity in subjective well-being between married and unmarried women reflects the societal pressures and limited resources available to single women. This highlights the need for tailored financial inclusion policies that address the diverse needs of Nigerian women across various life stages.

Household factors associated with subjective well-being

Numerous studies have examined the influence of household head gender on women's subjective well-being, providing valuable insights into this dynamic. For example, Adhikar et al. [2] conducted research in South Africa and reported that households headed by males generally report greater subjective well-being than those headed by females. This finding aligns with the argument that female-headed households are more likely to face heightened poverty risk, which can negatively affect subjective well-being. Additionally, Shadabi et al. [20] further explored the psychosocial challenges faced by female heads of household. Their study indicated that the burden of managing multiple roles often leads to increased stress, which in turn detracts from life satisfaction and mental health, placing women in female-headed households at a disadvantage compared with their male counterparts.

In the context of Nigeria, the study's findings on the role of geographical region in shaping women's subjective well-being resonate with similar research conducted in other regions. For example, Fang et al. [10] examined the effects of urban, work, and social environments on subjective well-being in China and revealed that regional differences significantly influence residents' well-being. In particular, the study highlighted that factors such as housing prices and pollution levels were particularly influential in the eastern region, whereas economic development and environmental pollution had greater impacts in the western region. This suggests that regional economic and environmental conditions are crucial in shaping the well-being of women. Similarly, research by Elburz et al. [8] in Turkey explored regional disparities in subjective well-being, emphasizing the importance of geographical context. The study revealed that the level of urbanization and the size of cities were significant determinants of well-being, with larger cities offering more amenities but also posing challenges such as congestion and higher living costs. These urban dynamics, as highlighted by the study, can have varying effects on women's subjective well-being, depending on the regional context.

Implications of the findings

From a policy perspective, the results provide evidence that can inform several ongoing national programs and strategies in Nigeria. For example, the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) Act, which seeks to achieve universal health coverage, could benefit from these findings by prioritizing outreach to women, particularly those in rural areas and female-headed households who are identified as highly vulnerable. Similarly, the National Gender Policy and programs under the Federal Ministry of Women Affairs can leverage these insights to design interventions that address the educational and economic barriers faced by women. Expanding initiatives such as the National Cash Transfer Programme (NASSCO) and the National Social Safety Nets Project (NASSP) to specifically target widowed and divorced women could help mitigate the sharp declines in well-being observed among these groups.

Regional disparities in subjective well-being also highlight the need for place-based interventions, such as region-specific poverty alleviation schemes and mental health services, particularly in the northeast and northwest zones, where poverty and conflict remain prevalent. These findings can also guide the implementation of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in Nigeria, especially Goals 3 (good health and well-being) and 5 (gender equality).

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including its use of a nationally representative dataset and a Bayesian multilevel analytical framework, which allows for deep insights into the relationship between financial inclusion and subjective well-being. Additionally, the study's focus on Nigerian women provides valuable, context-specific data to inform gender-sensitive policies to improve the quality of life.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional nature of the data limits the ability to infer causality. Additionally, unobserved variables, such as cultural attitudes or social capital, may influence the findings but were not directly measured. While Bayesian methods account for some hierarchical effects, potential biases from missing data imputation and self-reported measures remain concerns.

Summary and conclusion

The findings of our study underscore the need for life-stage-specific and inclusive social policies to address the diverse needs of Nigerian women. For vulnerable groups such as elderly women and female-headed households, gender-sensitive mental health support and social safety nets are particularly important. Our study suggests expanding educational opportunities and offering emotional and financial support for single, divorced, or widowed women. The positive association between health insurance and improved well-being calls for broader and more equitable access to healthcare services. Regional disparities in subjective well-being indicate that policy interventions must be context-specific, addressing both the unique social challenges of each region and the intersecting factors of gender, ethnicity, and household dynamics.
