What Information on Mammographic Screening is Available to Women in Quebec, Ontario and Canada: Results from a Document Analysis
Authors: Alexandra Larocque, Isabelle Trop, Nathalie Gaucher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInformation provided to women invited to participate in mammography screening is crucial in supporting informed consent. Expert recommendations remain divided, and the ethical framing of autonomy, especially in shared decision-making, often fails to reflect the realities of clinical practice. This study examines how public-facing documents communicate screening information and explores whether relational autonomy offers a more ethically coherent approach. A document analysis was conducted using the READ method. Documents published between 1999 and 2023, from Quebec, Ontario or Canada, containing the keywords ‘breast cancer screening’ and ‘screening mammography’ were included and assessed using a 16-category grid. Fifty-one documents were included (Qc: 16, On: 10, Can: 25) and 11 were excluded. 10 per cent included women from the public, but the majority of contributing experts were women (57 per cent). 96 per cent of documents were considered inaccessible based on the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level score. Benefits were mentioned more often than risks (90 per cent vs 37 per cent) and 16 per cent confused diagnosis and screening. Women under 50 were overrepresented (37 per cent) and racialized women underrepresented (58 per cent). This analysis reveals biases in how screening information is designed and communicated. Relational autonomy offers a more inclusive framework for evaluating and improving screening communication.

Background

Population-based screening, considered as public health intervention, aims at reducing breast cancer mortality. Breast cancer screening raises distinctive ethical concerns regarding autonomy, in part because it is a publicly organized intervention in which invitations to participate come from government authorities rather than individual clinical encounters. These invitations occur in a context where fear of cancer is socially prominent, participation is implicitly framed as responsible citizenship, and the presentation of benefits has historically been emphasized while potential harms, such as false positives and overdiagnosis, are much less discussed. Moreover, access to balanced and comprehensible information about screening benefits and harms remains uneven. Together, these features position breast cancer screening as a paradigmatic case in which autonomy is not only an individual attribute, but an ethical problem embedded in the intervention itself (Entwistle et al., 2008; Marmot et al., 2013; Carter, 2016).

Ethical analyses of screening participation have traditionally relied on a procedural conception of autonomy, in which respect for individuals is operationalized through the provision of information and the protection of voluntary choice (Stoljar, 2024). However, this narrow framing has been increasingly challenged by relational accounts of autonomy, which emphasizes that autonomy is not only the capacity of making independent choices, but is also shaped by relationships, cultural norms or power asymmetries (Baylis et al., 2008). In the context of breast cancer screening, where invitations are standardized and participation is socially promoted, relational autonomy offers a particularly salient lens for examining how decisions are enabled or constrained in practice, while avoiding the reductive logic of decision-making in screening as information processing

Specifically, publicly available screening materials play a critical role in shaping participation decisions, particularly given that many women encounter screening information outside of clinical encounters. When such materials emphasize participation, or frame benefits and harms asymmetrically, they may undermine both informed decision-making and the feasibility of shared decision-making. In such cases, clinicians are required to navigate pre-structured informational environments that may already orient patients towards a preferred choice. Consequently, reliance on information materials as standalone tools may limit the conditions necessary for deliberation, which remains essential to shared decision-making.

While ethical literature has extensively debated autonomy and shared decision-making in screening, less attention has been paid to how publicly available materials operationalize these concepts in practice. In particular, there is limited empirical analysis of how breast cancer screening documents present benefits and harms and whether they implicitly endorse individual, relational or shared decision-making approaches.

Engaging in Screening Conversations

While informed decision-making (IDM) and shared decision-making (SDM) are often invoked as mechanisms for respecting patient autonomy, they rest on distinct normative assumptions. IDM is typically grounded in an individualistic conception of autonomy, where individuals are expected to make voluntary choices based on adequate disclosure of relevant information (Whitney et al., 2004; Elmore et al., 2010). Autonomy, in this view, is primarily procedural: if information is provided and coercion is absent, the resulting choice is considered autonomous.

Population-based cancer screening poses particular challenges for IDM. Unlike many clinical decisions, screening invitations are typically initiated outside the clinical encounter, embedded in public health messaging and framed against cultural expectations that valorize early detection (Gigerenzer et al., 2009; Gigerenzer, 2015; Løberg et al., 2015). These features complicate the assumption that individuals encounter screening information as neutral decision-makers deliberating in isolation. In such contexts, autonomy cannot be adequately understood as a matter of information transfer alone.

SDM, by contrast, aligns more closely with relational conceptions of autonomy, which emphasize that decision-making capacities are shaped by interpersonal relationships and social context. Rather than treating external influence as inherently autonomy-undermining, relational autonomy recognizes that relationships, including those with clinicians, can enable meaningful deliberation, particularly in contexts characterized by uncertainty or value-sensitive trade-offs (Lewis, 2019).

SDM has therefore been proposed as a more ethically appropriate model for screening decisions because it reframes the encounter as a relational and context-sensitive process rather than a purely informational transaction. However, the feasibility of SDM depends not only on clinician engagement but also on publicly available information that precedes and structures screening conversations.

Cultural and Social Expectations on Screening

Beyond clinical encounters, breast cancer screening decisions are shaped by powerful cultural and social narratives. Public health campaigns and media representations do not merely convey information about screening; they contribute to cultural scripts that frame screening as a moral imperative and blur its clinical boundaries.

One dominant script is the moralization of screening participation. Screening is frequently portrayed as a duty or an act of responsible self-care, such that choosing not to participate may be implicitly framed as negligent or irrational. From the perspective of relational autonomy, such moral framing is ethically problematic because it transforms what should be a preference-concordant health decision into a normative expectation, thereby exerting social pressure (Dobias et al., 2001).

A second influential script involves the narrative conflation of screening with diagnosis and survival (McGannon et al., 2016). Survivor testimonies and emotionally salient media narratives often imply that early detection through screening is the primary, or sole, reason for survival, obscuring the distinction between asymptomatic screening and diagnostic investigation. These narratives may reinforce exaggerated perceptions of benefit while rendering potential harms invisible, thereby distorting the informational conditions under which decisions are made.

Second, survivor testimonies in screening campaigns contribute to the confusion between screening asymptomatic individuals for potential cancer and diagnosing and treating symptomatic individuals. For example, the diagnosis of an Australian celebrity’s breast cancer outside of a population-based screening programme led to an augmentation of 115.8 per cent of mammography screening bookings in never-screened and a 42 per cent increase for already screened in the 2-week mediatic coverage of her story (Chapman et al., 2005).

Importantly, these scripts do not operate uniformly across populations, particularly given that popular media remains a dominant source of screening information (Gigerenzer et al., 2009). Studies further indicate that messaging strategies vary by socioeconomic status, with simplified or directive messaging disproportionately targeting marginalized groups (Dobias et al., 2001). Ensuring accessibility is therefore essential, especially in light of Canada’s high functional illiteracy rates (ABC Life Literacy Canada, 2018). When considered together, these disparities in framing and access raise concerns not only about autonomy but also about justice, as it suggests that some women are systematically afforded less opportunity for deliberation than others.

In that sense, this study examines publicly available online breast cancer screening documents from 2 Canadian provinces, Quebec and Ontario, to assess how, and if, information on screening benefits and harms is presented and to identify the decision-making models implicitly or explicitly promoted within these materials.

Methods

Study Context

Screening in Canada is province-based, meaning that each province has its own jurisdiction over the organization of its screening services. Although recommendations at a national level are provided through different authorities, the implementation or not of a programme, as well as its specifications, is at each province and territory’s discretion. Out of the 13 Canadian provinces and territories, only Nunavut does not have a screening programme. All available programmes have specific geographical, clinical or organizational criteria. A portrait of breast cancer screening as of November 2023 in Canada is available in Table 1 (My Breast Screening, 2021). The most significant guideline followed by Canada at a national level would be the Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care’s recommendations.

The primary scope of this research is to assess the available information for women living in Quebec. However, as a comparison point, documents from Ontario were also included. Comparing Quebec and Ontario with Canada more broadly provided good insight into both provincial and national breast cancer screening programmes. These two provinces were specifically chosen as the two most populated provinces in Canada, each with its own majority language (English and French). These two provinces were especially interesting because of the similarities between the two programmes. Although Ontario’s screening criteria have recently changed, screening criteria in Quebec and Ontario were the same until November 2023, when this analysis was conducted. Previous studies on public health have also chosen Quebec and Ontario as comparators because of the similarities these two provinces share while presenting different legislation and cultures (Smith et al., 2011; Gagnon et al., 2022).

In Quebec, mammograms are recommended for women between 50 and 74 (Quebec Government, 2022; Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care, 2024). This includes cis-gendered women, transgender men who did not undergo a mastectomy, transgender women who have been under hormonotherapy for at least 5 years, Two-Spirit people and non-binary people in that age group. However, this article focuses on screening at a whole-population level, and as such, screening among the LGBTQIA2+ community has not been specifically investigated. The challenges specific to this community deserve a specific analysis, which is beyond the scope of this article. Asymptomatic women insured by the Quebec government and between the ages of 50–74 years old receive a mailed invitation to undergo mammography screening every two to three years (Quebec Government, 2020a, 2020b). In Ontario, mammograms are recommended every two years for women between the ages of, at the time of the study, 50–74 years old, as well as every year for people aged 30–69 years old falling under the high-risk screening program (Cancer Care Ontario, 2015). Women aged 40–49 were ineligible during this study, but eligibility was extended in Ontario to include this age group as of October 2024. Eligible women also receive a letter in the mail from the Ontario Breast Screening Program (OBSP). In Ontario only, women can also self-refer every 11 months to increase mammogram frequency. As it is the case in Quebec, women need a requisition to continue screening after 75 years old.

Methodology

For the purpose of this study, a quantitative document analysis was conducted. This method consists of a thorough and systematic analysis of multiple documents (Dalglish et al., 2020). The term ‘document’ is to be understood in its broadest sense, including printed documents (leaflets, posters, notes, calendars, etc.) as well as virtual documents, such as web pages (Bowen, 2009). A quantitative document analysis seemed to be the best approach to evaluate potential associations among screening approaches, inclusion of women and promotion of autonomy.

By including documents easily accessible for women from the public, i.e., mostly on the Internet, this method is useful when dealing with a lot of grey literature. The READ method developed for health policy and public health documents (ready materials, extract data, analyse data, distil) was followed (Dalglish et al., 2020). A reading grid (Bowen, 2009; Manganello and Blake, 2010; Morgan, 2022) was developed by the research team to identify the primary and secondary outcome measures in mutually exclusive categories (i.e. present/absent/not reported) (Appendix A). A primary version of 19-items was piloted by the research team and reduced to 16-items to focus on the primary and secondary outcomes described below.

Document identification strategy

Documents from Quebec, Ontario and Canada (at a national level only) were included in the document analysis. A Google search using the keywords ‘breast cancer screening’ and ‘screening mammography’, both in French and in English, was conducted to find information sources most likely available to women. Only sources directly available on the Internet via the above-mentioned search engine were included, which means that documents only available on social media or on databases, i.e. not available to every woman, were not included. Moreover, the age group targeted by breast cancer screening is not as active on social media, such as Instagram and Twitter, compared to other generations, but does search for information on health programs using web browsers (Cangelosi et al., 2022), which prompted the decision to focus on documents available via Google directly. Inclusion criteria were: documents from Ontario, Quebec or Canada (accessible to Quebec and Ontario citizens); published in French and/or English; available directly on the Internet; published between April 1999 (date of publication of the first Quebec guideline on breast cancer screening) and November 2023. The target audience of the documents was women directly. Documents targeting healthcare professionals or available only with access to a database or with an account (e.g. social media) were not included. Documents were first separated by authorship (government, public health agency, foundation, professional association, etc.) and then by type of document (web page, pamphlet, information document, etc.).

To ensure minimal selection bias, a 12-step structured approach to documenting a search strategy was followed (Kable et al., 2012). As such, research started with a purpose statement to ensure the terms of the research are clear (1). The search was limited to the Google search engine (2) and clear limits (3) and inclusion and exclusion criteria (4) were identified. The terms used to search for the included documents were listed (5) and documented the search process as shown in Figure 1(6). Each document was then assessed for relevance and quality using Kridel’s criteria (7–10) and documented to avoid duplicates (8). The total number of included and excluded documents is presented in Figure 1 (9). No critical literature review was included since this is not the purpose of this article (11), but the full reference list is available for accuracy purposes (12).

Outcome Measures

Primary outcome measures

Risks and benefits: The presentation of risks and benefits was analysed and collected in the reading grid. Documents presenting at least one potential risk of breast cancer screening (e.g. overdiagnosis, radiation, false positive) or at least one potential benefit (e.g. reassurance, expanded lifespan, etc.) were identified as presenting risks or benefits of screening, respectively.

Approaches to screening: Each document was assigned one of three approaches (SDM, IDM or recommendation-based approach) based on the vocabulary used. Documents that encouraged women to obtain the information needed to make an informed decision, but did not explicitly encourage a conversation with a clinician based on values and preferences, were included in the IDM category. Documents encouraging women to have a conversation with a clinician, highlighting preferences and values in making a health decision, were included in the SDM category. Documents stating a recommendation to follow, regardless of women’s preferences or desire to gain more knowledge on screening, were included in the recommendation-based category (Murray et al., 2007), meaning that current recommendations on screening were presented as what should be followed regardless of preference, values or conversations with a clinician, representing the more paternalistic approach of the three. Documents that did not fit in any categories were identified as non-applicable, like documents with images only.

Secondary outcome measures

Position on screening: Documents including explicit statements encouraging women not to get screened were placed in the ‘against screening’ category while those containing the opposite statement were placed in the ‘for screening’ category. Documents containing neither statement but that encouraged the reader to decide for herself based on the information shared were placed in the ‘raising awareness’ category.

Illustrations: Illustrations were analysed in each document, first to determine if the document was illustrated or not. If the document was illustrated, the presence of at least one white woman and/or at least one racialized woman was noted to determine the representativity of the illustrations. The age of the pictured women was estimated: pictures depicting women aged below 50 were identified in the ‘underage’ category while pictures showing women that were 50 to 75 years old were identified in the ‘age-appropriate for screening’ category.

Confusion between diagnostic and screening tests: documents with any of the following were identified as confusing diagnosis and screening: symptomatic women, women too young to be included in screening, survivors of breast cancer without screening (McGannon et al., 2016). Documents with illustrations of women younger than 50 were therefore also included in this category.

Moralizing statements: Moralizing statements, often referred to as normative recruitment, depict screening as a duty (Hofmann, 2020) and indicate a moral obligation or a morally good way forward, where a woman who chooses not to participate in screening could be seen as not placing sufficient value on her health (Brotzman et al., 2022). Documents presenting at least one moralizing statement were identified as moralizing.

Informed consent: Documents using the keywords ‘informed consent’, ‘free and informed consent’ or inviting women to ask questions and gain information before deciding were included in this category.

Inclusion of women: Inclusion of women was assessed both in their role as experts and as members of the public. Expert participation was identified in proportion of the scientific committee (0 to 25 per cent, 26 to 50 per cent, 51 to 75 per cent or 76 to 100 per cent). Public participation was identified as long as one woman from the public participated in the design. Documents for which this information was not publicly available were identified as non-applicable.

Literacy score: Literacy scores were determined using the FKGL score. Documents with a score under 8 were considered accessible (equivalent to being accessible to people in the 8th grade). Documents scoring 8 and above were considered inaccessible in terms of literacy, following the recommendation of many health organizations to aim at a literacy level between 6th and 8th grade (Walters and Hamrell, 2008; Williamson and Martin, 2010; Jindal and MacDermid, 2017). The FKGL score was chosen as it provides a clearer reference point for the general population, as well as an objective measure.

Data Extraction and Analysis

AL identified the main contents according to the reading grid developed. Data was computed in an Excel database to obtain descriptive statistics; exemplary quotes were identified to illustrate and define each coding category. Independent coding was performed on 10 per cent of documents by a research assistant to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the data; a kappa score was calculated to assess interrater reliability.

The FKGL score was assessed using the following formula: [0.39 × average length of sentences + 11.8 × average number of syllables per word − 15.59]. All included documents were manually verified by AL to ensure the validity of the provided score using 30 sentences selected in each document (first and last 10 sentences, as well as 10 sentences in the middle) for documents of at least 30 sentences. Documents of less than 30 sentences were analysed in full. Descriptive analyses were conducted using Excel (Microsoft Inc). Measures of central tendency (mean and median) were calculated for FKGL scores. Chi-square tests (χ2) were conducted using SPSS (v. 25, IBM Inc.) to compare documents advocating for SDM or IDM to recommendation-based approaches and to compare documents’ contents whether women from the public were included in document design or not.

Document Appraisal

After all corresponding documents were included, a second round of exclusion was conducted to ensure rigorous results: authenticity, credibility, representativeness and meaning (Kridel, 2015). These four criteria guided the selection of the documents to ensure that the documents (i) are genuine (authenticity), i.e. that the authorship was traceable and trustworthy; (ii) do not present faulty information or directly contradict scientific evidence (credibility); (iii) are similar and comparable to other documents on the same topic to ensure a form of coherency between the analyzed documents (representativeness); and (iv) that the content of the document is easy to understand and meaningful in regard to the context in which it was written, i.e. that the content is appropriate for its target audience and interpretable (meaning) (Morgan, 2022).

Results

Characteristics of Included Documents

Figure 1 shows the flow diagram for the selection of documents, including reasons for exclusion. Most documents were excluded based on having the wrong target audience or avoiding redundancy. Fifty-one documents were reviewed in full and included in the document analysis. The reading grid and data extraction process were found to have a high interrater reliability (κ = 0.98).

Methodological Criteria

Kridel’s criteria in this analysis served as a solid theoretical and methodological framework to exclude documents. The authenticity criteria led to the exclusion of documents with unclear or impossible-to-confirm authorship. The credibility criteria excluded documents such as blog posts or forums where the information presented was neither credible nor validated and would have affected the analysis of the presentation of risks and benefits.

The representativeness criteria excluded documents that were too different from the other documents, e.g. documents discussing issues outside the scope of the study. Documents that did not target women, were not meaningful or easily understandable were excluded based on meaning. Documents were from Ontario (20 per cent), Quebec (31 per cent) and Canada (49 per cent). Less than half (39 per cent) were available in both English and French. The majority of documents were from health authorities or hospitals (22 per cent) and foundations (20 per cent). Most documents were web pages (37 per cent) or information documents (31 per cent), i.e. posters, one-pagers, etc. Most (80 per cent) did not cite references to the facts or statistics included (Table 2).

Primary Outcomes

Risks and benefits: Potential benefits and risks were explained in 90 per cent and 37 per cent of the documents, respectively (Table 3). Benefits were most often described in terms of increased life expectancy due to early detection: ‘It’s still impossible to prevent breast cancer, but screening can detect it at an early stage. This increases the chances of a cure’ (Quebec Government, 2022). Regarding risks, documents focused on the risk of additional investigations, sometimes false positives and less often overdiagnosis: ‘Some breast cancers that appear on a mammogram may never progress to the point where a woman has symptoms during her lifetime. Therefore, some women may have surgery or treatment for a breast cancer that would never have been life-threatening’ (Ontario Government, no date-b). More than half (55 per cent) presented potential benefits exclusively, while 35 per cent included both risks and benefits. No document exposed risks exclusively.

Approach to screening: Table 3 presents documents’ general approaches to screening. Recommendation-based was the approach most often chosen (57 per cent). Documents from Canada presented mostly IDM (40 per cent) and a recommendation-based approach (44 per cent). Most documents from Ontario presented a recommendation-based approach (90 per cent), with the OBSCP stating that ‘breast cancer can be impossible to see or feel […]. That’s why regular breast cancer screening at the right time is so important’ (Ontario Government, no date-a). Documents from Quebec were also focused on a recommendation-based approach, although government documents adopted SDM for the most part. For example, the PQDCS states that ‘the decision whether or not to undergo mammography as part of the PQDCS is up to you! This decision depends on what’s important and your values’ (Quebec Government, 2022).

However, there was a greater proportion of documents that presented the risks of screening among those that encouraged IDM or SDM in comparison to those that made a recommendation to screen (Table 4; 88 per cent vs. 17 per cent, P < 0.001, χ2 = 20.864); documents that encouraged IDM or SDM also used moralizing statements less frequently than those that recommended screening (6 per cent vs. 45 per cent, P = 0.007, χ2 = 7.160). Examples of moralizing statements found in the analyzed documents are ‘take control of your breast health with early detection’ (Quebec Breast Cancer Foundation, no date-a, no date-b, no date-c) or‘I prioritize myself’ (CIUSSS Estrie - CHUS, 2015).

Secondary Outcomes

Position on screening: Table 3 presents documents’ positions on breast cancer screening. No document positioned itself against screening. About one-third (37 per cent) of documents were neutral and raised awareness on screening, while 63 per cent included explicit statements to get screened, such as ‘evidence clearly indicates that women between the ages of 50 and 69 should have a mammogram every two years’ (Public Health Agency of Canada, 2009) or ‘just book it, breast cancer screening can save your life’ (Niagara Health, no date). All documents from Ontario included explicit statements to get screened.

Illustrations: Less than half (37 per cent) of the documents were illustrated (Figure 2). Of the 19 illustrated documents, 89 per cent showed at least one picture of a white woman, while 58 per cent showed at least one picture of a non-white woman. Over a third of the documents (37 per cent) pictured at least one woman aged less than 50 years old.

Discussion

Tensions Between IDM and Practice: Is SDM Possible?

This study examined how publicly available breast cancer screening documents in Quebec and Ontario present information about benefits and harms, and how they frame decision-making for women invited to participate. The findings reveal a marked asymmetry in these materials, in which potential benefits are emphasized more consistently than potential risks. In parallel, decision-making is predominantly framed as an individual responsibility with limited acknowledgment of the different factors that shape how information is received and interpreted.

Screening decisions have historically been guided by an individualistic conception of autonomy, emphasizing informed consent and voluntary choice, even as screening itself operates at a population-level (Nijsingh, 2007; Carter, 2016; Dobrow et al., 2018). Promoting individual autonomy in this context is ethically complex because the information intended to support such decisions is centrally produced and communicated to large populations. Introducing SDM, which requires integrating individual preferences with standardized information, therefore places substantial transparency demands on document producers. The predominance of benefit-focused messaging in this study suggests that autonomy, when operationalized solely through information provision, may be insufficient to support ethical screening decisions.

Meanwhile, the findings indicate that greater transparency is achievable. Documents that adopted IDM or SDM approaches were significantly more likely to include information on risks and less likely to rely on moralizing language. While relational autonomy does not resolve all ethical and practical challenges inherent to SDM, it provides a more ethically attentive framework for identifying imbalances in how information is produced, framed and communicated.

Nevertheless, moralizing statements remain prevalent. A previous study of Ontario women found that a majority of screening participants described non-participants as irresponsible (Abelson et al., 2018). In the present study, Ontario documents were particularly directive, with all including explicit calls to screen and most adopting a recommendation-based approach. When risks are minimized or omitted in such contexts, individuals may feel pressured into participation, especially if non-participation is framed as irresponsible. Autonomy, under these conditions, becomes conditional, dependent not only on access to information, but on freedom from normative pressure, reflecting a more paternalistic orientation in documentation available on screening.

Provincial Policy Differences and Implementations Challenges

The ethical tension between autonomy and directive messaging is further complicated by provincial differences in approaches to screening. Since 2008, Quebec has formally encouraged clinicians to promote SDM in screening discussions (Ministry of Health and Social Services, 2014), whereas Ontario’s policy explicitly recommends screening to every eligible woman (Ontario Government, no date-b). Despite Quebec’s more autonomy-oriented policy, very few documents in this study promoted SDM. This gap between policy and practice reflects the challenges of implementing SDM in population-level communication, particularly when documents are designed to function as standalone resources without clinician involvement.

This disconnect between what the scientific and ethical literature identifies as best practice and the general population’s needs, expectations and capacities is well documented (Amélie et al., 2022). While clinicians may view SDM as an ideal means of supporting autonomy, empirical studies suggest that many women struggle to understand the concept of SDM and might even express openness to a certain degree of paternalism. As a result, policymakers face a tension between promoting deliberative ideals and designing materials that are accessible, comprehensible and usable in real-world contexts.

Even when clinicians endorse SDM in theory, practical barriers, such as time constraints, discomfort with uncertainty or lack of training, can hinder its implementation. Although many clinicians endorse SDM in principle, a non-negligible proportion continue to practice in a more paternalistic manner (Murray et al., 2007). Expecting publicly available documents to compensate for these limitations by placing the full burden of information interpretation on women risks undermining the very autonomy that SDM is intended to protect.

Conflicting Evidence and Information Gaps

A core challenge to both IDM and SDM in breast cancer screening is the presence of ongoing scientific uncertainty and conflicting evidence, particularly regarding overdiagnosis (Barratt and Glasziou, 2012; Esserman, 2012). The persistence of conflicted evidence, sometimes referred to as the ‘mammography wars’, likely impact the quality and accuracy of the information presented in public-facing screening documents (Pace and Keating, 2014; Løberg et al., 2015).

This imbalance is further shaped by clinicians’ ethical commitments, such as the imperative to ‘do no harm,’ which may be interpreted as prioritizing early detection and the life-saving potential of screening (Hoffmann and Del Mar, 2017). This positive framing can lead clinicians to underestimate that screening itself carries risk. As a result, screening is presented not as a choice, but as an expectation, reflecting the broader cultural and institutional tendency to treat participation as normative. This undermines informed consent and shifts the ethical stance from SDM to compliance (Elton, 2020).

In this study, most documents either did not provide sources or only provided untraceable, incomplete evidence. For women seeking to understand the evidence underlying screening recommendations, this lack of transparency introduces an additional burden: they must independently seek out information that should have been made accessible. Autonomy also requires that individuals be able to interpret and engage with information (Lewis, 2019). Without this, the ethical promise of IDM or SDM remains unfulfilled.

Scripts and Long-term Effects

Beyond moralizing statements, other scripts, such as confusing diagnosis with screening or survivor stories, continue to shape perceptions of breast cancer screening. For instance, survivor testimonies often imply that early detection through screening is the only reason for survival, obscuring the distinction between asymptomatic screening and symptomatic diagnosis. These narratives can also imply that adverse outcomes are the result of insufficient self-care, subtly reinforcing blame and pressure (McQueen et al., 2011).

Visual representations in screening materials further contribute to these scripts. In Quebec, many documents featured images of young, white women, suggesting that screening is primarily relevant to younger or less diverse populations (McWhirter et al., 2012; Aguirre-Camacho et al., 2025). Such illustrations may unintentionally promote early screening among populations for whom it is not recommended, while also reinforcing a narrow and exclusionary view of who screening is for.

The ethical implications of screening extend beyond the initial decision to participate investigated here. Psychological distress following false-positive results can persist for years (Brodersen and Siersma, 2013) and influence future screening behavior, as reflected in lower retention rates among women who experience false positives (Ministry of Health and Social Services, 2014). Yet such long-term consequences were not addressed in the documents analyzed. A relational approach to autonomy would require acknowledging these experiences and supporting women, rather than assuming that participation is a one-time, static choice (Shih et al., 2018).

Public Engagement

The limited explicit reference to informed consent and the high literacy demands of the materials further complicate the autonomy landscape. When individuals cannot access or understand information, their ability to make informed choices is compromised. Although women were well-represented among expert authors, their inclusion as members of the public was rare, suggesting limited engagement with the public and with diverse backgrounds in the development of these materials.

This lack of representation is ethically significant. Persistent disparities in screening participation among women from diverse ethnic backgrounds reflect deeper issues of representation and inclusion (McLeod et al., 2000; Marlow et al., 2015; Bhargava et al., 2018). A single, homogeneous representation is insufficient, and risks reinforcing existing inequities by failing to resonate with or address the needs of diverse populations.

Emerging initiatives, such as individualized risk-based screening programs and community-rooted campaigns, offer promising alternatives (Lessard, 2017; Étude PERSPECTIVE, 2023). By tailoring information to personal and contextual factors and engaging trusted community members, such approaches demonstrate how autonomy can be relationally supported (Stoljar, 2011). However, these initiatives remain exceptions rather than the norm.

Finally, health literacy remains a major structural barrier to autonomy. In Quebec and in Canada, approximately 50 per cent of the population is considered functionally illiterate (Fondation pour l’alphabétisation, 2022). Yet only a small proportion of documents were accessible at lower reading levels. Achieving informed consent requires attention not only to simplifying language but also to diversifying formats, including visual aids and multimedia resources, to ensure that information is genuinely accessible (Lazris and Rifkin, 2016).

Building Ethical Conditions for Autonomous Screening Decisions

Taken together, these findings challenge the assumption that autonomy in breast cancer screening can be fulfilled through information alone. While IDM emphasizes disclosure and transparency, this study shows that autonomy also depends on the conditions under which information is received and interpreted (Entwistle et al., 2008). Without attention to relational factors, such as clinician support, cultural representation and literacy, information alone cannot ensure autonomous decision-making.

These findings also point toward an important conceptual clarification that emerges from this study: the need to distinguish between autonomy and agency. The relational factors identified in this analysis may be more accurately understood as shaping women’s agency, understood as their practical capacity to act within specific social and institutional contexts (Hersch et al., 2011; Bolt et al., 2022), rather than autonomy as a normative ideal. While autonomy represents an ethical ideal, agency captures how decisions are actually made within constrained environments. Breast cancer screening materials may therefore influence not only what women know but also how and whether they can act on that knowledge (Dodds, 2000).

Recognizing this distinction has both theoretical and practical implications. Conceptually, it invites a reframing of screening decision-making models around agency rather than autonomy alone. Practically, it suggests that communication tools should be evaluated not only for information completeness but for how they shape the conditions under which women can deliberate and act. A commitment to ethical and inclusive communication, therefore, requires transparent presentation of benefits and harms, acknowledgment of uncertainty and value trade-offs, avoidance of moralizing language that frames participation, and representation of diverse values and experiences. Evidence from decision-aid research suggests that such approaches can support knowledge and preference-concordant choices without undermining participation (Stacey et al., 2024).

Strengths and Limitations

This document analysis provides a comprehensive exploration of factors influencing information presentation and decision-making approaches promoted in publicly available documents on breast cancer screening in Canada. Attention to several important outcomes includes novel findings, such as attention paid to the involvement of women both as experts and non-experts. The systematicity of the analyses and clarity of concepts’ definitions allowed for excellent interrater reliability.

A primary concern with document analyses is the risk of selection bias. Since meaningful, credible and representative documentation on screening appeared to be consensual within the studied provinces, the number of documents included was deemed sufficient (Kridel, 2015; Dalglish et al., 2020).

It is worth noting that documents offering more detailed information on potential risks present a different interpretation of screening effectiveness compared to those that present little to no discussion of risks. Nonetheless, there is a consistent approach to both design and content across the documents. Since the documents reviewed were issued for the most part by the same political entities, it is unsurprising that none of them explicitly questioned or opposed breast cancer screening. A similar search in a medical database would have offered a more nuanced view on screening, but these documents would not be as readily available to the general population.

Using the FKGL score, the analysis produced clear and concise results regarding the literacy accessibility of documents. Given the frequent use of numerical data, graphs and statistics in the documents, it may have been valuable to assess the numeracy level as well. Accessibility, both in terms of literacy and numeracy, remains fundamental to enabling informed consent.

Finally, documents including women from the public represented a very small sample (n = 5). Consequently, the significance of the findings relating to this subgroup is hypothetical but can inform next steps for future research.

Conclusion

Publicly available documents on breast cancer screening in Quebec, Ontario and Canada emphasize the potential benefits of screening while offering limited discussion on risks. Rather than supporting IDM or SDM, these documents often present screening as a default. In the context of limited primary care access and automated mailed invitations, such documents play an increasingly central role in shaping women’s understanding and choices. While several of the proposed recommendations align with strengthening informed consent, they are equally relevant to the ethical implementation of shared decision-making. Future research should explore how these practices affect women’s agency and engagement with screening information, as well as the impact of involving women, both as experts and participants, in the design of public-facing documents.
