Should weight loss be the first-line treatment for obstructive sleep apnoea? A pro–con debate
Authors: Sanjay R. Patel, Silke Ryan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a common disorder associated with substantial morbidity. Although obesity is an important pathogenetic factor in OSA, the incorporation of weight loss into routine OSA care is currently limited. The development of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists has revolutionised obesity management and raises questions about the role of weight loss in OSA care. A debate was held at the 8th Sleep and Breathing Conference in April 2025 on whether weight loss should be first-line treatment for patients with OSA. Arguments in favour include the large and growing proportion of OSA attributable to excess weight, the effectiveness of modern weight-loss therapies in producing significant weight loss and the extent to which weight loss produces effects on clinical outcomes beyond OSA, such as the positive impact on cardiovascular and metabolic disease incidence and control. Conversely, arguments against include the fact that not all patients with OSA have obesity, the limited impact of weight loss on OSA resolution and the considerable data gap with obesity strategies in OSA including the lack of direct comparisons with CPAP on patient-centred outcomes and the impact on long-term health benefits in OSA patients specifically. Clearly, while more research is needed and treatment decisions should be personalised, clinicians need to gain competency with the use of novel weight-loss treatments as an essential part of OSA care.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) conveys a substantial global public health burden, mainly due to its high, steadily rising prevalence and its association with cardiometabolic diseases leading to considerable mortality, morbidity, healthcare utilisation and cost burden [1–3]. For decades, treatment of the condition has been largely dependent on continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) [4]. CPAP effectively abolishes the sleep-disordered breathing of OSA, improves daytime symptoms and quality of life, and it has transformed the lives of many OSA patients [5]. However, its benefit on various cardiometabolic outcomes remains uncertain and, thus, its indication for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients with OSA remains questionable at least. Furthermore, CPAP therapy is cumbersome and long-term adherence rates are only moderately satisfactory [6, 7]. In addition, the treatment does not address the root causes of OSA and therefore, has to be continued on a long-term basis to remain effective. Consequently, alternative treatment strategies are urgently required.

OSA is intimately linked with overweight/obesity with 60–70% of OSA subjects having this shared comorbidity [8]. Obesity contributes to the pathogenesis of OSA in multiple ways [9, 10]. It narrows the cross-sectional area of the upper airway (UA) by increased fat deposition in the pharyngeal walls and possibly through external compression from superficially located fat masses [11, 12]. It also decreases lung volumes, particularly functional residual capacity, with subsequent reduction in longitudinal traction of the pharynx promoting UA collapsibility [13] and greater ventilatory instability [14]. In addition, it contributes to an increased ventilatory feedback loop mediated by central leptin resistance [15] (figure 1). Despite the multiple effects of obesity on OSA pathogenesis, the potential impact of weight-loss interventions as treatment for OSA has been, until recently, poorly investigated. Studies evaluating lifestyle modifications, weight-loss drugs or bariatric surgeries have been impacted by several limitations, such as nonrandomised design, small sample sizes or short treatment durations, and have predominantly been performed in individuals who were intolerant or unwilling to use CPAP. For patients with OSA and comorbid obesity, weight loss is effective in improving the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI); however, even with bariatric surgery, the most effective option to date, a >50% reduction in AHI or complete remission of OSA is only achieved in two-thirds of cases at best [16, 17]. However, despite the considerable benefits of bariatric surgery, it is not feasible or scalable to the large proportion of patients with OSA. Glucagon-like-peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have revolutionised the role of weight-loss drugs in obesity management, due to the magnitude and sustainability of body mass index (BMI) reduction they achieve, leading to a critical paradigm shift in the understanding of obesity as a holistic disease. The proposed mechanisms of actions of GLP-1 agonists include delayed gastric emptying, increased satiety and appetite suppression [18]. In addition, they may directly target adipose tissue through the attenuation of inflammation and “browning”, with downstream improvements in metabolic function [19]. The most effective agent in this class on the market so far is tirzepatide, a dual gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 agonist, leading to a mean weight loss of 21% at maximal dose over 72 weeks [20]. Its efficacy in OSA was investigated in the SURMOUNT-OSA trials [21]. Subjects with moderate to severe disease (AHI >15 events·h−1) and obesity were randomised to lifestyle modification plus either tirzepatide at maximal tolerated dosage or placebo over 52 weeks. The trials were performed in two groups, as follows: group 1 (n=234) included those naïve to CPAP and group 2 (n=235) patients currently on CPAP. Tirzepatide led to a robust reduction in AHI by 20 and 24 events·h−1, or 48 and 56%, respectively, with associated improvements in several patient-related outcomes, as well as reductions in blood pressure and C-reactive protein concentrations.

The results of SURMOUNT-OSA, together with the rapidly expanding use of GLP-1 agonists, have triggered considerable optimism among clinicians and patients that a potentially curative treatment for many OSA sufferers may have been identified. However, there are also significant concerns and gaps in our knowledge, warranting a rather cautious approach in the appraisal of weight loss as a primary OSA treatment. The pros and cons of this subject were discussed in an exciting and stimulating debate at the 8th Sleep and Breathing Conference in Antwerp in April 2025.

Pro: weight loss should be the first-line treatment for OSA (S.R. Patel)

Despite the excitement generated by the proposed framing of OSA as a disease caused by the interaction of multiple endotypes, it is important to recognise that the overwhelming majority of OSA cases result from a single pathway, namely a collapsible UA. People with an airway critical pressure (Pcrit) greater than −2 cm of water universally have OSA and account for over 80% of patients [22]. While craniofacial anatomy can contribute to an elevated Pcrit, excess weight plays a primary role in driving UA collapsibility at a population level.

In 2003, nearly 60% of all OSA cases in the United States were attributable to excess weight, meaning successful treatment of overweight and obesity would eliminate the majority of OSA cases [23]. In the subsequent two decades, the ongoing overweight/obesity pandemic has further increased the proportion of OSA due to excess weight.

Beyond excess weight being the most important cause of an elevated AHI, obesity-related respiratory events are also more pathological. The severity of oxygen desaturation caused by a respiratory event increases with greater levels of adiposity, leading to worsening severity of intermittent hypoxia for the same AHI [24]. Cluster analyses demonstrate that patients with an OSA–obesity subtype characterised by high airway collapsibility also have greater risk of OSA consequences, ranging from daytime sleepiness to cardiovascular disease [25].

Critics will point out that weight-loss therapies rarely normalise the AHI in patients with moderate to severe OSA and this is true. While weight-loss interventions frequently normalise the AHI in patients with mild OSA, in those with more severe OSA, despite a 50% or greater reduction, the AHI rarely normalises [26, 27]. However, even if not cured, weight loss can improve the success rates of secondary treatment [28]. In addition, it should be recognised that the lack of AHI normalisation may not matter. Change in the AHI has never been validated as a predictor of clinically meaningful outcomes. Particularly in the setting of weight loss, change in the AHI is an unreliable measure of clinical benefit. One reason for this is that weight loss not only improves outcomes via improvements in OSA, but it also improves these outcomes through OSA-independent pathways (figure 2). For example, obesity contributes to daytime sleepiness not only via causing OSA, but also by causing systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [29, 30]. Similarly, beyond OSA, overweight and obesity contribute to a host of comorbidities, including low back pain and osteoarthritis, gastro-oesophageal reflux, asthma and depression, all of which can contribute to sleep disruption and unrefreshing sleep. Conversely, weight loss, reduced caloric intake and regular exercise can all improve sleep quality and daytime energy levels even in those without OSA, providing added benefits of weight-loss treatments that are not fully captured by changes in the AHI. Similarly, weight-loss interventions, including dietary modifications and exercise, reduce blood pressure, prevent diabetes and lower the risks of both cardiovascular events and dementia independent of any OSA-mediated effect [31–33].

The American Thoracic Society published a clinical practice guideline in 2018 recommending behavioural weight loss, combining diet, exercise and counselling, as a component of any OSA treatment plan for patients who are overweight or have obesity [34]. This was based on an evidence-based review of nine randomised clinical trials (RCTs) in patients with OSA, as well as broader evidence in the general population. Two RCTs of behavioural weight loss in OSA have since been published that further strengthen the evidence base. The INTERAPNEA trial randomised men with moderate to severe OSA already on CPAP to a lifestyle intervention or control. At 6 months, the lifestyle intervention produced a net weight loss of 5.7 kg compared with the control group, resulting in large improvements in OSA-related symptoms, including sleepiness, sleep quality, mood and sleep-related quality of life, as well as robust improvements in blood pressure and insulin resistance [35, 36]. In MIMOSA, patients with moderate to severe OSA were randomised to CPAP alone, CPAP plus diet modification or CPAP plus a full lifestyle intervention. At 6 months, the lifestyle intervention group lost 12.6 kg more than the CPAP alone group, which translated to large improvements in daytime sleepiness and insomnia symptoms [37]. At 12 months, there was still a net loss of 11 kg relative to CPAP alone, sufficient to produce large improvements in blood pressure and insulin resistance [38].

Beyond behavioural interventions, we now have more aggressive methods to induce weight loss. Bariatric surgical procedures have become less morbid and so more acceptable to patients. Both gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy are now routinely performed laparoscopically with low peri-operative risk [39]. In patients with OSA, nonrandomised studies indicate that bariatric surgery produces large improvements in daytime sleepiness [40]. Furthermore, in the general population, the results of long-term cohort studies suggest that bariatric procedures reduce the risk of myocardial infarction and cardiovascular mortality by over 50% [41]. Most recently, GLP-1 agonists have emerged as an effective weight-loss option. The SURMOUNT-OSA results have electrified the sleep community regarding the feasibility of utilising weight loss as a large-scale treatment strategy for OSA [21].

Overall, weight-loss interventions produce greater improvements in several clinical outcomes important to patients with OSA compared to traditional treatments such as CPAP. For example, while CPAP improves the Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS) score by 2.4 points, behavioural weight loss improved ESS scores by 4.7–5.8 points in RCTs [35, 37, 42]. Tirzepatide improves the ESS score by an additional 0.9–1.4 points beyond lifestyle alone, while bariatric surgery produces a 5.5-point reduction in ESS score [40, 43].

Similarly, the effects of weight loss on blood pressure outstrip the measly reductions of 1 mmHg systolic and 2 mmHg diastolic observed with CPAP [42]. Relative to control, behavioural weight loss produced reductions of 16 mmHg systolic/9 mmHg diastolic in INTERAPNEA and 3 mmHg systolic and 2 mmHg diastolic in MIMOSA, while tirzepatide provided additional reductions of 6 mmHg systolic and 2 mmHg diastolic on top of lifestyle effects in SURMOUNT-OSA [21, 35, 38].

Weight loss also prevents and reverses diabetes, which other OSA therapies such as CPAP do not [42]. RCTs have demonstrated that tirzepatide reduces the risk of incident diabetes by a stunning 93%, while bariatric surgery leads to diabetes remission in 37% of patients (compared to 3% in controls) at 3 years [44, 45]. Similarly, RCTs have demonstrated that the GLP-1 agonist, semaglutide, reduces cardiovascular event risk by 20% as compared to results from the SAVE trial, where CPAP increased cardiovascular risk by 10% [46, 47].

In summary, RCTs have consistently demonstrated that weight-loss interventions reduce OSA symptoms and consequences and the effects are larger than produced with CPAP. If the goal of treating OSA is to treat the patient and improve their health, rather than worrying about improving the polysomnogram, it is obvious that weight loss should be the first-line treatment for OSA. If other treatments are to be considered, they should be rigorously tested as adjunctive therapies and shown to provide additional clinical benefit beyond weight loss.

Con: weight loss should not be the first-line treatment for OSA (S. Ryan)

Over decades, our field has largely failed to address the detrimental effects of obesity on OSA pathophysiology. Certainly, recent studies, particularly those on GLP-1-based pharmacotherapies, have yielded promising results and stimulated a change in this perspective. However, is the accumulated evidence sufficient and are we ready to celebrate weight-loss-targeting therapies as the “holy grail” in the management of OSA? The answer to these questions is clearly “no” and several arguments support this position.

Firstly, it is important to acknowledge that a substantial proportion of OSA patients do not have obesity, defined as a BMI of ≥30 kg·m−2. In clinical cohorts, this accounts for approximately 40–50% of patients, while in community populations the proportion is less than 50%, reflecting a substantial referral bias [8, 48–51]. OSA is primarily a UA disease and although obesity is a significant contributing factor for UA collapse, the pathophysiology of OSA is not dependent on it. Notwithstanding the potentially beneficial effects of weight-loss and lifestyle modifications for general health in people without obesity, a significant impact on OSA severity is unlikely and in many countries those individuals would not qualify for surgical or pharmacological weight-loss options.

Secondly, improvements in the AHI with weight loss are not linear and most strategies, including bariatric surgery as the most effective intervention, do not result in complete remission [16, 17]. This was also noted in SURMOUNT-OSA, where half of the subjects on tirzepatide were left with presumed clinically relevant disease, i.e. an AHI >15 events·h−1 or an AHI of 5–15 events·h−1 in conjunction with an ESS score ≥10 points, at the end of the trial [21].

Thirdly, all studies investigating the effect of weight loss in OSA have focused on improvement of the AHI as the primary outcome parameter. However, the AHI has increasingly been questioned as a suitable surrogate marker for clinical consequences. In particular, it correlates poorly with patient-centred outcomes. Improvements in factors such as quality of life, daytime sleepiness, mood and cognitive functioning matter most to those affected by OSA, yet only a minority of studies have included such measures, with many only commenting on changes in the ESS [16]. The authors of the SURMOUNT-OSA trial reported improvements in sleep quality and daytime sleepiness, assessed via the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [21], in both groups; however, this tool is rarely used in the OSA field. Moreover, in a recently published, more detailed analysis of patient-related outcomes from SURMOUNT-OSA, no significant improvement in the Functional Outcome of Sleep Questionnaire total score was observed at week 52 and a significant reduction in the ESS was noted only in CPAP-naïve participants (group 1) [43]. Importantly, comparative studies against CPAP are also lacking, despite CPAP remaining the benchmark treatment for symptomatic OSA, with substantial benefits in patient-related outcomes, even in those with mild disease [42, 52].

Another important unanswered question relates to the impact of weight loss on OSA-associated cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, which is central in the ongoing critical debate on the management of those who display no or only little symptoms. The position of CPAP in this regard remains uncertain, despite the accumulation of a vast number of bench and clinical studies over four decades. Its benefit in the secondary prevention of major cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events has particularly been called into question by the negative results of three RCTs [47, 53, 54]. However, as extensively discussed, these trials were considerably impacted by numerous significant and inevitable limitations, such as the preclusion of the most symptomatic patients and suboptimal CPAP adherence. Subsequently, data derived from post hoc analyses, large real-world cohorts and, more recently, a detailed meta-analysis incorporating not only RCTs but also well-characterised cohorts have supported favourable outcomes with CPAP therapy [48, 55–57]. Furthermore, CPAP has been consistently shown to improve endothelial function and blood pressure [58–60]. Although the latter was also demonstrated with tirzepatide in SURMOUNT-OSA, most notably in CPAP-naïve subjects, a detailed exploration of the role of GLP-1-based pharmacotherapies and other weight-loss strategies on “hard” cardiovascular outcomes is as yet outstanding. Importantly, OSA subjects may have worse cardiovascular outcomes following weight-loss management strategies than matched controls [61]. Therefore, we cannot simply extrapolate results derived from non-OSA populations to those with OSA. Certainly, as a word of caution, we recently reported results from a proof-of-concept randomised controlled study in which improvements in early vascular inflammation, evaluated by 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography, was observed with CPAP but not with GLP-1-mediated weight loss [62]. In addition, in C57BL/6 wild-type mice fed on a high-fat diet, liraglutide failed to improve intermittent hypoxia-mediated insulin resistance despite significant effects on food intake and body weight [63]. Although newer-generation GLP-1 pharmacological agents achieve greater weight loss than liraglutide and thus may have led to a superior outcome, this remains only speculative at this stage.

A further problem, which has been largely ignored in our field, relates to the challenge of maintaining weight loss. All studies re-evaluated OSA parameters following a specific weight-loss intervention but did not investigate the long-term trajectories. Relapse following weight loss is common and after lifestyle interventions approximately 80% of weight lost is expected to be regained over the next 5 years [64, 65]. This is not due to lack of motivation or compliance but is driven by potent biological mechanisms, including metabolic adaptation, i.e. persistence of lower resting metabolic rate, lower energy consumption during weight-bearing activities or increased appetite mediated by increased levels of the hunger hormone ghrelin and reduction of the satiety hormone leptin [65]. Obesity also relapses after discontinuation of anti-obesity medications, including GLP-1-based agents, and, therefore, long-term administration is required [66]. However, there is still a paucity of data on long-term effectiveness, side-effects and adherence, particularly in real-world populations. Furthermore, the high cost, limited availability and lack of reimbursement for these agents in many countries have already led to great disparities in obesity management, with those of lower socioeconomic status being most disadvantaged. This critical point needs to be addressed and incorporated into further evaluations of the role of weight management in OSA and, indeed, many other conditions.

In summary, several arguments exist against the preposition to shift to weight loss as sole treatment of choice for OSA. However, a substantial proportion of those arguments are based on insufficient data and premature advice rather than definitive negative results. For all of us working in the field, it must be our standard and our duty to our patients to seek the truth, to perform appropriate studies, to critically evaluate the results and to make recommendations founded on the evidence. Until then, the jury is still out.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the answer to this debate is likely to lie somewhere in the middle. A summary of the arguments on each side is presented in table 1. Some of the arguments raised by the pro and con sides are quite similar and could be swung either way. Undoubtedly, further extensive research is needed to clarify the role of weight-loss strategies in the management of OSA. Such research should, among other priorities, investigate long-term outcomes and “hard” cardiometabolic end-points and include RCTs comparing obesity-focused interventions to CPAP as the current benchmark treatment. With specific respect to GLP-1-based drugs, information on long-term benefit, safety and adherence in OSA populations is also urgently required.

However, it is without question that targeting obesity in OSA will benefit a large proportion of patients and, with the growing obesity pandemic, the relevance of combating excess weight as a strategy to address OSA is only going to expand. The substantial demand from patients for GLP-1 agonists is a reflection of the need for effective strategies to tackle the burden of obesity. Even with the substantial advances achieved with the current generation of GLP-1 agonists, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, further breakthroughs are just around the corner. These include drugs combining additional effects, including triple hormone (GLP-1, GIP and glucagon) receptor agonists such as retatrutide [67], and combined GLP-1 and amylin receptor agonists such as amycretin [68] or semaglutide/cagrilintide [69]. Other innovations include developing potentially more acceptable formulations of GLP-1 receptor agonists such as monthly injectable maridebart cafraglutide [70] or oral orforglipron [71]. Our field needs to be prepared for this evolution and, thus, there is an urgent need to develop robust platforms to rapidly conduct adequately powered research studies focused on answering key questions about the use of these drugs and weight-loss strategies in general in OSA. At the same time, physicians caring for patients with OSA need to develop greater familiarity and expertise in providing obesity-focused care.

However, it has to be acknowledged that the heterogeneity and complexity of OSA demand holistic, personalised treatment strategies and that there is simply no “one-size-fits-all” treatment approach. It is logical to assume that weight loss will have the greatest impact on OSA severity in phenotypes in which excess weight plays an important role in pathogenesis. Using BMI ≥30 kg·m−2 as the traditional criterium to define obesity, we have already highlighted that a substantial proportion of OSA subjects do not meet this cut-off. Then again, weight-loss strategies may also benefit subjects in the overweight category and, in many countries, indications for GLP-1 analogue-based interventions already include those with a BMI ≥27 kg·m−2 in association with a relevant comorbidity, including OSA, thereby opening up these options to a wider population. Crucially, BMI is an incomplete measure of the adverse impact of excess adiposity and, especially relevant to OSA, it does not capture the amount of excess fat deposition surrounding the UA, a key contributor to UA collapsibility. Measures such as neck circumference may be superior in predicting the benefits of weight loss; however, to date, there is a clear lack of detailed studies on the optimal measure. Management of excess weight should also be considered in combination with other OSA treatment strategies and may also open up a wider variety of modalities once reductions in anthropometric measures and OSA severity are achieved. Notably, the identification of phenotypes that benefit the most from obesity-focused interventions, whether alone or in combination with other treatments, represents only one, albeit a crucially important, avenue of progress, and the debate surrounding weight-loss management in OSA should not blind the field to the need to investigate other treatment modalities.

Concerningly, the Global Burden of Disease study forecasts that the worldwide prevalence of adult overweight and obesity will exceed 50% by 2050 [72] (figure 3). Although the fast-evolving field of pharmacological weight-loss therapies generates optimism that this figure will be attenuated, this remains speculative at this stage, particularly as medical treatments may be preferred by many people over preventative strategies, including lifestyle modifications such as a healthy diet and regular exercise [73]. In fact, the World Health Organization has recently issued conditional recommendations that GLP-1 agonists be used in combination with lifestyle modification for the long-term treatment of obesity [74]. Regardless of those considerations, there is simply no treatment, whether pharmacological, surgical or lifestyle-based, that could mitigate the burden of obesity on such massive global scale [75]. The focus has to shift from reactive treatment to proactive comprehensive prevention measures. Primary prevention, i.e. targeting the root causes of obesity through healthy dietary and physically active lifestyles, and high-quality research to define effective preventive interventions have to be the top priority of national governments and other public health institutions. Broad multistakeholder collaborations, including medical societies as key middle actors, are essential to advocate for these priorities and to engage key policymakers [76, 77]. Structured and holistic prevention strategies will not only reduce the direct burden on people living with obesity and related conditions, but will also reduce economic costs and strengthen national health systems [78–80]. The high costs, limited accessibility and lack of reimbursement for GLP-1-based drugs and bariatric surgery have already led to substantial disparities, disproportionately affecting individuals of lower socioeconomic status, who are already at increased risk of obesity. This problem of inequity will further amplify with the increasing prevalence of obesity [81].

The cost and availability of obesity strategies also need to be incorporated into shared patient–physician decision-making when devising personalised treatment plans for OSA. While debates about the role of obesity treatment will persist for some time and areas of uncertainty remain, it is clear that the recent advances in treating obesity are going to forever change the approach to caring for patients with OSA.
