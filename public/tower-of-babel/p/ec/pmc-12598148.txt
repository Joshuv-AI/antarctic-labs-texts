Complete Unilateral Adrenalectomy is Recommended to Prevent Residual Disease in Unilateral Primary Aldosteronism
Authors: Satoshi Nagai, Yuichi Yoshida, Shotaro Miyamoto, Yoshinori Ozeki, Shinro Hata, Hirotaka Shibata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 55-year-old man with hypertension and hypokalemia was diagnosed with primary aldosteronism (PA). Adrenal vein sampling confirmed right-sided lateralization, and computed tomography (CT) revealed a right adrenal tumor. Complete unilateral adrenalectomy was initially planned, but due to surgical challenges associated with a retroperitoneal approach and visceral obesity, he underwent laparoscopic partial adrenalectomy, removing an adrenal tumor. However, histopathological examination showed that the lesion was CYP11B2-negative, and hypertension and elevated aldosterone levels persisted, indicating residual aldosterone-producing tissue. A follow-up CT scan confirmed complete resection of the previously identified tumor, raising concerns about microscopic or multifocal aldosterone-producing nodules. Subsequently, complete unilateral adrenalectomy of the remaining right adrenal gland was performed, resulting in normalization of aldosterone levels and well-controlled blood pressure with nifedipine (80 mg/day). Pathological examination revealed a well-demarcated nodular lesion continuous with adrenal tissue. Immunohistochemical staining for CYP11B2 was positive, confirming a right aldosterone-producing nodule. PA often involves multinodular or microscopic aldosterone-producing lesions, which partial adrenalectomy may not completely remove. This case highlights the limitations of partial adrenalectomy in unilateral PA and supports complete unilateral adrenalectomy as the preferred surgical approach to achieve complete disease resolution.

Introduction

Primary aldosteronism (PA) is the most common cause of secondary hypertension, characterized by renin-independent excessive secretion of aldosterone from the adrenal glands. This hormonal imbalance results in hypertension and hypokalemia due to sodium retention and potassium loss [1, 2]. PA significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular events, including coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and stroke. Notably, patients with PA have a higher cardiovascular risk compared to those with essential hypertension, independent of blood pressure levels [1, 2]. The treatment approach for PA depends on its subtype; bilateral disease is managed medically, whereas lateralizing disease is treated surgically. The standard surgical method is laparoscopic complete unilateral adrenalectomy [1, 2]. Recently, partial adrenalectomy has been proposed to preserve adrenal cortical function, particularly in cases of small, solitary adenomas with a low risk of malignancy [3]. However, it has been reported that PA may involve multinodular or microscopic aldosterone-producing lesions, raising concerns that partial resection might not provide complete disease control [4, 5]. We report a case of PA in which hypertension and elevated aldosterone levels persisted following partial resection of the right adrenal gland. After complete resection of the remaining right adrenal gland, aldosterone levels normalized, blood pressure improved, and the patient showed significant clinical improvement. This case highlights potential limitations of partial adrenalectomy in PA and supports complete unilateral adrenalectomy as the preferred surgical approach for definitive biochemical and clinical remission.

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old man had been treated for hypertension since the age of 50, receiving nifedipine (80 mg/day). Despite antihypertensive therapy, his home systolic blood pressure ranged from 130 to 160 mmHg. In December 2022, he visited his primary care physician for muscle weakness. Laboratory evaluation revealed spontaneous hypokalemia, with a serum potassium level of 3.3 mmol/L [normal reference range (RR): 3.6-4.8 mmol/L], prompting a screening test for PA. Subsequent biochemical testing showed an elevated serum aldosterone (ALD) level of 11.9 ng/dL [International System of Units (SI): 329.8 pmol/L] [RR: 1.0-14.2 ng/dL (SI: 28.8-394.7 pmol/L)], suppressed serum direct renin concentration of 1.2 pg/mL (RR: 3.2-36.3 pg/mL), and an ALD-renin ratio of 99.1 (≥40), strongly suggesting PA. Serum potassium was 3.0 mmol/L despite potassium supplementation of 72 mmol/day. Persistent muscle weakness was suspected to be due to hypokalemic myopathy. His medical history included a cholecystectomy performed at 27 years of age. He had a 30-year history of smoking 15 cigarettes per day. He was also overweight, weighing 89.9 kg, with a body mass index of 27.9 kg/m² and a body fat percentage of 25.4%. The patient was referred to our department in February 2023 for further evaluation and treatment.

Diagnostic Assessment

Confirmatory tests were performed to diagnose PA according to the 2021 clinical practice guidelines of the Japan Endocrine Society [2]. The captopril test, saline infusion test, and oral salt loading test all yielded positive results, confirming the diagnosis of PA. Computed tomography (CT) revealed bilateral nodular adrenal glands and an 11 × 15 mm right adrenal tumor with a CT value of less than 10 Hounsfield units (Fig. 1A and 1B). The dexamethasone suppression test and 24-hour urinary catecholamine metabolite measurement ruled out autonomous cortisol secretion and pheochromocytoma, respectively. Since the patient preferred surgical treatment if unilateral disease was confirmed, adrenal vein sampling (AVS) was performed to determine lateralization. Successful catheter insertion was confirmed by a selectivity index (SI ≥ 5 indicates successful insertion), calculated based on cortisol ratios. After ACTH stimulation, the lateralization ratio was 73.2 (≥4), and the contralateral ratio was 0.06 (≤1). Based on these results, right-sided lateralization was diagnosed (Table 1). No complications from AVS were observed.

Treatment

Until surgery, he was treated with esaxerenone (maximum dose, 5 mg/day), a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, to control blood pressure and potassium levels. In July 2023, he underwent laparoscopic right adrenalectomy. The retroperitoneal approach was selected because of visceral obesity (body mass index 28 kg/m², abdominal circumference 101 cm), making the transabdominal approach technically challenging, and his previous laparoscopic cholecystectomy, which raised concerns about potential abdominal wall adhesions. During the operation, however, complete removal of the right adrenal gland was judged to be technically difficult due to visceral obesity, and the surgical team decided to resect only the CT-identified adrenal tumor. A right partial adrenalectomy was thus performed. Histopathological examination revealed irregularly arranged compact cells with clear and eosinophilic cytoplasm consistent with adrenal adenoma. Immunohistochemical staining for CYP11B2 was negative within the tumor (Fig. 2).

Outcome and Follow-up

His hypertension and hypokalemia (2.9 mmol/L) persisted postoperatively, along with elevated ALD levels [32.6 ng/dL (SI: 904.9 pmol/L)] and suppressed direct renin concentration levels (1.0 pg/mL) (Table 2). A follow-up CT scan confirmed partial resection of the right adrenal gland, including the tumor identified preoperatively (Fig. 1C). We also used 3D Slicer (Version 5.6.2, http://www.slicer.org) [6] to generate 3D images. The right adrenal gland was manually segmented in both preoperative and postoperative CT images (Fig. 3A). The 3D viewer confirmed the resection of the preoperatively identified tumor along with surrounding tissue (Fig. 3B).

In February 2024, he underwent laparoscopic complete right adrenalectomy via a transperitoneal approach. Dense adhesions were noted on the adrenal dorsal aspect due to the prior procedure; however, no postoperative complications occurred. Postoperatively, ALD levels promptly decreased to 1.4 ng/dL (SI: 38.8 pmol/L), and serum potassium improved to 4.2 mmol/L. Discussion

Partial adrenalectomy is recognized as an effective treatment for small, solitary, benign tumors. Compared to complete unilateral adrenalectomy, this approach may preserve adrenal function and is associated with a lower incidence of postoperative hypoglycemia and adrenal insufficiency [3]. Additionally, by maintaining adrenocortical function, partial adrenalectomy may be especially beneficial for patients with hereditary syndromes, such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, von Hippel-Lindau disease, and neurofibromatosis type 1, who have an increased risk of developing bilateral adrenal disease. These patients may develop new lesions in the contralateral adrenal gland over time, making preservation of adrenal function advantageous. Further potential benefits of partial adrenalectomy include reduced surgical invasiveness, decreased intraoperative blood loss, shorter hospital stays [4], and fewer postoperative complications, such as lower mean blood loss and reduced length of hospitalization [5]. Ishidoya et al [7] reported improvement of hypertension in all patients undergoing complete unilateral adrenalectomy. In contrast, hypertension and hyperaldosteronism persisted in 6.9% of patients who underwent partial adrenalectomy. Additionally, multiple microadenomas were found in pathological specimens from 27.0% of patients who underwent complete unilateral adrenalectomy. Nanba et al [8] described cases in which immunohistochemical examination of resected adrenal tumors showed that lesions detected by CT scans were negative for CYP11B2. Subsequently, the Histopathology of Primary Aldosteronism consensus [9] classified lesions into aldosterone-producing adenoma (APA) and aldosterone-producing nodule (APN), which can be identified by imaging tests or intraoperative macroscopic findings, and ALD-producing micronodules, multiple ALD-producing nodules or micronodules, and ALD-producing diffuse hyperplasia, detectable only by histopathological examination. The consensus recommends complete unilateral adrenalectomy as the surgical treatment for unilateral PA because autonomous ALD-secreting lesions can only be conclusively diagnosed via postoperative histopathological examination and not by even segmental AVS. Indeed, CYP11B2 immunostaining of resected adrenal glands in the TAIPAI study [10, 11] revealed that the largest tumor was not always CYP11B2-positive, supporting the recommendation for complete unilateral adrenalectomy of the affected side.

In our case, because the patient had visceral obesity and a history of laparoscopic cholecystectomy, a retroperitoneal adrenalectomy was initially attempted instead of a transabdominal procedure. However, the surgery proved challenging, resulting in partial adrenalectomy. Consequently, although the adrenal tumor appeared to have been adequately resected on CT images (Fig. 1), there was no postoperative improvement in blood pressure, serum potassium, or ALD levels (Table 2). Additionally, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the resected adrenal tumor specimen contained only CYP11B2-negative cells (Fig. 2). During the second surgery, a transabdominal complete unilateral adrenalectomy was performed, considering the risk of conversion to laparotomy, resulting in clinical and biochemical improvement (Table 2). Histopathological examination identified an APN measuring less than 10 mm in diameter, distinct from the initially resected adenoma. Immunohistochemical staining confirmed CYP11B2 positivity in this APN. No additional CYP11B2-positive cells were detected elsewhere in the adrenal gland (Fig. 5). Initially, the case was considered to be an APA, but the initially resected tumor was nonfunctional, and complete unilateral adrenalectomy revealed another APN as the cause of PA. Removal of the nonfunctional adrenocortical adenoma did not improve PA, whereas resection of the APN resulted in clinical and biochemical improvement. A previous study [8] reported the presence of both CYP11B2-positive and CYP11B2-negative tumors in completely resected adrenal glands. However, our report highlights that PA does not improve if smaller CYP11B2-positive tumors remain after removal of a larger tumor identified on CT. In recent years, diagnostic methods have advanced, and [11C]metomidate positron emission tomography-CT has been shown to detect lesions smaller than 1 cm and to be noninferior to AVS in localizing lesions [12, 13]. In addition, segmental adrenal venous sampling has been reported to improve the accuracy of identifying areas of ALD excess within the adrenal gland compared with standard AVS, potentially making it useful for partial adrenalectomy [14]. Moreover, alternative treatments to adrenalectomy have been developed for PA, including percutaneous CT-guided radiofrequency ablation [15], IV radiofrequency catheter ablation [16], and transcatheter superselective adrenal artery embolization [17]. Nevertheless, due to the heterogeneous pathological phenotype of PA, preoperative imaging tests and adrenal vein sampling often fail to precisely identify the location and extent of the lesion responsible for ALD excess. As it remains unclear how effectively these newer methods eliminate the lesion, such interventions may be unsuitable for patients like ours, whose condition does not improve merely by reducing tumor volume. In conclusion, when unilateral PA is diagnosed by AVS, complete unilateral adrenalectomy appears to be the most appropriate treatment.
