Triglyceride index as a predictor of mortality after cardiac surgery
Authors: Huili Li, Fei Xiao, Haiqiang Ren, Fei Xu, Hao Che, Huadong Zhu, Chenghui Zhou, Sheng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a marker for insulin resistance, has been linked to adverse cardiac outcomes. Its role in predicting mortality following cardiac surgery remains unclear.MethodsThis study analyzed 1,810 cardiac surgery patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, categorized by TyG index levels. Mortality was the primary outcome, assessed through Cox proportional hazards regression, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and restricted cubic splines.ResultsOver a 13-year follow-up, 83 patients died. Higher TyG index levels were associated with increased mortality risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.206, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.121–1.297, p < 0.001). Time-dependent ROC analysis showed areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.914, 0.857, and 0.801 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. The TyG index-based model outperformed established scoring systems in predicting mortality.ConclusionsElevated TyG index levels are significantly associated with higher mortality risk after cardiac surgery, highlighting its potential as a prognostic marker in this population.

Introduction

In recent years, cardiac surgery has evolved significantly, with advances in surgical techniques and postoperative care enhancing patient outcomes. Despite the complexity of procedures, improvements in surgical methods and perioperative management have led to a notable decrease in in-hospital mortality. However, the long-term prognosis of patients undergoing cardiac surgery remains a critical area of focus. Studies have shown that, although survival rates have improved, long-term outcomes are significantly influenced by factors such as the patient’s age, the presence of comorbid conditions, and the occurrence of postoperative complications. For example, a study published in 2020 highlighted the profound impact of postoperative complications on long-term survival, even when early postoperative deaths are excluded.1 Additionally, recent research has emphasized the importance of perioperative myocardial injury in predicting 30-day mortality and long-term survival. This study indicated that perioperative myocardial injury, reflected by perioperative high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T release, is associated with expected 30-day mortality but not with 5-year mortality.2 These insights underlined the ongoing need to optimize both in-hospital care and long-term management to improve the overall prognosis of patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Given the pressing need to identify new biomarkers and strategies for cardiac surgical patients, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a promising candidate. The TyG index was computed according to the triglyceride (TG) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) concentrations according to the formula ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2].3 Unlike other more complex scoring systems, the TyG index stands out for its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and reliability, making it an appealing option for clinical application.4

Insulin resistance (IR), characterized by diminished tissue responsiveness to normal levels of plasma insulin,5 was a key aspect of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM). It was also known to contribute significantly to the development of cardiovascular diseases.6,7 Studies have demonstrated that MetS accelerates cardiovascular disease progression and worsens outcomes.8 Similarly, DM was recognized as a risk factor impacting cardiac surgery results and patient prognosis.9 These insights underscored the potential significance of IR in determining outcomes following cardiac surgery.

Traditional methods for measuring IR, such as the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique and the homeostasis model assessment for IR, had intrinsic limitations.10 In contrast, the TyG index was regarded as a surrogate marker of IR.11 Recent studies have linked the TyG index to various cardiovascular conditions, including the development of cardiovascular disease,12 increased risk of myocardial infarction,13 heart failure,14 in-stent stenosis,15 and the severity of coronary artery disease.16

Despite these indicated associations, there’s a notable gap in research regarding the TyG index’s relationship with follow-up mortality after cardiac surgery. The prognostic value of the TyG index in cardiac surgery patients remained an open question. Investigating the TyG index as a potential predictor for cardiac surgery patients could be crucial in identifying high-risk individuals for improved healthcare management and prompt interventions.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the role of the TyG index in predicting mortality following cardiac surgery. We plan to conduct this assessment using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, aiming to contribute valuable insights into this area.

Baseline characteristics

Patients undergoing cardiac surgery were categorized into quartiles based on their TyG index upon hospital admission, as detailed in Table 1. The quartiles were defined as follows: Q1 spanned 6.98 to 8.44, Q2 ranged from 8.45 to 8.87, Q3 encompassed 8.88 to 9.37, and Q4 extended from 9.38 to 11.77. The median TyG indices for each respective quartile were 8.179 (IQR: 7.928–8.328), 8.671 (IQR: 8.569–8.773), 9.138 (IQR: 9.002–9.265), and 9.784 (IQR: 9.569–10.071). Higher quartile rankings correlated with an increased BMI, as well as elevated incidences of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, renal disease, and both complicated and uncomplicated diabetes. The prevalence of myocardial infarction also rose with higher TyG quartiles. Additionally, patients with higher TyG index exhibited increased levels of serum creatinine (Scr), platelets (PLT), white blood cells, neutrophil granulocytes, lactate (LAC), and alanine transaminase (ALT) compared to their lower-quartile counterparts. No significant variances in-hospital or intensive care unit (ICU) stay durations were observed across the quartiles. Differences in baseline characteristics between survivors and non-survivors post-surgery were presented in Table 2. Non-survivors tended to be male, with greater instances of peptic ulcer disease, cerebrovascular and peripheral vascular diseases, complicated diabetes, liver and renal diseases, and infective endocarditis. Biochemical comparisons showed higher Scr, white blood cell counts, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, LAC, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and ALT levels, but lower hemoglobin (Hb) levels, in the non-survivor group. Hospital and ICU stays were longer for survivors, averaging 8 days compared to 6 days and 3 days versus 2 days, respectively (both p < 0.001). TyG index was notably higher in non-survivors, with a mean of 9.07 against 8.87 in survivors (p = 0.014).

Discussion

The long-term prognosis following cardiac surgery is a critical area of study, holding significant implications for patient care and healthcare strategies. Understanding the factors that influence postoperative outcomes is essential for improving survival rates and quality of life for cardiac patients. Among these factors, the role of metabolic health, particularly IR, has emerged as a key area of interest.17 IR, identified as a state where glucose uptake and utilization are suboptimal, has been linked to increased cardiovascular risk across various populations.18 This relationship is attributed to disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as to factors like oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and ectopic lipid storage.5,18,19 In this context, our study highlights the TyG index as a valuable predictor for follow-up mortality in cardiac surgery patients. We found that a higher TyG index correlates with an increased risk of follow-up mortality, a finding that remains significant even after adjusting for various risk factors. The TyG index was a topic that has been rarely discussed on follow-up mortality.

Numerous studies have already established the connection between the TyG index and mortality, both general and cardiovascular, in different populations and those with cardiometabolic disorders.20,21 For instance, Tao and colleagues have reviewed the TyG index’s reliability as a surrogate for IR, highlighting its utility in risk stratification and predicting cardiovascular outcomes.12 Furthermore, it has been shown that the TyG index independently predicts hospitalization and ICU mortality in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD).22 Liu et al. observed that an elevated TyG index, indicative of more severe IR, has a nonlinear association with mortality, suggesting threshold points beyond which the risk significantly increases.23 Studies across different age groups also shed light on the TyG index’s impact on mortality. In younger adults, each unit increase in the TyG index was linked to a substantially higher risk of all-cause mortality.24 Similar findings were reported in elderly patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), where the TyG index served as an independent predictor of mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events.25 A recent study by Liao et al. involving a diverse ICU adult patient cohort reinforced the TyG index as an independent mortality predictor.21

Despite these findings, there’s a noticeable gap in the literature specifically addressing the TyG index’s role in predicting follow-up mortality among cardiac surgery patients. Studies have shown that IR, particularly in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, is associated with an increased risk of heart failure and mortality.26 This suggests a critical role for IR in patient prognosis. In the realm of cardiac surgery, type 2 diabetes, especially when treated with insulin, markedly elevates long-term mortality risk post-coronary artery bypass grafting in ACS patients.27,28 High TyG index values have been linked to increased postoperative cardiovascular events, highlighting the index’s potential as an early indicator for necessary lifestyle changes. However, a significant gap remains in the current body of research regarding a direct assessment of the correlation between the TyG index and follow-up mortality in cardiac surgery patients.

Our study contributes significantly to this body of research. By employing Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, we have shown that higher IR, as measured by the TyG index, is closely linked with follow-up mortality in cardiac surgery patients. This finding, derived from univariate Cox regression and lasso regression analyses, provides new insights into the impact of the TyG index on this specific patient group, addressing a previously unexplored aspect in the literature. In this model, it was also observed that elevated LAC levels, increased activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and postoperative AKI were associated with a higher risk of follow-up mortality. Conversely, lower bicarbonate levels were found to reduce this risk. This underscores the critical importance of maintaining a stable internal environment in perioperative patients for long-term outcomes. Restricted cubic splines regression models further demonstrated a linear relationship between the TyG index and the risk of follow-up mortality. Following variable selection through multivariate logistic regression, we plotted ROC curves for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year predictions, which showed robust predictive performance. When comparing our model, which includes the TyG index, to existing severity-of-disease scoring systems for predicting all-cause mortality, our model outperformed the SOFA, APSIII, LODS, OASIS, and SAPSII models. Additionally, integrating the TyG index into a risk model with traditional factors enhanced its predictive capacity.

While the precise biological mechanisms linking the TyG index to follow-up mortality are not fully understood, several pathways have been proposed, predominantly related to IR. The TyG index is associated with endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and accelerated atherosclerosis processes.29,30 IR is also linked to hyperglycemia and elevated free fatty acids, which can lead to immune cell infiltration in adipose tissue and macrophage activation.31 This, in turn, results in the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, contributing to systemic inflammation.32 Furthermore, IR can affect endothelial nitric oxide production and promote a prothrombotic state due to increased coagulation activity.33,34 However, to enhance the robustness and predictive power of models exploring these relationships, future work could benefit from utilizing ensemble approaches. By combining multiple modeling techniques—such as random forests, gradient boosting, and neural networks—an ensemble approach can mitigate the weaknesses of individual models, leading to more accurate and reliable predictions. This method could be particularly useful in capturing the complex interactions and nonlinear relationships that may exist between TG, glucose levels, and associated health outcomes, such as lipoma and liposarcomas.35 Utilizing echocardiography can further enhance the current research findings. Echocardiography not only provides detailed structural information of the heart but also allows for real-time observation of cardiac function dynamics, revealing hemodynamic changes.36 By combining echocardiography results with the biomarker data from the current study, such as TG and glucose levels, we can gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between these metabolic parameters and cardiac function. This integrated approach could help identify earlier stages of heart disease and provide a basis for personalized treatment, thereby improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion

An elevated TyG index is a significant predictor of mortality in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Our results suggest that it aids in the early detection of IR in these patients, leading to improved risk assessment and informed interventions. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating the TyG index into daily clinical practice. Further prospective studies are needed to validate our results.

Limitations of the study

The primary strength of our study lies in establishing the TyG index as a strong, independent predictor of increased follow-up mortality in cardiac surgery patients. However, several limitations are noted. Firstly, as a single-center retrospective study, it cannot conclusively determine causality, and there may be residual confounding factors despite our multivariate analysis. Secondly, while we observed that the TyG index did not impact in-hospital or ICU mortality in cardiac surgery patients, the reasons behind this were not explored and will be the focus of future research. Additionally, factors affecting hospital stay duration were not investigated. Thirdly, due to limited clinical information, we could not compare the TyG index with other IR indicators like HOMA-IR and HIEC in predicting follow-up mortality. Fourthly, our analysis was solely focused on the prognostic value of the TyG index in cardiac surgery patients’ follow-up mortality. Finally, the data originated from a specific country, which may limit its applicability to other contexts, although the diverse racial makeup of the patient cohort lends some degree of representativeness.

Experimental model and study participant details

The experimental models and participant details in this study were all obtained from the MIMIC database. MIMIC-IV, a freely available database, comprisesed over 76943 ICU admissions occurring between 2008 and 2019 at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts. The MIMIC-IV database encompassed demographic information, vital signs, laboratory test results, and diagnoses using the Ninth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9). We present the ICD names corresponding to the diseases in Table S1, related to Table 1. One author (Chenghui Zhou) complied with requirements for access to the database and was responsible for the data extraction. Individual patient consent was not required for this study, as patients' identities were not ascertainable through the health information within the databases consulted. To facilitate access to these databases, one of the authors obtained the appropriate certification and proceeded to extract the necessary variables for this investigation.

Inclusion criteria: Age greater than 18 and less than 80 years, patients undergoing cardiac thoracotomy. Exclusion criteria: Lack of triglyceride index records; absence of hospitalization data; lost to follow-up; data missing exceeds 30%. For individuals with multiple hospital admissions, only the initial entry was considered. A total of 1,810 patients were included in the study, with 1,727 survivors and 83 non-survivors. Based on quartile grouping, the distribution was as follows: TyG index Q1 (n=459), TyG index Q2 (n=442), TyG index Q3 (n=461), and TyG index Q4 (n=448). The average age is 60 years, and 497 patients are male.

Method details

Using pgAdmin tools for PostgreSQL, comprehensive data retrieval was performed from the MIMIC-IV database, version 1.22.1, encompassing a broad spectrum of patient information. This information was categorized into four primary domains: (1) demographic details including age, gender, weight, height, and body mass index (BMI); (2) existing comorbid conditions such as atrial fibrillation, sepsis, cardiac insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, nephropathy, and paraplegia; (3) laboratory metrics covering erythrocyte, leukocyte, hemoglobin, thrombocyte counts, along with electrolyte and renal function markers like sodium and creatinine, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and triglycerides (TG); and (4) admission illness severity quantified by scores including but not limited to the Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS-II), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), and the Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA). The follow-up period commenced from admission and concluded at the time of death. The Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index value was computed using the formula ln [fasting TG (mg/dl) × FBG (mg/dl)]/2. All laboratory data and severity indices were extracted from records compiled within the first 24 hours of ICU admission.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was performed according to the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. The data is publicly available (in the MIMIC-IV database), therefore, the ethical approval statement and the requirement for informed consent were waived for this study.

Primary outcome and clinical definition

The main focus of this research was to monitor follow-up mortality after cardiac surgery. Follow-up mortality refers to deaths occurring during a specified period after a patient has been part of a study or after receiving a particular treatment. In-hospital mortality is used to describe patients who die while admitted to a hospital. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) was identified based on the criteria set by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO), involving a rise in serum creatinine (SCr) levels of 0.3 mg/dL or more from the baseline within a 48-hour window.37

Quantification and statistical analysis

For the analysis of continuous data, the independent Student's t-test was employed, with results reported as means and standard deviations (SD). In cases where normal distribution could not be assumed, as determined by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, median values and interquartile ranges (IQR) were used, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for comparisons (Tables 1 and 2). For categorical data, frequencies and percentages were the descriptive statistics of choice, with comparisons made via the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test when any expected frequency was five or less. Participants in the study were stratified into quartiles based on their Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index measurements before cardiac surgery. To evaluate the incidence of primary outcomes among the different TyG strata, a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted (Figure 2). Differences in survival rates across the quartiles were then examined using log-rank testing. Cox regression analyses, both univariate and multivariate, were applied to derive the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between various factors and primary outcomes (Table 3). To evaluate whether an increased TyG index had incremental predictive value for follow-up mortality, we compared the fully past model with and without the TyG index, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were obtained (Table 5). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
