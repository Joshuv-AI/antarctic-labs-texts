Evaluation of the Ligand‐Independent Activation of a VHH‐Based Anti‐CD19 SynNotch Receptor by Dual‐Luciferase Assay
Authors: Yasaman Asaadi, Fatemeh Rahbarizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLigand‐independent activation poses a significant challenge for synthetic receptors, limiting their dynamic range. Given the difficulties and inconsistencies in evaluating this crucial aspect in many studies, we utilized a dual‐luciferase assay to assess the background activity of the SynNotch receptor. These receptors are modular, orthogonal constructs designed to detect and respond to specific extracellular signals by expressing a targeted protein. In this study, we engineered an anti‐CD19 SynNotch receptor by incorporating a nanobody into the antigen‐binding domain, leveraging the advantageous properties of camelid VHHs. We then evaluated the performance of the final construct, focusing on its functionality and ligand‐independent activation using the dual‐luciferase assay. The results revealed that although reporter expression significantly increased in the presence of the antigen, there was also a high level of background expression, which could hinder the performance of the SynNotch receptor. Additionally, the SynNotch receptor exhibited a 24‐h delay in reaching its peak activation level in response to the antigen and in returning to baseline levels in its absence. Therefore, the optimization of the SynNotch construct is essential to enhance the receptor's dynamic range.

Introduction

With the rise of synthetic biology, many groups have aimed to apply engineering principles to biological systems [1]. A variety of biological yet synthetic tools have been developed to achieve the modularity, controllability and orthogonality characteristics of engineering, including synthetic receptors [2]. To date, several synthetic receptors—such as MESA [3], chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) [4], and Tango [5]—have emerged as innovative devices for circuit design [6]. Among these, the SynNotch receptor stands out due to its advantageous properties, making it suitable for a range of applications. This receptor operates through cleavage‐mediated transcriptional activation in response to the recognition of surface antigens [7]. When an antigen binds to the extracellular domain, it triggers cleavage, releasing an intracellular transcription factor (TF). This TF then translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to its specific upstream activating sequence (UAS) and initiates the transcription of a desired downstream gene [8]. This platform provides modularity in both the system's input and output: Any desired antigen‐binding domain can be integrated to detect various molecular inputs in the extracellular domain, whereas the output can be customized by modifying the coding sequence next to the United States. Moreover, because of the synthetic nature of the TF, this receptor operates independently of native signalling pathways and can function in any cell type, including neurons [8, 9, 10]. Nonetheless, ligand‐independent activation (LIA) is a common challenge across all synthetic receptors [6], and SynNotch is no exception [11].

Insight

Synthetic biology enabled scientists to build and design various biological yet synthetic devices. However, these tools have a long way to the clinic without proper characterization. Due to various characterization problems by fluorescent reporters, we take the advantage of the dual‐luciferase assay as a cheap, easy and reliable method to characterize the SynNotch receptor as a proof of concept. We also benefited from camelid VHH as the targeting agent of the receptor because of its superior properties and smaller size. This new method saves a significant amount of time and money in the design and optimization process of synthetic receptors.

Numerous studies have focused on modifying the structure of synthetic receptors to reduce ligand‐induced activation (LIA) and increase their dynamic range [11, 12]. However, in all these studies, a fluorescent protein is employed as a reporter for receptor characterization. This method requires testing each receptor separately, resulting in several time‐consuming and expensive rounds of virus production and flow cytometry.

In this study, we used a dual‐luciferase assay as a dependable, affordable, and simple method to characterize the SynNotch receptor, providing an alternative to traditional flow cytometry‐based assays. As proof, we constructed an anti‐CD19 SynNotch receptor with a nanobody as the recognition domain and a GAL4‐VP64 TF in the extracellular module. To carry out the dual‐luciferase assay, we cloned the response element into a standard Firefly luciferase expression vector. The receptor's binding to CD19 leads to the dissociation of the TF, which then enters the nucleus and initiates the transcription of Firefly luciferase as an experimental reporter. At the same time, Renilla luciferase is constantly expressed as a control reporter. The method described herein is also applicable for evaluating ligand‐independent activation (LIA) in other synthetic receptors.

Materials and Methods

SynNotch Receptor and Response Element Construct Design

The SynNotch receptor was created by fusing an anti‐CD19 VHH [13] to the minimal regulatory region of mouse Notch1 (NM_008714) spanning from Ile1427 to Arg1752, along with the GAL4‐VP64 TF. An N‐terminal CD8a signal peptide (SP) (MALPVTALLLPLALLLHAARP) was incorporated to facilitate the receptor's expression on the cell surface. The Murine Notch1 domain was extracted from mouse kidney tissue through RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and PCR amplification, whereas the GAL4‐VP64 sequence was synthesized by #GenScript. The SynNotch construct was assembled using standard restriction digestion and overlap extension PCR cloning. The final construct was then cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector under the regulation of the CMV promoter. To leverage the dual‐luciferase assay, five copies of the GAL4 target sequence, followed by a minimal CMV promoter sequence, were cloned upstream of Firefly luciferase within the multiple cloning site of the pGL4.14 vector from #Promega. All constructs were ultimately verified by Sanger sequencing.

Cell Culture

HEK 293T cells were cultivated in DMEM with high glucose, supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1× GlutaMax, and 100 U/mL penicillin along with 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). Nalm6 cells were grown in RPMI (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin, and 1× GlutaMax. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Transient Transfection

The day before transfection, 50,000 cells were plated in each well of a 24‐well plate, ensuring that the cells reached 60%–80% confluency by the day of transfection. Transient transfections were carried out using PEI from Sigma. The PEI stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 1 µg/µL in double‐distilled water, adjusted to a pH of 7.0, and sterilized through a 0.22 µm filter. The PEI stock solution was stored at 4°C and allowed to warm to room temperature before use. For each well, a total of 1 µg of mixed plasmid DNA was dissolved in 100 µL of PBS and combined with 100 µL of PEI in PBS (comprising 97 µL PBS and 3 µg PEI). The DNA/PEI/PBS mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min and then added to the cells after a 2‐h exposure to FBS‐free medium.

Dual‐Luciferase Assay

The dual‐luciferase assay was performed using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System kit (Promega# E1910). After 48 h post‐transfection, cells were washed with PBS, and 100 µL of the provided passive lysis buffer (PLB) was added to each well. Following a 15‐min shaking period, the enzymes were released from the cells into the supernatant. Subsequently, 20 µL of the supernatant from each well was transferred to a standard luminometer tube, where 100 µL of LARII buffer was added according to the standard protocol. After pipetting, the tube was placed in the luminometer to measure the luminescent activity of Firefly luciferase. Then, 100 µL of Stop & Glo reagent was added to the same tube to assess Renilla luciferase activity.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted at least three times, and data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Results are presented as mean ± SEM. A two‐tailed t‐test was used to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between groups, with *p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Generation of CD19 Sensing and Firefly Luciferase Producing SynNotch in HEK‐T Cells

The final anti‐CD19 SynNotch receptor was constructed, incorporating a leader sequence, anti CD19 VHH, Notch core and GAL‐VP64 TF, and cloned into a standard pcDNA3.1 vector under the control of a CMV promoter (Figure 1A). The recombinant PGL4.14, which has the GAL4 response element upstream of Firefly luciferase, was co‐transfected with the control vector PGL4.74 and the pcDNA3.1 construct carrying the anti‐CD19 SynNotch into HEK293T cells. This engineered SynNotch detects CD19 through the anti‐CD19 VHH present in its extracellular domain. When the antigen binds, the intracellular TF is released, allowing it to enter the nucleus and trigger the transcription of Firefly luciferase by attaching to its specific response element. At the same time, the control vector consistently produces Renilla luciferase through its HSV‐TK promoter (Figure 1B).

High LIA With Significant Higher Expression of the Reporter in the Presence of Antigen

Previous studies have shown the effects of transfected SynNotch coding vectors on LIA. In this experiment, varying amounts of the vector were transfected, and after 24 h, the cells were co‐cultured with Nalm6 (CD19+) and K562 (CD19−) cell lines at a 1:1 ratio. Figure 2 illustrates that LIA is considerably high in the absence of the antigen, whereas the normalized fold change in activity increases with the amount of SynNotch coding vector up to a certain point—200 ng. Beyond this threshold, increasing the amount of the receptor coding vector no longer affects the reporter activity. As shown in Figure 2, reporter expression was significantly higher only in the 200 ng transfected group, whereas no significant differences were observed in the other groups, particularly the 500 ng group.

Reporter Expression With the Increasing Amount of Input

To assess the receptor's response to the antigen, engineered cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of plate‐bound antigen. As anticipated, reporter expression showed a positive correlation with the amount of antigen presented; notably, high reporter expression was recorded in control wells lacking antigen (Figure 3). Utilizing these data, we calculated the primary and lumped metrics as outlined by Manhas et al. [14]. The anti‐CD19 SynNotch receptor functions within a dynamic range of 0–100 ng; beyond this range, it reaches a plateau where increasing antigen levels no longer impact the receptor's response. The fold induction of this receptor is only 1.67, which is relatively low due to significant background signal. This suggests a need for further optimization of the receptor construct and other system variables.

Sensitivity of the receptor to the increased amount of plate‐bound antigen. (A) The receptor responds to the antigen, and reporter expression increases with the growing amount of plate‐bound antigen. (B) Primary and lumped metrics were calculated for the anti‐CD19 SynNotch receptor.

Reporter Expression During a Time Course

In the next experiment, we aim to measure how long it takes for reporter expression to reach its peak after exposure to the antigen, as well as the time it takes for the reporter to return to baseline after the removal of CD19 + cells. To accomplish this, 24 h post‐transfection, the engineered HEK cells were co‐cultured with the Nalm6 CD19 + cell line, and a luciferase assay was conducted at 1, 3, 20, and 24 h thereafter. At the 24‐h time point, the Nalm6 cell lines were removed from the flask through three washes with PBS. The luciferase assay was performed at 4 and 24 h after the elimination of the Nalm6 cells. Figure 4 shows that reporter expression levels off after 20 h, and once the antigen is removed, receptor activity drops sharply, returning to its normal level after 24 h.

Discussion

Synthetic receptors are increasingly used as vital tools in synthetic biology [14]. However, their accurate characterization is crucial to ensure their functionality as controllable devices within various genetic circuits [15]. Key attributes of synthetic receptors, such as dynamic range and ligand‐independent activation, must be thoroughly characterized to avoid unwanted background activity [16]. In this study, we employed the dual‐luciferase assay as a standard and simple method for the characterization of synthetic receptors.

Although flow cytometry is a common method for characterization, it is not ideal for transiently expressed reporters due to potential errors and variabilities in cell plating, transfection efficiency, pipetting inconsistencies, and toxicity, which can compromise the statistical significance of the data [17]. Although stable transfection can reduce sample‐to‐sample variability, it necessitates multiple rounds of virus production and transduction. Additionally, using fluorescent reporters demands time‐consuming and expensive flow cytometry‐based assays to assess their expression. Here, we leverage the dual‐luciferase assay to measure reporter expression through a simple transient transfection and luminometric assay, employing vector normalization to yield reliable data on reporter expression and receptor activity.

Among the various designed receptors, the SynNotch receptor stands out because of its exceptional features, such as modularity and orthogonality. This makes it a highly suitable candidate for many applications [7]. However, there are conflicting reports regarding the leakiness and background activity of this receptor [9, 11]. In this study, we investigate the dynamic range of SynNotch and, for the first time, employ an anti‐CD19 nanobody in the antigen‐binding domain. Nanobodies are small antibody fragments characterized by low immunogenicity and high stability, making them a practical alternative to traditional scFvs [18]. Therefore, we use these compact targeting agents to decrease the total receptor size.

As previous studies have stated that the amount of SynNotch coding vector can affect LIA [11], we investigated reporter expression by exposing CD19+ and control cell lines to varying amounts of SynNotch coding vector. As shown in Figure 2, whereas there was a notable difference between CD19‐exposed cells and the control in the 200 ng group, the background activity was exceptionally high. In a different experiment, engineered cells were subjected to progressively higher levels of plate‐bound antigen to ensure a SynNotch response to CD19. The results showed high LIA even without any antigen present, whereas reporter expression rose as CD19 levels increased. We further calculated the primary and lumped metrics of this receptor as recommended by Manhas et al. [14]. The low fold induction resulting from high background activity has been documented in other studies as well [11, 12]. The conventional approach to mitigating this background signal involves sorting out the auto‐active cells using flow cytometry or other methods. This process is labour‐intensive and introduces an additional step to the already complex manufacturing process of cell therapy. Several studies have attempted to address this issue by modifying the receptor construct. In the original paper introducing the SynNotch receptor, the authors stated that this leakiness would decrease by adding EGF repeats before antigen recognition [8]. However, Yang et al. demonstrated the inefficiency of this method and indicated that adding a hydrophobic sequence (QHGQLWF) before the TF was more effective in reducing background activity [11]. In a recent study by Zhu et al., a new receptor was designed on the basis of SynNotch, named SNIPR, devoid of high background activity with superior properties, such as higher expression, smaller size, titratable activity, and lower immunogenicity [12].

Because the system's response rate is vital for preventing unwanted activities, we performed another experiment to study reporter expression over time. The results indicated that SynNotch exhibits about a 20‐h delay in reacting to stimuli before it reverts to its baseline level. This delay explains the findings of Srivastava et al., who noted that the SynNotch CAR logic gate could only distinguish between tumour cells and adjacent normal cells when they were separated, not when they were co‐localized [19]. In the absence of antigen, the TFs released from SynNotch stay activated, leading to the expression of the gene of interest (GOI), in this case, the CAR. This delay is a key feature of the receptor; decreasing it allows the system to function more precisely. As a result, a smaller gap between cancerous and normal bystander cells is needed for the SynNotch‐CAR AND gate platforms to operate with specificity.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a dual‐luciferase assay as a simple yet dependable method to accurately characterize SynNotch receptors. This method is also useful for evaluating the background activity of various synthetic receptors, as well as for designing novel receptors and improving their dynamic range. The findings of this study demonstrate the background activity of the SynNotch receptor, aligning with the results of similar investigations. However, it is noted that this lower intrinsic activity (LIA) could be significantly reduced by optimizing the receptor construct.
