Effect of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract on the growth performance, haemato-biochemical parameters, gut health, and antioxidant activity in broiler chickens
Authors: Binti Ma’rifah, Edjeng Suprijatna, Dwi Sunarti, Luthfi Djauhari Mahfudz, Sri Kismiati, Teysar Adi Sarjana, Rina Muryani, Hanna Dzawish Shihah, Nur Maulida Wahyuni, Sugiharto Sugiharto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract (EAC) as a phytobiotic feed additive on the growth performance, haemato-biochemical parameters, gut health, and antioxidant activity in broiler chickens. A total of 280 one-day-old chicks were allocated to four treatments with seven replicates and 10 broilers per replicate (CON: control group/basal diet; EAC1: basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg EAC; EAC2: basal diet supplemented with 1000 mg/kg EAC; EAC3: basal diet supplemented with 1500 mg/kg EAC. Growth performance was significantly enhanced (P < 0.05), as evidenced by increased body weight gain (days 15–21) and feed efficiency (days 15–35) in all treatment group compared with the control group. Haemato-biochemical parameters were positively influenced, with leukocytes and lymphocyte levels being significantly lower (P < 0.05) in the EAC2 group than in the control group. The uric acid concentration was significantly reduced (P < 0.05) in EAC1 and EAC3 groups compared to the control group. Gut health indicator improved, including the relative weight of the small intestine, liver, abdominal fat, length of duodenum and jejunum, weight and length ratio of the jejunum and ileum, ileum crypt depth, and lactic acid bacteria count in the ileum, were significantly higher (P < 0.05) in the EAC1 group than in the others groups. In conclusion, the encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract is beneficial for enhancing growth performance, haemato-biochemicals parameters, and gut health in broiler chickens.Keywords: Herbal plant supplementation, Body weight gain, Blood profile, Microbial count, Digestive tract

Introduction

Broiler chickens are susceptible to various stressors that can disrupt the antioxidant system and compromise performance (Nicol et al., 2024). Tropical climate conditions are stressors that can affect the performance and productivity of broiler chickens. Heat stress has a significant impact on poultry welfare and production (Akbarian et al., 2016). Heat stress can also harm physiological, immunological, and microbiological conditions, causing impaired broiler performance (Sugiharto et al., 2017). Heat stress can trigger oxidative stress, affect mitochondrial function and affect metabolic system and energy balance. Indeed, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) may increase due to heat stress, a by-product of cellular respiration. Oxidative stress occurs when reactive species or free radicals exceed the antioxidant capacity of the body, resulting in imbalance and cell damage that can cause disease (Emami et al., 2021).

Antioxidants are widely used in broiler chicken production to prevent the adverse effects of oxidative stress. Along with the trend of using natural ingredients in broiler production, farmers are now using phytobiotics or herbs as a source of antioxidants (Obianwuna et al., 2024). One of the herbal plants widely available in Indonesia that functions as a phytobiotic feed additive is the sweet starfruit leaf (Averrhoa carambola L.). According to Luan et al. (2021), Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract has the potential to be an antioxidant because it contains large amounts of flavonoids (apigenin), saponins, steroids, and tannin. Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract also contains butane, 1,1-diethoxy-3-methyl, and hexadecanoic acid methyl ester (methyl palmitate), which can be used to synthesize organic acids and have antibacterial properties (Astiti & Ramona, 2021). In addition to possessing antimicrobial and antiparasitic activities, apigenin can inhibit the HMG-CoA reductase enzymes and pancreatic lipase, which reduce the synthesis of endogenous cholesterol, lower cholesterol in the liver and increase cholesterol secretion in the excreta (Alaidaileh et al., 2019). Averrhoa carambola L. also has beneficial effect, including hypocholesterolemic, hypotensive, hypoglycemic, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, anti-infective, and immune-boosting effects (Lakmal et al., 2021).

Little research has been conducted about Averrhoa carambola L. leaves in broiler chickens. However, some studies have found in rats showing that dietary supplementation with the methanolic extract of Averrhoa carambola L. leaves reduced total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein (Alaidaileh et al., 2019; Abduh et al., 2023), reduced oxidative stress (Yang et al., 2020a; Luan et al., 2021; Ramadan et al., 2022), lowered blood sugar, and increased cholesterol secretion in the feces (Lakmal et al., 2021). Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract supplementation could also effectively reduce total cholesterol, LDL-c, triglycerides and oxidative stress at doses of 300 to 1000 mg/kg (Abduh et al., 2023; Ramadan et al., 2022; Alaidaileh et al., 2019). A high inclusion level (>1500 mg/kg) of phytochemical feed additive gives a strong odor and flavor that influences growth performance (Ali et al., 2021). Herbal extract supplementation is typically aimed at improving growth performance (Vasilopoulos et al., 2022) and intestinal health status, which is closely related to the balanced microbial populations. Zhu et al. (2019) reported that cellular processes and signaling, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, and response to infectious diseases were among the numerous fuctions of the ileal microbiota that were differentially enriched among treatments after 14 days. Ali et al. (2024) reported dietary treatment does not have a significant impact on feed intake and performance as the age increases from 35 to 47 days. In this respect, a study on the application of Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract in broiler chickens in days 15 to 35 is essential. The use of herbs for broiler chickens may be hampered by their low bioavailability of active ingredients (Sugiharto & Ayasan, 2023) and limiting due to their high volatility, odor, rapid breakdown, and low availability in the lower intestines (Ramadan et al., 2020). Indeed, the active compounds in herbs are quickly degraded and damaged during storage (Awad et al., 2021). Therefore, active compounds, such as polyphenols must be protected to prevent oxidative damage (Chu et al., 2021). Previous studies have demonstrated that protection is required to effectively deliver active compounds to the animal gut (Yang et al., 2020b, Yang et al., 2020a; Sugiharto & Ayasan, 2023). Encapsulation can increase the stability, palatability, and bioavailability of herbal supplements, potentially increasing their effectiveness in enhancing the health and production traits of broiler chickens (Sugiharto & Ayasan, 2023). Similarly, encapsulation can improve the shelf life of leaf extract (Jafari et al., 2022). The selection of the coating material is a crucial step in the encapsulation process. Water-soluble coating materials offer simple and affordable preparation methods (Sugiharto & Ayasan, 2023). Hydrophilic natural polysaccharides can be used to stabilize bioactive compounds. Owing to its hydrophilic surface, maltodextrin is a natural polysaccharide that readily dissolves in water (Sha et al., 2024). Therefore, the stability and bioavailability of active compounds used as feed additives can be improved by combining maltodextrin with plant extracts during encapsulation.

This study aimed to investigate the effect of different levels of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract on growth performance, haemato-biochemical parameters, gut health, and antioxidant activity of broiler chicken.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

The Committee of Animal Ethics of the Faculty of Animal and Agricultural Sciences, Universitas Diponegoro approved the present study (Approval Number: 60–01/A-04/KEP-FPP).

Collection and extraction of plant leaves

Averrhoa carambola L. leaves were collected from the Demak Region, Central Java, Indonesia. Leaves were cleaned, washed, and drained at room temperature without direct sunlight. The leaves were ground in a mixer grinder and extracted using ethanol 70 % at room temperature for 72 h at a 1:5 (w/v) ratio, pooled, and filtered using a cotton plug and Whatman No.1 filter paper. The filtrate was evaporated using a rotary evaporator. After evaporation, the filtrate was encapsulated with maltodextrin as a coating material using the freeze-drying method (Jamdar et al., 2021).

Experimental design and diets

A total of 280 Lohmann broiler chicks with an average initial body weight of 45.48 ± 3.78 g were raised in this feeding trial in an environmentally controlled pen. The birds were fed a commercial diet containing 23 % crude protein, 5 % crude fat, 5 % crude fiber, and 7 % ash (according to the feed label) from 1 to 14 d of age. On day 15 onward, the chicks were randomly divided into four treatment groups with seven replicates and 10 birds each. Dietary treatment (15–35 days) groups were as follows: CON: control group/basal diet; EAC1: basal diet supplemented with 500 mg/kg encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract (EAC); EAC2: basal diet supplemented with 1000 mg/kg EAC; EAC3: basal diet supplemented with 1500 mg/kg EAC. The chicks were provided with ad libitum access to water during the experiment. Feed was formulated according to the Indonesian National Standard (Table 1). The temperature was set to 33 °C for the first week and periodically reduced until the end of the experiment. Continuous lighting was applied throughout the day, and during the trial, the airflow inside the broiler house was controlled using an installed tarp and blowers. Chick was vaccinated against Newcastle disease (ND) and infectious bronchitis (IB) at four days of age and the Gumboro vaccine at day 16. Body weight and feed intake were recorded weekly. The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated by dividing the feed intake by body weight gain.. Income over feed cost (IOFC) was calculated based on total revenue minus total feed cost at the time of the experiment.

Complete blood count

On day 35, blood samples were taken from the wing veins of 28 broilers (seven birds in each treatment group) for complete blood counts. Samples for complete blood counts were collected using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacuum tubes. Parameters such as haemoglobin, red blood cell count, and white blood cell count were determined using an automated blood analyser (Prima Fully-auto Hematology Analyzer, PT. Prima Alkesindo Nusantara, Jakarta, Indonesia).

Blood biochemical analysis

The blood was placed in a vacutainer without an anticoagulant and left at room temperature for about 2 h until coagulate and centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to produced the blood serum (Sugiharto et al., 2022). The lipid profile (total cholesterol, total triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein) and serum level of uric acid and creatinine were determined using enzyme-based colorimetric techniques with a direct method. The albumin concentration was determined using spectrophotometric methods (Comolli et al., 2021). Serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT) and serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT) enzyme activity were determined using a kinetic enzymatic reaction. Serum biochemical assays were performed according to the manufacturer‘s instructions (DiaSys Diagnostic System GmbH, Holzheim, Germany). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) concentration in the serum was determined using a commercial kit (CheKine™ Micro Superoxide Dismutase Activity Assay Kit, Abbkine, USA). Malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration in the serum was determined using a commercial kit (CheKine™ Micro Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit, Abbkine, USA).

SOD and MDA activities were measured according to the methods described by Agusetyaningsih et al. (2022). The ability of the sample to decrease pyrogallol auto-oxidation was used to investigate the activity of SOD. The sample was then mixed with 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.2) and one mM pentaacetic acid diethylenetriamine. Pyrogallol (final concentration, 0.2 mM) was used to initiate the reaction, and the absorbance was estimated kinetically. SOD levels are presented in U/mL. Malondialdehyde activity was detected using a thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reactive material test. Each sample was vortexed, treated with 8.1 % sodium dodecyl, and left at room temperature for 10 min; the controls received the same treatment. Following the incubation period, the samples were treated with 20 % acetic acid and 0.6 % TBA before immersion in a water bath for 1 h at a temperature of 90–95 °C. The supernatant was treated with a 15:1 combination of butanol and pyridine, vortexed and centrifuged. The MDA levels were measured in nmol/mL.

Organ weight and gastrointestinal tract

The chick was slaughtered, de-feathered, and eviscerated, and their intestines and internal organs were removed (empty condition). Specific organs were measured and weighed, including the spleen, thymus, Bursa of Fabricius, small intestine, caecum, heart, liver, proventriculus, gizzard, pancreas, and abdominal fat. The eviscerated carcass weight was calculated as a proportion of the live body weight. The small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) was measured for the relative ( % BW) weight and length. The pH of the small intestine was measured using a digital pH meter (OHAUS portable pH meter). Hematoxylin and eosin-stained five μm sections of duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were used to histologically examine the small intestinal segments. The villus height and crypt depth in each segment were determined using an optical microscope with a digital camera (Leica Microsystems Gmbh, Wetzlar, Germany). The villus height/crypt depth ratio was determined by calculating the villus height divided by crypt depth.

Ileal and cecal microbial count

On day 35, ileal and cecal contents were collected from 7 birds per treatment. The bacterial population in the ileal and cecal were counted, as previously described by Sugiharto et al. (2018). Coliform and lactose-negative enterobacteria were counted as red and colorless colonies on MacConkey agar (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) after aerobic incubation at 38 °C for 24 h. The number of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) was determined on de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Agar (Merck KGaA) after aerobic incubation at 38 °C for 48-h duration.

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS (IBM Corp., Version 29.0.2.0, New York). Duncan’s multiple analysis was used when the treatment had a significant effect (P < 0.05) among the treatments. The influence of feed additives levels was determined using an orthogonal polynomial contrast test for linear and quadratic effects and P value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Growth performance

The effect of the encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract (EAC) on the growth performance of broiler chicken is shown in Table 2. The Average body weight gain on days 15 to 21 increased (P < 0.05) in EAC1, EAC2, and EAC3 groups compared to the control group. Average body weight gain on days 22 to 28 and days 15 to 35 was increased (P < 0.05) in the EAC1 and EAC3 groups compared to that in control groups. The regression test further showed linear and quadratic effects (P < 0.05) of the treatments on average body weight gain from days 15 to 21 and days 22 to 28. The average feed intake on days 15 to 21 was higher (P < 0.05) in EAC1 than in EAC3. The regression test further showed linear and quadratic impact (P < 0.05) of treatments on average feed intake days 15 to 21 and showed quadratic impact (P < 0.05) of treatments on average feed intake days 22 to 28. A significantly increased (P < 0.05) feed conversion ratio (FCR) on days 15 to 21 and days 22 to 28 was found in EAC3, but the FCR on days 15 to 35 significantly increased (P < 0.05) in EAC1, EAC2, and EAC3 compared to the control group. The regression test further showed linear and quadratic impact (P < 0.05) of treatments on FCR from days 15 to 21, days 22 to 28, and days 15 to 35. Overall, encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract significantly (P < 0.05) increased growth performance but did not alter (P > 0.05) income over feed cost (IOFC).

Digestive tract profile

The effect of the encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract on the digestive tract profile of broiler chicken is shown in Table 6. The length of duodenum and jejunum, weight and length ratio of jejunum, and weight and length ratio of ileum were increased (P < 0.05) in broilers fed EAC1 compared to control, EAC2, and EAC3. The regression test further showed a quadratic effect (P < 0.05) of the treatments on the duodenal length of broilers. Discussion

The average body weight gain and feed intake increased with encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract (EAC) supplementation, and the feed conversion ratio decreased. This result agrees with Hossain et al. (2024), who reported an increased average final body weight and feed conversion ratio (FCR) in Hanhyup-3-ho chickens supplemented with an herbal mixture extract. This result was also reported by Dokou et al. (2023a), who noted that supplementation with probiotics-encapsulated plant extract increased the final body weight of broiler. Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract contains bioactive compounds such as phenols, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and many others (Lakmal et al., 2021). The phenolic compounds can modulate metabolic processes by synchronizing the availability of plasma glucose and amino acid and optimizing nutrient-improved FCR and broiler growth (Sekh & O'Shea, 2023). Indeed, bioactive compounds in phytobiotics have a positive effect (Agusetyaningsih et al., 2022). Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract has high potential as an antioxidant, immune-enhancing, anti-inflammatory, and digestive benefits (Aladaileh et al., 2019; Lakmal et al., 2021), which are crucial for growth improvement. The growth improvement observed in this study can also be attributed to the improved feed efficacy in broiler chickens receiving encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract.

The current investigation showed that supplementation with encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract influence the level of leukocytes and lymphocytes. Leucocytes and lymphocytes in EAC2 were lower than thos in control and EAC3. Supplementation of 1000 mg/kg resulted in the lowest leukocyte levels, and increased at supplementation of 1500 mg/kg. Several studies have reported an increase in the activity of some serum antioxidant enzymes (El-Damrawy et al., 2023). Phytochemicals shown to possess immunostimulatory properties include flavonoids, vitamin C, and carotenoids. Phytochemical feed additives may help the immune response when animals are in immune-suppressed condition by influencing lymphocyte activity (Valenzuela-Grijalva et al., 2017). Buryacov et al. (2023) demonstrated that including complex phytobiotic feed additives in broiler diets increases leukocyte concentration. A significant increase in leucocyte levels indicates an enhanced immune response. Phytobiotics are recognized for their ability to modulate both humoral and cellular immunity, which is crucial for enhancing overall health and disease resistance of broilers (El Ghany & Soyad, 2020). The high levels of white blood cells may be positive since it is due to an increase in the overall immune protective state of the body (Buryacov et al., 2023). Leukocytes, or white blood cells, play a crucial role in the immune system, in response to infections and inflammation (El-Damrawy et al., 2023). The increase in lymphocyte levels was similar to the result of Budriesi et al. (2024), who reported that plant-based additives from herbal extract can stabilize blood metabolites and improve intestinal health, which are critical for maintaining healthy lymphocyte levels. This phenomenon is attributed to the presence of bioactive compounds, particularly flavonoids. These compounds exhibit antibacterial properties, which can reduce pathogen load and indirectly support immune function in broilers through various mechanisms, including pathogen inhibition, immune cell modulation, and protection of gut and liver health (Choi et al., 2022). This antibacterial effect may lead to a healthier gut environment, which is crucial for optimal immune function, including lymphocyte production (Pratama, Yudiarti, Sugiharto, & Ayasan, 2022). These results were supported by the orthogonal polynomial contrast test for quadratic effect, showing that the leukocytes and lymphocytes increased (P < 0.05) with the increased level of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract in a broiler diet. Heterophils lymphocytes (H/L) ratio was lower (P < 0.05) in CON, EAC1, and EAC3 than in EAC2. This finding was similar to that of Khan et al. (2022), who noted that the heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio of broiler chickens was lower with dietary leaf extract. The H/L ratio helps to measure stress and immune function; it is essential to consider other factors influencing chicken health, such as environmental conditions. This ratio reflects the balance between heterophils, key players in the immune defense, and lymphocytes involved in adaptive immunity. A higher H/L ratio indicates stress or disease susceptibility (Ribeiro et al., 2024). The orthogonal polynomial contrast test for linear effects revealed that MCH value decreased (P < 0.05) with the increased level of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract in broiler diet, and linear and quadratic effects revealed that MCHC value decreased (P < 0.05) with the increased level of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract in broiler diet. A previous study examined phytobiotics, which led to lower MCH and MCHC values, suggesting that particular combinations of phytobiotics might adversely affect these erythrocyte indices (Sugiharto et al., 2018). Phytobiotics may induce oxidative stress, which can reduce the deformability of erythrocytes and affect their function. Oxidative stress decreases erythrocyte deformability and increases methemoglobin formation, impairing oxygen transport and leading to lower MCH and MCHC levels (Obianwuna et al., 2024). Phytobiotics may also influence the phosphorylation state of erythrocyte proteins, which play a role in erythrocyte aggregation and can impact MCH and MCHC.

The HDL-c concentration was higher (P < 0.05) in control and EAC1 than in EAC2 and EAC3. These results were supported by Khatun et al. (2023), in which high-density lipoprotein levels at maximum doses of 0.25 g/kg feed were decreased with the increasing doses of probiotics. HDL-c is a circulating cholesterol that plays a role in reducing blood cholesterol and preventing the formation of atherosclerosis plaque. A low HDL-c level is considered an important risk factor for cardiovascular disease and constitutes one of the criteria for metabolic syndrome (Ito & Ito, 2020). Recent perspectives emphasize the functional quality of HDL-c rather than its quantity. HDL-c functionality, such as its role in cholesterol efflux and anti-inflammatory properties, may be more relevant to cardiovascular health than HDL-c levels (Khatun et al., 2023). Phytobiotics can influence the regulatory pathways of HDL-c metabolism, including gene expression and protein modifications, which might result in decreased HDL-c levels (Lee et al., 2020). These inferences were supported by the orthogonal polynomial contrast test for linear and quadratic effects, showing that HDL-c value decreased (P < 0.05) with increased levels of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract in a broiler diet. HMG-CoA reductase is a rate-limiting enzyme involved in cholesterolgenesis is a critical step in treating hyperlipidemia is the inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase, which lowers endogenous cholesterol synthesis. HMG-CoA reductase, a pivotal enzyme in the mevalonate pathway, plays a central role in cholesterol biosynthesis and homeostasis and catalyzes the conversion of HMG-CoA to mevalonate, a critical step in the production of cholesterol and other isoprenoids. Beyond its enzymatic function, HMG-CoA reductase is a key regulatory node in maintaining the cellular and circulatory cholesterol balance (Faulkner & Jo, 2022). The Averrhoa carambola L. extract boosted cellular cholesterol absorption and reduced endogenous cholesterol synthesis by downregulating SREBP-2 and HMG-CoA reductase (Abduh et al., 2023). The triglyceride concentration was lower (P < 0.05) in CON and EAC1 than in EAC2 and EAC3. This finding was similar to that of Alaidelah et al. (2019), who reported that supplementation with Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract 1000 mg/kg can reduce triglycerides in rat blood serum by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase and pancreatic lipase activities. Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract exhibits strong antioxidant properties, reducing lipid peroxidation and boosting antioxidant defenses such as glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase, which can help manage oxidative stress in broilers, potentially influencing lipid metabolism (Abduh et al., 2023). Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract also has anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting lipoxygenase, which may also improve lipid profiles by reducing inflammation-induced lipid alterations (Rahma et al., 2020).

Supplementation with encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extracts reduced (P < 0.05) the serum uric acid level in broiler chickens. These inferences were supported by the orthogonal polynomial contrast test for linear and quadratic effects, showing that uric acid value decreased (P < 0.05) with the increased levels of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract. Supplementation of phytobiotics in broiler chicken diets can influence physiological parameters, including serum uric acid levels. This result was similar to those of Safaei-Cherehh et al. (2020) and Basit et al. (2020), who stated that supplementing broiler diet with phytobiotics can lower uric acid concentration. Phytobiotics can modulate the gut microbiota and enhance immune function, indirectly influencing metabolic processes related to uric acid (Sizova et al., 2022). Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract has an intense antioxidant activity that may improve efficacy, enhance nutrient absorption, and reduce protein catabolism (Alibemani et al., 2020). Flavonoid compounds in phytobiotics can inhibit xanthine oxidase, which reduces uric acid production by binding to its active site, preventing the enzyme from catalyzing the oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine and subsequently to uric acid (Li et al., 2024). Quercetin in Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract demonstrates potent xanthine oxidase inhibition by fitting into the enzyme's active site and forming hydrogen bonds (Sun et al., 2024). Flavonoids also exhibit antioxidant properties by scavenging free radicals and reducing oxidative stress, which are beneficial for the management of gout and hyperuricemia (Alsawaf et al., 2022).

The relative weight of the small intestine, liver, and abdominal fat decreased with supplementation of 1000 and 1500 mg/kg encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract. This finding was in agreement with that of Aladaileh et al. (2019), who reported a significant decrease in relative organ weight in rats treated with 1000 mg/kg methanolic extract of Averrhoa carambola leaf. Phytobiotics, including those derived from plant extracts such as Averrhoa carambola L., have been shown to improve gut morphology by increasing villus height and crypt depth, which can enhance nutrient absorption and overall gut health. Better gut health can lead to more efficient nutrient utilization and potentially reduce the relative weight of the small intestine owing to enhanced body weight (Basit et al., 2020; Yasmin et al., 2022; Pirman et al., 2021). The methanolic extract of Averrhoa carambola L. has been shown to decrease hepatic cholesterol and triglycerides, which can lead to a reduction in liver weight. This extract can also improve antioxidant defenses in the liver, reduce oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation, prevent liver damage, and reduce liver weight by maintaining healthier liver tissue (Aladaileh et al., 2019). The decreased weight of abdominal fat obtained in the present study aligns with the findings of Rivas-Caceres et al. (2024) and Hossain et al. (2024), who reported decreased abdominal fat with dried extract of herbal plant in broilers. Averrhoa carambola L. may have reduced fat by inhibiting the HMG-CoA reductase and lipase pancreatic enzyme, which can reduce fat synthesis (Aladaileh et al., 2019). This reduction in abdominal fat can be attributed to the modulation of fat metabolism pathways through lipid metabolism genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and storage, leading to decreased fat deposition (Ramadan et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024).

The length of the duodenum and jejunum, weight and length ratio of the jejunum, and weight and length ratio of the ileum were increased in broilers fed with EAC1 compared to control, EAC2, and EAC3. The increased length of duodenum and jejunum and weight-length ratio in the jejunum and ileum in EAC1 agree with the finding of Rivas-caceres et al. (2024), who showed that extract supplementation in broiler chickens significantly increased intestinal length and weight. Leaf extract has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that enhance the health of Microbiota and may contribute to improved intestinal function and features (Farahat et al., 2021). Using phytobiotics from leaf meals has been shown to significantly improve gut morphology and structure (Basit et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2024). Bioactive compounds in plants like Averrhoa carambola L. leaf can act as natural growth promotors by enhancing nutrient absorption and improving gut health (Maharani et al., 2024). Improving the absorptive surface area of the intestine can enhance nutrient absorption and gut health (Ramadan et al., 2020). These results were supported by the orthogonal polynomial contrast test for quadratic effect, showing that duodenum length decreased (P < 0.05) with the increased level of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract in the broiler diet.

The crypt depth of the ileum was greater in EAC1 than in control, EAC2 and EAC3. Indeed, nutrient absorption efficiency cannot be measured using only the crypt depth parameter. In most cases, villus height-to-crypt depth ratio determines the effectiveness of nutrient absorption by the small intestine. Improved villi height or villus height-to-crypt depth ratio is usually associated with more efficient nutrient absorption and better performance (Zhang et al., 2023). Intestinal histomorphology has been used to evaluate the health of the gut. Longer villi and lower crypt depth indicate better nutrient absorption, less tissue turnover rate, and fewer maintenance requirements for building new enterocytes (Lakmal et al., 2021). The phytobiotic from the encapsulated extract of Averrhoa carambola in the current study did not negatively affect intestinal function because the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio was not significant, and body weight gain was significantly increased.

The count of lactic acid bacteria in the ileum was higher in EAC1 than in control and EAC3. This result was agree with the finding from Dokou et al. (2023b) that state, herbal plant extracts encapsulated with cyclodextrin can increase the number of lactic acid bacteria. The synergistic effect between the active ingredients, which is characterized by antioxidant and antimicrobial activity in addition to the stimulant property of digestive enzymes, may be responsible for improving lactic acid bacteria in EAC1. This positively impacts the gut microbial ecosystem and nutrient utilization (Farahat et al., 2021). Flavonoids commonly found in leaf extract have been attributed to the inhibition of DNA gyrase and cytoplasmic membrane function and are acceptable as antimicrobial agents (Alvares-Martinez et al., 2020). The microbial community in the gut can positively or negatively affect gut health and performance. As harmful bacteria, coliforms can increase gut thickness, accelerate the gut tissue turnover rate, and compete with the host for feed nutrients. Lactobacilli is a beneficial bacteria that can inhibit pathogenic bacteria via competitive exclusion, bacteriocin or acid production, and immune system stimulation, improving bird growth and health (Yadav & Jha, 2019). Averrhoa carambola L. is attributed with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities (Y. Yang et al., 2020) which could be essential in enhancing gut health by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby bolstering overall gut health (Hossain et al., 2024). Phenolic and phenolic metabolites can shape the gut microbiota by inhibiting the growth of various pathogens and increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus (Aykin-Dincer & Dincer, 2024). Averrhoa carambola L. as phytobiotic feed additive did not change the count of coliform and LNE in ileum and cecum of broiler in the present study. This finding was different from Abouelezz et al. (2019) who reported that phytobiotic feed additive have been shown to modulate the gut microbiota by reducing phatogenic bacteria and reducing the cecal population of lactose-negative enterobacteria. This finding differed from that of another study, perhaps due to differences in nature of phytobiotic ration, enviromental condition, and others condition during study. Low doses of phytobiotics can promote the development of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus by providing essential nutrients and creating a favorable environment for their proliferation, at elevated concentrations, phytobiotics may exert antimicrobial properties (broad-spectrum effect) that can suppress Lactobacillus growth (Hashemitabar & Hosseinian., 2024).

Conclusion

Encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract, administered at dosages from 500 to 1500 mg/kg, has been shown to enhanced growth performance, as evidenced by increased body weight gain and improved feed efficiency. Supplementation at 1000 mg/kg positively influenced blood parameters, including reductions in leucocyte and lymphocyte levels as well as decreased uric acid concentration. Administration of 500 mg/kg of encapsulated Averrhoa carambola L. leaf extract was found to improve gut health and morphology, as indicated by an increase in the relative weight of the small intestine and elevated counts of lactic acid bacteria in the ileum. The leaf extract of Averrhoa carambola L. is rich in active compounds such as flavonoids, saponins, steroids, and tannins, which possess potential antioxidant, immune-enhancing, anti-inflammatory, and digestive benefits. Further research is needed to determine the optimal dosage and assess its impact on meat product quality.
