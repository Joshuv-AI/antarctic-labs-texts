GbWRKY11 Enhances Verticillium Wilt Resistance Through Activating Jasmonic Acid Biosynthesis in Cotton
Authors: Jiale Chen, Haoran Yue, Hongwei Pei, Guoli Feng, Ningshan Wang, Nijiang Ai, Baoliang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe WRKY transcription factor is a key regulatory protein involved in defence hormone signalling and plays a pivotal role in plant hormone‐mediated disease resistance. However, the specific mechanism by which WRKY transcription factors regulate the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway to confer resistance against Verticillium wilt in cotton remains poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrated that GbWRKY11 expression in Gossypium barbadense was induced by both Verticillium dahliae and methyl jasmonate (MeJA), and its encoded protein functioned as a nuclear transcription activator. Functional analyses revealed that GbWRKY11 enhances Verticillium wilt resistance by modulating JA pathway‐related gene expression in both cotton and Arabidopsis. Exogenous MeJA application restored resistance in GbWRKY11‐silenced plants, further supporting its role in JA‐mediated immunity. Mechanistically, GbWRKY11 directly binds to the W‐box motif in the promoter of GbLOX5, a key JA biosynthesis gene, and activates its transcription. Silencing GbLOX5 compromised cotton resistance to Verticillium wilt, confirming the importance of JA synthesis in this defence response. Our findings elucidate the molecular mechanism by which GbWRKY11 mediates immune responses against Verticillium wilt, providing novel insights into the genetic resources associated with disease resistance in G. barbadense .Keywords: cotton, GbWRKY11, jasmonic acid, verticillium wilt

Introduction

Verticillium wilt (VW), one of the most destructive plant diseases, affects a broad range of crops including cotton, potato, tomato, eggplant and strawberry (Fradin and Thomma 2006). This soil‐borne disease is caused by the fungus Verticillium dahliae, which colonises plant roots and produces hyphae in xylem vessels, impairing water and nutrient uptake (Zhang et al. 2014) and severely reducing fibre yield and quality. V. dahliae microsclerotia can persist in soil for up to 14 years, posing a severe threat to cotton production. To date, no effective control measures exist, making the identification of disease‐resistance genes for breeding and use in production the most economical solution (Inderbitzin and Subbarao 2014).

Jasmonic acid (JA), a fatty acid‐derived phytohormone, mediates plant immunity against mechanical wounding, herbivores and pathogens (Chini et al. 2016). The biosynthesis of JA is initiated in chloroplasts, where α‐linolenic acid is converted to 12‐oxo‐phytodienoic acid (OPDA) through the sequential catalysis by lipoxygenase (LOX), allene oxide synthase (AOS) and allene oxide cyclase (AOC) (Wan and Xin 2022). OPDA is subsequently transported from chloroplasts to peroxisomes, where it is converted into (+)‐7‐iso‐JA via catalysis by 12‐oxophytodienoate reductase 3 (OPR3) and a series of β‐oxidation steps, thereby completing JA biosynthesis (Wasternack 2007). JA is conjugated with isoleucine by the enzyme jasmonic acid resistant 1 (JAR1) to form the bioactive hormone jasmonoyl‐isoleucine (JA‐Ile) (Wasternack and Song 2016). Under resting conditions, jasmonate zim‐domain (JAZ) proteins bind to transcription factors (TFs) such as myelocytomatosis 2 (MYC2) from the basic‐helix–loop–helix (bHLH) family and recruit transcriptional corepressors including novel interactor of JAZ (NINJA) and topless (TPL), thereby collectively suppressing the transcription of JA‐responsive genes (Zhai et al. 2013; Balbi and Devoto 2008). Under pathogen invasion or stress conditions, JA‐Ile is rapidly synthesised. The ubiquitin ligase coronatine‐insensitive 1 (COI1), a component of the SCFCOI1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, mediates ubiquitination of JAZ proteins. The ubiquitinated JAZ proteins are subsequently degraded by the 26S proteasome, thus activating MYC2 transcription and expression of JA‐responsive genes (Hu et al. 2023). Disruption of JA signalling in Arabidopsis thaliana increases susceptibility to pathogens (Fradin et al. 2011; Jiang and Yu 2016; Campos et al. 2014). In cotton, JA pathway components such as REVEILLE2 (GausRVE2) (Liu, Cai, et al. 2023), thaumatin‐like protein 1 (GhTLP1) (Zhou et al. 2023) and 12‐oxo‐phytodienoic acid reductases (GhOPR9) (Liu, Liu, et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2020) modulate VW resistance. Conversely, Brassinosteroid insensitive 2 (BIN2) negatively regulates VW resistance by interacting with JAZ proteins (Song et al. 2021). Notably, certain pathogens can exploit the JA pathway to facilitate infection (Thatcher et al. 2009, 2012; Shi et al. 2024).

WRKY TFs, among the largest TF families in plants, harbour a conserved WRKYGQK motif and zinc finger domains (C2H2 or C2HC) that bind W‐box cis‐elements to regulate stress responses (Javed and Gao 2023; Li et al. 2020). WRKY proteins integrate immune signals including salicylic acid (SA), JA, mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Wani et al. 2021; Goyal et al. 2023; Wang, Cheng, et al. 2023). While AtWRKY11/17 suppress immunity but upregulate AOS and LOX2 in Arabidopsis (Journot‐Catalino et al. 2006), OsWRKY72 negatively regulates rice immunity by repressing AOS1 (Hou et al. 2019). Conversely, VvWRKY1 enhances grape downy mildew resistance by activating LOX and JA biosynthesis (Marchive et al. 2013). In cotton, GhWRKY55/70 regulate VW resistance via JA signalling (Ma et al. 2024; Xiong et al. 2019), yet the mechanistic links between WRKY TFs and JA genes in cotton–V. dahliae interactions remain poorly understood.

Here, we demonstrate that GbWRKY11 positively regulates VW resistance via JA biosynthesis in cotton and Arabidopsis. Mechanistically, GbWRKY11 binds the GbLOX5 promoter to activate JA biosynthesis. Our findings reveal a dual regulatory mechanism underlying GbWRKY11‐mediated resistance and provide insights for cotton breeding.

Results

GbWRKY11 Confers VW Resistance in Cotton Through JA Pathway

WRKY TFs are key regulators of plant immunity, modulating defence‐related gene expression through binding to W‐box promoter elements (Javed and Gao 2023). Our transcriptome analysis identified GbWRKY11 as a V. dahliae‐responsive gene in Gossypium barbadense roots, with reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) validation showing significant pathogen induction (Figure S1a). Tissue‐specific expression profiling revealed constitutive GbWRKY11 expression across all examined cotton tissues (Figure S1b). Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated close homology between GbWRKY11 (GB_A07G2733) and Arabidopsis AtWRKY11 (Figure S1c), a known negative regulator of Pseudomonas syringae immunity that modulates JA biosynthesis genes (Fuenzalida‐Valdivia et al. 2024; Journot‐Catalino et al. 2006). Functional characterisation showed that the GbWRKY11 promoter was strongly activated by both V. dahliae and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) (Figure S1d,e), suggesting JA pathway involvement; dual‐luciferase assays confirmed GbWRKY11's ability to transactivate W‐box‐containing promoters (Figure 1a–c); and subcellular localisation revealed nuclear accumulation of GbWRKY11‐GFP, consistent with its predicted transcriptional regulatory function (Figure 1d).

GbWRKY11 Positively Regulates VW Resistance via JA Biosynthesis

To functionally characterise GbWRKY11's role in VW resistance, we performed virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) in cotton. Following successful silencing (Figure 2a,b), VIGS plants inoculated with V. dahliae showed increased disease susceptibility (leaf chlorosis, elevated disease index), higher fungal biomass in roots (Figure 2c–e), enhanced stem vascular discolouration and fungal recovery (Figure 2f,g). Molecular analysis revealed that GbWRKY11 silencing significantly downregulated JA biosynthesis genes (AOS, LOX5, AOC4), JA signalling components (COI1, JAZ1), and pathogenesis‐related genes (PR1, PR5) (Figure 2h). Notably, MYC2 expression remained unchanged, suggesting GbWRKY11 acts independently of this core JA regulator.

The protective function of GbWRKY11 was further validated by transiently overexpressing the gene in susceptible Junmian1 cotton using the virus‐induced gene overexpression (VOX) system. For the VOX system, CLCrV:SU was used as a positive control and successfully induced leaf yellowing (Figure S2a). The overexpression of GbWRKY11 was verified by RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure S2b). Consistently, the transgenic plants showed a reduction in disease symptom severity (Figure S2c–f) and an upregulation in the expression of JA pathway genes (LOX5, AOC1/4, AOS, COI1) and PR1 (Figure S2g).

Collectively, GbWRKY11 positively regulates VW resistance through modulation of JA pathway genes, but is independent of MYC2 regulation. Both silencing and overexpression studies provide complementary evidence.

Ectopic Expression of GbWRKY11 Confers VW Resistance in Arabidopsis

To validate GbWRKY11's conserved function in VW resistance, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis overexpressing this cotton WRKY TF. No developmental phenotype changes were observed in GbWRKY11‐overexpressing A. thaliana (Figure 3a). Significant GbWRKY11 overexpression was confirmed by RT‐qPCR (Figure 3b). Enhanced disease resistance was indicated by reduced disease symptoms (leaf chlorosis/wilting) (Figure 3c) and lower disease index and fungal biomass (Figure 3d,e).

To investigate whether ectopic expression of GbWRKY11 affects the JA pathway during immune responses in A. thaliana , we analysed JA‐related gene expression levels by RT‐qPCR. The results demonstrated that JA biosynthesis genes (AOS, AOC1, LOX5) were upregulated, the expression of defence markers (PDF1.2, PR1, PR5) increased, but there were no changes in LOX2 or MYC2 expression (Figure 3f). This cross‐species validation demonstrates that GbWRKY11's resistance function is conserved in Arabidopsis, which operates through JA pathway modulation. Its mechanism is independent of MYC2 regulation.

MeJA Treatment Partially Restores VW Resistance in GbWRKY11 ‐Silenced Plants

Given the crucial role of JA signalling in defence against necrotrophic pathogens (Li et al. 2022; Wan and Xin 2022), we investigated whether exogenous MeJA could compensate for GbWRKY11 silencing‐induced susceptibility. RT‐qPCR confirmed effective GbWRKY11 silencing (Figure 4a,b). Plants were then treated with 200 μM MeJA via soil drenching alongside standard V. dahliae inoculation, using double distilled water as a mock control. After 25 days, MeJA‐treated plants exhibited attenuated disease symptoms (Figure 4c), reduced disease index, lower root fungal biomass, less severe vascular browning and slower fungal recovery rates (Figure 4d–g). This pharmacological complementation experiment provides reliable evidence that GbWRKY11's role in VW resistance operates primarily through JA signalling and likely functions upstream of JA biosynthesis.

Based on the results, we suggest that MeJA partially restored resistance in silenced plants and GbWRKY11 function is directly linked to JA‐mediated defence even if recovery was incomplete compared to empty vector controls.

GbWRKY11 Directly Activates GbLOX5 to Enhance VW Resistance

Given the significant alterations in JA pathway‐related gene expression observed upon either silencing or overexpression of GbWRKY11, we hypothesise that GbWRKY11 participates in the regulation of JA biosynthesis. Using PlantCARE (PlantCARE, a database of plant promoters and their cis‐acting regulatory elements), two W‐box cis‐regulatory elements were identified in the GbLOX5 promoter (Lescot 2002), suggesting GbLOX5 as a putative downstream target of GbWRKY11 (Table S2). To determine whether GbWRKY11 activates the GbLOX5pro, dual‐luciferase (LUC) assays were performed. These assays showed GbWRKY11 that strongly activated GbLOX5pro (Figure 5a–c). A yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assay confirmed direct binding of GbWRKY11 to the P2 promoter region (Figure 5d). An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) further revealed that the WRKY11‐MBP fusion protein bound to a biotin‐labelled probe containing the TTGACC motif at the P2 site of the GbLOX5 promoter, and the binding could be inhibited by the competitive probe but not by a mutant probe (Figure 5e, Figure S3). VIGS‐mediated silencing of GbLOX5 compromised VW resistance (Figure 5f–l) and the phenotypic effects mirrored those of GbWRKY11 silencing.

Therefore, GbLOX5 is a direct transcriptional target of GbWRKY11. This WRKY‐LOX regulatory module is essential for JA‐mediated resistance, which provides a mechanistic link between GbWRKY11 and JA biosynthesis. Our findings reveal a crucial regulatory module in cotton's defence against VW.

Discussion

Genome‐wide identification of WRKY family members has been accomplished in numerous plant species, with extensive investigations into their roles in defence responses (Khoso et al. 2022). WRKY TFs participate in diverse aspects of plant immunity through either recognition of W‐box cis‐elements in promoters or protein–protein interactions, and also regulate plant growth/development and abiotic stress responses (Goyal et al. 2023; Song et al. 2023; Mahiwal et al. 2024; Charvin et al. 2023). In A. thaliana , 49 WRKY genes were induced by pathogens or the defence‐related phytohormone salicylic acid (SA), underscoring their critical involvement in defence signalling (Yang et al. 2025). Recent studies demonstrated that AtWRKY7, AtWRKY11 and AtWRKY17 function as negative regulators of immune responses, modulating both JA pathway signalling and alternative splicing events in Arabidopsis (Fuenzalida‐Valdivia et al. 2024). Our study establishes GbWRKY11 as a key positive regulator of VW resistance through JA pathway modulation, contrasting with its Arabidopsis homologue AtWRKY11's known role as a negative immune regulator. GbWRKY11 promotes resistance, suggesting species‐specific neofunctionalisation in cotton, adaptation to vascular pathogen pressure and differential regulation of JA signalling components. This finding expands our understanding of WRKY TF evolution and functional diversification in plant immunity (Figures 2 and 3, Figure S2).

Mechanistically, the JA signalling pathway exhibits intricate crosstalk with WRKY TFs during plant immunity. For instance, OsWRKY31 modulates SA, JA and indole acetic acid (IAA) homeostasis via phosphorylation‐ and ubiquitination‐dependent mechanisms to coordinate blast resistance in rice, while OsWRKY72 binds the OsAOS1 promoter to negatively regulate JA biosynthesis (Hou et al. 2019; Wang, Han, et al. 2023). In cotton, GbWRKY1, GhWRKY33 and GhWRKY70 collectively mediate VW resistance through JA pathway regulation (Li et al. 2014; Ji et al. 2023; Xiong et al. 2019).

Our study demonstrates that GbWRKY11 directly activates the JA biosynthetic gene GbLOX5 (Figure 5a–e) but is independent of MYC2 in JA pathway modulation. Exogenous MeJA application partially restored resistance against V. dahliae in GbWRKY11‐silenced cotton plants (Figure 4). Silencing GbLOX5 significantly compromised VW resistance (Figure 5f–l), These findings collectively suggest that GbWRKY11 modulates disease resistance through JA homeostasis regulation. JA exhibits differential roles against distinct pathogen types. Conventional understanding suggests that JA plays a positive role in plant defence against necrotrophic pathogens and herbivorous insects, while it negatively regulates resistance to biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens (Mengiste 2012). However, studies have demonstrated that certain necrotrophic pathogens can exploit the JA pathway to facilitate their colonisation. For instance, the coi1 mutant exhibits enhanced resistance to Fusarium oxysporum in Arabidopsis (Thatcher et al. 2009). Additionally, tenuazonic acid produced by Alternaria and other phytopathogenic fungi can induce JA biosynthesis and signalling to accelerate infection (Shi et al. 2024). To comprehensively characterise the functional network of GbWRKY11, future studies will require transcriptomic profiling of both GbWRKY11‐silenced cotton and transgenic Arabidopsis lines. Such approaches will elucidate potential crosstalk between GbWRKY11 and other defence‐related pathways, ultimately defining its multidimensional role in VW resistance.

WRKY TFs orchestrate plant immunity through cooperative interactions among family members, often mediated by reciprocal transcriptional regulation via W‐box cis‐regulatory elements in their promoters. For instance, studies in A. thaliana have demonstrated synergistic action among AtWRKY7, AtWRKY11 and AtWRKY17. The wrky7/11/17 triple mutant exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to bacterial pathogens compared to wrky7/11 and wrky11/17 double mutants, revealing synergistic action within this WRKY regulatory module (Fuenzalida‐Valdivia et al. 2024). Previous studies have revealed that cotton GhWRKY41 not only binds W‐box elements within its own promoter but also forms homodimers to establish a positive feedback loop enhancing VW resistance (Xiao et al. 2023). This autoregulatory pattern is evolutionarily conserved, as evidenced by CaWRKY40b, PcWRKY1, CsWRKY33, all demonstrating capacity to self‐regulate through promoter binding during immune activation (Ifnan Khan et al. 2018; Turck et al. 2004; Wang, Li, et al. 2023). The promoter of GbWRKY11 contains multiple W‐box cis‐regulatory elements (Table S3), suggesting potential binding sites for autoregulation or other WRKY family members. This indicates that GbWRKY11 may collaborate with additional WRKY TFs to co‐regulate JA pathway activation and VW resistance in cotton.

In this study, we demonstrate that GbWRKY11 functions as a positive regulator of VW resistance in both cotton and A. thaliana by modulating the JA biosynthesis. Further investigations revealed that GbWRKY11 directly binds to the GbLOX5 promoter, collectively forming a regulatory module to enhance disease resistance (Figure 6). These findings provide novel insights for breeding VW‐resistant cotton cultivars.

A proposed working model illustrating the regulation of Verticillium wilt resistance by GbWRKY11 via the jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis pathway in cotton. Upon recognition of Verticillium dahliae invasion by cell membrane‐localised pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), immune signals are transduced to the nucleus, where GbWRKY11 binds to the GbLOX5 promoter to promote its transcription, thereby activating the JA pathway to enhance resistance.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

In this study, we used the following plant materials: resistant G. barbadense ‘Hai 7124’, susceptible Gossypium hirsutum ‘Junmian 1’, A. thaliana ecotype Columbia‐0 (Col‐0, wild type) and Nicotiana benthamiana. Cotton and N. benthamiana were grown at a 16‐h light/8‐h dark photoperiod under cool‐white fluorescent light (120 μmol photons m−2 s−1) with 70% relative humidity at 30°C ± 2°C/25°C ± 2°C (day/night) conditions. Arabidopsis plants were subjected to a 10‐h light/14‐h dark photoperiod under cool‐white fluorescent light (120 μmol photons m−2s−1) with 70% relative humidity at 22°C ± 1°C conditions.

Identification of WRKY11‐Related Proteins in Arabidopsis

We performed BLASTp algorithm searches against the A. thaliana database (TAIR—Home) using the full‐length protein sequence of GbWRKY11 as the query. Phylogenetic analysis was conducted in MEGA X (neighbour‐joining method; 1000 bootstrap replicates) (Kumar et al. 2018).

Verticillium dahliae Preparation and Inoculation

The V. dahliae strain V991 was grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) (25°C, 4–5 days) then transferred to potato dextrose broth (200 g/L potato, 12.5 g/L glucose, pH 7.0) (25°C, 150 rpm, 4 days). Hyphae were filtered and spores diluted to 1 × 107 spores mL−1 for inoculating cotton and A. thaliana . Cotton plants were inoculated using the impaired‐root method (Gong et al. 2018). For A. thaliana , roots were inoculated by root‐dipping (90 s) followed by replanting in their original soil.

Evaluation of Resistance to V. dahliae

Resistance was assessed by foliar symptoms, stem vascular discolouration, fungal biomass, fungal recovery assays and disease index (DI) (Gong et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2011; Liu, Cai, et al. 2023).

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Assays

Total RNA was extracted from cotton and A. thaliana tissues using the RNApure Fast Plant Kit (CWBIO). First‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed with the HiFiScript All‐in‐one RT Master Mix for qPCR (CWBIO). The synthesised cDNA was diluted to 100 ng/μL for qPCR analysis. qPCRs were conducted using SuperStar Universal SYBR Master Mix (CWBIO) on a LightCycler 480 System (Roche). For normalisation, GbHistone (cotton) and AtActin (Arabidopsis) were employed as internal reference genes. RT‐qPCR analysis was carried out according to the published standard protocol (Nolan et al. 2006). The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

VIGS and VOX

Coding sequences of GbWRKY11 and GbLOX5 were obtained from the Cotton database (http://cotton.zju.edu.cn). Gene‐specific fragments (300–500 bp) of GbWRKY11 and GbLOX5 were cloned into pTRV2 (EcoRI/BamHI) (Gao et al. 2013) and transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (OD600 = 1.8–2.0). TRV:00 (empty vector) and TRV:CLA served as controls. The CLCrV (Cotton Leaf Curl Multan Virus) vector was used for transient overexpression in G. hirsutum Junmian 1. The full‐length coding sequence of GbWRKY11 was cloned into the CLCrV (SpeI/AscI) vector (Gu et al. 2014) and transformed into A. tumefaciens LBA4404. They were filtrated into 10‐day‐old cotton seedlings. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Genetic Transformation of Arabidopsis

The full‐length coding sequence of GbWRKY11 was ligated into pBI121 (XbaI/SmaI) and transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 to transfer into Arabidopsis. Transgenic A. thaliana plants were generated using the floral dip method (Clough and Bent 1998). The primers used in this study are detailed in Table S1.

Subcellular Localisation

GbWRKY11 was ligated into pBInGFP (KpnI/SmaI) and transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The recombinant strain was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves, followed by 48 h incubation. Subcellular localisation of GbWRKY11‐GFP fusion protein was observed via confocal microscopy (Leica; TCS SP8) using NLS‐RFP as a nuclear marker. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Dual‐Luciferase Assay

The dual‐luciferase assay was performed following previously reported protocols with minor modifications (Zhao et al. 2024). Vectors of GbWRKY11 in pBI121 (XbaI/SmaI) (effector) and GbLOX5 promoter or 5×W‐box‐mini35Spro in pGreenll‐0800 (SmaI/BamHI) (reporter) were constructed. The empty pBI121 vector served as the negative control. Co‐transformed constructs were delivered into N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium infiltration (OD600 = 0.6–0.8) and incubated for 48 h. Firefly luciferase (LUC) signals were detected using an in vivo plant imaging system (LB985 Night SHADE). Subsequently, the relative LUC activities were quantified using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Y1H Assays

GbWRKY11 was ligated in pGADT7 (EcoRI/BamHI) vector (prey). The GbLOX5 promoter W‐box region was constructed in pAbAi (KpnI/SalI) (bait). Yeast transformation and interaction validation were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol for the Y1HGold‐pAbAi Yeast One‐Hybrid interaction kit (Coolaber). The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

EMSA

The full‐length coding sequence of GbWRKY11 was cloned into the pMAL‐c5x (EcoRI/HindIII) vector and expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta. Recombinant protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16°C for 24 h in Luria Bertani medium with shaking. The WRKY11‐MBP fusion protein was purified using amylose resin (NEB, E8021V). A biotin‐labelled DNA probe encompassing the W‐box cis‐element at the P2 site of the GbLOX5 promoter was synthesised. Purified WRKY11‐MBP protein was incubated with this probe using a Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Signal detection and imaging were carried out with a Fusion X7 chemiluminescence detector.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using GraphPad or Excel (Student's t‐test; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ns, not significant, p > 0.05).
