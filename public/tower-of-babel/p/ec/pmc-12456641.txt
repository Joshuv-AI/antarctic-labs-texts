Large left internal jugular vein determined during cardiac surgery: A case report
Authors: Kadİrİye S. Elden, Helİn Ozler, Hasan Hepaguslar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This case report presents the significant enlargement of the left internal jugular vein (IJV) detected during ultrasound (US)-guided central venous catheterization (CVC) in an adult patient undergoing cardiac surgery. An 81-year-old female patient who had severe mitral valve regurgitation, severe tricuspid valve regurgitation, and pulmonary hypertension was scheduled for Mitral Valve Replacement and DeVega Tricuspidoplasty. The patient had many comorbidities, so she was on antihypertensive (nebivolol), antithrombotic (rivaroxaban), and antidiabetic (insulin) medications. After anesthesia induction, the patient was positioned for CVC. Ultasound guidance showed that the anteroposterior diameter of the right IJV was small and the degree of overlapping of carotid artery (CA) was high. Left side was evaluated before starting the procedure. Marked enlargement of the left IJV and less overlapping of CA was observed. Left IJV catheterization was decided and performed without any complication. The surgery lasted for 3 hours and was completed uneventfully. Although right IJV is usually preferred for CVC where central intravenous access is required, it is advisable to evaluate the left IJV when the right one has a small diameter and there is a significant overlapping of right CA. In these cases, the choice for CVC side should be based on the data which is obtained from both sides.

Introduction

We aimed to report a case in which the significant enlargement of the left internal jugular vein (IJV) was detected during ultrasound (US)-guided central venous catheterization (CVC) in an adult cardiac patient.

Case Report

An 81-year-old female patient weighing 45 kg, 165 cm tall with a BMI of 20 kg/m2, was scheduled for Mitral Valve Replacement and DeVega Tricuspidoplasty. The patient had several coexisting diseases. She had hypertension, coronary artery disease (a stent in the circumflex artery), congestive heart failure, type 2 diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and hypothyroidism.

During physical examination, she had no chest pain but had dyspnea. The patient had bilateral pretibial edema (+2). Atrial fibrillation rhythm with a heart rate of 95 beats/min was noted on ECG. In addition, left axis deviation and minimal anterolateral ST depression were observed. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe mitral regurgitation, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and pulmonary hypertension, and her LVEF was 50–55%.

Laboratory investigations indicated hemoglobin as 10.8 g/dL, hematocrit as 32.6%, and fasting blood glucose as 140 mg/dL. Mild elevation in renal function tests (BUN 30.1 mg/dL, creatinine 1.22 mg/dL, e-GFR 42) was noted. Liver function tests and electrolytes were within normal values.

The patient was on antihypertensive (nebivolol), antithrombotic (rivaroxaban), diuretic (furosemide), antidiabetic (insulin) medications, and thyroid hormone replacement (levothyroxine) therapy. She had recently been under antibiotic treatment because of pneumonia.

After applying standard monitoring, intravenous access was established, and the patient was sedated with midazolam (1 mg). Radial artery cannulation under local anesthesia with lidocaine was performed. Anesthesia was induced with fentanyl (5 µg/kg) and pentothal (5 mg/kg); then, rocuronium (1 mg/kg) was administered for endotracheal intubation.

The patient was placed in the Trendelenburg position, a cylindrical pillow was placed under the shoulders, and slight left rotation and extension were applied to the head for ease of CVC. To evaluate the right IJV under sterile conditions, the US probe (M7 Ultrasound, Mindray, China) was placed at the cricoid level, perpendicular to the skin in the transverse section (90 degrees in the antero-posterior plane) and with its entire surface in contact with the skin. Care was taken to ensure that US probe did not cause any pressure to the IJV. It was observed that the anteroposterior and transverse diameters of the right IJV were found to be small, and the degree of overlapping of carotid artery (CA) was approximately 40% [Figure 1a].

Since the small diameter of IJV and high risk of CA puncture considered a difficulty in venous catheterization from the right side, left side US imaging was obtained after obtaining sterile conditions. Ultrasound guidance showed us marked transverse enlargement of the left IJV compared with the right side and also, less overlapping of CA (approximately 5%) [Figure 1b]. Then, CVC was successfully performed from the left IJV at the first attempt without any complication. The surgery lasted for 3 hours and was performed uneventfully. The patient was transferred to the cardiothoracic intensive care unit. Tracheal extubation was performed at the 3rd postoperative hour. The patient was transferred to the ward on the 2nd postoperative day.

Discussion

In our case, CVC was not performed from the right IJV due to the possible difficulty of catheterization because of smaller diameter of right IJV and the risk of CA puncture because of significant overlapping right CA. We performed CVC successfully from the left side which was found larger in diameter of the IJV compared with that of the right side.

Internal jugular vein is the primarily chosen route in case central intravenous access is required. In addition, the right IJV is generally preferred because it has many advantages over the left IJV.[123]

Main reasons for choosing the right IJV catheterization are the larger diameter of the right IJV, direct route to the superior vena cava and right atrium, and lower risk of thoracic duct injury.[145] A survey conducted in France revealed that 97% of anesthesiologists choose the right side for CVC.[6] Moreover, left IJV cannulation is supposed to be more difficult with a higher rate of complications.[3]

In 2009, Tartiere et al.[6] found that the right IJV had a significantly larger anteroposterior diameter and larger cross-sectional area than the left one in the adult population. Lobato et al.[7] showed that the cross-sectional area of the right IJV was greater than that of the left IJV in the majority of healthy volunteers. Similarly, Bos et al.[8] reported that larger diameter and cross-sectional area of the right IJV and less overlapping of the right CA than those of the left IJV at the level of the cricoid cartilage in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Such findings have also been confirmed in intensive care patients.[9]

Postoperative review of coronal view of chest in our case by computed tomography scan showed significant larger diameter of the left IJV when compared with the right one. Any external compression causing the larger diameter of the left IJV could not be detected [Figure 2]. We thought that the larger left IJV might have occurred as a result of a congenital anatomical variation.

Coronal CT of the chest demonstrating that no external compression of the left brachiocephalic vein as shown by arrow

Pathological stenosis surrounded by abnormal venous collaterals or physiological thinness of the left IJV were reported as anatomical variations causing larger diameter of the left IJV than the right one in Thai adults.[10]

Most experts agree that ultrasound guidance plays an important role in accurately locating the vein without causing any damage to neighbour vascular structures.[278] In our case, ultrasound not only revealed the difference in diameter between the right and left IJVs but also highlighted the anatomical differences in the localization of the CA. The degree of overlapping of CA of the right side in our patients contradicts the findings of BOS et al.’s study[8] in which they reported a lower incidence of overlapping between the right IJV and the right CA compared with the other side.

When the right side IJV has smaller diameter and/or more overlapping of right CA is observed, it is advisable to evaluate the left IJV before beginning the procedure and consider the CVC side based on the obtained data from both sides.
