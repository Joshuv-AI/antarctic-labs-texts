Perceived Impacts and Concerns in the Daily Life of People with Chronic Kidney Disease: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Ana Rita Pedro, Beatriz Raposo, Ana Soraia Cunha, Catarina Simões, Daniela Brandão, Hugo Martinho, Filipa Bernardo, Marisa Pardal, João Couceiro, Edgar Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) significantly impacts individuals’ daily lives, affecting physical health, psychological well-being, social interactions, and autonomy. Understanding the concerns and impacts of CKD is essential to creating patient-centred care strategies. This study aimed to explore the perceived impacts and concerns of individuals living with CKD.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with individuals with CKD and healthcare professionals in Portugal. Data were analysed using Framework Analysis to identify key perceived concerns and impacts of CKD on daily life.ResultsThe findings are structured around two main analytical themes – perceived impacts and perceived concerns. Within each theme, analytical categories were identified to capture the range of participants’ accounts. The study revealed that CKD has a significant impact on multiple aspects of daily life. Eighteen categories of perceived impacts were identified, encompassing challenges in maintaining social relationships, family dynamics and professional responsibilities, as well as impacts on autonomy, quality of life, and economic stability. Psychological and emotional distress were also frequently reported. Physical health impacts included fatigue, dietary restrictions, sleep disturbances and limitations in physical activity. Regarding perceived concerns, nine categories comprising a total of sixty-six items were identified. Participants reported concerns related to personal, social and family life, lifestyle changes, and the preservation of autonomy. Concerns related to the disease itself and to complications arising from it were also highlighted. The remaining categories focused on concerns regarding treatment, including haemodialysis/dialysis, kidney transplantation and pharmacological treatment.ConclusionThis study highlights the complex and multidimensional impact of CKD on daily life. By providing insight into patient-defined concerns and perceived impacts, the findings support the discussion of more holistic and person-centred approaches to CKD care. Addressing personal needs alongside clinical management may contribute to improvements in quality of life and long-term outcomes for individuals with CKD.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities in kidney function or structure (or both) present for more than 3 months with associated health implications, typified by a slow deterioration in kidney function, which causes waste materials to build up and fluid imbalances in the body [1]. CKD is a major global public health challenge, affecting more than 10% of the world’s population and contributing to significant morbidity and mortality [2]. Similar prevalence rates are observed in Portugal, where it is estimated that around 1 million people are affected (∼10% of the population) [3]. Because of ageing populations and the growing incidence of risk factors, including obesity, diabetes, and hypertension, its prevalence continues to rise [4, 5]. Untreated CKD can progress into end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), requiring kidney transplantation or dialysis, both of which place substantial financial, physical, and psychological strains on individuals and healthcare systems [6–8].

Beyond its physiological manifestations, CKD presents complex challenges affecting multiple life domains, even if well managed. Individuals with CKD deal with a variety of issues that impact physical and mental health, social interactions, employment, and financial security [8–10]. The disease frequently demands dietary restrictions, lifestyle changes, and adherence to intricate treatment regimens. These requirements are challenging to maintain as poor adherence to medication, diet, and other treatments is common in CKD [11, 12]. The difficulties experienced by those who have CKD are further exacerbated by the condition’s association with mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression, sleep disorders, fatigue, and cognitive impairment [13–15].

Healthcare professionals play a crucial role in CKD management and the support of individuals with CKD [16]. Yet their perceptions of the challenges individuals with CKD face may differ from those of the individuals. While nephrologists, nurses, and other healthcare professionals often prioritise disease progression, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes, individuals may be more concerned with autonomy, mental well-being, and social participation [17, 18]. This potential perception gap underscores the need for comprehensive, patient-centred approaches to CKD care that address both clinical and psychological dimensions. Within this context, deepening the knowledge base on how individuals experience their disease helps elucidate patient perspectives, informing discussions of care practices that are more closely aligned with lived experience.

While a large amount of research has looked at the lived experiences of individuals with CKD, especially regarding quality of life, symptom load, and treatment-related difficulties, much of the literature has traditionally focused on clinical outcomes and treatment efficacy. Comparatively little focus has been placed on obtaining a thorough and integrated knowledge of the wider effects of CKD across several life domains or on considering the viewpoints of both healthcare professionals and people with CKD [19–21]. This emphasis reflects, in part, the fact that CKD is often diagnosed at a late stage [20] and that, in earlier stages, it is challenging to determine whether signs and symptoms are attributable to CKD itself or to other comorbid conditions or factors as the biological basis of symptom manifestation in early disease is less well understood [21]. Consequently, fewer studies have considered the full spectrum of challenges faced by individuals with CKD across all stages of the disease. A more in-depth exploration of these issues, including early stages of the disease, is essential for informing healthcare policies, improving support services, and developing interventions that address not only disease management but also the broader challenges associated with living with CKD.

This research provides a thorough and comprehensive assessment of the main impacts and concerns of CKD on daily life, as perceived by both individuals living with CKD and healthcare professionals. Rather than distinguishing between these two groups, the study aimed to capture a broad understanding of the experiences, challenges, and priorities associated with CKD beyond clinical outcomes. By providing a comprehensive understanding of patient-defined concerns and perceived impacts, this study has the potential to inform the development of more targeted interventions aimed at improving care delivery, quality of life, and overall well-being among people living with the disease.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employs a qualitative research design using semi-structured interviews to identify perceived concerns and perceived impacts of CKD on daily life. Although both individuals with CKD and healthcare professionals were interviewed, the analysis did not aim to compare these groups. Instead, it focused on identifying and contextualising concerns and impacts as they relate to the lived experience of CKD. This study was conducted as part of the broader CONCORDIA CKD project, which aims to deepen the understanding of patient experiences and needs across the spectrum of CKD.

Inclusion Criteria and Recruitment of Participants

Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure diverse perspectives from individuals with CKD and healthcare professionals. The sampling followed the rule of maximising the difference in characteristics and perspectives of the participants.

The inclusion criteria for individuals with CKD were individuals living with CKD with no indication for haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, pre-dialysis, or kidney transplantation, aged 18 years or older, and residing in Portugal. The inclusion criteria for healthcare professionals were actively practicing in Portugal and providing care to individuals with CKD.

Participants with CKD were recruited through an external provider specialising in research and market research, with experience in recruiting participants for scientific studies. Healthcare professionals were recruited from a database held by the research team.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted in a semi-structured format using a predefined interview guide, which allowed participants to express their experiences in their own words while ensuring that key topics relevant to the study objectives were systematically explored. Separate but conceptually aligned interview guides were used for people living with CKD and healthcare professionals. For individuals living with CKD, participants were asked (1) what they considered to be their main concerns regarding chronic kidney disease and (2) how CKD impacted their daily lives. For healthcare professionals, the questions focused on their perceptions of (1) the main concerns of individuals living with CKD and (2) the impact of CKD on patients’ daily lives. The concepts of concerns and impacts were intentionally left open and explored based on participants’ accounts, supporting the use of a qualitative approach to enable an in-depth exploration of lived experience [22, 23]. Follow-up and probing questions were used when appropriate to encourage elaboration and explore emerging topics in greater depth. All interviews were conducted individually and remotely via the Zoom platform. With participants’ informed consent, the sessions were audio recorded and had an approximate duration of 30–40 min. Given that both individuals with CKD and healthcare professionals were interviewed using comparable interview guides addressing the same themes, thematic saturation was assessed across the dataset as a whole rather than separately for each informant group [24].

Data Analysis

An external provider specialising in research and market research, with experience in transcription, was responsible for the full verbatim transcription of all interviews, following predefined transcription rules to ensure consistency and standardisation in the application of formatting, punctuation, anonymisation labels, and notation of pauses or non-verbal expressions. The interviews were conducted and the data analysed by the research team.

Data analysis followed the principles of the Framework Analysis [25]. All interviews were transcribed verbatim. The analytical process comprised the following stages: transcription; familiarisation with the interviews, which included reading the transcripts and reviewing analytic notes; coding; development of a working analytical framework; application of the analytical framework; and interpretation of the data. The research team reached consensus regarding the final analytical categories within each theme.

Analysis was conducted at a semantic level, focussing on the explicit content of participants’ accounts. The analysis of the content was based on what participants explicitly expressed, without seeking to go beyond the data [26].

Although the concept of impacts and concerns was initially left open to allow participants to define what they specifically meant to them, during data analysis, the working distinction was based on impacts as lived effects of CKD and concerns as fears, expectations, or uncertainties that the disease brings.

MAXQDA 2022® software was used as a support tool for organising, managing, and structuring the qualitative data throughout the analytical process, particularly during coding, framework development, and application of the analytical framework.

Results

Characteristics of the Participants

Among the ten participants with CKD, sex was equally distributed, and the mean age was 45.7 years (SD = 14.30), with ages ranging from 21 to 69 years. Six participants had a university-level education, while four had completed secondary education. Time since diagnosis varied substantially, from approximately 18 months to 30 years. None of the participants underwent haemodialysis, dialysis, or pre-dialysis, and none had an indication for kidney transplantation. Participants predominantly presented comorbid conditions commonly associated with CKD, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and obesity. Most participants were managed conservatively through lifestyle measures and pharmacological treatment; however, treatment approaches varied widely. These ranged from dietary management alone (1 participant), to clinical follow-up without medication (2 participants), to complex pharmacological regimens including one or more of the following: antihypertensives, antidiabetics, iron supplementation, diuretics, vitamin D, lipid-lowering agents, and uric acid-lowering medications (7 participants). The healthcare professionals who participated in the study were 60% female. They represented a diverse range of specialities (three nurses, three nephrologists, and four general practitioners). The professionals were engaged in different healthcare contexts, including hospital consultations, dialysis clinics, and primary care units.

Overview of Findings

The findings are structured around two main analytical themes – perceived impacts and perceived concerns. Within each theme, analytical categories were identified to capture the range of participants’ accounts. To ensure clarity regarding the source of each quotation, a coding system was adopted, with P1–P10 identifying participants living with the disease and P11–P20 identifying healthcare professionals.

Perceived Impacts

Regarding the first theme, the analysis identified specific impacts across a broad range of areas, encompassing 18 categories. Participants described difficulties in maintaining relationships and having a social life (“Of course I often think about it daily… for example, when I was younger, and I would go out and be with my friends and socialise and I would think… ‘I've got CKD, and I can't drink alcohol like my friends do’… I used to worry about that.” – P03), as well as strain on family life and the need for increased support from family members (“Often, it even affects the family itself… if they are patients who, for example, are already retired, they sometimes end up needing family support to take them to an appointment, and then it also affects the family because the children also have to be absent or have to adjust.” – P11).

Challenges in working life, such as maintaining employment, work productivity, and job security, were also mentioned (“There are people who work, who have an active life, and have to combine their work with the clinic’s timetable. So, there’s an impact here because you have to worry about the time from here to work…” – P16). Psychosocial and emotional well-being were significantly affected, with increased levels of anxiety, depression, and emotional distress (“…psychological issues… more anxiety, more depression, all of which can also have an impact.” – P10). Physical well-being was also impacted, with fatigue, pain, and other health-related limitations frequently mentioned (“…is one of the frequent complaints of CKD patients, in addition to tiredness, shortness of breath… swelling, oedema and shortness of breath.” – P15).

Quality of life was generally reduced, with participants reporting things like lower life satisfaction and a diminished ability to engage in meaningful activities (“But I think there will be another constraint when dialysis starts… it could affect quality of life.” – P10, referring to this as an anticipated scenario). Nutrition was affected by the necessity for strict dietary modifications and restrictions (“We haven't had salt in anything for many years… since I was 8 years old… I'm 21 now…” – P08), and adjustments to daily routines and/or tasks were often required to accommodate treatment schedules (“If I go anywhere, the medication follows… the fact that I always have a bottle of water next to me, whether I feel like it or not…” – P05).

Autonomy and independence were compromised due to disease progression and treatment demands (“But then, obviously, as time goes by, the dependency becomes more blatant… people then begin to internalise the dependency and then the phase of loss of quality of life, loss of autonomy, of all those aspects linked to independence begins, which becomes very exuberant indeed” – P14), and the economic impact was notable, with increased financial burdens resulting from medical expenses and potential loss of income (“The monthly money coming home [income] is going to be lower because they can't work as much, and if they do, there are more absences… in other words, the income goes down a lot” – P13).

Self-image and self-esteem were affected, with changes in body perception and confidence levels (“It has an impact when I want to put on an outfit and I don't feel… of course it has an impact. My belly gets higher [i.e., becomes more swollen/bloated], but that's it… we have to work on that” – P01). Leisure activities were reduced, with individuals participating less in recreational pursuits (“The fact that people have a catheter in their belly can limit their ability to go to the beach, to do some sporting activities… and people, especially younger people, who are more eligible for this technique, see it as a constraint” – P18). Travel and trips were restricted due to treatment and health constraints (“Yes, the practice of booking haemodialysis for where I'm going on holiday, isn't it? Because you can't go those days without doing it…” – P02, referring to this as an anticipated scenario), and self-care became more challenging, affecting personal hygiene and health maintenance (“Of course, all this can be adjusted over time and they will adapt, but for some more or less, it always requires some changes in these hygiene issues.” – P11).

Intimate and sexual life were negatively impacted, affecting romantic and sexual relationships (“… can affect the quality of life, particularly in aspects such as love relationships, sexuality itself can be affected…” – P10). The ability to engage in physical exercise was diminished (“Sometimes I'd like to do a bit more exercise and I can't get my body to respond…” – P10), and disruptions in quality of sleep and rest were common (“Sometimes I wake up at night in pain and can't get back to sleep” – P06). Finally, impacts on family planning and/or pregnancy were expressed, highlighting another important dimension of CKD's impact (“Because pregnancy can have a negative impact on kidney disease, accelerating its progression, and because the outcome of the pregnancy itself can also be worse for the foetus.” – P18).

Perceived Concerns

In the second theme, participants identified a range of concerns related to CKD, spanning 9 categories. These categories were then divided into multiple items, making up 66 perceived concerns.

Concerns related to personal, social, and family life included changes in quality of life, professional and family life, limitations in vacations and leisure activities, and concerns about burdening family and friends (“Whether or not it will have an impact on my family life” – P03). Economic concerns, hereditary aspects of the disease, family planning, and reduced life expectancy were also highlighted (“Oh, and that life expectancy decreases, of course.” – P04). Many participants mentioned the stigma and misunderstanding from others regarding CKD (“Sometimes people feel stigmatised because they're constantly pricked [having blood drawn or needles inserted] and often go to hospital… if they have something contagious… the people they live with think they might have something contagious because of it…” – P17), as well as challenges in adjusting daily routines (“Concerns me in the sense that it might inhibit me from some everyday activity” – P06), ensuring social support in advanced stages of the disease, and sleep disturbances.

Concerns related to lifestyle included weight control, dietary restrictions, water intake, alcohol consumption, and the ability to engage in physical exercise (“For example, another aspect that people are very concerned about is physical exercise, sports, the problem that this might have on kidney disease, etc.” – P14).

Concerns related to psychological well-being encompassed self-image and body perception, change in psychological well-being, and cognitive decline (“I used to be able to read a book for three hours straight and now I don't concentrate as much… I have to repeat the pages because it's a cognitive decline that doesn't allow me to concentrate as well as I used to.” - P10). Concerns related to autonomy and functionality included the development of physical disabilities, dependence on possible future dialysis or other individuals, driving ability (“… they may feel more tired and then many are afraid that they won't be able to drive… others have physical limitations and aren't able to at all, even if they wanted to…” – P11) and medication dependence, and the availability of sanitary facilities during travel. “So I always have to be close by, or I always have to have somewhere with access to a toilet.” – P07).

Concerns related to the illness and healthcare were also prominent. Participants expressed concerns such as distress over the lifelong nature of CKD, its silent progression, and uncertainty about disease evolution (“That's my concern… the unpredictability of the disease and the impact it could have on the stage of life I'm in at the time.” – P03) and kidney function. Gaps in therapeutic innovations and adequacy of information provided by healthcare professionals were also noted, particularly regarding sexuality, mental health, and self-care (“The health centre in my hometown, where I lived, wasn't very informed about our disease… I knew it existed, but I didn't know about these concerns… I, having this disease, was never asked to do a blood test…” – P08). Access to healthcare, specialist availability, and transportation was another recurring theme (“This is also one of the biggest concerns for a patient because the transports take a long time to pick up the patients… often they drop them off early at the clinic and they have to wait 1 hour, 1 hour and a half for their turn to be treated… then 4 hours of treatment and then maybe another 1 hour or 2 hours waiting for the ambulance to pick them up to take them home” – P16).

Concerns regarding complications arising from CKD or other illnesses included managing blood pressure and/or diabetes, pain related to the disease and/or treatment, along with symptoms and manifestations of CKD, hospitalisation risks, fear of death, and managing multiple chronic conditions (“But, yes, I think I'm very young and these three diseases are serious illnesses… It makes me a little worried” – P05, referencing CKD, diabetes and hypertension). Participants also mentioned concerns about the impact of CKD on receiving treatment for other conditions, as well as the effects on sexual health (“One or two people talk about sexuality… but that's still something… practically nobody talks about it, but they have also shown concern, especially the younger ones.” – P16) and kidney failure.

Concerns of invasive treatments such as haemodialysis and dialysis were also referred (“The main concern when people come to the clinic with kidney disease is whether or not they are at risk of haemodialysis.” – P15), with participants expressing concerns about the burdensome nature of these treatments, the time-consuming nature of them, possibly experiencing symptoms despite undergoing haemodialysis, and the physical effects of haemodialysis/dialysis equipment. Issues surrounding vascular access for treatment and concerns about lifelong dialysis dependency were also specified (“An adjustment that I know won't be easy and that will last a lifetime.” – P10, referring to this as an anticipated scenario). Some participants mentioned concerns about the ability to perform dialysis at home due to technical and practical challenges (“When there's peritonitis, of course it's always an incident where they often blame themselves… ‘what did I do, I didn't do anything wrong’… there's a load of responsibility here that can lead them, over time, to burnout…” – P11).

Concerns related to invasive treatment of kidney transplantation included potential rejection, inability to receive a transplantation, risks associated with the procedure, health complications before transplantation, waiting list positioning, lack of a donor, and the post-donation health of a living donor (“There's also a transplantation phase, the immediate surgical phase, which is also a tough period because there's the surgical part, there's the risk of infection, there's the risk of rejection, there's a whole series of aspects…” – P14).

Concerns related to drug treatment included the strict medication regimen, kidney burden from medications, starting or changing therapies, drug interactions, polypharmacy, long-term effects of medication use, and side effects (“I already take 5 pills a day. I had this idea that when people take this medication, it's because of their age… I still don't feel old enough to make this constant routine part of my daily life.” – P05).

Discussion

The study’s conclusions offer a thorough grasp of the complex effects of CKD, which include issues with physical, psychological, social, economic, and lifestyle factors. The wide-ranging and interrelated character of the difficulties associated with CKD is highlighted in this study because participant narratives often linked difficulties across multiple domains, suggesting that impacts were perceived as interconnected rather than isolated. The findings support the idea that CKD is a complex, life-altering illness that impacts almost every element of a person’s well-being rather than just being a medical ailment [27, 28].

The impact of CKD on autonomy and day-to-day functioning was mentioned as well. Managing daily routines, keeping a job, and maintaining an active social life were all common challenges mentioned by participants. These results are in line with other research showing that the cost of care, regular checkups, and the progressive nature of the illness can all lead to a greater reliance on others and a loss of independence [29, 30]. The study also supports prior research on the economic impact of CKD, demonstrating that the financial burden of managing the disease – including medication costs, dietary requirements, and potential loss of income – further exacerbates stress and diminishes quality of life [31, 32].

Another important aspect that surfaced was the emotional and psychological impact that CKD can bring. Aspects such as loneliness, anxiety, body image, and cognitive deterioration are reported by the participants. This supports the necessity for psychological support as a crucial component of CKD treatment and is consistent with the body of research on the mental health impact of chronic diseases [33, 34].

Concerns about social misunderstanding and stigma were also common, indicating that more work is required to raise public knowledge and strengthen social support networks for those with CKD. The study also sheds light on concerns regarding disease progression, treatment burden, and healthcare accessibility. Numerous participants highlighted the emotional toll of having a “silent” illness and the unpredictable nature of CKD development. Furthermore, the concerns about the suitability of information given by medical professionals – specifically regarding sexuality, mental health, and long-term self-care – indicate a need for better education of individuals with CKD and communication between them and healthcare professionals.

In terms of treatment-specific aspects, this study emphasises the concerns associated with possible future dialysis and drug treatment plans, such as difficulties with time commitment, physical side effects, and drug interactions. These results are consistent with other studies on the discomfort associated with dialysis, highlighting the necessity of supporting measures to enhance quality of life while undergoing treatment [35, 36]. Concerns with kidney transplantation, such as concerns about complications, rejection, and even the health of the living donor after donation, also highlight how crucial it is for individuals with CKD and healthcare professionals to communicate openly and honestly when making transplantation decisions.

The study’s conclusions have important implications for patient support systems, healthcare policy, and clinical practice. Understanding the concerns of individuals living with CKD and the impacts of the disease can enable healthcare services to be more responsive to patients’ real needs and aligned with the healthcare system capabilities and its stakeholders to be closer and more effective. This study expands current knowledge beyond isolated outcome measures and offers a more thorough understanding of unmet needs throughout the disease trajectory by systematically mapping perceived impacts and perceived concerns across a wide range of life domains and by integrating patient and healthcare professional perspectives. Additionally, the findings also point to several areas in which participants reported unmet informational and communication needs. Available evidence indicates that patients with chronic conditions often hold subjective concerns, interpretations, and informational needs that are not fully elicited or addressed during routine clinical encounters (e.g., [37, 38]). Furthermore, increasing evidence has highlighted the importance of patient-centred communication and shared understanding, demonstrating that recognising and exploring patients’ subjective perceptions is essential to improve communication quality and align perspectives during clinical interactions (e.g., [37–39]).

The breadth of categories identified in the Results section, spanning psychological, economic, lifestyle, and functional impacts, as well as others, highlights the need for multidisciplinary care approaches to CKD treatment [40, 41]. A more holistic approach to care should be taken by healthcare practitioners, emphasising autonomy, well-being, and quality of life in addition to illness control [42]. As reflected in the Results, participants reported gaps in the adequacy of information provided by healthcare professionals. These issues surfaced in the “illness and healthcare” domain, suggesting that non-clinical elements of living with CKD may not be sufficiently addressed by existing communication strategies. Filling up the information gaps about the course of CKD, available treatments, and lifestyle changes may enable individuals with the disease to make better decisions and increase adherence to management plans [43, 44]. CKD is often associated with poor patient adherence, particularly in its early stages. Understanding patients’ needs and how they experience their disease may be crucial to promoting their engagement in their own care [45]. Furthermore, fostering peer support networks and offering mental health assistance may lessen the psychological toll of chronic kidney disease and feelings of loneliness [46, 47].

While this qualitative study provides an in-depth understanding of the perceived concerns and perceived impacts of living with CKD, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, as with all interview-based qualitative research, the findings reflect the perspectives of those who chose to participate, which may introduce selection bias. Second, although semi-structured interviews were used to ensure consistency across interviews, the interaction between interviewer and participant may have influenced the narratives produced; however, this relational aspect is a recognised and unavoidable feature of qualitative research. The relatively high educational level of the participants may also influence the nature of the concerns expressed, potentially limiting the transferability of the findings to populations with lower educational attainment, among whom CKD is more prevalent [48].

Longitudinal evaluations of the effects of CKD should be investigated in future studies, monitoring shifts in priorities and concerns as the disease progresses. Furthermore, additional research using quantitative approaches may complement the qualitative findings presented in this study, allowing for the identification of broader trends and associations between disease severity and specific concerns or impacts. In this regard, a key next step of the present research project is a quantitative study, conducted through an online survey directed at individuals living with CKD, aimed at evaluating the perceived concerns and impacts of the disease and identifying the factors associated.

In conclusion, this study highlights the complex and far-reaching impact of CKD on individuals’ lives, recognising the complexity of living with the disease, which extends beyond medical concerns to encompass psychological, social, economic, and functional challenges. By providing a comprehensive understanding of patient-defined concerns and perceived impacts, this study not only contributes to expanding the evidence base on the lived experience of CKD but also has the potential to inform the development of more targeted interventions aimed at improving care delivery, quality of life, and overall well-being among people living with the disease. These findings reinforce the importance of more holistic and person-centred approaches to CKD care that consider not only clinical management but also the psychological, social, economic, and functional dimensions identified in this study. The integrated consideration of these dimensions plays a central role in improving overall well-being and long-term outcomes for people living with CKD.

Statement of Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the National School of Public Health of the NOVA University Lisbon (CE-ENSP), which granted ethical approval (CE-ENSP no 15/2022), and all participants provided informed consent prior to taking part and authorisation for audio recording of the interviews. In accordance with the legal limits and where applicable, participants’ data subjects had the right to withdraw their consent, request rectification of their data, request erasure, limitation of processing, object to opposition to processing, request data portability, and submit a complaint to the CNPD or other competent body. The data collected can only be accessed and processed by the researchers involved in this project and is stored on a password-protected digital device with antivirus and security software. No personal identifying data were recorded or stored at any stage of the study. All interview data were anonymised immediately after transcription. Only the principal investigator has access to the anonymisation key and is, therefore, the only person able to reverse the anonymisation process, if strictly necessary. All researchers are bound by professional secrecy, and personal data are handled with strict confidentiality. Any scientific communication of the results of this study will ensure that participants cannot be readily identified. The data will be retained for 5 years after the completion of the study and will be used solely for research purposes.
