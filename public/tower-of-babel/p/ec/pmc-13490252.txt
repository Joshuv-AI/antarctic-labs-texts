Detection of microaneurysms causing macular edema associated with branch retinal vein occlusion using optical coherence tomography and optical coherence tomography angiography and the effect of focal photocoagulation
Authors: Taiji Hasegawa, Mizuha Kakehashi, Moeko Kawai, Ichiro Maruko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine the efficacy of focal photocoagulation for microaneurysms causing macular edema associated with branch retinal vein occlusion. We also compared the efficacy of treatment with fluorescein angiography (FA) as a method of targeting microaneurysms with the use of optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA).MethodsThis retrospective study included 45 eyes with macular edema caused by microaneurysms. All eyes received focal photocoagulation for microaneurysms, with FA used to detect microaneurysms in 19 eyes and OCT/OCTA in 26 eyes, depending on the treatment time. Eyes with only focal leakage on FA and eyes with edema relatively confined to the microaneurysm area on OCT/OCTA were included in this study. Eyes without macular edema in the fovea after 12 months of focal photocoagulation alone were classified as responsive. Eyes with residual edema or with additional anti-VEGF therapy during the study were classified as unresponsive.ResultsTen eyes (52.6%) in the FA group and 12 eyes (46.2%) in the OCT/OCTA group were responsive (P = 0.6676). In responsive eyes, both FA and OCT/OCTA groups had significantly improved visual acuity at 12 months. In unresponsive eyes, visual acuity improved significantly in the FA group but not in the OCT/OCTA group.ConclusionThe combination of OCT and OCTA can be used to identify edema-causing microaneurysms without the use of FA. However, compared to FA, OCT and OCTA alone may not clearly distinguish between cases in which focal photocoagulation is indicated and those in which it is not.Keywords: Microaneurysm, Focal laser photocoagulation, Optical coherence tomography angiography, Branch retinal vein occlusion, Telangiectatic capillaries

Introduction

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) is a relatively common retinal vascular disorder, and it is often accompanied by macular edema [1, 2]. It has been shown that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays a major role in the macular edema in eyes with BRVO by increasing the vascular permeability [3, 4]. In fact, many studies have shown that anti-VEGF therapy rapidly reduces macular edema and improves visual acuity [5, 6]. Currently, anti-VEGF therapy has become the first-line treatment for eyes with macular edema associated with BRVO. However, there are some eyes in which anti-VEGF therapy is less effective in resolving macular edema [7–9].

In the chronic stage of BRVO, after the intraretinal hemorrhages have resolved, microvascular abnormalities such as microaneurysms develop in the area of the BRVO [10]. These abnormal vessels can contribute to macular edema. Previous study has reported that the mean number of anti-VEGF treatments was significantly higher in eyes with microaneurysms secondary to BRVO than in eyes without microaneurysms [7]. We have also reported that eyes with only focal leakage from microaneurysms on fluorescein angiography (FA) images tend to resist anti-VEGF therapy [9]. Then, previous studies have reported that focal laser photocoagulation is effective in treating macular edema caused by microvascular abnormalities [10–13].

Recent advances in optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA) have made it possible to evaluate the fine structure of retinal tissue and the fine structure of the vascular network [14]. Then, many studies have compared the detection of microaneurysms by OCT and OCTA with that by FA, which has long been the gold standard examination to detect the retinal vascular changes [15–17]. It has been reported that the number of microaneurysms seen with OCTA is less than that seen with FA [17–19]. A previous OCT study showed that 90% of microaneurysms with severe leakage on FA were surrounded by cystoid spaces with edema on OCT [18]. It is considered important to use not only OCTA but also OCT B-scan and OCT en face image to find microaneurysms to be treated.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine the efficacy of focal laser photocoagulation in eyes with macular edema caused by microaneurysms associated with BRVO. In addition, we compared the efficacy of treatment using FA as a method of targeting microaneurysms versus using OCT and OCTA imaging.

Patients and methods

Patients and examinations

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tokyo Women’s Medical University Hospital. The procedures used in this study conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients after explanation of the procedures to be used and possible complications. This study included 45 eyes with macular edema associated with BRVO caused by microaneurysms. All eyes received focal laser photocoagulation for microaneurysms causing macular edema. Eyes were included if they had macular edema associated with microaneurysms and a central foveal thickness (CFT) of 250 μm or greater as measured by OCT. FA (TRC-50DX; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) or OCT and OCTA were performed to determine the microaneurysms to be treated. Swept-source OCT (DRI-OCT; Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to obtain cross-sectional images passing vertically through the fovea. The affected retinal thickness was defined as the retinal thickness 1.5 mm from the fovea on the side of the BRVO as seen in a vertical OCT section. In this study, baseline was defined as the follow-up visit at which the first focal laser photocoagulation for microaneurysms was performed. Patients whose follow-up was less than 12 months after baseline were excluded from this study.

Examination method for detecting microaneurysms causing macular edema

All patients were examined at the Tokyo Women’s Medical University Hospital from April 2016 to March 2023. We performed FA or OCT and OCTA to determine the microaneurysms to be treated. Each patient was examined with one of two patterns depending on the time of initial focal laser photocoagulation in our department. From April 2016 to March 2019, 19 eyes used FA imaging to detect microaneurysms causing macular edema and were considered the FA-guided group; from April 2019 to March 2023, 26 eyes used OCT and OCTA imaging instead of FA imaging and were considered the OCT/OCTA-guided group.

In the FA-guided group, eyes with only focal leakage from microaneurysms were included in this study (Fig. 1). Eyes with both focal leakage from microaneurysms and moderate or severe leakage from the occluded vessel were excluded. OCTA images were obtained using the AngioVue OCTA system (Avanti; Optovue Inc, Fremont, California, USA). OCTA scans of 3 × 3 mm or 6 × 6 mm were used to evaluate the presence of microaneurysms in the superficial, deep, and full thickness layers of the retina. OCT B-scans and en face OCT images acquired simultaneously with the OCTA scans were also used to evaluate the presence of microaneurysms appearing as annular hyperreflective lesions. The presence of cystoid spaces adjacent to microaneurysms on OCT has been reported to correlate with a positive leakage status of microaneurysms on FA [18]. Therefore, microaneurysms adjacent to cystoid spaces showing edema in the OCT/OCTA-guided group were identified as microaneurysms to be treated with focal laser photocoagulation. In the OCT/OCTA-guided group, only cases in which the edema was relatively confined to the area surrounding the microaneurysms were considered microaneurysms-related edema and were included in the study (Fig. 1).

FA-guided and OCT/OCTA-guided focal laser photocoagulation

Focal laser photocoagulation was performed with a 577 nm yellow laser (IQ577; IRIDEX, Mountain View, California, USA). The parameters were as follows: spot size 50 μm; pulse duration 50-70 msec; and laser power 60-80 mW using an Area Centralis contact lens (Volk Optical Inc, Mentor, Ohio, USA). Out of the parafoveal area (> 1500 μm), we sometimes used a spot size (100 μm). All laser photocoagulation procedures were performed by the same surgeon (T.H). Additional laser photocoagulation was performed if no regression of microaneurysms and improvement of macular edema was observed one month after laser photocoagulation. If macular edema did not decrease 3 months after initial laser photocoagulation, anti-VEGF therapy was added after obtaining patient consent. Eyes with no edema in the fovea after 12 months of focal laser photocoagulation alone were classified as the responsive eyes, and eyes with residual edema in the fovea after 12 months or with anti-VEGF therapy added during the study were classified as the unresponsive eyes.

Statistical analyses

Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was measured with a Landolt chart, and decimal acuities were converted to logarithm of the minimal angle of resolution (logMAR) for statistical analyses. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to determine the significance of differences in patient characteristics between responsive eyes and unresponsive eyes to focal laser photocoagulation. The relationship between response to focal laser photocoagulation and the presence of previous treatment or the method of microaneurysm identification was analyzed by chi-square tests. Fisher’s exact probability test was used when the expected cell count was less than 5. Baseline and 12-month measurements were compared using Wilcoxon signed rank tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with JMP software (version Pro 18; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Results

All 45 eyes received focal laser photocoagulation for microaneurysms to treat macular edema associated with BRVO. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the patients included in this study. FA was used in 19 eyes and OCT/OCTA in 26 eyes to determine microaneurysms requiring treatment. Prior to the first focal laser photocoagulation, 36 (80.0%) of 45 eyes had undergone previous treatment for macular edema. Thirty-two eyes received intravitreal injections of ranibizumab or aflibercept, 4 eyes were treated with sub-Tenon injections of triamcinolone acetonide. Nine eyes had no treatment prior to baseline.

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was created, and the cut-off values for the Youden index were calculated. This allowed for the determination of the number of targeted microaneurysms that could potentially resolve macular edema with focal laser photocoagulation therapy, as well as the degree of macular edema. The area under the curve (AUC) for the ROC curve for the number of microaneurysms was 0.71. Meanwhile, the AUCs for baseline CFT and affected retinal thickness at 1.5 mm were 0.84 and 0.74, respectively. Analysis of cut-off values revealed that focal laser photocoagulation therapy could potentially resolve macular edema if the microaneurysm target count was three, the baseline CFT was 524 μm, and the affected retinal thickness at 1.5 mm was 443 μm.

At 12 months from baseline, the mean BCVA was significantly better in the responsive eyes than in the unresponsive eyes (P = 0.0006). The responsive eyes showed a significant improvement in BCVA at 12 months compared to baseline, while the unresponsive eyes showed no significant improvement between baseline and 12 months (P < 0.0001, P = 0.3983, respectively). In the unresponsive eyes, 16 of 23 eyes were received additional anti-VEGF therapy and the other 7 eyes did not receive additional treatment despite the presence of residual edema at the fovea.

Comparisons between FA-guided laser group and OCT/OCTA-guided laser group

In Table 3, the differences between the FA-guided and the OCT/OCTA-guided groups are examined separately for responsive and unresponsive eyes. In responsive eyes, there were no significant differences between the FA-guided and OCT/OCTA-guided groups at baseline or at 12 months. In responsive eyes, both FA-guided and OCT/OCTA-guided groups had significantly improved visual acuity and reduced CFT at 12 months compared to baseline. In the unresponsive eyes, visual acuity improved significantly and CFT decreased significantly in the FA-guided group at 12 months compared to baseline (P = 0.0313, P = 0.0039, respectively). In contrast, visual acuity did not improve and CFT did not significantly decrease in the OCT/OCTA-guided group (P = 0.6406, P = 0.0564, respectively).

Discussion

Our results showed that focal laser photocoagulation was able to eliminate macular edema in 22 (48.9%) of 45 eyes in which only the microaneurysms were found to be involved in macular edema. The number of targeted microaneurysms was significantly lower in the responsive eyes than in the unresponsive eyes (P = 0.0142). Subsequently, baseline CFT and affected retinal thickness at 1.5 mm were significantly lower in the responsive eyes than in the unresponsive eyes (P < 0.0001, P = 0.0052, respectively). Thus, in cases where the number of microaneurysms is small and the degree of macular edema is not severe, focal laser photocoagulation is likely to eliminate macular edema. In our previous study of BRVO, we reported that macular edema caused by microaneurysms was much less responsive to anti-VEGF therapy than macular edema caused by exudation from the entire BRVO lesion [9]. Therefore, we believe that focal laser photocoagulation should be considered as a treatment option in chronic cases where microaneurysms are the cause of macular edema.

It has been reported that the walls of fresh microaneurysms in eyes with diabetic retinopathy initially appear thin with endothelial proliferation [20]. As the wall of the microaneurysm thickens, a number of degenerative changes occur, including endothelial cell loss, periodic acid hyalinization, and the appearance of fat in the wall [20]. Although anti-VEGF therapy has been shown to reduce the number of microaneurysms in eyes with diabetic macular edema, it is less clear which microaneurysms are more likely to disappear [21, 22]. Mori and colleagues speculate that fresh microaneurysms, which occur with endothelial cell proliferation, are sensitive to anti-VEGF therapy, whereas advanced microaneurysms have hyaline and fatty deposits and are much less sensitive to anti-VEGF therapy [21]. In this study, 32 of 45 eyes received anti-VEGF therapy prior to the initial focal laser photocoagulation, and the microaneurysms we treated in this study may have had characteristics of advanced microaneurysms. Recently, Castro Farías and colleagues proposed a new nomenclature using the term “telangiectatic capillaries (TelCaps)” to refer to microvascular lesions with diameters of a few hundred micrometers associated with various retinal vascular diseases [23, 24]. TelCaps are associated with chronic and refractory forms of macular edema and with the presence of hard exudates. They are better characterized by late-phase indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) than by FA [23, 24]. There are no clear criteria to distinguish microaneurysms from TelCaps. Since ICGA imaging was not performed in this study, it is impossible to assess the extent to which TelCaps are present. However, it is likely that larger, advanced microaneurysms and TelCaps share many characteristics.

The percentage of responsive eyes to focal laser photocoagulation was 52.6% (10/19 eyes) in the FA-guided-group and 46.2% (12/26 eyes) in the OCT/OCTA-guided group (P = 0.6676). In responsive eyes, there were no significant differences between the FA-guided and OCT/OCTA-guided groups at baseline or at 12 months. Previous studies have reported that the number of microaneurysms seen with OCTA is less than that seen with FA [15–17]. However, Schreur and colleagues reported that in diabetic macular edema, microaneurysms located in the thickened retina with focal leakage on FA were significantly more likely to be detected by OCTA than non-leaking microaneurysms located in the non-thickened retina [16]. Thus, microaneurysms associated with retinal edema may be more likely to be seen on OCTA than microaneurysms that do not cause edema. Previous studies reported that the presence of cystoid spaces adjacent to microaneurysms on OCT B-scan correlated with positive leakage status seen on FA [18, 19]. A study using en face OCT imaging reported that 87.5% of 320 leaking microaneurysms in eyes with diabetic macular edema on FA imaging matched microaneurysms on en face OCT imaging [25]. Nearly (97.5%) of the matched microaneurysms were associated with retinal edema [25]. Maltsev and colleagues reported that the efficacy of navigated focal laser photocoagulation of microaneurysms planned with en face OCT is a non-inferior alternative to FA [26]. Other previous studies also reported that a combination of OCT B-scan and OCT en face image is a useful method for identifying microaneurysms adjacent to cystoid spaces [24, 27]. In this study, OCT en face image and OCT B-scan were used in addition to OCTA to identify microaneurysms adjacent to the cystoid spaces. This may have been as good as the focal laser photocoagulation results for microaneurysms obtained with FA. Previous OCTA study has investigated the relationship between microaneurysm flow status and retinal edema [28]. Gao and colleagues reported that the probability of having fluid near microaneurysms in eyes with diabetic retinopathy was 80.1% when the OCTA blood flow signal was co-localized and 63.0% when it was not [28]. However, considering that a certain percentage of microaneurysms cause retinal edema even when the blood flow signal of the microaneurysm is not identified by OCTA, it is difficult to identify the microaneurysm that causes macular edema based only on microaneurysm blood flow. Therefore, we believe it is important to evaluate a combination of OCT B-scan, OCT en face image, and OCTA to identify the microaneurysms causing macular edema. Based on the results of this study, it is acceptable to select focal laser photocoagulation based on OCT and OCTA findings without performing FA in cases where edema is relatively confined to the area surrounding microaneurysms and the number of microaneurysms causing the macular edema is three or fewer.

In the unresponsive eyes, BCVA did not improve and CFT did not significantly decrease in the OCT/OCTA-guided group although visual acuity improved significantly and CFT decreased significantly in the FA-guided group at 12 months compared to baseline. In the unresponsive eyes, there may be two possible reasons why OCT/OCTA-guided group did not improve both visual acuity and CFT at 12 months. One is that OCT and OCTA alone may only partially identify edema-causing microaneurysms compared to FA in severe macular edema. In responsive eyes, the number of targeted microaneurysms was similar in the FA-guided group (2.8 ± 0.9) and OCT/OCTA-guided group (2.8 ± 1.8), whereas in unresponsive eyes, the number of targeted microaneurysms was lower in the OCT/OCTA-guided group (3.7 ± 2.1) than in the FA-guided group (5.0 ± 2.2). It has been reported that as central retinal thickness increases, the leaky microaneurysms on FA and the microaneurysms on en face OCT are less likely to coincide [25]. In this study, baseline CFT was significantly greater in the unresponsive eyes than in the responsive eyes. Therefore, it may have been more difficult to detect microaneurysms with en face OCT in the unresponsive eyes. Another is that the OCT/OCTA-guided group may have included more cases of macular edema with exudation from the entire BRVO lesion in addition to microaneurysms than the FA-guided group. In the unresponsive cases, the mean baseline affected retinal thickness at 1.5 mm was greater in the OCT/OCTA-guided group at 464.0 ± 121.1 μm and in the FA-guided group at 413.9 ± 105.2 μm. Previous FA study reported that the affected retinal thickness at 1.5 mm from the fovea is higher in cases of exudation from the entire BRVO lesion, whereas the affected retinal thickness at 1.5 mm is lower in cases of exudation from microaneurysms and the edema tends to be localized near the fovea [9]. Although this study attempted to exclude cases with moderate or severe exudation from the entire BRVO lesion based on FA and OCT/OCTA findings, it may have failed to exclude some of them in the OCT/OCTA-guided group (Fig. 4). When the type of macular edema is determined by OCT/OCTA alone without FA, it may not be possible to clearly distinguish between exudation from the microaneurysms alone and exudation from the entire BRVO lesion in addition to the microaneurysms. In such cases involving exudation from the entire BRVO lesion, focal laser photocoagulation alone, with no or delayed additional anti-VEGF therapy may explain the lack of significant improvement in visual acuity and significant reduction in CFT at 12 months in the unresponsive eyes. Therefore, if focal laser photocoagulation of targeted microaneurysms does not result in a reduction of macular edema, FA should be performed to determine if there is exudation from the entire BRVO lesion and the addition of anti-VEGF therapy should be considered.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias. As mentioned above, the OCT/OCTA-guided group may have included more cases of macular edema with exudation from the entire BRVO lesion in addition to microaneurysms than the FA-guided group. Second, all laser photocoagulation procedures in this study were performed by one surgeon. The results of laser photocoagulation may be influenced by the skill of the surgeon, and we believe that the reproducibility of the results of this study needs to be verified in a multi-surgeon study. Since it was recently reported that precise focal laser photocoagulation with a navigated system resulted in a higher rate of microaneurysm closure in diabetic macular edema than conventional manual laser photocoagulation [13, 26], we believe it is important to develop a system that does not depend on the skill of the surgeon.

In conclusion, focal laser photocoagulation therapy can be considered as a treatment option for macular edema associated with BRVO when it is caused only by microaneurysms. Furthermore, if the estimated number of microaneurysms causing the macular edema is three or fewer, focal laser photocoagulation can be selected based only on OCT and OCTA findings, without performing FA. Although OCT and OCTA alone may not be sufficient to differentiate between edema caused by microaneurysms alone and edema caused by the entire BRVO lesion in addition to microaneurysms compared to FA, we believe that the method of treating microaneurysms adjacent to cystoid spaces using OCT and OCTA as the cause of edema is effective.
