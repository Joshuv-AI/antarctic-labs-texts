Long-term anorectal function in rectal cancer patients managed by a watch-and-wait strategy after total neoadjuvant treatment: a cross-sectional study

Background

The standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) has traditionally been radiation-based neoadjuvant therapy followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) [1]. However, for patients who achieve a clinical complete response (cCR) to total neoadjuvant treatment (TNT), a non-operative approach known as the watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy may be an alternative. This strategy aims to potentially improve the quality of life and functional outcomes compared to those who undergo TME surgery. While there is increasing evidence of favorable oncological outcomes associated with the W&W approach [2, 3], the functional outcomes of the preserved rectum require further investigation.

In this study, we conducted a thorough evaluation of late rectal radiotoxicity and long-term anorectal function in patients who were managed with a W&W policy. We employed various assessment tools, including the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group/European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (RTOG/EORTC) scale, the Late Effects of Normal Tissues/Subjective Objective Management Analytic (LENT/SOMA) system, the Wexner score, the Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS) score and the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Bowel Function Instrument (MSKCC BFI). Furthermore, we investigated the risk factors contributing to long-term anorectal dysfunction.

Methods and materials

Patients

The study was approved by the institutional review board of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. We selected patients managed with the W&W strategy from a prospective database of primary rectal cancer patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) at our institute from December 2014 to November 2020. All W&W patients had reached cCR, defined as the absence of a clinically detectable primary tumor after TNT according to the criteria: (i) a flat white scar with telangiectasia and no ulcer or nodularity, (ii) a dark T2 signal with no visible lymph nodes on MRI, and (iii) no visible tumor on diffusion-weighted MRI [4]. Participants required a minimum of two years’ follow-up without disease progression. Exclusions were made for prior fecal incontinence, inflammatory bowel disease, stage IV disease, or incomplete data.

Total neoadjuvant treatment

The regimen for all patients included 5000 cGy administered in 25 fractions across 5 to 7 weeks, with concomitant use of sensitizing chemotherapeutic agents (capecitabine, capecitabine-irinotecan, or capecitabine-oxaliplatin), followed by 3 to 7 cycles of capecitabine-based consolidation chemotherapy. One patient with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status received immunotherapy, and a subset of patients underwent 1–3 cycles of induction chemotherapy with XELOX before radiotherapy. The overall strategy adhered to TNT principles, aiming to deliver all planned therapy before potential surgery. However, the number of consolidation chemotherapy cycles was individualized based on treatment response and patient tolerance. Although most patients completed a guideline-defined TNT, a minority received modified regimens.

Instruments

Late rectum toxicity was evaluated with the RTOG/EORTC grading and the LENT/SOMA scoring system, while long-term anorectal function was assessed using the Wexner score, LARS score, and MSKCC BFI. The RTOG/EORTC scale measures six grades of radiation toxicity, from no toxicity to side effect-induced death [5]. The LENT/SOMA system, developed in 1995, assesses radiation-induced side effects across four domains: subjective, objective, management, and analytic, on a scale from 0 (no toxicity) to 4 [6]. The Wexner scale evaluates fecal incontinence based on frequency across five dimensions, resulting in a score between 0 (no incontinence) to 20 (complete incontinence) [7]. The LARS score is a concise scoring instrument for the evaluation of bowel function after sphincter-preserving procedures with or without radiotherapy for rectal cancer. The score ranges from 0 to 42 points, with classification of patients into three groups: no LARS (0–20 points), minor LARS (21–29 points), and major LARS (30–42 points) [8]. As for MSKCC BFI, fourteen questions are grouped into three subscales—diet, urgency/soilage, and frequency - and four separate questions are added to obtain a total score ranging from 18 to 90, with a 90-point score corresponding to the best possible bowel function [9].

Study design

This cross-sectional study drew on medical records and patient interviews. Clinical and demographic data were sourced from medical records and institutional databases. Telephone interviews were conducted by professional radiation oncologists using a semi-structured interview guide based on the aforementioned scales. Patients were briefed on the study’s objectives, anonymity was assured, and informed consent was obtained. Disease status was confirmed, medical histories were reviewed, and inquiries were made regarding performance status, rectal complaints, bowel function, and other adverse effects. Defecation patterns over the preceding four weeks were recorded, and recent physical examination findings were reviewed to complete the LENT/SOMA objective assessment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were shown as means and standard deviations (SD) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). For continuous variables, two-tailed Student’s t-test for independent samples was used. Categorical variables were analyzed by Pearson’s chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered significant. In a bivariate regression model, clinical factors were added to determine whether they significantly altered the risk of developing LARS. The manuscript was prepared following Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline.

Results

Patient characteristics

98 rectal cancer patients managed with the W&W strategy initially identified were narrowed down to 70 for inclusion in this study. Reasons for exclusion comprised death, tumor regrowth or distant metastasis (25 patients), loss to follow-up (2 patients), and one declined participation (shown in Fig. 1).

CONSORT diagram of patient selection

Patients’ demographics are provided in Table 1. The cohort’s mean age was 57 years, with males constituting 70% (49/70). Patients with locally advanced rectal cancer (stage II ~ III) account for 78.6%. Fifty-six patients (80.0%) had low rectal cancer (tumor lying in < 5 cm from the anal verge), and fourteen patients (20.0%) had mid-high rectal cancer (tumor lying in 5–10 cm from the anal verge). The median tumor distance from the anal verge was 3 (IQR 2) cm. Fifty-eight patients (82.9%) patients completed TNT as planned. The median time from the end of TNT to anorectal function assessment spanned 44 months (IQR 38).

Late rectal radiotoxicity

Late rectal toxicity was assessed using both the RTOG/EORTC criteria and the LENT/SOMA system. As illustrated in Fig. 2, late rectal toxicity of grade I or higher occurred in 41.4% of the patients. Grade II toxicity was noted in a single patient who experienced moderate diarrhea managed with regular antidiarrheal medication. No cases of more severe toxicity were reported. Using LENT/SOMA criteria, mild symptoms were more frequently identified. The most common issue was sphincter control, affecting 60% (42/70) of patients, followed by increased stool frequency (27/70, 38.6%), pain (20/70, 28.6%), bleeding (18/70, 25.7%), tenesmus (16/70, 22.9%), and mucosal loss (13/70, 18.6%). Stricture and ulceration were rare, observed in 2.9% (2/70) and 1.4% (1/70) of patients, respectively. The median LENT/SOMA total score was 2 (IQR 4).

Long-term anorectal function

Long-term anorectal function was evaluated using the Wexner score, the LARS score, and the MSKCC BFI. Overall distributions are illustrated in Fig. 3 and detailed item-level results for each scoring system are available in Supplementary Tables 1–3. The median Wexner score was 2 (range 0–11, IQR 3). 27.1% (17/70) patients had no incontinence, while the maximum score observed was 11 in a single patient. Most patients (56/70, 80.0%) indicated that incontinence had “never” or “rarely” affected their lifestyle. The median LARS score was 16 (range 0–37, IQR 21). Minor LARS (scores 21–29) and major LARS (scores 30–42) were observed in 17.1% (12/70) and 15.7% (11/70) of patients, respectively. Regarding anal continence, the majority of patients maintained good anal control, with only 6 (8.6%) reporting uncontrolled flatus at least once per week and 1 (1.4%) reporting liquid stool leakage at least once per week. Bowel movement frequency was generally normal in 90.0% (63/70) patients, while 10.0% (7/70) experienced markedly increased frequency (4–7 times per day). Bowel urgency was reported by 44.3% (31/70) patients to varying degrees, a proportion slightly different from that indicated by the LENT/SOMA system due to differences in grading criteria (shown in Supplementary Table 2). The MSKCC BFI yielded a median total score of 82.5 (range 63–90, IQR 9), indicating generally preserved bowel function. Multivariate analysis for anorectal dysfunction

Patients were stratified into two groups by LARS score: Group 1 (scores 0–20, no LARS, n = 47) and Group 2 (scores 21–42, minor and major LARS, n = 23). Univariate analysis indicated smoking history, drinking history, tumor maximal diameter, and body mass index (BMI) as correlating with anorectal dysfunction (P < 0.2) (shown in Table 2). Given the established significance of tumor distance from the anal verge as a critical risk factor for anorectal dysfunction in TME patients, as evidenced by numerous studies [10, 11], this variable was retained for inclusion in the multivariate analysis. Smoking history emerged as an independent risk factor for LARS symptoms [odds ratio (OR) 6.562, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.561–27.590]. In multivariate analysis, drinking history appeared to be protective (OR = 0.138, 95% CI 0.029–0.654), although no significant association was observed in univariate analysis.

Discussions

This study evaluated late rectal toxicity and long-term anorectal function in rectal cancer patients adopting a W&W approach after TNT. Our findings indicate that approximately one-third of patients experienced LARS symptoms after a minimum of two years of follow-up, which is somewhat less severe than reported in prior studies [12, 13]. The predominant symptom was fecal urgency, and notably, there was an observed trend of poorer long-term anorectal function among patients with a history of smoking.

Literature on the bowel function outcomes of rectal cancer patients under W&W is scarce [12–16]. To our knowledge, this study is among the largest and most detailed investigations of functional outcomes in this setting. While late rectal toxicity was generally mild, a considerable number of patients reported anorectal complaints, particularly issues with sphincter control. The LENT/SOMA scoring system proved more sensitive than RTOG/EORTC grading in detecting late toxicity in W&W patients, cons istent with its multidimensional structure.

In international consensus recommendations on key outcome measures for organ preservation after (chemo)radiotherapy in patients with rectal cancer [17], various frequently used instruments, such as the Wexner score [7], the LARS score [8], the MSKCC BFI score [9], the Vaizey score [18], are considered for measuring anorectal function, while patient-reported LARS score is recommended as the best-available method of measuring anorectal function [17]. Nevertheless, neither scale is tailored for patients undergoing chemoradiotherapy exclusively, prompting us to supplement these scales with comprehensive patient interviews. This approach helped identify potential misalignments in the questionnaire items for W&W patients. For example, the MSKCC BFI dietary subscale was difficult for many patients to interpret, possibly due to cultural dietary variation or the less severe impact of chemoradiotherapy on bowel function compared with surgery. As neoadjuvant therapies evolve, the choice of a W&W strategy may become more prevalent, underscoring the need for a bespoke anorectal function evaluation system, akin to the efforts of Pennings AJ et al. [19].

As for clinical risk factors, our analysis suggests that smoking may independently increase the risk of long-term anorectal dysfunction. The general detrimental effects of smoking on vascular health, tissue repair, and inflammatory response might exacerbate the treatment-related impacts on anorectal function. Interestingly, drinking history appears be associated with better functional outcomes. Some studies suggest that moderate alcohol consumption could be beneficial to health through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [20], altering the gut microbiome composition [21], and cardiovascular benefits [22], which might contribute to better overall healing and tissue repair, including in the anorectal region. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as residual confounding related to drinking patterns, tobacco use, diet, and lifestyle cannot be excluded. Further research is warranted to clarify this relationship. Tumor proximity to the anal verge did not significantly affect anorectal function in this cohort, which might be attributed to the majority of our patients having low rectal cancer, with the furthest tumor distance being only 7 cm.

Comparative studies of W&W against other organ-preserving methods, such as transanal endoscopic microsurgery [16] and sphincter-preserving total mesorectal excision (SPTME) [14, 23], also indicated preferred functional results for W&W strategy. However, our study did not compare W&W with SPTME as the functional outcomes of TME have been well-documented, and our patient cohort would likely have undergone abdominal perineal resection (APR) rather than SPTME if not for the W&W strategy, rendering direct comparisons challenging.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center, cross-sectional analysis with a relatively small patient cohort, which may limit the generalizability. Second, the absence of baseline anorectal function data and serial follow-up assessments at multiple time points limited our ability to evaluate dynamic functional changes over time or perform trajectory analyses. Third, treatment heterogeneity should be acknowledged. Although most patients completed TNT, the number of consolidation chemotherapy cycles varied and one patient received immunotherapy. Finally, currently available instruments such as the Wexner score, LARS score, and MSKCC BFI were not specifically designed for patients managed with a W&W approach after chemoradiotherapy, and certain subscales (e.g., the dietary domain of the MSKCC BFI) appeared less applicable. These limitations highlight the need for larger, prospective, multicenter studies employing standardized assessment tools tailored to W&W patients.

Conclusion

In summary, rectal cancer patients who achieved cCR after TNT and were managed with a W&W strategy generally exhibited mild late rectal toxicity and preserved satisfactory long-term anorectal function, although fecal urgency and sphincter-related complaints might be common problems. Smoking history emerges as a significant independent risk factor for long-term anorectal dysfunction. These findings support the functional advantages of W&W in appropriately selected patients while underscoring the need for prospective, multicenter studies with standardized longitudinal assessments to validate these results and refine patient counseling.
