Radiomics in FAI: current status and perspectives
Authors: Hariharan Subbiah Ponniah, Eros Montin, Srikar Namireddy, Riccardo Lattanzi, Kartik Logishetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is caused by abnormal contact between the femur and acetabulum, resulting in pain, limited motion, and early osteoarthritis. Existing imaging techniques for diagnosing FAI face considerable challenges. Radiomics involves the quantitative extraction and analysis of imaging features using advanced algorithms, often combined with machine learning (ML), to enhance diagnostic and prognostic precision. When integrated with ML, radiomics can identify patterns beyond conventional imaging measurements, potentially enabling automated, precise, and reproducible assessment of hip morphology and pathology. Early studies demonstrate its potential to differentiate between normal, symptomatic, and asymptomatic cam-type hips. However, challenges persist, including the standardization of imaging protocols, feature selection, access to large datasets, and the explainability of models. Introduction

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is a clinical condition characterized by abnormal contact between the femur and acetabulum, leading to reduced range of motion (ROM), pain, and potential early osteoarthritis. Diagnosis of FAI, seen typically in active young adults, relies on clinical symptoms and physical examination correlating with imaging findings.1-3 Surgical treatment is indicated for patients who have failed conservative management and do not have radiological joint degeneration, with hip arthroscopy for these patients being the gold standard.4 Imaging for FAI includes plain radiographs of the hip joint and pelvis, CT, and MRI. Imaging is used to classify the type of FAI (classically cam-, pincer-, or mixed-type), assess rotational deformity, diagnose coexisting labral and chondral injury, and identify joint degeneration. Radiographs enable quantification of cam- and pincer-type morphology, including measurement of the hip joint angles,5-7 or the presence of signs of acetabular retroversion;8 but they lack sensitivity compared to multiplanar imaging.9,10 Radiographs can miss diagnoses of anterior and posterior over-coverage as well as fail to adequately account for femoral orientation and pelvic tilt, which can result in FAI even when femoral and acetabular morphology appear normal.10 CT enables quantification of FAI morphologies, including the calculation of lateral centre edge angle and α angle around the acetabular and femoral neck clock-face, respectively. It also facilitates measurement of femoral head coverage. However, CT exposes patients to radiation, and cannot assess injuries to the labrum and chondral surfaces: the sequelae of FAI bony morphology, associated with pain, and the harbinger for developing osteoarthritis.11 Thus, MRI is the recommended imaging modality.12,13 Despite advances in cartilage-specific sequences,14 MRI analyses remain qualitative and user-dependent, and thus have varying correlation with intraoperative findings.15-17 Developing MRI-based quantitative diagnosis of FAI sub-types and quantitative measurement of labral and chondral injury would be the gold standard, for which the emerging field of radiomics has potential.

Radiomics is a processing technique which transforms medical images into high-dimensional quantitative data. Radiomics can extract thousands of features from medical images that are not visually apparent.18,19 These features are broadly categorized into three classes: intensity features, shape and size features, and texture features. They can be analyzed using machine learning (ML) models to recognize patterns of disease. For FAI, radiomics may enable diagnosis and quantification of pathomorphologies using groups of features that go beyond current, manually performed angle measurements, recognition of anatomical signs, or qualitative descriptions of injury. It may enable more accurate diagnosis of FAI, novel patterns of disease, and predictors of outcome.

Origins of radiomics and machine learning

Brief history of machine learning

ML, a subset of AI, leverages mathematical models and algorithms to create automated systems for decision-making and predicting outputs from given data.20-22 Advancements in computational power and the ability to acquire larger datasets over the last 20 years have enabled ML to transform patient care in numerous medical fields, including orthopaedics.20 ML is broadly divided into two categories: supervised, which relies on labelled data for pattern recognition, and unsupervised, which identifies patterns in unlabelled data.20-22 DL, a specialized subset of ML modelled after the brain’s neural connections, employs convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to automatically extract and classify features from data and has proven particularly effective in medical imaging tasks such as pancreatic tumour detection, prostate tumour grading, cardiac chamber segmentation, predicting burn candidacy in plastic surgery, and fracture detection in orthopaedics.20-24

Brief history of radiomics

Radiomics, first introduced by Lambin et al25 in 2012, focuses on the quantitative analysis of medical images using various image characterization algorithms, resulting in high-dimensional data typically analyzed with ML techniques. It enables the identification of features beyond the ability of the human eye. It facilitates pattern recognition that enhances diagnostic and predictive accuracy, while mitigating the intra- and interobserver variability present in manual interpretation of medical imaging.18,19 The growth of radiomics can be attributed to advancements in ML and DL algorithms, increased computational power, and the availability of large high-quality shared imaging databases.26,27 The field of oncology is the earliest adopter of radiomics, which has enabled visualization and mapping of tumours, aiding in cancer diagnosis, recurrence risk prognostication, patient survival, assessment of metastatic potential, treatment response, and planning.28-31 Radiomics research is expanding to other medical disciplines, including neurology, cardiovascular disease, liver disease, and orthopaedics.32-37

Radiomic analysis pipeline

Typically, radiomic analysis consists of a pipeline comprising of several steps (Figure 1): image acquisition, image processing, segmentation, feature extraction, feature selection, and finally, model development.18,19 Radiomics can be performed on a variety of imaging methods ranging from radiographs to CT and MRI. To maintain reproducibility and reduce the impact of varying image acquisition parameters that occur in clinical settings, harmonization and normalization are often performed prior to further processing.19 Following this, segmentation of the image is conducted either manually or automatically using DL, outlining regions of interest (ROI) in 2D imaging or volume of interest (VOI) in 3D imaging to facilitate feature extraction. Pre-defined radiomic features are then extracted within each ROI/VOI from either original or filtered images.

Overview of the radiomic analysis pipeline. The process starts with image acquisition, followed by segmentation to delineate regions of interest. Preprocessing ensures standardized image data for consistent analysis. Radiomic features—such as shape, intensity, and texture—are extracted and analyzed using feature selection methods, including clustering and heatmaps, to identify the most relevant features for distinguishing outcomes. These selected features are then used to train machine-learning models, with their performance evaluated using metrics like the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.

Various types of radiomic features have been standardized by the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) in 2016,38 and can be broadly categorized into three classes: intensity and histogram based first order statistics (FOS) of voxel intensities, shape and size features, and texture features of the VOI/ROI.39 Texture features refer to the spatial relationships of voxels/pixels within a VOI/ROI and include the following (Figure 2): gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), which describes the likelihood of similar pixels appearing together; gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), which details the length of consecutive pixels with the same gray level; gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), which outlines the number of connected voxels with the same gray level intensity forming gray level zones; gray-tone difference matrix (GTDM), which quantifies the difference between a gray value and the average of neighbouring voxels, gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), which measures gray level dependency on the central voxel within a region, gray level distance zone matrix (GLDZM), which assesses the size and distribution of regions with similar gray levels, independent of their connectivity; and absolute gradient, which measures the magnitude of intensity changes within a region, providing insight into variations in voxel intensity.18

Visualization of texture features and spatial relationships within volume of interest (VOI)/region of interest (ROI). The original VOI/ROI matrix is shown at the top, with gray levels represented spatially. Below, matrices illustrate various texture features: the gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) captures pixel pair occurrences; the gray level run length matrix (GLRLM) measures lengths of consecutive pixels with the same gray level; the gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM) defines zones of connected pixels with uniform gray levels; the gray level dependence matrix (GLDM) shows gray level dependency on central voxels; the gray-tone difference matrix (GTDM), represented as a vector or single-column matrix, sums absolute differences between each gray level and the average intensity of neighbouring pixels; and the gray-level distance zone matrix (GLDZM) quantifies homogeneous intensity zones by counting occurrences based on gray level and zone size.

Following extraction, the number of features may exceed the sample size of the original data, posing a risk of overfitting. To mitigate this, ML or statistical analysis are often employed to select the most relevant features for distinguishing outcomes. This selection process is crucial for building the final model for classification and ensuring the generalizability of the model.18,19,40

The future of radiomics in FAI

Although randomized clinical trials have demonstrated that surgery generally yields superior outcomes compared to physiotherapy, a considerable proportion of patients do not achieve favourable results.41-43 This highlights the need to improve our understanding of the underlying pathology and develop efficient and accurate diagnostic, classification, planning, and predictive tools. Several studies have shown the use of a radiomic ML (R-ML) approach to enhance the diagnostic capabilities of FAI.44-47 Hogdon et al44,45 demonstrated the use of ML to differentiate between normal, symptomatic, and asymptomatic cam-type hips using texture profiles in acetabular subchondral bone in both CT and MRI. The use of texture analysis revealed an increase in bone density and an alteration in the distribution of the bone mass, possibly reflecting the biomechanical variation in cam-type hips. A recent study by Montin et al46 presented a R-ML driven pipeline for feature extraction from 3D hip MRI, resulting in the identification of FAI morphology in a small sample of hips with 97% accuracy. A follow-up article by the same authors reported 100% accuracy for the diagnosis of FAI using radiomic analysis versus 74% accuracy achieved using traditional radiological measurements.47 Montin et al48 also demonstrated that deep learning models for hip segmentation on 3D MRIs perform comparably to manual segmentation (Dice similarities coefficient of 95% and 97% for the acetabulum and femur, respectively). These early studies demonstrate the potential for automated MRI segmentation and radiomics-based diagnostics for FAI, but further research is needed with larger datasets, and to correlate radiomics features with clinical outcomes.

Advancements in imaging may also facilitate an improved understanding of the chondral pathologies that are present in FAI. The spherical anatomy and thinness of the cartilage in hips pose a substantial challenge in its assessment.49 Several studies have advocated for the use of quantitative MRI parameters such as T2* sequencing for the segmentation of cartilage, demonstrating accurate and reproducible results.50,51 However, such imaging techniques are subject to long scan times and thus not feasible to be used in clinical practice,49,52 while their validity is based on correlation between the clinical arthroscopic findings or simple descriptive statistics.53-56 Recent advancements in imaging techniques may improve the utility of these quantitative MRI parameters: Lattanzi57 describes the implementation of magnetic resonance fingerprinting (MRF), a novel technique enabling the extraction of multiple parameters from a single fast scan. When overlapped, this provides complementary tissue information, enabling automatic segmentation of cartilage from surrounding structures. By integrating the various reconstructed quantitative MRI parameters, this technique can facilitate a reliable visualization of cartilage along with its biochemical properties. This comprehensive approach can provide the foundation for employing a R-ML methodology to gain deeper insights into chondral lesions and other cartilaginous changes associated with FAI. The quantitative description of chondral injury using radiomics features has the potential to improve diagnosis and classification of early OA, and measure the effectiveness of novel biological interventions.

Current commercially available ROM simulation software to visualize impingement and map points of impingement are dependent on CT-based 3D reconstructions of the bony surfaces.58,59 In addition to exposing patients to ionizing radiation, it fails to consider the surrounding cartilaginous structures and soft-tissue. Several studies have demonstrated the ability of software using MRI segmentation to construct models for testing ROM, with performance comparable to CT-based models, without the exposure of high levels of radiation.60-65 A R-ML-based automated pipeline may provide sufficient information to construct a more comprehensive model that accounts for chondral pathologies, both pre- and post-surgical intervention.

Prospective studies should explore whether FAI classifications extend beyond the established cam, pincer, and mixed stratifications, and assess the potential for subclassifications. Studies to date have demonstrated the use of a R-ML approach to differentiate between normal and FAI hips, but further work may reveal the ability to differentiate between the different phenotypes, between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, as well as improve our understanding of FAI in the presence of other concurrent hip pathomorphologies. Additionally, R-ML could aid in the prognostication of patients with FAI, helping to better guide treatment pathways.42,66A few studies have speculated about a potential window between the appearance of chondral changes and the symptomatic presentation in FAI.44,45 A R-ML approach could quantify these early changes, indicating where hip arthroscopy is less likely to deliver meaningful improvements or long-term joint preservation.

Recommendations for a R-ML framework for FAI

For successful adoption of R-ML in managing FAI, the following considerations must be addressed.

Standardization of imaging acquisition and processing

To ensure the generalizability and comparability of results, as well as to prevent inconsistencies in extracted radiomic features for the same condition, it is crucial to identify methods to standardize imaging. This involves detailing acquisition parameters, segmentation criteria, and implementing stringent quality control measures to prevent noise and artifacts that may affect reliability.

Feature selection

Feature extraction using radiomics can produce a large number of features relative to the available data. Accounting for all these features can increase computational costs as well as the likelihood of generating false positives.28,67 Several studies advocate for the ‘one in ten’ as a rule of thumb, suggesting that ten samples are needed for each radiomic feature included in the model.28,67,68 Furthermore, the reproducibility and generalizability of these features must be considered, as they can be affected by variations in image quality, resolution, and scaling, which depend on the operators, equipment used, and institutions at which they are performed. These issues can be addressed through test-retest studies, phantom studies, robust segmentation, and standardization.69

Sample size

Current FAI radiomics studies are limited by the scarcity of original MRI datasets, potentially leading to overfitting and poor generalizability of models. Despite data augmentation advances, small sample sizes remain a barrier. A minimum of 100 data cases per FAI type is essential for robust model development.68,70 Establishing publicly available MRI datasets could also enhance research efforts.

Region of interest/volume of interest selection

Selecting the ROI or VOI for feature extraction is pivotal for prediction accuracy in FAI studies. Due to the variability of radiomics features across different locations, it is helpful to evaluate multiple ROIs/VOIs and identify the most relevant one based on the study’s focus, whether it pertains to bone pathology, soft-tissue, ROM, or pre- and post-intervention analysis.

Explainability of features

Explainability of selected features is vital for their use in radiomics within clinical practice. A common complication of ML and DL algorithms is the lack of transparency in feature selection.71-73 Radiomics offers a platform to control the features used in model development, but further work is needed to validate these features against existing imaging scores and/or arthroscopic findings. This validation enhances the credibility of selected features and improves our understanding of the underlying pathology at the imaging level, ensuring that radiomic-driven insights are both reliable and clinically relevant.

Conclusion

FAI presents clinical challenges largely due to the limitations of current imaging methods – radiographs, MRI, and CT. Radiomics – combined with ML – provides quantitative metrics to describe medical imaging and appears to be highly sensitive and specific for diagnosing FAI morphology and differentiating between symptomatic and asymptomatic hips. While further validation is required with larger and more diverse datasets, and clinical correlation, it has the potential to be developed into an automated, highly accurate AI diagnostic and prognostic tool in FAI.
