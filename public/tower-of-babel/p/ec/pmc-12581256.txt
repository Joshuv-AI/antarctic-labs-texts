Prevalence of hip dislocation and subluxation among children with cerebral palsy: an institution-based cross-sectional study from a Sub-Saharan country
Authors: Yemisrach Derese Kifle, Behaylu Yibe Woldemeskel, Bemnet Taye Gebregiogis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHip dislocation in children with cerebral palsy (CP) presents significant challenges, including pain, and severe contractures, which impair positioning, sitting, standing, and walking. This study investigated the prevalence and factors associated with hip displacement in a cohort of children with CP in a resource-limited setting lacking a dedicated hip surveillance program.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from August 1, 2023, to January 30, 2024, at Tikur Anbesa Hospital, Ethiopia’s largest tertiary hospital. The study included 141 children with CP who met the inclusion criteria and underwent hip radiography. The hip migration percentage was assessed, with hip subluxation defined as a migration percentage between 30% and 80% and hip dislocation defined as a percentage greater than 80%. The migration percentages were analyzed in relation to sociodemographic and clinical data via statistical tests.ResultsThe study cohort predominantly consisted of children with spastic tetraplegic CP (56.7%), and 52.6% had GMFCS levels IV and V. A high comorbidity rate was observed, with 98% of the children having additional conditions, the most common being epilepsy (59%). The prevalence of hip displacement was 28%, with 88% of these cases classified as hip subluxation and nearly 11% as dislocation. Displacement was more common in the left hip (56.4%) than in the right hip or both hips. Osteopenia was present in 3.5% of the children. Factors associated with hip displacement included home rehabilitation, frequency of carrying by caregivers, GMFCS levels, and being aged 5–10 years.ConclusionsThis study highlights the significant prevalence of hip displacement among children with CP in a resource-limited setting, despite being managed at a tertiary hospital. The findings underscore the need for the implementation of hip surveillance programs and timely interventions to prevent or mitigate hip displacement, thereby enhancing the quality of life and functional outcomes of these children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06276-2.

Background

Cerebral palsy (CP) encompasses a range of disorders that result from nonprogressive abnormalities in the developing fetal or infant brain. These abnormalities lead to permanent central nervous system dysfunction, manifesting as abnormalities in movement, posture, and motor function. Although the underlying causes of CP are non-progressive, the clinical manifestations can evolve as the central nervous system matures [15] [25].

In the United States, CP occurs in approximately two cases per 1,000 live births, making it the most frequent cause of physical impairment in children. However, the prevalence of CP is significantly greater in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) than in high-income countries (HICs). Studies from LMICs indicate a prevalence of 2.3 to 3.7 per 1,000 in LMICs, whereas the prevalence in HICs is approximately 1.6 to 2.9 per 1,000 [15] [18].

One of the notable musculoskeletal abnormalities associated with CP is hip displacement, which ranks as the second most common issue following foot and ankle abnormalities [2] [13]. The initial immaturity of the hips in CP patients allows the femoral head to dislocate because of the forces exerted by spastic and retracted muscles [7, 19]. However, recent reports have implicated the role of abnormal proximal femoral growth and secondary acetabular dysplasia as a primary cause of hip displacement, related to ambulatory status and abductor function [14]. Hip displacement is estimated to occur in 25–60% of CP patients, with complete hip dislocation affecting 10–15% of these individuals (Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Wagner [11],). Children categorized at Level V on the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) are at the highest risk, with the likelihood of dislocation increasing with the severity of gross motor limitations (Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Wagner [11],; Pountney & Green [23],. Hip displacement in CP often occurs already at 2–3 years of age. Range of motion is a poor indicator of hips at risk. GMFCS is valid and reliable from 2 years of age, and it is internationally accepted [1].

Early detection of hip displacement is critical, as it often begins as early as 2–3 years of age (Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Wagner [11]),. Clinical and radiological assessments are necessary to identify at-risk hips, as range of motion alone is not a reliable indicator. Radiographic monitoring, which focuses on the migration percentage (MP), is essential for screening. An MP of ≥ 33% signifies the need for enhanced observation, whereas an MP of ≥ 40% generally requires surgical intervention. For children whose MP is between 33% and 40%, treatment should be based on clinical indicators and the progression of MP over time (Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Persson [12],. The likelihood of hip displacement is influenced by both the GMFCS level and the CP subtype (Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Persson [12, 24],. Although CP subtypes can be difficult to identify before age four, the GMFCS provides a valid assessment as early as age two (Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Wagner [11]),. Consequently, radiographic examinations should be tailored based on the child’s age and GMFCS classification [5]; Hägglund, Lauge-Pedersen, & Wagner [11]),.

Progressive hip displacement leading to dislocation can cause severe discomfort, functional impairments, and a diminished quality of life [31]. Additional complications, such as scoliosis, severe contractures, and pelvic obliquity, further affect mobility and increase the risk of skin problems [28]. Regular screening and timely surgical intervention are crucial, as it has been shown to reduce both the incidence and severity of hip dislocation [7, 17]. Structured monitoring programs in high-income countries have successfully lowered the frequency of hip dislocation among children with CP, and similar approaches could be beneficial in LMICs [8].

Despite the substantial impact of CP on physical, functional, and mental well-being [10], research on the prevalence and factors associated with musculoskeletal problems, including hip displacement, remains limited in LMICs, particularly in Ethiopia. Improved hip surveillance through clinical assessments and radiological examinations may lead to earlier identification, timely intervention, and improved functional outcomes.

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of hip dislocation and subluxation among children with CP and describe the clinical conditions associated with an increased risk of hip dislocation and subluxation in CP patients.

Methods

The study was conducted at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Established in 1974, TASH is the largest tertiary hospital in the country and operates under the management of Addis Ababa University. It serves as the primary teaching facility in Ethiopia, where it educates over 350 residents and 300 medical students annually and treats approximately 400,000 patients each year. The Pediatric Neurology and Developmental Clinic at TASH sees between 500 and 700 children and adolescents aged 0–18 years, with 25–30% of these children being diagnosed with cerebral palsy (CP) during follow-up.

This study employed a prospective cross-sectional design and was conducted exclusively at TASH’s Neurology and Developmental Clinics from August 1, 2023, to February 30, 2024. The source population consisted of all children attending the Neurology Clinic at TASH during the study period. The inclusion criteria were CP patients aged 2–18 years attending the Neurology and Developmental Clinic at TASH during the study period, whereas the exclusion criteria included patients with hip dislocation due to trauma or other accidents, patients with incomplete data, and those unwilling to participate.

The sample size was calculated via the single population proportion formula with a 95% confidence interval and a 5% precision level. Given the lack of prior data on the prevalence of hip dislocation and subluxation among CP patients in Ethiopia, an estimated prevalence of 50% was used. The initial calculated sample size was 384. After applying the finite population correction, given that the number of children visiting the Neurology Clinic was less than 10,000, the adjusted sample size was 169. With a 10% nonresponse rate factored in, the final sample size was set at 214. All eligible CP patients attending the Neurology and Developmental Clinic during the study period were included in the analysis to meet this sample size requirement.

Data collection involved reviewing medical records and interviewing caregivers via a structured checklist (Appendix 1) that captured clinical characteristics and sociodemographic data. Gross Motor Function was assessed via the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS). The CP motor types included dyskinetic, ataxic, and spastic, with spastic CP further classified into diplegic, tetraplegic, triplegic, and mono/hemiplegic. Anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiographs were performed for participants meeting the inclusion criteria to assess hip displacement by measuring the migration percentage (MP) from the radiograph. The MP is defined as the proportion of the femoral capital epiphysis’s bony width that falls lateral to the Perkins line, a vertical line drawn from the acetabulum’s lateral bony boundary. MP was categorized as typical (MP < 33%), subluxated (MP 33–80%), or dislocated (MP >80%) [23, 30].

A senior musculoskeletal radiologist conducted both qualitative and quantitative assessments of the radiographs and examined signs of osteopenia, such as increased radiolucency, vertical striation, end plate thinning, accentuation of cortical borders, and migration percentage. To ensure data quality, 5% of the samples were reviewed via standardized checklists. Any issues identified during the preliminary examination were addressed before data collection began. Each question was correctly coded, and continuous oversight was provided by the primary investigator throughout the pretest and data collection phases. The data collected was reviewed daily for consistency and completeness.

The independent variables included sociodemographic factors such as current age, sex, parents’ educational status, residence, family income, and caregiver of the child; disease-related factors such as age at diagnosis of CP, type of CP, and GMFCS level; comorbidities such as developmental delay, epilepsy, scoliosis, ankle deformities, and fractures; and previous interventions such as physiotherapy, surgical interventions, and medications. The dependent variable was the prevalence of hip dislocation and subluxation.

The data was processed via ODK version 1.25.2 and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical data and means, medians, modes, and standard deviations for continuous data, were used to summarize the data. Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests were used to examine the relationships between hip displacement and potential risk factors. Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level with a 95% confidence interval. The results are presented in text, tables, and charts.

Ethical clearance was obtained from Research and Ethics Committee of the Pediatrics and Child Health Department’s Research and Publications Committee of the School of Medicine, College of Health Sciences and Addis Ababa University Then the study was approved by institutional ethics committee of AAU.

Results

This study analyzed the medical records and AP plain pelvic radiograph of 141 children and adolescents. In terms of age distribution, the mean age of the participants was 3.7 years (SD 4.9). 40.4% of the children were in the 5–10 years age group, whereas 39% were aged 2–5 years. The remaining 20.6% were between 10 and 18 years old. The majority of the children were male, accounting for 58.9%, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.4:1. Most of the children, 66.7%, were from Addis Ababa, followed by 19.1% from urban areas outside Addis Ababa and 14.2% from rural areas. Among the caregivers, 63.1% were mothers only, 32.6% were both mothers and fathers, and the remaining caregivers included fathers only, grandparents, and NGO representatives. Among the mothers, 46% were housewives, whereas 23.6% were self-employed (Table 1).

Discussion

With a sample size of 141 children, our study found that the prevalence of hip displacement was 28%, with 17% experiencing hip subluxation and 11% experiencing hip dislocation. This rate is notably higher than that reported in the Bangladesh study, which found 20% subluxation and approximately 5% dislocation [15]. The difference may be attributed to the greater proportion of children with severe CP in our cohort (53%) compared to the Bangladeshi study (32%). The high prevalence in our study may also be explained by the fact that it was conducted in a tertiary center, where more severe cases are referred. Conversely, our findings revealed a lower prevalence of hip displacement compared to the Malaysian study, which had a sample size of 75 children, the majority (69%) having a GMFCS score of V, with about 90% classified as GMFCS III or above. That study reported 55% subluxation and 15% dislocation [4]. The Malaysian study focused exclusively on spastic CP, which is generally associated with a higher risk of hip displacement. However, our study did not find a significant association between CP subtypes and hip displacement.

Our study found that GMFCS level V was associated with a significantly increased risk of hip dislocation and subluxation compared to GMFCS level I (AOR = 8.1, 95% CI = 4.19–50.23). This finding aligns with a study by Soo et al. [26], which reported that children with severe motor impairment had the highest hip displacement risk. Their study found an incidence of 0% for children with GMFCS level I and 90% for those with GMFCS level V. Compared to children with GMFCS level II, those classified as levels III, IV, and V had significantly higher relative risks of hip displacement (2.7, 4.6, and 5.9, respectively). Our findings further support the notion that GMFCS is a more stable and reliable measure of motor function and associated risks than CP subtypes. CP subtypes lack universal classification criteria and can be difficult to determine at a young age, whereas GMFCS has been validated as a stable and reliable tool, applicable from as early as two years of age [16].

In our setting, there is no hip surveillance program, and none of our patients has received operative or non-operative interventions. A study by Li et al. [17] highlights the need for hip surveillance programs with timely non-operative and operative interventions. The primary goal of such programs is to identify children at risk of hip displacement, monitor their hip development, and offer early appropriate interventions [6]. Given the strong association between GMFCS V and hip displacement, early orthopedic assessments and targeted surveillance programs should prioritize children in this category.

Our analysis also revealed that children aged 5–10 years had a higher risk of hip displacement than those aged 2–5 years, with an AOR of 7.5 (95% CI = 1.39–40.02). This finding is consistent with the Malaysian study, which reported a mean age of dislocation of 7.3 years [4]. However, our results differ from those of Minciu [21], who found the highest prevalence (55.5%) in children aged 1–5 years, with lower rates in older age groups (26.4% in 6–10 years and 17.7% in those above 10 years). Similarly, Terjesen [27] reported a trend of hip displacement in quadriplegic CP patients under five years of age who were unable to walk. These discrepancies may be due to early surveillance and intervention programs in some settings, which could contribute to lower displacement rates in older children. Since the primary aim of hip surveillance is to prevent hip dislocation, children with CP should be diagnosed early, allowing surveillance programs to be instituted by the age of two and preventive measures to be implemented as soon as hip displacement is detected.

Maternal education and caregiving practices also influenced the risk of hip displacement. Maternal education levels were associated with a higher likelihood of hip displacement, with the highest risk observed among mothers with secondary education ([AOR] = 16.3, 95% CI = 4.20–63.55). We were unable to find a similar study correlating maternal education with hip displacement risk. Given that the highest risk was observed among those with secondary education rather than those with lower education levels, we cannot establish a conclusive correlation. We recommend a prospective study to further investigate this finding.

The frequency of baby carrying also emerged as a significant factor influencing hip displacement risk. Less frequent carrying appeared to have a protective effect against hip displacement ([AOR] = 0.01, 95% CI = 0.001–0.22). After adjusting for GMFCS level, baby carrying remained a significant independent risk factor. The most widely accepted model of hip subluxation/dislocation suggests that an imbalance in muscle length and strength around the hip leads to acetabular dysplasia and subsequent subluxation [23]. We initially hypothesized that frequent back carrying in an abducted position might increase muscle length and strength, thereby reducing displacement risk. However, our results indicate the opposite, suggesting that carrying techniques, postural support, positioning methods, or cultural practices may play a role. Given that most of the children in our study had severe functional impairments and relied heavily on their caregivers, this aligns with findings from a study in our center, which reported that children with higher GMFCS levels depend on their mothers for daily living and transportation [29]. The confidence interval for the odds ratio for back carrying is very large this may indicate a high level of uncertainty about the true effect. A larger sample size might be needed to draw conclusions that are more precise. Underscores the need for further research to examine whether specific carrying methods, duration, and support influence hip displacement risk differently.

Rehabilitation settings also emerged as a critical factor. Our findings indicate that children receiving rehabilitation at home by caregivers had a significantly higher risk of hip displacement than those receiving professional rehabilitation (AOR = 9.3, 95% CI = 1.72–12.21). After adjusting for GMFCS level, this association remained significant (AOR = 4.52, 95% CI = 1.36–15.02, p = 0.014), suggesting that home-based caregiver rehabilitation is an independent risk factor. A hip radiographic follow-up program, combined with neurodevelopmental treatment (NDT) and postural management, is effective in modifying the natural progression of hip dislocation in children with CP. Conservative postural management has been shown to prevent hip displacement progression [22]. This may explain why professional physiotherapy is associated with lower hip dislocation rates. However, systematic reviews suggest that hip surveillance and conservative management alone may not fully prevent hip displacement in CP patients [3] [9] [20].Our study did not specify the type or frequency of rehabilitation received, making it difficult to establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship. There are limited pediatric rehabilitation center in Addis Ababa; Most rehabilitation centers are privately owned in Addis Ababa and often do not accept children with additional needs, such as seizures or behavioral challenges, which, combined with the financial difficulties of families with a child with cerebral palsy, further limits access to these services. Further prospective studies are needed to examine whether rehabilitation influences hip displacement risk or whether socioeconomic factors, such as limited access to therapy, contribute to higher GMFCS scores and poorer outcomes.

The limitations of this study include the relatively small sample size. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causation, as we only collected data at a single point in time. Another limitation is the reliance on parental recall, which introduces the potential for recall bias, particularly concerning past medical history. We tried to mitigate the limitation by including all children who fulfilled the inclusion and are willing to participate in the study. Tried to implement bivariate and multivariate analysis adjusting to the cofounding factors, and implement chart review to reduce recall bias.

Conclusion

This study represents one of the few investigations in cerebral palsy children and one of its kind into assessing hip displacement among children with cerebral palsy in Ethiopia. Our findings indicate a significant burden of hip displacement within this population, particularly among those with severe forms of CP aged 5–10 years, always carried on the caregiver’s back, and those receiving home rehabilitation services by caregivers.

Our results lay the groundwork for demonstrating the high prevalence of hip dislocation and associated risk factors. In addition, lack of hip surveillance program and intervention. The introduction of a comprehensive hip surveillance program and timely intervention is essential for early detection and prevention of complications and improves quality of life for these children and their families. Future research should aim to confirm these findings with larger sample sizes, multicenter, prospective study and explore additional factors contributing to hip displacement in CP patients.
