A chromosome-level genome assembly of Myrmica angulata (Hymenoptera: Formicidae)
Authors: Congcong Du, Zhilin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnts, one of the most representative groups among invertebrates, exhibit a typical latitudinal diversity gradient, with a rich variety of species. The genus Myrmica Latreille, 1804 is a species-rich (over 200 species) group within the Myrmicinae and mainly distributed in the Holarctic region, making it a good model for biogeography and adaptive evolution analysis. To this end, we present a chromosome-level genome of Myrmica angulata achieved through the integration of PacBio, Illumina, and Hi-C reads. The assembled genome was 464.36 Mb whose 97.36% was anchored into 24 pseudochromosomes, with a contig N50 of 1.752 Mb, a scaffold N50 of 18.23 Mb, and 98.5% BUSCO completeness. It contained 248.99 Mb (53.62%) repeat sequences, 1,036 non-coding RNAs, and 17,066 protein-coding genes. The high-quality genome assembly of M. angulata lays the foundation for further exploration of the genetic mechanisms underlying their ecological adaptation to high-latitude and high-altitude environments, and provides a valuable reference for understanding the adaptive evolution of ants.Subject terms: Entomology, Biogeography

Background & Summary

Exploring the mechanisms of biodiversity formation is a central issue in ecology, biogeography, and evolutionary biology. Ants, as one of the most representative groups among invertebrates, are not only species-rich—comprising 16 subfamilies, 346 valid genera, 14,343 valid species1; — but also highly abundant, with a population estimated almost 20 × 1015 individuals, representing approximately 20% of human biomass, and exceeding the combined biomass of wild birds and mammals2. Furthermore, ants are widely distributed, thriving a variety of environments3, and play key roles in providing diverse and valuable ecological services. In addition to their ecological significance, their unique biological traits— such as social organization4, myrmecochory5, phenotypic diversity6 and parasitism7—have fascinated numerous biologists. Unveiling the genomic characteristics of ants can provide a foundation for exploring the evolutionary and molecular mechanisms underlying their adaptive evolution. However, genomic studies on ants are still in the nascent stages, which hinders our comprehensive understanding of the adaptive evolution of this group.

The Myrmicinae Lepeletier de Saint-Fargeau, 1835 is the most representative subfamily of the Formicidae, exhibiting a diverse range of species that follow a typical latitudinal gradient distribution pattern8, with the majority of its groups concentrated in tropical and subtropical regions3. However, the genus Myrmica Latreille, 1804 stands out within the Myrmicinae, being a species-rich (over 200 species) group distributed throughout the Holarctic region9. Interestingly, compared to the Nearctic region, the Myrmica communities in the Palaearctic region are also found in the high-altitude mountainous areas of tropical and subtropical regions in southern East Asia and Southeast Asia9. This raises several questions: how is the distribution pattern of the genus Myrmica formed? Is the difference in its latitudinal distribution between the Nearctic and Palaearctic regions possibly related to the unique glacial history of the two regions in the Quaternary period? What genomic adaptation mechanisms underlie its distribution characteristics in high latitudes and altitudes? Moreover, the species of this group have been categorized into morphologically defined species groups, which has been a topic of ongoing controversy. Obtaining genomic data from Myrmica species is crucial for resolving the above questions related to classification, biogeography, and evolutionary processes.

Up to now, genomic data for 58 ant species have been reported, laying a foundation for exploring their adaptive evolution (as of October 2024 from NCBI). Cicconardi et al.10 found that the subalpine ant Tetramorium alpestre experiences strong positive selection on related genes due to complex environmental selection pressures during its adaptation to high-altitude environments. For instance, there is a higher expression of stv and other heat-shock proteins; simultaneously, the enzymatic activity of Hex-T1 is enhanced, thereby regulating its physiological adaptability. Among these, the genomes of 13 ant species have been assembled at the chromosomal level (as of October 2024 from NCBI). Although genome data are abundant in Myrmicinae, no genomic data have been reported for species of the Myrmica to date. This gap significantly hampers our exploration of the adaptive mechanisms behind the above mentioned unique distribution patterns of Myrmica and greatly limits our understanding of genome diversity and evolution within the Myrmicinae species.

Here, we present a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of Myrmica angulata Radchenko, Zhou & Elmes, 2001, a representative species from the genus Myrmica, which inhabits decaying wood in the forests of high-altitude regions of southern East and Southeast Asia. It is also helpful to clearly indicate the species group to which this species belongs. The repeat elements, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), and protein-coding genes of this genome were annotated. Additionally, the chromosomal synteny analysis of the M. angulata genome with those of Solenopsis invicta Buren, 1972 and Ooceraea biroi (Forel, 1907) was also performed.

Methods

Animal materials and sequencing

Specimens of M. angulata were collected in 2021 from a wild ant nest in the Dayaoshan National Nature Reserve in Guangxi, China (24.116778°N, 110.198144°E, 948 m) and stored frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80 °C at Guangxi Normal University (specimen code: GXNU228632) for further research. In this experiment, only adult female worker ants were utilized for high-quality genomic DNA and RNA extraction. All sequencing work was completed at Beijing Berry Genomics Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

For DNA sequencing, total DNA was extracted using the QIAGEN DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Germany). Subsequently, the quality of the DNA was assessed using the following three methods: 1) DNA size and degradation were evaluated by 0.75% agarose gel electrophoresis; 2) DNA purity was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA); 3) precise quantification was performed using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) to obtain high-quality DNA.

To estimate information such as the genome size and heterozygosity of M. angulata, high-quality genomic DNA was used to construct a PCR-free library with an insert size of 350 bp using the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform.

To obtain a complete genome sequence, a library with an insert size of 40 kb was constructed using the SMRTbell™ Template Prep Kit 2.0 (PacBio, CA, USA). This process mainly involved the fragmentation of quality-controlled DNA, followed by concentration, damage repair, end polishing, purification, adapter ligation, and digestion of unsuccessful ligation products. Finally, purification was performed using magnetic beads, and the constructed DNA library was precisely quantified with the Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) before long-read sequencing was completed on the PacBio Sequel II platform.

To assist genome assembly and map construction, Hi-C sequencing was performed. First, samples were fixed with formaldehyde to crosslink protein-DNA and DNA-DNA interactions within the cells. Then, DNA was digested with the restriction enzyme MboI, and the ends were repaired and circularized using T4 DNA ligase. After decrosslinking, the DNA was purified and fragmented into 300–700 bp segments. DNA fragments involved in interactions were captured using streptavidin magnetic beads for library construction, and sequencing was finally completed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Additionally, to assist with genome annotation, RNA was extracted from various tissues of M. angulata using the TRIzol™ Reagent kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). After purification, a 250 bp RNA library was constructed using the TruSeq RNA v2 kit (Illumina, CA, USA), and transcriptome sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Genome survey

A preliminary assessment of genome size, heterozygosity, and other characteristics was conducted using K-mer analysis. First, raw data from Illumina short reads were subjected to quality control and trimming using BBTools v38.8211. The clumpify.sh tool was employed to remove duplicate sequences, while bbduk.sh was used for quality control, which included removing bases with a quality score below 20, filtering out sequences shorter than 15 bp, and eliminating poly-A/G/C tails longer than 10 bp, along with correcting bases using overlapping reads. Next, K-mer analysis was performed using Genomescope v2.012, setting the maximum K-mer depth threshold to 100,000. K-mer frequencies were evaluated using the khist.sh tool in BBTools, with a K-mer length of 21. Finally, another K-mer analysis was conducted with Genomescope, using parameters set to -k 21 -p 2 -m 100,000. The analysis results indicated an estimated genome size of 463.99 Mb, characterized by low heterozygosity (left peak) and high repeat content (right peak), with repetitive sequences accounting for approximately 232.65 Mb (50.14% of the total genome) (Fig. 1a).

Genome assembly

To construct a high-quality chromosome-level genome, a combination of PacBio third-generation data assembly, correction with second-generation data, and chromosome anchoring using Hi-C data were employed. First, NextDenovo v2.4.013 was used for the initial assembly of PacBio data, filtering out sequences shorter than 1 kb. Subsequently, Minimap2 v2.2214 was employed to align the quality-controlled second-generation Illumina data to the preliminarily assembled genome, and samtools v1.1015 was used to convert the data format. Next, NextPolish v1.3.016 was used for one round of long-read correction and two rounds of short-read correction to improve assembly accuracy.

Chromosome anchoring was completed using 3D-DNA v.18092217. Juicer v.1.6.218 was first used for Hi-C data quality control, followed by chromosome anchoring using 3D-DNA v.18092217 with default parameters, and finally manual correction using Juicebox v.1.11.0818. To ensure genome purity, MMseqs2 v12-113e319 was used to remove potential contaminant sequences, and the data was submitted to NCBI for validation, generating the final chromosome-anchored genome. A total of 24 pseudochromosomes were ultimately anchored, with an anchoring rate of 97.36%. Among them, the interaction intensity within pseudochromosomes was higher than that between pseudochromosomes (Fig. 1).

The final genome assembly results showed a genome size of 464.362 Mb, with 444 scaffolds and 1,293 contigs. The longest scaffold was 32.925 Mb, and the longest contig was 11.629 Mb. The N50 values were 18.23 Mb for scaffolds and 1.752 Mb for contigs, with a GC content of 39.16% (Table 1). The 24 pseudochromosomes totaled 452.084 Mb, closely matching the genome survey results.

The genome annotation includes repetitive sequences, protein-coding genes, and non-coding RNAs. For repetitive sequence annotation, RepeatModeler v2.0.2a20 with LTR search (-LTRStruct) was used to build a de novo repeat library. The final repeat database was generated by merging Dfam_3.321 and RepBase-2018102622, and RepeatMasker v4.1.223 was applied for genome annotation. The results identified 729,530 repetitive elements, accounting for 53.62% of the genome. The most abundant types were DNA elements (19.53%), LTR elements (14.91%), unknown elements (11.00%), LINEs (5.44%), and simple repeats (1.25%).

For protein-coding gene prediction, MAKER v3.01.0324 was employed by integrating three strategies: ab initio prediction was performed using BRAKER v2.1.625, which automatically trained Augustus v3.3.426 and GeneMark-ES/ET/EP 4.68_3.60_lic27, integrating transcriptome and protein homology data to enhance accuracy. StringTie v2.1.628 was used for transcriptome-guided assembly, and GeMoMa v1.829 helped predict genes based on homologous species. A total of 17,066 protein-coding genes were identified. The average length of protein, gene and intron were 583.1aa, 6,283.8 bp, and 319.5 bp, respectively (Table 2). BUSCO v3.0.230 analysis revealed a high completeness score of 99.5% (Table 2).

For functional annotation of protein-coding genes, Diamond v2.0.11.14931 with the parameters -very-sensitive -e 1e-5 to search UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/) for gene function annotations. By comparing with comprehensive databases, conserved sequences, domains, Gene Ontology (GO) annotations, and pathways (KEGG, Reactome) of the proteins in the gene set were predicted. InterProScan 5.48-83.032 was primarily used to search the Pfam33, SMART34, Superfamily35, and CDD36 databases. Additionally, eggNOG-mapper v2.1.537 was employed for functional annotation against the eggNOG v5.038 database. In total, 16,158 genes matched entries in UniProtKB, while InterProScan identified conserved domains in 12,599 genes, and 16,094 GO terms and 14,967 KEGG annotations were assigned (Table 3).

For non-coding RNA (ncRNA) annotation, Infernal v1.1.439 was used to annotate miRNAs, rRNAs, and snRNAs by comparing the genome with the Rfam database. tRNAscan-SE v2.0.940 was used to predict tRNAs. In total, 1,036 ncRNAs were identified, including 92 miRNAs, 175 rRNAs, 57 snRNAs, 176 tRNAs, and 493 other ncRNAs.

Synteny analysis

The synteny analyses were performed between the chromosome-level genome assemblies of M. angulata, Solenopsis invicta Buren, 1972 and Ooceraea biroi (Forel, 1907). Protein sequences were aligned using MMseq219 with parameter of “s 7.5–alignment-mode 3–num-iterations 4 -e 1e-5–max-accept 5”. Subsequently, chromosomal synteny was analyzed using MCScanX46 with default parameters. TBtools v1.069241 was used to visualize genome synteny (Fig. 2). The chromosomal collinearity between M. angulata and S. invicta is more apparent compared to the relationship between M. angulata and O. biroi. Overall, the chromosomal differentiation between species is significant.

The PacBio, Illumina, Hi-C and transcriptome data can be accessed using the identification numbers SRR31842302, SRR31842301, SRR31842300, and SRR31842299, respectively42–45. The genome assembly result is available in the NCBI database46.

Technical Validation

The completeness of the genome assembly was evaluated using BUSCO v3.0.230 and the insecta_odb10 database (n = 1,367 orthologues), based on single-copy orthologs in insects. Additionally, second-generation Illunina data, transcriptome data, and third-generation PacBio raw data were aligned to the assembled genome to assess data utilization and assembly completeness. Minimap2 v2.2214 was used for alignment, and Samtools v1.1015 was employed to calculate alignment rates. The results showed that the BUSCO assessment indicated a genome completeness of 98.5%, with alignment rates of 97.38%, 94.96%, and 99.02% for second-generation survey, RNA, and third-generation PacBio data, respectively. In addition, BUSCO analysis revealed that the completeness score of protein gene annotation reached 99.5% (Table 3). These findings demonstrate that the M. angulata genome assembly achieved a high level of continuity and completeness.
