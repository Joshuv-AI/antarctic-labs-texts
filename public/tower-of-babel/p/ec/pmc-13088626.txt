The NAT10/ac4C axis: a central nexus linking RNA acetylation to immunometabolic signaling in disease
Authors: Caiyun Shang, Xiaoxue Wang, Yanling Huang, Jingyi Zhu, Xueqian Liu, Heng Zhang, Wuxiang Guan, Haojie Hao, Fang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA modifications represent a fundamental layer of epitranscriptomic regulation. Among them, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C)—a highly conserved and functionally versatile mark deposited by N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10)—has recently emerged as a critical coordinator of immunometabolic homeostasis. In this review, we synthesize current evidence establishing the NAT10/ac4C axis as a central signaling hub that integrates immune regulation and metabolic control through post-transcriptional modulation of key transcripts. We summarize mechanistic advances demonstrating how ac4C shapes innate and adaptive immunity—including macrophage polarization, T cell activation, and B cell responses—while concurrently directing metabolic programs across glucose, lipid, and amino acid pathways via effects on RNA stability and translation. We further highlight convergent immunometabolic mechanisms by which dysregulated NAT10/ac4C signaling drives diverse pathological states. In cancer, aberrant ac4C activity promotes tumor-intrinsic growth, immunosuppressive microenvironment remodeling, and metabolic rewiring. Similar NAT10-linked immunometabolic circuits underlie autoimmune diseases, sepsis, and metabolic inflammatory disorders. Finally, we evaluate the landscape of NAT10 inhibitors and discuss opportunities for mechanism-guided therapeutic exploration. Together, this review positions the NAT10/ac4C axis not merely as an RNA-modifying pathway, but as a dynamic, disease-relevant integrator at the intersection of epitranscriptomics, immunity, and metabolism.Keywords: NAT10, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), RNA epitranscriptomics, Immune regulation, Metabolic reprogramming, Therapeutic targeting

Introduction

In recent years, RNA modifications have emerged as a pivotal layer of epitranscriptomic regulation, introducing additional complexity to post-transcriptional gene expression and cellular homeostasis. By altering RNA chemical structures, these modifications modulate RNA stability, translational efficiency, and molecular interactions, profoundly reshaping gene expression programs [1–3]. Among the numerous chemical marks identified to date, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most extensively characterized [1]. Mechanistically, m6A typically occurs within a DRACH consensus sequence and is installed by a multicomponent complex (METTL3-METTL14-WTAP) [4, 5], recognized by YTH domain-containing proteins [6, 7], and can be removed by demethylases such as FTO and ALKBH5 [8, 9]. m6A primarily regulates mRNA stability, splicing, export, and translation in a context-dependent manner, with widespread roles across development, immunity, and disease [10–12]. In contrast, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) represents a highly conserved yet relatively understudied RNA modification that differs mechanistically from m6A in that, based on current evidence, its regulatory effects appear to be more directly associated with alterations in RNA chemical properties and structural stability, while dedicated ac4C-recognizing proteins have not yet been conclusively identified [13, 14].

ac4C was first identified in yeast transfer RNA (tRNA) in 1966 [15] and was later recognized as an evolutionarily conserved RNA modification across all domains of life [16]. It is broadly distributed in diverse RNA classes, including tRNAs [17, 18], ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) [18], messenger RNAs (mRNAs) [19], long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [20], primary microRNAs (pri-miRNAs) [21], and viral RNAs [22, 23]. Functionally, ac4C fine-tunes codon recognition, stabilizes transcripts, modulates translation initiation, and influences RNA–protein interactions as well as higher-order RNA structures, underscoring its central role in RNA metabolism and cellular physiology [19, 24–27]. It is important to note that the landscape of ac4C in mammalian mRNAs remains under active reevaluation. Early transcriptome-wide profiling studies reported thousands of putative ac4C sites in human mRNAs and suggested that ac4C modification can enhance mRNA stability and translation efficiency [19]. However, subsequent work using ac4C-seq failed to detect reproducible ac4C sites in human or yeast mRNAs, instead identifying only robust ac4C in archaeal RNAs, highlighting challenges in sensitivity and specificity of current mapping methods [14]. Recent critical reassessment of RedaC:T datasets also demonstrated that the apparent mRNA ac4C signals reported in earlier studies were largely due to technical artifacts and lacked reproducibility across replicates [28]. Conversely, a follow-up response reanalyzing these datasets reaffirmed the occurrence of C → T mismatches consistent with ac4C under certain analytical conditions, underscoring the complexity and context-dependence of ac4C detection [29]. In addition, recent reviews have discussed methodological discrepancies and biological heterogeneity that contribute to divergent interpretations of ac4C's prevalence and function in mRNA metabolism [13]. Together, these studies suggest that ac4C in mammalian mRNAs may be of low abundance, technically challenging to detect, and potentially restricted to specific transcripts or cellular contexts, rather than representing a widespread modification [28].

In the field of epitranscriptomics, RNA modifications are commonly described using the conceptual framework of “writers,” “readers,” and “erasers.” “Writers” refer to enzymes that catalyze the installation of specific chemical modifications on RNA,“readers” are RNA-binding proteins that selectively recognize modified nucleotides, and “erasers” are enzymes capable of removing these modifications to confer reversibility. N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10), is currently the sole confirmed eukaryotic “writer” responsible for ac4C deposition and belongs to the Gcn5-related N-acetyltransferase (GNAT) family [30, 31]. Originally identified as human N-acetyltransferase-like protein (hALP) [31], NAT10 was first linked to telomerase regulation by promoting human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) transcription and modulating telomerase complex dynamics [31]. Subsequent studies established NAT10 as a dual-function acetyltransferase targeting both proteins [32] and RNAs [33]. Early work demonstrated that NAT10 acetylates multiple protein substrates—including hisones [31, 34], α-tubulin [32, 35], mitotic regulators (p53, MORC2, Eg5, CCDC84, and PARP1) [36–40], the nucleolar RNA helicase DDX21 [41], and the nuclear transcriptional regulator Che-1(also known as AATF) [42]—thereby influencing chromatin organization, cytoskeletal remodeling, centrosome duplication, cell-cycle progression, nucleolar RNA metabolism, rRNA biogenesis, and DNA damage response. In addition, NAT10 acetylates the metabolic enzyme ACLY [43], functionally linking it to cellular metabolic regulation. More recently, NAT10 was identified as the enzyme responsible for depositing ac4C on rRNA to support ribosome biogenesis and translational fidelity [44], on tRNA to promote stability and maturation [33, 45, 46], and on mRNA—particularly within coding sequences (CDS) and 5′ untranslated regions (5′ UTR)—to enhance mRNA stability and translational efficiency [19]. Collectively, NAT10-mediated protein and RNA acetylation regulate a wide range of cellular processes, including ribosome assembly [33, 44, 47], cell division [32, 48, 49], DNA damage response [50–52], immune regulation [53–55], development [56–58], autophagy [42, 59, 60] and apoptosis [36, 61, 62]. Dysregulation of these pathways is implicated in cancer [63], metabolic disorders [64], infection-related inflammation and injury [23], and aging-related diseases [65], highlighting NAT10 as a promising therapeutic target.

From a translational perspective, the NAT10–ac4C axis presents compelling therapeutic opportunities. Selective inhibition of NAT10 could rectify aberrant gene expression, enhance anti-tumor or anti-infection immunity, and restore metabolic homeostasis. In parallel, advances in sensitive and quantitative ac4C detection technologies may enable early diagnosis, patient stratification, and pharmacodynamic monitoring, thereby bridging fundamental epitranscriptomic discoveries with precision medicine. Together, ac4C and its catalytic enzyme NAT10 constitute a central nexus integrating RNA biology, metabolism, and immunity. Elucidating their dynamic regulation will not only deepen our understanding of post-transcriptional gene control but also pave the way for innovative immunometabolic therapies across cancer, infection, and metabolic inflammatory diseases.

The ac4C modification: machinery, distribution, and detection

Enzymatic machinery of ac4C: writers, erasers, readers

ac4C is generated through the transfer of an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to the amine group at the N4 position of cytidine. This adenosine triphosphate (ATP)–dependent acetylation reaction releases coenzyme A (CoA) as a byproduct [13] (Fig. 1A). The modification is evolutionarily conserved and present in diverse RNA classes—including tRNA, rRNA, and mRNA—highlighting its fundamental role in RNA metabolism [19, 33, 44, 66–69]. Yet, despite this shared catalytic logic, its regulatory partners and functional outcomes vary among organisms.

In eukaryotes, NAT10 serves as the “writer” protein responsible for RNA ac4C modification. Equipped with both catalytic and RNA-binding domains, NAT10 is capable of directly recognizing and acetylating RNA substrates, and to date remains the only identified eukaryotic enzyme that catalyzes RNA ac4C formation [70]. Its activity is often facilitated by cofactors: for instance, the protein adapter THUMP domain containing 1 (THUMPD1) assists tRNA acetylation, while the small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) SNORD13 guides site-specific acetylation in rRNA [33, 71]. In yeast, the homologous enzyme Kre33 performs a similar function in tRNA and rRNA acetylation, often cooperating with Tan1 as an adapter [33]. Notably, in 2018, NAT10 was first shown to catalyze ac4C modification in mRNA, establishing ac4C as an epitranscriptomic mark [19]. Whether additional auxiliary factors are required for mRNA acetylation remains to be determined.

Beyond its enzymatic activity, NAT10 subcellular localization is dynamically regulated and closely linked to substrate accessibility. Under basal conditions, NAT10 predominantly localizes to the nucleolus, consistent with its established role in rRNA processing and ribosome biogenesis [32]. However, NAT10 can redistribute in response to cellular stress. For example, PARP1-dependent PARylation promotes its relocalization from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm upon DNA damage [50], suggesting that stress-induced spatial shifts may expand its substrate spectrum. Aberrant localization has also been observed in disease contexts. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), NAT10 accumulates in the cytoplasm and associates with cytoskeletal components, contributing to tumor progression and metastasis [72]. Notably, loss of nucleolar localization signals can further drive NAT10 translocation to the cytoplasm and cell membrane, where it enhances migratory and invasive capacities in HCC cells [72]. Similarly, membrane-associated NAT10 has been detected at the invasive front of colorectal carcinoma, where its redistribution correlates with tumor progression and metastatic potential [35]. Viral infection provides another striking example of functionally relevant relocalization. During enterovirus 71 (EV71) infection, NAT10 translocates to the cytoplasm, enhancing ac4C modification of viral RNA and promoting viral replication [22]. These findings indicate that dynamic spatial redistribution of NAT10 constitutes an important layer of regulation that enables context- and cell-type–specific targeting of distinct RNA substrates.

In prokaryotes, ac4C has been detected in tRNA and rRNA but not in mRNA, suggesting that mRNA acetylation may represent a eukaryote-specific evolutionary adaptation to support more sophisticated gene regulation. Prokaryotic ac4C formation is catalyzed by distinct enzymes, such as tRNAMet cytidine acetyltransferase (TmcA) [17], which typically works in concert with chaperones or RNA cofactors to ensure site specificity. These modifications contribute to ribosomal stability and translational accuracy (Fig. 1B).

Unlike other RNA modifications such as m6A or 5-methylcytosine (m5C), bona fide ac4C “erasers” or “readers” have not yet been conclusively identified. The apparent chemical stability of the N4-acetyl bond suggests that ac4C may be a relatively irreversible modification under physiological conditions, although emerging evidence—such as SIRT7-mediated deacetylation of rRNA ac4C [73]—indicates that reversibility may occur in specific contexts. Interestingly, several studies have reported that ac4C can enhance the binding of certain RNA-binding proteins [74, 75], hinting at the existence of potential “readers” that interpret this modification. Nevertheless, the identification and characterization of such readers remain an intriguing open question. Advances in ac4C-specific affinity profiling and structural studies may eventually uncover dedicated proteins that recognize and interpret this modification.

Overall, ac4C is indispensable for RNA homeostasis and efficient protein synthesis across life domains, though the enzymatic machinery and substrate repertoire differ substantially between prokaryotes and eukaryotes. The unique occurrence of ac4C in eukaryotic mRNA underscores the regulatory importance of NAT10 and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target.

Landscape of ac4C distribution across RNA species

ac4C is present across a broad spectrum of RNA classes—tRNAs, rRNAs, mRNAs, lncRNAs, pri-miRNAs and viral RNAs—but its localization and functional consequences are highly RNA-type and position dependent.

In tRNAs, ac4C exhibits species-specific distribution and functions. In bacteria, it is restricted to the wobble position (position 34, ac4C34) of elongator tRNAMet, where it ensures accurate decoding of AUG and prevents misreading of near-cognate AUA isoleucine codons, thereby preserving translational fidelity [67, 76]. This modification is typically installed by TmcA [17]. In archaea, ac4C occurs at multiple tRNAs sites, accumulates in a temperature-dependent manner, and is required for growth at elevated temperatures [14]. In eukaryotes, ac4C is found specifically at position 12 (ac4C12) of the 11CCG13 motif in the D-arm of class II tRNASer and tRNALeu with long variable arms [77, 78]. Biogenesis of tRNA ac4C in eukaryotes depends on Kre33 (yeast) or NAT10 (human) and requires the adaptor protein Tan1/THUMPD1 [33, 78]. The primary known function of ac4C12 is to stabilize tRNA under heat stress [79, 80] (Fig. 2A).

Localization of ac4C modifications across RNA species. A tRNA: ac4C occurs at position 34 in bacterial tRNAMet and at position 12 in the D-arm of eukaryotic class II tRNASer/Leu; B rRNA: Conserved ac4C sites are found in helix 34 and helix 45 of 18S rRNA; C mRNA: ac4C is predominantly enriched in CDSs, with decreasing levels from the 5′ UTR to the 3′ UTR; D lncRNA: ac4C is present on specific cancer-associated lncRNAs; E pri-miRNA: ac4C is located on stem-loop structures at conserved motifs; F Viral RNA: ac4C occurs at multiple sites on viral RNAs, including ZIKV, DENV, HCV, HIV-1, EV71, IAV, KSHV, SINV, and MVC

In rRNAs, yeast Kre33 mediates ac4C modification at position 1773 in helix 45 of 18S rRNA, a modification essential for 18S rRNA maturation, as its inhibition leads to precursor 23S rRNA accumulation [44, 47]. Acetylation at C1297 and C1815 on yeast small-subunit rRNA is essential for normal growth, as NAT10 loss abolishes acetylation at these sites, causing abnormal accumulation of large-subunit and slow growth, which is rescued by wild-type NAT10 [81]. Comparative studies in yeast and humans have revealed two conserved ac4C sites on the 18S rRNA of the 40S ribosomal subunit: one in helix 34 of the 3′ major domain (C1280 in yeast; C1337 in humans), critical for translational accuracy, and another in helix 45 of the 3′ minor domain near the decoding site (C1773 in yeast; C1842 in humans). Inhibition of Kre33 or NAT10 markedly reduces 40S ribosome production and 18S rRNA levels, underscoring the fundamental role of ac4C in ribosome biogenesis [33, 82] (Fig. 2B).

In mRNAs, ac4C is predominantly enriched within the CDS, particularly near translation start sites, with its abundance tapering from the 5′ UTR toward the 3′ UTR [19, 63]. The effect of ac4C on mRNA translation is highly position-dependent. Within CDSs, ac4C enhances the stability of target transcripts in a NAT10-dependent manner and promotes translation elongation by facilitating decoding at cytidine-containing wobble positions, thereby boosting the overall translational output of acetylated mRNAs [19]. In contrast, ac4C in the 5′UTRs and AUG-flanking Kozak sequences predominantly modulate translation initiation. Specifically, 5′UTRs acetylation promotes initiation at upstream sequences, competitively inhibiting annotated start codons, whereas ac4C within Kozak sequences structurally alters the interaction between mRNA and initiator tRNAiMet, ultimately suppressing initiation efficiency [19, 25]. These findings collectively highlight a dual regulatory role of ac4C in mRNAs, promoting translation elongation within CDSs while imposing context-dependent constraints on translation initiation (Fig. 2C).

In lncRNAs, NAT10-mediated ac4C regulates cancer-associated transcripts. For example, ac4C in CTC-490G23.2 strengthens its interaction with the splicing factor PTBP1, promoting CD44 alternative splicing and driving metastasis [20]. In nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), ac4C modification of lncRNA SIMALR activates eEF1A2, which in turn facilitates translation of ITGB4/ITGA6, thereby promoting tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion [83] (Fig. 2D).

In pri-miRNAs, NAT10/THUMPD1 bind stem-loop structures and catalyze ac4C formation at conserved motifs, thereby increasing binding affinity and facilitating pri-miRNA processing [21](Fig. 2E).

Beyond host RNAs, ac4C is increasingly recognized as a crucial regulator of viral RNAs. Transcriptome-wide analyses of positive-sense RNA viruses—including Zika virus (ZIKV), dengue virus (DENV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), poliovirus, and human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1)—revealed widespread ac4C remodeling of both viral and host RNAs, suggesting dynamic regulation to optimize viral gene expression [84]. Functional studies further demonstrated that ac4C enhances HIV-1 transcript stability and gene expression, thereby promoting viral replication [85], and directly influences replication and pathogenicity of EV71 [22]. In negative-sense RNA viruses such as influenza A virus (IAV), ac4C and other RNA modifications have been detected in viral genomic RNA, where they are implicated in modulating replication efficiency and host adaptation [86]. Among DNA viruses, NAT10-dependent ac4C stabilizes the long noncoding PAN RNA in Kaposi’s sarcoma–associated herpesvirus (KSHV), promoting viral reactivation and IFI16-related inflammasome activation [23]. Moreover, host-encoded RNAs can also be targeted to influence viral infection. Sindbis virus (SINV) replication relies on NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of host LY6E mRNA, revealing a mechanism whereby host transcript remodeling contributes to viral replication [87]. Similarly, in the minute virus of canines (MVC), a site-specific ac4C mark on viral RNA coordinates with the viral protein NP1 and the host factor CPSF5 to orchestrate alternative splicing and polyadenylation events essential for viral replication [74] (Fig. 2F).

Technological arsenal: mapping ac4C modifications

Accurate detection of ac4C is essential for elucidating its role in RNA metabolism and diseases. Because ac4C does not disrupt the canonical C-G pairing, it escapes detection by conventional RNA sequencing [88]. To overcome this limitation, several complementary strategies—ranging from biochemical quantification to high-throughput sequencing and computational prediction—have been developed to improve sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility. These methods collectively enable comprehensive mapping of ac4C sites, facilitating functional studies and biomarker discovery.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-based quantification

LC–MS provides precise identification and quantification of ac4C at the nucleoside level. Leveraging the superior separation capacity of liquid chromatography coupled with the high sensitivity and selectivity of mass spectrometry, LC–MS is particularly effective for analyzing modified nucleosides in stable structural RNAs such as tRNA and rRNA, where modification stoichiometry is generally high [89]. However, detecting low-abundance ac4C in mRNA remains challenging due to potential signal suppression and low stoichiometry. Bulk poly (A) RNA measurements are often confounded by residual rRNA contamination, which can contribute substantial ac4C signals and obscure the true origin of the modification. Given the relatively high stoichiometry of ac4C in rRNA compared to mRNA, even minimal rRNA carryover can substantially inflate apparent mRNA ac4C levels, leading to overestimation of transcriptome-wide acetylation if stringent purification and validation are not performed [13]. Moreover, ac4C is intrinsically labile: neutral-pH storage at room temperature for 18 h results in ∼25% deacetylation, and even isotopically labeled internal standards are prone to similar degradation, compromising accurate quantification [89, 90]. Despite optimized ionization and fragmentation, the sensitivity of conventional LC–MS may be inadequate for reliably detecting ac4C in mRNA [89] (Fig. 3A). Importantly, LC–MS does not provide transcript- or nucleotide-level positional information, and therefore cannot resolve site-specific dynamics without additional fractionation or complementary mapping approaches [91].

Antibody-based immunodetection

Acetylated RNA immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (acRIP-Seq) is a high-throughput method for transcriptome-wide profiling of ac4C. In this approach, total RNA is extracted, fragmented, and incubated with specific anti-ac4C antibodies or control IgG to enrich modified RNA fragments, which are then purified, reverse-transcribed, amplified, and sequenced to map ac4C sites [92] (Fig. 3B). Applied to human mRNAs for the first time in 2018, acRIP-Seq identified over 4,000 ac4C peaks, linking ac4C to translation initiation, mRNA localization, and translational regulation [19]. Since then, acRIP-Seq has been widely adopted to profile ac4C in diverse biological and disease models [93, 94]. While this method enables the detection of thousands of ac4C-enriched regions, the observed enrichment can be influenced by factors such as mRNA abundance, sequence context, and antibody specificity or affinity, potentially leading to false positives or incomplete recovery [89]. To mitigate these issues, recent protocols employ validated monoclonal antibodies and optimized crosslinking conditions to improve sensitivity and reduce background noise [92]. Besides, acRIP-Seq does not achieve single-nucleotide resolution; instead, it identifies broad enriched regions (typically ~ 100–200 nt), precluding precise annotation of the modified cytidine and limiting quantitative interpretation at the site level. Moreover, enrichment signals do not directly reflect modification stoichiometry, as highly expressed transcripts are preferentially recovered regardless of the true fraction of acetylated cytidines, potentially conflating expression level with modification abundance [13].

Chemical reduction-based sequencing (ac4C-Seq)

ac4C-Seq is a chemical-based sequencing method that enables quantitative, single-nucleotide resolution mapping of ac4C. The method exploits two chemical properties of ac4C: its N4-acetylation withdraws electron density from the pyridine ring, making it susceptible to reduction by hydride donors, and its hydrolytic lability allows deacetylation under mild alkaline conditions [89, 95]. These properties create a distinctive chemical signature detectable as C → T transitions in sequencing, which can be abrogated by alkali pre-treatment. In ac4C-Seq, sodium cyanoborohydride reduces ac4C to N4-acetyl-3,4,5,6-tetrahydrocytidine under acidic conditions, and the reduced nucleobase is misread as U instead of C during reverse transcription, producing characteristic C-to-T transitions detectable by high-throughput sequencing [14, 96] (Fig. 3C).

This approach provides a direct and quantitative readout of ac4C at nucleotide resolution and allows assessment of modification kinetics without relying on antibodies. Compared with immunoprecipitation-based methods, ac4C-Seq minimizes bias and can be combined with antibody-based enrichment to improve sensitivity. The library preparation can be completed within four days, enabling transcriptome-wide profiling under diverse physiological conditions. Notably, ac4C-Seq suffers from a critical technical limitation: it exclusively leverages C → T misincorporation detection for the annotation and quantification of ac4C sites. This strategy is prone to underestimating the actual ac4C modification abundance within RNAs, given that the reduced ac4C nucleobase is capable of eliciting reverse transcription termination events that interfere with accurate signal capture [97]. Moreover, reduction efficiency is highly sensitive to reaction conditions—including reagent choice (e.g., NaBH₄ vs. NaBH₃CN), pH, temperature, and incubation time—resulting in substantial variability across studies and potentially contributing to discordant ac4C site maps reported in the literature [13]. Incomplete chemical conversion, RNA secondary structure constraints, and limited accessibility of ac4C residues can further generate false negatives, while harsh reaction conditions may induce unintended RNA damage or off-target modifications that complicate data interpretation [13]. Additionally, negative controls such as chemical deacetylation or NAT10 depletion may not fully eliminate ac4C signals, introducing challenges in distinguishing true modification-dependent signals from residual background. However, recent methodological improvements, including the use of modified dNTPs during reverse transcription, optimized reduction controls, and improved sequencing adaptors, enhance sensitivity, signal-to-noise ratio, and accuracy, addressing limitations such as partial selectivity and underestimation due to C-to-T reliance [98].

Linear Amplification of Complementary DNA Ends and Sequencing (LACE-Seq)

The limited availability of rare cell types has posed significant challenges for ac4C profiling. LACE-Seq method, originally developed for mapping RNA-binding protein targets in single or low-input cells [99], has since been adapted for low-input ac4C detection (Fig. 3D). This strategy markedly reduces RNA requirements while preserving high sensitivity and specificity, yielding stronger ac4C signals on key maternal transcripts and outperforming conventional acRIP-Seq in both reproducibility and signal-to-noise ratio. Recently, LACE-Seq was successfully applied to profile NAT10-mediated mRNA acetylation during mouse oocyte meiotic maturation, enabling transcriptome-wide ac4C mapping from as few as 50 oocytes. However, the levels of ac4C-modified maternal mRNAs are excessively low at developmental stages, which may not be effectively detected by the ac4C LACE-Seq method [49]. Furthermore, the linear amplification steps required for low-input RNA may introduce coverage bias and preferential amplification of background fragments, thereby affecting quantitative comparability between samples. When ac4C stoichiometry is extremely low, even sensitive low-input approaches may fail to reliably capture true modification events, highlighting an inherent sensitivity floor in rare-cell applications [49].

Computational prediction

Although ac4C modifications are widely distributed across the human transcriptome, only a few modified transcripts have been detected using previously described experimental methods. To complement these approaches, several computational tools have been developed to predict ac4C sites based on RNA sequence and structural features. Early work includes PACES (2019), a random forest-based model that uses position-specific dinucleotide sequence profiles (PSDSP) and k-nucleotide frequencies (KNF) to identify potential acetylation motifs in human mRNAs [69]. While PACES performs well in cross-validation and independent benchmarks, it predicts motifs rather than exact sites, struggles with repetitive CXX motifs, and cannot generalize across tissues or species. Building on this, Alam et al. introduced XG-ac4C (2020), which employs eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) combined with electron–ion interaction pseudo-potentials (EIIP) and trinucleotide-based PseEIIP, achieving improved predictive performance [100]. Subsequent models have further advanced ac4C site prediction: DeepAc4C (2021) integrates convolutional neural networks with physicochemical properties and distributed sequence representations [101]. iRNA-ac4C (2023) uses gradient-boosted decision trees that incorporates nucleotide composition, chemical properties, and cumulative nucleotide frequency [102]; and the transformer-based TransAC4C (2024) enables cross-species ac4C site prediction by integrating sequence and structural features [103]. Additionally, ac4C-AFL employs adaptive feature learning directly on raw mRNA sequences for accurate site identification [104] (Fig. 3E). Together, these computational tools provide a powerful resource for high-throughput prediction and functional analysis of ac4C modifications across diverse contexts. However, most predictive models are trained on datasets generated by antibody enrichment or chemical reduction methods, which themselves contain technical biases; consequently, computational outputs may propagate upstream experimental artifacts. In addition, sequence-based models often fail to incorporate dynamic factors such as RNA structure, NAT10 availability, cellular state, and subcellular localization, limiting their ability to accurately reflect context-dependent acetylation in vivo. Therefore, computational prediction should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires rigorous experimental validation to confirm site specificity and functional relevance.

Summary and functional implications

The enzymatic machinery, RNA-type–specific distribution, and rapidly evolving detection technologies portray ac4C as a conserved yet highly adaptable layer of the epitranscriptome. By enhancing transcript stability, optimizing translation, and modulating RNA–protein interactions, ac4C equips cells with a rapid-response layer of gene-expression control. These transcriptome-wide mapping efforts now reveal a unifying theme: NAT10-mediated ac4C is not a passive posttranscriptional mark but an active regulatory system with the capacity to remodel cellular physiology.

Building on this mechanistic foundation, the subsequent section focuses on how the NAT10/ac4C axis operates within higher-order physiological contexts. In particular, we examine its emerging role in orchestrating innate and adaptive immune responses, shaping inflammatory circuits, and contributing to immune remodeling in disease. These observations position ac4C as a previously underappreciated but increasingly indispensable node linking RNA epigenetics to immune homeostasis.

NAT10/ac4C as a master regulator of immunity

NAT10 functions as a central regulator of RNA stability, translation efficiency, and chromatin accessibility, establishing a foundation for cellular differentiation and functional specialization. For example, NAT10 stabilizes ANP32B mRNA, an acidic nuclear phosphoprotein involved in histone regulation and nucleosome dynamics that influences transcriptional programs and lineage commitment [105], via ac4C modification to modulate chromatin architecture and embryonic stem-cell differentiation [58], a mechanism that may extend to immune cell development and homeostasis. Beyond developmental settings, NAT10 orchestrates immune processes by fine-tuning the stability and translation of key immune transcripts, thereby shaping the activation, differentiation, and effector functions of both innate and adaptive immune cells. Notably, dysregulation of NAT10-mediated ac4C modification has been associated with autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and tumor immune evasion.

Innate immunity: macrophage polarization and neutrophil pyroptosis

NAT10 has emerged as a pivotal regulator of innate immune responses, coordinating macrophage and neutrophil functions across diverse inflammatory and pathological contexts. In macrophages, NAT10 modulates activation, polarization, and metabolic programming under both acute and chronic settings. During acute inflammatory responses such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced endotoxemia, NAT10 expression is markedly upregulated in macrophages, where it stabilizes ETS2 mRNA, which encodes an ETS-family transcription factor that drives MAPK-dependent pro-inflammatory gene expression [106]. This stabilization enhances ETS2 translation and thereby amplifies cytokine production and inflammation-induced cardiac dysfunction [107]. Similarly, in LPS-stimulated macrophages and periodontal disease models, NAT10 enhances NOX2 expression and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, promoting NF-κB–mediated inflammatory gene expression, a process reversed by ROS scavengers or NF-κB inhibitors [108].

In chronic diseases and cancer, NAT10 exerts context-dependent effects on macrophage polarization. In atherosclerosis, NAT10 maintains pro-inflammatory M1 polarization by stabilizing TLR9 mRNA, a pattern-recognition receptor that senses CpG DNA and triggers innate inflammatory cascades [109]; loss of NAT10 skews macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby alleviating vascular inflammation and plaque formation [110]. In contrast, within tumor microenvironments (TME), NAT10 fosters M2-type polarization through multiple mechanisms. In gastric cancer (GC) liver metastasis, it stabilizes CXCL2 and KLF5 transcripts via ac4C modification, which enhances M2 macrophage recruitment and immune suppression. A positive feedback loop involving M2-derived oncostatin M (OSM) and STAT3 signaling further upregulates NAT10 in tumor cells, amplifying tumor-promoting inflammation [111]. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), NAT10 promotes tumor growth by stabilizing CCL2 mRNA through ac4C modification, thereby enhancing M2 macrophage infiltration [112]. Beyond cell-autonomous regulation, NAT10 also mediates intercellular communication between tumor cells and macrophages. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, tumor-derived exosomes deliver NAT10 protein into macrophages, where it acetylates FASN mRNA to promote fatty acid synthesis and lipid metabolic reprogramming. This metabolic shift reinforces M2 polarization and contributes to an immunosuppressive TME [113] (Fig. 4A).

NAT10 regulates immune cell functions through ac4C-modified RNAs. In neutrophils, NAT10 modulates pyroptosis during sepsis through ac4C-mediated stabilization of ULK1 mRNA, which activates the cGAS–STING pathway and downstream inflammasome signaling. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of NAT10 attenuates these pyroptotic responses and alleviates inflammation and tissue injury [114] (Fig. 4B).

Adaptive immunity: T and B lymphocyte responses

In addition to innate immunity, NAT10 shapes adaptive immune regulation through ac4C-dependent stabilization of key transcripts that govern T lymphocyte activation, proliferation, effector functions, and exhaustion, while concurrently modulating B-cell signaling, differentiation and antibody production.

In T cells, NAT10 is dynamically expressed across CD4⁺, CD8⁺, and tumor-infiltrating subsets, modulating activation, proliferation, effector functions, and exhaustion through ac4C modification of critical transcripts. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), CD4⁺ T cells display a reprogrammed ac4C landscape characterized by elevated NAT10 expression and aberrant activation of inflammatory pathways, suggesting a role for NAT10 in abnormal T cell activation and cytokine production [115]. NAT10 further promotes T cell activation and proliferation by acetylating and stabilizing RACK1 protein, thereby regulating ribosome formation and cellular metabolism, underscoring its essential role in immune regulation [116].

Within the TME, NAT10 exerts complex regulatory effects on T cells. In prostate cancer, NAT10 stabilizes immunosuppressive transcripts via ac4C, suppressing CD8+ T cell activation and cytotoxicity, thereby promoting immune evasion [117]. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), NAT10/ac4C stabilizes JunB mRNA, enhancing glycolytic metabolism and inducing an immunosuppressive environment that indirectly impairs T cell function [118]. Similarly, in NPC, NAT10-dependent stabilization of DDX5 mRNA—which encodes a DEAD-box RNA helicase that functions in transcriptional regulation, RNA processing and the control of inflammatory signaling [119]— leads to upregulation of HMGB1, suppression of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell function, and promotion of tumor immune evasion. Notably, NAT10 knockdown enhances sensitivity to anti–PD-1 therapy, highlighting its potential as a combinatorial immunotherapy target [120]. Conversely, NAT10-mediated stabilization of MYC mRNA promotes T cell proliferation and antiviral immunity [121], highlighting a context-dependent role in immune activation. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), NAT10 maintains the pathogenicity of CD4⁺ T cells by stabilizing BCL2-associated athanogene 3 (Bag3) mRNA, encoding a co-chaperone that promotes apoptosis resistance and autophagy and thereby supports T cell survival under stress [122]. NAT10 deficiency impairs steady-state T cell development and pathogenic function [123]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), NAT10-driven ac4C modification of LAMB3 mRNA activates the FAK/ERK pathway, upregulates PD-L1 expression, and suppresses CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxicity, thereby driving tumor progression [124] (Fig. 4C).

In B lymphocytes, NAT10 exhibits dual and context-dependent functions. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), NAT10 is upregulated and stabilizes SLC30A9 mRNA, which encodes a mitochondrial zinc transporter implicated in cellular metabolic homeostasis [125]; this stabilization activates the AMPK/mTOR pathway to promote tumor cell proliferation and therapeutic resistance [126]. Conversely, under normal immune conditions, NAT10 mediates ac4C modification of NIK mRNA in B cells, enhancing its stability, activating the non-canonical NF-κB pathway, and promoting IgA secretion to support mucosal immune homeostasis [127]. These findings indicate that NAT10 not only functions as an oncogenic driver in B-cell malignancy but also participates in maintaining normal B-cell function, highlighting its dual roles in immune homeostasis and pathological states (Fig. 4D).

Collectively, these findings establish NAT10 as an emerging master regulator of immune homeostasis. Through ac4C-dependent stabilization of key transcripts, NAT10 links metabolic, inflammatory, and transcriptional programs, coordinating innate and adaptive immune responses in a context-dependent manner. Its roles in acute inflammation, autoimmunity, and tumor immune evasion underscore NAT10 as a promising target for therapeutic immune modulation.

NAT10/ac4C as a central node of metabolic reprogramming

Beyond its central role in immune regulation, NAT10 exerts broad control over cellular metabolism mainly through ac4C-mediated stabilization of key transcripts. By modulating glucose, lipid, and amino acid pathways, NAT10 integrates RNA epitranscriptomic regulation with metabolic reprogramming, acting as a central node that links immune signaling and metabolic circuitry across diverse physiological and pathological contexts.

Governing glucose metabolism and glycolytic flux

NAT10 reprograms glycolysis in cancer through ac4C modification of critical transcripts. In cervical cancer, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of FOXP1 mRNA enhances its translation. FOXP1 encodes a forkhead box transcription factor that regulates gene expression programs involved in metabolism, proliferation, and immune modulation [128], and its upregulation increases GLUT4- and KHK-dependent glycolytic flux, lactate production, tumor progression, and immunosuppression [129]. Under hypoxia, NAT10 acetylates SEPT9 mRNA in GC, which encodes a septin family GTP-binding protein implicated in hypoxia signaling [130]. ac4C-mediated stabilization of SEPT9 enhances HIF-1α activity, establishing a positive feedback loop that reinforces hypoxia-driven glycolytic reprogramming and sustains tumor growth [131]. In HCC, NAT10 promotes chemoresistance by acetylating ACLY, which encodes ATP citrate lyase, a key metabolic enzyme that converts citrate into acetyl-CoA in the cytosol and nucleus [132], thereby linking glucose-derived citrate metabolism to epigenetic and metabolic adaptation [43]. Similarly, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ac4C modification of ENO1 mRNA enhances glycolysis and inhibits apoptosis [61]. ENO1 encodes α-enolase, a glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate, a rate-limiting step in glycolytic flux and energy production, thereby supporting tumor metabolic reprogramming [133]. Moreover, in osteosarcoma, NAT10 drives YTHDC1-dependent m6A modifications of LDHA and PFKM mRNAs via ac4C acetylation, promoting lactate production and energy supply to support tumor growth [134] (Fig. 5). These findings illustrate a multilayered epitranscriptomic regulation of glycolysis, with cancer-type specificity.

Orchestrating lipid synthesis and storage

NAT10 exerts multifaceted control over lipid metabolism through ac4C-dependent stabilization of mRNAs encoding key enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis, uptake, oxidation, and storage. In cancer, NAT10 promotes lipid metabolic reprogramming by stabilizing ac4C-modified transcripts such as ELOVL6, ACSL1–4, ACADSB, and ACAT1, thereby sustaining anabolic lipid flux that supports tumor growth and survival [64]. Pharmacological inhibition of NAT10 with Remodelin disrupts mitochondrial fatty acid metabolism and reduces lipid accumulation in cancer cells [135], underscoring its role in lipid homeostasis and metabolic adaptation. In ovarian cancer, m⁶A-driven translation of NAT10 enhances ACOT7 mRNA acetylation, rewiring fatty acid metabolism to suppress ferroptosis and facilitate tumor progression [136], illustrating a cross-talk between ac4C and m⁶A epitranscriptomic pathways.

Beyond cancer, NAT10 also governs physiological lipid homeostasis and metabolic disease. In the liver, NAT10 promotes lipogenesis by stabilizing Srebf1, Scap, and Srebp-1c mRNAs, while its suppression alleviates metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and steatohepatitis (MASH) [137, 138]. NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of KLF9 mRNA further promotes adipogenesis, highlighting its role in lipid anabolic and differentiation programs [94]. Interestingly, during cardiac regeneration, NAT10 suppresses mitochondrial genes Uqcr11 and Uqcrb independently of ac4C, affecting mitochondrial lipid-related function and tissue repair [139] (Fig. 5). Collectively, these studies position NAT10 as a key epitranscriptomic hub that integrates ac4C- and m6A-dependent RNA regulatory networks to coordinate lipid metabolism across oncogenic, metabolic, and regenerative contexts.

Regulating amino acid metabolism and utilization

NAT10 also regulates amino acid metabolism to support tumor growth. In acute myeloid leukemia, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of SLC1A4 and HOXA9/MENIN mRNAs enhances exogenous serine uptake and de novo serine biosynthesis, promoting leukemogenesis and maintaining leukemia stem cell stemness [93]. In osteosarcoma, NAT10 stabilizes ATF4 mRNA via ac4C modification, activating ASNS transcription and promoting asparagine biosynthesis to sustain tumor growth and metastatic potential. Targeting NAT10 disrupts this ATF4/ASNS axis and suppresses osteosarcoma progression, underscoring its pivotal role in amino acid metabolic adaptation [140] (Fig. 5). Together, NAT10 emerges as an important regulator linking RNA acetylation to amino acid metabolism critical for tumor proliferation and survival.

Intersection of metabolism and immunity

NAT10 operates at the nexus of immune regulation and metabolic reprogramming through ac4C-dependent stabilization of key transcripts. In tumors, NAT10-driven glycolytic and lipid reprogramming shapes the microenvironment to suppress antitumor immunity. In cervical cancer, for instance, NAT10-mediated enhancement of FOXP1 translation elevates GLUT4 and KHK expression, which not only boosts glycolysis in tumor cells but also fosters an immunosuppressive milieu by acidifying the extracellular environment and altering the cytokine landscape, thereby inhibiting T cell function and promoting immune evasion [129]. Under hypoxic conditions, NAT10 induction and SEPT9 stabilization amplify HIF-1α signaling, reinforcing a positive feedback loop that drives glycolytic addiction and tumor proliferation [131]. Such metabolic rewiring may further alter nutrient availability within the TME, potentially intensifying metabolic competition with infiltrating T cells and deepening immune dysfunction.

Exosomal NAT10 derived from tumor cells also contributes to metabolic–immune coupling. By enhancing ac4C acetylation of FASN mRNA in macrophages, it promotes lipid accumulation and M2 polarization [113], fostering immunosuppressive niches that may predispose to T cell exhaustion and dendritic cell dysfunction. Beyond its cell-intrinsic effects, NAT10 regulates immune–metabolic crosstalk through secreted factors. In GC liver metastasis, NAT10-mediated stabilization of CXCL2 and KLF5 recruits M2 macrophages, which secrete OSM to activate STAT3 in tumor cells, reinforcing NAT10 expression and establishing a feedforward NAT10–CXCL2/KLF5–M2–OSM–STAT3 loop [111]. This axis may also modulate metabolic adaptation and immune suppression in the TME, pointing to a potential link between NAT10 activity and tumor immune–metabolic interactions.

Thus, NAT10 functions as an epitranscriptomic integrator of immunometabolic signaling, coordinating nutrient utilization and immune effector functions. Its dual role—enhancing anabolic metabolism in tumor cells while reprogramming the metabolic adaptability of immune cells—positions NAT10 as a central node in pathologies such as cancer, autoimmunity, and infection, offering a promising target to recalibrate maladaptive immunometabolic circuits and restore immune homeostasis.

NAT10/ac4C in human diseases

Cancer: driving tumorigenesis, immune evasion, and metabolic addiction

Building on its central role in coordinating immune responses and metabolic programs, NAT10 is frequently dysregulated in various cancers. Through ac4C-dependent modulation of mRNA stability and translation, as well as protein acetylation, aberrant NAT10 activity drives tumor initiation, progression, and therapy resistance. Its oncogenic functions can be broadly categorized into three interconnected mechanisms:

aPromoting proliferation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis

Promoting proliferation, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis

NAT10 drives tumor aggressiveness across multiple cancer types by orchestrating proliferation, EMT, metastasis, and therapy resistance. In bladder cancer, it stabilizes key transcripts such as BCL9L, SOX4, and AKT1, promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion [141, 142]. Concurrently, NAT10 contributes to therapy resistance by acetylating DNA repair–related transcripts, thereby enhancing cisplatin resistance and supporting tumor survival under genotoxic stress [141]. Its activity is further modulated by protein interactors; for example, CLIC3 binds to NAT10 and inhibits its ac4C modification of p21 mRNA, reducing p21 expression and accelerating cell cycle progression and tumor growth [143].

In prostate cancer, NAT10-mediated acetylation of HMGA1 and KRT8 drives EMT and metastatic dissemination [144]. In gastric cancer (GC), multiple NAT10-dependent pathways converge to promote tumor aggressiveness. The ac4C modification of COL5A1 enhances EMT and invasiveness in Helicobacter pylori-associated GC [145], while H. pylori infection also induces NAT10 to stabilize MDM2 mRNA, thereby suppressing p53 signaling and promoting proliferation [146]. Neutrophil extracellular traps augment NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of SMYD2 mRNA, further facilitating cancer cell migration and invasion [147]. In addition, NAT10 undergoes phase separation to regulate YTHDF1 RNA splicing, supporting GC progression [148]. NAT10 overexpression activates the AKT pathway, promoting EMT and tumorigenesis [149], and enhances TNC mRNA ac4C modification, further driving EMT and metastatic potential [150]. Interestingly, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) provides an antitumor counterbalance in GC: its envelope glycoprotein BALF4 promotes NAT10 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby suppressing tumor progression [151]. This loss-of-function evidence not only underscores the indispensable role of NAT10 in sustaining GC aggressiveness but also reveals a viral strategy to attenuate oncogenic signaling through NAT10 depletion.

In colorectal cancer (CRC), NAT10 stabilizes KIF23 mRNA, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to promote tumor progression [152]. Moreover, NAT10 promotes micronuclei formation and activates the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), inducing genomic instability and sustaining cancer cell proliferation [153]. It also facilitates CRC progression by promoting PPAN-dependent DNA damage repair, maintaining genomic integrity to support tumor survival [154]. At the metastatic level, competitive binding between DDX21 and SIRT7 enhances NAT10-mediated ac4C modification, which promotes CRC metastasis and angiogenesis [155]. In parallel, NAT10 stabilizes NANOGP8 mRNA, maintaining cancer stemness and chemoresistance, and ultimately contributing to malignant progression [156].

In NPC, NAT10 acetylates the lncRNA SIMALR, which activates eEF1A2 to enhance the translation of integrin subunits ITGB4 and ITGA6. This cascade strengthens cell adhesion, migration, and invasion, illustrating how NAT10 regulates noncoding RNAs to drive EMT and metastatic phenotypes in NPC [83]. Furthermore, NAT10 inhibition triggers ac4C-dependent ferroptosis, overcoming sorafenib resistance and highlighting its role in regulating metabolic vulnerabilities in NPC [157].

In melanoma, NAT10 promotes melanogenesis and melanoma proliferation by regulating microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) expression [158]. In parallel, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of DDX41 and ZNF746 enhances chemoresistance, further supporting its oncogenic function by stabilizing transcripts critical for tumor survival under therapeutic stress [159].

In esophageal cancer (ESCA), NAT10 exerts multifaceted oncogenic effects. It catalyzes ac4C modification of tRNA to enhance EGFR mRNA translation, thereby activating EGFR signaling, driving proliferation, and promoting gefitinib resistance [46]. Concurrently, ac4C acetylation of ATP6V0E1 mRNA increases lysosomal acidification and promotes metastasis [160]. NAT10 also governs ncRNA networks to reinforce tumor aggressiveness; for instance, ac4C modification of lncRNA CTC-490G23.2 facilitates PTBP1-mediated CD44 alternative splicing, promoting migration and metastasis [20]. Clinically, elevated NAT10 expression correlates with poor survival and enhanced tumor proliferation, highlighting its integral role in ESCA progression [161].

In NSCLC, NAT10 promotes malignancy through convergent signaling pathways, including EMT signaling, NF-κB activation, TRIM44/PI3K/AKT-mediated proliferation, and SGK2 mRNA acetylation, which impairs autophagy. Conversely, NAT10 silencing enhances cisplatin sensitivity, revealing its broad impact on proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance [59, 162–164].

In HCC, NAT10 sustains tumorigenesis through diverse molecular programs. It stabilizes mutant p53 to maintain proliferation and disease progression [165], promotes HMGB2 translation via eEF2 to drive tumor growth [75], and activates the ac4C–DDIAS–PI3K–Akt axis to enhance survival signaling [166]. Moreover, NAT10 facilitates migration, invasion, and metastasis through both nucleolar relocalization and EMT induction [72, 167], emphasizing its pleiotropic oncogenic potential in HCC.

In cervical cancer, circMAST1 binds NAT10 and blocks its ac4C modification of YAP mRNA, thereby reducing YAP stability and translation and inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and invasion [168]. In oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), NAT10 acetylates MMP1 mRNA, enhancing MMP1 expression and promoting EMT, migration, and invasion [169]. In osteosarcoma, NAT10 inhibition by Remodelin suppresses proliferation and migration [170], while YY1-driven NAT10 activation increases β3 mRNA ac4C modification, enhancing adhesion, migration, invasion, and metastatic potential [171].

Extending its oncogenic role beyond solid tumors, NAT10 also drives hematologic malignancies such as multiple myeloma. It acetylates CEP170 mRNA to enhance translation efficiency and stabilizes GPR37 mRNA, collectively promoting cancer cell proliferation and disease progression [172, 173]. Together, these studies establish NAT10 as a pan-cancer epitranscriptomic driver, whose activity—whether modulated by pharmacological inhibitors, transcriptional regulators, or direct RNA acetylation—orchestrates proliferation, metastasis, and therapy resistance across diverse malignancies (Fig. 6).

bShaping the immunosuppressive TME

Shaping the immunosuppressive TME

Beyond these intrinsic effects, NAT10 extends its oncogenic influence to the TME, shaping immunosuppressive niches that facilitate immune evasion. In prostate cancer, NAT10 disrupts the CCL25/CCR9 axis, impairing CD8+ T cell chemotaxis and cytotoxic infiltration [117]. Across multiple tumor types, NAT10 is involved in regulating expression of immune checkpoints, including PD-L1 and CTLA-4, thereby reinforcing immune evasion. In PDAC, NAT10 stabilizes LAMB3 mRNA via ac4C, activating FAK/ERK signaling to enhance proliferation and PD-L1 expression [124]. Similarly, in multiple cancers, NAT10 acetylates NPM1 to increase PD-L1 transcription, reinforcing immunosuppressive signaling [174]. In TNBC, NAT10 drives malignant progression via ac4C-mediated JunB stabilization and glycolysis; notably, NAT10 inhibition elevates CTLA-4 on T cells, and when combined with anti-CTLA-4 therapy, effectively restores anti-tumor immunity [118]. In cervical cancer, NAT10 paradoxically promotes T-cell infiltration yet establishes an immunosuppressive milieu through glycolytic remodeling [129]. In GC, NAT10 acts as a pivotal regulator that couples metastatic progression with immune suppression [175], while in GC liver metastasis, it stabilizes CXCL2 and KLF5 mRNA to recruit M2 macrophages, fostering an immune-tolerant niche [111]. In CRC, NAT10 expression correlates with immunosuppressive signaling and poor patient prognosis, suggesting that its inhibition could enhance antitumor immunity and sensitize cancer cells to therapy [176]. Additionally, in NPC, NAT10 suppresses T-cell immunity and promotes tumor progression through the DDX5/HMGB1 axis [120], further underscoring its central role in shaping an immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 6).

cDriving metabolic reprogramming

Driving metabolic reprogramming

NAT10 serves as a central regulator of cancer metabolism, orchestrating glycolysis, redox balance, amino acid, and lipid homeostasis to support tumor growth and adaptability. In cervical cancer, it enhances glycolysis by stabilizing FOXP1 mRNA, upregulating GLUT4 and KHK, and increasing lactate production [129]. Under hypoxic conditions in GC, NAT10 stabilizes SEPT9 mRNA, forming a feedforward loop with HIF-1α to sustain glycolytic addiction [131]. In HCC, NAT10 acetylates ACLY, functionally linking glucose-derived citrate metabolism to epigenetic regulation and chemoresistance [43], and stabilizes SLC7A11 mRNA to repress ferroptosis and support metastatic potential [177]. Additionally, NAT10 acetylates HSP90AA1 mRNA, amplifying ER stress signaling and promoting both metastasis and lenvatinib resistance [178]. In NSCLC, NAT10-mediated ac4C acetylation of ENO1 mRNA enhances glycolysis and inhibits apoptosis, thereby facilitating tumor growth and metabolic adaptation [61]. In colon cancer, NAT10 promotes tumor progression by inhibiting ferroptosis through ac4C-mediated stabilization of FSP1 mRNA [179]. In osteosarcoma, NAT10 promotes metabolic plasticity through multiple routes, including stabilizing ATF4 mRNA to activate ASNS and asparagine biosynthesis [140], and enhancing YTHDC1-dependent m6A modifications of LDHA and PFKM to fuel glycolysis [134]. In OC, m6A-driven NAT10 translation facilitates fatty acid metabolic rewiring by acetylating ACOT7 mRNA, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and promoting tumorigenesis [136] (Fig. 6).

Collectively, these studies position NAT10 as a multimodal oncoprotein that integrates epitranscriptomic regulation with metabolic and immune signaling. Through ac4C-dependent control of key transcripts, NAT10 endows cancer cells with metabolic resilience, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance, establishing it as a nodal regulator of tumor adaptability and progression.

Metabolic and inflammatory diseases: MASLD, sepsis, and autoimmunity

Beyond cancer, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification plays a key role in non-malignant diseases characterized by immunometabolic dysregulation. In MASLD and MASH, NAT10 is upregulated and stabilizes Srebf1, Scap, and Srebp-1c mRNAs, activating lipogenic enzymes and promoting hepatic lipid accumulation [137, 138] (Fig. 7A). Similarly, ac4C modification of KLF9 mRNA promotes adipogenesis, further highlighting NAT10’s role in lipid metabolism and obesity [94]. This lipogenic shift exacerbates liver steatosis and inflammation, illustrating NAT10’s role in metabolic dysregulation.

In sepsis, NAT10 drives multi-organ injury through diverse mechanisms, contributing to immunometabolic dysregulation. It stabilizes GABABR1 mRNA, leading to neuronal hyperinhibition and cognitive dysfunction [180], and activates the ROS/NLRP3 pathway to promote muscle atrophy [181]. In the lung, NAT10 exacerbates sepsis-associated pulmonary injury by integrating metabolic and immune pathways: ac4C acetylation of TFRC mRNA promotes iron overload and ferroptosis [182], modification of ULK1 mRNA activates the STING pathway and induces neutrophil pyroptosis [114], and acetylation of HMGB1 mRNA impairs mitochondrial function in epithelial cells, aggravating conditions such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa–induced acute lung injury [183]. Interestingly, neutrophil-specific NAT10 expression may confer protective effects by modulating pyroptosis in sepsis, highlighting its cell type–specific roles in inflammation [184] (Fig. 7B).

In autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, NAT10 similarly modulates immune and metabolic signaling. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), NAT10 and ac4C levels are elevated in synovium and fibroblast‑like synoviocytes, where ac4C-dependent stabilization of PTX3 mRNA promotes synovial fibroblast invasion and inflammation [185]. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), transcriptomic profiling of CD4⁺ T cells reveals altered ac4C patterns and dysregulated NAT10 expression associated with immune and metabolic pathways [115], and mechanistic work suggests ac4C modifications of genes such as USP18 and GPX1 contribute to pathogenic signaling. In ankylosing spondylitis (AS), reduced NAT10 expression correlates with disease activity, implying a complex regulatory role in autoimmune pathogenesis [186]. Beyond classical autoimmune diseases, recent studies also identify a critical role for NAT10 in T cell-mediated inflammatory disorders in mouse models, where NAT10 deficiency impairs T cell activation and proliferation by modulating RACK1 stability [116]. Moreover, in inflammatory bowel disease, NAT10 expression in CD4⁺ T cells from ulcerative colitis patients correlates with inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, and NAT10 deficiency disrupts T cell homeostasis and pathogenicity [123]. These heterogeneous observations underscore that NAT10 expression and activity can be modulated differently across autoimmune and inflammatory settings, reflecting disease‑specific and cell type–specific roles. Collectively, these findings position NAT10 as a critical regulator of immunometabolic crosstalk in inflammatory and metabolic diseases (Fig. 7C).

Other pathologies: cardiovascular diseases and aging

In addition to its roles in immunometabolism, NAT10 participates in fundamental cellular processes that underlie diverse pathologies. It is indispensable for oocyte maturation and female fertility through regulation of maternal mRNA decay [49, 187], and ensures mitotic fidelity via spindle assembly and chromatin regulation [32, 37]. NAT10 also modulates DNA damage responses and apoptosis through interactions with key regulators such as p53 [36], and contributes to nuclear architecture and integrity, as exemplified by its ability to correct nuclear defects and ameliorate aging-related phenotypes in Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS) [188].

In cardiovascular pathologies, NAT10 contributes to disease progression through multiple mechanisms. During cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling, NAT10 upregulation enhances ac4C modification of CD47 and ROCK2 mRNAs, inhibiting macrophage phagocytosis and promoting fibrosis [189]. In cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury, it stabilizes Mybbp1a mRNA, activates p53, and suppresses SLC7A11, thereby aggravating ferroptotic damage [190]. Conversely, in heart regeneration models, NAT10 inhibition reduces expression of mitochondrial genes such as Uqcr11 and Uqcrb, affecting lipid metabolism and repair processes [139] (Fig. 7D).

In aging-related contexts including HGPS, NAT10 inhibition improves nuclear integrity, reduces DNA damage, partially modulates lipid metabolism, and ameliorates cardiovascular and pulmonary phenotypes [65] (Fig. 7E), highlighting the interplay between epitranscriptomic regulation, aging, and metabolic homeostasis. Furthermore, NAT10 is implicated in infection-related pathologies by stabilizing viral and host RNAs (e.g., KSHV PAN RNA and LY6E mRNA) [23, 87, 191, 192], further connecting ac4C-mediated epitranscriptomic regulation to infection-related diseases.

Taken together, these findings illustrate that NAT10’s multifaceted roles in RNA modification, immunometabolism, and fundamental cellular homeostasis contribute broadly to human disease, spanning metabolic disorders, inflammatory syndromes, cardiovascular pathologies, aging, and infection.

Therapeutic targeting of the NAT10/ac4C axis

Current landscape of NAT10 inhibitors: from remodelin to novel compounds

NAT10-mediated ac4C modification plays a pivotal role in a wide range of diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, sepsis, aging-related conditions, and immune dysfunctions. By regulating mRNA stability and translation efficiency, NAT10 influences essential cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming, establishing it as a promising therapeutic target, particularly in the context of cancer immunotherapy.

Current NAT10 inhibitors primarily function by targeting its acetyltransferase activity or disrupting its interaction with RNA substrates. Among these, Remodelin is one of the earliest identified NAT10 inhibitors, shown to specifically suppress NAT10 acetyltransferase activity and correct nuclear morphology defects in laminopathic cells [188]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that NAT10 inhibition by Remodelin can also modulate ac4C-mediated RNA regulation in cancer cells, affecting proliferation and migration [46, 135, 162]. Panobinostat, an FDA-approved histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, has also been found to bind directly to the NAT10 catalytic domain, demonstrating efficacy in preclinical models of HCC [75]. Through virtual screening approaches, additional compounds such as Paliperidone and AG-401 [140], and the newly identified G-749 have been screened and validated as potential NAT10 inhibitors [160]. These agents reduce ac4C modification levels, downregulate expression of key stress-responsive genes and suppress tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion—effects that are further enhanced in combination therapy regimens. Other promising candidates include Fludarabine [93], Fosaprepitant, Leucal, Dantrolene [193], and Cpd-155 [194], which have been screened for their potent NAT10 inhibitory activity and superior binding properties (Table 1). These compounds expand the arsenal of potential therapeutic agents for targeting NAT10 in human diseases.

Immunotherapeutic potential: boosting antitumor immunity and ameliorating immunometabolic diseases

The inhibition of NAT10 holds remarkable immunotherapeutic potential, offering a unique strategy to simultaneously target tumor-intrinsic vulnerabilities and reverse immunosuppression within the TME. By downregulating ac4C modification, NAT10 inhibitors can disrupt multiple pathways that are co-opted by cancer cells to sustain growth, evade immune surveillance, and establish metabolic dominance.

In the context of cancer immunotherapy, NAT10 inhibition may synergize with existing modalities such as immune checkpoint blockers. For instance, by suppressing the ac4C-mediated stabilization of PD-L1, CD47, and other immune checkpoint transcripts, NAT10 inhibitors could alleviate T cell exhaustion and enhance phagocytosis, thereby re-sensitizing tumors to immunotherapies. Moreover, given NAT10’s role in promoting glycolytic flux and lactate production—key contributors to TME acidification and T cell dysfunction—its inhibition could ameliorate the metabolic barriers to antitumor immunity. Combining NAT10 inhibitors with metabolic modulators or adoptive cell therapies may thus yield synergistic efficacy.

Beyond cancer, targeting NAT10 offers promising avenues for treating autoimmune and immunometabolic diseases. In conditions such as SLE and RA, where NAT10-mediated stabilization of inflammatory transcripts drives pathogenesis, inhibition could dampen aberrant immune activation. In metabolic disorders like MASLD/MASH, where NAT10 fosters lipogenesis and inflammation, inhibiting its activity may break the cycle of metabolic dysfunction and immune-mediated liver damage. Similarly, in sepsis-associated immunometabolic paralysis, modulating NAT10 might restore immune homeostasis and mitigate organ damage.

In summary, NAT10 represents a central node linking epitranscriptomic regulation to immune and metabolic pathways. Its inhibition offers a novel and complementary therapeutic approach not only for boosting antitumor immunity but also for ameliorating a range of immunometabolic diseases. With ongoing translational efforts, NAT10 inhibitors are poised to emerge as valuable assets in the future of immunotherapy.

Conclusion and outlook

NAT10-mediated ac4C modification constitutes a sophisticated epitranscriptomic mechanism that fine-tunes post-transcriptional gene expression at the intersection of immunity, metabolism, and cellular homeostasis. Through targeted acetylation of diverse RNAs, the NAT10/ac4C axis modulates transcript stability, translation efficiency, and molecular interactions, thereby orchestrating a broad spectrum of physiological and pathological processes from immune cell activation to anabolic reprogramming. In cancer, it drives a triad of pro-tumorigenic processes: intrinsic malignancy (proliferation, EMT, metastasis), metabolic adaptation, and immunosuppression. Analogous mechanisms, centered on the dysregulated immunometabolic transcripts, underpin its roles in autoimmune, septic, and metabolic inflammatory disorders. Supported by compelling preclinical evidence that its inhibition can reverse drug resistance and reinvigorate antitumor immunity, NAT10 emerges as a promising therapeutic target.

However, several key mechanistic black boxes remain to be illuminated to guide therapeutic development. A central challenge is to decipher the principles governing NAT10 substrate selection—how it prioritizes among mRNA, tRNA, rRNA, and non-coding RNAs in a context-dependent manner. This selectivity likely reflects multiple, overlapping factors: differences in NAT10 expression and subcellular localization, the presence of auxiliary RNA-binding proteins that guide substrate recognition, intrinsic RNA features such as sequence and structure, and cellular states including metabolic activity, stress responses, or disease-associated signaling. Together, these layers create a dynamic and context-specific ac4C landscape, explaining why NAT10 regulates distinct RNA subsets across different cell types, developmental stages, and pathological conditions. Equally important is to understand the dynamic regulation of ac4C in response to metabolic and inflammatory cues, and its potential cross-talk with other RNA modifications (e.g., m6A, m5C) within coordinated epitranscriptomic networks. Deconvoluting this multilayered complexity will require integrative approaches combining high-resolution mapping, structural biology, computational modeling, and physiologically relevant in vivo systems.

Concurrently, translational advancement faces three critical bottlenecks. First, achieving inhibitor selectivity and ensuring safety is paramount, given NAT10's involvement in essential cellular processes like ribosome biogenesis. This demands the development of next-generation inhibitors with optimized pharmacokinetic profiles and minimal off-target effects. Second, standardized, high-resolution methodologies for detecting ac4C modifications are lacking, creating barriers for patient stratification and pharmacodynamic monitoring as well as objective evaluation of therapeutic outcomes. Third, effective drug delivery systems—such as nanoparticle-based or tissue-specific platforms—are needed to enhance target engagement while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Looking ahead, the future of targeting the NAT10/ac4C axis will likely lie in rational combinatorial strategies. Beyond the development of highly selective NAT10 inhibitors with optimized pharmacokinetics and tissue- or immune cell–specific delivery, the most promising direction will be to integrate these agents with immunometabolic modulators—such as immune checkpoint blockade, or drugs targeting glycolytic flux, fatty acid oxidation, or amino acid metabolism, thereby reprogramming pathological immune–metabolic circuits. In summary, NAT10 serves as a pivotal epitranscriptomic hub, and its therapeutic exploration represents a frontier at the intersection of RNA biology, immunology, and metabolism. Bridging the deep mechanistic understanding of ac4C dynamics with cutting-edge drug development will be key to unlocking a new class of immunometabolic therapies.
