Optical parameters extraction of soil and its microplastics contamination using terahertz spectroscopy
Authors: M Meenaakumari, S Shoba, E Manikandan, KA Karthigeyan, SA Nithyapriyan, V Meikeerthivasan, S Hemeshwar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary focus of this study investigates the potential of continuous-wave terahertz frequency-domain spectroscopy(CW-THz-FDS) as a rapid, non-destructive technique for detecting microplastic contamination in soil. Focusing on low-density polyethylene (LDPE) as a model microplastic, we prepared soil mixtures with varying LDPE concentrations (1 %,5 %,10 %,15 % by weight). We used a THz-FDS system operating between 0.1 and 0.7 THz to analyze the optical properties (transmission coefficient, attenuation and refractive index) of these mixtures and LDPE samples separately. Notably both soil and LDPE particles were of micrometer scale. Our results revealed distinct, concentration-dependent variations in these properties. The refractive index for the higher concentration (15 %) ranged from 2.2 to 2.3, while the attenuation coefficient varies between 10 and 14 cm−1. Crucially, we identified polylactic acid [PLA] as a suitable sample holder material due to its high transmission in the THz range. Consequently, this study suggests that terahertz spectroscopy can be a promising technique for detecting microplastic contamination in soil, offering a non-destructive and sensitive method for environmental monitoring.Keywords: Terahertz spectroscopy, Micro plastic, Electro-optic sampling, Non-invasive

Introduction

Microplastics are plastic particles smaller than 5 mm in size, they are known to contaminate majority of aquatic ecosystems across the globe affecting numerous organisms and have gained worldwide attention over last decade [[1], [2], [3]]. Moreover, studies prove that the microplastics present in soil environments represent a large reservoir of microplastics [4]. During the past few decades, anthropogenic trash in terrestrial and aquatic habitats has drastically expanded, with 60–80 percent of it being plastic [5]. Around 280 million tons of plastics are being produced throughout the world on a mass scale, starting in the 1950s. All kinds of plastic waste crumble into smaller pieces when exposed to sunlight, wind, rain and other environmental conditions. Microplastics are generally of low density which means these fragments can easily be picked up by winds and can fly around to different places [6]. Important sources of microplastic pollution are found to be waste water discharge and bio solids, which are major by-products of waste-water-treatment plants. Plastic mulches are another rising concern. Mulch is a protective cover spread on top of the land in agriculture to suppress the growth of weeds and conserve water in crop production and landscaping [7].

Use of plastic much film, as covering material in agriculture consumes 2 % of global produced microplastics. Plastic mulch is commonly used as an alternative to organic mulch because of its cheap cost and easy removal process, it can result in degradation of soil health. Due to presence of the inorganic materials, there will be changes in soil structure with highly-probable risk of increase in contamination by microplastics, the crops and plants may be exposed to further stress and damage [8]. Places in and around landfills, industrial areas, terrestrial habitats, like agroecosystem are highly-likely to be exposed to microplastics manufactures to countless different polymers [9].

Microplastics are generally classified into two categories, primary microplastics and secondary microplastics. Primary microplastics are microplastics which are made on purpose, Microplastic beads used as scrubbers in cosmetic products is an example. Secondary microplastics form from the degradation of larger plastics [10]. In a study, twelve different kinds of secondary microplastics were evaluated where they were mixed with sandy soil (0.4 %) and incubated at 25 °C for 31 days to see how they would affect the soil's characteristics and microbiological activity. On the third, eleventh, and thirty-first days, the samples' pH levels, respiration rates, and enzyme activity were examined. The impacts of microplastic form and polymer kinds were discovered to change with incubation time [11].

The study demonstrates that soil is rife with microplastics. Microplastics in soil are still poorly understood, albeit [12]. A thorough comparison of the prevalence of microplastics in soil from various research communities is not possible due to the absence of harmonization or uniformity in the quantification and methodology utilized in sample collection, processing, and analysis [13]. As a result, it is crucial to provide a critical review of the sampling and analytical techniques used to evaluate the presence of microplastics in soil. It is necessary to clarify the interconnection and source-pathway of these particles that have been discovered in soil and other environmental matrices [14]. Microplastics present in soil can affect biophysical properties of the soil. The microplastics present in the soil can radically alter the bulk density, soil aggregate stability, and soil structure in bulk soils and microbe storehouse. Due to these changes, rate of evaporation of water, water availability, and microbial activities in the soil have been notably affected [15]. Microplastics are highly hydrophobic in nature i.e., they tend to stay away from water. To do this they react with other hydrophobic particles and form an outer layer with these particles, these particles can be toxic to environment. It is found that the microplastic pollution in land had resulted in decrease in population of species that live and maintain the health of the soil, insects like mites and larvae are few examples [16].

Generally, when plastics breakdown into smaller fragments they attain new physical properties and chemical properties and due to this change in properties they interact with their surroundings differently, the chemical effects are more hazardous at the decomposition stage. Additives like Bisphenol A(BPA) and phthalates which are known for disrupting hormone systems of organisms, leach out of these particles. Many techniques have been reported in the literature for the detection of microplastics in various mediums. In Ref. [17], the transmitted or ultra-short electromagnetic pulses is measured and, on a Fourier transform of recorder waveforms, precise determination of the permittivity of materials over a wide bandwidth is made possible. But it has less penetration depth than microwave thereby it cannot penetrate liquid or metal which also lead to false detection of hazardous substance in neutral sample.

The Hyperspectral Imaging of microplastics detection is reported which consists of two components push-broom scanner and ROV (remotely operated vehicle), this technology analyzes a wide range of light. Light hitting individual pixels is resolved into different spectral bands to distinguish them. Due to the limited imaging distance, image correction is required as light attenuation increases with increasing distance [18]. Using nuclear magnetic resonance(NMR) characterization approach, which makes use of several forms of 3D information, it is possible to study the microstructure, flaws, and crystallography of many materials with sub-micron resolution. The approach can identify many common plastic materials, apart from polytetrafluoroethylene, which contains no hydrogen, because the detection of plastic particles depends on the sensitivity of neutrons to hydrogen as components of the plastic compounds [19]. The near infrared spectroscopy is also adopted for the characterization of mciroplastics. In Ref. [20], the overtone vibration of plastic C-H bonds shows specific spectral reflectance characteristics in near-infrared wavelength ranges, which shows different polymer types. The system is unable to detect microplastics smaller than 60 μm. Thermal degradation is one of the disadvantages where the polymer changes its properties when the temperature is increased. So, many techniques are available but there is no standard solution for microplastics detection and quantification. As of now, micro Raman and FTIR are considered as a suitable method for the identification.

Materials and methods

Our proposed solution leverages terahertz frequency-domain spectroscopy (THz-FDS). THz-FDS is a powerful and versatile technique for material characterization, finding applications in the study of diverse materials such as organic compounds, polymers, and biological tissues. It operates by generating continuous terahertz radiation and subsequently measuring the frequency-domain waveforms following their interaction with the sample.

The THz-FDS method typically employs a photomixer source. This source utilizes two distributed feedback (DFB) lasers to generate slightly frequency-shifted continuous waves. A Schottky diode detector, known for its rapid response time, is then used for signal detection. The process commences with the generation of continuous waves at 1551 nm and 1549 nm by the DFB lasers. These waves are subsequently combined using a dedicated splitter/combiner module. Planar concave lenses are then employed to collimate the combined beam, ensuring its parallel nature. Finally, focusing lenses direct the beam onto the sample. Following interaction with the sample, the transmitted signal is detected by the Schottky diode. The incident terahertz wave induces a change in the sample's refractive index, which can be measured. Finally, the amplitude and phase information of the transmitted terahertz waves can be extracted from the detector. This data allows for the calculation of the frequency-dependent refractive index. By analysing the resonant frequency within the acquired spectrum, the type of microplastic can be identified.

The transmission data is the raw data obtained from measuring the THz wave after it passes through the material. The data represents the electric field strength of the THz waves changes with frequency. Identification of suitable sample holder

To proceed with the spectroscopy of the microplastics, it is important to find a suitable holder material which have less absorption and will not interfere much with the process. An ideal holder material should have the following characteristics - low Attenuation (Attenuation refers to the reduction of the intensity of radiation beam as it traverses matter), low Reflection Coefficient and High Transmission Coefficient. Hence, we have experimented with 5 different materials - ABS (Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene), Resin, PLA (Polylactic acid), PC (Polycarbonate), PET (Polyethylene terephthalate). These holder samples are fabricated using 3D printing technique and the images of the fabricated samples are given in Fig. 3a. We have done Terahertz- Frequency Domain Spectroscopy (THz-FDS) for these 5 materials and obtained the reflection as well as transmission coefficient which is shown in Fig. 3b and c correspondingly. In reflection mode, the sample is placed on top of a single photoconductive antenna or photo mixer, and the terahertz wave is directed onto the sample. Reflection mode is often used to study the properties of samples with a large surface area, such as bulk materials or powders. The ratio between the amplitude of the reflected wave to the incident wave is known as the reflection coefficient. It is a parameter which indicates the amount of reflection a wave faces due to the discontinuity in the transmission medium.

a) 3D printed sample holders Obtained b) Reflection c) Transmission coefficient of the 3D printed sample holders using THz-FDS d) Attenuation of different materials for the incident THz radiation.

From Fig. 3b it is inferred that the materials Resin, PLA, and PC have comparatively low reflection coefficient and can be considered as a sample holder material. Then setup is rearranged to transmission mode to find out and compare the transmission coefficients of our materials. Transmission coefficient is the measure of amount of an electromagnetic wave that passes through a surface or a sample element. In this mode, the sample is placed between the source and a detector, and the terahertz wave is directed through sample. This mode is used to measure the absorption properties of the sample, such as absorption coefficient and refractive index. It is often used to study properties of thin samples, such as thin films or coatings. The intensity or total power of a transmitted wave in relation to an incident wave is described by the transmission coefficient. From Fig. 3c it is noticed that PLA and PC have the highest transmission coefficients which we desire. Also using the THz-FDS transmission spectra it is possible to extract the attenuation of our materials. Attenuation is the reduction in the intensity of radiation beam as it travels through the target material. Fig. 3d represents the comparison of the attenuation of the 6 different sample holders.

From the analysis of reflection coefficients, transmission coefficients and attenuation, it is revealed that PLA's superior properties for THz applications. Notably, PLA exhibits high transparency in the THz range, allowing the signal to pass through with minimal interference. Additionally, its low cost, ease of fabrication using 3-D printing, and eco-friendly nature such as biodegradability and lower energy consumption during production make it a practical and sustainable choice [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Therefore, PLA was chosen as the Sample holder for further research.

Extracting the properties

Refractive index is an important parameter in THz-FDS because it is an important metric for the material which affects the propagation of terahertz pulses through the material. Refractive index of a material is related to its density and polarizability can provide information about its electronic and molecular structure. In addition, refractive index can also affect the absorption properties of a material.

From Fig. 7 it is understood that the microplastics has lower refractive index when comparing with soil samples this is because microplastic have lower molecular polarizability and density, which reduces their ability to bend or change the direction of light. But, in case of soil it has complex mixture of organic and inorganic materials, such as minerals, clay, and organic matter, which have a range of refractive indices and it is also evident that the refractive index decreases with increase in concentration of microplastic and in the untreated soil the difference is noticed below 0.3 THz. But, in case of treated soil where the organic compounds are removed the difference is obtained below 0.5 THz. From this comparison we can came to conclusion that the presence of organic compounds does have influence in detection of microplastic.

Conclusion

Microplastics are now considered a significant class of emerging environmental contaminants. However, research on microplastics is primarily focused on aquatic environments, while the role of microplastics in soil pollution remains inadequately. To address this knowledge gap, this study investigated THz frequency-domain spectroscopy for detecting microplastic contamination in soil. We used LDPE as a model microplastics in soil samples with varying levels of concentration (1 %,5 % &10 %,15 %). Our results demonstrate a noticeable influence of increasing microplastic concentration on refractive index and attenuation in soil samples. This is likely due to fundamental difference in material properties between soil and microplastics. However, the presence of the organic matter in soil can affect detection. Additionally, we observed a distinction in the THz response between untreated and treated soil samples. Notably, the refractive index at 0.5 THz for treated soil samples exhibited a more unique response in the range (0.1–0.7 THz) compared to untreated soil samples. This suggest that the thermal treatment process might alter the interaction between THz waves and the microplastics, potentially making treated microplastic contamination easier to identify with a consistent spectral signature. Future research should focus on numerous other types of microplastics present in the environment and on developing a quantitative model like machine learning to estimate the concentration of microplastics in soil based on its THz response.

Challenges and scope for further studies

Despite its potential, implementing Terahertz Frequency-Domain Spectroscopy (THz-FDS) for field identification of microplastics faces several limitations, particularly the influence of environmental factors like water content on THz wave propagation. High moisture levels, common in field conditions, can lead to signal attenuation, hindering microplastic detection. Furthermore, the inherent heterogeneity of soil, with variations in

organic matter, mineral composition, and particle sizes, contributes to background noise and scattering effects, potentially masking microplastic signatures. Additionally, the sensitivity of THz-FDS is influenced by microplastic size and shape, making the detection of smaller, irregularly shaped particles challenging. The low power output of current THz-FDS systems presents an additional constraint, limiting analysis to surface-level detection. Developing higher-power THz systems would enable greater penetration depth, facilitating the identification of microplastics embedded within the soil profile.

While the current findings are valuable for the specific soil type and the microplastic concentrations studied. Future research can address this challenges by developing a database encompassing diverse soil compositions including composition, moisture, pH and microplastic characteristics like polymer type, size, shape. This database would capture the significant impact these factors have on the interactions with THz radiation and pave the way for more robust THz-based microplastic detection method.
