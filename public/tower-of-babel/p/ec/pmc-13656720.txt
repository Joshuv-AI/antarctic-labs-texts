Left ventricular pseudoaneurysm rupture following prosthesis infective endocarditis after closure of ventricular septal rupture
Authors: Yu-san Chien, Tin-ann Chen, Chih-Wei Ten, Wei-Li Hung, Kang-Hong Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular pseudoaneurysm (LVP) is a rare but life-threatening complication of myocardial infarction or cardiac surgery, typically arising within the first few years after ventricular septal rupture (VSR) repair. Delayed rupture associated with prosthetic infection is extremely uncommon and carries high mortality.Case presentationWe report the case of a 47-year-old man with a history of post-infarction VSR repair using the infarct exclusion technique. The patient remained stable for nearly nine years before undergoing transcatheter closure of a residual VSR with an Amplatzer occluder. Within a year, he presented with septic shock following recurrent urosepsis and bacteremia. Imaging revealed a large LVP (9.8 × 5.1 cm) with rupture at the prior ventriculotomy site, accompanied by vegetations on both the surgical patch and occluder. Emergent redo surgery was performed, requiring radical debridement, explantation of all prosthetic material, and two-patch reconstruction with bovine pericardium. The postoperative course was complicated by serial multidrug-resistant infections and Candida albicans fungemia, followed by drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS syndrome) secondary to amphotericin B. With tailored antimicrobial therapy, vital organ support, and multidisciplinary care, the patient gradually recovered and was discharged on postoperative day 84.ConclusionsThis case highlights the potential for extremely delayed pseudoaneurysm rupture after VSR repair, triggered by prosthetic infective endocarditis. Successful management required aggressive surgical debridement, complete prosthetic explantation, innovative reconstruction, and meticulous postoperative infection control. Introduction

Left ventricular (LV) pseudoaneurysm, the result of contained myocardial rupture, where the rupture site is sealed by pericardium or scar tissue rather than by intact myocardium, is an uncommon but life-threatening complication that typically arises after acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [1, 2]. Other reported contributing conditions included post-cardiac surgeries [3], transcatheter cardiac procedures [4–6], infection [7, 8], myocarditis [9], or chest trauma [10]. Without prompt diagnosis and management, pseudoaneurysm rupture can lead to catastrophic hemodynamic collapse and death.

In the setting of post-infarction ventricular septal rupture (VSR) repair, pseudoaneurysm formation at the area of the ventricular suture lines is rare [11]. A review of the literature identified only a handful of cases, occurring from 1.5 to 4 years after the VSR repair with varied operative strategies, ranging from direct suture to patch closure using bovine pericardium. Outcomes have been heterogeneous, with some patients surviving in stable condition for years, while others succumbed to sepsis or other postoperative complications [12–15]. Notably, infection as a precipitating factor has been reported in only a minority of cases, and fungal involvement (mycosis) is even less common [8, 11].

We report a rare case of LV pseudoaneurysm rupture secondary to prosthetic infective endocarditis following device closure of a residual VSR nearly 10 years after the index MI and surgical repair. This case is distinctive not only for its prolonged latency but also for the complex interplay of prosthetic infection, residual shunt, and pseudoaneurysm formation, culminating in rupture. Detailed multimodal imaging, intraoperative findings, and a complicated postoperative course—marked by multidrug-resistant infections and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) syndrome—provide valuable insights into the diagnostic challenges and surgical considerations in this high-risk scenario.

Case report

A 47-year-old morbidly obese man (BMI 43.1 kg/m²) with poorly controlled hypertension and a history of VSR secondary to acute myocardial infarction (AMI) was transferred to our hospital for evaluation of an 8-week history of intermittent fever.

In September 2015, he presented with AMI complicated by pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock. Emergent percutaneous coronary intervention was performed with stenting of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery and intra-aortic balloon pump support. On the second day, infarct exclusion repair of an anteroapical VSR was performed using the one-patch method, combined with coronary artery bypass grafting of the right coronary artery using a saphenous vein graft. Intraoperatively, myocardial necrosis with a longitudinal septal slit and apical rupture was observed, with a pre-closure Qp/Qs of 1.76. The defect was closed with a folded bovine pericardial patch secured by pledgeted Prolene sutures from the LV side, and the ventriculotomy was closed with Teflon-felt–reinforced sutures. His postoperative course was uneventful: the intra-aortic balloon pump was removed on day 3, he was extubated on day 5, and discharged on day 13.

At one year, echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 43.0% and a residual left-to-right shunt at LV apex across the upper edge of the patch and through the VSR. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the heart and great vessels demonstrated aneurysmal dilatation and wall thinning of the left ventricular apex, as well as a 1.6 cm defect at the anteroseptal segment of the mid-left ventricular wall. Coronary angiography showed in-stent restenosis of the LAD, and balloon dilatation was performed with deployment of an additional drug-eluting stent (Xience Xpedition 2.75 mm × 38 mm) from the proximal to distal LAD. The patient was discharged on optimized medical therapy for heart failure.

Five years later (2020), he presented with worsening exercise intolerance and fatigue. Echocardiography showed a LVEF of 48% and a stationary residual left to right shunt (1.45–1.71 cm) through upper edge of the VSR patch and the defect of anterior superior ventricular septum. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a step-up in oxygen saturation from 63% (right atrium) to 72% (main pulmonary artery), with Qp of 3.21 L/min/m², Qs of 2.24 L/min/m², and a Qp/Qs ratio of 1.43. An attempt at transcatheter device closure of the residual shunt failed due to wiring difficulty; contrast extravasation was noted at the right ventricular outflow tract but without hemodynamic consequence. He continued medical therapy with sacubitril/valsartan, ivabradine, and carvedilol.

In 2024, nine years after the index AMI, he was hospitalized for repeating transcatheter device closure of the VSR. Pre-procedural contrast-enhanced CT revealed a 2.8 × 1.4 cm contrast-filled cavity between the patch and septum, with two identifiable communications to the right ventricle, consistent with residual VSR. Hemodynamic studies indicated a Qp/Qs of 1.31, Rp/Rs of 0.08, and pulmonary vascular resistance of 2.06 Wood units, with an oxygen step-up from 70% (right atrium) to 76% (main pulmonary artery). Coronary angiography showed 30% in-stent stenosis of the LAD, 99% stenosis of the OM2 branch of the left circumflex artery, and an ectatic right coronary artery with a patent saphenous vein graft. Left ventriculography confirmed the flow passage from LV through the patch and septum to the right ventricle. A 16 mm Amplatzer™ P.I. Muscular VSD occluder (9-VSDMUSCPI-016) was successfully deployed, leaving a small residual shunt (0.39 cm) on post-procedural imaging.

Ten months later, the patient was admitted to a local hospital with fever and turbid urine, raising concern for urinary tract infection (UTI). Initial blood and urine cultures were negative. He was treated with a 7-day course of intravenous antibiotics and discharged. One month later, he presented with recurrent fever, intermittent hypotension, generalized edema, and laboratory findings of leukocytosis (13,700/µL), elevated C-reactive protein (19.26 mg/dL), procalcitonin (6.3 ng/mL), and NT-proBNP (8,820 pg/mL). He was found unconscious in his bathroom and was admitted to the ICU in septic shock with acute kidney injury. Blood from two separate venipuncture sites and urine cultures both grew Escherichia coli. Abdominal CT revealed no evidence of complicated UTI. Despite three weeks of antibiotic therapy, he remained febrile with persistently elevated inflammatory markers. Serial chest radiography showed progression of hazy opacity over the left lower lung field. He was transferred to our hospital for further evaluation.

On arrival, he was hypotensive with a rapid hemoglobin drop (10 → 7.3 g/dL within 12 h). Transthoracic echocardiography (Fig. 1A) showed multiple suspected vegetations on the VSR patch and LV side of interventricular septum, a residual left-to-right shunt measuring 0.39 cm, and large amount of fluid accumulation with heterogenous echogenicity and septation outside the LV apex. Color Doppler confirmed communication between the pericardial fluid and LV apex, compatible with pseudoaneurysm rupture secondary to prosthetic infective endocarditis. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a large contrast-filled cavity (9.8 × 5.1 cm) adjacent to the LV apex, with direct communications to LV (Fig. 1B).

Given these findings, emergent surgical repair was undertaken. Following redo sternotomy and institution of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), antegrade cardioplegia was administered to achieve cardiac arrest. Dense adhesions between the LV apex, pseudoaneurysm sac, and adjacent lung were carefully dissected. The pseudoaneurysm sac was fibrotic and thin-walled, surrounded by pericardium and the left lower lobe. Within the sac, a large heterogeneous hematoma mixed with turbid effusion was evacuated. A 10-mm defect was identified, communicating directly with the LV cavity at the site of prior Teflon felt closure (2015 operation) (Fig. 2). The Teflon felt, sutures, and adjacent necrotic myocardium were excised. Multiple vegetations were noted on the previous bovine pericardial patch and within the mesh of the implanted VSD occluder (Fig. 3). These were completely explanted, and the LV cavity was irrigated with 20,000 mL of saline. Given the extent of myocardial resection, a two-patch repair strategy was used: the residual VSD and excluded LV region were closed with a bovine pericardial patch using multiple interrupted 2 − 0 Prolene pledgeted sutures, while the LV ventriculotomy was closed with a second bovine pericardial patch. The patient was successfully weaned from CPB, and meticulous hemostasis was achieved. Total bypass time was 486 min, with an aortic cross-clamp time of 284 min. Hemoadsorption therapy with CytoSorb was integrated into the bypass circuit and continued postoperatively, for a total treatment duration of 505 min.

Pathologic examination of intraoperative specimens — including the explanted bovine pericardial patch, left ventricular myocardial tissue, and fibrous tissue overlying the occluder—demonstrated extensive necrosis and suppurative inflammation with granulation tissue formation. However, no microorganisms were identified on Gram staining, and intraoperative cultures were negative. According to the 2023 Duke–International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases criteria, this case fulfilled the definition of definite infective endocarditis, meeting one major and three minor criteria [16]. The major criterion was imaging evidence of vegetations on prosthetic material (VSR patch and VSD occluder) demonstrated on transthoracic echocardiography and confirmed intraoperatively. Minor criteria included (1) persistent fever > 38 °C, (2) presence of predisposing prosthetic cardiac material, (3) positive blood cultures for Escherichia coli obtained prior to broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy at our institution, and (4) elevated inflammatory markers.

Postoperatively, blood cultures were obtained every 72 h or whenever new signs of sepsis developed, and surveillance continued until two consecutive weeks of negative results were achieved. Early postoperative bloodstream infections were polymicrobial and predominantly healthcare-associated. Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) was isolated on postoperative days (POD) 1 and 3; methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (MRCNS) on POD 5, 7, and 11; carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) on POD 7 and 9; and persistent candidemia due to Candida albicans on POD 11, 14, 18, and 21. A preoperative stool culture obtained because of diarrhea was positive for Clostridioides difficile. Mediastinal drain cultures remained negative, and no clinical or imaging evidence of deep sternal wound infection was identified.

Antimicrobial therapy was adjusted according to susceptibility testing and infectious disease consultation. Treatment included oral vancomycin and fidaxomicin for C. difficile colitis; intravenous daptomycin and teicoplanin for resistant gram-positive organisms; meropenem, polymyxin B, minocycline, ceftolozane/tazobactam, and ceftaroline for multidrug-resistant gram-negative pathogens; and sequential antifungal therapy with micafungin followed by liposomal amphotericin B for persistent candidemia.Source control measures included radical intraoperative debridement with complete removal of infected prosthetic material, scheduled exchange of central venous catheters (POD 7 and POD 21), and removal of the urinary catheter on POD 14. Surveillance imaging and clinical assessment revealed no evidence of mediastinitis or intra-abdominal abscess.

Four weeks after surgery, the patient developed clinical features consistent with drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), temporally associated with initiation of liposomal amphotericin B. He presented with diffuse erythematous maculopapular eruption involving > 30% of body surface area and marked eosinophilia (3,366/mm³). No significant hepatic dysfunction, new renal deterioration beyond septic acute kidney injury, lymphadenopathy, or cardiopulmonary involvement was identified. Based on the RegiSCAR criteria, the constellation of rash extent, hematologic abnormality (eosinophilia), temporal relationship to drug exposure, and exclusion of alternative causes was consistent with probable DRESS syndrome [17]. Liposomal amphotericin B was discontinued, and antifungal therapy was switched to anidulafungin plus fluconazole. Topical corticosteroid therapy was initiated. The rash and systemic symptoms gradually improved over the subsequent two weeks. Figure 4 demonstrated the summary of the hospital course.

Discussion

Left ventricular pseudoaneurysm is a rare but catastrophic complication, distinguished from true aneurysm by the absence of intact myocardial tissue at the aneurysm wall. While most cases arise after myocardial infarction or cardiac surgery, development at the site of VSR repair is uncommon. Rupture of an LVP is almost universally fatal without urgent surgical repair, yet operative mortality remains substantial given the technical demands and critical condition of these patients.

Comparison with literature

The infarct exclusion method, first described in 1995, has been widely used for acute post-infarction VSR repair by securing a pericardial patch over the septal rupture without extensive debridement of necrotic tissue. This approach preserves LV geometry and has been associated with improved survival [18]. However, because the ventriculotomy itself must be closed with sutures (often buttressed with felt), this region may remain structurally vulnerable, especially in patients with large myocardial defects or those with rupture near the LV free wall. To address this concern, modifications such as the two-patch method [19] and right ventricular approach [20] have been developed. Nonetheless, pseudoaneurysm formation has still been reported even after these refinements [20].

In our patient, the single-patch infarct exclusion method was used at the index operation, with Teflon-reinforced sutures to close the ventriculotomy. Follow-up imaging confirmed LV integrity for nearly nine years. The catastrophic event occurred less than one year after transcatheter occluder implantation and was preceded by urosepsis with documented Escherichia coli bacteremia. We hypothesize that hematogenous seeding to the prosthetic patch and occluder during bacteremia may have resulted in prosthetic infective endocarditis. The extensive necrosis observed at the ventriculotomy felt line, together with macroscopic vegetations on both the patch and device, is consistent with infection-related structural compromise. Although direct microbiological confirmation at the ventriculotomy site was not obtained, this temporal sequence is compatible with progressive prosthetic infection ultimately weakening the prior ventriculotomy closure and precipitating pseudoaneurysm formation and rupture.

Infective etiology has only rarely been implicated in delayed LVP rupture. Handa et al. reported a case of Candida albicans infection involving a pericardial patch as the cause of rupture [11]. However, polymicrobial and multidrug-resistant infections involving both a surgical patch and a transcatheter device, as in our patient, have scarcely been documented.

Surgical and technical considerations

This case highlights the formidable challenges of redo surgery in the setting of active infection. Dense pleuropericardial adhesions, extensive myocardial necrosis, and friable tissue around previously implanted prosthetic material required meticulous dissection and radical debridement. Both the bovine pericardial patch and Amplatzer occluder were heavily colonized with vegetations and required complete explanation. Given the size of the myocardial defect, reconstruction was performed with a two-patch technique using bovine pericardium, allowing repair of both the septal defect and the ventriculotomy site. The prolonged CPB time (486 min) and aortic cross-clamp time (284 min) reflected the extraordinary technical complexity of the procedure.

Adjunctive CytoSorb hemoadsorption was employed intraoperatively and postoperatively. This extracorporeal blood purification device uses porous polymer beads to adsorb inflammatory cytokines, bilirubin, myoglobin, and certain medications such as anticoagulants. It has been reported to attenuate systemic inflammation, reduce transfusion requirements, and improve hemodynamic stability after cardiac surgery [21]. For patients with infective endocarditis, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated an association between intraoperative CytoSorb use and improved in-hospital survival, however the available evidence remains largely observational and subject to confounding [22]. Although its application in our patient who had been through prolonged CPB under overwhelming infection might have an uncertain but favorable impact, no firm conclusions can be drawn given the single-case nature of this report.

Postoperative complications and infection control

The patient’s postoperative course was further complicated by serial multidrug-resistant bloodstream infections and Candida albicans fungemia, necessitating multiple adjustments in antimicrobial therapy and a prolonged treatment regimen. Subsequently, he developed DRESS syndrome after treatment with liposomal amphotericin B. DRESS is a serious, delayed-onset hypersensitivity reaction, typically occurring 2–4 weeks after drug initiation, and is characterized by eosinophilia, multiorgan involvement, and paradoxical worsening even after withdrawal of the causative agent [23]. Systemic corticosteroids are commonly recommended in moderate-to-severe cases, particularly when significant organ dysfunction is present.

In this patient, however, we elected to avoid systemic corticosteroids because of the high risk of exacerbating ongoing prosthetic infection and persistent candidemia. Instead, management focused on prompt withdrawal of liposomal amphotericin B, transition to alternative antifungal agents, topical corticosteroids, and supportive care. The patient’s dermatologic and hematologic abnormalities gradually resolved over the subsequent two weeks. This approach was chosen under the unique context of uncontrolled prosthetic infection and should not be interpreted as a general recommendation against systemic corticosteroid use in DRESS.

Lessons for clinical practice

Several important lessons emerge. First, patients with residual shunts, prosthetic patches, or device closures require long-term vigilance, as pseudoaneurysm rupture may occur even many years after apparently stable repair. Second, persistent fever in such patients should always raise suspicion for prosthetic endocarditis, even in the absence of early positive cultures. Third, in the event of rupture, prompt surgical referral is mandatory despite prohibitive operative risks, as survival without intervention is virtually nonexistent. Finally, multidisciplinary management—including surgical, infectious disease, and critical care collaboration—is critical for navigating the complex postoperative course of these high-risk patients.
