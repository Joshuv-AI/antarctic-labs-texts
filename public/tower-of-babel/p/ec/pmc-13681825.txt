Associations of uric acid with wearable sensor-derived digital gait characteristics in older adults: vigor, symmetry, and stability
Authors: Chi Zhang, Ye Liu, Yushan Zhang, Jing An, Yuting Kang, Jie Zhang, Guoqing Fan, Zehong Huo, Ping Zeng, Hong Shi, Ji Shen, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveElevated uric acid (UA) has been linked to impaired nervous system and skeletal muscle function. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between UA and digital gait characteristics in older adults.MethodsWe enrolled 651 older adults (≥ 60 years) who completed a wearable device-based walking test and blood sample detection. Multidimensional sensors were used to measure 15 gait parameters. Asymmetry index (AI) and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated for each parameter. Multiple linear regression analyses were employed to examine the associations between UA and gait characteristics.ResultsThe mean age of all participants was 70.45 ± 6.12 years, with a mean UA concentration of 298.60 ± 92.39 μmol/L. After full adjustment for demographic characteristics and multiple health indicators, per 1-SD increase in UA was positively associated with swing time (β = 6.953, P = 0.007). Conversely, UA was negatively associated with thigh swing work (β = − 0.023, P = 0.003), foot landing control force (β = − 0.134, P < 0.001), stride frequency (β = − 1.074, P = 0.049) and stride length (β = − 0.014, P = 0.012). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons, all these associations remained statistically significant except for stride frequency. Higher UA levels were associated with higher AIs of thigh acceleration (β = 0.034, P = 0.013), ground reaction force (β = 0.033, P = 0.002), foot landing control force (β = 0.048, P < 0.001) and toe-off angle (β = 0.061, P = 0.001). UA was positively associated with CVs of swing time (β = 0.032, P < 0.001), ground reaction force (β = 0.015, P = 0.001), foot landing control force (β = 0.018, P = 0.001) and toe-off angle (β = 0.035, P = 0.002). All these associations with gait symmetry and stability indices remained statistically significant after FDR correction. Exploratory analyses identified high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione reductase as potential biomarkers linking the association between UA and gait performance.ConclusionHigher UA levels are associated with poorer gait performance in terms of vigor, symmetry, and stability. The underlying inflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms need to be elucidated in future experimental studies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07993-8.Keywords: Uric acid, Gait mobility, Wearable sensor, Older adults

Introduction

Gait performance is an effective reflection of skeletal muscle mass and function, providing a robust methodology for comprehensively understanding motor impairment and functional decline [1]. Gait speed is widely recognized as a gold standard indicator of overall mobility and physical function, with strong associations to disability, falls, and mortality [2, 3]. However, gait speed alone is insufficient for assessing the subtle alterations in gait mobility characteristics that accompany aging. The use of wearable devices to measure gait performance is becoming increasingly common in clinical practice, offering valuable insights into functional deterioration and disease manifestations. Beyond standard indicators such as step frequency, gait speed, and swing work, wearable sensors can capture micro-level movement parameters through continuous monitoring. This allows for the identification of specific deficits such as gait asymmetry and instability [4].

Previous studies have often employed periodic, kinematic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal indices to evaluate gait performance. These metrics have been widely used to sensitively assess functional status and predict fall risk in clinical populations, particularly among individuals with neurological or musculoskeletal impairments [5]. Building on this foundation, wearable sensors now leverage these principles to enable continuous, real-world monitoring of gait. The resulting rich biomechanical data facilitates the assessment of gait coordination and balance, thereby driving innovation in digital mobility assessments and personalized interventions [6, 7]. The onset of gait abnormalities in older adults often coincides with changes in specific physiological biomarkers, however a connection that has been relatively underexplored in previous research. Therefore, integrating quantitative gait metrics with biomarker profiles is crucial to further elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying gait deficits and to improve the identification of older adults at higher risk. Gait performance in older adults can be characterized along three interrelated dimensions. Gait vigor reflects lower-limb movement amplitude and is closely related to muscle strength. Gait symmetry indicates left–right limb coordination and is a sensitive indicator of early neuromuscular deficits. Gait stability captures step-to-step consistency and balance control and is strongly associated with fall risk. Together, these dimensions provide a more complete assessment of gait health than any single metric alone.

Uric acid (UA) has attracted attention as a potential biomarker for gait impairments due to its diverse roles in physiological and pathological processes. As the final product of purine metabolism in humans, UA functions as an important endogenous antioxidant under normal conditions, effectively neutralizing reactive oxygen species and protecting both neuronal cells and vascular endothelium. Abnormal UA levels, whether elevated or reduced, can trigger a range of pathophysiological effects, including enhanced inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and detrimental impacts on musculoskeletal and nervous system function [8]. For instance, low UA has been identified as a risk factor for malnutrition in Parkinson’s disease patients experiencing freezing of gait [9]. Conversely, hyperuricemia has been linked to chronic inflammation, sarcopenia, frailty, increased susceptibility to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and reductions in gait speed [10–12]. Kang and colleagues indicated that for each 1 unit increase in UA, the risk of frailty in older adults increased by 22% [13]. Another study demonstrated that elevated UA levels are a potentially independent risk factor for relative muscle loss among US adults [14]. While extensive research links uric acid to musculoskeletal pathophysiology, its association with gait characteristics in older adults remains unclear. Elevated UA may be associated with reduced gait vigor through its detrimental effects on skeletal muscle integrity and contractile function. The resulting muscle weakness and impaired neuromuscular coordination may further contribute to decreased gait symmetry and compromised balance control, thereby increasing gait instability. Therefore, UA is theoretically relevant to all three dimensions of gait performance, and its association with multidimensional gait characteristics warrants systematic investigation. Mechanistically, elevated UA promotes reactive oxygen species generation through xanthine oxidase and NADPH oxidase pathways, suppresses endogenous antioxidant defenses, and triggers innate immune activation with subsequent release of proinflammatory cytokines [15, 16]. Persistent oxidative stress and systemic inflammation may impair skeletal muscle contractile function and disrupt neuromuscular signal transmission, potentially accounting for the link between UA and gait alterations [14, 17]. We therefore designed exploratory mediation analyses to investigate whether inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers serve as intermediate pathways underlying this association.

The present study examines how circulating UA levels relate to multidimensional gait characteristics recorded by wearable sensors, encompassing periodic, kinematic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal metrics along with asymmetry and variability indices. We further aim to examine the potential mediating effects of specific inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers on this relationship, ultimately providing insights into novel biomarkers and evaluation approaches for early identification of motor decline and targeted intervention planning.

Methods

Study population

From February to August 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional study from five communities in Beijing using convenience sampling. A total of 937 older adults were initially recruited via community notice announcements. Among them, 51 failed to complete wearable gait testing and 217 did not provide blood specimens, leaving 669 participants with complete gait and blood data. We further excluded 17 individuals with abnormal gait or uric acid data, as well as one participant diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease, resulting in a final analytical sample of 651 participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (No. 2022BJYYEC-260–03), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Gait measurement

Gait characteristics were assessed using a multi-sensor wearable motion analysis system (IDEEA, MiniSun LLC, USA). The IDEEA device consists of a central processing unit connected to seven miniature motion sensors, whose precision and test–retest reliability have been validated in multiple experimental and clinical studies [18–21]. Three-dimensional accelerometers were affixed to the participants’ sternum, thighs, ankles, and feet to continuously record gait-related signals. All investigators received unified and standardized training on device operation and gait data collection, and passed a qualification assessment before conducting the tests. Sensor calibration was performed per the manufacturer’s protocol before each test to ensure measurement accuracy, and the system applied standardized sampling and built-in filtering to acquire reliable signals. After verifying proper sensor placement, participants were instructed to walk 12 m on a flat, straight, non-slip indoor path at their habitual pace, without using any assistive devices. All tests were conducted in a quiet, well-lit environment free of obstacles and distractions.

To ensure reliable signal acquisition, the sensors adopt manufacturer-standardized sampling and built-in filtering. Raw kinematic signals are captured at a high frequency of 3200 Hz, after which onboard microprocessors apply Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and wavelet analysis to eliminate high-frequency electrical noise and movement artifacts. The refined data are then down-sampled to an effective output rate of 64 Hz. To eliminate the confounding effects of initial acceleration and terminal deceleration phases on gait dynamics, raw gait data were processed using the IDEEA system’s standardized algorithm, which automatically identified and excluded gait cycles from the acceleration and deceleration segments of the walk. Only steady-state gait cycles from the middle walking segment were retained for analysis. Gait recordings with sensor detachment, severe signal loss, or visible walking interruptions such as pauses or abnormal acceleration were excluded from analysis. No participants in this study exhibited severe signal loss or missing sensor data. Processed data were downloaded to a computer and analyzed using the ActView software suite. A total of 15 gait parameters were derived including periodic (double-limb support time, initial limb support time, terminal limb support time, swing time, step time, and stride time), kinematic (thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, and toe-off angle), kinetic (ground reaction force and foot landing control force), and spatiotemporal (gait speed, stride frequency, step length, and stride length) domains. The detailed process for gait data collection and analysis is presented in Fig. 1.

Procedure for measuring gait characteristics using wearable sensors

Furthermore, an asymmetry index (AI) was computed for each gait variable to quantify inter-limb differences, where greater AI values reflected reduced gait symmetry. The AI was determined using the following formula: AI =|Left parameter – Right parameter|/[0.5 × (Left parameter + Right parameter)]. To assess gait consistency within each participant, the coefficient of variation (CV) for every gait parameter was derived from multiple strides during the walking test. CV was defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean, with higher CV values representing poorer gait stability. A median of 20 valid steady-state strides (IQR: 18–22) per participant were used to calculate AIs and CVs. CV and AI values were computed uniformly across all participants using the same set of valid steady-state strides. CV computations were fully standardized across all participants, with uniform stride inclusion criteria, calculation formulas, and data processing workflows applied via the ActView software suite.

Blood sample and laboratory examination

Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all participants before breakfast, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Biochemical indicators were measured using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (RayBiotech, USA) including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL_C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL_C), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) and UA. In this study, UA showed an approximately normal distribution, with the clinical cut-offs for hyperuricemia (high UA) defined as serum UA concentration > 420 μmol/L in men and > 360 μmol/L in women. We also measured a panel of circulating inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers to evaluate potential mechanisms. The panel included high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione reductase (GR), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px). All analytes were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (RayBiotech, USA), which demonstrated inter-assay and intra-assay CVs of 1.9–7.1% and 2.6–8.6%, respectively.

Covariates

A series of covariates were considered based on previous studies [22, 23]. Demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, and marital status) and data on current smoking, current alcohol consumption, and exercise habits were collected during face-to-face interviews. A dietary diversity score (ranging from 8 to 32) was obtained by evaluating the consumption frequency of eight food groups. Height and weight were measured to calculate body mass index (BMI). Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Mini-cog test, which consists of a 3-word recall task and the clock drawing test [24]. The Barthel Index was used to assess activities of daily living (ADL), with a total score < 95 indicating impaired ADL. Mental health status was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with a total score > 4 indicating depressive symptoms. After two consecutive measurements of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) using an electronic sphygmomanometer, the average values were calculated. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg, DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg, self-reported diagnosis of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Dyslipidemia was identified by meeting any of the following criteria: TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.0 mmol/L, or LDL-C ≥ 3.4 mmol/L. Diabetes was defined as fulfilling any of the following conditions: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, self-reported diagnosis of diabetes, or use of glucose-lowering medications. Polypharmacy status, categorized by the number of daily medications use (0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3), were also collected.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed, and as median with interquartile range (P25–P75) when the distribution was non-normal. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions (n [%]). Correlations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. As shown in the frequency distribution histogram in Supplementary Fig. 1, UA followed a normal distribution. A quadratic term test was first performed to explore the potential non-linear relationship between UA and gait parameters. Overall, the quadratic term test revealed P > 0.05 for most gait parameters, except for foot landing control force, the AI of swing time, and the CV of thigh acceleration and stride frequency in the total sample (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the results of the quadratic term test were generally consistent between the high UA group and the normal UA group, with no notable differences present between the two groups. The scatter plots of linear fitting between UA and the 45 gait parameters are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Consistent with the quadratic term test results, most parameters showed no obvious non-linear trends, further supporting the adoption of a linear model for subsequent regression analyses. In addition, we also plotted representative spline curves of UA with 15 gait vitality parameters, and found that the vast majority showed linear or approximately linear relationships (Supplementary Fig. 3). To further explore the relationships between UA levels and individual gait parameters, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted with UA included as a continuous variable per 1-SD increment and each single gait parameter entered as the dependent variable separately. Given the multidimensional nature of gait assessment involving 45 dependent variables, the regression analyses were conducted in an exploratory framework. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction method was also applied to adjust for multiple comparisons and control the probability of type I errors across all gait parameter analyses. To assess the potential multicollinearity among all independent variables in the multivariable linear regression models, the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values were calculated in the fully adjusted model. All covariates presented with VIF values ranging from 1.02 to 1.44 and tolerance values ranging from 0.69 to 0.98, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity among variables (Supplementary Table 2). Residual plots and Q-Q normal plots for all 45 gait parameters in the fully adjusted Model 3 were summarized in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. Residual diagnostics indicated that the residuals were approximately normally distributed with no discernible patterns of heteroscedasticity or non-linearity, confirming that the key assumptions of linear regression were adequately met. To explore the potential pathways, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were first calculated to examine pairwise correlations among UA, inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers, and gait parameters. Mediation analyses were subsequently conducted to explore the mediating effects of these biomarkers on the UA-gait parameter associations. Robust linear regression was adopted to address the discrepancies in distribution and dimensionality of potential mediating factors. The mediation effects were estimated with 1000 bootstrap re-sampling, and the proportion of mediation (PM) was calculated for each mediator. A mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) of PM did not include zero. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Result

Sample characteristics

The mean age of the 651 participants was 70.45 ± 6.12 years, and 57.14% were female. The mean UA concentration was 298.61 ± 92.39 μmol/L. Table 1 summarizes the demographic information, health status, and blood biomarkers all participants across UA levels. Compared with the normal UA group, the high UA group presented with significantly older age and higher BMI. A significantly higher proportion of hypertension was observed in the high UA group. In terms of blood biomarkers, the high UA group had markedly lower levels of TC and TG, while inflammatory biomarkers including h-CRP and IL-6 were significantly elevated. Additionally, the high UA group showed significantly lower SOD and GR compared with the normal UA group.

Descriptions of gait parameters

The measurements of all 15 gait parameters, as well as their AIs and CVs, are presented in Fig. 2. As shown in Table 2, participants in the high UA group exhibited distinct gait impairments compared with the normal UA group. In terms of gait vigor parameters, the high UA group showed significantly prolonged terminal limb support time (P = 0.008), lower foot landing control force (P = 0.005), lower stride frequency (P = 0.038), and shorter step length (P = 0.015) and stride length (P = 0.003). For gait symmetry parameters, the high UA group had a markedly higher AI of toe-off angle (P < 0.001), indicating greater bilateral gait asymmetry. Regarding gait stability parameters, the high UA group presented significantly elevated CVs in initial limb support time (P = 0.049), swing time (P = 0.022), and toe-off angle (P = 0.005), reflecting increased step-to-step variability and poorer gait stability.

Correlations of UA with gait vigor, symmetry, and stability

As shown in Fig. 3, UA was significantly correlated with multiple gait performance parameters. Specifically, UA exhibited positive associations with double-limb support time (r = 0.15, P = 0.001), terminal limb support time (r = 0.18, P < 0.001), swing time (r = 0.16, P < 0.001), and step time (r = 0.16, P < 0.001). Conversely, UA showed negative associations with thigh swing work (r = − 0.13, P = 0.001), ground reaction force (r = − 0.13, P = 0.002), foot landing control force (r = − 0.20, P < 0.001), toe-off angle (r = − 0.17, P < 0.001), gait speed (r = − 0.14, P = 0.001), stride frequency (r = − 0.18, P < 0.001), step length (r = − 0.11, P = 0.005), and stride length (r = − 0.15, P < 0.001). Besides, UA was found to be positively correlated with the AIs for thigh acceleration (r = 0.09, P = 0.023), ground reaction force (r = 0.13, P = 0.001), foot landing control force (r = 0.11, P = 0.007), toe-off angle (r = 0.15, P < 0.001), and step length (r = 0.11, P = 0.006). UA was also positively associated with the CVs for initial limb support time (r = 0.10, P = 0.031), swing time (r = 0.19, P < 0.001), thigh acceleration (r = 0.13, P = 0.001), thigh swing work (r = 0.12, P = 0.003), ground reaction force (r = 0.15, P < 0.001), foot landing control force (r = 0.14, P < 0.001), toe-off angle (r = 0.18, P < 0.001), step length (r = 0.09, P = 0.019), and stride length (r = 0.09, P = 0.021).

After FDR correction, UA remained significantly correlated with numerous gait parameters across all three dimensions. Among gait vigor indicators, significant associations were observed with double-limb support time (FDR-q = 0.001), terminal limb support time (FDR-q < 0.001), swing time (FDR-q < 0.001), step time (FDR-q < 0.001), stride time (FDR-q < 0.001), thigh swing work (FDR-q = 0.001), ground reaction force (FDR-q = 0.003), foot landing control force (FDR-q < 0.001), toe-off angle (FDR-q < 0.001), gait speed (FDR-q = 0.001), stride frequency (FDR-q < 0.001), step length (FDR-q = 0.006), and stride length (FDR-q < 0.001). In terms of gait symmetry, UA was also associated with the AI of ground reaction force (FDR-q = 0.007), foot landing control force (FDR-q = 0.026), toe-off angle (FDR-q = 0.001), and step length (FDR-q = 0.026). With respect to gait stability, significant correlations were found with the CV of swing time (FDR-q < 0.001), thigh swing work (FDR-q = 0.008), ground reaction force (FDR-q < 0.001), foot landing control force (FDR-q < 0.001), toe-off angle (FDR-q < 0.001), step length (FDR-q = 0.043), and stride length (FDR-q = 0.043).

Multivariable analyses of the association between UA and gait parameters

Table 3 presents the results of multivariable linear regression analyses examining the associations between UA levels (included as per 1-SD increment) and multidimensional gait parameters. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which accounted for age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, smoking, drinking, habitual exercise, dietary diversity, BMI, ADL, cognitive impairment, depressive symptom, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and polypharmacy, higher UA levels were positively associated with swing time (β = 6.953, P = 0.007), and negatively associated with thigh swing work (β = − 0.023, P = 0.003), foot landing control force (β = − 0.134, P < 0.001), stride frequency (β = − 1.074, P = 0.049) and stride length (β = − 0.014, P = 0.012). For gait symmetry, higher UA was significantly correlated with higher AIs of thigh acceleration (β = 0.034, P = 0.013), ground reaction force (β = 0.033, P = 0.002), foot landing control force (β = 0.048, P < 0.001) and toe-off angle (β = 0.061, P = 0.001). Regarding gait stability, UA levels were positively associated with higher CVs of swing time (β = 0.032, P < 0.001), ground reaction force (β = 0.015, P = 0.001), foot landing control force (β = 0.018, P = 0.001) and toe-off angle (β = 0.035, P = 0.002). After FDR correction for multiple testing, most of the above associations remained statistically significant at the FDR-q < 0.05 threshold. These included positive associations of UA with swing time, the AIs of thigh acceleration, ground reaction force, foot landing control force and toe-off angle, as well as the CVs of swing time, ground reaction force, foot landing control force and toe-off angle. UA was also negatively associated with thigh swing work, foot landing control force and stride length. All these associations remained statistically significant with FDR-q < 0.05. Only the association between UA and stride frequency, which was nominally significant in the uncorrected analysis (P = 0.049), was attenuated to non-significance after FDR correction (FDR-q = 0.168).

Mediation analyses

Analysis of inflammatory and oxidative stress markers revealed potential statistical pathways underlying the association between UA and gait performance. As shown in Fig. 4, UA was positively associated with hs-CRP (β = 0.006, P = 0.002), IL-6 (β = 0.315, P < 0.001), and GSH-Px (β = 0.193, P = 0.012), while it was inversely associated with SOD (β = −0.012, P < 0.001) and GR (β = −0.022, P = 0.011). We found that hs-CRP and IL-6 were significantly positively correlated with periodic parameters but negatively correlated with kinematic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal parameters. In contrast, SOD and GR showed significant positive correlations with kinetic and spatiotemporal parameters. Moreover, hs-CRP was positively correlated with the CVs of gait speed (r = 0.10, P = 0.013) and step length (r = 0.08, P = 0.026). IL-6 was positively correlated with the asymmetry and variability of thigh acceleration, ground reaction force, and foot landing control force (all P < 0.05). However, SOD was negatively associated with the AIs of ground reaction force (r = −0.11, P = 0.008), foot landing control force (r = −0.12, P = 0.004), and step length (r = −0.10, P = 0.025), as well as the CVs of thigh acceleration (r = −0.09, P = 0.032), ground reaction force (r = −0.11, P = 0.007), and foot landing control force (r = −0.09, P = 0.022). GR was negatively associated with the AI of thigh swing work (r = −0.09, P = 0.032) and the CVs of thigh acceleration (r = 0.08, P = 0.039) and toe-off angle (r = −0.08, P = 0.036).

Potential inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers linking uric acid to gait parameters (A Correlations between UA and inflammatory/oxidative stress biomarkers; B Correlations between inflammatory/oxidative stress biomarkers and gait parameters; C Mediation effects of inflammatory/oxidative stress biomarkers on the association between UA and gait performance)

After adjusting for all covariates, we identified specific biomarkers which significantly mediated the association between UA and gait parameters. For gait vigor, IL-6 mediated the associations of UA with thigh swing work (PM: 6.01%, 95% CI: 1.27%–25.72%) and foot landing control force (PM: 3.98%, 95% CI: 0.71%–14.53%). SOD mediated UA’s relationship with foot landing control force (PM: 6.14%, 95% CI: 0.83%–23.56%) and thigh swing work (PM: 5.61%, 95% CI 0.26%–34.41%). GR showed a significant statistical mediation effect, partially explaining the associations between UA and stride frequency (PM: 13.09%, 95% CI 0.42%–66.87%), thigh swing work (PM: 9.33%, 95% CI 1.61%–35.28%), foot landing control force (PM: 7.31%, 95% CI 1.35%–21.11%) and stride time (PM: 13.22%, 95% CI 0.35%–77.70%). For gait symmetry, IL-6 significantly mediated the relationship between UA and the AI of thigh acceleration (PM: 9.62%, 95% CI: 1.33%–55.08%). For gait stability, SOD mediated UA’s association with the CV of ground reaction force (PM: 3.62%, 95% CI: 0.05%–17.88%). These results suggested that IL-6, SOD and GR may serve as potential statistical mediators in the association between UA and gait performance in older adults.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed sensitivity analyses to mitigate potential biases arising from severe physical and cognitive impairments. After excluding 43 participants with impaired ADL, UA remained positively associated with swing time and stride time, while demonstrating negative associations with thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, foot landing control force, stride frequency, and stride length (Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, UA maintained consistent negative correlations with AIs and CVs of thigh acceleration, ground reaction force, foot landing control force, and toe-off angle. Furthermore, after excluding 15 participants with Mini-cog score = 0, the associations between UA and metrics of gait vigor, symmetry, and stability remained consistent with the primary findings (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

Based on wearable sensor-based measurements, we demonstrated that higher UA levels were associated with impaired gait vigor, symmetry, and stability. Our findings offer new insights into the utility of UA as a novel biomarker of gait mobility dysfunction, potentially reflecting its association with impaired skeletal muscle integrity and function.

In this study, we incorporated periodic, kinematic, kinetic, and spatiotemporal gait measurements and further analyzed the asymmetry and variability of each gait characteristic. These high-dimensional gait mobility features provided more systematic and comprehensive biomechanical information regarding neuromuscular coordination compared with gait speed alone. The high UA group exhibited significantly older age, higher BMI, greater hypertension prevalence, and elevated inflammatory markers compared with the normal UA group, all of which are established determinants of gait impairment. These baseline imbalances could partially account for the unadjusted gait differences observed between groups, underscoring the importance of multivariable adjustment to disentangle the independent contribution of UA. Even after adjusting for demographic and health-related factors, UA was negatively associated with multiple gait vigor indicators, including thigh acceleration, thigh swing work, ground reaction force, foot landing control force, stride frequency, and stride length (all P < 0.05). These findings are consistent with evidence linking high UA to reductions in muscle strength and relative muscle mass, suggesting that UA is associated with oxidative stress and inflammation, caused by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) in muscle tissue, which may be related to neuromuscular coordination and gait performance [14, 25, 26]. We acknowledge that the reported effect sizes are small in absolute terms. These estimates reflect the change in raw gait parameters associated with a one standard deviation increase in serum uric acid, and the magnitude depends on the original measurement unit of each gait parameter. For example, swing time, stride length, and foot landing control force are measured in milliseconds, meters, and arbitrary units, respectively, so the numerical betas are not directly comparable across outcomes. Therefore, individual effect sizes may not translate into immediate clinically detectable functional impairment at the person level. However, subclinical changes in gait variability and kinetic parameters often precede clinically detectable mobility decline, and small alterations accumulated over time or combined with other risk factors may contribute to accelerated gait deterioration in older adults. From this perspective, the primary value of our findings is to identify serum uric acid as an early biomarker of multidimensional gait decline rather than as a determinant of large clinical change.

Burke et al. reported that hyperuricemia and gout in older adults were linked to slower walking speed, supporting our observation that higher UA concentrations compromise gait propulsion [11]. Similarly, Zheng et al. found a positive association between UA and double-support time in patients with cerebral small vessel disease [27], indicating reduced gait control and compensatory changes in gait periodicity, which may represent a common pattern in aging populations. In contrast, Wu et al. observed lower serum UA levels in Parkinson’s patients with freezing of gait, suggesting a potential neuroprotective role of UA. Such discrepancies may arise from population differences. In community-dwelling older adults without neurological disease, the pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory effects of elevated UA may predominate and impair gait, whereas in neurodegenerative disorders, its antioxidant capacity could be more relevant, and lower UA levels may weaken neuroprotection. While moderate UA levels may help maintain muscle mass and strength due to antioxidant properties [28, 29], excessive UA appears detrimental, affecting gait symmetry and stability, which may reflect its dual role as both an antioxidant and a pro-oxidant depending on concentration and context. Notably, several studies suggest a potential non-linear U-shaped association between uric acid levels and gait impairment, whereby both excessively high and low UA levels may be related to slower walking speed [11, 27]. Another study also found moderate levels of UA were associated with better handgrip strength [30]. However, in the current study, quadratic term tests yielded non-significant results for the vast majority of gait parameters, and scatter plot visualizations confirmed the absence of curvilinear trends. Our data therefore support a predominantly linear association between UA and gait performance within the UA range observed in this community-dwelling population. The directionally distinct correlations between UA and different gait parameters should be interpreted as reflecting different mechanistic pathways within a linear framework rather than evidence of a U-shaped non-linear mechanism. Non-linear associations may emerge in populations with a wider UA distribution, which would require larger samples to detect. By promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and vasoconstriction, UA may directly or indirectly contribute to impaired muscle function and neuromuscular control, which may be associated with altered gait performance. This underscores its potential utility as a biomarker of gait decline, which warrants further investigation in diverse populations and clinical settings.

Existing evidence suggests that the relationship between UA and gait impairment may involve a multifaceted network of metabolic, neurotrophic, inflammatory, and oxidative mechanisms, reflecting its potential dual effects [15, 31]. As the most abundant endogenous antioxidant in human plasma, UA is known to scavenge hydroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen, thereby protecting skeletal muscle fibers and neural tissues from oxidative injury under physiological conditions. At physiological levels, UA has been proposed to function as an endogenous antioxidant that may protect neuromuscular tissues from oxidative damage, thereby supporting normal muscle contraction and neural transmission. This antioxidant capacity is, however, concentration- and context-dependent. When UA exceeds its solubility threshold or when the microenvironment becomes pro-inflammatory, it may paradoxically convert into a pro-oxidant, generating free radicals that propagate oxidative chain reactions and amplify inflammatory cascades. However, when UA exceeds its physiological range, it may shift toward pro-oxidant activity, intensifying oxidative stress and inflammation [32, 33]. These changes could lead to muscle cell damage and joint inflammation, consistent with findings by Dempsey et al. that UA induces endothelial dysfunction and aggravates inflammatory responses [34]. In the current study, UA elevation was predominantly mild to moderate, a range in which the pro-oxidant effects of UA likely begin to predominate over its antioxidant protection, consistent with the linear negative associations observed between UA and gait parameters. In addition, fine motor control of gait relies on intact cerebrovascular and neural networks. Hyperuricemia may aggravate cerebral small vessel disease and promote urate crystal deposition, leading to vascular stenosis, reduced perfusion, and white matter disruption. Such changes could impair the basal ganglia–thalamus–cortex circuit responsible for gait coordination, resulting in reduced foot placement precision and swing power [27]. A bidirectional association also exists between UA and physical activity. Higher UA levels correlate with fewer daily steps, possibly due to joint pain or muscle weakness. Although hyperuricemia frequently coexists with components of metabolic syndrome, such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, the association between UA and gait parameters remained significant in our study after adjusting for these confounders in the multivariate analysis. This suggests that the relationship between UA and gait performance is not merely dependent on other metabolic diseases but may be independently associated with gait function through independent mechanisms, such as systemic inflammation and vascular endothelial damage.

As shown in Fig. 4, specific inflammatory markers and oxidative stress markers were identified, potentially indicating the underlying statistical pathways that may account for the association between UA and gait mobility. Formal mediation analyses further quantified these pathways and confirmed IL-6, SOD and GR as significant mediators, with IL-6 mediating UA’s associations with gait vigor and symmetry, SOD with gait vigor and stability, and GR showing significant statistical mediation of UA’s associations with multiple gait vigor parameters. Although the proportions mediated were generally modest, mostly below 15%, these findings suggest that elevated UA may disrupt neuromuscular function by shifting toward pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory states. Higher UA levels were associated with increased IL-6, which in turn correlated with reduced kinetic and spatiotemporal performance and greater gait asymmetry and variability. Conversely, UA-related reductions in SOD and GR may further impair muscle contractile efficiency and movement precision, exacerbating gait instability. These exploratory results suggest that systemic inflammation and oxidative stress may represent intermediate pathways associated with hyperuricemia and gait impairment in older adults, independent of metabolic comorbidities. These analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mechanisms.

Another novel finding of this study is the positive association between UA and the asymmetry or variability of thigh acceleration, ground reaction force, foot landing control force, and toe-off angle, indicating that elevated UA may be associated with impaired neuromuscular control, thereby contributing to altered lower-limb coordination and muscle function during locomotion. Experimental evidence suggests that elevated UA can promote intracellular ROS production via NADPH oxidase activation, increased xanthine oxidase activity, and mitochondrial electron leakage, leading to oxidative damage in skeletal muscle cells [16, 35]. This pro-oxidant effect may trigger inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired nutrient delivery, while also disrupting metabolic and renal homeostasis, collectively resulting in maladaptive muscle activation and reduced synchronization during gait [36, 37]. In addition to muscular effects, chronic oxidative and inflammatory stress related to hyperuricemia may compromise peripheral nerve integrity and neuromuscular junction signaling, diminishing the precision and timing of motor unit recruitment [38]. These peripheral neuro-muscular alterations, combined with the central vascular and white matter disruptions described above, could collectively impair motor coordination and feedback control [27]. Such intertwined mechanisms may manifest as greater asymmetry and variability in lower-limb kinematic and kinetic parameters, suggesting that the detrimental oxidative, inflammatory, and neurovascular consequences of elevated UA may exceed its potential neuroprotective properties, ultimately contributing to gait instability in older adults.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the design prevents any conclusions regarding causality between UA and gait performance. The mediation analyses are exploratory and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of causal pathways, because the temporality between UA, biomarkers, and gait parameters cannot be established. Longitudinal investigations are needed to determine whether UA can predict changes in gait over time. Second, as all participants were recruited from urban regions in China, the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations with different demographic or environmental backgrounds. Furthermore, older adults who are able to complete the walking test may have better physical and cognitive function, which could introduce potential selection bias. Third, we did not collect detailed information on purine-rich dietary intakes such as red meat and alcohol consumption, nor did we assess the use of urate-lowering medications such as allopurinol. Besides, several factors known to influence both UA and gait performance were not measured or adjusted for, including renal function, diuretic use, history of gout, osteoarthritis, musculoskeletal pain, peripheral neuropathy, and cardiovascular disease burden. These factors can directly influence serum UA levels and may introduce residual confounding that we could not fully account for. Future studies should incorporate detailed dietary assessments and medication inventories to more precisely evaluate the association between UA and gait parameters. Additionally, kinetic parameters are estimated indirectly via accelerometry rather than force plates, and cross-validation with reference biomechanical measurement methods is warranted in future investigations. Fourth, although our study suggests an explanatory role for inflammation and oxidative stress in the relationship between UA and gait, the integration of multi-omics and animal experiments is still needed to further validate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Finally, the simultaneous evaluation of 45 gait parameters renders this study inherently exploratory. While FDR correction was applied to control false discovery rates, the primary associations identified should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in independent datasets before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

Conclusion

Higher UA is associated with inferior gait vigor, symmetry, and stability performance, potentially partially explained by inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the causal relationship between UA levels and gait performance. By employing wearable sensors for a fine-grained, multidimensional assessment of gait parameters, our study provides novel insights into the complex interplay between UA, neuromuscular coordination, and skeletal muscle function.
