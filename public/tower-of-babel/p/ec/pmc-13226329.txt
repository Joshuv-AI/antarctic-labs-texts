Hotspots and Trends in Research on Postpartum Pelvic Floor Dysfunction: A Visualization and Bibliometric Analysis Using CiteSpace
Authors: Qianqian Zhou, Yiwen Jiang, Qing Cai, Leilei Zhu, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) is a common condition that affects many women after childbirth. A considerable number of studies have been published on postpartum PFD; however, there is a lack of systematic quantification and comprehensive summary of these studies. This study employed bibliometric methods to systematically analyze the research status, hotspots, and emerging trends in postpartum PFD from 2005 to 2024.MethodsStudies that were concerned with postpartum PFD were extracted from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), covering the period from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2024. Authors, countries, institutions, references, and keywords in the field were visualized and analyzed using CiteSpace software.ResultsOver the past two decades, the number of publications has increased, with 1149 publications identified. International Urogynecology Journal was the most-cited journal. The research institution with the most publications was the University of Oslo. Kari Bø is the most prolific author in this field. Keyword analysis suggested that stress urinary incontinence, anal incontinence, chronic pain, mode of delivery, and transperineal ultrasound have become research hotspots in recent years.ConclusionsThis study provides a reference for current research hotspots and trends in the field of postpartum PFD through a bibliometric analysis. Future efforts should foster global collaboration, strengthen multidisciplinary studies, and advance the exploration of AI-based diagnostic and therapeutic technologies.Keywords: Postpartum period, Pelvic floor disorders, Women’s health, Bibliometrics

Introduction

Pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) refers to a group of disorders characterized by the interrelated symptoms of damaged pelvic floor support structures, injuries, or dysfunction. These conditions primarily cause urinary incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse, and pelvic pain [1]. Injuries caused by pregnancy and childbirth are primary predisposing factors for PFD. During pregnancy, increased intra-abdominal pressure due to a growing uterus, coupled with a change in the axis of the lumbar spine, can cause a significant amount of stretching in the nervous, muscular, fascial, and ligament structures of the pelvic floor. This can result in anatomical and functional changes [2]. Postpartum PFD has a high incidence rate, with a global prevalence ranging from 20% to 45% [3–5]. The consequences of postpartum PFD are manifold, giving rise to physical ailments and deleterious psychological effects, including anxiety and depression. This can result in a marked decrease in postpartum women’s quality of life, exerting a detrimental effect on family relationships and social interactions [6].

Rising maternal health awareness and growing academic interest have driven a marked global increase in postpartum PFD research since the early twenty-first century. Research in this field has gradually shifted from focusing solely on etiology to multidisciplinary strategies for comprehensive prevention and management, such as early screening, postpartum rehabilitation guidance, and psychological interventions [1, 7]. However, given the complexity and diversity of research in this field, a comprehensive visualization of its overall development, knowledge structure, and hotspot evolution remains lacking. Bibliometrics, a methodology dedicated to analyzing disciplinary development trends, can effectively address this challenge [8].

CiteSpace is a classic and widely used visualization tool in bibliometrics and scientific knowledge mapping. It can analyze research data across multiple dimensions, including authors, institutions, and keywords, and visualize results to help researchers interpret complexities and identify valuable insights [9]. This study employed CiteSpace to conduct a systematic visualization analysis of publications on postpartum PFD over the past two decades, aiming to: (1) map collaborations among leading authors, institutions, and countries to highlight key research forces and their interactions; (2) analyze the current research status and frontiers of the field, helping researchers quickly recognize prevailing hotspots; and (3) track the evolution of keywords to project future trends and offer data-driven insights to advance broader and deeper research in this domain.

Materials and Methods

Article Retrieval

Data were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database on February 25, 2025.

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and written informed consent were not required for this bibliometric analysis. The literature search was performed using the search strategy: TS = (“postpartum” OR “after childbirth” OR “puerperal” OR “postnatal”) AND TS = (“pelvic floor dysfunction” OR “pelvic organ prolapse” OR “sexual dysfunction” OR “urinary incontinence” OR “chronic pelvic pain” OR “fecal incontinence” OR “obstructed defecation”). In total, 1551 publications were identified using the search strategy. The publication period was restricted to January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2024, and the language was limited to English. The document types were limited to “article” and “review,” resulting in a total of 1372 publications.

Literature Screening

EndNote was used to remove duplicates and records with identical titles. To ensure data reliability, two researchers independently screened titles and abstracts, excluding irrelevant or incomplete records. Disagreements were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached. In total, 1149 publications meeting the inclusion criteria were extracted for bibliometric analysis in this study; the detailed screening process is presented in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of literature screening for this study

Analysis Software

A bibliometric analysis was conducted using CiteSpace (version 6.2. R3). This Java-based software is tailored to identify and visualize key trends and research frontiers in the scientific literature [10]. In this study, CiteSpace was used to perform bibliometric analyses across the following dimensions:

A collaboration network analysis of authors, institutions, and countries that visualizes the spatial distribution and intensity of collaborative relationships within a research community.

Co-citation analysis of journals and references aimed at identifying core academic journals and foundational literature constituting the intellectual base of the field.

Keyword analysis

Keyword analysis

Co-occurrence analysis was used to detect research hotspots and core themes by evaluating the frequency and strength of co-occurrences between author keywords and KeyWords Plus.

Cluster analysis, applying a Log-likelihood ratio (LLR), was used to partition a co-occurrence network into thematic clusters representing different research frontiers.

Burst detection, which uses Kleinberg’s algorithm to identify keywords with a sudden surge in usage during specific time periods, was used as a key indicator of emerging trends and shifts in research focus.

Parameter Setting

A total of 1149 screened publications were imported into CiteSpace for analysis. The time span was set from January 1, 2005 to December 31, 2024, with 1 year per time slice. The Top N parameter was set to 50, and the g-index was selected with a scale factor of k = 25. All other parameters were kept at their default settings, and the types of nodes analyzed included authors, institutions, countries, cited journals, references, and keywords. The frequency and centrality of the nodes, as calculated using CiteSpace, were employed to identify research hotspots, key turning points, and emerging trends in postpartum PFD.

Keyword Burst

In CiteSpace, burst keywords are employed to reveal developmental trends and research frontiers within a given field. On the basis of the keyword co-occurrence analysis, the Burstness option in the control panel was selected, with the γ parameter set to 1.5 while keeping all other settings at their default values. After clicking on Refresh, a table of burst keywords was generated.

According to the burst keyword analysis (Fig. 6), “1st delivery” and “parity” remained active research topics for the longest duration. In contrast, the strongest citation bursts of the keywords were “stress incontinence” (burst strength = 9.77) and “cesarean section” (burst strength = 7.01).

In the early stages (2005–2013), burst keywords such as “stress incontinence” and “cesarean section” mainly reflected research focusing on childbirth outcomes and their anatomical and physiological impacts on the urogenital system. During the middle stage (2014–2019), the emergence of terms like “ultrasound” and “levator ani muscle” indicated a growing application of pelvic floor imaging and electrophysiological techniques. During the latest period (2020–2024), emerging keywords—including “diastasis recti,” “disease,” “pain,” and “association”—reflect a shift in research focus. Notably, the rise of “association” points to increasing efforts to investigate interconnections among various conditions, underscoring a broader move toward multidisciplinary approaches.

Discussion

General Information

Over the past two decades, the number of publications on postpartum PFD has steadily increased from 14 in 2005 to 104 in 2024 (Fig. 2). This growth trajectory highlights the increasing attention that maternal pelvic floor health has received from the academic community. The modest decline observed in 2024 does not signify waning research interest but rather heralds a paradigm shift toward evidence integration—a natural progression that emphasizes the imperative for the systematic integration of existing findings to inform future investigative directions. The author collaboration network reveals that early-stage collaborations are relatively fragmented and lack strong central connections. However, since 2015, collaboration trends have strengthened significantly and are characterized by an increase in international cooperation and deeper connections among previously independent researchers. The three most prolific authors were Kari Bø (33 publications), Ingrid E. Nygaard (12 publications), and Marie Engh (7 publications).

Notably, Kari Bø has maintained enduring and intensifying collaborative ties. Since 2006, she has established close collaborations with 28 researchers, including Engh, primarily focusing on the efficacy of pelvic floor muscle exercises in the prevention and treatment of pelvic floor disorders such as urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse [11, 12]. It is noteworthy that all three of the most prolific authors came from backgrounds in obstetrics, gynecology, or rehabilitation medicine, highlighting the field’s strong emphasis on clinical translation.

According to an analysis of publication output and collaboration networks at the national and institutional levels, the top ten most productive countries are primarily located in Europe, North America, and Asia. Among them, the United States, China, and the United Kingdom rank among the top three globally, accounting for approximately 50% of the total publications. These countries serve as major contributors to the field; however, international collaborations tend to be regionally confined, with limited direct cooperation among the three leading countries. This may result in a regional data bias, potentially hindering the long-term development of the field.

At the institutional level, the University of London, with the highest centrality score, has established a relatively systematic transnational collaboration network with 31 other institutions. This research mainly focuses on the epidemiological investigation of postpartum PFD, optimization of rehabilitation interventions, and the application of new technologies [13, 14]. Nevertheless, institutional collaborations still exhibit limited geographical spans, as most partnerships are formed within the same country or with neighboring countries.

Consequently, the geographical limitations of collaborations at both national and institutional levels have emerged as a common issue. Strengthening global collaborative networks is urgently required to promote long-term development in this field. Moreover, existing research findings are predominantly derived from data from high- and middle-income countries, with a marked lack of epidemiological studies and risk-factor analyses in low-income regions. In the future, core contributing countries should be encouraged to collaborate with institutions in low-income areas to conduct postpartum PFD research, enhance their local research capacity, and contribute to the establishment of a global data-sharing platform.

A citation analysis of journals and references revealed that the International Urogynecology Journal was the most frequently cited journal (866 citations), followed by the British Journal of Obstetrics and Gynaecology and Neurourology and Urodynamics. These journals are recognized as high-impact core publications in the field, reflecting the broad scope and well-structured knowledge system of the current research and further confirming the high quality of cited literature.

These journals also represent ideal platforms for publishing high-impact studies in the field of postpartum PFD. In terms of cited literature, the most frequently cited work was a cohort study by Blomquist et al. in 2018, who systematically analyzed the causal relationship between the mode of delivery and postpartum PFD [15]. This study provided critical evidence for the prevention and management of postpartum PFD.

In addition, the study published by Borello-France et al. in 2006, focused on the incidence of postpartum fecal incontinence and urinary incontinence among primiparous women. This study provides a solid foundation for subsequent research in this area [16]. Further analysis of the types of highly cited publications revealed that cohort studies accounted for the majority, with a predominant focus on identifying the risk factors for postpartum PFD. By contrast, reviews and experimental studies are underrepresented. Therefore, future research should place greater emphasis on systematic reviews to synthesize existing evidence and generate clearer clinical guidance. At the same time, more experimental studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of PFD interventions and expand the diversity of study populations, thereby strengthening the evidence base for the prevention and management of postpartum PFD.

Research Hotspots

Postpartum Changes in Urination and Defecation

Analyses of keyword co-occurrence, clustering, and bursts revealed that stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and anal incontinence are among the most frequently studied and rapidly emerging topics in postpartum PFD research.

SUI refers to the involuntary leakage of urine from the external urethral orifice during a sudden increase in intra-abdominal pressure despite the absence of symptoms under normal conditions [17]. Its prevalence among postpartum women is as high as 26% [5]. While not posing a direct threat to life, SUI has a profound impact on patients’ social contact, work, and sex life, ultimately diminishing quality of life and contributing to psychosocial issues such as disease-related stigma, anxiety, and depression [18, 19]. Among these issues, discussions on anxiety and depression are often more visible, whereas stigma is easily overlooked, leading patients to deliberately conceal their conditions, avoid seeking medical care, and experience social isolation [20]. However, most existing assessment tools for urinary incontinence rarely incorporate stigma-related components. Specifically, an evaluation of 11 outcome measurement tools for urinary incontinence interventions revealed that six tools did not include any items related to shame, whereas the remaining five contained only minimal content addressing this dimension [21]. The disconnect between the psychosocial burden of postpartum SUI and the limitations of current clinical assessment tools suggests that existing measures may fail to capture the full scope of patients’ experiences. This gap may hinder the development of holistic, patient-centered intervention strategies.

In recent years, continuous prevention systems for urinary incontinence have been extended to cover both the antenatal and postnatal periods. Specifically, antenatal preventive measures centered on pelvic floor muscle training have been shown to effectively enhance pelvic floor strength and resilience, mitigate obstetric trauma to the pelvic floor, and reduce the incidence of postpartum urinary incontinence [22]. Moreover, early postpartum assessment of PFD, combined with individualized rehabilitation strategies, plays a critical role in the prevention and management of postpartum urinary incontinence [23, 24]. It is worth noting that some women who do not experience urinary incontinence after childbirth may still develop it after menopause, primarily because of a decline in estrogen levels, which leads to reduced elasticity and tone of the pelvic supportive tissues [25]. However, the current research on the continuum from the postpartum period to menopause remains limited, hindering the development of evidence-based strategies for long-term management.

Anal incontinence following childbirth, characterized by the involuntary loss of flatus or stool, is mainly attributed to trauma to the anal sphincter complex and associated nerve damage during delivery. The prevalence reported in the literature varies significantly, ranging from 7% to 74% [26]. A study showed that approximately 86% of women had not disclosed a history of anal incontinence and were identified after clinical screening [27]. This indicates that postpartum anal incontinence is largely underestimated by patients, highlighting a significant gap between actual disease burden and patient self-reporting.

Therefore, future research should focus on addressing the current limitations in three key areas.

Assessment tools should be optimized by incorporating sufficient items related to shame and embarrassment to enable a more comprehensive evaluation of both physiological symptoms and psychosocial status.

Mode of Delivery: A Major Risk Factor for Postpartum PFD

A substantial body of evidence indicates that the mode of delivery is closely associated with the development of postpartum PFD. The stretching forces of the pelvic floor tissues during vaginal delivery, particularly as the fetus passes through the birth canal, can damage the muscles, fascia, and ligaments, significantly increasing the risk of postpartum PFD. The cumulative effect of such injuries is particularly evident in women with multiple vaginal deliveries, which further increases the risk of postpartum PFD [28]. In contrast, cesarean section can reduce direct trauma to the pelvic floor tissues to some extent; however, it does not completely eliminate the risk of postpartum PFD, as pregnancy itself exerts physiological effects on the pelvic floor [29]. It is worth noting that most existing studies have focused on the period between 6 weeks and 6 months postpartum, likely because this timeframe is considered the critical period for pelvic floor recovery. Research conducted at this stage provides important clinical evidence to support early intervention strategies [30, 31]. However, long-term follow-up studies with durations exceeding 5 years remain scarce, resulting in a lack of sufficient longitudinal data to determine whether postpartum PFD symptoms worsen, improve, or develop over time.

These findings underscore several key directions for future research:

To strengthen long-term follow-up studies aimed at elucidating the natural course of postpartum PFD and clarifying symptom trajectories over time.

Ultrasound: An Essential Tool in the Diagnosis of Postpartum PFD

The results of keyword clustering analysis revealed a growing emphasis on the advanced utilization of imaging modalities in the study of postpartum PFD. Transperineal ultrasound, characterized by its noninvasiveness, dynamic capability, and high-resolution imaging, enables real-time assessment of both morphological and functional changes in the pelvic floor muscles. Specifically, it allows for the dynamic evaluation of the levator hiatus area, pelvic floor displacement, and sequential manifestations of pelvic organ prolapse [32]. Studies have confirmed that the thickness of the levator ani muscle, measured using transperineal ultrasound, is significantly negatively correlated with the incidence of postpartum urinary incontinence, whereas the degree of abnormal pelvic floor displacement shows a significant positive correlation with the severity of pelvic organ prolapse [33]. Recent studies have further confirmed that ultrasound examinations can detect pelvic floor support structural injuries at an early stage, particularly during the 42-day postpartum screening period, providing imaging-based evidence to guide the development of individualized rehabilitation strategies [33]. In addition, transperineal ultrasonography can effectively guide patients in performing proper pelvic floor muscle training. It provides real-time visualization of muscle movement direction, thickness changes, and organ positioning during contraction, thereby helping prevent incorrect contractions (e.g., bladder neck descent) or excessive straining [34, 35]. In clinical practice, it is recommended that ultrasound-based assessment parameters be used to dynamically adjust rehabilitation training programs, thereby enhancing the safety and effectiveness of pelvic floor muscle training.

Clinical practice should emphasize the combination of ultrasound assessment results with rehabilitation programs. Future research should focus on optimizing the quantitative ultrasound measurement system, systematically exploring the correlation between imaging findings and clinical symptoms, and actively promoting the application of artificial intelligence in automated analyses to enhance diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.

Postpartum Pain: An Emerging Research Focus

Postpartum pain can be categorized as acute or chronic. Although pregnancy- and delivery-related acute pain typically subsides over time, approximately 35% of women report persistent pain—referred to as chronic postpartum pain—between 8 months and 12 years after childbirth [36]. Chronic postpartum pain refers to a chronic pain syndrome that persists for more than 3 months after childbirth, primarily manifesting as localized or multisite pain. It mainly includes pelvic girdle pain, chronic pelvic pain, and perineal pain, with an incidence ranging from 1% to 18% [37]. It affects not only physical health but is also closely associated with psychosocial dysfunction. Clinical evidence suggests that the prevalence of anxiety and depression is significantly higher in women with chronic postpartum pain than in those without [38]. Its substantial impact on women’s long-term health and chronic postpartum pain remains under-recognized and under-addressed in clinical practice, largely owing to its lower prevalence than that of acute postpartum pain. A study conducted in 2022 found that health information related to chronic postpartum pain was often insufficient or inaccurate. Most women did not receive information regarding the possibility of persistent postpartum pain and were unaware of when and where to seek help [39]. On the basis of the current situation, future research should prioritize strategies to improve disease literacy among postpartum women, such as health education campaigns, evidence-based informational resources, and multimedia outreach. Additionally, developing and evaluating multidisciplinary interventions that incorporate rehabilitation medicine, psychological support, and other relevant disciplines may foster comprehensive biopsychosocial recovery after childbirth.

Growing Focus on Psychosocial Dimensions

In the keyword co-occurrence analysis, “quality of life” and “postpartum depression” appeared as high-frequency terms with 153 and 26 occurrences, respectively (Table 2). Notably, quality of life formed an independent cluster tag (#6), highlighting its central role in postpartum PFD research. Studies have shown that postpartum women with PFD report significantly lower quality of life scores than healthy postpartum women. In particular, symptoms such as urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse often lead to social withdrawal, further exacerbating the psychological burden on affected individuals [40].

Keyword burst analysis further revealed that “postpartum depression” has remained a research hotspot since 2015. Postpartum depression is a depressive disorder that occurs during the postpartum period and is characterized by low mood, loss of pleasure, diminished interest, fatigue, reduced activity, and decreased energy. It has been reported to co-occur with PFD in up to 32% of cases [41, 42]. The physical discomfort caused by PFD may lead to decreased self-esteem and emotional disturbances, while depressive symptoms can further reduce patient adherence to rehabilitation, thereby creating a vicious cycle [43, 44].

Recently, increasing attention has been paid to the psychosocial aspects of PFD. Notable developments include the development of valid and reliable condition-specific quality-of-life questionnaires for women with PFD [45]; prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms and their association with PFD [46]; and effect of depression and anxiety on the success of pelvic floor muscle training for PFD [47]. However, current studies rarely include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on psychological interventions for postpartum pelvic floor disorders (PFD) or longitudinal cohort studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries, resulting in a lack of relevant robust clinical evidence. Therefore, future efforts should focus on developing interdisciplinary interventions that integrate pelvic floor rehabilitation with psychological therapies. Greater emphasis should be placed on incorporating psychosocial factors into the rehabilitation process to promote holistic recovery across the physical, psychological, and social domains.

Limitations

Although tools such as CiteSpace offer valuable insights into the development and trends of research on postpartum PFD, the inherent limitations should also be recognized. First, the data were derived solely from the WoSCC, which may have introduced selection bias. Future studies should consider integrating multiple databases such as Scopus and PubMed to obtain a more comprehensive and representative view of the research landscape. Second, this study included only English-language publications, which may have led to the omission of important studies published in other languages. Although the dominance of English as the primary language of science may mitigate this issue to some extent, future studies should consider incorporating multilingual databases, such as CNKI and SinoMed, to better capture region-specific research developments.

Conclusions

The field of postpartum PFD has developed rapidly in recent years, with the United States, China, and the United Kingdom emerging as leading contributing countries. The University of Oslo and Kari Bø have been identified as the most influential institution and author, respectively, while the International Urogynecology Journal is the most frequently cited in this field. Future research should emphasize a life-course perspective, integrating studies across different stages of women’s lives. There is also a need to incorporate psychosocial dimensions and promote interdisciplinary investigations in mental health interventions. Moreover, efforts should be made to optimize quantitative ultrasound systems and explore the application of artificial intelligence in automated diagnosis. Establishing global, multicenter research networks is essential to address data gaps in low-income regions and provide a more robust evidence base for the treatment of postpartum pelvic floor dysfunction.
