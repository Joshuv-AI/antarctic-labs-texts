Enhanced high-temperature energy storage performances in polymer dielectrics by synergistically optimizing band-gap and polarization of dipolar glass
Authors: Minzheng Yang, Weibin Ren, Zenghui Jin, Erxiang Xu, Yang Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Polymer dielectrics play an irreplaceable role in electrostatic capacitors in modern electrical systems, and have been intensively studied with their polarization and breakdown strength (Eb) optimized for high discharged energy density (Ud) at elevated temperatures. Small molecules have been explored as fillers, yet they deteriorate thermal stability of matrix which limits their optimal loading to ~1 wt%. Herein, we develop a polymer blend dielectric consisting of common polyimide and a bifunctional dipolar glass polymer which are synthesized from two small molecule components with wide band-gap and large dipole moment. The bifunctional dipolar glass with large molecular weight not only maintains thermal stability of polymer blends even at a high loading of 10 wt%, but also induces substantial enhancement in polarization and Eb than any of individual components does, achieving an ultrahigh Ud of 8.34 J cm−3 (150 °C) and 6.21 J cm−3 (200 °C) with a charge-discharge efficiency of 90%.

Introduction

Polymer dielectrics are considered promising candidate as energy storage media in electrostatic capacitors, which play critical roles in power electrical systems involving elevated temperatures, such as hybrid electric vehicles, oil & gas exploration, aircraft, and geothermal facilities1–6. With ever increasing demand for device miniaturization, system integration and higher reliability7, it is imperative to increase the discharged energy density (Ud) of dielectric materials. In general, Ud can be expressed as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${U}_{{{{\rm{d}}}}}=\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon }_{0}{\varepsilon }_{{{{\rm{r}}}}}{{E}_{{{{\rm{b}}}}}}^{2}$$\end{document}Ud=12ε0εrEb2where the ε0 is the vacuum dielectric permittivity (8.85 × 10−12 F m−1), and the εr and Eb are the dielectric constant and breakdown strength of polymer dielectrics, respectively. εr can be simply defined as the ratio of polarization (P, total electric dipole moment per volume in dielectrics) to electric field. Eb is closely related to the insulating properties, such as leakage current and band gap (Eg). In order to achieve higher Ud in wide temperature ranges, it is imperative to simultaneously improve εr, Eb and their thermal stability. Unfortunately, negative couplings between these three aspects have been found in polymer dielectrics for long, which makes the improvement of Ud challenging1. For instance, high-εr dielectrics tend to contain dense high polar groups, which can promote charge migration and decrease Eg and Eb. Besides, insulating properties may also be deteriorated due to charge transfer effect induced by parallel stacking of high-content benzene rings in heat-resistant aromatic polymers. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop strategies that can comprehensively improve the energy storage performances of polymer dielectrics.

Filler incorporation is an extensively used, easily operated strategy for enhancing energy storage performances. As for increasing εr, fillers with high εr are generally chosen according to the mixing rules of composites8–13. Fillers with wide Eg13–16 or high electron affinity17–20 are usually selected to suppress charge migration hence higher Eb. Among various candidates, small organic molecules exhibit multiple advantages as fillers compared to their inorganic counterparts and thus have been intensively studied. Organic molecules exhibit better structural compatibility to polymer matrix, which is favorable for less interfacial defects and better insulating properties21,22. Plus, small molecules possess smaller sizes (~1 nm) and better flexibility than rigid nano-sized inorganics (10–100 nm), thus exhibiting more effective mitigation of the distorted interfacial electric field, which is induced by the mismatch of εr and interfacial charge concentration23,24. Moreover, the εr of organic molecules can be increased by grafting groups with high dipole moment (μ)25–27. However, the small molecules may also act as plasticizer by weaken the interaction between polymer chains, resulting in degraded structural thermal stability of polymer dielectrics which is usually reflected by sharply decreased glass transition temperature (Tg)28. As a result, the optimal filler contents of most reported polymer-molecule composite dielectrics without significant matrix-filler interaction are not more than 1 wt%, which is much lower than those of polymer-inorganic composites (> 10 wt%) and largely limits the improving effects of small molecules17–20,29–33.

The structural thermal stability of polymer-based dielectrics is closely correlated with the interaction between molecules2,34. Compared to small molecules, high-molecular-weight additives, such as another polymer, are conducive to achieve strong interchain interactions and high cohesive energy, and thus providing new opportunity of maintaining high structural stability in polymer blend dielectrics at elevated temperatures. In this work, we employed 1,2,4,5-cyclohexanetetracarboxylic dianhydride (HPMDA) with wide Eg and 3,3’-sulfonyldianiline (NS) containing high-μ group as the functional components and polymerized them to synthesize a bifunctional dipolar glass (DG) polymer. A series of polymer blend dielectrics were then fabricated through blending the DG and common polyimides (PI). The DG possesses the molecular weight larger than 30000 g mol−1, thus maintaining the Tg above 300 °C and performance thermal stability of polymer blend dielectrics with its content up to 10 wt%. Besides, the functional components respectively enhance the polarization and charge trapping, which can be further promoted by the non-linear chain configuration of DG. As a result, polymer-DG blends exhibit improved εr from 2.78 to 3.36 (@103 Hz, 25 °C) and Eb from 551.11 MV m−1 to 761.21 MV m−1 (@200 °C) concomitantly, which boosts an ultrahigh Ud with the charge-discharge efficiency of 90% (Ud90) of 8.34 J cm−3 (150 °C) and 6.21 J cm−3 (200 °C). In addition, charge-discharge cycling stability up to 5 × 104 cycles under high electric field of 600 MV m−1 at 200 °C and large-scale preparation prospects are also achieved in the polymer-DG blend dielectrics.

Results and discussions

Characterizations of bifunctional DG and polymer-DG blends

The structure design of bifunctional DG is shown in Fig. 1A. We chose HPMDA as the wide-Eg component and NS as the large-μ component. HPMDA is a widely used dianhydride for synthesis of PI. Compared to 1,2,4,5-benzenetetracarboxylic anhydride (PMDA) with benzene ring used for Kapton-PI, HPMDA has a saturated alicyclic structure. Therefore, it exhibits a much larger Eg (6.70 eV for HPMDA versus 4.71 eV for PMDA, Figure S1) and weaker intra-/interchain charge transfer effect, which are both beneficial to improving the insulating properties and Eb18,35,36. Moreover, the excellent structural stability under heat and mechanical impacts is maintained for HPMDA owing to its cyclic structure37. NS contains a sulfone group, which possesses a large μ of 4.5 D and has been extensively grafted into polymer chains to fabricate dipolar glass polymers with high εr26,38–43. We first calculated the energy band structures of HPMDA, NS, and DG by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. As shown in Fig. 1B and Figure S1, HPMDA exhibits a large Eg of 6.70 eV while NS has the Eg of 4.62 eV. The synthesized DG retains the Eg of 5.30 eV, which is still much larger than that of common PI such as 3.70 eV for fluorinated PI (FPI for short. Its chemical structure is shown in Figure S2). DG also exhibits the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO, −1.80 eV) than any of its monomer components, i.e., −1.53 eV for HPMDA and −1.26 eV for NS, which suggests improved electron affinity and enhanced charge trapping capability. Furthermore, the electrostatic potential distribution in DG exhibits no significant variation than those in its monomer components (Fig. 1B). DG also shows a typical amorphous characteristic and a Tg of 292.61 °C according to X-Ray diffraction (XRD, Figure S3) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Figure S4), respectively. Based on the gel permeation chromatography (GPC) results (Figure S5), the number-average molecular weight of DG reaches 38577 g mol−1, yielding a degree of polymerization of 88, which is close to that of FPI (120, Table S1). According to the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy results (Figure S6 and Table S2), the characteristic absorption bands of DG can be found, such as 1193.29, 1301.44 cm−1 for S = O, 1375.15 cm−1 for C–N, and 1716.50, 1791.42 cm−1 for C = O. 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of synthesized DG are shown in Figure S7. It can be seen that all peaks can correspond to H or C atoms in certain positions in DG.

FPI-HPMDA, FPI-NS composites and FPI-DG blends were then prepared through a simple solution-casting process. The cross-section morphology of the dielectric films was observed using scanning electron microscope (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and atomic force microscope (AFM). As shown in Fig. 1C, Figure S8 & S9, the cross-sections are smooth without voids and impurities, indicating that these films are of high structural integrity. In addition, there is no phase separations of DG, HPMDA, or NS in polymers according to above results. To examine the residual solvent of polymer blends, we tested the 1H NMR spectrum as shown in Figure S10. As seen, no obvious peak corresponding to DMF ( ~ 2.9 ppm) or NMP ( ~ 2.85 ppm) is observed, indicating high purity of the dielectrics. According to the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curve (Figure S11), FPI-HPMDA, FPI-NS, FPI-DG dielectrics exhibits no significant weight loss among wide temperature range up to 200 °C, indicating good thermal stability in practical operation conditions.

We then performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to obtain the cohesive energy density of FPI composites and blends. As shown in Fig. 1D and Figure S12, the total cohesive energy density of FPI (3.31 × 108 J m−3) markedly decreases with the introduction of HPMDA and NS, e.g., 2.91 × 108 J m−3 for FPI-8 wt% HPMDA and 2.81 × 108 J m−3 for FPI-8 wt% NS. In stark contrast, the introduction of same content of DG enhances the interchain interactions of FPI, yielding an increased total cohesive energy density of 3.39 × 108 J m−3. Consistent with the improved cohesive energy density, FPI-DG blend shows denser chain packing and higher calculated density (1.35 g cm−3) than FPI-HPMDA (1.115 g cm−3) and FPI-NS (1.151 g cm−3).

To verify the simulation results, interchain spacing of FPI composites and blends were determined by XRD results as shown in Fig. 1E and Figure S13 & S14. It can be seen that the DG indeed densifies the chain packing of FPI and thus delivers a decreased interchain spacing from 0.615 nm for FPI to 0.566 nm for FPI-8 wt% DG, which is favorable for high structural stability under mechanical, electrical, and thermal impacts. In comparison, the densifying effects of its monomer components are largely limited. For example, the interchain spacing of FPI-8 wt% HPMDA (0.587 nm) and FPI-8 wt% NS (0.594 nm) are only slightly lower than that of FPI. Furthermore, the Tg of FPI composites and blends according to the DSC results were shown in Fig. 1E and Figure S15 & S16. As the addition of HPMDA or NS, the Tg of FPI-HPMDA and FPI-NS composites sharply fall from 323.4 °C to 256.9 °C for FPI-8 wt% HPMDA and 247.0 °C for FPI-8 wt% NS, respectively. By contrast, FPI-DG blends maintain thermal stability well and a Tg of 321.7 °C for FPI-8 wt% DG is obtained. Similarly, DG provides better mechanical strengthening effect than the two components in its monomer according to nanoindentation test results. As shown in Fig. 1E, the Young’s modulus of FPI-8 wt% DG (5.42 GPa) is higher than those of FPI-8 wt% HPMDA (5.24 GPa), FPI-8 wt% NS (5.35 GPa), and FPI (5.00 GPa). These results are all in line with our hypotheses that the DG with large-molecular-weight can enhance the structural stability of polymers more efficiently than its monomer components.

Polarization of polymer-DG blends

The dielectric spectra of FPI composites and blends versus frequency and temperature were tested as shown in Fig. 2A and Figure S17 & Figure S18. It can be seen that FPI-DG, FPI-HPMDA, and FPI-NS all exhibit a stable εr among the frequency range from 10−1 to 106 Hz. NS can increase the εr of FPI due to its high polar SO2 groups. For example, an improved εr of 3.56 is obtained for FPI-8 wt% NS versus 2.78 for FPI (1 kHz, 25 °C). On the contrary, HPMDA exhibits no significant improvement on εr, e.g., 2.96 for FPI-8 wt% HPMDA. As for the FPI-DG blends, 8 wt% of DG in FPI can induce a εr of 3.36, which is 20% higher than that of FPI. More notably, the dielectric properties of FPI-DG are much more stable than FPI-HPMDA and FPI-NS at wide temperature ranges. For example, as temperature reaches 200 °C, FPI-8 wt% DG maintains a lower dielectric loss (tanδ) of 0.0081 at 1 kHz compared to 0.0097 for FPI. To further explore the mechanism of the remarkable improvement on εr of DG, we implemented MD simulations to calculate the εr and free volume of FPI-8 wt% DG and FPI-8 wt% NS. As shown in Fig. 2B, FPI-8 wt% DG shows a calculated εr nearly 3 times of that of FPI-8 wt% NS despite of less SO2 groups, which confirms the essential role of non-linear chain configuration in improving polarization. The εr disparity of two dielectrics can be attributed to two factors. Firstly, introduction of NS largely loosens the chain packing of FPI owing to weakened intra-/interchain interaction, giving rise to a big void in the simulation cell as shown in the left inset of Fig. 2B and a decreased calculated density of FPI-8 wt% NS as depicted in Fig. 1D. Therefore, the total volume filled with dipoles and overall polarization of FPI-8 wt% NS are both decreased. Besides, the majority of the free volume in FPI-8 wt% DG is dispersed around SO2 groups (the S atoms are shown in red and free volume is shown in blue in the right inset of Fig. 2B and Figure S19). This free volume promotes the rotation of SO2 groups to respond electric field and contributes to higher orientation polarization. In comparison, the free volume in FPI-8 wt% NS concentrates in the voids rather than around SO2 groups, which not only fails to enhance polarization but also compromises the structural stability. We calculated the average distance between SO2 groups and adjacent polymer chains as shown in Fig. 2B. The average spacing of FPI-8 wt% DG (5.32 Å) is much higher than that of FPI-8 wt% NS (3.71 Å), thus further confirming that DG chains with non-linear configuration generate more free volume around polar groups due to steric hindrance for promoting polarization44. Note that the average spacing here indicates the local free volume around SO2, but not the average distance between adjacent DG and FPI chains.

Additionally, AFM-IR were performed to examine the response of SO2 groups to IR radiation in FPI-8 wt% DG and FPI-8 wt% NS. Under the IR radiation with the wavenumber of 1308 cm−1 (S = O asymmetric stretching, Figure S20), the SO2 groups in FPI-8 wt% DG (lower) exhibits comparable absorbance intensity to FPI-8 wt% NS (upper) as shown in Fig. 2C and Figure S21. It is noteworthy that the former contains only about half the quantity of SO2 groups in the latter, indicating a significantly enhanced contribution to the vibrational polarization of each SO2 group in FPI-DG blends45.

Breakdown strength and insulating properties of polymer-DG blends

As most of the operation conditions of polymer dielectrics involve high temperatures, we tested the high temperature Eb of FPI-DG, FPI-HPMDA, and FPI-NS as shown in Fig. 2D and Figure S22. It can be seen that DG significantly boosts the Eb of FPI, e.g., from 579.32 MV m−1 to 784.10 MV m−1 for FPI-8 wt% DG at 150 °C. At 200 °C, this improvement of 35.34% further increases to 38.12%, i.e., 761.21 MV m−1 versus 551.11 MV m−1. In comparison, high-Eg HPMDA can only slightly increase the Eb to 625.79 MV m−1 (200 °C) at 5 wt% loading. As the HPMDA content further rises, its Eb decreases due to deteriorated thermal stability. In addition, when NS is introduced into FPI, its Eb sharply falls below 300 MV m−1, e.g., 280.53 MV m−1 for FPI-8 wt% NS at 200 °C. The effect comparison between DG and HPMDA, NS confirms the necessity of non-linear chain configuration for achieving high Eb.

To shed more light on the insulating property enhancement of FPI-DG blends, the leakage current density of FPI composites and blends were tested as shown in Figure S23. DG is more efficient in suppressing the leakage current density than HPMDA, e.g., 1.17 × 10−7 A cm−2 for FPI-8 wt% DG compared to 2.10 × 10−7 A cm−2 for FPI-8 wt% HPMDA and 8.47 × 10−7 A cm−2 for FPI-8 wt% NS at 200 MV m−1 and 200 °C. Similarly, the improvement on optical Eg of DG is larger than that of HPMDA according to ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra as shown in Figure S24 and S25. For example, 8 wt% of DG can increase the Eg of FPI from 3.7 eV to 3.74 eV. Despite the much higher Eg of HPMDA (6.70 eV) than that of DG (5.30 eV), 8 wt% of HPMDA only induces a moderate increase in Eg of 3.73 eV. Note that even though there may be a difference between tested optical Eg and real Eg, comparison of the four values of same test conditions still makes sense for analyzing the insulating properties of polymer dielectrics40. And, since the 92 wt% of these dielectrics is FPI, the difference between their optical Eg should be slight. However, the size relationships of them are clear, which are highlighted in the inset of Figure S24.

We further tested and analyzed thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) curves to study the charge trapping characteristics of FPI, FPI-8 wt% DG, FPI-8 wt% HPMDA, FPI-8 wt% NS as shown in Figs. 2E, F, Figure S26, and Table S346,47. Two peaks can be fitted in all TSDC curves, in which peak 1 (dashed black line) at lower temperature can be attributed to β relaxation of polymer chains, and the peak 2 (dashed red line) at higher temperature corresponds to the charge injection from electrodes. As for peak 2, higher temperature and smaller area are preferred which indicate higher injection barrier and less injected charges. In TSDC curves of FPI composites and blends, another peak (peak 3 in dashed blue line) was observed which indicates the introduced charge traps of new components. For this peak, high temperature is desired for better charge trapping capability. As seen, peak 3 of FPI-DG blends is located at higher temperature (228.09 °C) than FPI-HPMDA (216.28 °C) and FPI-NS (226.50 °C), suggesting deeper trap introduced by of DG. Besides, peak 2 of FPI-DG is also observed at higher temperature and exhibits smaller area (220.47 °C and 0.468 × 1020 m−3) than FPI (199.12 °C and 0.58 × 1020 m−3), FPI-HPMDA (215.38 °C and 0.504 × 1020 m−3), and FPI-NS (184.38 °C and 0.958 × 1020 m−3), indicating better charge injection suppression of DG with enhanced intra-/interchain interactions and wide band gap. To highlight the advantages of organic DG over inorganic components, we prepared FPI-boron nitride (BN, has a similar band gap of 5.72 eV) composites with an optimal content of 3 wt% (determined by the high temperature Eb, Figure S27). The TSDC curve and trap parameters of FPI-3 wt% BN can be seen in Figure S26 and Table S3. Even though BN introduces new traps with a temperature location of 209.18 °C (peak 3), its peak 2 exhibits a lower temperature (193.69 °C) and larger area (1.164 × 1020 m−3) than pure FPI, which can be attributed to the polymer-inorganic interface with dense defects and poor insulating properties.

Furthermore, α relaxation of polymers (glass transition), can be observed in TSDC curves as shown in the inset of Fig. 2E. The Tg of FPI-DG (287 °C) according to TSDC curve is still close to that of FPI (296 °C) and much higher than those of FPI-HPMDA (265 °C) and FPI-NS (256 °C), which is consistent with the DSC results and indicates the maintained structural stability of FPI-DG blends at high temperature.

Energy Storage Performances of Polymer-DG blends

We summarized the energy storage performances of FPI-DG blends at high temperatures (150 °C and 200 °C) according to their electric displacement-electric field (D-E) loops as shown in Fig. 3A, B, and Figure S28 & S29. As seen, DG remarkably improves the Ud and charge-discharge efficiency (η) of FPI. At 150 °C, a high η above 90% is retained for FPI-8 wt% DG until breakdown and an ultrahigh maximum Ud (Udmax) and Ud90 of 8.34 J cm−3 is obtained, which is 48% higher than the Udmax and 145% higher than the Ud90 of FPI (5.64 J cm−3 and 3.40 J cm−3), respectively. At 200 °C, FPI-8 wt% DG still maintains a Udmax of 7.72 J cm−3 and a Ud90 of 6.21 J cm−3, which means the improvements further increase to 73% (4.46 J cm−3 for FPI) and 215% (1.97 J cm−3 for FPI). More importantly, as a representative performance indicator, the Ud90 of FPI-8 wt% DG at high temperatures surpass most of the reported polymer-based dielectrics as shown in Fig. 3C (150 °C)17,18,20,39,40,48–51 and 3D (200 °C)14,15,17,20,40,48,50,52,53. In comparison, HPMDA shows much weaker improving effect than DG, and only a Ud90 of 4.47 J cm−3 (150 °C) and 3.32 J cm−3 (200 °C) for FPI-8 wt% HPMDA are yielded. As for FPI-8 wt% NS, despite its higher Ud than FPI-DG at the same electric field owing to higher εr, the poor insulating property and low Eb severely limits its η and Udmax, which are even worse than those of pure FPI.

To prove the performance improvement generality of the bifunctional DG on polymer dielectrics, we employed other common heat-resistant polymers, such as PEI (Ultem 1000) and soluble PI (SPI for short, Matrimid 5218), and prepared PEI-DG and SPI-DG blends (The chemical structures of these two polymers are shown in Figure S2). The dielectric spectra and breakdown electric field Weibull distribution of them were shown in Figure S31 and S32. It can be seen that DG delivers comprehensive performance improvement on these two polymers. A εr improvements of 8.9% (from 3.25 to 3.54 at 1 kHz, 25 °C) and a Eb improvement of 28.0% (from 433.99 MV m−1 to 555.49 MV m−1 at 200 °C) are simultaneously obtained for PEI-8 wt% DG. As for SPI-8 wt% DG, the enhancements are even more remarkable, i.e., 13.7% for εr (from 3.06 to 3.48 at 1 kHz, 25 °C) and 28.9% for Eb (from 464.77 MV m−1 to 599.15 MV m−1 at 200 °C), respectively. The TSDC curves with the trap parameters of PEI, SPI, and their blends are shown in Figure S33 and Table S4. As seen, the introduction of DG induces a new peak (peak 3) with a high temperature of 208.77 °C for SPI-8 wt% DG and 212.04 °C for PEI-8 wt% DG, respectively. Moreover, the suppressed charge injections can be observed in both blends according to higher temperature and lower area of peak 2.

The energy storage performances of PEI-8 wt% DG and SPI-8 wt% DG were summarized in Figure S34 and S35. As seen, the improvements of DG on Ud and η of polymers are both substantial. In particularly, we compared the high temperature Udmax and Ud90 of polymers and polymer-DG blends as shown in Fig. 3E, F. Besides FPI-8 wt% DG, PEI-8 wt% DG also maintains the η higher than 90% before breakdown and thus achieves a same Udmax and Ud90 of 5.48 J cm−3 at 150 °C, which are more than 1.78 and 4.15 times of those of pure PEI (3.08 J cm−3 and 1.32 J cm−3). SPI-8 wt% DG also demonstrates a remarkably improved Udmax and Ud90 compared to SPI at 150 °C, i.e., 5.18 J cm−3 versus 2.96 J cm−3 and 2.50 J cm−3 versus 1.50 J cm−3, respectively. At 200 °C, the Ud of PEI-8 wt% DG and SPI-8 wt% DG are still much higher than those of corresponding polymers, e.g., 4.10 J cm−3 versus 0.61 J cm−3 (improvement of 572%) and 2.39 J cm−3 versus 0.80 J cm−3 (improvement of 199%) for Ud90, which further confirms the excellent effects of the bifunctional DG.

Reliability and mass production

We operated the charge-discharge tests of FPI-8 wt% DG at 200 °C to examine its cyclic stability. The D-E loops of different cycles and corresponding Ud and η were summarized in Figure S36 and Fig. 4A, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4A, the blend exhibits excellent performance stability during 50000 cycles under the electric field as high as 600 MV m−1, which has never been achieved in other reports so far. The energy storage performances of FPI-8 wt% DG with different electrode diameters at 200 °C were also tested. As seen in Fig. 4B and Figure S37, the Eb of FPI-8 wt% DG with the electrode diameters of 3 mm, 8 mm, 12 mm are 761.2 MV m−1, 706.0 MV m−1, and 649.9 MV m−1, respectively. Therefore, a Eb retention ratio of 85.4% is obtained for FPI-8 wt% DG as the electrode diameter varies from 3 mm to 12 mm, which is even higher than that of pure FPI (83.8%). As a contrast, same content of HPMDA decreases the Eb more sharply with the increase of electrode diameter, yielding a lower retention ratio of 80.0%. The tested Ud and η of FPI-8 wt% DG with different electrode diameters are stable as well (Fig. 4C). The retention ratios of Ud and η at 600 MV m−1 with the electrode diameter of 12 mm versus those of 3 mm are as high as 97.0% and 99.7%. In order to examine the performances of our blend dielectrics in practical operation conditions, we tested the Eb and Ud of FPI-8 wt% DG blends at high temperatures with aluminum electrodes that are more commonly used in polymer film capacitors. As shown in Figure S38, FPI-8 wt% DG has a Eb of 769.62 MV m−1 and 748.10 MV m−1 at 150 °C and 200 °C, respectively, which are comparable to those for copper electrodes. A high Ud90 of 8.21 J cm−3 and 5.90 J cm−3 is also achieved at 150 °C and 200 °C as shown in Figure S39. Then, a big FPI-8 wt% DG blend film with the size of 24 cm × 15 cm were prepared using the solution-casting process to verify its prospect of large-scale preparation as shown in Fig. 4D. It can be seen that the film shows good transparency, flexibility, and surface smoothness. The energy storage performances of different regions in the film were tested and summarized in Fig. 4E. As seen, their D-E loops possess quite similar shape and size at 600 MV m−1 and 200 °C. The high temperature Eb of them is also close to that of smaller samples as mentioned above (761.2 MV m−1 at 200 °C). In addition, fast discharge test of FPI-8 wt% DG was operated. As shown in Fig. 4F, even at 200 °C, FPI-8 wt% DG yields a lower τ95 (the time spent to discharge 95% of the total charged energy, 3.20 μs) and a higher energy density (0.53 J cm−3) compared to biaxial oriented polypropylene (BOPP) at 70 °C (5.67 μs and 0.40 J cm−3). In conclusion, we synthesized a bifunctional DG polymer through condensation polymerization of two small molecules, i.e., HPMDA with wide Eg and NS with high μ, and developed a polymer-DG blend dielectric with a well-maintained thermal stability in wide temperature range. The DG has the large molecular weight over 30000 g mol−1 to guarantee the structural stability of overall dielectrics at elevated temperatures which can be proved by the maintained cohesive energy density, Tg, and improved density in polymer-DG blends. Besides, the HPMDA and NS in DG act as functional components to boost the Eb and εr of polymers simultaneously. More notably, the non-linear configuration of DG further enhances these two effects through mechanically strengthening the polymer, trapping charges and modulating the free volume around polar groups. As a result, the FPI-8 wt% DG blend achieves an ultrahigh Ud90 of 8.34 J cm−3 at 150 °C and 6.21 J cm−3 at 200 °C, which surpass most of the reported polymer-based dielectrics. Furthermore, the FPI-8 wt% DG exhibits stable performances during the long charge-discharge cycling of 50000 cycles under the electric field as high as 600 MV m−1 at 200 °C and potential in large scale manufacturing. This work demonstrates the remarkable comprehensive improvement of the bifunctional DG on high temperature capacitive energy storage properties of polymer dielectrics, and indicates a polymer blend strategy to develop high performance polymer-based dielectrics.

Methods

Materials

1,2,4,5-cyclohexanetetracarboxylic dianhydride (HPMDA) and 3,3’-sulfonyldianiline (NS) were obtained from TCI and dried at high temperature under vacuum before use. All reagents were used as purchased without further purification. Polyetherimide pellets (Ultem 1000, PEI) were purchased from General Electric (GE). PI with -CF3 groups (KPI-MX300F, FPI) and soluble PI (Matrimid 5218, SPI) powders were obtained from Kawamura Industries Co. LTD and PolyK, respectively. The super-dry N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP) and isoquinoline were supplied by J&K Scientific Ltd. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were obtained from China National Chemicals Corporation Ltd. Deionized water was used and all the chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of bifunctional DG

The bifunctional DG was prepared through a one-step method, involving the dissolution of equal molar amounts of HPMDA (10 mmol, 2.242 g, 98%) and NS (10 mmol, 2.483 g, 98%) in NMP (15 ml, super dry). The mixture was then subjected to reaction with isoquinoline (5 mmol, 0.646 g, 98%) as an imidization catalyst under a nitrogen atmosphere. This reaction occurred at 40 °C for 3 h, followed by 140 °C for an additional 3 h, and finally at 180 °C for 12 h. The resulting DG solution was subsequently precipitated into ethanol, yielding a white powder that was collected by filtration. Following this, the powder underwent vacuum drying at 100 °C for 12 h, ultimately yielding the final product. This sequence of processing steps ensured the efficient synthesis of high-quality DG.

Preparation of polymer films

As for PEI and SPI films, the PEI and SPI were dissolved in NMP with a concentration of 150 mg mL−1 through vigorous stirring at 60 °C for 4 h. As for polymer composite and blend samples, a certain amount of DG/HPMDA/NS was added into the solution and the final clear solution were obtained after vigorous stirring at 60 °C for 4 h. The as-prepared solution was cast onto clean glass slides and moved into a vacuum oven at 45 °C for 12 h, and 220 °C for 4 h successively to remove the residual solvent. Finally, the films were peeled off from the glass slides and annealed in a vacuum oven at 110 °C for 2 h to evaporate the water. As for FPI composites and blends, the FPI was dissolved in DMF with a concentration of 100 mg mL−1 and the rest of the steps are the same as above. The thickness of the films is ~10 μm.

Characterization

SEM cross-sectional micromorphology of films was characterized by a ZEISS MWRLIN compact. TEM images were acquired with a JEOL JEM2010 electron microscope. AFM topology images were obtained with a commercial scanning probe microscope (Asylum Research MFP-3D) in the tapping mode. Silicon cantilevers with a conducting Pt/Ir coating layer (PPP-EFM, Nanosensors) were used. The relative molecular weight and molecular-weight distribution were determined by GPC Agilent PL-GPC50 with differential refractive index (RI) detector. One and two mixed-C columns (Plgel mixed, 5 μm pore size, 300 mm × 7.5 mm) were utilized for FPI and DG, respectively. GPC measurements were performed at 40 °C using HPLC grade DMF as the eluent with a flow rate of 1.0 ml min−1. Polystyrene standards were used for conventional calibration. XRD patterns were measured by a Rigaku D/max-2550 in a 2theta range of 5–30 °. DSC curves were obtained by a DSC Q2000 (TA Instrument). The samples were first cooled down to 25 °C with a rate of 10 °C min−1. After an isothermal stage for 5 min, the DSC curves were recorded by heating the samples up to 450 °C with a rate of 10 °C min−1. All stages were carried out in nitrogen atmosphere. The data sampling interval is 0.2 s pt−1. Young’s moduli were measured by a nano-indenter (Keysight Technologies G200) using a diamond indenter with a Triangular pyramid shape (TB26375). The nominal radius of indenter is 20 nm and the indentation depth is 500 nm. 5 samples were tested for each data point. Square polymeric films with the size of 2 cm × 2 cm were used for Young’s moduli tests. AFM-IR measurements were implemented using a NanoIR3 (Bruker, USA). Contact-mode Si tips coated with gold of spring constant 0.07-0.4 N m−1 (PR-EX-NIR2-10, radius ≈ 20 nm) were used. UV-Vis spectroscopy were carried out using a Lambda 1050+ instrument (PerkinElmer) with a 150 mm-diameter sphere and slit width of 2 nm. The data is recorded in the absorption mode with a wavelength of 250–800 nm and a data interval of 2 nm. TGA curves were obtained using a Q5000IR (TA instrument) with a heating speed of 10 °C min−1 in nitrogen atmosphere from room temperature to 900 °C. FT-IR spectra were obtained in a Thermo. Nicolt Is50 FT-IR spectrometer over a wavenumber range of 450–4000 cm−1. NMR spectra were tested using a Quantum-IPlus 600-2 instrument. The test frequency for proton and carbon NMR are 600 MHz and 150 MHz, respectively. The DMSO-d6 were used for solvents.

Copper electrodes with diameters of 3 mm and 11.5 mm were sputtered on the both sides of films for characterization under high electric field (D-E loop, breakdown strength, and leakage current) and low electric field (dielectric spectra and thermally stimulated depolarization currents), respectively. Dielectric spectra were measured by Novocontrol broadband dielectric/impedance spectrometer (Novocontrol Technologies GmbH& Co. KG.) with a liquid nitrogen cooling system over a frequency range of 10−1–106 Hz and a temperature range of 25–200 °C. Thermally stimulated depolarization currents were obtained by Novocontrol broadband dielectric/impedance spectrometer as well. The samples were first polarized at 200 °C using an electric field of 40 MV m−1 for 10 min. Then the samples under the electric field were cooled down to −100 °C with the rate of 10 °C min−1. After that, samples were heated up to 300 °C with the rate of 3 °C min−1, during which the TSDC density was recorded.

D-E loops were measured using a polarization loop & dielectric breakdown test system (PolyK Technologies, LLC). The samples were soaked in high temperature resistant silicone oil on a heating stage to reach the test temperatures. Breakdown electric field was determined with a Trek 20/20B instrument. The voltage applied on samples increases with a rate of 500 V s−1 until breakdown. Breakdown strength of each sample were calculated by a two-parameter Weibull statistic. Leakage current tests were performed using a multiferroic ferroelectric test system (Radiant Technologies, Inc. Premier II). Fast charge-discharge tests were carried out with a PK-DIS20012 system (PolyK Technologies, LLC). The load resistor and internal resistor were chosen as 20 kΩ and 110 Ω, respectively.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation

DFT calculation for geometry optimization of polymers and molecules and their electrostatic potential distribution was conducted using a DMol3 module of Materials Studio software utilizing the hybrid method with a three-parameter Becke-style hybrid functional (B3LYP)54–56 exchange-correlation functional with the large basis set (DNP 4.4)57,58. The vacuum level is aligned with 0. The DG chain has a degree of polymerization of 4, and the self-consistent field (SCF) convergence limit is 1.0e-5.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

MD simulation was carried out through a large-scale parallel Atomic/Molecular simulation software (LAMMPS23)59,60. During the MD simulation process, all atom (OPLS-AA) force field61 is used to simulate the interaction between atoms. The structure of simulation cells was constructed with PACKMOL software62,63 and the components (DG, HPMDA, NS) were added at a ratio of 8 wt%. The initial cell parameters of structure are a = 76.1902 Å, b = 76.1902 Å, and c = 76.1902 Å. Before MD simulation, the energy of the initial model was minimized by using the steepest descent algorithm64. Then, under the NPT ensemble (T = 298 K, and P = 101 kPa), the temperature was controlled by the Nose-Hoover temperature controller, and the structural relaxation of 100 ps (with a time step of 0.1 fs) was performed to bring the model to equilibrium. In the MD simulation process, the NVT ensemble (T = 298 K) and a total time step of 200 ps MD simulation with 0.1 fs time step was performed. The calculated results were visualized and graphed using OVITO software65. The dielectric constant was obtained with MATLAB and Python software by fitting the calculated dipole data.
