Nanoengineered Cement Mortar: Effects of Nanosilica Particle Size and Water-to-Binder Ratio on Mechanical and Durability Performance
Authors: Sattawat Haruehansapong, Wachirakorn Senawang, Saofee Dueramae, Sunisa Panti, Akkadath Abdulmatin, Wunchock Kroehong, Tawich Pulngern, Weerachart Tangchirapat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effects of nanosilica (NS) particle sizes and water-to-binder (W/B) ratios on the compressive strength, microstructure, sulfate resistance, and acid resistance of cement mortar. NS particles with diameters of 12, 20, and 40 nm were incorporated at 9% replacement levels and tested at W/B ratios of 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85. The results showed that the 40 nm NS particles, at a 9% replacement, provided the highest compressive strength and resistance to both sulfate and acid attacks, particularly at a W/B ratio of 0.65. The study also found that NS enhances the microstructure and pozzolanic reactions of cement mortar more effectively than silica fume. The use of larger NS particles (40 nm) resulted in improved overall performance, with reduced porosity and increased mechanical strength. These findings highlight the role of NS in enhancing the strength and long-term durability of cement mortar under multiple aggressive environments, providing practical insights beyond conventional short-term studies.

Introduction

Concrete structures are increasingly exposed to aggressive environments due to rapid urbanization and industrialization, leading to problems such as cracking, shrinkage, and expansion caused by chemical and physical deterioration. Among the most severe factors, sulfate attack is common in subterranean environments and seawater, especially under alternating wet–dry conditions, where expansive products such as ettringite accelerate cracking and shorten the service life of structures. Acidic environments, such as acid rain and industrial wastewater, also pose serious threats by chemically eroding concrete surfaces, reducing their compressive strength and structural integrity. To mitigate these effects, strategies include lowering the water-to-cement ratio (W/C), using sulfate-resistant cement, and incorporating pozzolanic materials such as rice husk ash, silica fume (SF), and other supplementary cementitious materials to refine pore structure and enhance durability.

In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising avenue for enhancing the properties of building materials, including concrete. Nanotechnology typically employs nanosized particles, known as nanomaterials, which offer unique properties and capabilities. Nanosilica (NS) has gained popularity as a material for improving concrete properties due to its small particle size and high fineness in comparison to conventional cement powder, enabling effective pozzolanic reactions. However, most earlier studies that incorporated nanoparticles into cementitious materials primarily focused on NS, which boasts a significantly larger specific surface area and reactivity than condensed SF, making it potentially more effective. , NS plays a critical role in impacting the microstructure of concrete by facilitating the release of calcium hydroxide (CH) during cement hydration, leading to the growth of more hydrated products and overall microstructural improvement. −

In the existing literature, it has been well established that incorporating nanosilica (NS) into mortar or concrete, even at small dosages, can significantly improve the mechanical properties of cementitious materials. Studies by Senff et al. and Zhidan et al. demonstrated that NS enhances both short-term and long-term strength. However, increasing NS content generally decreases flowability and setting times, as reported by Sun et al. and Ltifi et al. The water-to-binder (W/B) ratio plays a critical role in these effects. For example, Xiao et al. showed that the benefits of NS on strength and durability are more pronounced at higher W/B ratios. Previous studies confirmed that strength development depends on NS dosage, W/B ratio, and curing time, with compressive strength increases of about 20 and 12% observed at W/B ratios of 0.40 and 0.48, respectively. Senff et al. and Zapata et al. found optimal dosages of 3–3.5% NS with particle sizes of 10–25 nm, while Arega et al. and Torabian et al. reported that NS improved rheology and early strength, particularly at higher W/B ratios. Conversely, Jo et al. demonstrated that larger NS particles (40 nm) at up to 12% significantly enhanced compressive strength. Beyond mechanical improvements, NS enhances chemical durability.Babaluabalu and Sunil and later studies , confirmed that NS mitigates expansion and weight loss caused by sulfate attack, while Chen et al. reported reduced deterioration under acid exposure.

Various NS particle sizes and other nanomaterials have been studied to optimize performance. NS is the most widely used due to its relatively lower cost compared to other nanomaterials, though it remains more expensive than traditional pozzolanic additives. Research has shown that high NS dosages can significantly increase strength, , while NS-modified mortars exhibit superior acid resistance , and improved impact resistance and surface durability, making them suitable for applications such as road pavements. However, most previous studies have focused on single or dual particle sizes at fixed W/B ratios, limiting the understanding of combined effects. For instance, Huang et al. reported that NS refined pore structure and reduced pore connectivity, enhancing sulfate resistance, with coarser NS (50 nm) performing better than finer NS (10 nm). Meng et al. found that 50 nm NS yielded higher compressive strength, while 15 nm NS produced a denser microstructure. Similarly, Xu et al. observed that smaller NS (20 nm) improved mechanical properties and water absorption of geopolymer concrete, whereas larger NS (100 nm) offered better resistance to sulfate attack. In oil-well cement pastes, Mtaki et al. showed that 40 nm NS achieved higher early compressive strength and pozzolanic activity than 10 and 20 nm NS due to better dispersion. Moreover, Yuan et al. demonstrated that NS particle size influenced epoxy/silica composite coatings, affecting adhesion, abrasion, and corrosion resistance. Collectively, these findings highlight the complex interplay between NS dosage, particle size, and W/B ratio, emphasizing the need for systematic studies that address their combined effects under multiple aggressive environments.

However, most of these investigations have been limited to either short-term performance or isolated durability scenarios. The present study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the combined effects of different NS particle sizes and W/B ratios on mortar performance. The three NS particle sizes (12, 20, and 40 nm) were selected to represent commercially available grades and to investigate the balance between surface reactivity and particle dispersion. Similarly, the W/B ratios of 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85 were chosen to simulate field-relevant mortar mixes commonly used in wastewater systems and sulfate-rich environments. This study evaluates how these factors influence compressive strength, microstructure, and long-term durability under multiple aggressive environments, including sulfuric acid, hydrochloric acid, sodium sulfate, and magnesium sulfate. With exposure periods of up to 360 days, this integrated approach provides practical insights for designing cement mortars capable of withstanding chemically aggressive conditions while maintaining a balance between reactivity and workability

Materials and Experimental Procedure

Materials

Ordinary Portland cement of Type I (CT1) and Type V (CT5) was used as the primary cementitious material for experimentation. This investigation utilized three NS (AEROSIL, Evonik, Germany) particles with average diameters of 12, 20, and 40 nm and a silica purity of 99.8%. Table presents the properties of the CT1, CT5, SF, and NS particles. A polycarboxylate ether polymer-based SP (ADVA Cast 207, Grace Construction, Thailand) was employed to aid the dispersion of nanoparticles. Natural river sand with a specific gravity of 2.50 g/cm3 and accessible in the area was graded according to ASTM C778. The concentrations of sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) and magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) were both 10 molar, whereas the concentrations of hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4). Acid solutions were prepared by diluting commercial 37 wt % HCl and 98 wt % H2SO4 to 1 M using distilled water at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). All acid handling was conducted in a chemical fume hood with appropriate personal protective equipment (acid-resistant gloves, safety goggles, and lab coat). Dilution was carried out by slowly adding acid to water under constant stirring to prevent exothermic splashing. Waste acid solutions were neutralized with NaOH prior to disposal, following laboratory safety protocols.

Mix Proportions

The preparation of the cement mortar mixture samples was divided into two parts. In Part 1, samples of mortar containing NS and SF were prepared using a SP to control the flow table according to ASTM C1437 at W/B ratios of 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85. The SP used was a polycarboxylate ether-based type. Although the W/B ratios (0.65–0.85) were high, the presence of nanosilica or silica fume reduced workability due to high fineness. The SP dosage was adjusted slightly between 0.8–1.2% by binder weight to maintain a flow value of 110% ± 5%, as per ASTM C1437. No compatibility test with NS was conducted, but no visual signs of agglomeration or segregation were observed during mixing. The compressive strength and microstructure of cement mortar were tested. The composition of cement mortars in Part 1. Part 2 involved the preparation of the NS mortar samples by adjusting the W/B ratio without adding a SP and controlling the spreading flow to adhere to the required ASTM C1362 standard. Compressive strength and resistance were tested in various sulfate and acid solutions. The mix proportions used in Part 1 and Part 2 are summarized in Table a,b, respectively. These include different NS particle sizes and W/B ratios. A cement-to-sand ratio of 1:2.75 by weight was used for all mixes, following ASTM C109 standards. The preparations of cement mortar components were mixed following previous work by Jo. Nanosilica powders were incorporated into the mixing water prior to mortar preparation. Dispersion was carried out using a high-shear mixer operating at 2000 rpm for 5 min. No ultrasonication or chemical dispersants were employed in this study, in order to reflect typical field mixing practice. While this approach achieved adequate macroscopic dispersion, it is acknowledged that some degree of agglomeration may occur, particularly for smaller particle sizes.

Although the selected W/B ratios (0.65, 0.75, and 0.85) exceed the ASTM C109 recommendation of 0.485 for standard mortar mixes, they were intentionally chosen to simulate real-world field conditions in which water content may be higher due to lack of quality control or specific workability demands. This also enables evaluation of nanosilica performance under high-porosity conditions where its pozzolanic and filler effects may be more evident. In Part 2, the W/B ratio was extended beyond 0.85 (up to 1.21) to compensate for the absence of SP and maintain workability. This allowed investigation into the NS performance under less controlled conditions, relevant for field scenarios.

Test Procedure

Compressive Strength of Cement Mortar

The compressive strength test on the cement mortar was also divided into two parts. In Part 1, different diameters of NS particles (12, 20, 40, and 100 nm) were applied with cement replacement rates of 3, 6, 9, and 12% by weight of SiO2 nanoparticles. The W/B ratios for all mixtures were set at 0.65, 0.75, and 0.85. The compressive strength of the cement mortar samples was tested at 1, 7, and 28 days, as shown in Table a. In Part 2, the compressive strength of NS concrete at particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm with cement replacements of 3, 6, 9, and 12% was tested at 7, 28, 90, 180, 270, and 360 days and compared to Portland cement types I and V, as indicated in Table b. For each mixture, test specimens with a size of 5 × 5 × 5 cm were prepared following ASTM C109. Each compressive strength result represents the average of three specimens. Microstructure of Cement mortar

For the microstructure, only cement mortar with 9% NS and W/B ratios between 0.65 and 0.85 was employed. The cement mortar was crushed to a size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 cm, extensively washed with acetone to prevent hydration, and vacuum-dried at room temperature. At the age of 7 days, the samples were sent to laboratories for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). SEM imaging was performed using [NanoSEM 450, Nova] at an accelerating voltage of 10–15 kV with SE detector. Samples were dried, fractured, and sputter-coated with Au/Pd (∼5 nm). Representative magnifications and working conditions are reported in each figure caption. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out on a [D8 Discover, Bruker] using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Patterns were recorded in the 2θ range 3–70° with a step size of 0.02° and counting time 1 s/step. Powder samples were obtained by vacuum-drying fractured fragments, grinding to a fine powder, and mounting on low-background holders. Phase identification used the PDF-2 and -4 database; semiquantitative phase analysis followed Rietveld refinement where applicable. Using SEM, the hydration products of the specimens were examined. XRD can determine the number of hydration products by measuring their reaction frequencies. Dispersion was only assessed visually and by SEM of fractured samples, which showed no severe agglomeration at the macroscale. However, no DLS/zeta potential was conducted. Thus, the observed difference in performance among NS sizes may partly reflect dispersion quality.

Expansion Test of Cement Mortar

Samples of the NS mix measuring 2.5 × 2.5 × 28.5 cm were produced. The initial length of the cement mortar was measured quickly with an expansion gauge per ASTM C490. The cement mortar samples were divided into two groups: Group 1 was immersed in sodium sulfate, and Group 2 was immersed in magnesium sulfate. The length change of the cement mortar was then measured every 14 days until the test sample reached an age of 180 days. The change in length was determined and recorded. For the acid and sulfate resistance tests, mortar specimens were continuously immersed in the respective solutions for 180 days. Mass and dimensional changes were recorded at 14-day intervals. Before each weighing, specimens were removed from the solution, rinsed with distilled water, surface-dried with a cloth, and oven-dried at 105 ± 5 °C until constant mass. After measurement, specimens were reimmersed in freshly prepared solution. Test solutions were renewed every 14 days to maintain constant concentration, and all exposures were carried out at laboratory ambient temperature (25 ± 2 °C).

Weight Loss in Cement Mortar

Samples of cement mortar containing NS measuring 5 × 5 × 5 cm3 were prepared similarly to the compressive strength samples. The cement mortar samples were separated into two groups: those immersed in hydrochloric acid and those immersed in sulfuric acid. After being immersed in hydrochloric and sulfuric acid once every 14 days for 180 days, the weight loss was determined. The weight loss value was recorded and compared to the weight before immersing in the acid solution, and the weight loss percentage was calculated by performing a weight loss test per ASTM C267.

Results and Discussion

Compressive Strength

Effect of NS Particle Size and W/B Ratios (0.65–0.85) on Compressive Strength

The compressive strength of the cement mortar control sample (OPC), mortar with SF, and mortar with NS at ages 1, 7, and 28 days can be seen in Figure . Due to the appropriate pozzolanic activity and W/B ratio, a W/B ratio of 0.65 resulted in greater compressive strength after 1 day for the mortar than W/B ratios of 0.75 and 0.85 (Figure a). In addition, the result was unclear at the age of 1 day for the mortar because hydration reactivity decreases as the mortar ages. Figure b demonstrates the compressive strength of the mortar at 7 days. According to the study results, the minimum water curing time should be at least 7 days. , For all W/B ratios, the compressive strength of the 1 day mortar is more than the hydration reactivity, pozzolanic activity, and W/B ratio, according to the results. By increasing the W/B ratio from 0.65 to 0.85, the capillary apertures become larger, and the pozzolanic reaction of cement mortar decreases, resulting in reduced compressive strength. Figure c demonstrates the compressive strength of mortar after 28 days. Between W/B ratios of 0.65 and 0.75, the compressive strength varied considerably, whereas W/B ratios of 0.75 and 0.85 produced comparable compressive strengths. Appa and Bhanja reported similar observations in cement mortar and concrete with SF, as the compressive strength decreases gradually as the W/B ratio increases. The highest increase in compressive strength among all W/B ratios was found in cement mortar with an NS particle size of 40 nm after 28 days. It could be inferred that higher W/B ratios (0.75 and 0.85) resulted in an increase in porosity, − which is also supported by the SEM microstructure results. However, the compressive strength of cement mortar depends on the size of the embedded NS particles. The pozzolanic reaction and packaging ability (filling effect) of cement mortar with the optimal NS particle size (40 nm) were enhanced. The obtained results support the findings of Chaichana et al. It was discovered that NS particles as small as 10 and 15 nm can exhibit agglomeration and weak dispersion behaviors.

Figures and depict the variation in compressive strength of mortar with NS particle sizes of 12, 20, 40, and 100 nm at the mortar ages of 7 and 28 days, respectively, with various NS contents (3, 6, 9, and 12%) and W/B ratios (0.65, 0.75, and 0.85). For all NS particle sizes and W/B ratios at ages 7 and 28 days, a replacement NS content of 9% produced the greatest compressive strength at all ages. At the W/B ratio of 0.65, the compressive strength of cement mortar with SF and NS replacement content of 9% was significantly different from the OPC and the mortar with NS contents of 3, 6, and 12%, whereas, at the W/B ratios of 0.75 and 0.85, the compressive strength was slightly different. Otherwise, quantities greater than the optimal W/B ratio of 0.65 can decrease compressive strength rather than increase it. When greater W/B ratios are used, the reaction of cement and cementitious material in the cement mortar leads to a greater quantity of remaining water, creating a greater number of capillary pores, as confirmed by the SEM microstructure results. When the amount of water increases, the cement mortar becomes more porous, and the compressive strength decreases.

However, these findings were comparable to Jo et al.’s. Using an NS particle size of 40 nm and replacement contents of 3, 6, 9, and 12% by weight of cement at various W/B ratios (0.32, 0.35, and 0.48) and SP concentrations, they found that the addition of 12% NS provided the highest compressive strength at all W/B ratios. In another study by Senff et al., which used an NS particle size of 10 nm and replacement contents of 0, 3.5, and 7% by weight of cement at W/B ratios of 0.35, 0.47, and 0.59 and a fixed dosage of SP, the cement mortar containing NS 3.5% had the highest compressive strength among all W/B ratios. Zapata et al. investigated the compressive strength of mortar modified with an NS particle size of 25 nm at replacement contents of 1 and 3% by weight of cement with W/B ratios of 0.35 and 0.40. At both W/B ratios, the cement mortar with an NS replacement of 3% was found to be optimal. According to the research, the W/B ratio does not affect the replacement content of SF and NS. Adding a small amount of nanosilica (0–1%) may increase the stability and mechanical properties of cement mortar due to the NS content. In particular, the studies by Jo et al., Senff et al., and Zapata et al. suggest that the optimal replacement content of the NS mix is between 9 and 12%, 0 and 3.5%, and 1 and 3%, respectively. It should be noted that although average values and standard deviations are reported, no statistical significance tests such as ANOVA were conducted. The observed differences in compressive strength trends should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Future work will address this limitation by including a more robust statistical analysis.

Effect of NS Particle Size on Sulfate Resistance

Cement Mortar Aged in Tap Water

The study considered the compressive strength of the water-cured mortar of the control cement mortar using CT1 and CT5, as well as the compressive strength of the cement mortar containing NS with particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm, partially replacing CT1 at a W/B ratio of 9%. Figure a displays the relationship between compressive strength and curing time in water at 7, 28, 90, 180, 270, and 360 days for mortar with NS particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm at replacement contents of 9% by weight of cement. At 7, 28, and 90 days, the results indicated that the compressive strength of controlled cement mortar Type I (CT1) tended to increase. The mortar compressive strength was 18.1, 22.70, and 34.7 MPa at 180, 270, and 360 days, respectively. The compressive strength of the cement mortar appeared to stabilize or slightly increase, with values of 35.0, 35.1, and 35.8 MPa, respectively. At 7 days, the compressive strength of cement mortar Type V (CT5) was determined to be 15.6 MPa, a low compressive strength. CT5 developed more rapidly than CT1 at 28 days of age, and its compressive strength increased quickly; CT5 had a greater compressive strength than CT1, with values of 23.2, 35.5, 36.2, 36.7, and 37.7 MPa at ages 28, 90, 180, 270, and 360 days, respectively, which are 2 to 5% greater than those of CT1. At 7 days, the compressive strength of CT5 was 14% lower than that of CT1, even though CT5 is larger than CT1 at the same W/B ratio. The compressive strength of CT5 at 7 days was lower than that of CT1, but with increasing age, CT5 compressive strength tended to increase and become greater than CT1. This may be due to the tricalcium silicate (C3S) content of CT5; C3S is the primary component that gives concrete its compressive strength at an early age, and CT5 has a lower C3S content than CT1, resulting in slower early strength development. Dicalcium silicate (C2S), a compound that increases the long-term strength of PC and is present in greater quantities in CT5 than CT1, results in the development of a greater compressive strength than that of CT1 at an early age of 28 days, consistent with the study results of Nader et al.

Compressive strength of cement mortar PC type 1 and 5 and cement mortar with NS particle sizes 12, 20, and 40 nm. (a) Water, (b) magnesium sulfate, and (c) sodium sulfate.

Regarding the effect of NS cement mortar with particle sizes of 12 nm (NS12), 20 nm (NS20), and 40 nm (NS40), the study revealed that the compressive strength of the cement mortar increased with age for all NS particle sizes. The cement mortar was mixed with NS at 7, 28, 90, 180, 270, 270, and 360 days of curing. The compressive strengths of NS12 were 23.2, 26.6, 37.1, 37.3, 37.4, and 37.7 MPa, respectively. NS40 mortar had compressive strengths of 28.6, 32.3, 42.4, 42.5, 42.6, and 43.4 MPa, respectively. NS20 mortar had compressive strengths of 24.8, 27.6, 38.2, 38.3, 38.3, and 39.0 MPa, respectively. Different NS particle diameters (12, 20, and 40 nm) were shown to affect the pozzolanic reaction. NS with small particles, 12 and 20 nm, caused the cement mortar to have a less homogeneous texture and a gap to form between the cement paste. At the optimal NS particle size of 40 nm, the cement paste exhibited improved homogeneity, and there were no gaps between the cement pastes. When the NS particles are appropriately sized, they can fit into the gap between the cement paste, whereas a small particle size prevents the NS from fitting. Instead of filling the spaces between cement pastes, NS can be applied around the cement particles in a cement mortar. The increase in compressive strength is only due to the pozzolanic reaction. This is caused by the fact that the NS coating surrounding the cement particles results in less hydration than the pozzolanic reaction. When using NS with a particle size of 40 nm, it can fit between the cement paste. This results in excellent interparticle compaction, which makes the cement mortar denser and more homogeneous. Consequently, filling ability and a pozzolanic reaction are necessary to increase compressive strength. Consistent with the findings of Haruehansapong et al., NS with a particle size of 40 nm exhibited greater compressive strength than those of sizes 12 and 20 nm.

Cement Mortar Aged in Sulfate Solution

Figure b illustrates the relationship between compressive strength and soaking time in a sodium sulfate solution for NS cement mortar with 12, 20, and 40 nm particle sizes. The 9% substitution ratio demonstrated that the compressive strength of cement mortar mixed with NS of all three sizes decreased with immersion time in the sodium sulfate solution. At 360 days, the compressive strengths of NS12, NS20, and NS40 cement mortars were 9, 14, and 34.5 MPa, respectively. The compressive strengths of cement mortar containing NS with particle sizes of 12 and 20 nm were significantly lower. Thus, the sodium sulfate solution has the least corrosive effect on the compressive strength of the NS40 sample. Compared to CT1, cement mortar containing NS with particle sizes of 12 and 20 nm exhibited a significantly reduced compressive strength. A mixture containing NS with particle sizes between 12 and 20 nm has a very high water demand compared to those at 40 nm. Due to the quick pozzolanic reaction, NS increases the compressive strength of cement mortar. However, the amount of water may have a larger effect. Therefore, the overall compressive strength decreases more than CT1, and the decrease in compressive strength decreases the cement mortar’s resistance to sulfate corrosion. The compressive strength of NS cement mortar with a particle size of 40 nm was suitable, so the overall compressive strength of this NS cement mortar was 1.17 times greater than that of CT1 and 1.11 times that of CT5.

Figure c illustrates the relationship between compressive strength and immersion time in magnesium sulfate for CT1 and CT5 and NS cement mortar with particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm. The test results indicated that the compressive strength of cement mortar immersed in magnesium sulfate solution tended to produce consistent results in the same direction as that immersed in sodium sulfate solution. On the other hand, the decrease in compressive strength resulting from immersion in magnesium sulfate solution was more significant than that resulting from immersion in sodium sulfate solution for the same immersion duration. Due to the increased synthesis of ettringite and gypsum in the destructive mechanism of magnesium sulfate solution, magnesium sulfate corrosion occurs more often. The samples soaked in sodium sulfate solution had higher compressive strength than those soaked in magnesium sulfate solution.

Microstructure

SEM Analysis

To investigate the effect of W/B (0.65 and 0.85) on the microstructure of 7-day mortar, samples were taken from the center of the mortar specimens, with a focus on the hydrate of the calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and the calcium hydroxide (Ca­(OH)2) crystal. Figures – depict SEM micrographs of CT1, SF, NS12, NS20, and NS40, respectively, at a 0.85 W/B ratio. For the SEM analysis for the cement mortar with a W/B ratio of 0.65, the study refers to the research of Haruehansapong et al. These results indicate that the SF (Figure ) had a similar microstructure to that of the CT1 (Figure ), in which the C–S–H gel existed in isolation surrounded by and connected to many ettringite needles. Within the micropores, the amounts of calcium aluminum trisulfate hydrate (ettringite) and Ca­(OH)2 crystal were reduced. The silica particulates not only act as a chemical filler to enhance the microstructure of the cement mortar but also as a catalyst for the pozzolanic reaction.

Figures – depict SEM micrographs of the NS mix particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm and W/B ratios of 0.85, respectively. From Haruehansapong et al.’s research at W/B ratios of 0.65, the results indicate that adding NS made the hydrates denser, finer, and more homogeneous than the CT1 and cement mortar with SF. Due to their high activity, the NS particles located in the cement mortar as a nucleus further promote and accelerate cement hydration. A layer of C–S–H also covered the Ca­(OH)2 surface layer, and ettringite was visible within the fractures, whereas the number of Ca­(OH)2 crystals was reduced, as confirmed by the XRD test results. When examining the microstructure details of the NS mix at a W/B ratio of 0.85, the occurrence of C–S–H gel was slightly reduced, and a greater number of pores with larger dimensions were observed in the paste structure. This may result in a decrease in the compressive strength of the specimen. In other words, a lower W/B ratio increased the compressive strength of the NS mix particles of all sizes (12, 20, and 40 nm) as the microstructure of hydrate products became denser and more compact, pore volumes decreased, and pore sizes decreased. In addition, the compressive strengths of cement mortar in the test groups were superior to those in the groups with a higher W/B ratio. These SEM test results have been confirmed by compressive strength tests and describe all previously obtained behaviors. The more homogeneous microstructure observed in NS40 samples may partly reflect better dispersion due to particle size. This effect was not quantitatively validated (e.g., zeta potential or rheology), which is noted as a limitation.

Although the SEM images suggest greater agglomeration for smaller nanosilica particles (especially 12 and 20 nm), the dispersion method used in this study was limited to mechanical stirring without the use of sonication or surfactants. This may have affected the uniformity of NS distribution and thus influenced strength and microstructural development. The observed differences among particle sizes may therefore partially reflect dispersion quality rather than particle size effects alone. Future studies should consider enhanced dispersion techniques such as ultrasonic mixing, zeta potential analysis, or the use of dispersing agents to ensure reproducibility and isolate true particle size effects.

Indeed, the pozzolanic reaction of NS was considerably stronger than that of SF. The intensity of ettringite crystals, Ca­(OH)2, and C–S–H peaks decreased as the W/B ratio increased. This is because the pozzolanic reaction is decreased by increasing the quantity of water to which the various compounds in the cement reaction were reduced. The X-ray scattering pattern is determined by the sample’s internal structure. The variations in the structure of the specimen, such as those caused by an increased water-to-binder ratios, have the potential to change the pattern of diffraction. These changes may result in a decreased intensity of the observed peak. Consequently, reduced hydration products were produced, resulting in a decrease in the strength of the cement mortar, as confirmed by the SEM test results.

Expansion Test of Cement Mortar in Sulfate Solutions

Figure a displays the expansion and immersion time in sodium sulfate solution at 10 molar concentrations of CT1, CT5, NS12, NS20, and NS40. Comparing all three particle sizes of NS at 180 days, the percentage expansion of CT1 and CT5 cement mortars was 0.7618 and 0.2961, respectively. In addition, the expansion percentage of NS40 was 0.2471, which was 3.08 times less than CT1 and 1.19 times less than CT5. Consequently, replacing NS with high resolution and a suitable number of small particles enables the silica nanoparticles to penetrate well into the gaps of the cement mortar. This reduces the quantity of tricalcium aluminate (C3A) and gypsum that cause expansion, and NS with high SiO2 content directly affects the pozzolanic reaction rate, thereby enhancing the overall effect. Therefore, the expansion of the cement mortar at 180 days was less. Still, when the particle size varied to 12 (NS12) and 20 nm (NS20), the expansion percentages were 1.2349 and 0.9142, respectively, 1.62 and 1.20 times higher than CT1 and 4.17 and 3.09 times higher than CT5. The 12 and 20 nm particle sizes caused more significant expansion than the 40 nm particle sizes, both in a surface pozzolanic reaction instead of filling in the gaps of the cement mortar because NS12 and NS20 have very small particle sizes. On the other hand, the NS could be coated around the cement particles, causing instability, agglomeration, and a lack of particle compaction. Therefore, the NS particle size influences the expansion of the cement mortar.

Expansion and immersion time in (a) sodium sulfate and (b) magnesium sulfate of cement mortar with PC type 1 and 5 and cement mortar with NS particle sizes 12, 20, and 40 nm.

Figure b depicts the relationship between expansion and immersion time in magnesium sulfate for control mortar made from CT1, CT5, NS12, NS20, and NS40. It was found that the expansion and impact on the cement mortar caused by the magnesium sulfate solution occurred in the same direction as in the sodium sulfate solution immersion. The samples immersed in magnesium sulfate solution expanded less than those immersed in sodium sulfate solution. It should also be noted that the water-to-binder (W/B) ratios varied among mixes (0.65–1.21), which can strongly affect porosity and transport properties. Therefore, part of the durability performance observed (e.g., reduced expansion and weight loss) may not only arise from nanosilica particle size but also from the influence of higher or lower W/B ratios. The present study intentionally included high W/B ratios to simulate field conditions, but this factor may partly confound the durability trends. Future investigations should normalize durability results with transport parameters (e.g., sorptivity, MIP) or compare mixes at equal W/B ratios to isolate the pure effect of nanosilica particle size.

Weight Loss of Cement Mortar in Acid Solutions

Figure a illustrates the relationship between the weight loss percentage and the soaking time in a hydrochloric acid solution for CT1, CT5, NS12, NS20, and NS40. At 180 days, NS40 demonstrated less weight loss than CT1 and CT5. Due to the amount of calcium hydroxide used in the pozzolanic reaction that produced calcium silicate hydrate, the lowest weight loss was 8.78%, 1.78 and 1.52 times less than CT1 and CT5, respectively. As a result, the cement mortar material is denser and bonds to aggregates more effectively. The mortar alkalinity is decreased, which substantially reduces the corrosion caused by hydrochloric acid. Meanwhile, NS12 and NS20 had weight losses of 27.12 and 22.61%, respectively. Compared to CT1, these weight losses were 1.54 and 1.28 times greater, respectively. Due to the small particle size of NS, a higher W/B ratio proportional to resolution was required. The rate of weight loss decreases as the ratio of W/B decreases due to reduced hydration and pozzolanic reactions. Adding water lowers the yield of both reactions, but it also produces a void through which acid can more easily enter the reaction. As a result, the cement mortar has no adhesion properties, and the compressive strength effect also results in more significant weight loss. NS40 had the lowest percentage of weight loss compared to the CT1, CT5, NS12, and NS20 samples, which were denser. It was difficult for acid corrosion to penetrate, resulting in NS40 obtaining the greatest compressive strength. Previous research has indicated that NS has been utilized as a coating material. This research demonstrated that the size of the silica nanoparticles directly affected the coating material bonding strength and corrosion resistance. In particular, the NS40 can help reduce acid corrosion.

Weight loss and immersion time in (a) hydrochloric acid and (b) sulfuric acid of cement mortar with PC type 1 and 5 and cement mortar with NS particle sizes 12, 20, and 40 nm.

Figure b shows the weight loss test results for cement mortar mixed with NS immersed in a sulfuric acid solution. The effect was similar to immersion in a hydrochloric acid solution but with less corrosion. Because hydrochloric acid and sulfuric acid have different corrosive properties, hydrochloric acid reacts with calcium compounds in the cement mortar to form calcium chloride (CaCl2), which disintegrates in water and is effortlessly removed by washing. By contrast, sulfuric acid reacts with the calcium compounds in the cement mortar, forming calcium sulfate (CaSO4), which expands and cracks the concrete. It was found that NS40 had the least weight loss because the NS40 is appropriate for penetrating the cement mortar sample pores, while the NS12 and NS20 are too small. NS was coated around the cement particles as an alternative to filling the voids in cement mortar samples. Consequently, unstable adhesion and a lack of particle compaction made the cement mortar samples porous, allowing acids to enter the reaction faster. Therefore, the sample lost its adhesion properties, and the cement mortar weight loss percentage increased.

While nanosilica demonstrated superior performance in improving strength, microstructure, and durability compared to silica fume, its significantly higher cost may limit its widespread use in general concrete applications. Therefore, NS may be more suitable for specialized cases where long-term durability in aggressive environments is critical, such as wastewater treatment infrastructure or marine structures. Future cost–benefit analysis is recommended to evaluate the optimal NS dosage for practical applications.

Conclusions

The present study examined the effect of NS particle size and W/B ratio on the compressive strength, microstructure, and sulfate and acid resistance of cement mortar containing SF and NS. The following conclusions were drawn:1.It would be preferable to substitute NS, with average particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm, for cement mortar up to a maximum of 9%. However, the NS particle size affects the compressive strength of the cement mortar. The pozzolanic reaction and uniform particle dispersion of cement mortar with an NS particle size of 40 nm were better than those with NS particle sizes of 12 and 20 nm.2.For all W/B ratios (0.65, 0.75, and 0.85) and NS particle sizes (12, 20, and 40 nm), a replacement NS content of 9% provided the best compressive strength at all ages. The cement mortars with varying W/B ratios may suggest that the replacement content of SF and NS is unaffected by the difference in W/B ratio. In other words, a lower W/B ratio could increase the compressive strength of the NS mix particle sizes of 12, 20, and 40 nm more than a higher W/B ratio. This is because an improvement in the microstructure is better reflected in the compressive strengths of the cement mortar.3.NS particle size directly affects the development of compressive strength in cement mortar soaked in tap water and immersed in sulfate solution and acid solution. The NS40 had greater compressive strength than CT1, CT5, NS12, and NS20. This results from the combination of the pozzolanic reaction and the compaction of suitable particle size.4.NS can improve the interface structure more effectively than SF because its pozzolanic reaction was considerably more than that of SF. It may consume Ca­(OH)2 crystals, change their orientation, and lower the size and number of Ca­(OH)2 crystals at the interface. Adding more water, which reduced the various chemicals from the reaction in cement, decreased the pozzolanic reaction. As a result, fewer hydration products developed, which also caused the cement mortar strength to decrease. The results of the XRD pattern also support the results of the SEM.5.Expansion and weight loss of cement mortar decrease its resistance to sodium and magnesium sulfate and hydrochloric and sulfuric acid solutions. When immersed in a sulfate and acid solution for 180 days, it was discovered that NS 40 expanded less and showed less weight loss than CT1 and CT5. NS with smaller particle sizes of 12 and 20 nm increased the expansion and weight loss of the cement mortar.6.This study used 9% NS for durability evaluation based on compressive strength optimization. Further research should explore how different NS contents (e.g., 3%, 6%, 12%) influence long-term sulfate and acid resistance.7.While nanosilica particle size clearly influenced strength and durability, the varying W/B ratios across mixes may also contribute to the observed performance.
