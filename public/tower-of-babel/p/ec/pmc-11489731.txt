A Plasmodium late liver stage arresting GAP provides superior protection in mice
Authors: Akancha Mishra, Plabita Paul, Mrigank Srivastava, Satish Mishra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver-stage genetically attenuated malaria parasites (GAPs) are powerful immunogens that provide protection against sporozoite challenge. We previously generated two late liver-stage-arresting GAPs by deleting the stearoyl-CoA desaturase (Scd) or sporozoite conserved orthologous transcript 1 (Scot1) genes in Plasmodium berghei. Immunization with Scd or Scot1 GAP conferred complete protection against a sporozoite challenge. In a safety study, we observed rare breakthrough blood-stage infections in mice inoculated with high doses of sporozoites, indicating that both GAPs were incompletely attenuated. In this study, we generated a Scd/Scot1 GAP by dual gene deletion. This resulted in complete attenuation of the parasites in the liver and did not transition to blood-stage infection despite a high-dose sporozoite challenge. The Scd/Scot1 KO and WT GFP parasites were indistinguishable during blood, mosquito and early liver stage development. Moreover, Scd/Scot1 KO liver-stage schizonts exhibited an abnormal apicoplast biogenesis and nuclear division phenotype, failed to form hepatic merozoites, and exhibited late liver-stage arrest. Compared with early-arresting Speld KO immunization, late-stage liver-arresting Scd/Scot1 KO induces greater and broader CD8+ T-cell responses and elicits stage-transcending immunity that provides superior protection in C57BL/6 mice. These data prove that multiple gene deletions lead to complete attenuation of the parasite and support the development of late liver stage-arresting P. falciparum GAP.Subject terms: Live attenuated vaccines, Parasitic infection

Introduction

Despite worldwide efforts toward control and elimination, malaria remains a global disease burden, with 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths in 20221. The World Health Organization (WHO)-approved RTS,S vaccine2 might not be suitable for long-term usage. Developing highly effective and durable vaccines against malaria remains a key priority. The RTS,S vaccine is a hepatitis B virus-like particle consisting of a repeat and the C-terminal region of the P. falciparum CSP3. The protective effect of the RTS,S vaccine is short-lived, and its outcome depends on the transmission intensity in different endemic areas4. Efficacy studies revealed that RTS,S in adults in Gambia induced short-lived protection from infection5, but no significant protection was observed in Kenya6. R21/Matrix-M is the second malaria vaccine recommended by the WHO. R21/Matrix-M continued to meet the WHO malaria vaccine technology roadmap goal of a vaccine with at least 75% efficacy7. However, one of the major drawbacks of this approach is the lack of an efficient and durable immune response. A safe and effective malaria vaccine will likely be necessary to eradicate malaria.

Whole-sporozoite vaccines are ideal because they present a wide array of antigens compared to the single antigens used in subunit vaccines. Radiation-attenuated sporozoites (RASs) are metabolically active parasites that infect hepatocytes but fail to replicate DNA and arrest in the liver stages8,9. Immunization with the injectable formulation of the RAS vaccine PfSPZ has undergone many clinical trials in the past decade and provides 12-14 months of protection since the last immunization in malaria-naïve individuals10–14. Although RAS vaccines are highly protective from malaria, one drawback is that the parasites are arrested early in their development in the liver and lack late liver-stage antigens12. An alternative approach is to use genetically attenuated parasites (GAPs) as a whole-organism vaccine15,16. Targeted deletion of Plasmodium genes generates a homogeneous population of attenuated parasites with defined genetic identity, which may be designed to induce optimal protective immunity17. GAPs have an advantage over RASs because knockout parasites are clonal lines that do not rely on external technical variables like the radiation dosage18,19.

The first-generation GAPs are early liver-stage arresting, replication-deficient (EARD) GAPs generated by mutating the sporozoite-specific genes required to establish early liver-stage infection15,16. Various mutants have shown protective immune responses in rodent malaria models and humans. EARD GAPs, PbUIS3- 15, PbUIS4- 16, PbUIS3-/4- 20, Pbb9-/slarp- 21, Pfp52-/p36-/sap1- 22, were shown to confer sterile protection. Early liver-stage arrested parasites express a range of antigens but lack the antigens expressed during late liver-stage development. The late-arresting replication-competent (LARC) GAP vaccine also offers late-expressing antigens to induce superior protection in mice compared to RAS or EARD GAP immunizations19. LARC GAP induces larger CD8+ T-cell responses that provide superior protection. Additionally, the expression of blood-stage antigens during the late liver stage provides cross-stage immunity19.

The deletion of type II fatty acid synthesis (FASII) pathway genes resulted in late liver arrest in both P. yoelii and P. berghei parasites23–26. The attenuation level of the same gene differed between P. yoelii and P. berghei. FAS II mutants were completely attenuated in P. yoelii but showed occasional breakthrough infections in P. berghei. Two more well-studied mutants that lead to late liver-stage arrest include liver-specific protein 2 (LISP2) in P. berghei27–29 and PlasMei2 in P. yoelii30. Later, LISP2 KO was generated in P. yoelii. The infectivity of PlasMei2 and LISP2 KO parasites was evaluated at very high doses of sporozoites, and both showed incomplete attenuation of liver stage development31. For complete attenuation, P. yoelii PlasMei2 and LISP2 double-KO parasites, which are safe and induce preerythrocytic and cross-stage immunity, were generated31. PlasMei2 LARC GAP was generated in P. falciparum, and mutant parasites were completely attenuated in the liver and did not initiate blood-stage infection32. Recently, a LARC was generated by deleting the Mei2 and LINUP genes in P. yoelii and named LARC2. The PyLARC2 parasites did not form hepatic merozoites and failed to initiate blood-stage infection in mice. Immunization with PyLARC2 sporozoites elicited robust humoral and cellular immune responses and conferred sterile immunity against infection33. Next, they generated PfLARC2 parasite and produced cryopreserved PfSPZ-LARC2 that replicated robustly in liver-humanized mice but failed to transition to viable blood-stage infection33. These reports suggest that multiple gene deletions are important for creating a safe GAP.

Cellular and humoral adaptive immune responses are important for protection against malaria, and RAS and GAPs can induce both types of immunity34. Sera from immunized mice harbor sporozoite-neutralizing antibodies, recognize all stages of parasites and confer protection via passive transfer31,35–38. The humoral immune response mediated by antibodies contributes to protection at the initial stages of challenging infection, CD8+ T cells alone can confer sterilizing protection, and their depletion results in the loss of immunity39–41. Protection is mainly mediated by two memory subsets of CD8+ T cells, T effector and resident memory cells. Compared with CD8+ TEM cells, CD8+ TRM cells play a major role in the P. yoelii LARC GAP vaccine. Conversely, RAS elicits CD8+ TCM, but this protection is mainly attributed to TEM cells.

In this study, we tested the safety of two previously generated late liver-stage arresting Scd or Scot1 KO parasites42,43 and observed a rare breakthrough infection. To overcome this breakthrough infection, we generated a mutant with dual gene deletion (Scd/Scot1 KO) that arrests late during liver stage development and completely attenuates the parasite. Immunization of mice with P. berghei Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites elicited a protective CD8+ T-cell-mediated immune response, conferring long-term and superior protection against sporozoite challenge.

Results

Scd and Scot1 KO parasites exhibit rare breakthrough infections at very high doses of sporozoites

Stearoyl-CoA Δ9-desaturase (Scd) is an ER-resident protein that catalyzes the synthesis of oleic acid by desaturation of stearic acid44. Sporozoite conserved orthologous transcript 1 (Scot1) is expressed in sporozoites and liver stages43. We have previously shown that the deletion of Scd or Scot1 in P. berghei leads to late liver stage arrest and that KO parasites fail to initiate blood-stage infection up to a 50,000 sporozoite dose42,43. Immunization with Scd or Scot1 KO sporozoites conferred complete protection against a malaria challenge by sporozoites42,43. To assess the safety of the Scd or Scot1 GAP vaccine, we inoculated mice with higher doses of sporozoites (100,000 or 200,000) and observed a rare breakthrough infection (Table 1).

Scd/Scot1 KO parasites exhibit normal development during the blood and mosquito stages

To overcome the rare breakthrough infection in mice inoculated with higher doses of Scd or Scot1 KO sporozoites, we generated a mutant with dual gene deletion (Scd/Scot1 KO). We first generated a Scd KO marker free (MF parasite line by transfecting the EcoRV/NotI-digested Scd targeting cassette into Scd KO schizonts (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Successful generation of the marker-free Scd-KO line was confirmed by diagnostic PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Next, the Scd MF schizonts were transfected with the Scot1 targeting cassette. Deletion of the gene and correct integration were confirmed by GFP expression and diagnostic PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1B and C). We then compared the asexual blood stage propagation of Scd/Scot1 KO and WT GFP parasites. Groups of Swiss albino mice were intravenously injected with iRBCs, and growth was monitored by observing Giemsa-stained blood smears. We found that both Scd/Scot1 KO and WT GFP parasites grew at a similar rate (Supplementary Fig. S1D), suggesting that dual gene deletion did not affect blood stage development. Next, the parasites were transmitted to female Anopheles mosquitoes, and their development was observed. We found that the midgut and salivary glands of Scd/Scot1 KO parasites were infected comparably to those of WT GFP parasites (Supplementary Fig. S2A-F). These results suggest that dual gene deletion does not affect parasite development in the blood or mosquito stage.

Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites fail to initiate blood-stage infection in mice

To evaluate the infectivity of the Scd/Scot1 KO parasites, sporozoites were inoculated intravenously or through mosquito bites in C57BL/6 mice, and the appearance of the parasites in the blood was monitored through Giemsa-stained blood smears. The WT GFP parasites established blood-stage infection on day three post infection; however, all the mice inoculated with 50,000 Scd/Scot1 KO parasites failed to initiate blood-stage infection (Table 1). Even mice inoculated with 200,000 sporozoites did not become patent during the observation period of 20 days (Table 1). To quantify the liver-stage parasite burden, another two groups of C57BL/6 mice were inoculated with 5,000 WT GFP or Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites, and the livers were harvested at 36 hpi. Real-time PCR analysis of Pb18s rRNA transcripts indicated that the parasite burden was comparable in both groups (Fig. 1A). These data indicate that similar to individual Scd or Scot1 mutants, Scd/Scot1 KO parasites grow normally during the early to mid-liver stages.

Scd/Scot1 KO parasites exhibit defects in organelle biogenesis and hepatic merozoite formation

To further assess the stage-specific defects, parasites were visualized by indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA). Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites invaded hepatocytes normally (Supplementary Fig. S3). Further analysis of liver stage development revealed normal growth with comparable EEF numbers and areas as those of WT GFP (Fig. 1B–D). At the late time point (64 hpi), the WT GFP parasites matured into hepatic merozoites. In contrast, the Scd/Scot1 KO parasites failed to mature into hepatic merozoites and had reduced nuclear counts (Fig. 2A, B). To further estimate the formation of any merozoites, infected HepG2 cultures were observed for the formation of detached cells (known as merosomes in vivo) at 64 hpi. We found that WT-GFP-infected cells released detached cells into the culture supernatant, whereas this release was not detected in the Scd/Scot1 KO-infected cultures (Fig. 2C). To assess the infectivity of the merozoites, Swiss mice were intravenously inoculated with detached cells (10 detached cells/mouse). Since detached cells were absent in the Scd/Scot1 KO-infected cultures, the corresponding amount of culture supernatant was injected into the mice, and the appearance of the parasite in the blood was monitored through Giemsa-stained blood smears. All the WT GFP-detached cell-infected mice became patent on day 4 post infection, whereas all the mice inoculated with Scd/Scot1 KO-infected culture supernatant remained negative for blood-stage parasites until the observation period (Table 2). Next, we analyzed apicoplast development in Scd/Scot1 KO parasites because Scd KO parasites exhibit defects in apicoplast biogenesis42. We found fully differentiated apicoplasts in WT GFP, whereas they were impaired in Scd/Scot1 KO parasites (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that dual gene deletion completely attenuates the parasite during the late liver stage development.

Vaccination with Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites provides long-lasting protection

To study preerythrocytic protection, C57BL/6 mice were primed with 50,000 Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites, and the mock-immunized group received uninfected salivary gland debris (SGD). The mice were again divided into two groups: the first group received a single booster of 25,000 sporozoites, and the second group received two booster doses of 25,000 sporozoites at an interval of two weeks (Fig. 3A, D). The immunized mice and the control group were then challenged with 5,000 WT sporozoites ten days after the last immunization, which was day 24 (twice immunized) and day 38 (thrice immunized), respectively. All the SGD-immunized and uninfected control mice became patent on day 3 post challenge, whereas all the Scd/Scot1 KO-immunized mice were protected (Fig. 3B, E). All the protected immunized mice were rechallenged on day 54 (twice immunized) and day 68 (thrice immunized) with 5000 WT sporozoites along with the uninfected control group. We found that only 50% of immunized mice were protected in the twice immunized group (Fig. 3C), whereas all mice in the thrice-immunized group were protected from rechallenge (Fig. 3F). The protection of the thrice immunized mice was further examined after six months of immunization against infectious sporozoites. We found that 80 and 60% of the mice were protected when challenged on days 128 and 218, respectively (Fig. 3G, H). The mice that became patent showed a 4–5-day delay in establishing the infection. The data demonstrate that Scd/Scot1 KO GAP affords complete preerythrocytic stage protection and that a minimum of three immunization doses are required to elicit long-lasting protection.

CD8+ effector T-cell function is enhanced in Scd/Scot1 GAP-immunized mice

Immunization with a late liver stage-arresting GAP provides enhanced protection in mice, which is thought to be mediated by both cellular and humoral immune responses. Among the cell-mediated immune responses, CD8+ T cells play the most crucial role in the clearance of parasites19,45. Studies have shown the increased patrolling of these cells to liver sinusoids and revealed their effector role46. These cells also confer protection against adoptive transfers in naïve mice31,46. To further examine the protective effect in Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoite-immunized mice, we examined the course of T-cell responses in the immunized mice. The livers of both twice- and thrice-immunized mice were harvested 30 days after the last immunization on days 54 and 68, respectively. Purified nonparenchymal cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 4A). The gating strategy is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S4. The mice immunized thrice showed a 4-fold increase in the total lymphocyte count compared to a 2-fold increase in the twice immunized group (Fig. 4B). Analysis of T lymphocytes and their subsets, revealed a significant increase in CD8+ and CD4+ T lymphocytes in the thrice immunized groups compared to those in the SGD and twice immunized groups (Fig. 4C–E). The long-term protection induced by GAPs to subsequent challenges by infectious sporozoites is endured by antigen-experienced CD8+ memory T cells, which reside in the liver and spleen and play a critical role in parasite clearance31,45,47. Considering this, we quantified memory CD8+ T-cell subsets in immunized mice and detected significantly greater numbers of CD8+ CD44hi effector memory T cells (TEM) (CD62Llo KLRG1+) and CD8+ CD44hi tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) (CD62Llo CXCR6+) in thrice-immunized mice than in SGD-immunized and double-immunized mice (Fig. 4F, G, H). These results demonstrated the importance of CD8+ T cells and their memory subset of cells in priming sporozoite-specific CD8+ T-cell responses in the liver. Taken together, these results suggest that mice that received the three doses of sporozoites elicited long-lasting protection compared to the twice immunized group.

Vaccination with LA-GAP generates a more robust CD8 T cell response than EA-GAP

Immunization with Scd/Scot1 KO (LA-GAP) sporozoites provided long term protection in C57BL/6 mice. Next, we compared the cell-mediated immune response of the early (EA-GAP) and late (LA-GAP) arrested GAPs. For comparison, we used Speld KO (EA-GAP) parasites48. Speld KO parasites invade hepatocytes normally but arrest during early to mid-liver stage development48. The mice were thrice immunized with the EA or LA GAP sporozoites, and control mice received uninfected mosquito salivary gland debris (SGD). Livers were harvested ten days after the last immunization (Fig. 5A). T lymphocyte subsets were analyzed by flow cytometry using the gating strategy as summarized in Supplementary Fig. S4. The mice immunized with LA-GAP showed a 5.8-fold increase in the total lymphocyte count compared to a 3.5-fold increase in the EA-GAP immunized group (Fig. 5B, C). Analysis of T lymphocytes and their subsets revealed a significant increase in CD8+ and CD4+ T lymphocytes in the LA-GAP immunized groups compared to those in the SGD and EA-GAP immunized groups (Fig. 5D–F). We next quantified memory CD8+ T-cell subsets in immunized mice and found higher numbers of effector memory T cells (TEM) (CD8+ CD44hi CD62Llo KLRG1+) and tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) (CD8+ CD44hi CD62Llo CD69+) in LA-GAP immunized mice than in SGD-immunized and EA-GAP immunized mice (Fig. 5G–I). These results demonstrated that LA-GAP led to enhanced cell-mediated immune response compared to EA-GAP.

LA-GAP are capable of inducing both stage transcending and pre-erythrocytic immunity compared to EA-GAP

Preerythrocytic protection after GAP immunization has revealed the induction of antibodies against CSP, suggesting the importance of humoral immunity49,50. Next, we compared humoral immunity generated by EA and LA-GAP. To investigate the antibody response, sera were collected from naïve or immunized C57BL/6 mice and pooled (Fig. 6A). We used IFA to determine the serum reactivity against different stages of the parasites. We found similar immunostaining patterns using EA-GAP and LA-GAP sera with pre-erythrocytic stage parasites (Fig. 6B, C). Next, we immunostained blood stage parasites to determine the cross-stage reactivity of sera. While EA-GAP sera had no detectable reactivity, LAGAP sera recognized blood stages parasites robustly (Fig. 6D). To assess the inhibitory effects of the serum antibodies on the infectivity of sporozoites, the serum samples were preincubated with sporozoites and then added to the HepG2 culture. We found a significant decrease in the number of EEFs in the serum-incubated groups compared to that in the preimmune serum-incubated group. However, we found no difference in the sporozoite-neutralizing ability of early- and late-stage GAP-immunized mouse sera (Fig. 6E). To assess the role of antibodies against blood stage infection, C57BL/6 mice were injected with sera followed by challenge with iRBCs. While PIS and EA-GAP immunized mice showed similar growth patterns of parasites, LA-GAP-immunized mice exhibited slow growth with decreased parasitemia (Fig. 6F). These results show that GAP-immunized mice generate robust pre-erythrocytic humoral responses independent of their arrest in the liver. Stage-transcending immunity is only achieved in LA-GAP immunized mice.

Vaccination with late arrested GAP confers superior protection than early arrested GAP

Early and late GAP-mediated protection is largely CD8+ T-cell dependent19. We next compared the protection in early- and late arrested GAP-immunized mice. C57BL/6 mice were immunized three times with early and late arrested GAP sporozoites, and then one group was challenged with WT and another with GFP-luciferase-expressing infectious sporozoites (Fig. 7A). Liver-stage parasite burden was measured in vivo by assessing luciferase activity 42 h after sporozoite challenge. All SGD-immunized control mice exhibited a strong luciferase signal (Fig. 7B). Luciferase signals were close to background levels in all late arrested GAP-immunized mice, whereas luciferase signals were detected in early arrested GAP-immunized mice (Fig. 7B). After quantifying the luciferase signal intensity, a significant difference in the parasite load in the liver of early- and late arrested GAP-immunized mice was detected (Fig. 7C). By monitoring the patency, all the SGD-immunized mice became patent on day 3 post challenge, whereas all the Scd/Scot1 KO-immunized mice were protected. We found that only 55.5% of early arrested GAP-immunized mice were protected (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these data show that late liver stage-arresting sporozoites show higher potency under the tested conditions than early liver stage-arresting sporozoites.

Discussion

Subunit vaccines RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M have been the most successful among different malaria vaccines. However, the immunity provided by the WHO-approved RTS,S vaccine2 is short-lived and wanes over time51. R21/Matrix-M is well tolerated, reduced clinical malaria, and is being deployed52. However, neither vaccine provides >90% protection against homologous CHMI and hasn’t been tested against heterologous CHMI53. With the current burden and casualties being reported each year, the subunit vaccines are not as promising as the future of the malaria vaccine54. The future malaria vaccine demands targeting multiple antigens that could provide pre-erythrocytic and cross-stage immunity. The injectable formulation of the whole parasite vaccine that arrests during liver stage development offers a broad range of antigens. It was also proven to prevent the clinical symptoms in immunized individuals55. One such clinically advanced vaccine is made of irradiated, cryopreserved P. falciparum sporozoites (RAS), which has given the most promising outcomes on CHMI in malaria naïve individuals in the USA and Europe and also in malaria-endemic regions of Africa13,56–59. These attenuated parasites, attributing to their DNA damage due to radiation exposure, failed to complete liver stage development and offered a wide array of antigens on immunization, which subunits vaccines failed to deliver. RAS vaccines meet safety, tolerability, and efficacy requirements53. No breakthrough blood stage infections were reported from 2046 humans receiving 6538 doses of the PfSPZ vaccine53. Intravenous immunization with five doses of cryopreserved PfSPZ vaccine completely protects against CHMI10. In another approach by direct venous inoculations (DVIs) of live replication-competent Pf sporozoites (PfSpz-CVac) in CHMI trials with the combination of antimalarial chloroquine has produced greater parasite biomass and offered increased protection at lower doses than PfSpz vaccine60,61. However, questions of intrinsic safety and feasibility in the field implementation have given setbacks to this vaccine strategy62. Genetically designed parasites targeting the genes essential in the liver stage development have proven to give much control over the safety and efficacy in mass administration33. Increased attenuation with multiple gene deletions has been achieved previously31,63. In this study, we generated a fully late liver-arrested Scd/Scot1 GAP that cannot transition to blood-stage infection. The complete arrest of Scd/Scot1 GAP in the liver stage indicates that vaccine strains harboring multiple independent gene deletions are safer than single knockout GAPs. We further show that LA-GAP induces a higher frequency of CD8+ T cell responses than EA-GAP vaccination.

Immunization with Scd/Scot1 GAP confers broad and durable sterile protection. We observed long-lasting protection in mice immunized three times with Scd/Scot1 GAP compared to twice immunized mice. The livers of immunized mice were screened for T lymphocytes, and we found an increase in CD4+, CD8+ T lymphocytes, and CD8+ CD44hi memory T cells in the thrice immunized groups compared to twice immunized. This dose-dependent immunological response for establishing high-level protection is consistent with previous reports10,53. LA-GAP vaccines induce a higher frequency of CD8+ T cell responses and offer the potential advantage of having more robust immunogenicity and breadth than EA-GAP vaccines. Genetically engineered PfSpz lacking the genes b9 and slarp (PfSPZ-GA1), the first PfEA-GAP showed their safe administration in humans and immunogenicity was comparable to radiation-attenuated PfSPZ Vaccine64,65. A more recent clinical trial with PfLA-GAP, produced by mutating Pfmei2 (PfSPZ-GA2), which led to the late liver stage attenuation of the parasite, has offered a more enhanced protective immune response than PfSPZ-GA165. Immunity to preerythrocytic parasites is mediated by both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses. In addition to enhanced CD8+ T cell responses, the antibodies in the sera obtained from mice immunized with either RAS or GAP have been shown to neutralize sporozoites. However, LA GAP’s antibodies neutralize the sporozoite, act toward blood stage parasites, and offer stage-transcending protection (STP)31,38. The sera obtained from the Speld and Scd/Scot1 GAP-immunized mice recognized sporozoites and preerythrocytic stages and neutralized sporozoites at a similar rate. However, only Scd/Scot1 KO (LA-GAP) immunized mice sera recognized blood stage parasites and provided stage-transcending immunity. Our data show that the preerythrocytic humoral immune response generated by GAP-immunized mice is independent of their arrest in the liver, and only LA-GAP elicits stage-transcending protection.

The major protection against preerythrocytic parasites is mediated by CD8+ T cells in murine models66. We herein observed the enhanced CD8+ T cells, CD8+ CD44hi effector memory T cells (TEM) (CD62Llo KLRG1+) and CD8+ CD44hi tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) (CD62Llo CD69+) in LA-GAP immunized mice than EA-GAP. A comparative study including immunization with RAS, EARD, and LARC GAP showed that LARC GAP elicits stronger CD8+ T-cell-mediated immunity than RAS or EARD GAP19. These observations are consistent with our data that LA-GAP elicits enhanced effector and memory CD8+ T cell responses compared to EA-GAP, which provides superior protection in mice. In fact, it was shown that LA-GAP vaccination is superior to both EA-GAP and RAS19. Recently, a direct comparison of the durability of protection by four different rodent whole-sporozoite malaria vaccines (RAS, EA-GAP, LA-GAP and SPZ-CVac) revealed that EA-GAP provides the lowest overall protection. At the same time, RAS and LA-GAP elicited long-lasting protection compared to SPZ-CVac67,68. By comparison, our LA-GAP and EA-GAP data agree with the above results19,67,68. However, there was a discrepancy between Moita et al.67,68 and Butler et al.19 studies in immunity achieved with RAS and LA-GAP. Moita et al. linked the discrepancy to the immunization regimen and parasite species employed in the two studies. Moita et al.68 found that C57BL/6J mice vaccinated with PbRAS or PbLA-GAP elicited long-term protection. We evaluated protection in Scd/Scot1 GAP-immunized C57BL/6 mice receiving three doses of sporozoites. In agreement with the above report, we found 60% protection over a 31-week period. These data indicate that Scd/Scot1 GAP provides long-lasting protection like LA-GAP and RAS68. Overall, our results and previous research19,67,68 indicate that EA-GAP is less suitable for further development and use as a whole sporozoites vaccine. However, a head-to-head assessment of Scd/Scot1 GAP with other LA-GAPs and RAS is required to conclude its superiority over others. Our results also show that LA-GAP elicits immunity against blood-stage challenge. The enhanced protection upon immunization with LA-GAP can be attributed to the recognition of diverse arrays of antigens by CD8+ T cells. The overlapping antigens are primarily responsible for protecting against blood stage challenge. CD8+ T cells are first identified as central memory or effector memory cells based on the expression of the CD62L and CCR7 markers69. Another subset includes liver-resident memory cells, which have lost their circulation capabilities from their resident tissues and are frequently identified by decreased expression of CD62L and increased expression of the CD69, CXCR6, and CXCR3 markers on their surface70–72. Thus, protection is mainly aided by two subsets of the CD8+ T-cell population, effector memory (TEM) and resident memory (TRM) T cells19,40. Both cell types act in concert to eliminate the parasite. The effector function of CD8+ T cells is not fully resolved. Still, cytotoxicity and effector molecules such as interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) supposedly play central roles in parasite clearance40. Recent studies have highlighted the major role of CD8+ TRM cells in eliminating infected hepatocytes31,33,40,71,73,74. Intravital imaging studies have revealed increased retention of TRM cells in the liver sinusoids of immunized mice in search of a liver infection40,46,47. The downregulation of specific cell markers has confirmed the ultimate requirement of these cells for eliciting a protective immune response74. The passive transfer of these cells has also led to protection in challenged mice31.

Scd/Scot1 GAP vaccine was engineered by deleting the Scd42 and Scot143 genes. The development of Scd/Scot1 or Scd KO parasites is similar to other late-arresting rodent malaria FAS II GAPs in terms of their development in the liver. P. yoelii GAPs lacking fatty acid synthesis enzymes (FabB/F and FabZ) were completely attenuated23, but not Plasmodium berghei FabB/F75 and FabI KO26. The phenotypic variations of the orthologous genes in rodent malaria parasites emphasized the importance of analyzing the phenotype in P. falciparum. FASII-pathway is unsuitable for generating GAP in P. falciparum because it was found to be critical for the formation of sporozoites23,76. Whether Scd/Scot1 GAP can be translated into P. falciparum for clinical development remains to be ascertained. One of the genes deleted in this double KO is Scd. This endoplasmic reticulum enzyme42 catalyzes the biosynthesis of monounsaturated fatty acids from saturated fatty acids that are either synthesized de novo or derived from the host. Plasmodium FAS-II pathway is localized to the apicoplast77. The mosquito stage phenotype applies to apicoplast localized enzymes or other fatty acid-modifying enzymes that are localized to different compartments remains to be ascertained. Like Scd or Scot1 single knockouts, PlasMei2 and LISP2 KO showed incomplete attenuation during liver stage development31. Scd/Scot1 and Mei2/LISP2 double-KO parasites are safe and induce sterile immunity. Immunity elicited by PyMei2/LISP2 double-KO and PbScd/Scot1 GAP were studied in BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice, respectively. However, a direct comparison in the same combination is required to arrive at a conclusion.

Very recently, a LA-GAP named PyLARC2 was generated. Immunization of rodents with PyLARC2 through different immunization routes, including intramuscular administration, elicited robust humoral and cellular immune responses and conferred sterile immunity against infection. It will be interesting to see the immune response with Scd/Scot1 GAP via intramuscular administration. Previously, it was shown that intravenous immunization is more effective than intramuscular56. However, like the intravenous route, whether the intramuscular administration of sporozoites also engenders long-lasting sterile protection has yet to be ascertained. Immunization with PyLARC2 also elicited cross-stage and cross-species protective immune responses. We also found a cross-stage immune response, but it wasn’t protective, and cross-species protection in mice immunized with Scd/Scot1 GAP remains to be ascertained. Whether the superior cross-stage immunity achieved with PyLARC2 was due to Py/BALB/c combination remains a question. A head-to-head assessment of different LA-GAPs in Py/BALB/c and Pb/C57BL/6 mice combination is required to conclude their capability to elicit cross-stage immunity. Similar to PyLARC2, we found that cross-stage immunity was antibody-mediated, although the involvement of cellular immune responses remains to be evaluated. Further, cryopreserved PfSPZ-LARC2 was produced that replicated robustly in liver-humanized mice but did not transition to viable blood stage infection. A safety trial in malaria-exposed Africans is being planned with cryopreserved PfSPZ-LARC233. The data on LARC2 warrant its clinical assessment and support its further development as a whole sporozoite vaccine.

Different preclinical and clinical trials underscore the potential of LA-GAP based vaccines. Immunization with LARC2 sporozoites engendered long-lived immune responses against various infectious sporozoite challenges in diverse strains of mice33. The protective efficacy in Scd/Scot1 GAP immunized mice wanes over the 6 months. Hence, before translating Scd/Scot1 GAP in P. falciparum, a detailed evaluation of T cell functionality and the longevity of efficacy in different parasite/animal combinations is warranted. The safety, stringency, and mechanisms of protective immunity differ among parasite/mice combinations. These studies can be used to rapidly investigate and prioritize the Scd/Scot1 GAP for subsequent evaluation in humans.

Methods

Mice

Animal experiments were performed at the animal facility of CSIR-Central Drug Research Institute, India. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee at CSIR-Central Drug Research Institute (IAEC reference no: IAEC/2018/49 and IAEC/2021/107), and experiments were conducted in accordance with the committee for the purpose of control and supervision of experiments on animals (CPCSEA), India. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages under a 12 h light/dark period at a temperature of 23 °C ± 2 °C and 55 ± 10% relative humidity. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. Female Swiss albino mice were used for regular parasite passage and transmission, and C57BL/6 mice were used for in vivo sporozoite infection. For all the procedures, we sedated the mice using a recommended dose of 100–200 mg/kg body weight of Ketamine and 5-16 mg/kg body weight of Xylazine injected intraperitoneally. Infected mice were euthanized when they presented signs of severe discomfort and/or morbidity by exposure to carbon dioxide.

Parasites, mosquitos and cell lines

Plasmodium berghei ANKA (MRA 311) and P. berghei ANKA GFP (MRA 867 507 m6cl1) were obtained from BEI Resources, USA. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase (Scd KO) and sporozoite conserved orthologous transcript 1 (Scot1 KO) parasites were originally produced in the laboratory of S. Mishra42,43. To obtain sporozoites, parasites were routinely transmitted to female Anopheles stephensi mosquitoes, as described previously78. Human liver hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) cells were used for parasite liver stage development analysis and were maintained in DMEM (Sigma) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma), 0.2% NaHCO3 (Sigma), 1% sodium pyruvate (Invitrogen), and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. We routinely tested cell lines for mycoplasma contamination.

Generation of Scd/Scot1 KO parasites

The Scd (PlasmoDB ID: PbANKA_1110700) and Scot1 (PBANKA_1411000) double-KO (Scd/Scot1 KO) parasites were generated via double crossover homologous recombination. First, we generated a marker-free Scd-KO parasite line. For this purpose, the Scd targeting cassette was digested with EcoRV/NotI and religated after the GFP and hDHFR:yFCU cassettes were removed. The Scd marker-free (MF) cassette was transfected into Scd KO schizonts as previously described79. The mutant parasites were selected by intraperitoneal injection of 5-FC for 3 days, as previously described80. Genomic DNA was isolated from GFP-negative parasites that appeared after 5-FC drug selection. Marker-free Scd KO parasites were confirmed by diagnostic PCR using the primer pair 1045/1055. The clonal lines of marker-free Scd KO parasites were obtained by limiting dilution of the parasites. Next, the Scot1 targeting cassette was transfected into the schizonts of the marker-free Scd KO line as previously described79. The transfected parasites were selected by oral administration of the pyrimethamine drug (Sigma). Successful transfection of the Scot1 targeting cassette was confirmed by observing GFP signals under a fluorescence microscope. Clonal lines of Scd/Scot1 KO parasites were obtained by limiting dilution of the parasites. Genotyping was performed using primer pairs 1169/1225 and 1215/1170 for 5’ and 3’ integrations, respectively. The absence of the Scot1 ORF in the Scd/Scot1 KO parasites was confirmed by using the primers 1593/1594. All the primers used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. To analyze the asexual blood growth rate of Scd/Scot1 KO parasites, an equal number of WT GFP- or Scd/Scot1 KO-infected RBCs were intravenously injected into Swiss albino mice (5 mice/group). Giemsa-stained blood smears were prepared, and the growth rate was estimated by counting the parasites.

Mosquito stage analysis of Scd/Scot1 KO parasites

The WT GFP and Scd/Scot1 KO parasites were transmitted to female Anopheles mosquitos by allowing them to feed on infected mice as described previously48. On day 14, post blood meal, the midguts were dissected to compare the oocyst frequency, number, and sporulation pattern. The midguts were crushed, and the sporozoites per mosquito were quantified using a hemocytometer. Salivary glands were dissected on days 19–22 post infection, and the sporozoite numbers per mosquito was quantified.

In vivo infection and development analysis

To analyze the in vivo infection of salivary gland sporozoites, different numbers of Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites were inoculated intravenously or through mosquito bites in C57BL/6 mice. The appearance of parasites in the blood was monitored by making Giemsa-stained blood smears, and the prepatent period was determined. To estimate the parasite burden, another group of C57BL/6 mice was intravenously injected with 5000 WT GFP or Scd/Scot1 KO sporozoites, and the livers were harvested and homogenized in TRIzol (Invitrogen). Total RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two micrograms of RNA was reverse transcribed using reverse transcriptase and random hexamers in a 20 µl reaction as described previously81. Pb18s rRNA and mouse GAPDH transcripts were amplified using primer pairs 1195/1196 and 1193/1194 and quantified using a CFX Opus 96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad). The number of parasite transcripts was normalized to the Pb18s rRNA/mouse-GAPDH ratio. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Sporozoite invasion, hepatocyte infection and detached cell formation

To assess sporozoite infection in vitro, HepG2 cells were seeded in 48- (55,000 cells/well) or 24- (100,000 cells/well) well plates. For the invasion assay, HepG2 cells were infected with sporozoites (10,000/well) in a 48-well plate, and sporozoites were allowed to invade for 2 h and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). For EEF developmental analysis, HepG2 cells were infected with sporozoites (5000/well) in a 48-well plate, and the culture was maintained as previously described82. The culture was fixed at 36, 48, 62 and 64 hpi with 4% PFA. For the detached cell assay, 30,000 sporozoites were added to a 24-well plate, and the release of detached cells in the culture supernatant was observed at 64 hpi. The culture supernatant was collected, and the number of detached cells was counted using a hemocytometer. To analyze the infection of hepatic merozoites, detached cells or culture supernatant was injected intravenously into Swiss mice, and blood-stage infection was observed by making a Giemsa-stained blood smear.

Immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

For the invasion assay, fixed cultures were washed twice in PBS and blocked with 1% BSA/PBS, and the extracellular and intracellular sporozoites were immunostained with anti-CSP mouse antibody50 (diluted 1 µg/ml) before and after permeabilization as previously described83. Before and after permeabilization, signals were revealed using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG, respectively (diluted 1:500; Invitrogen, USA). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen), mounted using prolong diamond antifade reagent (Life Technologies), and visualized under a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope (100X (NA 1.30; oil) objective). For immunostaining of the EEFs, fixed cultures were washed twice in PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA) for 10 min at RT, washed in PBS, and blocked with 1% BSA/PBS. Primary antibodies were diluted in 1% BSA/PBS, incubated for 1 h at RT and then washed thrice with PBS. As described above, primary antibody signals were then detected using anti-mouse or anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor-conjugated IgG. To analyze growth, number, and area, EEFs were stained with upregulated in infectious sporozoite gene 4 (UIS4)15 (diluted 1:1,000). The EEFs were counted using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope, and the area of the EEFs was measured using NIS-Elements D software. Merozoite formation was visualized using a merozoite surface protein 1 (MSP1) antibody84 (diluted 1:5,000). Apicoplast development was visualized using acyl carrier protein (ACP) (diluted 1:1,000)85 antibody. Images were acquired using FV1000 software on a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus BX61WI) using a UPlanSAPO 100x (NA 1.4, oil) or 63x (NA 0.25, oil) objective or a Leica-SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) under a 100X oil immersion (NA 1.4, oil) objective or a Leica DM 3000 LED microscope using a 100X (NA 1.25, oil) objective. Images were processed using ImageJ Fiji software and deconvoluted using the “Deconvolution Lab2” plugin.

Sporozoite immunization and challenge

C57BL/6 mice were immunized twice or thrice with salivary gland sporozoites intravenously at intervals of two weeks. As a control, salivary gland debris from uninfected mosquitos was injected. Mice were challenged 10 days post last immunization by injecting infectious sporozoites intravenously or through mosquito bites, and blood-stage infection was monitored by making Giemsa-stained blood smears. Blood-stage-negative mice were rechallenged with infectious sporozoites to monitor long-lasting protection.

Immunized mouse serum reactivity

To visualize the reactivity of sera obtained from the immunized mice, different stages of parasites were stained with sera (diluted 1:50), and signals were revealed using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (diluted 1:500; Invitrogen). To visualize the sporozoites, EEFs and schizonts, anti-TRAP86, anti-UIS416 and anti-Spatr78 antibodies, respectively, were used. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. The coverslips were mounted using Prolong diamond antifade reagent. The images were acquired using FV1000 software on a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus BX61WI) with a UPlanSAPO 100×/1.4 oil immersion objective. To evaluate the effect of serum antibodies on sporozoite infectivity, 5000 WT sporozoites were incubated with immune and preimmune sera (diluted 1:50). As a positive control, 5 µg of anti-CSP antibody was used. The sporozoites were incubated for 20 min at 25 °C and added to the HepG2 culture. The culture was fixed at 36 hpi, the EEFs were immunostained with an anti-Hsp70 antibody87, and the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. The EEFs in the entire well were counted. To check the effect of sera on asexual blood stage parasite growth, C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with 250 μl of pooled serum and then challenged with 50 P. berghei iRBCs intravenously.

In vivo imaging of EEF development in whole bodies of live mice

Immunized mice were challenged with Pb-GFP-luciferase parasites88. Luciferase activity in mice was visualized through imaging of whole bodies using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum) as described previously89. Mice were anaesthetized, their belly shaved, and D-luciferin (100 mg/kg; Promega) was injected intraperitoneally. Bioluminescence images were acquired with a 10 cm FOV, medium binning factor and an exposure time of 120 seconds. Quantitative bioluminescence analysis was performed by measuring the luminescence signal intensity using the ROI settings of the Aura imaging software. ROI measurements are expressed as total flux (photon/second).

Isolation of nonparenchymal cells from the mouse liver

A single-cell suspension of liver cells was prepared as described previously90. Briefly, the immunized and control mice were anaesthetized and surgically exposed to visualize the portal vein and vena cava. The livers were perfused with 10 ml of perfusion buffer composed of Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) with 5 mM HEPES and 0.5 mM EDTA at a rate of 1 ml/min or until the liver turned pale. The perfused liver was harvested and crushed into small pieces in collagenase type IV buffer in a 60 mm petri dish. The liver was digested for 30 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation at 80 rpm. The collagenase digestion was terminated by adding 10 ml of cold PBS supplemented with 4% FBS and 0.5 mM EDTA. The nonparenchymal cells were then purified by gradient centrifugation using a 26% Histodenz-PBS solution. Total nonparenchymal cells were counted using a hemocytometer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Quantification of T-cell responses by flow cytometry

After enumerating the total number of nonparenchymal cells per infected liver, 1 ×106 nonparenchymal liver cells were resuspended in 100 µl of PFB (PBS+ 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS)). The cells were stained with FITC-conjugated CD3 (Invitrogen, 11-0031-82), PE-Cy5-conjugated CD4 (Invitrogen, 15-0042-82), PE-Cy7-conjugated CD8 (Invitrogen, 25-0081-82), Alexa Fluor 700-conjugated CD44 (Invitrogen, 56-0441-82), PerCP-eFluor 710-conjugated KLRG1 (Invitrogen, 46-5893-82), APC-conjugated CD62L (Invitrogen, 17-0621-82), PerCP-eFluor 710-conjugated CXCR6 (Invitrogen, 46-9186-82), PerCP-Cyanine 5.5 CD69 (Invitrogen, 45-0691-82) anti-mouse monoclonal antibodies on ice for 20 min. The cells were washed with 1 ml of PFB by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in 500 µl of PFB and acquired on a BD FACS Aria Fusion Flow cytometer (BD Bioscience). The acquired cells were gated on lymphocytes via the forward/side scatter to remove dead cells and debris, followed by hierarchical gating to select respective T lymphocyte subsets. Analysis was done using BD FACS DiVA software. Calculations of total number of cells were determined by expressing the cell type of interest as a percentage of lymphocytes based on forward and side scatter (FSC/SSC) and multiplying this number by the number of lymphocytes counted from each liver as previously described31.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± SEM or mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. The statistical significance of differences between two groups was analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, log-rank Mantel–Cox test or one-way ANOVA.
