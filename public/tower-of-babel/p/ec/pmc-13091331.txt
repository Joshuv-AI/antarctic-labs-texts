Retinal Ischemic Perivascular Lesions are Associated with Multifactorial Disease Burden and Severity of Diabetic Retinopathy
Authors: Kallista Zhuang, Shinji Kakihara, Amani A Fawzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo quantify retinal ischemic perivascular lesions (RIPLs) and evaluate their potential as a biomarker for diabetic retinopathy (DR) severity.DesignCross-sectional study.ParticipantsPatients with diabetes mellitus (DM) and spectrum of DR severity, recruited from a tertiary referral center.MethodsRetinal ischemic perivascular lesions were quantified using 6 × 6 mm OCT scans based on established criteria: perivascular location, focal thinning of the inner nuclear layer, disruption of the outer plexiform layer, and elevation of Henle's fiber layer. Two independent graders assessed RIPL counts, and intergrader reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient.Main Outcome MeasuresAssociation between RIPL count and DR severity using multinomial logistic regression. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis evaluated diagnostic performance of RIPL using area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity. Causal mediation analysis estimated the direct and indirect effects of systemic comorbidities on DR severity.ResultsOne hundred twenty-nine eyes were included: 51 with no DR (DM with no DR [DMnoDR]), 45 nonproliferative DR (NPDR), and 33 proliferative DR (PDR). Retinal ischemic perivascular lesion counts increased with DR severity: 1.18 ± 2.46 in DMnoDR, 3.69 ± 4.91 in NPDR, and 10.10 ± 10.44 in PDR. Eyes with higher RIPL counts had significantly greater odds of higher DR severity (NPDR vs. DMnoDR: odds ratio [OR] = 1.22, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.04–1.43, P = 0.0156; PDR vs. NPDR: OR = 1.11, 95% CI, 1.03–1.21, P = 0.0084; PDR vs. DMnoDR: OR = 1.36, 95% CI, 1.16–1.59, P = 0.0002). Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions were moderately predictive for vision-threatening DR, with an AUC of 0.779 (95% CI: 0.678–0.879); an optimized threshold of 3 RIPLs achieved sensitivity of 76% and specificity of 78%. Causal mediation analysis revealed that RIPLs mediated 30% of hypertension (HTN)’s effect on DR severity (P = 0.018), but not ischemic heart disease's effect (P = 0.092).ConclusionsRetinal ischemic perivascular lesions are discrete, quantitative OCT biomarkers associated with DR severity and partially explain the effect of HTN on DR severity. Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions may reflect the ischemic burden in the diabetic macula, and offer an additional marker of DR severity, bridging vascular and neural pathology, and capturing the impact of vascular comorbidities on DR.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions, Macular ischemia, Biomarkers, OCT

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a prevalent metabolic disease estimated to affect 38.4 million people across the age spectrum in the United States alone in 2021. Of these individuals, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has estimated that 26.43% (around 9.60 million people) are living with diabetic retinopathy (DR), a common complication of DM and an important cause of blindness.1 Diabetic retinopathy is generally viewed as a microvascular complication of diabetes, and recently the neurodegenerative aspect of DR pathophysiology is gaining interest. In this regard, it is intriguing that current grading systems, including the current gold standard systems for staging diabetic retinal disease, including the ETDRS severity scale and the International Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) severity scale, focus on the vascular features of diabetic retinal disease and overlook many important clinical features, especially the neuroretinal components.2,3 Retinal neuronal health is interlinked with vascular changes, so there is currently a concerted effort to consider the entire neurovascular unit (neurons, glia, and blood flow) when evaluating DR progression and pathophysiology.4, 5, 6

Given the association between vascular loss in the middle and deep capillary plexuses (DCP) and DR severity,7 the relevant structural impact of this vascular loss deserves more attention. Prior study has reported focal thinning of the middle retinal layers in diabetes, which correlates with DR severity and systemic vascular comorbidities such as renal and coronary artery disease.8 Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions, or RIPLs, are legacy lesions of paracentral acute middle maculopathy (PAMM). Whereas PAMM is an acute (and transient) consequence of capillary ischemia, appearing as hyperreflective bands in the inner nuclear layer (INL) on OCT, RIPL appears following the acute insult and persists.9, 10, 11, 12 Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions have also been referred to as chronic PAMM lesions or resolved PAMM lesions and are characterized by thinning of the INL, disruption of the outer plexiform layer (OPL), and relative elevation of the Henle fiber layer on OCT.12, 13, 14 Thus, these structural changes can act as long-term quantifiable biomarkers of retinal ischemic insults—linking retina neuronal integrity and ischemia in the middle capillary plexus and DCP.

Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions have also emerged as biomarkers that link the retina to the overall systemic ischemic status. Studies in nondiabetic individuals have identified RIPLs in association with ischemic heart disease (IHD), hypertension (HTN), and atrial fibrillation.12,15,16 These are also comorbidities that are prevalent in DM and may be associated with worse DR or higher risk of vision loss.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Recent studies have further explored the relationship between RIPLs and cardiovascular events, with Bousquet et al23 demonstrating a strong association between RIPLs and myocardial infarction among patients with coronary artery disease, while Bellanda et al24 found no significant association between RIPLs and major adverse cardiovascular events. Currently, the relationship between systemic cardiovascular health in DM and RIPL, as well as their relationship with DR severity, remains unclear. In this study, we sought to bridge this knowledge gap and determine whether the number of RIPLs could serve as a quantitative biomarker of DR severity, as well as reflect the impact of systemic cardiovascular comorbidities in individuals with DM across the entire spectrum of DR.

Methods

This study, which adheres to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and follows the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations, was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Northwestern University. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant prior to enrollment.

Study Sample

We recruited 232 patients with all ranges of DR severity, including no apparent DR (DM with no DR [DMnoDR]), mild, moderate, and severe nonproliferative DR (NPDR), treatment-naive proliferative DR (PDR), and quiescent PDR. Patients underwent macular OCT scanning between October 18, 2021 and September 21, 2023. We included patients with diabetes (type 1 or 2). If they were in the PDR quiescent group, patients should have no active neovascularization for 6 months and are stable treated with panretinal photocoagulation.

Patients were excluded if they had clinical evidence of diabetic macular edema involving the fovea (central subfield thickness >300 microns), any history of intravitreal anti-VEGF or intravitreal steroid treatment in the last 6 months, any ocular condition present that in the opinion of the investigator might affect the retinopathy status or alter visual acuity during the study, history of major ocular surgery (including cataract extraction, scleral buckle, any intraocular surgery, etc.) within prior 3 months or anticipated major surgery within the next 6 months after enrollment, or had glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) >10.0%. Images were excluded if there was poor OCT with <6 signal strength index, which would preclude precise and accurate image analysis, and if there were major motion artifacts.

Ultra-widefield scanning laser ophthalmoscope (Optomap Panoramic 200, Optos PLC) was used to obtain color fundus photographs. All eyes were graded using the ICDR severity scale as previously described.25 Diabetes mellitus with no DR was defined as no abnormalities, while mild NPDR included eyes with microaneurysms only. Moderate NPDR involves microaneurysms and hemorrhages. Severe NPDR met the 4:2:1 rule: >20 hemorrhages in all 4 quadrants, venous beading in 2 quadrants, or intraretinal abnormalities in 1 quadrant. Proliferative DR was defined by neovascularization or vitreous/preretinal hemorrhage. Vision-threatening DR (VTDR) includes severe NPDR (presence of intraretinal hemorrhages, microaneurysms, venous beading, or intraretinal microvascular abnormalities) or PDR (presence of neovascularization or vitreous hemorrhage).1 Two graders (K.Z. and S.K.) assessed ICDR, and if there was a discrepancy between their evaluations, a third grader (A.A.F.) made the final decision. Disagreements were resolved by a third grader. Demographic and clinical information of subjects were collected, including age, gender, axial length, visual acuity, HTN, IHD, peripheral vascular disease, renal disease, cerebrovascular disease, and dyslipidemia.

OCT Imaging

OCT imaging from 6 × 6 mm volumetric scans (400 2 x B-scans each of 400 A-scans) centered on the macula were obtained for the right eye of each participant, using the RTVue-XR Avanti system (HD Angio Retina Optovue, Inc, version 2017.1.0.151). The scanner operates with a beam wavelength of 840 ± 10 nm and at a bandwidth of 50 nm. The scan is acquired at a rate of 70 000 A-scans/sec. The system performs 2 successive B-scans at uniformly spaced, sequential locations on the retina.

RIPL Grading

The 6 × 6 mm OCT scans were analyzed for RIPL quantification. Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions were identified using the previously published criteria: perivascular location (Fig 1), focal thinning of the INL, OPL disruption, and Henle fiber layer compensatory expansion (Fig 1).12 For each volumetric scan, individual B-scans were reviewed by 2 independent and masked graders (K.Z. and S.K.). To determine if the RIPL grading criteria was consistent between 2 graders, an intraclass correlation coefficient was performed. After counting, a consensus between the 2 observers was required to identify RIPL to determine the final RIPL count.

As graders scrolled through the individual B-scans to identify RIPL, they used the reference line on the associated en face OCT image to reference the location of the cross-sectional B-scan onto the en face projection. Retinal ischemic perivascular lesion was associated with a well demarcated, darker area (hyporeflective on the DCP slab of en face OCT). The DCP slab is defined as the region from 10 μm above the INL to 10 μm below the OPL. En face OCT was used as a reference map to ensure that RIPL lesions on consecutive B-scans are counted as a single RIPL. To ensure consistent counting, the graders noted that RIPL layer disruptions get more pronounced toward the “peak” or center point of the RIPL and then fall back to the “baseline” retinal layer closer to the edge of the RIPL area. For each patient, the right eye was selected for subsequent statistical analysis. Patients with missing baseline information required for statistical analysis were also excluded from the statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. A multivariable, multinomial logistic regression model was performed to adjust for potential confounding factors, such as age, sex, and axial length. Other covariates included in the model include HTN, IHD, renal disease, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral vascular disease. As a sensitivity analysis, an additional multivariate multinomial regression model was fit, accounting for most recent HbA1c levels and DM duration in years, in addition to the other covariates. In an additional sensitivity analysis, RIPL count was categorized into 4 groups (0 RIPLs, 1-2 RIPLs, 3-5 RIPLs, and ≥6 RIPLs) and modeled as an unordered factor, with 0 RIPLs as the reference level. Predicted probability values from regression models were used to create a receiver operating characteristic curve. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to evaluate whether RIPL count could distinguish eyes with VTDR, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated for the AUC. Causal mediation analysis was conducted using the mediation package in R to assess whether RIPL mediates the association between systemic comorbidities (HTN, IHD, and renal disease) and DR severity. For each comorbidity, 2 linear regression models were specified. First, a mediator model was fitted using linear regression, in which RIPL count was regressed on comorbidity status. Second, an outcome model was fit, where DR severity was regressed on both RIPL count and comorbidity status. The average causal mediation effect and average direct effect for each comorbidity were estimated using nonparametric bootstrap CIs with 1000 simulations. The total effect was the sum of average causal mediation effect and average direct effect, and the proportion mediated by RIPL was calculated as the ratio of average causal mediation effect to the total effect. Each metric has an estimated effect, a 95% CI, and a P value for statistical significance of the estimated effect.26

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 129 eyes of 129 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) after excluding eyes with an absence of a 6 × 6 right eye scan (63 eyes), poor scan quality (Q-score <6; 3 eyes), other ocular conditions (e.g. edema, epiretinal membrane; 18 eyes), or missing baseline information (19 eyes). The average age was 57.2 ± 13.5 years. Among the participants, 103 (79.8%) had type 2 DM, while the remaining 20.2% had type 1 DM (Table 1). Clinical characteristics of this cohort were stratified by DR severity groups (Table 2)—51 eyes (39.5%) with DMnoDR, 45 (34.9%) with NPDR, and 33 (25.6%) with PDR. Axial lengths were significantly different between PDR versus DMnoDR (P = 0.018) and PDR versus NPDR (P = 0.0066). Hypertension was present in 41.2% of the DMnoDR group, 62.2% of the NPDR group, and 75.8% of the PDR group. Similar trends were observed for IHD and renal disease, with prevalence increasing progressively across DR severity groups. Dyslipidemia was present in 43.1% of patients with DMnoDR, 60.0% of patients with NPDR, and 44.4% of patients with PDR. Other systemic conditions, such as cerebrovascular disease and peripheral vascular disease, were noted in 1-3 patients across the groups.

Relationship between RIPL and Stages of DR

The intraclass correlation coefficient for RIPL counting between graders was 0.957 (95% CI, 0.940–0.970, P < 0.001). After resolving discrepancies, the final RIPL count was recorded for each eye, and the average RIPL count for each DR stage was calculated. The average RIPL count was 1.18 ± 2.46 RIPLs in DMnoDR, 3.69 ± 4.91 in NPDR, and 10.10 ± 10.44 in PDR. A box plot visualizing the number of RIPLs across DR stages showed a trend for higher RIPL counts with increasing DR severity, along with wide variability (Fig 2). A receiver operating characteristic curve was performed to examine the relationship between RIPL count and VTDR, which included severe NPDR, treatment-naive PDR, and quiescent PDR. The AUC was 0.779 (95% CI: 0.678–0.879) (Fig 3). Using Youden's J-index, the optimal cutoff was 2.5 RIPLs. As RIPLs are discrete, this corresponds to a threshold of ≥3 RIPLs and how well ≥3 RIPLs detect VTDR. At this cutoff, discrimination between non-VTDR and VTDR eyes achieved a sensitivity of 76% and a specificity of 78%.

Univariate Analysis

A univariate multinomial logistic regression was conducted to explore the associations between risk factors and DR severity (Table 3). The DMnoDR group served as the reference, and odds ratio (OR) was calculated accordingly. This analysis highlighted significant associations between DR severity and several factors, including the number of RIPLs, patient age, axial length, and HTN (P < 0.05). Specifically, RIPLs were significantly associated with DR severity: the OR was 1.26 for NPDR (95% CI, 1.08–1.47; P = 0.0035), and 1.41 for PDR (95% CI, 1.20–1.66; P = 0.000029).

Multivariate Analysis

A multivariate multinomial logistic regression was conducted to determine whether RIPLs were independently associated with DR severity after adjusting for significant covariates. This analysis (Table 4) demonstrates that RIPL count remained significantly associated with DR severity, with an OR of 1.22 in NPDR (95% CI, 1.04–1.43; P = 0.0156), and 1.36 in PDR (95% CI, 1.16–1.59; P = 0.00020). When comparing PDR versus NPDR, the OR was 1.11 (95% CI, 1.03–1.21; P = 0.0084), indicating that a higher number of RIPLs was associated with an increased likelihood of PDR. Axial length remained significant in the comparison between PDR and NPDR (OR = 0.47, 95% CI, 0.27–0.83; P = 0.0085), suggesting that eyes with longer axial length are associated with lower odds of having PDR compared with NPDR. On the other hand, HTN, IHD, and renal disease were no longer significant in multivariate analysis.

Causal Mediation Analysis

Causal mediation analysis evaluates the impact of an independent variable (variable X) on the dependent variable (variable Y) and determines the proportion of the effect that is mediated or explained by a “mediator variable” (variable M). The effect can be categorized into direct and indirect effect, depending on whether variable X is exerting its effect on variable Y independent of variable M (directly) or via variable M (indirectly), while the proportion mediated is an estimate of the proportion of the total effect that is mediated by variable M. The total effect considers the combination of indirect and direct effects of variable X on variable Y.26 To evaluate how the different comorbidities (HTN, IHD, and renal disease) interact with DR severity, we reasoned that RIPLs could serve as mediators for their effects on DR severity. Table 5 lists the results of this analysis.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that RIPLs are important OCT biomarkers of DR severity, with a higher number of RIPLs correlating with more advanced DR stages. Multivariate analysis confirmed that RIPLs are associated with DR severity, independent of comorbidities. Additionally, RIPLs were shown to partially mediate the effect of systemic HTN on DR severity. While RIPLs have been related to a wide range of systemic vascular diseases, most of these studies excluded diabetes, thus the relationship to DR severity remained unknown. Our study addressed this gap by quantifying the number of RIPLs across the DR spectrum and found that a threshold of 2.5 RIPLs predicts VTDR with a sensitivity of 76% and specificity of 78%. With an AUC of 0.779, RIPLs performed moderately as biomarkers for VTDR (Fig 3), a clinically important population in DR at a higher risk for vision loss, warranting timely evaluation and possible therapeutic intervention.27,28

For comparison to other imaging biomarkers, EyeArt AI system from EyeNuk is a US Food and Drug Administration approved tool for automated VTDR screening based on color fundus photography, with a sensitivity of 97% and a specificity of 90%.29 Strong performance is reasonable, as ETDRS DR staging is based on color fundus photography. Among noninvasive vascular markers, OCT angiography-derived metrics, such as vessel density, vessel length density, and geometric perfusion deficit, have been studied in relation to DR severity. While VTDR was not directly assessed, one study found that a cutoff of 2.5% DCP geometric perfusion deficit yielded an AUC of 0.955, with 97.2% sensitivity and 79.7% specificity for detecting clinically referable eyes.30 Deep capillary plexus vessel length density had comparable biomarker ability relative to DCP geometric perfusion deficit, with an AUC of 0.955, 96% sensitivity, and 84% specificity.30 Among structural OCT biomarkers, such as retinal layer thinning and disorganization, they are more commonly studied in the context of diabetic macular edema rather than DR severity staging.31 Despite a moderate biomarker performance, RIPLs contribute uniquely to the literature as an OCT-based marker studied in association with DR severity, and RIPLs can be detected on OCT without the need for OCT angiography or artificial intelligence-based fundus screening tools.

Retinal ischemic perivascular lesions appear after ischemic damage to the INL, a critical retinal layer where cell bodies of horizontal cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, interplexiform neurons, and Müller cells reside.32,33 Chronic ischemia leads to thinning of this layer, and concomitant disruption of the OPL and Henle fiber layer leads to formation of the RIPL lesion.12 Ischemia in the INL can increase the metabolic burden on surviving neurons and may impair metabolic waste clearance, oxygen supply, and visual signal transmission.6,33, 34, 35 Increased ischemia—higher percent nonperfusion area, decreased vessel density, and decreased blood flow—in the middle capillary plexus, is observed with increasing DR severity.7 Data from our univariate and multivariate analyses demonstrate that a higher RIPL count is associated with higher odds of greater DR stage. Specifically, relative to eyes without DR, each additional RIPL was associated with 22% higher odds of having NPDR and 36% higher odds of having PDR. When comparing eyes with PDR to those with NPDR, each additional RIPL increased the odds of PDR by 11%. In our sensitivity analysis with HbA1c and DM duration, RIPLs remained a robust biomarker, with each additional RIPL associated with 33% higher odds of PDR when compared with the DMnoDR group, and 12% higher odds of PDR when compared with NPDR. To determine whether the multinomial regression results were driven by outliers in PDR eyes, we fitted a multinomial model with RIPL burden as a categorical variable. We found that higher RIPL counts (3–5 RIPLs, ≥6 RIPLs) differentiate NPDR and PDR from DMnoDR but did not significantly distinguish NPDR from PDR. Overall, multivariable results support the idea that there is a dose-response relationship between RIPL burden and DR severity, with higher RIPL counts representing cumulative ischemic injury and correlating with more advanced DR stages.

Critically, RIPL remained significantly associated with DR in the multivariate analysis, while other predictors of DR severity (HTN, IHD, and renal disease) lost statistical significance, which suggested to us that there could be shared variance between RIPL and these systemic comorbidities. Prior studies have linked RIPLs to HTN and cardiovascular disease.15,16 To explore these potential relationships, we conducted causal mediation analysis to assess whether RIPLs mediate the effect of systemic comorbidities on DR severity. Our results indicate that RIPLs may explain approximately 30% of HTN's impact on DR severity, underscoring the complex and interwoven pathophysiological connections between HTN and DR. Hypertension is present in 40% of individuals >45 years old with type 2 DM.36 Population-based studies have shown that untreated, or poorly controlled, HTN significantly increases the likelihood of DR, and that higher systolic blood pressure is associated with VTDR.37 Moreover, a systematic review demonstrated that while blood pressure control reduces the incidence of DR over 4 to 5 years, it is less effective in mitigating DR progression and vision loss.38 This may be related to the early effect of HTN on retinal arteriolar thickening in patients with diabetes before the onset of clinical DR.39 Additionally, high blood pressure can damage retinal vascular endothelium and disrupt blood pressure autoregulation and, over time, contribute to retinal ischemia.40, 41, 42 As Sun et al3 noted, current DR staging does not account for systemic comorbidities. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence connecting HTN and DR by highlighting the potential role of RIPLs, which may serve as a useful biomarker in this context, capturing DR severity as well as systemic vascular morbidities.

We found that RIPLs may explain 25% of the relationship indirect between IHD and DR, but this mediation was not significant. However, the lack of significance may reflect the small sample size of patients with comorbid IHD (n = 23), or that there may be an alternative IHD-related retinal pathway that does not involve RIPLs. Similarly, RIPLs did not significantly mediate renal disease's impact on DR, likely due to limited sample size of patients with both DM and renal disease (n = 24).40,43,44

We note that longer axial length was significantly associated with lower odds of PDR, suggesting a potential protective effect of longer axial length against advanced DR stages. As shown in Table 4, patients with longer axial length had an OR of 0.47, when comparing PDR to NPDR. This inverse association is consistent with prior studies reporting protective effects of longer axial length against advanced DR.45,46 One study reported that with each millimeter increase in axial length, there was an OR of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.49–0.91; P = 0.01) for severe DR (defined as ETDRS >43).46

There are several limitations in this study. First, this study had fewer patients in the PDR group (n = 33) compared with the DMnoDR group (n = 51). The subgroups with comorbid IHD (n = 23), renal disease (n = 24), peripheral vascular disease (n = 4), and cerebrovascular disease (n = 7) were also small. This could reduce the generalizability of the findings to the wider population. Moreover, within the PDR subgroup, the distribution was highly unbalanced (treatment-naive PDR = 6 eyes vs. quiescent PDR = 27 eyes), which limited our ability to perform a separate analysis of treatment-naive PDR eyes. Second, because this is a cross-sectional study, the presence of an association does not allow us to infer the temporal influence of RIPL on DR progression, and similarly, causal mediation analysis cannot be used to assert causality. Third, although the intergrader agreement for RIPL identification was excellent (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.957), RIPL assessment remains inherently subjective. Variations in lesion appearance and potential confusion with other causes of INL thinning or image artifacts may introduce grading variability. Fourth, DR severity was graded based on clinical assessment following the ICDR criteria, which, while widely used in clinical practice, does not provide the same level of granularity as the full ETDRS scale. Finally, blood pressure was not quantitatively measured in this study and was recorded only as a categorical variable for the presence of HTN.16 Therefore, we did not perform a separate analysis to determine whether blood pressure independently influences RIPL count. Future studies with detailed hemodynamic data are warranted, as prior work has shown an association between HTN and chronic PAMM lesions.

In summary, our study suggests an important role for considering RIPLs in DR staging, as they not only correlate independently with DR severity but also bridge vascular pathology with retinal neurodegeneration, providing relevant clinical insights. We found that RIPL count of ≥3 identified patients with VTDR with 76% sensitivity and 78% specificity. Furthermore, given the multifactorial nature of DR, the role of RIPLs in mediating the effect of HTN on DR severity highlights their potential as a biomarker that captures the impact of systemic vascular comorbidities.
