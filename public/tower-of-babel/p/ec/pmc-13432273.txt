Smoking and Risk of Hepatic Steatosis Within the MASLD Spectrum in Korean Young Adults
Authors: Yongho Jee, Sun Ju Lee, Ho Soo Chun, Hyun‐Young Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is an increasing public health concern, particularly among young adults. Although smoking has been implicated in hepatic steatosis, large‐scale longitudinal evidence remains limited.MethodsWe analyzed data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service, including 3 496 144 adults aged 20–39 years who underwent health examinations between 2004 and 2007 and were followed through 2022. Hepatic steatosis was assessed using the Fatty Liver Index (FLI), a validated surrogate marker, and categorized as < 30, 30–59, or ≥ 60. Smoking exposure was evaluated at baseline according to smoking amount, duration, and pack‐years. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSmoking was associated with an increased risk of incident FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis across multiple exposure metrics. Among men, smoking ≥ 20 cigarettes/day was associated with the highest risk of FLI ≥ 60 (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.40–1.43), whereas 10–19 years of smoking was associated with an HR of 1.15 (95% CI 1.14–1.15). Among women, 10–19 years of smoking was associated with a 49% higher risk of FLI ≥ 60 (HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.43–1.56). Associations were generally stronger among participants with BMI < 25 kg/m2 or alcohol intake < 25 g/day. Risk estimates varied according to FLI threshold.ConclusionsSmoking was independently associated with an increased risk of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis in young adults. These findings support smoking prevention and cessation strategies as potential measures to reduce early‐onset steatotic liver disease.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a growing public health concern, particularly in Asian populations [1, 2]. The disease spectrum ranges from simple hepatic steatosis to steatohepatitis with varying degrees of inflammation and fibrosis [3]. MASLD is influenced by complex interactions between genetic susceptibility, metabolic dysfunction, and lifestyle‐related environmental factors [4]. Insulin resistance plays a central role in hepatic lipid accumulation and disease progression [5]. In addition to metabolic conditions such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia, lifestyle factors including smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and dietary patterns may contribute to hepatic steatosis and MASLD progression [5, 6].

Previous studies have demonstrated associations between smoking and hepatic steatosis or MASLD‐related phenotypes. In a meta‐analysis of 20 observational studies, smoking was significantly associated with increased odds of fatty liver disease with a pooled odds ratio of 1.110 (1.028–1.199) [7]. In middle‐aged and elderly Chinese individuals, the prevalence of hepatic steatosis and MASLD‐related phenotypes increased according to smoking exposure [8]. In addition, exposure to secondhand smoke has been associated with increased prevalence of hepatic steatosis among women [9].

Smoking has been shown to exacerbate insulin resistance and impair lipid metabolism, thereby contributing to hepatic fat accumulation and progression of steatotic liver disease [10, 11]. Proposed mechanisms include systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, tissue hypoxia, endothelial dysfunction, and altered adipose tissue distribution [10, 12]. Although obesity is a well‐established risk factor for MASLD, growing evidence suggests that lean individuals with steatotic liver disease may also experience increased cardiometabolic and cancer‐related risks [13]. However, the relationship between smoking exposure and hepatic steatosis among lean versus nonlean individuals remains insufficiently characterized.

Most previous studies examining smoking and steatotic liver disease have been based on cross‐sectional or relatively small cohort designs, and nationwide epidemiological evidence in young Asian adults remains limited. Furthermore, few studies have evaluated smoking exposure according to smoking amount, duration, and cumulative pack‐years in relation to hepatic steatosis across different severity thresholds.

Early adulthood represents a critical period for the development of long‐term metabolic trajectories, yet population‐level evidence regarding smoking‐related hepatic steatosis in this age group remains scarce. Understanding modifiable lifestyle‐related risk factors during young adulthood may therefore have important implications for early prevention strategies [14, 15].

Therefore, using data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), we aimed to investigate the longitudinal association between smoking exposure and incident hepatic steatosis defined by the Fatty Liver Index (FLI), a validated surrogate marker representing a proxy phenotype within the MASLD spectrum, in young Korean adults. We additionally explored whether these associations differed according to obesity status and alcohol consumption.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a prospective cohort study using data from the NHIS, which provides standardized annual or biennial health examinations for the general population. This study included individuals aged 20–39 years who underwent health checkups between 2004 and 2007 (N = 6 936 981). We excluded participants with missing or implausible values for sex, birth year, and anthropometric or laboratory measurements (including body mass index [BMI], waist circumference, triglycerides, and gamma‐glutamyl transferase [GGT]) and those with excessive alcohol intake (≥ 30 g/day for men and ≥ 20 g/day for women). Because GGT measurements were unavailable in some early health screening records, FLI was calculated only for examinations with complete data for BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and GGT. Health examination records lacking GGT values were excluded from FLI‐based outcome ascertainment. Participants with missing smoking‐related variables or invalid FLI calculation components were also excluded. The final analytic sample consisted of 3 496 144 individuals (61.97% men and 38.03% women) who were followed up until December 31, 2022 (Figure 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Ewha Womans University Seoul Hospital (Approval Number: SEUMC 2024‐06‐058; Date: July 15, 2024).

Flowchart of study population.

Measurement of Smoking Status

Smoking‐related variables were obtained from self‐reported questionnaires at baseline. Participants were classified according to smoking status as never, former, or current smokers. Additional metrics included smoking amount (< 10, 10–19, 20–39, and ≥ 40 cigarettes/day), smoking duration (< 5, 5–9, 10–19, 20–29, and ≥ 30 years), and pack‐years (nonsmoker, < 10, 10–19, 20–29, and ≥ 30 pack‐years). For pack‐year calculations, midpoints of each category were used (e.g., 7.5 cigarettes/day for the < 10 group) and multiplied by duration. As with all self‐reported measures, recall bias may have led to implausible values (e.g., unusually long smoking duration relative to age), although such cases were rare and unlikely to affect the overall findings.

Definition of FLI‐Defined Hepatic Steatosis

Hepatic steatosis was operationally defined using the FLI, a validated surrogate marker calculated from BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and GGT. Although FLI has been widely used in large‐scale epidemiological studies [16], this approach does not fully capture the current consensus definition of MASLD, which additionally requires the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors [17]. Participants were categorized as low risk (FLI < 30), intermediate risk (FLI 30–59), or high risk (FLI ≥ 60). FLI was calculated for health screening examinations with complete values for BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and GGT. Records with missing values in any FLI component were excluded from FLI‐based outcome ascertainment.

Incident outcomes were defined according to progression into each FLI category during follow‐up.

Outcome Ascertainment

The follow‐up time was calculated individually from the date of baseline examination (variable name: HME_DT_M) to the earliest occurrence of an incident FLI‐defined steatosis outcome, date of death, or the administrative censoring date (December 31, 2022), whichever came first. Thus, follow‐up did not begin from a fixed calendar date but from each participant's baseline examination date, thereby minimizing potential immortal time bias.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using the mean (SD) or frequency (%) by sex. Associations between smoking variables (status, amount, duration, and pack‐years) and FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression with time‐to‐event defined per FLI outcome category. Time‐to‐event was defined according to the interval between the baseline examination and the first subsequent health examination meeting the specified FLI threshold. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate relative hazards over longitudinal follow‐up in this repeated health screening–based cohort framework, consistent with prior large‐scale epidemiological studies using health examination data [18].

Models were stratified by sex and adjusted for potential confounders, including age, BMI, alcohol intake (converted to g/day), physical activity, systolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, and total cholesterol. The results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity analyses were used to examine associations within specific FLI risk strata (30–59 vs. ≥ 60). All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

Among the 3 496 144 participants included in the analysis, 61.97% were men and 38.03% were women. Men had a slightly higher mean age than women (31.99 vs. 30.56 years). Smoking prevalence was markedly higher among men, with nearly one‐third classified as current smokers (31.9%), whereas 94.44% of women were never smokers. Alcohol consumption was also more frequent and heavier among men: 78.02% reported alcohol use, with a mean daily intake of 6.96 g/day, compared with 50.01% and 3.82 g/day among women, respectively. The mean BMI was higher in men than in women (23.83 vs. 21.16 kg/m2) (Table 1).

Association of MASLD With Smoking Status by Gender

Association Between Smoking Exposure and Incident FLI‐Defined Hepatic Steatosis Among Men

Among men, higher smoking exposure was consistently associated with an increased risk of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis across FLI categories defined by an FLI ≥ 30, 30–59, or ≥ 60 (Table 2). In the adjusted model, compared to never smokers, men with pack‐year exposure of 20–29 had a 6.8% increased risk of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis at the FLI ≥ 30 threshold (HR 1.068, 95% CI 1.058–1.077), whereas the ≥ 30 pack‐year category showed attenuation of association (HR 0.959, 95% CI 0.932–0.987), indicating a nonlinear trend.

For the FLI ≥ 60 threshold, the adjusted HR increased progressively in the pack‐year group, reaching 1.219 (95% CI 1.171–1.268) in the ≥ 30 group (p for trend < 0.0001).

When smoking was categorized by cigarette amount per day, men who smoked 20–39 cigarettes/day had the highest adjusted risk for an FLI ≥ 60 threshold (HR 1.412, 95% CI 1.399–1.425). A clear dose–response relationship was observed (p for trend < 0.0001). A similar trend was observed for smoking duration. Compared to never smokers, those with 10–19 years of smoking had an increased risk of FLI ≥ 60 threshold (HR 1.145, 95% CI 1.137–1.152), whereas those aged ≥ 30 years had a modestly elevated risk (HR 1.109, 95% CI 0.995–1.235). These findings are summarized graphically in Figure S1.

Association Between Smoking Exposure and Incident FLI‐Defined Hepatic Steatosis Among Women

In women, the magnitude and consistency of the associations were generally less consistent than in men (Table 3). For the FLI ≥ 60 threshold, current smoking of 20–39 cigarettes/day was associated with a significantly increased adjusted risk (HR 1.349, 95% CI 1.047–1.738). Similarly, women with 10–19 years of smoking had a 1.5‐fold higher risk (HR 1.493, 95% CI 1.427–1.563), and those with 10–19 pack‐years had an HR of 1.421 (95% CI 1.253–1.612). Although the HR estimates were generally higher in the crude models, adjustment for confounders led to attenuation, especially in the highest pack‐year and duration groups. Nonetheless, significant linear trends were observed across all smoking indicators (p < 0.0001). Figure S2 summarizes HRs across smoking pack‐year groups among women.

Stratified Analyses by BMI and Alcohol Consumption

Tables 4 and S1–S7 present the stratified analyses according to BMI and alcohol intake. Among men with BMI < 25 kg/m2, the associations between smoking and incident FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis, particularly at the FLI ≥ 60 threshold, were generally stronger than those observed among men with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. For example, men with 20–29 pack‐years showed an adjusted HR of 1.425 for FLI ≥ 60 in the BMI < 25 subgroup. In contrast, associations among men with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 were generally attenuated and, in some exposure categories, showed inverse or nonlinear patterns.

Similarly, among men consuming < 25 g/day of alcohol, higher smoking exposure was generally associated with an increased relative risk of incident FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis (e.g., ≥ 20 cigarettes/day: adjusted HR of 1.414 for FLI ≥ 60), whereas associations were weaker or less consistent among heavier drinkers. Among women, stronger associations were also observed in subgroups with lower BMI or lower alcohol intake, although the patterns were generally less consistent than those observed among men.

Discussion

In this large nationwide cohort of young Korean adults, cigarette smoking was independently associated with an increased risk of incident FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis across multiple smoking exposure metrics. However, the magnitude and linearity of these associations varied according to exposure category, sex, and FLI threshold. The association was particularly pronounced in men, whereas in women, longer smoking duration and moderate cumulative exposure were associated with a substantially increased risk of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis despite the lower prevalence of smoking.

Similar results have been reported in previous studies. In a cohort study of healthy young and middle‐aged Korean adults from a single hospital in Korea, which followed participants for an average of 4.1 years, an increase in the incidence of NAFLD was observed among smokers [18]. In men, the incidence increased by 1.25 (1.21–1.29) for those with 10–19.9 pack‐years and by 1.36 (1.30–1.42) for those with more than 20 pack‐years compared to nonsmokers. In women, the incidence increased by 1.25 (1.04–1.50) times for those with 5–9.9 pack‐years and by 1.46 (1.17–1.81) times for those with 10 pack‐years compared to nonsmokers. Additionally, the degree of liver fibrosis is positively correlated with the amount of smoking [18]. In a retrospective study conducted in Japan over 10 years, a significant association between smoking status and newly diagnosed NAFLD was found in men (AOR 1.46, 95% CI 1.01–2.10), confirming that smoking is an independent risk factor (Brinkman index [BI] 1–399: AOR 1.77, 95% CI 1.02–3.07; BI ≥ 400: AOR 2.04, 95% CI 1.37–3.03) [19]. According to a population‐based study from China involving individuals aged 40 years or older, the prevalence of NAFLD was 1.45‐fold higher among former smokers and 2.29‐fold higher among heavy current smokers compared with never smokers [20]. When divided into three groups based on the degree of healthy smoking status, the correlation of MASLD prevalence in the intermediate and ideal smoking groups was relatively weak compared to that in the poor smoking group (decreased by 23% and 37%, respectively) [20]. Unlike many prior studies that relied on a single FLI cutoff (e.g., FLI ≥ 60), the present study evaluated multiple FLI thresholds (≥ 30, 30–59, and ≥ 60), allowing a more nuanced assessment of steatotic phenotypes across smoking exposure levels. This approach provides additional insight into how smoking may differentially influence milder versus more advanced steatotic phenotypes. By incorporating multiple smoking exposure metrics together with repeated screening–based outcome assessment in a nationwide young adult cohort, the present study expands existing epidemiological evidence regarding the relationship between smoking and steatotic liver disease phenotypes [7, 18].

In the present study, attenuation or nonlinear patterns were observed in certain exposure categories, particularly among men in the highest pack‐year group for lower FLI thresholds (e.g., FLI ≥ 30). Such findings may reflect several epidemiological phenomena, including survivor bias, behavioral modification among heavy smokers, or competing risks related to smoking‐associated morbidity [11]. In addition, reliance on baseline smoking assessment over a long follow‐up period may have introduced exposure misclassification due to smoking cessation or changes in smoking intensity, potentially leading to attenuation of observed associations.

There was a significant association between secondhand smoke exposure and the occurrence of NAFLD. Among lifelong nonsmoking women, exposure to secondhand smoke during both childhood and adulthood was associated with a 25% increase in NAFLD compared to nonexposed individuals (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.05–1.50) [8]. In another Korean study, an increase in NAFLD was confirmed only in women exposed to secondhand smoke. A meta‐analysis reported that passive smoking increased the risk of NAFLD by 1.38 times [8]. Although secondhand smoke exposure was not directly measured in the present study, these findings provide important contextual evidence supporting the broader role of tobacco exposures in NAFLD development.

Some studies have reported a positive association between smoking and liver fibrosis. For patients with NAFLD who smoke, thorough education on smoking cessation and consideration of priority screening and timely intervention for the prevention of liver fibrosis are necessary [21]. The results of this study regarding the association between BMI, alcohol consumption, and the occurrence of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis showed that the relationship between smoking and FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis was strongest in groups with BMI < 25 or alcohol intake < 25 g/day. In contrast, in individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 or in heavy drinkers, a tendency for a lower risk of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis or no association was observed. Although obesity is a major determinant of hepatic steatosis, previous studies suggest that smoking‐related metabolic effects may operate independently of obesity and may interact with body weight change in complex ways. For example, a Korean cohort study reported that BMI reduction was associated with lower NAFLD risk across smoking‐status groups, whereas smoking cessation accompanied by weight gain remained associated with increased NAFLD risk [18]. This suggests that the metabolic effects of smoking on the liver may have a more direct effect in normal‐weight nondrinkers. However, the ambiguity of the definition of NAFLD, which states that individuals should not consume more than 30 g of alcohol/day for men and 20 g for women, remains an issue. Some reports indicate that drinking less than 10 g in patients with NAFLD may have a positive effect on cardiovascular disease, indicating that the relationship between NAFLD and alcohol is still debated, necessitating further research [22, 23, 24]. One plausible explanation is that in obese individuals or heavy drinkers, dominant metabolic or alcohol‐related pathways may overshadow the incremental contribution of smoking to hepatic steatosis. In addition, residual confounding and exposure classification may be more pronounced in these subgroups. Importantly, hepatic steatosis in this study was defined using the FLI, a validated but indirect surrogate marker based on BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and GGT. Although FLI has been widely used in large‐scale epidemiological studies as a proxy indicator of hepatic steatosis, it does not fully capture the current consensus diagnostic framework of MASLD, which additionally requires the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors [17, 25]. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting associations between smoking and FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis rather than clinically confirmed MASLD. Because BMI and waist circumference are integral components of the FLI calculation, subgroup analyses stratified by BMI may partially reflect outcome construction rather than purely etiologic heterogeneity. Therefore, BMI‐stratified findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when comparing lean and nonlean phenotypes. Future studies incorporating imaging‐based diagnostics or validated MASLD algorithms will be necessary to further clarify these relationships.

Additionally, given the varying results reported regarding the progression of liver fibrosis due to moderate alcohol consumption, further studies in this area are needed [26]. The prevalence of hepatic steatosis is known to increase in obese patients, and a study from China reported that active smoking and BMI have a synergistic effect on the prevalence of MASLD, with the combined risk of smoking and obesity reaching 8.58 times [7]. However, there are reports that patients with lean steatotic liver disease have a higher incidence of cardiovascular disease and cancer [13]. The relationship between smoking and lean versus nonlean hepatic steatosis therefore warrants further investigation in future longitudinal studies. Notably, emerging research has revealed a potential countermechanism: Gut bacteria may alleviate smoking‐related NASH progression by actively degrading intestinal nicotine, suggesting novel therapeutic avenues to mitigate tobacco‐induced steatotic liver disease progression [27]. Although the present study does not establish causality, the observed associations between smoking exposure and incident FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis support the potential importance of smoking prevention and cessation in metabolic liver health.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that smoking exacerbates insulin resistance and impairs lipid metabolism, establishing it as a significant risk factor for the development of hepatic steatosis [10, 11, 12]. This association arises from the multifaceted role of smoking in promoting systemic inflammation, inducing oxidative stress, worsening insulin resistance, causing tissue hypoxia, and triggering free‐radical damage in hepatic tissues [10]. Smoking can influence the occurrence of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis through alterations in fat distribution associated with visceral obesity [14, 28]. Additionally, smoking may induce hepatic steatosis through chronic hypoxia, pro‐inflammatory effects, and stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system owing to increased catecholamine and glucagon secretion from nicotine [14, 29, 30]. In the context of a high‐fat diet, nicotine may stimulate hepatic lipogenesis through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, hepatocellular apoptosis, and decreased phosphorylation of adenosine‐5‐monophosphate–activated protein kinase [31].

This study had several limitations. First, the estimation of smoking amount through a self‐administered structured questionnaire may contain errors, and there is a possibility of underreporting that could vary by sex [32]. Second, uncertainty remains in the operational definition of hepatic steatosis based on FLI. Although FLI is a validated surrogate marker widely used in epidemiological studies, it does not directly measure hepatic steatosis through imaging or histologic assessment and may not fully reflect clinically confirmed MASLD. Third, smoking exposure was self‐reported and may have been subject to reporting bias, particularly among women, among whom smoking prevalence may be underreported in questionnaire‐based surveys [32].

Fourth, smoking exposure was assessed only at baseline, and longitudinal changes in smoking behavior during follow‐up were unavailable. Smoking cessation, relapse, or changes in smoking intensity may therefore have resulted in nondifferential exposure misclassification and attenuation of the observed associations [10, 11].

Nevertheless, this study has several important strengths. The analysis was based on a very large nationwide cohort of young adults derived from the NHIS database, enabling comprehensive longitudinal follow‐up in a relatively understudied age group. In addition, repeated nationwide health screening examinations allowed population‐level longitudinal assessment of FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis according to multiple smoking exposure metrics, including smoking amount, duration, and cumulative pack‐years. An additional strength of this study is the use of a nationwide health screening database rather than healthcare utilization–based diagnostic records alone.

In young adults, clinically recognized fatty liver disease may be substantially under‐ascertained because many individuals remain asymptomatic and have relatively low rates of healthcare utilization. Consequently, studies relying solely on ICD‐coded diagnoses may preferentially capture individuals who seek medical attention and undergo diagnostic evaluation.

By contrast, the NHIS health screening program provides population‐level assessments regardless of healthcare‐seeking behavior, enabling the identification of steatotic phenotypes in otherwise healthy or asymptomatic individuals. This feature is particularly valuable in young adult populations, in whom clinically diagnosed liver disease represents only a fraction of the underlying disease burden [33].

In conclusion, cigarette smoking was associated with increased risk of incident FLI‐defined hepatic steatosis among young adults, independent of obesity and alcohol intake profiles. These findings underscore the importance of smoking prevention and cessation strategies during early adulthood and support further longitudinal investigations incorporating imaging‐based outcomes, cardiometabolic risk profiling, and time‐varying exposure assessment.
