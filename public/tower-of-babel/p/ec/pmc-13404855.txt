Protocol for the Parent Anxiety Transmission and Habits (PATH) study: a randomised controlled trial testing parent interpretation bias as a key mechanism of intergenerational transmission of anxiety
Authors: Margaret A Chiffer, Daniel P Dickstein, Roni Itkin-Ofer, Jennie M Kuckertz, Boyu Ren, Doah E Shin, Alexandra L Silverman, Christian A Webb, Courtney Beard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnxiety disorders are the most common youth mental health problems in the USA. Most children do not receive treatment and among the few who do, many do not experience significant improvement. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel intervention targets to improve treatments for childhood anxiety. Past research demonstrates that parent anxiety plays a role in the development and maintenance of child anxiety, but empirical evidence for the mechanisms that drive this transmission is lacking. Interpretation bias, or the tendency to perceive threat in ambiguous situations, may lead parents to engage in anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours, which may transfer this cognitive bias to their children, resulting in youth anxiety. Given the current lack of empirical evidence supporting this theorised mechanism, the present study will test effects of parent interpretation bias on parent behaviour and child interpretation bias. Our central hypothesis is that parent interpretation bias influences child interpretation bias through its effects on anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours. The study will also evaluate potential moderators of these theorised relationships, such as parent sex, race and ethnicity, parent anxiety, child age and child sex.Methods and analysisWe will randomise 300 parents of children aged 7–12 to a smartphone app-delivered interpretation bias manipulation condition or a self-assessment control condition. Through a series of study assessments over the course of 3 months, parents and children will complete self-report, interview, behavioural and daily diary measures to assess the primary proximal outcome (anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours), the secondary distal outcome (child interpretation bias) and moderators. To test our central hypothesis, we will first use hierarchical linear modelling to assess whether parents in the interpretation bias manipulation condition show fewer anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours compared with control, and whether children of those parents show improved interpretation bias compared with control. We will then implement mediation models to evaluate whether a reduction in anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours mediates the association between parent and child interpretation bias.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this study was granted by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (2022P003245). Introduction

Anxiety disorders are the most common youth mental health problems in the USA, affecting an estimated 15%–32% of children.1 2 Children with anxiety disorders are at risk for serious, adverse long-term mental health outcomes, including suicide and substance use, with substantial functional impairment,3–5 as well as significant financial costs at the individual, familial and societal levels.6 Despite the staggering need, some estimate that only 18% of anxious youths receive any form of treatment.7 Of the few children who do receive treatment, approximately half of them do not experience significant improvement,8 9 indicating limited effectiveness of current treatments for youth anxiety. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel intervention targets to improve treatments for childhood anxiety.

Theoretical models of childhood anxiety highlight the critical role of parent interpretation bias in the development of youth anxiety disorders.10–12 Interpretation bias is the tendency to perceive threat in ambiguous situations.13 Greater interpretation bias may lead parents to underestimate their child’s ability to cope and thus engage in anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours, such as accommodation and overcontrol, which may transfer this cognitive bias to their children, promoting youth anxiety.11 Therefore, how parents interpret ambiguous situations may be a key mechanism underlying the intergenerational transmission of anxiety through the specific pathway of anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours.

Prior studies show that parent interpretation bias is associated with child interpretation bias14–17 and child anxiety.18 In addition, empirical data indicate that anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours, such as modelling fear responses and restricting child autonomy, play a role in the development of child anxiety.19 However, studies testing the link between parent interpretation bias and anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours are lacking. Only one published study has manipulated parent (N=18) interpretation bias in the context of youth anxiety, specifically social anxiety, which resulted in the expected changes in parent and child cognitive bias and social anxiety symptoms.20 To our knowledge, beyond this small pilot study, no other studies have manipulated parent interpretation bias to test the hypothesised role of parent interpretation bias in the intergenerational transmission of anxiety through anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours. Therefore, we do not yet understand how parent interpretation bias potentiates child interpretation bias and anxiety, which is a significant deficit in the current literature.

Moreover, most of what we know about the intergenerational transmission of interpretation bias and anxiety is based primarily on non-Hispanic white mothers. The few studies that have included fathers suggest that paternal behaviour may be more strongly associated with child interpretation bias and anxiety than maternal behaviour.21 22 In addition, cultural differences may be more likely to impact specific paths, such as the link between parenting behaviour and cognitive bias, but not other pathways, such as the link between child cognitive bias and anxiety.23 Indeed, one study in Latino families found that specific parenting behaviours (overcontrol) and child interpretation bias predicted child anxiety, supporting some links in the model in this population.23 However, low parental acceptance/warmth was not related to child anxiety, suggesting a potential cultural difference.23 Beyond this small group of studies, we know little about the effects of sociocultural factors on the theorised transmission of interpretation bias and anxiety from parent to child, which is another significant deficit in the current literature.

Present study

To address the current gaps in knowledge of the behavioural mechanisms of intergenerational anxiety transmission, the present study will investigate the influence of parent interpretation bias on child interpretation bias through its effects on anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours. Figure 1 summarises the study concept and research aims. The specific research aims are to: (1) manipulate parent interpretation bias to test theorised effects of parent interpretation bias on parent behaviour; (2) test theorised effects of parent interpretation bias on child interpretation bias; and (3) evaluate potential moderators of these theorised relationships, such as parent sex, race and ethnicity, parent anxiety, child age and child sex, to refine models of intergenerational anxiety transmission and inform future personalised interventions, which could include adapting treatment approaches based on parent and child anxiety severity, worry domains and demographic characteristics.

Study concept and research aims. WSAP, Word Sentence Association Paradigm.

We hypothesise that parents in the interpretation bias manipulation condition will demonstrate fewer anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours compared with the control. In addition, we predict that children of parents in the interpretation bias manipulation condition will show improved interpretation bias compared with children of parents in the control condition, and that a reduction in anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours will mediate the association between parent interpretation bias improvement and child interpretation bias. Further, we predict that parent baseline anxiety will moderate the relationship between parent interpretation bias and parent behaviour such that effects of interpretation bias manipulation on parent behaviour will be stronger in parents with higher baseline anxiety. We also hypothesise that parent sex will moderate the relationship between parent interpretation bias and child interpretation bias such that effects on child interpretation bias will be stronger in father-child dyads compared with mother-child dyads.

Methods and analysis

This study’s central methodology is to randomise 300 parents of children aged 7–12 to a smartphone app-delivered interpretation bias manipulation condition or a self-assessment control condition. Through a series of study assessments over the course of 3 months, parents and children will complete measures to assess the primary proximal outcome (anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours), the secondary distal outcome (child interpretation bias), moderators and sample characteristics.

All study procedures have been reviewed and approved by the Mass General Brigham (MGB) Institutional Review Board (2022P003245). All parents will provide verbal and written (electronic signature) informed adult consent and informed parent/guardian consent and all children will provide verbal informed assent. Recruitment for this study is active; it began in July 2023 and will continue through December 2027.

Participants

Sample size and power analysis

We determined the study sample size based on the N required to achieve 80% power to detect mediation based on effect sizes (r=0.15–0.38) published in the literature.14 15 17 24 25 We entered the average effect size for each hypothesised path of the mediation model (ie, representing the correlation coefficients between X→Y, X→M (mediator) and M→Y) into the Monte Carlo Power Analysis for Indirect Effects, running 5000 replications and 20 000 Monte Carlo draws per replication.26 We simulated the longitudinal data of child interpretation bias and anxiety-promoting parenting behaviours directly for both treatment arms based on hypothesised effect sizes of the paths and the expected rate of change in interpretation bias and parenting behaviour. Using the simulated data, we estimated the subject-specific slopes and pre-post changes from the mixed effects models. A test of the mediation effect was performed based on these estimates with the R package Mediation.27 The statistical power of a specific sample size was then estimated with 1000 replications of this updated simulation. The power analysis revealed that at least 283 subjects are needed to achieve 80% power with an alpha set at 0.05. A total sample of N=300, which accounts for dropout, will safely guarantee a power of 0.80 with these assumptions and ensure that we are adequately powered to detect associations of clinical importance. Distributions of the moderator will have a strong impact on the power of the proposed moderator analysis. With a sample size of 283 and assuming the interaction effect size is 50%–80% of the magnitude of the effect of parent interpretation bias on parent behaviour, the resulting power curves show that if the under-representative group (eg, fathers and parents of colour) is at least 30% of the sample, the power of the moderator tests will range from greater than 0.5 to greater than 0.9 across the range of hypothesised effect sizes. Thus, we will specifically aim to recruit 30% fathers and 30% parents of colour to increase power to detect moderation.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for both parent and child participants will be: (1) fluency in English to comprehend study procedures, (2) residence in the state of Massachusetts, (3) no history of mania or, for parents only, mania symptoms experienced more than 1 year ago, (4) no history of psychosis, (5) no changes to mental health treatment status for at least 8 weeks prior to enrolment and (6) no active suicidal ideation or suicidal behaviours within the past month.

Parent-specific inclusion criteria will be: (1) being 18+ years old, (2) having shared or full custody of their child due to daily measures assessing parenting behaviours over the course of a 28-day intervention period, (3) owning an Android or iOS smartphone, (4) having at least mild anxiety symptoms, as defined by a Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) score of at least 5,28 (5) having at least a mild level of interpretation bias due to the experimental manipulation seeking to reduce interpretation bias associated with parent anxiety, as defined by a Word Sentence Association Paradigm (WSAP) accuracy score below the healthy norm of 70%29 and (6) never having participated in a prior clinical trial on parent interpretation bias conducted by members of this study team.

Child-specific inclusion criteria will be: (1) being 7–12 years old at the time of study enrolment, (2) having no degree of intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder that would prevent informed assent or the understanding and completion of study procedures, per parent or clinician report and (3) a Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence Second Edition Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient Two-Subtest form (consisting of the vocabulary and matrix reasoning subtests) score of at least 80 (low average) to ensure understanding of study procedures.30 The specific child age range for this study was selected for several reasons. First, almost all prior work testing associations between parent and child cognitive bias or anxiety has used this age range, and parenting behaviours have shown stronger links to child anxiety compared with adolescents.31 Second, children younger than 7 years old will be excluded because some study assessments (eg, speech task) are not intended for use with younger children. Third, children older than 12 will be excluded because the adolescent development period introduces different influences on anxiety.

Recruitment

We will recruit participants from a variety of sources, including through MGB’s study recruitment website (Rally), medical records, social media sites (eg, Instagram and Facebook), ResearchMatch, partnerships with community organisations (eg, mental health clinics, childcare centres, family resource centres and parenting groups), flyers and in-person recruitment events (eg, farmers’ markets and family fun days).

We will seek a range of anxiety severity and functional impairment in parent participants. Therefore, we will recruit our sample across the range of functional impairment and levels of care, including psychiatric hospital patients, outpatients and community referrals, rather than recruiting based on DSM diagnosis. This distribution will allow for tests of moderation by baseline parent anxiety severity and functional impairment. We will examine the distribution of baseline scores for parent anxiety severity and functional impairment bi-annually to ensure that the scores are normally distributed, and if not, address any imbalance. For example, if we find that we are missing parents at the severe level of functional impairment, we will focus recruitment efforts on mental health clinics. On the other hand, if we find we are missing parents at the lower end of functional impairment, we will focus efforts on social media.

Experimental arms

HabitWorks app interpretation bias manipulation (HW condition)

The interpretation bias manipulation in this study is an intervention version of the WSAP29 delivered via the HabitWorks (HW) smartphone app.32 33 Only parents who are randomly assigned to the HW condition will complete the intervention version of the WSAP through exercises on the HW app.

In a single trial of the WSAP intervention, users see a fixation cross for 500 ms, followed by a priming word for 500 ms that reflects a benign or threatening interpretation (eg, ‘funny’ or ‘embarrassing’), followed by an ambiguous sentence (eg, ‘people laugh after something you said’), and users then must select whether the word and sentence are related or not.29 In the intervention version of this task, corrective feedback (‘Correct!’ or ‘Let’s try again’) is provided to encourage benign interpretations.32 Stimuli will target parent biases for self-relevant and child-relevant situations. Parents in the HW condition will complete 50 trials in each exercise (~5 min per session) and will be prompted to complete three exercises per week (12 total over 4 weeks) via the HW app.

The situations presented in the intervention version of the WSAP are personalised via brief checklists, including 8 demographic and 28 anxiety/worry domains. Parents may re-personalise at any time via the HW app dashboard. Each word-sentence pair in the WSAP intervention is coded according to what demographic and worry domains it reflects. A subset of word-sentence pairs (out of a pool of 800) is generated by matching the checklist of each user to the codes assigned to each word-sentence pair. For example, if the user reported worrying about individual social interaction and their child’s safety, and indicated that they are employed, a custom subset of word-sentence pairs is generated to include all pairs with combinations of those codes. Thus, parents in the HW condition will only see ambiguous situations that are relevant to their current worries and life situations. This personalisation process/algorithm has been successfully used in two prior studies of the app. Overall, the intervention version of the WSAP delivered via the HW app incorporates repetitive and quick practice, increasing individuals’ awareness of their cognitive biases and shifting their automatic responses. It enhances generalisation by including a range of real-life situations that are personally relevant, asking users to imagine themselves in each situation.

In addition to using the HW app, parents in the HW condition will also complete the following self-assessment measures: daily diary parenting behaviour surveys and weekly anxiety and depression symptom surveys.

Self-assessment control (SA condition)

We selected a self-assessment (SA) condition to control for time, attention from researchers, expectancy effects of any changes in parenting behaviour from practicing self-assessment, and use of an online or smartphone intervention. Parents in the SA condition will only complete daily diary parenting behaviour surveys and weekly anxiety and depression symptom surveys. Unlike parents in the HW condition, they will not complete the intervention version of the WSAP delivered via the HW app. Our pilot studies suggest that participants find this condition acceptable and will adhere to surveys.33

Procedure

Parents and children will participate in this study for about 3 months, with five assessments over the course of that time. Figure 2 shows the assessment timeline. Clinical research assistants (RAs) who hold bachelor’s degrees will run all assessments and administer all measures. Parents will have the option to complete assessments remotely via secure, HIPAA-compliant Zoom meetings or in person at McLean Hospital. Parents will receive a reminder email 1 week prior to each assessment with meeting access instructions (either a Zoom link or directions to McLean Hospital), and a reminder call 1 day before each assessment from an RA.

Study assessment timeline. HW condition, HabitWorks condition; SA condition, self-assessment condition; WSAP, Word Sentence Association Paradigm.

Participants may voluntarily withdraw from the study at any time. In addition, if, over the course of the study, a participant’s condition deteriorates significantly (eg, the participant reports active suicidal ideation), the participant will be removed from the study and directed to relevant resources by the study team. The research team will continuously monitor participant safety via self-report measures (see the Measures section) and at all points of contact throughout the study.

In addition, given the number of assessments, measures and tasks that parent and child participants will complete over the 3-month course of the study, we will take a number of steps to minimise and monitor participant burden through the study. For instance, with participant burden in mind, we have intentionally chosen measures that are brief by design (eg, diagnostic interviews that take 30 min or less to administer rather than 2 hours). The study intervention procedures were also designed not to be onerous (5-minute survey completion; 5-minute app-based exercise completion). Further, study assessments will be structured so participants have planned breaks throughout (eg, child has a break while parent completes measures and vice versa). RAs will also remind participants that they are welcome to take additional breaks at any time during assessments. RAs will monitor participant burden throughout the study and discuss with the principal investigator (PI) during weekly meetings. Should any concerns arise with specific parent-child dyads, the study team will take steps to further reduce participant burden (eg, skipping a sample characteristics measure if a child is tired).

Screening (10 min)

Parents who are interested in the study will complete a brief online screening survey via REDCap34 to assess eligibility. Parents will answer questions about themselves and their child and complete the GAD-7 and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) item 9.28 35 Parents with a GAD-7 score ≥5 and a PHQ-9 item 9 ≤1, and whose responses meet all other eligibility criteria, will be directed to complete a brief, online, interpretation bias screening measure (ie, a standard assessment version of the WSAP, which is not personalised and does not provide accuracy feedback).29 Parents with a WSAP accuracy score <70% will be eligible. An RA will review screeners immediately as parents complete them and will email those who are eligible to schedule their baseline assessment.

Baseline assessment (2 hours)

The baseline assessment will take place within 2 weeks of screening. Parent-child dyads will meet with an RA to learn about the study, decide to enrol and confirm eligibility. Parents will provide verbal and written (electronic signature) informed adult consent and informed parent/guardian consent, and children will provide verbal informed child assent (30 min). Then, the RA will administer baseline measures to the parent and the child individually (30 min each), and a diagnostic interview with the parent to evaluate parent psychiatric conditions (30 min; see the Measures section).

Randomisation

After the baseline assessment, the PI will randomly assign parents to the HW or SA condition using random number generation according to a 50:50 ratio. To ensure that both experimental conditions are similar on key baseline characteristics that will be tested as moderators, we will stratify parents based on anxiety severity at screening (mild to moderate: GAD-7 score of 5–14; severe: GAD-7 score ≥15) and interpretation bias at screening (high accuracy: WSAP accuracy 51%–69%; low accuracy: WSAP accuracy ≤50%). Only the PI and RAs will have access to the randomisation log.

Preintervention assessment (2 hours)

The preintervention assessment will take place 1–4 weeks after the baseline assessment. Parent-child dyads will meet with an RA to complete preintervention measures and behavioural tasks (see the Measures section). First, the RA will administer the preintervention measures to the parent and child individually (30 min each). Then, the parent and child will complete two behavioural tasks together: a speech task and an Etch-A-Sketch task (30 min). Finally, the RA will meet with the parent individually to onboard them to the intervention phase of the study (30 min). During this time, the RA will review the self-assessment (ie, daily diary and weekly symptom survey) procedures for both arms and help parents assigned to the HW condition to download the HW app.

Intervention phase (4 weeks)

The intervention phase will begin immediately after the preintervention assessment. Parents in both the HW and SA conditions will complete daily diary parenting behaviour surveys (5 min) online via REDCap for 28 days, which they will receive via email each evening at 19:00 Eastern Time and have 5 hours to complete. Parents in both conditions will also complete weekly symptom surveys (5 min; GAD-7 and PHQ-9; see the Measures section) during the intervention phase, which they will have 24 hours to complete each week. Parents in the SA condition will complete their weekly symptom surveys online via REDCap, accessed via phone or computer, and parents in the HW condition will complete their weekly symptom surveys in the HW app.

While all parents will complete daily diary parenting behaviour surveys and weekly symptom surveys, only parents in the HW condition will complete the intervention version of the WSAP delivered via the HW app. The HW app will prompt these parents to complete three WSAP exercises per week (~5 min per session) at times of their choosing. To improve intervention adherence, all parents, regardless of condition, will receive weekly email reminders to complete intervention procedures.

Mid-Intervention assessment (30 min)

The mid-intervention assessment will take place 2 weeks after the intervention phase begins. Parents will complete a series of mid-intervention measures online via REDCap. After they receive the link to the surveys via email, they will have 1 week to complete them.

Postintervention assessment (2.5 hours)

The postintervention assessment will take place at the conclusion of the 4-week intervention phase (2 weeks after the mid-intervention assessment), and there will be a 1-week window to complete it. Parent-child dyads will meet with an RA to complete postintervention measures and two behavioural tasks. First, the RA will administer the postintervention measures to the parent and child individually (30 min each). Then, the parent and child will complete a speech task and an Etch-A-Sketch task together (30 min). Finally, the RA will conduct a diagnostic interview (both parent and child report; 30 min each) to evaluate the child’s psychiatric conditions.

Follow-up period (4 weeks)

The follow-up period will begin immediately after the postintervention assessment. Parents and children will have no study procedures to complete during this time. However, during the follow-up period, parents will receive an email with their return of results and mental health resources. The return of results will include a summary of how parent anxiety and depression symptoms, child anxiety symptoms and parenting behaviours changed from the preintervention assessment to the postintervention assessment. The return of results will be accompanied by mental health information sheets corresponding to any psychiatric conditions for which the parent and/or child met criteria in their respective diagnostic interviews. All parents will also receive a link to the resources page on the study’s website, which includes information about mental health resources and other supports, such as food, housing and financial assistance programmes.

Follow-up assessment (30 min)

The follow-up assessment will take place 4 weeks after the post-intervention assessment, and there will be a 1-week window to complete it. The purpose of the follow-up assessment is to explore the relationships of interest between parent and child interpretation bias and anxiety over a longer period of time. Parents will asynchronously complete a series of follow-up measures online via REDCap. Children will meet with an RA to complete their follow-up measures synchronously. After the follow-up assessment, participation in the study will end.

Compensation

Parents will receive a maximum of US$320 for their participation in the study, as follows: $50 for the baseline assessment, $50 for the preintervention assessment, $20 for the mid-intervention assessment, $100 for the postintervention assessment and $20 for the follow-up assessment. During the intervention phase, parents will also receive $10 for each week in which they complete at least one daily diary parenting behaviour survey, and an additional $10 if they complete at least 70% of the daily diary parenting behaviour surveys in a week, for a total possible of $80 during the intervention phase of the study. Children will receive a maximum of $40 paid via gift cards for their participation in the study, a $10 gift card for each study assessment they complete.

Primary proximal outcome

Behavioural tasks

We selected two behavioural assessments, a speech task36 and an online Etch-A-Sketch task,37 to elicit anxiety-promoting parent behaviours depending on parents’ interpretation of the ambiguous situation. Parents and children will complete both behavioural tasks at the preintervention and postintervention assessments. For the speech task, children will prepare and give a short presentation for 3–5 min on a topic they choose from a list: my hobbies, my ideal day, my family or my favourite holiday. The parent and child will have 5 min to prepare together. After a brief introduction from the parent, the child will then give the speech without parent involvement, but parents will be told that if help is needed, they may decide what is appropriate. Next, parents and children will use the Etch-A-Sketch Online (ESO) website to work together to copy two drawings, a rectangle with an X through it and a house. Parents use the O and M keys to draw lines that go up and down, and children use the A and D keys to draw lines that go side to side. Parent-child dyads will have 8 min total to try copying both drawings, and they may not use each other’s keys. These tasks have been extensively used with parent-child dyads in this study’s age range.

Both behavioural tasks will be audio- and video-recorded. Trained coders (who are not RAs), masked to condition (ie, no access to randomisation log), will then code parenting behaviours during the tasks. Coders will never be unblinded until they are done coding and complete data entry. RAs will not conduct behavioural coding because they will have access to the randomisation log and run study assessments and will therefore not be masked to condition. New coders will be trained by experienced coders. Training will include reviewing background literature and the coding manual, discussing the coding process with supervisors and coding at least six training videos. To pass training and move on to independent coding, coders must achieve 83.33% reliability. Coders will meet quarterly to check inter-rater reliability. Six specific parenting behaviours will be coded: warmth, encouragement, overprotection and intrusiveness, promotion of avoidance, expressed anxiety and threat augmentation. Parenting behaviours will be coded during the 5 min speech preparation period and the 8 min ESO completion period. We will follow the coding protocol developed by Creswell and colleagues, and we will adapt the protocol to include examples relevant to the ESO task.36

Family Accommodation Scale-Anxiety (FASA)

The Family Accommodation Scale-Anxiety (FASA) is a 13-item, self-report measure evaluating frequency of modification of family routines/schedules due to child anxiety and participation in child anxiety symptoms over the past month.38 Parents will complete the FASA at the preintervention, mid-intervention, postintervention and follow-up assessments.

Egna Minnen Betraffande Uppfostran Parent-Report Short Form (s-EMBU-P)

The Egna Minnen Betraffande Uppfostran Parent-Report Short Form (s-EMBU-P) is a 23-item, self-report measure evaluating parenting behaviours in the domains of rejection, emotional warmth and overprotection.39 Parents will complete the s-EMBU-P at the preintervention, mid-intervention, postintervention and follow-up assessments.

Daily Diary Parenting Behaviour Surveys

We will create a daily diary, self-report measure of parenting behaviours comprising the FASA and items from the s-EMBU-P each day during the intervention phase of the study (28 days total). The prompt will ask about behaviours that occurred during the past day, and items will be slightly modified to reflect a 24-hour period.

Secondary distal outcome

Behavioural Task Evaluation-Child Report

Like prior studies,10 20 36 before and after each behavioural task at the preintervention and postintervention assessments, children will report their anxiety around the task (‘How do/did you feel about doing this task?’, 0—not scared/worried at all to 10—extremely scared/worried), perceived performance (‘How well do you think you will do/did this task?’, 0—not well at all to 10—extremely well), and the amount of help needed to do the task (‘How much help do you think you will/did you need to do this task?’ 0—none at all to 10—a lot).

Ambiguous Scenario Questionnaire-Child Report

The Ambiguous Scenario Questionnaire (ASQ) is a self-report measure comprising 12 ambiguous scenarios and two possible interpretations.40 41 Responses are coded dichotomously as ‘threat’ or ‘non-threat’, and total scores range from 0 to 12. This widely used task has adequate internal consistency.36 Children will complete the version of the ASQ with child-relevant scenarios as a secondary measure of child interpretation bias at the preintervention, postintervention and follow-up assessments.

Manipulation check

As a manipulation check, parents will complete the adult version of the ASQ (ASQ-A) at the preintervention, mid-intervention, postintervention and follow-up assessments.40 41 In addition, before and after each behavioural task at the preintervention and postintervention assessments, parents will rate their own anxiety around the task, their child’s task performance and how much they needed to help their child, with all questions on a 10-point scale. Parents will also report how they think their child responded to the anxiety, performance and help questions.

Moderators and sample characteristics

Additional measures will be administered throughout the study to assess moderators and sample characteristics. For a full list of these measures and the assessments at which participants will complete them, see table 1.

Data analysis plan

All study data will be collected, entered and stored via secure, HIPAA-compliant research applications. To promote data quality, we will follow a double data entry procedure. In addition, we will perform quarterly data quality checks.

Aim 1: effect of parent interpretation bias on parent behaviour

Given the nested (hierarchical) structure of the data (ie, repeated assessments nested within individuals), we will use hierarchical linear modelling (HLM) and test group×time interactions, specifying random intercept and slope. The primary outcome is observer coding of parenting behaviours (average of the relevant behaviours across the speech and ESO tasks), and the secondary outcome is daily diary parenting behaviour surveys (28 timepoints). For the primary outcome, positive behaviours (warmth and encouragement) will be analysed separately from negative behaviours (overprotection and intrusiveness, promotion of avoidance, expressed anxiety and threat augmentation). For daily diary surveys, items from the FASA and s-EMBU-P scales will be analysed separately. Multiple testing will be addressed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate.42

Aim 2: effect of parent interpretation bias on child interpretation bias

We will conduct similar HLM analyses to test group×time interactions, with child ratings of their expected performance on the tasks as the primary outcome. We will examine child ratings on the ASQ-C as a secondary, self-report outcome. Mediation analyses will be conducted using the R package Mediation.27 Within the mediation models, the independent variable (X) will be experimental condition, and the dependent variable (Y) will be the subject-specific slope of change in child interpretation bias (assessed via child report of expectations during behavioural assessments) from baseline to post (analyses with the secondary measure, the ASQ-C, will also be conducted). The mediator (M) is the change in parenting behaviours from preintervention assessment to postintervention assessment (assessed via behavioural tasks and daily diary parenting behaviour surveys).

Aim 3: evaluation of moderators

Moderators will be tested via elastic net regularisation (ENR) using the glmnet package in R43 by modelling group×time×moderator variable interactions (simultaneously in a single model). Tuning of ENR’s alpha and lambda parameters will be performed using the CARET package’s resampling grid search.44 The 10-fold ENR models will be repeated 100 times to generate stable estimates of interaction effects.

Missing data

We will use multiple imputation based on chained equations to impute missing data and perform sensitivity analysis to examine the impact of different missingness assumptions on the results. We will account for potentially informative dropout using inverse probability of censoring weights procedures.45

Ethics and dissemination

As stated previously, ethical approval for this study has been granted by the MGB Institutional Review Board (2022P003245), and the PI has registered the study on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05665491) as of 19 December 2022. Any protocol modifications will be submitted to and approved by the MGB Institutional Review Board and communicated to relevant parties as necessary. All personally identifiable information about potential and enrolled participants will be kept completely confidential and will be collected and stored via secure, HIPAA-compliant research applications. Data will not be shared in any individually identifiable form, unless required by law.

The PI will monitor all aspects of this study via weekly research team meetings. Oversight of this study will also be provided by an Independent Safety Monitor (ISM) who will review cumulative safety monitoring and study progress reports (including participant confidentiality, participant eligibility, recruitment, retention, data quality and management) on an annual basis and as needed. The ISM will evaluate whether the trial meets stopping criteria. Given that this study is testing mechanisms and moderators, rather than intervention efficacy, there is no need for an early efficacy or futility stopping rule. This study will be stopped prior to its completion if: (1) the intervention is associated with adverse effects that call into question the safety of the intervention; (2) difficulty in study recruitment or retention will significantly impact the ability to evaluate the study endpoints; (3) any new information becomes available during the trial that necessitates stopping the trial; or (4) other situations occur that might warrant stopping the trial.

We will comply with the registration and reporting requirements of Public Law 110-85 and in accordance with the NIH Policy on the Dissemination of NIH-Funded Clinical Trial Information (NOT-OD-16-149). We will follow all the relevant guidelines and timelines to ensure that results are efficiently and transparently reported. All individual, de-identified participant data will be accessible by the end of the study via repositories such as the Open Science Framework. Results of this study will be shared with the scientific community through presentations of study data at national conferences (eg, annual meetings of the Association for Behavioral and Cognitive Therapies and the Anxiety and Depression Association of America), and publication of study results in peer-reviewed scientific journals. We will also share de-identified data via the National Clinical Trials Database, a part of the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Data Archive (NDA), as required by our funding agency, the NIMH. Discussion

This study will provide the most robust and comprehensive test of theorised intergenerational transmission of interpretation bias and anxiety to date, and data regarding potential moderators of these theorised associations will support theory refinement. Daily diary data may prove valuable for others’ research on parenting behaviour or to develop into a clinically useful tool. Our data-sharing plan will ensure such efforts multiply and spread, rather than being restricted to only our team. Future work built on this critical data can test whether WSAP-based interpretation bias treatment impacts clinical outcomes, such as child anxiety and functioning. Should this study find that targeting parent interpretation bias has downstream effects on child outcomes, future work could also test a parent intervention in a randomised controlled trial. Ultimately, this study can catalyse the development of mechanism-based interpretation bias treatments and facilitate a precision medicine approach for child anxiety with scalable methods. Given that only 18% of children in need of anxiety treatment receive it,7 a scalable, parent-only treatment approach may reduce the public health impact of both adult and child anxiety disorders.
