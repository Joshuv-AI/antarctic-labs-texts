LILRB3 inhibition reverses immunosuppression in glioma: a nanoparticle-based therapeutic strategy
Authors: Jiefu Yu, Xiangguo Cui, Guangcai Shao, Jiaxin Li, Qi Li, Qingyu Liang, Nu Li, Xiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioma is a highly aggressive brain tumor characterized by a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment dominated by M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). This study utilized machine learning and single-cell multi-omics technologies to investigate the role of LILRB3 in glioma immunosuppression and evaluated the therapeutic potential of BM@nano-siLILRB3. In a tumor-bearing mouse model, core immune cells and key genes in glioma progression were identified. In vitro studies revealed that silencing LILRB3 suppressed M2 phenotypes in macrophages and inhibited glioma cell proliferation and invasion. In vivo experiments confirmed that LILRB3 promoted TAM M2 polarization, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment and accelerating glioma progression. The BM@nano-siLILRB3 nanoplatform effectively targeted the BBB, suppressed LILRB3 expression, reprogrammed immune responses, and led to substantial tumor growth inhibition. Overall, targeting LILRB3 with BM@nano-siLILRB3 shows promise in mitigating immunosuppression and hindering glioma progression, providing a novel therapeutic avenue for glioma treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04287-4.

Introduction

Glioma is among the most common malignant tumors of the central nervous system and comprises diverse pathological subtypes, including astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, and glioblastoma [1–3]. The treatment of glioma, particularly high-grade tumors such as glioblastoma, remains challenging because of high recurrence rates and poor survival outcomes [4]. Although therapeutic strategies including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy have advanced in recent years, the overall prognosis of glioma patients remains unsatisfactory [5–7]. The five-year survival rate for glioma patients is low, partly due to the tumor’s high invasiveness and post-treatment recurrence [8–10]. Furthermore, pronounced cellular heterogeneity and a complex tumor microenvironment (TME) further complicate glioma progression and therapeutic resistance, making it a major focus and challenge in neuro-oncology research [11].

The TME plays a crucial role in tumor development and progression, especially the composition of the immune microenvironment, which profoundly impacts tumor behavior [12–14]. In glioma, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent a dominant immune cell population and exhibit dual functions in tumor growth, invasion, and immune evasion [12, 15, 16]. Notably, M2-polarized macrophages are key drivers of immunosuppression, secreting anti-inflammatory and immune-inhibitory factors that facilitate tumor escape from host immune surveillance [17–19]. Therefore, elucidating the roles and regulatory mechanisms of M2 macrophages in glioma is critical for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

With rapid advances in biotechnology, single-cell multi-omics and machine-learning approaches have become powerful tools for studying tumor biology [20]. Single-cell sequencing enables high-resolution characterization of cellular heterogeneity within tumors and their microenvironment, revealing distinct cell types and functional states [21]. Concurrently, machine-learning methods are particularly effective in analyzing large-scale biological data, facilitating the identification of potential biomarkers and disease mechanisms from complex datasets [22–24]. Together, these technologies not only enhance our understanding of the dynamic glioma microenvironment but also provide new opportunities for discovering therapeutic targets [25].

Among immune regulatory molecules, LILRB3 is an immune checkpoint that has been increasingly implicated in multiple cancers and plays an important role in regulating immune responses [26, 27]. By interacting with its ligands, LILRB3 influences macrophage polarization, particularly by promoting the M2 phenotype, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive TME [28, 29]. Therefore, investigating the role and mechanism of LILRB3 in glioma may provide a theoretical basis for developing new immunoregulatory strategies [26, 29].

Although mechanistic studies have established the critical role of LILRB3 in tumor immunosuppression, the effective delivery of molecular therapeutics such as siRNA into the brain, particularly across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and into the tumor immune microenvironment, remains a major challenge for clinical translation [13, 30]. In recent years, biomimetic nanoplatforms have emerged as a promising approach to overcome this barrier, owing to their advantages in immune camouflage, BBB penetration, and targeted delivery.

This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which LILRB3 drives M2 macrophage polarization within the immunosuppressive glioma microenvironment by integrating single-cell multi-omics and machine-learning approaches. By systematically investigating the function of LILRB3, we sought to clarify its contribution to glioma progression and evaluate its potential as a therapeutic target. We hypothesized that LILRB3 and its downstream signaling pathways play pivotal roles in shaping the immunosuppressive milieu of glioma, thereby promoting tumor development and therapeutic resistance. Furthermore, building on the mechanistic findings, we introduced a microglia-mimicking biomimetic nanoplatform, biomimetic angiopep-2-modified nanoparticle loaded with siRNA targeting LILRB3 (BM@nano-siLILRB3), designed to achieve BBB-crossing and targeted silencing of LILRB3, thereby validating its therapeutic potential and offering a novel avenue for clinical translation. Collectively, this work offers both mechanistic insight and a novel therapeutic framework, with potential implications for improving clinical outcomes in glioma patients.

Materials and methods

Single-cell sequencing data analysis

In this study, we performed single-cell sequencing analysis on tumor tissue and adjacent tissue samples from a glioma mouse model. Data were analyzed using the Seurat package in R. Cells were filtered according to the following criteria: nFeature_RNA > 500, nCount_RNA > 1,000, nCount_RNA < 20,000, and mitochondrial gene percentage (percent.mt) < 10. Batch effects were corrected using canonical correlation analysis (CCA), followed by data normalization with the LogNormalize method. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then conducted, and the top principal components were selected for t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)–based clustering. Cell-type annotation was performed using the SingleR package with the MouseRNAseqData reference dataset [31, 32].

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Bulk RNA sequencing was conducted on tumor and adjacent tissues from three glioma mouse models. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R, with thresholds of |logFC| > 1 and p < 0.05. Volcano plots were generated with ggplot2, and heatmaps of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were produced using the heatmap package [33, 34].

Functional enrichment analysis of DGEs

Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were carried out using the ClusterProfiler package in R, with p < 0.05 considered significant. GO analysis included biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC), enabling comprehensive functional characterization of candidate target genes [35].

LASSO regression analysis

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to identify key genes associated with glioma progression. The candidate gene expression matrix was analyzed using the glmnet package in R. Association analysis between LILRB3 expression and M2 macrophages

The expression levels of LILRB3 in glioma patients were analyzed using the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Furthermore, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) regulatory network linking LILRB3 and M2 macrophage marker genes was constructed using the GeneMania website.

Cell culture, macrophage polarization, and sh-RNA treatment

Human glioma cell lines (U87, LN229, U251), human monocytic cells (THP-1), murine glioma cells (GL261), microglial cells (BV2), and mouse brain microvascular endothelial cells (bEnd.3) were obtained from Zhejiang Meisen Cell Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). Glioma cells, BV2, and bEnd.3 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT), while THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, 61870127). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10099141 C) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122).

To induce M2 macrophage differentiation, THP-1 cells were seeded in six-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well and treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma, P1585) for 24 h to generate macrophages. Subsequently, cells were stimulated with 20 ng/mL IL-4 and 20 ng/mL IL-13 (PeproTech) for an additional 48 h to promote M2 polarization.

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting LILRB3 was cloned into the pGMLV-SC5 lentiviral vector (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China). Lentiviral particles were produced in 293T cells using the Lenti-HG Mix plasmid (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) and subsequently concentrated. The resulting lentivirus was added to M2 macrophages differentiated from THP-1 cells. After 48 h, transduced cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Merck, MA0318). The sequences of sh-RNA used were as follows: sh-LILRB3-1: GTGGAGGTTCACATGCTATTA; sh-LILRB3-2: GTCACAGCAAACACAGGACAT (predesigned shRNA); sh-NC: MISSION® pLKO.1-puro non-target shRNA control plasmid DNA (SHC016, Sigma, MISSION® pLKO.1-puro non-target shRNA control plasmid DNA targets no known genes from any species | Sigma-Aldrich).

The cell grouping in the cell proliferation, wound healing test, and invasion experiments included: (1) U87 glioma cells group, (2) U87 glioma cells + M2 macrophage-conditioned media group, (3) U87 glioma cells + sh-NC M2 macrophage-conditioned media group, and (4) U87 glioma cells + sh-LILRB3 M2 macrophage-conditioned media group [37–40].

Acquisition of conditioned medium (CM)

M2-polarized macrophages differentiated from THP-1 cells were seeded in 6-cm dishes (2 × 106 cells per dish) and transduced with the indicated lentiviruses as described above. After incubation, the culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged (500 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), followed by filtration through a 0.2-µm membrane. The resulting CM from different treatment groups were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent experiments [39].

Co-culture assay

To simulate the interaction between macrophages and glioma cells, an indirect Transwell co-culture system was established. M2-polarized macrophages treated with sh-NC or sh-LILRB3 (2.5 × 105 cells) were seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (5 μm pore size, Corning), while glioma cells (U87 or U251, 2.5 × 105 cells) were seeded into the lower chamber. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of co-culture, the inserts were removed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (G1061, Solarbio). Migrated macrophages were counted in three randomly selected fields under a microscope to evaluate their chemotactic migration toward glioma cells [41, 42].

Western blot (WB) assay

Cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, QPBCA). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) [43]. The membrane was blocked in 5% skim milk (BD Biosciences, USA) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-CD11B (Abcam, ab133357, 1:1000), anti-CD204 (Abcam, ab315834, 1:1000), anti-CD206 (Abcam, ab64693, 1:1000), anti-CD163 (Abcam, ab182422, 1:1000), anti-LILRB3 (Abcam, ab271287, 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (Abcam, ab8245, 1:500). After washing with 1× TBST (3 × 5 min), membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies—goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab6721, 1:2000) or goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, ab6728, 1:2000)—for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing with TBST (3 × 5 min). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34577) and imaged using a G-Box imaging system (Syngene, 680X, USA). Band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis, and target protein expression was normalized to GAPDH [43]. Each experiment was repeated three times for statistical reliability.

Real-time quantitative PCR experiment

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, R0016). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop One/OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific), and only samples with A260/A280 ≈ 2.0 and concentration > 5 µg/µL were used. Reverse transcription was performed using a cDNA synthesis kit (CW2569M, Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Quantitative PCR was carried out on a CFX-Touch 96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Primer sequences were designed by Shanghai Genomic Institute and are listed in Table S1. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal reference [43]. The experiment was performed in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence staining experiment

Cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min), followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (15 min). After blocking with 5% BSA (1 h, room temperature), cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-LILRB3 antibody (Abcam, ab271287, 1:30). After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150160, 1:1000, 1 h, room temperature). Nuclei were counterstained using ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen, P36962). Fluorescent images were captured using a confocal microscope.

Paraffin-embedded mouse tissue sections were dewaxed and subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (Beyotime, P0083). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD206 (Abcam, ab64693, 1:1000) and LILRB3 overnight at 4 °C, followed by staining with Alexa Fluor® 488 goat anti-rabbit (Abcam, ab150157, 1:1000) and Alexa Fluor® 594 goat anti-mouse (Abcam, ab150160, 1:1000) secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence signals were visualized and imaged using a Leica fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to assess the distribution and expression of M2 macrophages in tumor tissues [44].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

M2-polarized macrophages differentiated from THP-1 cells were cultured for 24 h, after which cell-free supernatants were collected. The levels of IL-10 and TNF-α were measured using ELISA kits (Beyotime, PI528 and PT518). To further assess M2 polarization, the concentrations of TGF-β (KE1373), IL-1RA (KE1030), and CCL22 (KE1203) in the supernatants were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Immunoway) [45]. Cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated for each assay [46]. All samples were measured in triplicate.

Cell proliferation experiment

M2-polarized macrophages treated with lentivirus (untransfected, sh-NC, or sh-LILRB3) were used to generate CM, which was mixed with fresh culture medium at a 1:2 ratio. This mixture was applied to U87 glioma cells seeded in 96-well plates. Cell proliferation was first evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, HY-K0301) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 72 h of incubation, CCK-8 reagent was added and incubated for 1 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) to determine cell viability. To further assess proliferation, U87 cells were co-cultured with M2 macrophages for 48 h and analyzed using an EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (RiboBio, C10310-1). Cells were incubated with 200 µL EdU (37 °C, 2 h), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (20 min), and permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 (10 min). Subsequently, cells were incubated with Apollo® reagent (100 µL, 30 min), followed by nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst (30 min). Fluorescence images were captured using a Leica inverted fluorescence microscope. The proliferation rate was calculated as the percentage of EdU-positive (red) cells relative to the total number of Hoechst-positive (blue) nuclei [38, 47].

Wound healing assay

U87 glioma cells were co-cultured with M2 macrophage–CM in 96-well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well. When cell confluence reached approximately 90%, uniform scratches were generated using the IncuCyte system (Essen BioScience, USA). Cells were then washed and cultured in DMEM containing 1% FBS. Wound closure was monitored and imaged at 0, 24, and 48 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) [39].

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion was assessed using Transwell chambers (Corning, CLS3422; 8.0-µm pore size) pre-coated with Matrigel. U87 cells from different treatment groups were resuspended in DMEM containing 2% FBS at a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL, and 200 µL of the cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL DMEM containing 20% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed. The membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (5 min) and stained with crystal violet (5 min). Invaded cells were counted in randomly selected microscopic fields to evaluate invasive capacity [44]. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Establishing animal models

Male BALB/c nude mice (BALB/c nu/nu, 4–6 weeks old; Charles River Laboratories, USA) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. shRNA-treated M2 macrophages were mixed with GFP-labeled U87MG glioma cells (Zhejiang Meisen Cell Technology Co., Ltd., CTCC-0323-Luc1) at a 4:3 ratio, and the cell suspension was subcutaneously co-injected into nude mice to establish tumor-bearing models. Mice were randomly assigned to two groups: sh-NC and sh-LILRB3, with six mice per group. After implantation, mice were monitored for 2 weeks. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and approved protocols [46, 47].

Tumor growth monitoring and weighing

The distribution and growth of GFP-labeled tumor cells in nude mice were tracked and recorded using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, USA). At the experimental endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised and immediately weighed to assess tumor burden and growth [38].

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. H&E staining was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0105S). Histopathological changes were examined and imaged under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) [48].

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were sectioned at 3 μm thickness, baked at 65 °C for 30 min, and processed using an automated IHC staining system (BOND-RXm, Leica, Australia). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580, 5 µg/mL) and IL-6 (Abcam, ab290735, 1:100). Stained sections were imaged using a BX43 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and quantitative analysis was performed using the IHC Profiler plugin in ImageJ software [40].

Construction and surface modification of the BM@nano-siLILRB3

BV2 microglial cells were washed twice with PBS, collected by scraping, and resuspended in ice-cold PBS. Cells were lysed at 4 °C using a probe sonicator (Scientz-IID, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, China) at 200 W in an intermittent mode (2 s on / 3 s off, 3 min). The lysate was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min to remove nuclei and debris, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min to collect the membrane fraction. The membrane pellet was resuspended in PBS, concentrated using 100 kDa Amicon ultrafiltration tubes (Millipore, USA), and extruded 11 times through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane (Whatman, USA) to generate BV2 cell membrane–derived vesicles (BM vesicles).

Custom-designed siRNA targeting LILRB3 (siLILRB3; RiboBio, China) and the M2 macrophage–targeting cationic lipid DMG-PEG-M2pep (Xi’an Ruixi Biological Technology Co., China) were mixed in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) at a 1:5 mass ratio and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to allow self-assembly into nanocores (nano-siLILRB3). The hydrodynamic diameter of nano-siLILRB3 was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK) and was maintained at 120 ± 5 nm.

To fabricate BM@nano-siLILRB3, BM vesicles and nano-siLILRB3 were mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio and subjected to probe sonication (VCX130, Sonics, USA) at 30 W for five cycles (10 s per cycle with 5 s intervals) to induce membrane fusion and encapsulation. Subsequently, DSPE-PEG₂k-Angiopep-2 (AVT Pharmaceutical Tech, China) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with gentle stirring to enable surface insertion of the BBB-targeting peptide. The final nanoplatform was purified using 100 kDa ultrafiltration tubes to remove free components and resuspended in PBS, yielding the BM@nano-siLILRB3 formulation for subsequent experiments.

Characterization of BM@nano-siLILRB3

To characterize the structural and physicochemical properties of BM@nano-siLILRB3, BM vesicles, nano-siLILRB3, and BM@nano-siLILRB3 were deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids (Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., China), air-dried at room temperature for 10 min, and negatively stained with 2% sodium phosphotungstate (10 s). After blotting excess solution with filter paper, nanoparticle morphology and membrane coating were examined using a transmission electron microscope (TEM; HT7800, Hitachi, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of nanoparticles were measured using a DLS analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK). All samples were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes and analyzed at 25 °C. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± SD.

The coating efficiency of BM was evaluated via WB analysis of typical BM membrane protein markers, including CSF1R (ab254357, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), TMEM119 (ab210405, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), CX3CR1 (ab308613, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), and IBA1 (ab178847, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), with ATP1A1 (ab76020, 1:5000, Abcam, UK) used as the internal control to assess protein retention during membrane coating.

In vitro siRNA release kinetics assay

BM@nano-siLILRB3 containing 1.5 mg siRNA was dispersed in 400 µL PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator (100 rpm). At predetermined time points, samples were collected and centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 10 min) to separate the released siRNA from nanoparticles. The supernatant was further filtered using 300 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration centrifugal tubes to remove residual nanoparticles. The concentration of released siRNA in the supernatant was quantified using EvaGreen fluorescent dye (Biotium, USA), with excitation/emission wavelengths set at 485/528 nm. The cumulative release percentage was calculated based on the total siRNA loading amount. All experiments were performed in triplicate [49].

Nanoparticle stability evaluation

BM@nano-siLILRB3 was stored at 4 °C and 37 °C for 7 days, and its hydrodynamic diameter, PDI, and zeta potential were measured every 24 h using DLS (Malvern Instruments, UK). All samples were stored in PBS buffer and protected from light throughout the study.

In addition, BM@nano-siLILRB3 was dispersed in PBS containing 0.1% Solutol HS15 or in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, at a final concentration of 0.5 mg siRNA/mL. The samples were incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator (140 rpm) and collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days [50]. Particle size, PDI, and zeta potential were determined by DLS, while morphological changes on days 8 and 14 were further examined by TEM [49, 51].

Confocal microscopy for nanoparticle internalization and membrane fusion

BM vesicles were labeled with DiO (D275, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and siLILRB3 was labeled with DIR (RiboBio, China). Labeled BM@nano-siLILRB3 was incubated with GL261 cells for 4 h, followed by washing with PBS (3 times) and fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Cell Signaling Technology, #8961, 1:1000). The colocalization of green (DiO) and red (DIR) fluorescence was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; LSM 980, Zeiss, Germany) to evaluate nanoparticle internalization and membrane fusion.

Stability of siRNA in serum

Free siLILRB3 and BM@nano-siLILRB3 were separately incubated in serum-containing medium (10% FBS) at 37 °C for 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h. At each time point, samples were collected and RNA was extracted using the Trizol method (Invitrogen, USA). The integrity of siRNA was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Construction of the in vitro transwell-BBB model and permeability assay

To evaluate the BBB-penetrating ability of BM@nano-siLILRB3, an in vitro model was established using Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, Corning, USA). Brain microvascular endothelial cells bEnd.3 (ATCC, USA) were seeded in the upper chamber at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2, while GL261 cells were seeded in the lower chamber. Equal volumes of complete medium (high-glucose DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin/streptomycin) were added to both chambers. Co-culture was maintained for 5 days with daily medium replacement until the transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) reached ≥ 150 Ω·cm², indicating successful formation of a barrier.

For the permeability test, DiR-labeled Free siLILRB3, nano-siLILRB3, and BM@nano-siLILRB3 were added to the upper chamber at a final siRNA concentration of 100 nM. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, the medium from the lower chamber was collected, and DiR fluorescence (excitation 748 nm, emission 780 nm) was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200 PRO, Switzerland) to determine siRNA translocation efficiency across the BBB model.

Co-culture BBB model with M2-polarized macrophages

To verify the M2 macrophage-targeting capability of BM@nano-siLILRB3, a co-culture BBB model incorporating M2-polarized macrophages was established. BV2 cells were seeded into the lower chamber of a Transwell insert at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and treated with IL-4 and IL-13 (20 ng/mL each, PeproTech, USA) for 24 h to induce M2 polarization. Simultaneously, bEnd.3 endothelial cells (ATCC, USA) were seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2 to form a complete endothelial barrier.

After 5 days of co-culture, once the TEER value reached ≥ 150 Ω·cm², DiR-labeled Free siLILRB3, nano-siLILRB3, or BM@nano-siLILRB3 (final siRNA concentration 100 nM) was added to the upper chamber and incubated at 37 °C for 6 or 12 h. The inserts were then removed, and M2-polarized BV2 cells in the lower chamber were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min), followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (Cell Signaling Technology, #8961, 1:1000, 5 min). DiR fluorescence (Ex 748 nm/Em 780 nm) was visualized using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany), and intracellular fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) to assess nanoparticle uptake by M2 macrophages.

In vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging

DiR-labeled free siLILRB3, nano-siLILRB3, or BM@nano-siLILRB3 was intravenously administered to mice via the tail vein at a dose of 1 mg siRNA/kg. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed at 6, 9, 12, and 24 h post-injection using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA) to monitor biodistribution. At 6, 12, and 24 h, mice were euthanized and brains were harvested for ex vivo imaging. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer, USA) by measuring the total radiant efficiency within defined regions of interest (ROI), allowing comparison of brain accumulation among different formulations over time.

Immunofluorescence localization of microglia in brain tissue

Frozen brain Sect. (10 μm thickness) were air-dried at room temperature and permeabilized and blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 / 1% BSA (Gibco, USA) for 60 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-Iba1 primary antibody (Abcam, ab178847, 1:500). The red fluorescence corresponded to the DiR-labeled siLILRB3 signal. On the following day, sections were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab150077, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, #8961, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Colocalization was visualized using a confocal microscope (LSM 980, Zeiss, Germany).

Biosafety evaluation of BM@nano-siLILRB3

To assess the in vivo biocompatibility of BM@nano-siLILRB3, hemolysis assays, hematological and biochemical analyses, and histological evaluations of major organs were conducted. For the hemolysis assay, venous blood from C57BL/6 mice was anticoagulated with lithium heparin, and red blood cells were incubated with various concentrations of BM@nano-siLILRB3 (3.125–200 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 3 h. CMH reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200 PRO, Switzerland). A hemolysis rate of < 5% was considered acceptable.

For systemic toxicity analysis, mice received BM@nano-siLILRB3 via tail vein injection for three consecutive days. Blood samples were collected on day 4. Hematological parameters, including hemoglobin (HGB), white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), and platelets (PLT), were measured using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XS-800i, Japan). Serum biochemical indices, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA), were analyzed using a biochemical analyzer (Cobas c501, Roche, Switzerland). Heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and stained with H&E (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Histological changes and inflammatory infiltration were examined under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Establishment of GL261-Luc glioma mouse model and treatment grouping

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, SPF grade; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., strain code 219) were housed under barrier conditions at 22 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% relative humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. GL261-Luc glioma cells (1 × 105 cells in 5 µL PBS) were stereotactically implanted into the right caudate nucleus using the following coordinates: 2 mm lateral to the midline, 1 mm posterior to the bregma, and 3.0 mm in depth. On day 7 post-implantation, tumor establishment was confirmed by bioluminescence imaging. Mice with comparable tumor signals were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): PBS, free siLILRB3, nano-siLILRB3, and BM@nano-siLILRB3. All formulations were administered via tail vein injection at a dose of 100 µg siRNA/kg every other day for a total of six injections [52].

Bioluminescence imaging for tumor progression monitoring

On days 7, 10, 14, and 18, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg; YEASEN, China) 10 min before imaging. Bioluminescence signals were acquired using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA) with an exposure time of 60 s and auto-adjusted sensitivity. Tumor burden was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer, USA) based on the total photon flux within the brain region of interest.

Detection of tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis

After euthanasia, mouse brains were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (24 h) (Solarbio, China), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval using 0.01 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 95 °C, 15 min), followed by PBS washes.

For proliferation analysis, Ki67 immunofluorescence staining was performed. Sections were blocked with 1% BSA (1 h) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Ki67 antibody (Abcam, ab16667, 1:200), followed by Alexa Fluor® 594–conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150080, 1:1000, 1 h, room temperature in the dark). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Cell Signaling Technology, #8961, 1:1000, 5 min).

Apoptosis was assessed using a TUNEL assay kit (Roche, 11684795910). TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells exhibited green fluorescence (FITC). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM 980, Zeiss, Germany), and the percentages of Ki67- and TUNEL-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis of the TME

Tumor tissues were excised and dissociated into single-cell suspensions in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA), passed through 70 μm cell strainers (Corning, USA), and subjected to red blood cell lysis using ACK buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After debris exclusion by FSC/SSC gating, CD45⁺ leukocytes were selected and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against F4/80 (BioLegend, 123107, clone BM8, 1:200), CD86 (BioLegend, 105011, clone GL-1, 1:200), CD206 (BioLegend, 141707, clone C068C2, 1:200), CD3 (BioLegend, 100203, clone 17A2, 1:200), CD8 (BioLegend, 100711, clone 53 − 6.7, 1:200), NK1.1 (BioLegend, 108709, clone PK136, 1:200), Foxp3 (BioLegend, 118903, clone QA20A67, 1:100), and CD4 (BioLegend, 100405, clone GK1.5, 1:100). Data were acquired using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, USA) to quantify the proportions of M1 and M2 macrophages, natural killer T (NKT) cells, CD8+ T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs).

Immunofluorescence detection of M1/M2 phenotypes and treg cells

Brain tissues from glioma-bearing mice were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (24 h) (Solarbio, China), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 mol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 95 °C, 15 min), followed by blocking with 5% BSA (1 h, room temperature).

For M1/M2 macrophage phenotype analysis, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with Alexa Fluor® 488–conjugated anti-Iba1 (Abcam, ab225260, 1:200) in combination with either anti-iNOS (Abcam, ab283655, 1:200) for M1 macrophages or anti-CD206 (Abcam, ab64693, 1:200) for M2 macrophages. After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, ab150080, 1:1000, 1 h, in the dark), followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (LSM 980, Zeiss, Germany), and the percentages of Iba1+iNOS+ and Iba1+CD206+ cells were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

For Treg detection, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-Foxp3 antibody (ab75763, 1:200, Abcam, UK), followed by Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab150080, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) as the secondary antibody. After nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, slides were mounted and examined under a confocal microscope. The number of Foxp3+ cells was quantified to assess Treg cell infiltration.

Detection of cytokine expression in tumor tissues

Total protein was extracted from tumor tissues using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China), and protein concentrations were determined by the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentrations of TNF-α (ab208348), IFN-γ (ab100689), CXCL10 (ab260067), IL-6 (ab222503), and IL-10 (ab255729) were measured using ELISA kits (Abcam, UK). Optical density was recorded using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200 PRO, Switzerland), and cytokine levels were calculated based on standard curves and normalized to total protein content (pg per g of tissue protein).

Statistical analysis

Data were derived from at least three independent experiments and presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD); for comparisons between the two groups, a two-sample t-test was applied. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test if the ANOVA results indicated significant differences to compare differences among groups. For non-normally distributed or unequal variance data, the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was utilized. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R language, with a significance level set at 0.05 and a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Quality control, filtering, and PCA of glioma tissue scRNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from mouse glioma tissues were analyzed using the Seurat package in R. After quality control and normalization, the distribution of cellular RNA counts was obtained (Figure S1A). Cell cycle–associated S and G2/M scores were then calculated (Figure S1B), and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for downstream analysis (Figure S1C), indicating substantial transcriptional heterogeneity in both radio-responsive and radio-resistant glioma tissues.

PCA was performed based on these highly variable genes to reduce dimensionality and enable subsequent UMAP clustering (Figure S1C). Heatmaps and bubble plots illustrating gene expression patterns across the top four principal components are shown in Figures S1D–E. The statistical significance of each PC was evaluated using the JackStrawPlot method, in which PCs with low p values (solid lines above the dashed threshold) were considered informative (Figure S1F). Based on this analysis, the first 50 PCs were selected for downstream clustering and cell annotation, demonstrating the robustness and reliability of the PCA results for further single-cell analyses.

Single-cell clustering reveals key cellular populations in the glioma immune microenvironment

To characterize the cellular composition of glioma tissues, we first applied the FindClusters function to determine the optimal clustering resolution. Unsupervised UMAP analysis classified all cells into five distinct clusters (Fig. 1A-B). Cluster-specific marker genes were then identified, and their expression patterns were visualized on UMAP plots (Fig. 1C). We then utilized the Bioconductor/R package “SingleR” to annotate the 5 cell clusters, resulting in the classification of 4 cell types: M2 macrophages, Oligodendrocytes, Astrocytes, and Microglia (Fig. 1D).

We further compared the relative abundance of these cell types between the two samples (Fig. 1E), revealing a significant enrichment of M2 macrophages in tumor tissues (Fig. 1F). M2 macrophages are commonly associated with anti-inflammatory and reparative processes within the TME, thereby promoting glioma growth [53]. Based on this, we further investigated the regulatory mechanisms of M2 macrophages in glioma.

Screening of LILRB3 as a key gene in M2 macrophage polarization in glioma

To identify key genes involved in M2 macrophage polarization in glioma, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis on mouse glioma tumor tissues. Differential expression analysis identified 300 DEGs (Fig. 2A-B). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly associated with hypoxia-related and immune-regulatory processes. GO analysis showed strong enrichment in cellular responses to hypoxia, while KEGG pathway analysis highlighted HIF-1 signaling, central carbon metabolism in cancer, and IL-17 signaling. Reactome analysis further indicated that these DEGs were mainly involved in collagen formation, IL-10 signaling, and glycolysis (Fig. 2C-E), all of which are closely linked to macrophage polarization and tumor progression.

To further identify core genes driving M2 polarization, the 300 DEGs were subjected to LASSO regression analysis using the L1 penalty. The trajectories of regression coefficients under the L1 constraint are shown in Fig. 3A. Using α = 1 and 10-fold cross-validation, the model achieved the minimum root mean square error when five variables were retained, yielding five candidate genes: LILRB3, KCNS1, SLC6A8, GREM1, and SLC2A5.

Expression analysis revealed that all five genes were significantly upregulated in glioma tissues compared with controls, with LILRB3 showing the most prominent increase (Fig. 3B). This expression pattern was further validated in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 3C).

To explore the functional relevance of LILRB3 in macrophage polarization, a PPI network was constructed using GeneMANIA, incorporating classical M2 macrophage markers (CD163, CD204, CD206, IL-10, and Arg1). The resulting network revealed strong regulatory associations between LILRB3 and M2-related genes (Fig. 3D). Consistently, single-cell RNA-seq analysis demonstrated a pronounced co-expression of LILRB3 and CD163 within M2 macrophage clusters (Fig. 3E), suggesting the potential involvement of LILRB3 in the regulation of M2 macrophage polarization during glioma progression.

Specific expression of LILRB3 in M2 macrophages

To validate the single-cell sequencing results, we first examined LILRB3 expression in mouse glioma tissues. WB analysis showed that LILRB3 protein levels were significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared with normal brain tissues (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated strong co-localization of LILRB3 with the M2 macrophage marker CD206 in glioma tissues, confirming substantial co-expression of LILRB3 and M2 macrophages in the tumor samples (Fig. 4B). We next assessed LILRB3 expression in vitro using THP-1–derived macrophages. Successful M2 polarization was confirmed by the upregulation of macrophage markers CD11B and CD204, as well as the M2 markers CD206 and CD163, as detected by WB and RT-qPCR (Fig. 4C-D). Comparative expression analysis showed that LILRB3 was markedly upregulated in M2 macrophages relative to undifferentiated THP-1 cells, whereas its expression was minimal in glioma cell lines (U87, LN229, and U251) (Fig. 4E-F). These results were further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4G), supporting the specific enrichment of LILRB3 in M2 macrophages.

We employed lentivirus-mediated RNA interference technology to silence the LILRB3 gene in THP-1-differentiated M2 macrophages, aiming to investigate the impact of this gene on M2 macrophage polarization and function. As depicted in Fig. 4H, the expression of LILRB3 in M2 macrophages treated with sh-LILRB3-2 was significantly reduced compared to the control group, warranting the use of sh-LILRB3-2 in subsequent experiments. To confirm the specificity of sh-LILRB3, the expression levels of other members of the LILRB family (LILRB1 and LILRB2) were examined by qPCR. The results showed that sh-LILRB3 treatment did not significantly affect the expression of these related genes (Figure S2A). In addition, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that sh-LILRB3 treatment did not induce a significant reduction in macrophage viability (Figure S2B), indicating that the observed phenotypic changes were primarily attributable to LILRB3 silencing rather than nonspecific cytotoxic effects.

WB analysis revealed that the M2 macrophage markers CD206 and CD163 were markedly reduced in LILRB3-silenced M2 macrophages compared with controls (Fig. 4I), indicating that LILRB3 is essential for maintaining the M2 polarization state. Consistently, ELISA assays showed significant alterations in cytokine secretion following LILRB3 knockdown, characterized by a pronounced decrease in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and a concomitant increase in the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α (Fig. 4J). To further evaluate the M2 polarization status, the expression of additional key M2-associated factors was assessed. The secretion of TGF-β, IL-1RA, and CCL22 was significantly reduced in sh-LILRB3–treated macrophages compared with the sh-NC group (Fig. 4K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that LILRB3 is a key regulator of M2 macrophage polarization and their immunosuppressive function in the glioma microenvironment.

LILRB3 mediates M2 macrophage polarization to promote glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To determine how LILRB3-mediated M2 macrophage polarization affects glioma progression, U87 cells were cultured with CM derived from M2 macrophages treated with different shRNAs. CCK-8 assays showed that CM from M2 macrophages significantly enhanced glioma cell proliferation compared with glioma cells cultured alone (Fig.5A). In contrast, CM from sh-LILRB3–treated M2 macrophages markedly attenuated this pro-proliferative effect, whereas sh-NC had no significant impact (Fig. 5A). These results were further confirmed by EdU incorporation assays (Fig. 5B). In the wound healing test, we observed that M2 macrophages enhanced the migration ability of glioma cells (Fig. 5C). In contrast, M2 macrophages treated with sh-LILRB3 inhibited the migration capability of glioma cells (Fig. 5C). Finally, in the Transwell invasion assay, M2 macrophages treated with sh-LILRB3 significantly hindered the invasion ability of glioma cells (Fig. 5D). These experimental findings underscore the critical role of LILRB3 in modulating macrophage function and its impact on glioma cell behavior, providing new potential targets for immunotherapy in glioma.

To further validate the results of the conditioned medium experiments and to better recapitulate the TME, a Transwell co-culture system of M2 macrophages and glioma cells was established. The chemotactic migration of M2 macrophages toward glioma cells was then assessed. The results showed that the number of sh-NC–treated M2 macrophages migrating toward glioma cells in the lower chamber was significantly higher than that in the sh-LILRB3 group, indicating that LILRB3 knockdown may impair the chemotactic ability of M2 macrophages (Fig. 5E). These findings, validated across multiple glioma cell lines, support a role for LILRB3 in promoting tumor progression by regulating M2 macrophage behavior.

LILRB3 mediates M2 Macrophage polarization to promote glioma tumor formation in vivo

To investigate the in vivo role of LILRB3 in M2 macrophage–mediated tumor promotion, a xenograft model was established by subcutaneously co-injecting GFP-labeled U87MG glioma cells with M2 macrophages treated with different shRNAs into nude mice. Tumor growth was monitored using caliper measurements and in vivo fluorescence imaging. Mice receiving sh-LILRB3–treated M2 macrophages exhibited a markedly reduced tumor growth rate and significantly smaller tumor volumes compared with the control group (Fig. 6A). At the experimental endpoint, tumors were excised and weighed, confirming a significant reduction in tumor mass in the sh-LILRB3 group (Fig. 6B).

Histopathological analysis further supported these findings. H&E and IHC staining revealed that tumors from the sh-LILRB3 group displayed less aggressive features, including clearer tumor boundaries, decreased cellular proliferation, and reduced inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 6C). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining for the M2 macrophage marker CD206 demonstrated a substantial decrease in M2 macrophage infiltration in tumors from the sh-LILRB3–treated group (Fig. 6D). Together, these in vivo data indicate that LILRB3 is essential for maintaining the tumor-promoting M2 macrophage phenotype and for establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates glioma growth.

Construction of BM@nano-siLILRB3 for targeted silencing of LILRB3

After establishing that LILRB3 knockdown effectively suppressed M2 macrophage polarization and immunosuppressive function, we engineered a biomimetic nanoplatform to enable efficient in vivo delivery of siLILRB3 and overcome biological barriers. BV2 microglial cell membranes were isolated and purified to generate biomimetic membrane (BM) vesicles. siLILRB3 was electrostatically assembled with the M2 macrophage–targeting lipid DMG-PEG-M2pep to form stable nanocores, which were subsequently fused with BM vesicles under ultrasonic treatment. The resulting membrane-coated nanostructures were further modified with DSPE-PEG2k-Angiopep-2 to impart BBB–penetrating capability, yielding the final construct, BM@nano-siLILRB3. This design integrates immune camouflage, M2 macrophage targeting, and BBB penetration to achieve efficient and selective silencing of LILRB3 in the glioma microenvironment (Fig. 7A).

TEM imaging (Fig. 7B) revealed that nano-siLILRB3 exhibited compact spherical morphology, BM vesicles appeared as hollow spheres, and BM@nano-siLILRB3 displayed a typical core–shell structure with continuous membrane layers, confirming successful membrane coating. CLSM further confirmed this result (Fig. 7C): DiO-labeled BM (green) and DIR-labeled siLILRB3 (red) showed strong intracellular colocalization, with merged yellow fluorescence indicating efficient fusion of the membrane and siRNA nanocores. DLS demonstrated that the nano-siLILRB3 core had an average diameter of approximately 120 nm, which increased to ~ 135 nm following BM coating, consistent with the thickness of the membrane shell (Fig. 7D). Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 7E) indicated a surface charge shift from approximately − 18 mV for the nanocore to -22 mV after BM coating, reflecting the membrane-like surface characteristics of the final formulation.

To verify the integrity of BM membrane coating, we performed protein analysis on BV2 cells, BM vesicles, nano-siLILRB3, and BM@nano-siLILRB3. WB results (Fig. 7F) showed that characteristic BV2 membrane proteins—CSF1R, TMEM119, CX3CR1, and IBA1—were highly enriched in both BM and BM@nano-siLILRB3, but absent in nano-siLILRB3, demonstrating effective retention of membrane proteins during the coating process.

In addition, the storage stability of BM@nano-siLILRB3 was systematically evaluated. As shown in Fig. 7G, the particle size, PDI, and zeta potential remained highly stable over 7 days at both 4 °C and 37 °C, with no detectable aggregation or structural deterioration, indicating excellent colloidal stability of the nanoplatform. To evaluate cellular uptake efficiency, BM@nano-siLILRB3 was co-incubated with GL261 glioma cells and visualized by confocal microscopy (Fig. 7H). DIR-labeled siLILRB3 (red) exhibited strong intracellular colocalization with DiO-labeled BM (green), producing a pronounced yellow signal in merged images, confirming efficient nanoparticle internalization and preserved membrane–core integrity within cells.

The protective effect of BM coating on siRNA stability was further examined in serum. Free siLILRB3 was rapidly degraded and became nearly undetectable within 8 h, whereas intact siRNA bands were still clearly detectable in BM@nano-siLILRB3 samples after 24 h of incubation (Fig. 7I), demonstrating that BM encapsulation markedly enhances resistance to nuclease-mediated degradation.

We further evaluated the long-term stability and siRNA release behavior of BM@nano-siLILRB3 under simulated physiological conditions. The long-term stability results (Fig. 7J–K) showed that in PBS and in culture medium containing 10% FBS, BM@nano-siLILRB3 exhibited no significant changes in particle size, PDI, or zeta potential over a 14-day period. TEM analysis in complete culture medium further confirmed that the nanoparticles maintained an intact morphology without obvious aggregation or degradation. The siRNA release kinetics results (Fig. 7L) demonstrated that BM@nano-siLILRB3 displayed a sustained-release profile in PBS (pH 7.4), with a cumulative release of approximately 65% within 24 h. The release curve showed an initial burst release of about 30% during the early phase (0–12 h), followed by a stable and gradual release phase, indicating that this nanoplatform is capable of achieving sustained siRNA delivery under physiological conditions.

BM@nano-siLILRB3 significantly enhanced BBB penetration and achieved brain-targeted delivery

To comprehensively evaluate the BBB-penetrating capability of BM@nano-siLILRB3, we first established an in vitro BBB Transwell model. As shown in Fig. 8A, nano-siLILRB3 exhibited significantly higher BBB permeability than free siLILRB3, while BM@nano-siLILRB3 achieved the highest translocation efficiency, surpassing both control formulations. These results indicate that nanocarrier-based encapsulation markedly improves siRNA transport across the BBB, and that the biomimetic membrane coating further amplifies this effect. In addition, we compared the BBB penetration efficiency between the Angiopep-2-modified group (BM@nano-siLILRB3) and the unmodified group (BM@nano-siLILRB3 without Angiopep-2). The results showed that the Angiopep-2-modified nanoparticles exhibited a significantly higher BBB penetration efficiency than the unmodified counterparts, confirming that Angiopep-2 further enhances the BBB-penetrating capability of the BM coating (Fig. 8B).

In a co-culture BBB model incorporating M2-polarized macrophages (Fig. 8C), BM@nano-siLILRB3 demonstrated the highest accumulation in M2 cells at both 6 and 12 h. Accumulation of nano-siLILRB3 was also markedly higher than that of free siLILRB3, consistent with the quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis (Fig.8D). Notably, BM@nano-siLILRB3 lacking M2pep modification (BM@nano-siLILRB3 w/o M2pep) displayed significantly reduced fluorescence intensity in M2 macrophages, comparable to that of nano-siLILRB3, indicating loss of M2-specific targeting. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while all nanoparticle formulations improved BBB permeability relative to free siRNA, BM@nano-siLILRB3 achieved the most efficient BBB penetration and M2 macrophage–specific uptake. The M2pep ligand is essential for selective targeting of M2 macrophages, whereas the biomimetic membrane and Angiopep-2 jointly confer robust BBB-crossing and immune-camouflaging properties to the nanoplatform.

In vivo fluorescence imaging (Fig. 8E) revealed that BM@nano-siLILRB3 exhibited sustained accumulation in the brain region from 6 to 24 h after tail vein injection, with fluorescence intensity significantly higher than that of nano-siLILRB3 and free siLILRB3. Notably, the BM@nano-siLILRB3 without M2pep modification (BM@nano-siLILRB3 w/o M2pep) showed markedly weaker fluorescence signals in the brain compared with the fully functionalized BM@nano-siLILRB3, and its intensity was comparable to that of the nano-siLILRB3 group. Ex vivo imaging of dissected brain tissue further confirmed this pattern (Fig. 8F): BM@nano-siLILRB3 consistently displayed the strongest fluorescence signals at 6, 12, and 24 h, followed by the nano-siLILRB3 and BM@nano-siLILRB3 w/o M2pep groups, whereas free siLILRB3 rapidly diminished within 8 h. Semi-quantitative analysis (Fig. 8G) similarly demonstrated that BM@nano-siLILRB3 achieved the highest fluorescence intensity in brain tissues, with intermediate levels observed for nano-siLILRB3 and BM@nano-siLILRB3 w/o M2pep, and the lowest signal detected in the free siLILRB3 group.

Finally, immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 8H) showed that the red fluorescence of BM@nano-siLILRB3 (DIR-labeled siLILRB3) exhibited a high degree of colocalization with Iba1-positive microglia. In contrast, limited accumulation was observed in the nano-siLILRB3 and BM@nano-siLILRB3 w/o M2pep groups, which was markedly lower than that in the fully functionalized BM@nano-siLILRB3 group, while free siLILRB3 showed almost no detectable colocalization signals. These results indicated that nanocarrier-based delivery substantially improved the intracerebral distribution of siRNA, and that BM encapsulation further enhanced its brain-targeting capacity and selective enrichment within microglia.

To evaluate the in vivo biosafety of BM@nano-siLILRB3, hemolysis, hematological, biochemical, and histopathological analyses were performed (Figure S3A). Hemolysis results (Figure S3B) showed that red blood cells exposed to BM@nano-siLILRB3 (3.125–200 µg/mL) exhibited < 5% hemolysis, indicating good blood compatibility. Complete blood count analysis (Figure S3C) revealed no significant differences in HGB, WBC, RBC, or PLT between treated and control mice, indicating no hematological toxicity. Serum biochemical markers of liver and kidney function (ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA) remained within normal ranges (Figure S3D). Histological examination of major organs showed intact tissue architecture without inflammation or damage (Figure S3E).

Taken together, BM@nano-siLILRB3 demonstrated excellent biosafety across hematological, biochemical, and histopathological dimensions.

BM@nano-siLILRB3 significantly suppressed glioma growth and promoted apoptosis in vivo

The antitumor efficacy of BM@nano-siLILRB3 was evaluated in the GL261-Luc orthotopic glioma model. On day 7 after tumor implantation, mice were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: PBS, free siLILRB3, nano-siLILRB3, and BM@nano-siLILRB3, followed by intravenous administration and longitudinal monitoring by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 9A). As shown in Fig. 9B, tumor signals in the PBS group increased rapidly, and free siLILRB3 exhibited only minimal inhibitory effects. In contrast, nano-siLILRB3 moderately slowed tumor growth, while BM@nano-siLILRB3 demonstrated the most pronounced antitumor activity. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 9C) further confirmed that BM@nano-siLILRB3 significantly reduced tumor photon intensity on days 10, 14, and 18, outperforming both nano-siLILRB3 and free siLILRB3.

To explore the underlying mechanisms, we performed Ki67 and TUNEL staining on tumor sections. As shown in Fig. 9D, the proportion of Ki67-positive cells remained high in the PBS and free siLILRB3 groups but was reduced in the nano-siLILRB3 group and lowest in the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group, indicating a superior inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation. Corresponding quantification (Fig. 9E) further demonstrated a significant decrease in Ki67 positivity following BM@nano-siLILRB3 treatment. Conversely, TUNEL staining revealed the highest proportion of apoptotic cells in the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group (Fig. 9D & F), markedly exceeding that of the nano-siLILRB3 and free siLILRB3 groups, suggesting a strong pro-apoptotic effect.

In summary, BM@nano-siLILRB3 effectively suppressed glioma progression in vivo, primarily by inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis.

In addition, to directly verify whether BM@nano-siLILRB3 successfully silenced the target gene in vivo, the mRNA and protein expression levels of LILRB3 in brain tumor tissues were examined after treatment. RT-qPCR analysis showed that, compared with the PBS group, LILRB3 mRNA expression remained unchanged in the free siLILRB3 group, was slightly reduced in the nano-siLILRB3 group, and was significantly downregulated in the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group (Fig. 9G). WB analysis further confirmed that LILRB3 protein expression in tumor tissues from the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group was markedly lower than that in all other groups, and was also significantly reduced compared with the nano-siLILRB3 group (Fig. 9H). Together, these data provide direct molecular evidence that BM@nano-siLILRB3 can efficiently deliver siRNA to brain tumor sites and effectively silence LILRB3 expression in vivo, thereby supporting its observed antitumor phenotype.

BM@nano-siLILRB3 reprogrammed the glioma immune microenvironment

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of BM@nano-siLILRB3, we systematically analyzed the phenotypic and functional alterations of tumor-associated immune cells in the GL261 glioma model (Fig. 10A). Flow cytometry revealed (Fig. 10B) that the proportion of CD206+ M2-like macrophages was elevated in both the PBS and free siLILRB3 groups. Although nano-siLILRB3 partially reduced this population, BM@nano-siLILRB3 markedly suppressed M2 polarization. Correspondingly, the proportion of CD86+ M1-like macrophages significantly increased in the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group (Fig. 10C), indicating effective promotion of M1 polarization. Immunofluorescence analysis further validated these findings (Figs. 10D-E): BM@nano-siLILRB3 substantially increased the proportion of Iba1+iNOS+ cells while reducing Iba1⁺CD206⁺ cells, thereby inhibiting immunosuppressive polarization and enhancing immune activation.

Moreover, BM@nano-siLILRB3 significantly promoted the infiltration of effector lymphocytes. Flow cytometric analysis (Figs. 10 F-G) showed that treatment with BM@nano-siLILRB3 led to a notable increase in the proportions of CD3+NK1.1+ NKT cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells, both markedly higher than those in the nano-siLILRB3 and free siLILRB3 groups, suggesting potent immunostimulatory effects. We further assessed changes in Tregs and cytokine profiles within the tumor tissue. Flow cytometry results (Figure S4A) demonstrated a high proportion of CD4+Foxp3+ Tregs in the PBS and free siLILRB3 groups. This proportion decreased significantly in the nano-siLILRB3 group and was lowest in the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group. Consistent immunofluorescence data (Figure S4B) confirmed a marked reduction in Foxp3⁺ cell infiltration following BM@nano-siLILRB3 treatment, further supporting its capacity to suppress Treg accumulation.

Simultaneously, cytokine profiling showed that BM@nano-siLILRB3 significantly upregulated the levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and CXCL10 (Figures S4C-E), reflecting enhanced pro-inflammatory and chemotactic signaling that supports antitumor immune activation. In contrast, immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-10 were significantly reduced in the BM@nano-siLILRB3 group (Figures S4F-G). Although nano-siLILRB3 also partially improved the cytokine profile, its effects were notably weaker than those achieved by the BM-coated formulation.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that BM@nano-siLILRB3 robustly reprograms the glioma immune microenvironment by suppressing M2 macrophage polarization, promoting M1 polarization, enhancing NKT and CD8+ T-cell infiltration, reducing Treg accumulation, and reshaping the cytokine milieu to favor sustained antitumor immunity.

Discussion

This study strengthens the understanding of the immunosuppressive role of M2 macrophages in glioma through single-cell sequencing technology. The infiltration of M2 macrophages is significantly associated with glioma occurrence, consistent with previous reports showing that M2-polarized macrophages promote tumor growth, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance across multiple cancer types [54–56]. By leveraging single-cell–resolution data, this study substantially improves the precision of data processing and analysis, enabling deeper insight into the interactions and functional heterogeneity of distinct cellular subpopulations. Moreover, it reveals how M2 macrophages are selectively regulated by specific signaling pathways within the glioma microenvironment, thereby promoting tumor progression.

Regarding the role of LILRB3, this study not only confirms its regulatory function in macrophage polarization but also identifies a novel mechanism highly associated with M2 polarization through machine learning techniques. Our single-cell sequencing combined with machine learning analyses identified M2 macrophages as the immune cell subset most closely associated with glioma progression, with LILRB3 being highly co-expressed within this population. This finding is consistent with multiple reports demonstrating that TAMs play a dominant role in shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment of gliomas [57, 58]. Although other immune cell types, such as CD8⁺ T cells and regulatory T cells, were also detected, their abundance in the sequencing data was relatively low and their correlation with LILRB3 expression was weak. Therefore, this study focused on macrophages for in-depth functional validation. Future studies may further extend these findings by investigating the regulatory effects of LILRB3 on other immune cell subsets. Collectively, these results expand our understanding of LILRB3 as an immunomodulatory molecule.

Compared with previous studies, our work provides a more detailed molecular framework explaining how LILRB3 promotes M2 macrophage polarization through modulation of macrophage signaling pathways. Whereas earlier research primarily emphasized the role of LILRB3 in suppressing T-cell–mediated immunity, its involvement in macrophage polarization has remained largely unexplored. Therefore, this study fills an important gap in understanding the macrophage-centered immunoregulatory functions of LILRB3 in the glioma microenvironment [59, 60]. Notably, other members of the LILRB family, such as LILRB1 and LILRB2, have been reported to participate in the regulation of immunosuppression in multiple types of tumors [61, 62]. However, studies specifically addressing the role of LILRB3 in the polarization of TAMs in glioma remain limited. In contrast, previous attempts to reprogram TAMs using CSF1R inhibitors have often been challenged by insufficient targeting specificity or systemic adverse effects [63].

It should be noted that LILRB3 is a human myeloid cell–specific receptor and lacks a direct homolog in mice [64]. In this study, the in vivo glioma model was based on murine GL261 tumors within an intact mouse immune system. Therefore, the potential role of LILRB3 in human glioma was inferred indirectly by analyzing changes in downstream immunoregulatory molecules functionally associated with LILRB3, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, in murine macrophages. Moreover, the in vitro experiments relied primarily on THP-1–derived M2 macrophages, which differ from primary human macrophages in their transcriptional programs and functional phenotypes. These differences may influence the precise delineation of LILRB3-mediated regulatory mechanisms. Future studies incorporating primary human macrophages or patient-derived tumor-associated macrophages will be essential to further validate and refine these findings and to improve their translational relevance.

By applying machine-learning approaches to identify key regulatory factors, this study demonstrates the power of modern computational biology in extracting biologically meaningful insights from complex datasets. Compared with traditional biostatistical methods, machine learning is better suited to handling large-scale, high-dimensional data and uncovering nonlinear patterns and interactions that are critical for understanding intricate biological processes. In this context, our machine-learning–based analysis successfully identified LILRB3 as a pivotal regulator of M2 macrophage polarization in glioma. The novelty of this strategy lies in its high efficiency and precision, providing a robust and versatile framework for future investigations in tumor immunology.

In recent years, nanocarrier-based delivery systems have emerged as a major research focus for overcoming the BBB and achieving targeted therapy for gliomas. For example, liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles have been explored for the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents or siRNA; however, their immunogenicity and targeting efficiency still require further improvement [65–67]. In this study, shRNA-mediated downregulation of LILRB3 in M2 macrophages led to a marked reduction in M2-associated markers, confirming its critical role in macrophage polarization and in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that perturbation of key regulatory molecules can profoundly alter macrophage phenotypes. Moreover, ELISA-based profiling of IL-10 and TNF-α further demonstrated that LILRB3 silencing shifts macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory state, providing mechanistic insight into its immunoregulatory function and highlighting potential biomarkers for monitoring therapeutic response.

Using a nude mouse xenograft model, we further validated the functional role of LILRB3 in glioma progression and found that silencing LILRB3 in macrophages significantly suppressed tumor growth. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that modulation of immune regulatory pathways can profoundly influence tumor development [68]. Notably, the present study uniquely delineates the macrophage-centered immunosuppressive role of LILRB3 in glioma, thereby providing an experimental foundation for the development of LILRB3-targeted therapies. Importantly, nude mice were employed in this study to specifically evaluate the T cell–independent, macrophage-mediated tumor-promoting effects of LILRB3, enabling isolation of its direct role in macrophage polarization. In contrast, subsequent therapeutic experiments using BM@nano-siLILRB3 in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice bearing GL261 tumors demonstrated that this nanoplatform could remodel the entire immune microenvironment, including T-cell–mediated antitumor responses. This two-tiered experimental strategy partially compensates for the inherent limitations of the nude mouse model. Similar approaches have been reported in prior studies [47, 69], supporting the validity of our experimental design. Future investigations in additional immunocompetent models will be necessary to further delineate the broader immunoregulatory network governed by LILRB3 across multiple immune cell populations.

Although this study identifies LILRB3 as a key driver of glioma-associated immunosuppression, the efficient in vivo silencing of this target remains a major translational challenge. Nucleic acid–based therapeutics, such as siRNA, are hindered by rapid degradation, poor systemic stability, and limited permeability across the BBB, making the development of an effective delivery system essential for clinical application.

To address these obstacles, we engineered a biomimetic nanoplatform, BM@nano-siLILRB3, specifically designed for targeted delivery to the glioma immune microenvironment. Coating the siRNA-loaded nanocores with microglia-derived membranes endowed the system with immune camouflage and intrinsic brain tropism, while Angiopep-2 modification conferred enhanced BBB penetration. This multifunctional platform exhibited excellent physicochemical stability, effectively protected siRNA from nuclease degradation, and achieved efficient BBB traversal, enabling precise targeting of immunosuppressive macrophages within glioma tissue. Functionally, BM@nano-siLILRB3 not only suppressed M2 macrophage polarization and promoted M1 reprogramming, but also enhanced CD8+ T-cell and NKT-cell infiltration, resulting in broad immunological remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. In vivo, this strategy produced superior antitumor efficacy compared with free siRNA and uncoated nanocores, highlighting its strong potential for translational glioma immunotherapy.

Taken together, this study not only elucidated the molecular mechanisms of LILRB3-mediated immunosuppression in glioma through single-cell multiomics and machine learning (Fig. 11A), but also proposed and validated an innovative nanotherapeutic strategy (Fig. 11B). Compared with previously reported single-target delivery systems, BM@nano-siLILRB3 integrates the immune camouflage properties of microglial membranes with the BBB–penetrating capability of Angiopep-2, thereby achieving dual targeting of TAMs and reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment. This strategy provides a novel direction for precision immunotherapy in glioma. The design of BM@nano-siLILRB3 offers new insights into overcoming the bottlenecks of siRNA delivery and enabling BBB-crossing targeted therapy. This platform demonstrates promising potential for clinical translation. Future optimization, such as tuning particle size, morphology, and structural properties, as well as incorporating stimulus-responsive release mechanisms, may further enhance its stability and targeting efficiency, providing a new tool for personalized nano-immunotherapy of glioma [70]. Moreover, with the integration of preclinical models and larger-scale validation studies, this strategy holds promise as a valuable adjunct to glioma immunotherapy and may offer new opportunities to improve patient outcomes.

This study revealed the critical role of LILRB3 in glioma-associated immunosuppression using single-cell multiomics and machine learning, and further achieved targeted BBB-crossing intervention via the BM@nano-siLILRB3 nanoplatform. However, several limitations remain. First, in the single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, each group included only one biological sample. Therefore, these data were primarily used in an exploratory manner to characterize the major cellular populations and their transcriptional features within the glioma immune microenvironment, and the findings require further validation in studies with larger sample sizes. Second, although the animal experiments confirmed the effectiveness of LILRB3 silencing, differences in the immune microenvironment and genetic background between murine models and human gliomas remain, and the translational potential of these results in clinical settings needs to be further evaluated. Moreover, this study mainly focused on the role of LILRB3 in M2 macrophage polarization, whereas the immunosuppressive microenvironment of glioma also involves multiple immune cell types, including dendritic cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and T cells, whose interactions have not yet been systematically elucidated. Finally, although BM@nano-siLILRB3 exhibited favorable in vivo stability and therapeutic efficacy, its long-term safety, pharmacokinetic properties, and performance in complex clinical contexts require further investigation.

Future research may advance along several important directions. First, large-scale preclinical studies and multicenter clinical trials will be required to rigorously evaluate the efficacy and safety of LILRB3-targeted silencing strategies in human glioma. Second, a deeper investigation of the crosstalk between LILRB3 and other immune checkpoints or metabolic pathways could enable the construction of a more comprehensive immunoregulatory network, thereby supporting the development of rational combination therapies. At the nanomedicine level, further optimization of the scalable and standardized manufacturing of BM@nano-siLILRB3 will be essential to facilitate clinical translation. In parallel, combining BM@nano-siLILRB3 with immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti–PD-1 antibodies) or CAR-T cell therapy may yield synergistic antitumor effects, as demonstrated in other nano-immunotherapy strategies [71–73].

In conclusion, this study not only elucidates the immunoregulatory role of LILRB3 in glioma at a mechanistic level but also establishes a nanoplatform-based therapeutic framework for targeted immunomodulation. With continued refinement and validation, LILRB3-targeted nanotherapy holds strong promise as a novel approach for reversing glioma-associated immunosuppression and improving patient outcomes.
