Dose reduction and discontinuation of non‐tumor necrosis factor (TNF) biologic and targeted synthetic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) for people with rheumatoid arthritis in remission or low disease activity
Authors: Yuanteng J Li, Maria Angeles Lopez-Olivo, Yimin Geng, Jean H Tayar, Maria E Suarez-Almazor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To determine the benefits and harms of dose reduction or discontinuation of non‐TNF biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs in people with rheumatoid arthritis who are in remission or have low disease activity.

Background

Description of the condition

Rheumatoid arthritis is a systemic chronic autoimmune disease characterized by symmetric polyarticular joint pain in the small joints, such as those in the hands and feet. In addition to causing inflammatory arthritis, long‐term uncontrolled rheumatoid arthritis may increase the risk of stroke, mental health disorders, pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, cancer, poor quality of life, and physical deterioration [1]. A 2021 meta‐analysis of 67 cohort studies showed rheumatoid arthritis to have a pooled prevalence of 0.46% (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.37 to 0.57%) for the period of 1986 to 2014 [2]. The burden of rheumatoid arthritis cannot be overstated, as it leads to not only increased morbidity and mortality but also increased cost of care. A 2019 systematic review showed that the annual indirect cost, defined as productivity losses due to morbidity and mortality, is estimated to range from USD 59 to USD 22,444 per person with rheumatoid arthritis [3]. The annual direct cost, defined as drug costs, inpatient costs, outpatient costs, and other healthcare‐related costs, is estimated to range from USD 401 to USD 67,306 per person with rheumatoid arthritis. [3]. Specifically, drug cost accounts for most of the direct cost associated with treating rheumatoid arthritis [3].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Over the past three decades, the management of rheumatoid arthritis has focused on the early initiation of treatment with a treat‐to‐target strategy (the goal of achieving disease remission or low disease activity based on disease activity scores), which has dramatically reduced the disease burden [4]. Although the use of non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and glucocorticoids is still an important part of rheumatoid arthritis management, the early use of disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) remains the foundation for limiting long‐term disease damage [5]. There are several types of DMARDs based on their structure and mechanism of action. Conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs) such as methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, and leflunomide were designed to target multiple immune pathways [6]. Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) are biologically derived medications that target specific immune pathways to control disease inflammation [6]. Because these biologically derived proteins cannot be exactly reproduced, they are further classified into bio‐originators and biosimilars, depending on whether they are developed by the first or subsequent manufacturers. Per the US Food and Drug Administration, a biosimilar is defined as a biopharmaceutical that is highly similar to the reference product notwithstanding minor differences in clinically inactive components and for which there are no clinically meaningful differences in purity, potency, and safety between the two products [7]. For rheumatoid arthritis, treatment options include tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors (adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, etanercept, golimumab, and infliximab), interleukin (IL)‐6 inhibitors (sarilumab, tocilizumab), IL‐1 inhibitor (anakinra), T‐cell co‐stimulation inhibitor (abatacept), and selective B‐cell inhibitor (rituximab). Finally, targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) are synthetically derived oral small molecule janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors. JAK inhibitor options for rheumatoid arthritis include baricitinib, tofacitinib, and upadacitinib.

When triple csDMARD therapy (methotrexate, sulfasalazine, and hydroxychloroquine) fails to adequately control rheumatoid arthritis, biologic agents and tsDMARDs are added to achieve disease remission [8, 9]. Although the use of biologic agents and tsDMARDs has revolutionized the management of rheumatoid arthritis, they have added risks of their own, including an increased risk of infection and other rare adverse events, which may differ across drug classes [1011, 12, 13, 14]. Hence, it is important to investigate reducing the dose or discontinuing DMARDs once people with rheumatoid arthritis have achieved remission, not only to minimize the risk of infection, but to also lessen treatment burden, such as fewer monitoring visits, fewer laboratory tests, and lower costs. However, tapering or withdrawing DMARDs may increase the risk of disease flare, and the balance of benefits and risk remains uncertain. In people treated early with TNF inhibitors, dose reduction of TNF inhibitors may maintain clinical remission, but more data are needed for tapering of non‐TNF biologic agents and tsDMARDs [9, 15, 16]

Why it is important to do this review

The European Alliance of Association for Rheumatology (EULAR) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) currently recommend that dose reduction of DMARDs may be considered for people with rheumatoid arthritis who are in sustained remission for at least six months, but there is no established long‐term evidence to support this strategy [17, 18]. The Australian Living Guideline for the pharmacological management of inflammatory arthritis also makes the conditional recommendation to consider stepwise reduction in the dose of b/tsDMARDs until cessation or the lowest effective b/tsDMARD dose is achieved in people with rheumatoid arthritis who have sustained low disease activity or remission for at least six months [9]. All three guidelines do not recommend abrupt cessation of b/tsDMARD without prior dose reduction [9, 17, 18]. Several factors may influence the likelihood of successful dose reduction or discontinuation. Fixed dose‐reduction approaches may differ from disease activity‐guided tapering strategies in their ability to maintain disease control, as the latter adjusts treatment to an individual’s current disease status [19]. The type of biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD may also matter, as these agents target different components of the immune response. People with residual low disease activity may be more prone to flare than those in full remission, who have more complete suppression of inflammation [20]. The presence or absence of concomitant csDMARD therapy may further modify outcomes, as ongoing background treatment may help maintain disease control during tapering [20]. Although biosimilars are designed to be highly similar to their reference products, distinguishing between biosimilars and bio‐originators allows us to explore whether any differences in clinical outcomes emerge during dose reduction or discontinuation.

Another Cochrane review summarizes the evidence regarding dose reduction and discontinuation of TNF inhibitors in rheumatoid arthritis [16], therefore this Cochrane review will assess the benefits and harms of dose reduction or discontinuation of other bDMARDs or tsDMARDs in rheumatoid arthritis.

The two Cochrane reviews will provide up‐to‐date evidence to assist clinicians and consumers in making evidence‐informed decisions about the use of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs in people with rheumatoid arthritis, and will inform the Australian Living Guideline and other guidelines. The review will be conducted according to the guidelines recommended by Cochrane Musculoskeletal [21].

Objectives

To determine the benefits and harms of dose reduction or discontinuation of non‐TNF biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs in people with rheumatoid arthritis who are in remission or have low disease activity.

Methods

We will follow the Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting the review and PRISMA 2020 for the reporting [22, 23].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (e.g. cluster randomized and parallel‐group designs) and controlled clinical trials that use quasi‐randomized allocation methods (e.g. date of birth, alternate allocation). We will exclude randomized cross‐over trials [24].

We will include studies reported as full text, those published as abstract only, and any unpublished data. However, while we will describe the characteristics of studies published as abstract only, unpublished data, and ongoing studies, we will not include their results in the data syntheses. There will be no language or publication date restrictions.

Types of participants

We will include studies that recruited adults aged 18 years and older with a diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis based on either the 1987 ACR classification criteria or 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, with sustained low disease activity (defined as Disease Activity Score in 28 joints [DAS28] between 2.6 to 3.2; Clinical Disease Activity Index [CDAI] of > 2.8 and ≤ 10; or Simplified Disease Activity Index [SDAI] of ≤ 11) or remission (defined as DAS28 of < 2.6; CDAI of ≤ 2.8; SDAI of ≤ 3.3, Boolean‐based definition of scores on the tender joint count, swollen joint count C‐reactive protein [CRP, in mg/dL], and patient global assessment [0 to 10] are all ≤ 1), or as otherwise defined by the study authors [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31].

We will include studies with mixed disease populations if the majority (e.g. 80% or more) have rheumatoid arthritis, or if data for those with rheumatoid arthritis are reported separately.

Types of interventions

We will include trials comparing dose reduction or discontinuation of non‐TNF bDMARDs (including all relevant biosimilars) or tsDMARDs. We will avoid the use of the terms 'weaning' or 'tapering,' as unlike in the case of oral glucocorticoids, where these terms are frequently used, the ultimate goal is not necessarily to completely stop the medication.

Eligible comparisons will be the continuation of the same non‐TNF bDMARDs or tsDMARDs, without any dose reduction or discontinuation. We will include studies in which participants receive co‐interventions, provided the co‐interventions are applied similarly in all treatment groups. We will also include studies in which modifications to concomitant rheumatoid arthritis medications, such as csDMARDs, NSAIDs, and/or glucocorticoids, are allowed.

We will exclude studies comparing different dose‐reduction or discontinuation strategies. We will also exclude studies in which the dose is reduced owing to side effects, drug availability, planned surgery, or pregnancy.

Timing of outcome assessment

We will measure the critical and important outcomes at the following time points.

For the proportion of participants with persistent remission, proportion of participants with low disease activity, proportion of participants with a flare, proportion of participants with radiological progression, mean disease activity, mean function, and mean health‐related quality of life, we will extract outcomes at the latest time point reported in each trial up to 12 months (the primary time point), and a secondary time point if data are reported beyond 12 months. We will extract data on adverse events (serious adverse events and withdrawals due to adverse events) at the end of the trial period.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

We will import all titles and abstracts retrieved by electronic searching to a reference management software (EndNote) to remove duplicates [45]. All remaining records will be imported into Covidence to facilitate subsequent independent duplicate screening of titles and abstracts and potentially eligible full‐text papers [46].

Two review authors (YJL and JT) will independently screen titles and abstracts of studies identified by the search for potential relevance. We will retrieve the full‐text study reports or publications of all studies deemed potentially relevant, and two review authors (YJL and JT) will independently screen the full texts and identify studies for inclusion, and identify and record reasons for exclusion of the ineligible studies. We will resolve any disagreements through discussion or in consultation with a third review author (MLO or MSA) if required. We will identify and exclude duplicates and collate multiple reports of the same study under a single reference ID so that each study, rather than each report, is the unit of interest in the review. We will record the selection process in sufficient detail to complete a PRISMA flow diagram [23] and 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table.

Measures of treatment effect

We will analyze dichotomous data as risk ratios (RRs), or Peto odds ratios (ORs) when the outcome is a rare event (approximately less than 10%), and use 95% CIs. For continuous data, we will present results as mean differences (MDs) where possible and 95% CIs.

Where trials used different measures for the same outcome or concept, we will use the reported outcome measure that was the highest in our hierarchy as an index measure [54]. We will transform means and SDs of other outcome measures to the scale of the index instrument and pool the data using MD as the summary estimate. For the mean disease activity score, we will scale to the 0‐to‐10 SDAI as the index instrument. For function, we will assume the 0‐to‐3 (where zero is the best function) HAQ‐DI as the index instrument.

Where different scales are used to measure the same conceptual outcome (e.g. quality of life), we will calculate standardized mean differences (SMDs) instead, with corresponding 95% CIs. SMDs will be back‐translated to a typical scale (e.g. 0 to 10 for pain) by multiplying the SMD by a typical among‐person SD (e.g. the SD of the control group at baseline from the most representative trial) [55].

Unit of analysis issues

For trials with more than two arms, we will describe all study groups in the 'Characteristics of included studies' table, but we will only include intervention groups that meet our review criteria in the analysis. When the variance of the difference between intervention and comparator is not reported, we will calculate this from the variances of all trial arms. When a study includes two eligible interventions and a single control group, we will either combine the data from the two intervention arms if this makes sense (creating a single pair‐wise comparison). If it does not make sense to combine the data from the two arms, we will equally split the data from the 'shared' arm across the other arms (i.e. for dichotomous data, we will halve the numbers of participants and events in the control group; for continuous data, we will use the same mean and SD in both arms but will divide the number of participants equally across both arms). When studies report only differences between treatment groups, as opposed to mean effects for each group, we will analyze data using the generic inverse variance function [55].

For cluster trials, we will adjust for the clustering effect when necessary. If a trial reports effect estimates that are already adjusted for clustering, we will use the reported values. If clustering has not been accounted for, we will adjust the data using the design effect, calculated from the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) and average cluster size. When an ICC is not reported, we will use an external ICC from similar studies or perform sensitivity analyses using a range of plausible ICC values.

Dealing with missing data

We will contact investigators or study sponsors to verify key study characteristics and to obtain missing numerical outcome data where possible (e.g. when a study is identified as abstract only, or when data are not available for all participants).

For dichotomous outcomes (e.g. number of withdrawals due to adverse events), we will calculate the withdrawal rate using the number of participants randomized in the group as the denominator.

For continuous outcomes (e.g. mean change in pain score), we will calculate the MD or SMD based on the number of participants analyzed at that time point. If the number of participants analyzed is not presented for each time point, we will use the number of randomized participants in each group at baseline.

Where possible, we will calculate missing SDs from other statistics, such as standard errors, CIs or P values, according to the recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [56]. If SDs cannot be calculated, they will be imputed (e.g. from other studies in the meta‐analysis).

Reporting bias assessment

We will create and examine a funnel plot to explore possible small‐study biases if at least 10 studies are included in a meta‐analysis. In interpreting funnel plots, we will examine the different possible reasons for funnel plot asymmetry as outlined in Chapter 13 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and relate this to the results of the review. If we are able to pool more than 10 trials, we will undertake formal statistical tests to investigate funnel plot asymmetry following the recommendations in Chapter 13 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [57].

To assess outcome reporting bias, we will check trial protocols against published reports. For studies published after 1 July 2005, we will screen ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP trial registers for the a priori trial protocol. We will evaluate whether selective reporting of outcomes is present.

Meta‐analysis of numerical data

We plan to pool data for the above two planned comparisons to provide estimates of benefit and harm. We will only combine studies judged to be clinically comparable, and we will not pool studies with clearly incompatible tapering approaches (see planned subgroup analyses).

We plan to synthesize effect estimates using a random‐effects meta‐analysis model, given the assumption that there is likely to be clinical diversity, and that different studies are estimating different intervention effects.

When pooling of data is not possible, we will present effect estimates and 95% CIs of each trial in tables, and summarize the results in the text.

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

We will assess the homogeneity of the included studies to determine whether meta‐analysis is appropriate. We will assess clinical homogeneity by comparing key characteristics of the included studies, such as eligibility criteria, baseline disease activity, duration of disease, and prior concomitant treatments. We will assess methodological homogeneity by examining study design features. We will assess statistical heterogeneity visually by inspecting the forest plot for obvious differences in effect estimates and the overlap of CIs, and statistically using the Chi2 test (with P < 0.10 indicating heterogeneity) and the I2 statistic.

As recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, we will interpret the I2 value as follows:

Equity‐related assessment

We will extract contextual factors and characteristics of the population relevant for addressing potential issues in health equity using the PROGRESS‐Plus framework. This framework captures differences across groups related to place of residence; race, ethnicity, and culture; occupation; sex; religion; education; socioeconomic status; social capital; age; disability; and sexual orientation [49, 58].

Health literacy of the population may be another potentially important issue of relevance to health equity that is not currently captured in the PROGRESS‐Plus framework. The WHO describes health literacy as “the cognitive and social skills which determine the motivation and ability of individuals to gain access to, understand and use information in ways which promote and maintain good health” [59]. In both developing and developed countries, health and social policies are emerging that highlight health literacy as a key determinant of a person’s ability to optimally manage his or her health and ensure equitable access to and use of services [60, 61, 62]. We therefore also plan to extract information regarding the health literacy of the study population, if available, based upon the nine domains of the HLQ as listed below [48].
