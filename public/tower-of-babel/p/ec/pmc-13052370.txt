Assessment of early return to drinking in surviving patients with alcohol‐associated hepatitis
Authors: Thomas G Cotter, Mack C Mitchell, Wanzhu Tu, Qing Tang, Yunpeng Yu, Samer Gawrieh, Craig J McClain, Ramon Bataller, Naga Chalasani, Vijay H Shah, Arun J Sanyal, Gyongyi Szabo, Srinivasan Dasarathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent alcohol use is a major determinant of liver‐related outcomes in patients recovering from alcohol‐associated hepatitis (AH). However, the timing and predictors of return to drinking (RTD) are not well‐studied.MethodsWe analyzed alcohol use among patients with AH enrolled in two AlcHepNet multicenter studies: a Phase 2b randomized controlled trial and a prospective observational study. TimeLine FollowBack (TLFB) assessed drinking at each visit. RTD was defined as any alcohol use since the previous visit. The cumulative incidence of RTD was evaluated using the Fine–Gray method, with death as a competing risk. Factors associated with RTD were evaluated using univariate and multivariate Cox regression.ResultsAmong 518 patients alive at Day 28, RTD occurred in 7.7%, 21.7%, and 30.8% at 30, 90, and 180 days, respectively. Patients with moderate AH (mAH, MELD 11–19, n = 103) had a higher RTD incidence at 180 days than those with severe AH (sAH, MELD ≥20, n = 415) (44.3% vs. 27.5%; p = 0.01). RTD was associated with higher AUDIT scores, family history of alcohol use disorder (AUD), lower education, greater alcohol use at baseline, lower MELD scores, and less ascites (all p ≤ 0.01). In multivariable analysis, >20 drinking days in the prior month was associated with increased risk of RTD (HR: 3.46, 95% CI: 2.21–5.39), whereas college education or higher was protective (HR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.32–0.88).ConclusionRTD occurred in 22% of AH patients within 90 days postrecovery, highlighting the need for early AUD interventions. Frequent drinking days at baseline and lower education were strongly linked to early RTD.Keywords: alcohol use disorder, alcohol‐associated hepatitis, alcohol‐associated liver disease, relapse

INTRODUCTION

Hospitalization for alcohol‐associated hepatitis (AH) has a high short‐term mortality, with rates approaching 30% at 90 days, and AH survivors remain at risk of future morbidity and mortality due to recurrent alcohol use (Bataller et al., 2022; Patidar et al., 2023). Patients who seek help for an alcohol use disorder (AUD) have lower alcohol consumption, report fewer problems related to drinking, and are more likely to achieve remission from an AUD (Moos & Moos, 2006). While a number of studies have evaluated alcohol use after liver transplantation, there are a paucity of data on the clinical characteristics of patients who remain abstinent after hospital discharge following an episode of AH. A small retrospective study demonstrated recurrent AH in 18% of AH survivors (Potts et al., 2013) and a French study of 398 patients with severe AH (sAH) found a cumulative incidence of first alcohol relapse of 25.2% at 1 year (Louvet et al., 2017). Return to drinking (RTD) was associated with increased mortality after 6 months, whereas survival in the first 6 months was related more to the severity of AH at the time of presentation (Louvet et al., 2017). Another European study of 142 AH survivors followed by routine clinical care for a median of 55 months emphasized the importance of abstinence for long‐term survival (HR 0.53; p = 0.03) and found that older age and lack of prior AUD treatments were independently associated with complete abstinence (Altamirano et al., 2017). Finally, in a multicenter registry of patients admitted with AH across 28 Spanish hospitals, recurrent AH was common and independently associated with increased mortality, highlighting the clinical impact of returning to alcohol use after an index AH episode (Gratacos‐Gines et al., 2024).

Behavioral therapy and/or pharmacotherapy for AUD, either in combination or as monotherapy, reduces RTD among heavy drinkers and/or those with AUD (Knox et al., 2019). The optimal timing of therapy in AH survivors is uncertain and is typically based on anecdotal evidence of subjective clinical assessment of patient improvement to allow for engagement with therapy and/or minimize potential side effects of medications. Identifying the incidence and risk factors for recurrent alcohol use after recovery from an AH episode is needed to define a more systematic and timely effort at engaging with patients most at risk in order to prevent the associated morbidity and mortality.

We prospectively assessed self‐reported alcohol consumption at protocol‐specified intervals up to 200 days following an episode of AH in two well‐characterized concurrent studies: a randomized controlled trial (RCT) and an observational (OBS) cohort in patients with AH in order to determine (1) alcohol use among AH survivors and (2) risk factors for recurrent alcohol use. We also tested a number of a priori study hypotheses including (1) RCT participants would have lower RTD rates than OBS participants due to a perceived increased commitment to sobriety and more intensive outpatient follow‐up following severe illness; (2) patients with less severe AH (i.e., lower MELD scores) would have higher rates of RTD compared with those with sAH as the more rapid recovery from milder disease would enable RTD more quickly and might not be as strong a deterrent as sAH; and (3) obese participants would have lower rates of RTD as they would have a similar degree of severity of liver disease with less alcohol consumption and less severe AUD (Loomba et al., 2009).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Two prospective clinical studies were conducted by the Alcoholic Hepatitis Network (AlcHepNet), a national research consortium of eight partner institutions in the United States funded by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA). Formed in 2017, the network conducted both a randomized Phase 2b double‐blind controlled trial (RCT) and a prospective observational cohort study (OBS) (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT4072822 NCT 3850899) (Dasarathy et al., 2024; Gawrieh et al., 2024). Briefly, all participants were 21 years or older and diagnosed with AH clinically using published criteria: onset of jaundice (total bilirubin >3 mg/dL), regular heavy consumption of alcohol (average weekly intake >40 gm daily or >280 gm weekly for women and >60 gm daily or >420 gm for men for 6 months or more, with less than 8 weeks of abstinence before the onset of jaundice), AST >50 IU/L, AST/ALT >1.5 and both <400 IU/L, while other competing liver disease etiologies were generally excluded (Crabb et al., 2016). Of note, the OBS study permitted the inclusion of patients with chronic hepatitis C, hepatitis B, HIV, or Stage 1 (one lesion <2 cm) HCC. Full details of the inclusion and exclusion criteria have been published in previous reports (Dasarathy et al., 2024; Gawrieh et al., 2024; Tu et al., 2023). Enrolled RCT participants were assessed during follow‐up visits at 3, 7, 14, 28, 60, 90, and 180 days, and enrolled OBS participants at 4, 12, and 24 weeks. Clinical evaluation included in‐person or TeleHealth interviews using Timeline Follow‐back (TLFB) to assess alcohol consumption since the last study visit. Although all visits were planned to be in‐person, during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the protocol was modified to allow virtual visits when in‐person visits were not possible. Severe AH (sAH) was defined by a MELD score ≥20, while moderate AH was defined by a MELD score of 11–19. Only patients with sAH and MELD scores 20–35 were enrolled in the RCT, whereas the OBS cohort included participants with MELD scores of ≥11. All participants were counseled on alcohol cessation and offered a referral for AUD treatment, consistent with existing site‐specific standard clinical care (Principal Investigator and clinical team); however, no study‐mandated or standardized behavioral intervention (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy or dedicated substance use program) was implemented, and engagement with addiction services was not systematically recorded. Use of medications for AUD was captured retrospectively within 28 days of enrollment, and we report RTD rates stratified by MAUD exposure. Data from participants included in this study are part of other papers published or in preparation for future publication (Gawrieh et al., 2024). The protocols for RCT and OBS, informed consent forms, and subsequent protocol amendments were approved by the Western Institutional Review Board (WIRB). All subjects provided written informed consent in accordance with the Helsinki Agreement on Human Studies.

Statistical analyses

Values of sample means, medians, standard deviations (SD), and interquartile ranges (IQR) are presented for continuous variables. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. RTD was assessed from the Day 28 visit—this time point reflects the likely end of acute disease and the start of the period during which patients were to be clinically stable and at risk for RTD. Patients were followed prospectively, and participants lost to follow‐up were censored at the last available follow‐up visit. RTD was defined by (1) a TLFB score of >0; and/or (2) “yes” to the screening alcohol follow‐up question, “Are you currently drinking alcohol?” Cumulative incidence rates of the first RTD were estimated using the Fine–Gray method and presented graphically (Austin & Fine, 2017; Fine & Gray, 1999). All‐cause mortality was treated as a competing risk to RTD. Cox regression models were used to evaluate the effects of factors associated with RTD. Prospectively ascertained risk factors included participant demographics (e.g., age, biological sex, race/ethnicity), socioeconomic factors (e.g., education, employment, marital status), clinical characteristics (e.g., body mass index [BMI], MELD score, liver‐related decompensations, laboratory values), AUDIT score, and TLFB. Based on clinical judgment (a priori selection of clinically relevant covariates based on existing literature and biological plausibility) and univariate variable screening were used to determine variables included in the multivariate‐adjusted Cox model. The final model included markers of AUD (e.g., high numbers of daily drinks and drinking days per month) and liver disease severity (e.g., MELD score) with additional consideration to covariates, which appeared to have strong associations with RTD on univariable‐adjusted analysis. Two‐way interactions were also explored. Statistical analyses were completed using the SAS software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). P‐values of <0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Overall AH cohort

Assessments of alcohol use were available for analysis on 518 study participants (103 moderate AH, 415 sAH) who were prospectively followed at visit Day 28 or after (Figure S1). A comparison of baseline characteristics between those who RTD versus those who did not during the follow‐up among all AH participants is presented in Table 1. In brief, those who RTD had lower MELD scores (23.4 vs. 25.2; p = 0.01), fewer had ascites (61.4% vs. 75.2%; p < 0.01), consumed more drinks per month (233.8 vs. 130.3; p < 0.01) and drank on more days per month (20.9 vs. 13.9; p < 0.01), had higher baseline AUDIT scores (25.3 vs. 22.7; p < 0.01), and were more likely to report a natural parent with AUD (69.5% vs. 54.0; p = 0.01). Additionally, those who RTD had a lower BMI (mean 28.3 vs. 30.0 kg/m2; p = 0.03) and waist circumference (mean 103.8 vs. 107.9 cm; p = 0.11) compared to those who did not, but these differences did not reach statistical significance. Participant responses to the individual AUDIT items stratified by RTD status are presented in Table S3. In general, patients who RTD consumed more drinks/month (233.8 ± 259.2 drinks in RTD vs. 130.3 ± 169.0 drinks in those not RTD; p < 0.01); and had more drinking days in the 30 days before baseline (20.9 ± 8.8 days vs. 13.9 ± 10.9; p < 0.01) per TLFB questionnaire. The typical number of standard daily drinks was 12.1 ± 10.3 in those who RTD vs. 9.3 ± 8.1 in those who did not RTD; p < 0.01.

The 30‐, 90‐, and 180‐day cumulative incidences of RTD were 7.7%, 21.8%, and 30.8%, respectively in all patients (Figure 1A). The uni‐ and multivariate Cox regression analysis is shown in Table 2. In the univariate‐adjusted regression, higher educational status, higher MELD scores, and the presence of ascites were associated with significantly reduced hazards of RTD. In contrast, higher AUDIT scores, higher number of daily drinks, and higher number of drinking days were associated with significantly increased hazards of RTD (all p < 0.05). The dichotomized variable of >20 drinking days in the past 30 days had the strongest association with the risk of RTD and was included in the final multivariate Cox model. In the final model, completion of college/graduate education was associated with reduced RTD risk (HR: 0.53, 95%CI, 0.32, 0.88) and >20 drinking days in the past 30 days at baseline was associated with increased RTD risk (HR 3.46, 95%CI 2.21, 5.39).

We categorized the weekly average number of drinks as 0 drinks per week, 1–6 drinks per week, and seven or more drinks per week (Table 3). While the drinking amounts decreased significantly from the baseline, there appeared to be a gradual increase in drinking over time. Severe AH (including RCT vs. OBS)

The baseline characteristics of the 415 participants with sAH, stratified by RTD status, are presented in Table S1. Similar to the overall AH cohort, those who RTD had less severe liver disease at baseline (lower proportion with ascites), increased number of recent drinks per day and drinking days, higher AUDIT scores, and a positive family history of AUD compared with those who did not RTD (all p values < 0.05). The 30‐, 90‐, and 180‐day cumulative incidences of RTD were 7.4%, 18.9%, and 27.5%, respectively in those who RTD. The RCT cohort with sAH had cumulative incidences of RTD of 2.0%, 18.3%, and 29.8%, while the OBS sAH cohort had cumulative incidence rates of RTD of 9.2%, 18.9%, and 26.3%. No cumulative difference in RTD rates was observed between the RCT and OBS cohorts with sAH (p = 0.40). Responses to the AUDIT items from participants with sAH are presented in Table S4, stratified by RTD status.

Moderate versus severe AH

The baseline characteristics of the 518 participants stratified by RTD status and AH severity are shown in Table S2. Among those with moderate AH, the 30‐, 90‐, and 180‐day cumulative incidences of RTD were 8.9%, 32.6%, and 44.3%, respectively, which was significantly higher than the rates reported in the sAH cohort (preceding paragraph) (p = 0.01) (Figure 1B).

Pharmacological treatment of AUD and RTD

Pharmacological treatment of AUD (MAUD) was captured retrospectively within 28 days of enrollment. Rates of RTD in those who had received any of the pharmacological agents with anti‐AUD properties, including disulfiram, acamprosate, naltrexone, gabapentin, baclofen, and topiramate, were calculated for all patients with AH and by AH severity. Direct comparisons showed that RTD rates were higher in those who had received AUD pharmacotherapy (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

There are limited data on RTD following an episode of AH, especially in patients who participated in a treatment trial compared to those who were only followed up as part of an observational cohort. This prospective, multicenter clinical study on the short‐term natural history of alcohol use among well‐characterized patients after an episode of AH showed that RTD occurred in 22% of patients within the first 90 days and in 31% within 180 days. These data emphasize the need for early AUD interventions in patients with AH. Consistent with this, a prior study demonstrated that initiation of alcohol rehabilitation within 30 days of hospital discharge was associated with reduced alcohol relapse, readmission, and mortality in patients with AH, with baseline relapse rates of approximately 34%–37% (Peeraphatdit et al., 2020). In our study, there was a higher rate of RTD among those with mAH, with a cumulative incidence of nearly 50% at 6 months, while there was no difference in RTD rates between the RCT and OBS cohorts for patients with sAH. Drinking on more than 20 days in the past month and less than a college education were associated with early RTD.

A small retrospective study showed that nearly 20% of AH survivors develop recurrent AH due to RTD (Potts et al., 2013). A larger French study found that 20% of sAH survivors experienced an “alcohol relapse” (≥30 g/day) within 12 months (Louvet et al., 2017). Our study, focusing on the short‐term natural history of alcohol use among AH survivors, found a somewhat higher RTD rate of 23% of AH survivors RTD within the first 3 months. This finding reiterates the importance of identifying those patients at high risk of early relapse and defining optimal early AUD interventions for patients with AH during the index hospitalization, including linkage to outpatient care to prevent RTD. Because the quantity of alcohol that is harmful in patients with AH is unknown, we captured all categories of drinking, including occasional drinking (<7 drinks/week). The higher rate of RTD in our study may be related to the lower threshold that we used, and this may also have increased our relative event rate. The prospective follow‐up may also have enabled increased alcohol use detection rates. Regardless, any RTD is a pivotal outcome among patients hospitalized with AH, given the importance RTD has on longer term outcomes, including increased mortality (Altamirano et al., 2017; Louvet et al., 2017). The rates of RTD in our population (31% over 6 months) imply a higher rate than in the postliver transplant population (34% over 2–3 years) (Lee et al., 2018; Louvet et al., 2022). This difference likely reflects fundamental differences in patient selection, intensity of multidisciplinary addiction support, and external accountability in the posttransplant setting, underscoring the need for earlier, structured, and sustained addiction‐focused interventions following an episode of AH.

We identified risk factors for early RTD after an episode of AH. The number of drinking days prior to hospitalization is often overlooked as a risk factor for RTD, and it appeared to be stronger than other alcohol parameters for predicting RTD in our cohort. In fact, those who drank >20 days in the previous 30 days at the time of enrollment had more than a threefold increased odds of RTD. The level of education was also important. Patients with a college education or greater had half the odds of RTD versus those with less than college level of education. This finding suggests that we may need to tailor interventions to the characteristics of our patients. However, the level of education is also a surrogate for income and potentially for access to AUD treatments, which we did not measure in this study. While all patients with AH merit AUD evaluation and treatment, these findings also identify a subgroup that may benefit from improving access to care for AUD and/or more intensive treatment.

In addition, we tested several a priori hypotheses. RCT participants had similar RTD to OBS participants, contrary to what we had predicted. This may, in part, be explained by the increased loss to follow‐up among the OBS cohort (29%) versus RCT subjects (24%) among AH survivors at Day 28. A selection bias may also contribute to our finding of lower risk for RTD in the OBS cohort because the RCT patients had additional early follow‐up visits that may have increased the odds of RTD detection and/or provided support for not RTD. Patients with moderate AH (i.e., lower MELD scores) had higher rates of RTD compared with those with sAH that maybe related to lower motivation for abstinence in patients with less severe AH. More rapid recovery may also encourage earlier RTD. These data lay the foundation for early AUD interventions across the entire spectrum of AH, including for those with moderate AH. Future prospective studies to evaluate these novel findings are needed, including examining the high rate of RTD in moderate AH. In a recent study, Lactobacillus GG was shown to improve the MELD score and lower RTD in patients with moderate AH and might be considered for these patients (Vatsalya et al., 2023).

We had expected obese participants to have lower rates of RTD because of more severe ALD with obesity (Loomba et al., 2009; Lu et al., 2018). Higher BMI did have a trend to lower rates of RTD, but it was not statistically significant. Since both obesity and bariatric surgery have been shown to be related to alcohol use (Grucza et al., 2010; Ivezaj et al., 2019), further studies are needed to determine the relation between obesity and RTD in AH.

Limitations of our study include the loss to follow‐up at 28 days, which introduces selection bias. However, the low retention rates are recognized in patients with ALD and AUD, and our follow‐up rates are higher than many others reported (Borg et al., 2018). Characterization of AUD‐directed interventions represents an additional important limitation and reflects broader gaps in the evidence base for patients with AH. While several studies have examined AUD treatment in patients with ALD, including cirrhosis, patients with AH have historically been considered too medically fragile or high risk for routine use of MAUD. As a result, standards of care have evolved but remain incomplete, and integrated liver‐addiction treatment models were not discussed or implemented when the “AlcHepNet” studies were initiated. Available behavioral‐intervention data are limited to two studies, one evaluating inpatient rehabilitation and the other including inpatient or intensive outpatient rehabilitation, approaches that are not feasible or accessible for most patients and are strongly dependent on insurance coverage (Parlati et al., 2025; Peeraphatdit et al., 2020). In our study, all participants received counseling on alcohol cessation and were offered referral for AUD treatment at discharge, consistent with site‐specific standard clinical care; however, no study‐mandated or standardized behavioral intervention was implemented, and engagement with addiction services was not systematically recorded. However, relapse to drinking (RTD) rates are reported stratified by MAUD exposure. Because AUD treatments were not randomly assigned, the higher RTD rates observed among patients receiving MAUD may reflect confounding by indication, such as greater AUD severity, or initiation of pharmacotherapy in response to RTD rather than a causal effect of treatment on relapse risk. These limitations directly informed the design of the ongoing NIAAA‐funded Integrated Therapies for Alcohol‐Associated Liver Disease (ITAALD) study, which prospectively integrates standardized substance use‐focused interventions with liver‐directed care and longitudinal alcohol‐use assessment to better define strategies for preventing RTD in this population.

The absence of standardized, longitudinal capture of AUD treatment limits direct estimation of its impact on RTD in this cohort; however, existing data provide important context for interpreting our findings. In patients with ALD, pooled randomized and observational data indicate that engagement in AUD treatment is associated with a markedly lower risk of relapse, with reported hazard ratios of approximately 0.27 for AUD treatment overall and 0.23 for MAUD specifically (Singal et al., 2025). Complementing these findings, Mayo Clinic data demonstrate that early participation in structured alcohol rehabilitation following hospitalization for sAH is associated with approximately 90% lower odds of RTD at 30 days in observational cohorts (Peeraphatdit et al., 2020). Taken together, these data underscore that post‐AH relapse risk is highly modifiable. Accordingly, the RTD rates observed in our cohort should be interpreted as reflecting outcomes under heterogeneous, real‐world exposure to AUD treatment rather than optimized, systematic care pathways. If MAUD were consistently implemented, the proportion of RTD events that might be preventable could be substantial. As a conservative sensitivity estimate, assuming an approximate 75% relative reduction in RTD with effective MAUD (Singal et al., 2025) treatment of even 25%–50% of eligible patients could plausibly translate into a 20%–40% reduction in observed RTD events, depending on treatment eligibility, adherence, and relapse definitions.

In conclusion, our prospective studies in well‐characterized patients with AH of varying severity showed that RTD occurred in 22% of patients within the first 90 days and 31% within 180 days after recovery from AH. More than 20 drinking days in the past month and less than a college education were associated with early RTD. Our data show the need for early AUD interventions in AH, irrespective of severity.
