Effects of Phototherapy on Sleep, Fatigue and Quality of Life in Dermatology Patients: A Pilot Study
Authors: Hazel Ezgi Kaya, Asli Aksu, Pinar Ozdemir Cetinkaya, Birgul Ozkesici Kurt, Ali Tanakol, Ilknur Kivanc Altunay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSleep disturbances and fatigue are prevalent among dermatology patients. Owing to the growing evidence on the effects of light-based therapies on neuropsychiatric disorders, this study aimed to investigate how narrow-band ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) phototherapy influences sleep quality and fatigue.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 30 patients receiving or scheduled to receive NB-UVB phototherapy were included. Phototherapy was administered 2–3 times per week with eye protection. Sleep quality, fatigue, and dermatology-specific quality of life were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Piper Fatigue Scale (PFS), and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), respectively. Measurements were performed at study entry and at the one-month follow-up.ResultsThe mean age was 48.2±12.9 years, and 63.3% of the patients were male. The most common diagnoses were vitiligo (40%), mycosis fungoides (26.7%), and psoriasis vulgaris (13.3%). The median PSQI score decreased from 8 at baseline to 5 at the one-month follow-up (p<0.001). The median PFS score decreased from 3.88 to 2.38 (p=0.019). DLQI showed no significant change. Changes in PSQI and PFS were significantly correlated (r=0.435, p=0.016). Multiple regression analysis identified ΔPFS, total cumulative dose, and ΔDLQI as independent predictors of ΔPSQI.ConclusionNB-UVB phototherapy was associated with improvements in sleep quality and fatigue in dermatology patients over the course of treatment. These effects appear to be independent of changes in dermatology-specific quality of life and are likely mediated through neuroendocrine, circadian, and peripheral pathways. NB-UVB phototherapy may serve as an effective adjunctive treatment for sleep disturbances and fatigue in dermatology patients.

Increased exposure to natural daylight and reduced nocturnal exposure to artificial short-wavelength blue light are well-established factors associated with improved sleep duration and sleep quality.[1] Within this conceptual framework, the therapeutic use of light to treat sleep disorders was first investigated in 1990, when Rosenthal et al. [2] demonstrated the effectiveness of timed bright light exposure in patients with delayed sleep phase syndrome. Since then, light therapy has been studied across a broad range of sleep-related conditions, including insomnia, circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders, and sleep disturbances associated with Alzheimer’s disease, dementia, and other chronic neurological conditions.[3] Most of this literature has focused on visible-spectrum bright light, artificial light exposure, and infrared light and has predominantly evolved within the field of neuropsychiatry.

In dermatology, narrow-band ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) phototherapy is widely used as a therapeutic modality, and its effects on mood, depressive symptoms, and overall well-being in dermatological patients have been clearly documented.[4] However, the effects of photobiomodulation within the NB-UVB spectrum on sleep quality and fatigue have been evaluated only in a limited number of studies conducted in healthy populations,[5,6] and data from dermatology patients are lacking. Given the high burden of sleep disturbance and fatigue in dermatological diseases,[7] this study aimed to investigate the effects of NB-UVB phototherapy on sleep quality, fatigue, and dermatology-related quality of life by comparing study entry and one-month follow-up assessments.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective observational study was conducted in the dermatology department of a tertiary care center. Patients aged ≥18 years who were receiving or scheduled to receive NB-UVB phototherapy for dermatological indications were consecutively recruited. The exclusion criteria included a known diagnosis of primary sleep disorder, psychiatric illness, shift work, pregnancy/lactation, and inability to complete self-administered questionnaires. Analyses were performed using a complete-case approach; participants with missing outcome data were excluded from the final analysis. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was approved by the Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital Health Practice and Research Center ethics committee on September 2, 2025 (approval number: 5042) and conducted in accordance with the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Phototherapy Protocol

NB-UVB phototherapy was administered using a whole-body phototherapy unit emitting UVB radiation at a wavelength of 311–313 nm (Waldmann UV 7002, Germany). Treatment sessions were performed two to three times weekly on non-consecutive days in accordance with standard protocols. The eyes were protected with opaque goggles during each session. Skin phototype was determined prior to treatment according to the Fitzpatrick scale. The initial dose and subsequent dose increments were determined on the basis of skin phototype, underlying dermatological diagnosis, and clinical response, with adjustments made as required for treatment-related adverse effects. Patients were advised to avoid additional sun exposure between sessions. For each patient, the total number of sessions, total treatment duration (in seconds), and total cumulative dose (in J/cm2) during the study period were recorded as treatment-related parameters.

Outcome Measures

Sociodemographic data and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index, smoking and alcohol use, marital and employment status, educational level, comorbidities, duration of dermatological disease, and the presence of symptoms such as pruritus or pain, were recorded at the time of study entry. Disease severity was assessed using the Physician’s Global Assessment (PGA) and categorized as mild, moderate, or severe.

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the most widely used self-assessment tool, developed by Buysse et al.[8] and validated in Turkish by Ağargün et al.[9] The PSQI consists of seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. The questionnaire comprises seven questions scored on a 4-point Likert scale and four open-ended questions scored categorically on a 0–3 scale. The scores for each component, evaluated on a scale of 0–3, are summed to derive a global score ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores are predictive of sleep disturbance.

Fatigue was assessed using the Piper Fatigue Scale (PFS), developed by Piper et al.[10] and adapted into Turkish by Can et al.[11] It consists of 22 items, each rated from 1 to 10, and assesses the patient’s subjective perception of fatigue using four subscales, including behavioral/severity, affective meaning, sensory, and cognitive/mood. The sum of all subscales provides the global score, while the five open-ended questions in the scale are not included in the scoring. Higher scores reflect increased fatigue.

The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) was developed by Finlay et al.,[12] and its Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Ozturkcan et al.[13] The scale consists of 10 questions ranging from 0 to 3 on a 4-point Likert scale, with a global score ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores are indicative of a decline in quality of life.

The study population included both patients newly initiated on phototherapy and those receiving ongoing treatment. Baseline assessments were performed at study entry, irrespective of the prior duration of phototherapy. All questionnaires were administered at study entry (Month 0) and at the one-month follow-up (Month 1).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using visual methods (histograms and probability plots) and analytical methods. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean±standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables and as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between baseline and one-month follow-up were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed variables. Changes in PSQI and PFS were compared using the independent t-test for binary variables and one-way ANOVA for variables with more than two categories. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test with appropriate adjustments for multiple comparisons. Correlations between changes in questionnaire scores (ΔPSQI, ΔPFS, and ΔDLQI) and continuous clinical or treatment-related variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed variables. Associations between changes in questionnaire scores were assessed using appropriate non-parametric tests. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of change in sleep quality. Variables that showed a significant association with ΔPSQI in univariable analyses or were considered clinically relevant were entered into the regression model. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance values and variance inflation factors (VIFs). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.4. Based on the observed effect size (dz=0.86, determined using the final PSQI scores), a total sample size of 30 patients, a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test, an alpha level of 0.05, and the observed correlation between paired measurements (r=0.414), the achieved statistical power was calculated as 0.993.

Results

The study included 30 patients receiving or scheduled to receive phototherapy for dermatological conditions. The mean age of the cohort was 48.2±12.9 years, and 19 patients (63.3%) were male. The most common diagnosis was vitiligo (n=12; 40%), followed by mycosis fungoides (n=8; 26.7%) and psoriasis vulgaris (n=4; 13.3%). Other diagnoses included reactive perforating collagenosis (n=2; 6.7%), generalized morphea (n=2; 6.7%), lichen sclerosus et atrophicus (n=1; 3.3%), and generalized granuloma annulare (n=1; 3.3%). According to the Physician’s Global Assessment, 14 patients (46.7%) were categorized as having severe disease severity, 11 patients (36.7%) as having moderate disease severity, and 5 patients (16.7%) as having mild disease severity. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean number of phototherapy sessions was 11.83±2.59 (7–16), the median total treatment duration was 1381.5 seconds (426.0–1854.0), and the median total cumulative phototherapy dose was 6.10 J/cm2 (1.70–7.71).

The median PSQI score was 8 (4–11) at baseline and decreased to 5 (3–8) at the one-month follow-up, with a statistically significant difference between the two time points (p<0.001). A similar improvement was observed in fatigue levels, as reflected by a significant reduction in the median PFS score from 3.88 (2.11–5.65) at baseline to 2.38 (1.69–3.71) at the one-month follow-up (p=0.019). In contrast, the median DLQI score was 11.5 (7.0–18.0) at baseline and 9.5 (3.0–16.0) after 1 month, with no statistically significant difference between the two time points (p>0.05). Examination of the PFS subscales revealed a statistically significant decrease only in the cognition/mood subscale (p=0.002), without any significant changes in the remaining subscale scores (p>0.05).

Changes in PSQI and PFS scores were not statistically significantly associated with sociodemographic characteristics that could potentially affect sleep and fatigue, including age, gender, body mass index, smoking or alcohol use, marital status, educational level, employment status, and medical comorbidities (p>0.05). No significant associations were observed between changes in PSQI and any of the clinical variables. Similarly, changes in PFS scores were not associated with clinical characteristics, except for symptom status, which exhibited a significant effect (p=0.005). Post hoc analysis revealed that the reduction in PFS scores was lower in patients with pruritus than in those without pruritus (p=0.025) and lower than in those with other symptoms, such as pain or burning/stinging (p=0.010). No significant difference in PFS change was observed between patients without symptoms and those with non-pruritic symptoms (p=0.280).

Changes in PSQI scores were moderately and significantly correlated with changes in PFS scores (r=0.435, p=0.016) (Fig. 3). No significant correlations were found between PFS changes and any treatment-related parameters. Changes in PSQI scores were moderately and negatively correlated with total treatment duration (rho=−0.400, p=0.029) and total cumulative phototherapy dose (rho=−0.371, p=0.043) (Fig. 4). Changes in DLQI scores were not significantly correlated with PSQI changes (p>0.05), whereas a moderate and significant correlation was observed between DLQI and PFS changes (rho=0.501, p=0.005). When changes in DLQI scores were compared with treatment-related parameters, a moderate negative correlation was observed with total treatment duration (rho=−0.430, p=0.018).

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of change in PSQI scores (ΔPSQI). The overall model was statistically significant (F[3, 26]=6.836, p=0.002), explaining 44.1% of the variance in ΔPSQI (adjusted R2=0.376). Among the predictors, changes in PFS (ΔPFS) scores (β=0.707, p=0.001), total cumulative dose (β=−0.558, p=0.003), and changes in DLQI (ΔDLQI) scores (β=−0.546, p=0.012) were significant independent predictors of ΔPSQI. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed acceptable tolerance values (0.519–0.718) and variance inflation factors (VIFs ranging from 1.393 to 1.927), indicating no significant collinearity issues among the predictors.

Discussion

In this study, the effects of NB-UVB phototherapy were evaluated in patients with various dermatological conditions. Significant improvement in sleep quality and a reduction in fatigue levels were observed, and these changes, which were independent of the type of dermatological condition and correlated with total cumulative phototherapy dose, may occur before measurable changes in dermatology-related quality of life. These findings suggest that phototherapy can influence sleep and fatigue parameters during treatment, even prior to detectable changes in dermatology-specific quality of life, highlighting the relevance of patient-reported measures in capturing therapeutic benefits.

Photobiomodulation has been investigated for its effects on sleep quality in a variety of neurological conditions, including Alzheimer’s disease,[14] Parkinson’s disease,[15] primary sleep disorders,[16] and post-traumatic brain injury.[17] Meta-analytic evidence indicates that light therapy produces measurable improvements across multiple sleep-related outcomes. Significant effects have been reported for parameters such as circadian phase alignment, bedtime and sleep onset timing, sleep efficiency, subjective sleep quality, sleep onset latency, total sleep time, insomnia severity, and fatigue.[3] Collectively, these findings suggest that light therapy represents a biologically active, non-pharmacological intervention with the potential to exert clinically meaningful effects on both sleep and related daytime symptoms.

Light therapy exerts its effects on sleep primarily through the modulation of endogenous melatonin rhythms.[18] Light sources with similar visual brightness may differ substantially in their biological potency, particularly with respect to melanopsin-mediated pathways involved in circadian regulation.[19] Increasing evidence indicates that, independent of illuminance expressed in lux, the spectral characteristics of light play a critical role in the modulation of circadian rhythms, sleep, and alertness, as visually perceived brightness and melanopic stimulation may diverge at equivalent lux levels. Although Brown et al. [20] clearly distinguished melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, with peak sensitivity to blue–turquoise light in the visible spectrum at around 490 nm, from photopic visual sensitivity, which peaks at approximately 555 nm, NB-UVB light at 311 nm is unlikely to directly stimulate melanopsin-expressing retinal ganglion cells because of ocular filtering and routine eye protection during phototherapy. Nevertheless, light exposure is increasingly recognized to exert systemic effects beyond vision. Phototherapy may influence sleep and fatigue indirectly through cutaneous and neuroendocrine pathways, potentially interacting with circadian regulatory mechanisms independent of visual photoreception. These non-visual, skin-mediated mechanisms may help explain the improvement in sleep quality observed following NB-UVB treatment.

The skin has a circadian rhythm, and the melanocyte network in the skin functions as a photosensory system.[21] Ultraviolet (UV) light exposure activates melanocytes via dendritic extension, modulating catecholamine and neurotransmitter levels. Serotonin increases during light exposure, while melatonin rises during darkness, forming a reciprocal serotonin–melatonin switch that reflects photoperiod length, similar to the pineal gland.[22] Dose- and time-dependent changes in melanocytic enzyme activity, including N-acetyltransferase, hydroxyindole-O-methyltransferase, and triphasic dopaoxidase, regulate serotonin-to-melatonin conversion and catecholamine synthesis.[23] Melanocytes also express hormones such as adrenocorticotropic hormone, prolactin, and growth hormone in response to UV, linking light perception to neuroendocrine regulation.[23] In addition to melanocytes, keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts have been shown to actively participate in the regulation of cutaneous melatonin rhythmicity.[21,24] Moreover, low-dose ultraviolet B rays alter the mRNA expression of circadian clock genes (Per1, Clock, bmal1/mop3) in human keratinocytes, suggesting that superficial skin layers contribute to circadian rhythm modulation.[25] These chemical signals are integrated with dermal neural and vascular networks, transmitting environmental light information to the central nervous system through the intimate association of melanocytes with dermal nerves and vasculature and influencing circadian rhythms, pigmentation, and seasonal physiological changes.[22]

The application of light-based therapies in musculoskeletal conditions can be broadly categorized into two main approaches: low-level laser therapy in the red and near-infrared spectrum and narrow-band light-emitting diode-based photobiomodulation, which are primarily employed to enhance muscle performance and reduce exercise-induced fatigue,[26] and bright white or blue-enriched white light, commonly used in conditions such as fibromyalgia,[27] multiple sclerosis,[28] traumatic brain injury,[29] and chronic fatigue syndrome.[30] Phototherapy aimed at improving muscle performance demonstrates efficacy at local doses of approximately 5–6 J for small muscle groups and up to 275 J for larger muscles.[31] In our study, the median cumulative dose administered to the whole body was 6.10 J/cm2, which is unlikely to have been delivered to a single muscle group. Consequently, no significant changes were observed in the behavioral/severity, affective meaning, or sensory subdomains of the PFS; however, reductions in fatigue were reflected in improvements in the cognitive/mood subscale. These findings suggest that the observed fatigue reduction may be mediated by the effects of light therapy on sleep quality,[3,5,6] cognitive function,[32] and mood regulation, consistent with its reported therapeutic potential in seasonal affective disorder and other mood-related conditions.[33] Collectively, these studies highlight multiple peripheral mechanisms underlying cutaneous light-induced analgesia, including activation of peripheral opioid receptors,[34] modulation of transient receptor potential voltage channels by inhibition of protein kinase A/C signaling,[35] cytoskeletal alterations in Aδ- and C-fibers in nociceptive nerves to decrease nociceptive transmission,[36] reductions in local pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress, and promotion of M2 macrophage polarization in the dorsal root ganglion.[37] These convergent pathways likely act synergistically to reduce nociceptive transmission and hyperalgesia, providing a mechanistic rationale for the analgesic effects of light-based therapies. This framework may also explain why patients experiencing pain or burning sensations in our study exhibited greater fatigue improvement compared with those with pruritus, as the neural pathways and underlying mechanisms mediating pruritus are distinct and more dependent on dermatological improvement.

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design without a longitudinal control group and the inclusion of patients at different stages of phototherapy limit causal interpretation regarding the effects of NB-UVB phototherapy on sleep and fatigue. Although sleep quality improved significantly over the study period, dermatology-specific quality of life showed no significant change and was not directly correlated with PSQI score changes. However, multivariable regression suggested that individual changes in DLQI independently predicted improvements in sleep quality. These results indicate a potential temporal relationship, in which improvements in sleep quality may precede detectable changes in dermatology-specific quality of life, although causality cannot be confirmed. Second, the relatively small sample size and short follow-up period restrict the generalizability of our findings and preclude the assessment of long-term outcomes. Third, cumulative NB-UVB doses were relatively low and likely insufficient to directly influence isolated muscle fatigue, limiting mechanistic insights into dose-dependent effects. Finally, the heterogeneity of dermatological diagnoses and symptom profiles may have introduced variability in patient-reported outcomes. Although subgroup analyses suggest that improvements in sleep quality and fatigue may occur independently of the underlying dermatological condition, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample sizes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, NB-UVB phototherapy produces early improvements in sleep quality and reductions in fatigue among dermatology patients, effects that appear to be independent of measurable changes in dermatology-specific quality of life and are likely mediated through peripheral neuroendocrine, circadian, and mechanistic pathways. Given its independent and multidimensional effects on sleep and fatigue, phototherapy may be considered a prioritized adjunctive treatment for dermatology patients experiencing sleep disturbances and fatigue.
