Assessment of the minimal clinically important difference for the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index in patients with arthralgia at risk for progression to rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Stijn Claassen, Hanna W van Steenbergen, Annette H M van der Helm-van Mil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with arthralgia at-risk for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) experience considerable functional disability, though generally less than at RA diagnosis. Secondary prevention trials have shown that treatment can improve disability in patients with arthralgia. However, interpreting the clinical relevancy of the improvements in Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI) is hampered by the lack of a defined minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in this disease stage. Results from other disciplines than rheumatology suggested that the MCID depends on absolute severity values. Therefore, we hypothesised that the MCID for HAQ-DI in RA depends on absolute values. We aimed to investigate this and, if so, to determine the MCID for disability in the risk setting.MethodsWe studied the literature and determined the correlation of baseline HAQ-DI and MCID estimates in RA. To determine the MCID in arthralgia, we studied 97 patients treated with methotrexate in the TREAT EARLIER trial with HAQ-DI data at baseline and 12 months. At 12 months, a short-form 36 questionnaire anchor question compared patients’ general health to that of 1 year before. The MCID was determined using the mean change in HAQ-DI score of patients reporting ‘somewhat better’ and ‘somewhat worse’.ResultsIn RA, the MCID estimates ranged from −0.06 to −0.38, and higher absolute HAQ values correlated with a higher MCID. In the at-risk patients studied, the MCID for improvement was −0.07±0.28. Likewise, for deterioration, the MCID was +0.05±0.6.ConclusionIn arthralgia at-risk for RA, the MCID for improvement in HAQ-DI is −0.07. This is lower than generally reported in RA. This implies that in arthralgia, compared with RA, smaller improvements in HAQ-DI are clinically relevant.

Introduction

Patients with arthralgia at risk for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) already have a considerable disease burden at their first presentation, which is reflected by impaired physical functioning.1 This functional disability, as measured by Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI), increases towards the RA diagnosis. The functional disability is associated with the underlying MRI-detected subclinical joint inflammation, revealing its inflammatory nature.1 2 Recently, two secondary prevention trials in patients with arthralgia at-risk of RA demonstrated sustained improvements in functional disability after a course of Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs (DMARD) treatment.3 4 Although these findings are promising, especially for patients themselves, it is unknown what improvement is clinically relevant in this setting.

A minimal clinically important difference (MCID) can help evaluate whether the mean differences reported in a trial meet a clinically meaningful threshold of improvement. The MCID states the minimal change or difference in the outcome measure, which is perceived as important and beneficial by the clinician or patient.5 In other fields, such as that of chronic pain, it was demonstrated that MCID estimates of Visual Analogue Scale pain are context-specific. Two-thirds of the variations in MCID estimates of chronic pain in musculoskeletal disease were attributable to the baseline pain measurements, thus to pain severity.6 In addition, the study design and definition of improvement influenced the MCID. For RA, it is unclear whether the MCID for HAQ also depends on the degree of disability, that is, on the baseline HAQ value.

We hypothesised that severity of disability (baseline HAQ-DI) correlates with MCID estimates of disability in RA. If this is true, it implies that an MCID for the setting of arthralgia needs to be ascertained as arthralgia at-risk patients generally have better HAQ-DI levels than patients at RA diagnosis.1 To this end, we will first explore the relationship between baseline HAQ-DI and published MCID estimates in RA. If the literature search shows a dependency of absolute disability levels, we will proceed with determining the MCID in the at-risk phase of arthralgia. Our ultimate aim is to support the accurate interpretation of results from (future) prevention trials in patients with arthralgia at-risk for RA.

Methods

Literature search

We conducted a targeted literature review using PubMed until April 2025 with a core search string of “rheumatoid arthritis” and “minimal clinically important difference” AND “physical function” (details and the full search strategy are supplied in online supplemental file 1). For each study, we extracted the absolute MCID for improvement in HAQ, mean baseline HAQ, patient population and study method.

Patients

To determine the MCID of HAQ-DI in patients with arthralgia at-risk of RA, we studied the 119 patients treated with methotrexate in the TREAT EARLIER trial(3). This trial included adults with recent onset (within the past year) arthralgia suspected of progressing to RA, according to the rheumatologist, and MRI detected subclinical joint inflammation in hand or foot joints. By definition, clinical arthritis or another explanation for the arthralgia was not present, and patients were included regardless of autoantibody status. Further details on study design are provided in online supplemental file 2. Patients in the treatment arm received a single dose of IM glucocorticoids and a 1 year treatment of methotrexate up to 25 mg/week. Patients were followed on RA development with 4 monthly intervals.

HAQ-DI assessment

97 of the 119 patients included in the treatment arm of the trial who completed the HAQ-DI questionnaire (ranging 0–3) at baseline and at 12 months were selected for this analysis.7 As with the MCID determination for RA, only patients from the treated population were studied to ensure that the anchor reflected a true clinical change.810 Patients without treatment (placebo arm) were not studied; their disease course may vary in severity due to regression to the mean and/or nonspecific improvement, which could confound the findings. If patients developed RA prior to the 12-month mark (n=7, mean time to arthritis 209 days), the HAQ-DI score at the time of RA diagnosis was used. Details of baseline variables comparing patients with and without data are provided in online supplemental file 3.

Anchor

At 12 months (or at RA diagnosis), patients completed a short-form 36 questionnaire (SF-36). Question 2, from the SF-36 was used as the anchor for our analysis: ‘Compared with 1 year ago, how would you rate your health in general now?’ on a 5-point Likert scale labelled ‘much better’, ‘somewhat better’, ‘about the same’, ‘somewhat worse’ and ‘much worse’.11

Statistical analysis

Spearman’s rank was used to assess the correlation between baseline HAQ-DI and MCID estimates, as well as between changes in the anchor and HAQ-DI score to evaluate anchor validity. A correlation coefficient of 0.30–0.35 or higher was considered acceptable to justify the use of our anchor question for the HAQ-DI MCID calculation, based on previous recommendations.12 The minimal clinically important differences for both improvement and worsening were calculated using the mean HAQ-DI changes between baseline and 12 months of the subgroup who rated their general health as ‘somewhat better’ and ‘somewhat worse’ on the anchor question, similar to methods used previously in the literature.9

Results

Exploration literature on MCID in RA

Figure 1 presents an overview of the literature and the correlation between MCID estimates and baseline HAQ-DI scores. Reported MCID values for the HAQ-DI in RA varied substantially, ranging from –0.06 to −0.38. A strong positive correlation was observed between baseline HAQ-DI and MCID estimates (rs=0.89, p=0.019). These findings support the hypothesis that the MCID of HAQ-DI derived from RA populations cannot be applied to individuals with arthralgia at risk of RA. Therefore, we continued with determining the MCID specifically in this at-risk population.

Assessment of MCID

Of the 97 patients, 32 (33%) reported no change in general health compared with 1 year ago, whereas 14 (14%) reported ‘somewhat better’ and 19 (19%) somewhat worse. The correlation coefficient between the anchor question and the change in HAQ-DI was 0.45, representing a good correlation.

The estimate for the minimal clinically important improvement (MCII; ‘somewhat better’) was −0.071 (0.28) and the estimate for minimal clinically important worsening (MCIW; ‘somewhat worse’) was +0.046 (0.60).

Discussion

We observed that the MCID in HAQ-DI in RA varies considerably between studies and is correlated with their severity of disability at baseline. This dependency on the severity of the outcome has also been described for pain scores in chronic pain.6 This prompted us to determine the MCID for HAQ-DI in patients with arthralgia at risk for RA. Our data demonstrate that the MCII for HAQ-DI improvement in arthralgia is −0.07, and the MCIW for worsening is +0.05. Comparing these findings to those obtained in RA suggests that a smaller change on the HAQ-DI scale can be considered clinically relevant for both improvement and worsening in the setting of arthralgia at-risk for RA compared with at RA diagnosis.

Functional disability is an important outcome for individuals with arthralgia at-risk of RA development.13 This importance is underscored by the finding that two-thirds of individuals with arthralgia at-risk consider improvements in disease burden to be at least equally important to preventing the diagnosis of RA.13 Despite HAQ-DI being widely used in at-risk populations, it was unexplored which change in functional disability is clinically relevant to patients. Defining the MCID for HAQ-DI improvement is essential for interpreting the results of trials in this setting. Two recent secondary prevention trials showed that the HAQ-DI significantly decreased after treatment with abatacept and methotrexate, the improvements in HAQ-DI were −0.09 and −0.22, respectively.3 4 The current finding of an MCID of −0.07 suggests that both improvements in disability were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful to patients themselves.

In the at-risk setting, the absolute HAQ values are somewhat lower than those at RA diagnosis of patients with active disease (mostly around −0.20, figure 1). In line with this, we found a smaller MCID estimate (−0.07) compared with MCID values often found in patients with RA. This implies that patients with less severe disability (lower absolute values) need smaller improvements to achieve a clinically meaningful change in functional disability. Only one study in RA found an MCID of −0.06, which is similar to our findings; interestingly, the baseline HAQ in this study was relatively low (0.71) and similar to the HAQ in the at-risk population studied here.10 These findings may indicate that it is not the disease phase (arthralgia at-risk vs established RA), but the severity of the HAQ that determines the minimal clinically important difference estimates. This would also imply that the MCID found here can be generalised to other arthralgia at-risk for RA populations. We selected the at-risk population based on a clinical phenotype (that of clinically suspect arthralgia [CSA]) and a positive MRI. Other populations are selected on arthralgia and the presence of anti-citrillunated protein antibodies (ACPAs). Because the HAQ is similar in these populations (eg, the baseline HAQ in the TREAT EARLIER trial was 0.7; this was 0.6 in two other recent prevention trials of ACPA-positive arthralgia),4 14 the MCID identified here will be generalisable to other symptomatic at-risk settings. The only exception might be the asymptomatic at-risk population, for example, ACPA-positive symptom-free relatives of patients with RA.15 We did not find HAQ estimates from this at-risk setting, but because these at-risk individuals are asymptomatic, the HAQ would be expected to be around normal levels. Therefore, the MCID found here may not be representative for an asymptomatic at-risk setting.

This study has limitations. We studied the treatment arm of the TREAT EARLIER trial. The current sample size of 97 patients is lower than the sample size of some similar studies of MCID conducted in RA. This may have resulted in overlapping confidence intervals across anchor response categories. However, larger studies done in RA also showed overlapping confidence intervals.8 9 16 17 Nonetheless, our findings are consistent with our hypothesis of the correlation of baseline HAQ and MCID as seen in RA and align with results observed in RA, showcasing the robustness of our results.

In conclusion, the MCID estimates for HAQ-DI in arthralgia at-risk of RA are −0.07 for improvement and +0.05 for worsening. This provides patient-centred thresholds that enhance interpretation of current and future clinical trial outcomes in the arthralgia at risk for RA population. This implies that in arthralgia, compared with RA, smaller improvements in HAQ-DI are clinically relevant.
