A novel snake venom C-type lectin-like protein modulates blood coagulation by targeting von Willebrand factor and coagulation factor IX
Authors: Fan-Yu Zeng, Ren-Sheng Ji, Xiao-Qin Yu, Ya-Nan Li, Qi-Yun Zhang, Qian-Yun Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSnake venom C-type lectin-like proteins (CLPs) belong to the nonenzymatic proteins. To date, no CLP with both platelet and coagulation factors activating activities has been reported. In this study, a novel CLP, termed protocetin, with molecular weight of 29.986 kDa, was purified from the Protobothrops mucrosquamatus venom (PMV). It consists of α- and β-chains, with 67% similarity in their N-terminal sequence. Protocetin activates glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) by binding to von Willebrand factor (vWF), inducing platelet aggregation. It also activates the intrinsic coagulation pathway by binding to coagulation factor IX. After injection of protocetin into mice at dose of 0.5 µg/g or 1.5 µg/g, it resulted in activation of platelets, a notable reduction in platelet count and prolonged tail bleeding time. Additionally, the plasma activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was significantly extended, and the fibrinogen concentration was markedly reduced. Thrombelastogram comfirmed the anticoagulation effect of protocetin. Notably, no microthrombosis was observed in tissues of lung, liver and kidney within 1 h after injection of protocetin into the mice at dose of 0.5 µg/g. This study revealed protocetin as a novel CLP from PMV that has dual functions in activating platelet and coagulation factor IX, thereby modulates coagulation in vivo. Introduction

Venomous snakes utilize their venom to immobilize and digest prey, as well as fend off or weaken predators1. Snake venom compounds can induce a range of biological effects at the local or systemic level2. Therefore, it is necessary to study and characterize the life-threatening components of venom, and transform them into sources of life-saving and therapeutic effects.

The primary constituents of venom are enzymes and nonenzymatic proteins3, which account for 90-95% of the dry weight of the venom, nonenzymatic proteins interfere with the physiological processes of prey and are crucial for immobilizing prey by binding to specific receptors, ion channels, and plasma proteins1,4. Nonenzymatic proteins include three-finger toxins, proteinase inhibitors, CLPs (also known as snaclecs), nerve growth factors, etc4. CLPs, as nonenzymatic proteins, can act on various coagulation factors, platelets, and endothelial cell receptors, participating in hemostasis, thrombosis, inflammation, and hematogenous metastasis5. CLPs are beneficial for venom snake to immobilize prey and prey digestion6. All known CLPs are consisting of one or more heterodimers, with each polypeptide chain having an approximate molecular weight of 13–15 kDa. The amino acid sequence homology of CLPs ranges from approximately 30% to 90%7. Despite their structural similarity, their biological activities and mechanisms of action vary. Some of these proteins can bind to coagulation factors IX/X and α- thrombin to form a complex that blocks subsequent coagulation cascade reactions, thereby exhibiting anticoagulant activity8–13. Others inhibit or promote platelet activation by binding to platelet membrane glycoprotein b (GPIb) or membrane glycoprotein VI (GPVI)14–18. Additionally, some CLPs can bind to different sites on vWF, altering its spatial conformation so that it can bind to GPIb, thus inducing platelet aggregation19. To date, all known CLPs only possess the activity of binding to coagulation factor IX/X or platelet. In this paper, a novel CLP from PMV was reported, which modulates coagulation by binding to vWF and coagulation factor IX.

Materials and methods

Materials

All materials are list in Supplementary materials.

Purification

Briefly, 0.5 g of lyophilized PMV was dissolved in 3 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), which contained 0.147 mol/L NaCl and 0.01 mol/L Na2HPO4-NaH2PO4 buffer at a pH of 7.4. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and sampled using a Sephadex G-75 (2.6 cm×100 cm) that was balanced with PBS. The elution process continued with PBS at flow rate of 24 mL/h. Samples were collected at 4 mL per tube, and 200 µL from each tube were added to a UV microplate for measurement under 280 nm conditions. Fractions with platelet aggregation activity were pooled and desalinated with Sephadex G-25. After concentration, the sample was loaded onto a 1 mL Hitrap Heparin HP using an AKTA Prime protein chromatography system. PB (0.01 mol/L Na2HPO4 NaH2PO4 buffer, pH = 7.0) was used as the A-phase equilibrium for heparin affinity chromatography, and gradient elution was performed with B solution containing 1 mol of NaCl at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Fractions containing platelet aggregation activity were collected and sampled on 1 mL HiTrap SP by AKTA Prime. PB was used as a liquid equilibrium for anion exchange chromatography, and gradient elution was performed with B liquid containing 1 mol of NaCl at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The fractions containing platelet aggregation activity were collected, and the purity was determed via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE). The protein concentration was quantified using a BCA, after which the samples were stored in an ultralow temperature freezer at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Electrophoresis

The molecular weight of protocetin was determined by SDS‒PAGE following the method of Laemmli20 under reducing and nonreducing conditions. The detailed method is presented in the supplementary materials.

Isoelectric focusing (IEF) was performed to determine the isoelectric point of protocetin according to the method described by Robertson et al.21. The pH range of the amphoteric electrolyte amphorine was 3.5–10, and the pH gradient was determined using the KCl immersion method after electrophoresis.

Molecular weight determination by gel filtration

Gel filtration was conducted on a Sephacrycl S-100 (1.0 cm × 60 cm) to determine the apparent molecular weight of protocetin. The detailed method is presented in the supplementary materials.

Carbohydrate analysis

The carbohydrate content was analyzed by Schiff reagent staining after SDS‒PAGE according to the glycoprotein staining methods of Zhang and Cheng22.

Mass spectrometry

The accurate molecular weight of protocetin was measured by MALDI-TOF. For peptide fingerprint, the purified protocetin was subjected to reduced SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, the strip containing the protein was cut. Detailed method is presented in the supplementary materials.

Circular dichroism analysis

The absorption pattern of protocetin in the far−UV rang (190–260 nm) was detected and analyzed by using a Chirascan Plus circular dichroism spectrometer. The detailed method is presented in the supplementary materials.

N-terminal sequencing

The purified protocetin was subjected to 15% SDS-PAGE and then electroblotted onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. N-terminal sequencing was performed on the cut out stained bands using Edman degradation with automatic protein peptide sequencing (PPSQ-33 A, Beijing Baitai Paike Biotechnology Consulting Co., Ltd.).

Proteolytic activity assay

The fibrinogenolytic activity assay was conducted according to the method of Sun et al.23, with some modifications. Fibrinolytic activity assay was conducted following the methods described in Sun et al.23, with some modifications. The arginine esterase activity was tested according to the method of Sun et al.23 and Tu et al.24. Azocaseinolytic activity was measured according to the method of Sun et al.23 and Retzios et al.25. The detailed methods are listed in the supplementary materials.

Hemorrhagic activity

Hemorrhagic activity was tested according to the method of Ownby et al.26, with some modifications (The detailed method is listed in the supplementary materials.).

Bleeding time

Bleeding time was measured according to the method of Huang et al.27, with some modifications. Male KM mice, weighing between 18 g and 22 g were injected with 0.5 µg/g protocetin through the tail vein after anesthesia with intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). The normal control group received an equal volume of PBS. After 30 min of injection, the tail was cut at a depth of 1–2 mm, 1.0 cm away from the end of the tail, and then places the tail in normal saline prewarmed at 37 °C. The bleeding status was observed and the time taken for bleeding to stop was recorded. Mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation when the experiment was finished.

Effects of protocetin on platelet and blood clotting parameters in vivo

Assays for platelet count, platelet activation markers and coagulation function

Male KM mice weighing 18–22 g were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg) and then injected with 0.5 µg/g or 1.5 µg/g of protocetin via a tail vein. The control group received with the same volume of PBS. At different time intervals (0.5, 6, 12, and 24 h), the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection, and blood samples were drawn from the inferior vena cava. After the blood samples were collected, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The blood samples were anticoagulated with 3.2% sodium citrate (1:9 v/v citrate/blood). A portion of the blood sample was used for platelet count. From each group, 0.02 mL of anticoagulated whole blood was collected, and 0.78 mL of platelet dilution was added. After complete hemolysis, the cells were mixed well, and 10 µL of platelet suspension was injected into the blood cell counting plate counting pool. The cells were counted under a microscope, and the platelet count was calculated using the following formula: platelet count (cells/L) = platelet count within 5 squares (N) × 5 × 10 × 40 × 10∧6. The remaining blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was used to prepare anticoagulant plasma. The concentrations of PF4, β-TG, and P-selectin in the plasma were detected using ELISA kits. The APTT, PT, TT, and FIB were measured following the instructions of the assay kits.

Assays for thromboelastography and chromogenic substrate

Male KM mice weighing 18–22 g were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital and injected with protocetin via tail vein at a dose of 1.5 µg/g. The group of control received the same volume of PBS. At different time intervals (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h), the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection, and blood samples were drawn from the inferior vena cava. The blood samples were anticoagulated with 3.2% sodium citrate (1:9 v/v citrate/blood). 1 mL of anticoagulated whole blood was measured according to the methods of the thromboelastography ordinary cup test kit. After the blood samples were collected, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation.

The dose and plasma preparation of protocetin for mouse grouping and tail vein injection were the same as those described in Section “Assays for platelet count, platelet activation markers and coagulation function”. The prepared plasma was measured using the single time point method and enzyme kinetic method to determine the activity of the coagulation factor Xa substrate (S2765) and thrombin substrate (S2238) following the reference methods28. 0.01 mg/mL PMV, as positive control, PBS as negative control, 5 µL or 20 µL of plasma, 55 µL or 40 µL of PBS, each mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The absorbance at 405 nm was measured every 15 min using a BioTek Synergy multifunction enzyme labeler (Bio-Tek USA, Inc.).

Platelet aggregation assay

Preparation of platelets

Blood samples were collected from ether the rabbits heart puncture or the human forearm vein. Blood samples were collected from the rabbits once a week. At the end of the experiment, rabbits were euthanized by sudden cervical dislocation and subsequent exsanguination. The blood was mixed with 3.2% sodium citrate (1:9 v/v citrate/blood). A portion of the blood sample was used to prepare platelet−rich plasma (PRP) and platelet−poor plasma (PPP). The platelet count in the PRP was adjusted to 3 × 1011 platelets/L by using PPP.

KM mice weighing 20–23 g were anesthetized with 40 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital. Blood was obtained from the abdominal vein and mixed with 3.2% sodium citrate (1:9 v/v citrate/blood). A portion of the blood sample was used to prepare the PRP and PPP. The platelet count in the PRP was adjusted to 5 × 1011 platelets/L by using PPP. After blood samples were collected, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation.

The preparation of washed platelets was according to the method of Tai et al.14. Blood was collected from the forearm vein and mixed with 3.2% sodium citrate solution at a ratio of 9:1 for anticoagulation. The mixture was then centrifuged at 500 r/min for 10 min to obtain PRP. PRP was suspended in solution A (113 mM NaCl, 4.3 mM K2HPO4, 24.4 mM NaH2PO4, 4.3 mM Na2HPO4, 5.5 mM glucose, pH 6.5) and centrifuged at 250×g for 10 min. Washed platelets were suspended in solution B (20 mM HEPES, 140 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 5.5 mM glucose, pH 7.4), and the platelets count was adjusted to 3 × 1011 platelets/L using solution B. Before the platelet aggregation experiment, 2 mM CaCl2 was added, and the platelets were preincubated at 37 °C for 2 min.

Preparation of gel filtration platelets (GFPs): SD rats weighing 220–290 g were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta and mixed with 3.2% sodium citrate (1:9 v/v citrate/blood). Whole blood was then isolated at 99 ×g for 10 min to obtain PRP. The prepared PRP was loaded onto Sephacryl S-400 columns that had been equilibrated with platelet eluent (137 mmol/L NaCl, 2.5 mmol/L KCl, 0.3 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 12 mmol/L NaHCO3, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 5 mmol/L glucose, 0.35% BSA, pH 7.40). The PRP was eluted at a flow rate of 30 mL/h, and a white turbidity appeared after 9 mL. The elution continued until the eluent became clear. The collected sample solution was centrifuged at 396 ×g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. GFP was obtained by suspending the platelets in platelet eluent. The platelet count in the GFP was adjusted to 3 × 1011 platelets/L using platelet eluent. After blood samples were collected, rats were euthanized via cervical dislocation.

Assays for platelet aggregation

The platelet aggregation assay was conducted using an AG400 platelet aggregation analyzer (Shandong Tailixin Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) according to the method of Wang et al.29. A total of 270 µL of adjusted PRP was preincubated at 37 °C for 5 min, after which thrombin was added to assess the normal function of platelet aggregation. Under the conditions of normal PRP, 30 µL of different concentrations of protocetin were added. The maximum aggregation rate within 5 min was measured using a platelet aggregation instrument. The aggregation experiment of washing platelets was carried out according to the same method. Thrombin was used to assess the normal function of platelet aggregation. A total of 170 µL of human normal washed platelets was preincubated at 37 °C for 30 min with 30 µL of GPIb monoclonal antibody (final concentration: 0, 5, 10, 20, 50 µg/mL). The control group was incubated with 30 µL of PBS for the same duration. Next, 50 µL of washed platelet B solution and 20 µL of PPP were added, and 30 µL of protocetin was added at a final concentration of 60 µg/mL. The maximum aggregation rate within 5 min was measured using platelet aggregation analyzer.

In the GFP aggregation assay, 10 µL of vWF (5 µg) and varying amounts of protocetin (10 µg, 20 µg, 30 µg) or 30 µL of ristocetin (450 µg) were added to PBS to create a 50 µL solution. In the absence of vWF, protocetin (20 ug) and PBS were mixed into a 50 µL solution. This mixture was preincubated at 37 °C for 2 min and then added to 250 µL of GFP. The control group was 50 µL PBS added to 250 µL GFP. The aggregation curve was observed within 5 min.

Competition assay for ristoectin and protocetin: For the control group, 50 µL of PBS was preincubated at 37 °C for 4 min and subsequently added to 250 µL of GFP. In experimental group A, 10 µL of vWF (5 µg) and 30 µL of ristocetin (450 µg) were preincubated for 2 min, followed by the addition of 10 µL of protocetin (20 µg) or PBS. The mixture was further preincubated for an additional 2 min, after which 50 µL of the resulting solution was added 250 µL of GFP. In experimental group B, 10 µL of vWF (5 µg) and 10 µL of protocetin (20 µg) were preincubated for 2 min. Subsequently, 30 µL of ristocetin (450 µg) or PBS was added, and the mixture was again preincubation for 2 min. Finally, 50 µL of the resulting solution was added to 250 µL of GFP.

Assay for coagulation activity in vitro

Plasma preparation of mice: male KM mice weighing 18–22 g were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg) and then blood samples were drawn from the inferior vena cava. After blood samples were collected, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The blood samples were anticoagulated with 3.2% sodium citrate (1:9 v/v citrate/blood), then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min and the supernatant was anticoagulated plasma.

In vitro assay for determining coagulation function

For the APTT and PT assays, 100 µL of mice plasma was mixed with 20 µL of various amounts of protocetin. In the TT assay, 200 µL of mice plasma was mixed with 40 µL of various amounts of protocetin. For the fibrinogen concentration assay, 100 µL of mice plasma was mixed with 40 µL of various amounts of protocetin. The coagulation time was determined according to the instructions provided in the kit.

Coagulation factor-deficient plasma assay

Coagulation factor VIII, IX, XI, XII, II, V, VII, and X deficient plasmas were used in this assay. A total of 100 µL of mice plasma was mixed with 20 µL of PBS/protocetin (at a final concentration of 12.5 µg/mL). Subsequently, 50 µL of the mixed plasma and 50 µL of factor-deficient plasma were combined, and the coagulation time was determined following the instructions provided with the kit.

The coagulation assay of factor-deficient plasma with a series of dilutions was conducted according to the method of Sun et al.30. The detailed method is listed in the supplementary materials.

Coagulation factor correction assay

Correction of the in vivo sample was performed as follows: KM mice were intravenously injected with 1.5 µg/g protocetin, after 30 min of injection, blood was taken from the inferior vena cava after intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). After blood samples were collected, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The obtained blood samples were used to prepare PPP; 100 µL of PPP was mixed with 20 µL of coagulation factors, and 100 µL of the mixed plasma was subsequently added to 100 µL of prewarmed APTT reagent. After incubating at 37 °C for 5 min, 100 µL of CaCl2 was added to record the coagulation time.

The in vitro samples were corrected as follows: 10 µL of protocetin and 10 µL of coagulation factor (final concentration of protocetin: 12.5 µg/mL) were added to 100 µL mice plasma. 100 µL of the mixed plasma was then added to 100 µL of prewarmed APTT reagent, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 5 min. Finally, 100 µL of CaCl2 was added to record the coagulation time.

Assays for thromboelastography and chromogenic substrate

Thromboelastography determination: 200 µL of protocetin were added to 1 mL mouse whole blood (final concentration of protocetin were 5 and 10 µg/mL), mixed well, and incubated at 37 °C for 5, 10, and 15 min. The control group was added with an equal volume of PBS and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The determination of thromboelastography was the same as described in the section “Assays for thromboelastography and chromogenic substrate”.

Chromogenic substrate assay: 100 µL of mouse plasma was mixed with 20 µL protocetin, and the final concentration of protocetin were 12.5 and 100 µg/mL. The experimental method was the same as described in the section “Assays for thromboelastography and chromogenic substrate”.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR was utilized to analyze the binding between protocetin and FIX. This analysis was conducted using a Biacore 8× instrument provided by Beijing BioPlus Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The kinetic parameters, on-rate (kon) and off-rate (koff), for each interaction were determined by fitting the data to a 1:1 Langmuir model. The association and dissociation constants (KA and KD) were subsequently calculated as the quotient kon/koff and koff/kon, respectively.

Assay for microthrombus formation in mice

KM mice weighing 18–22 g were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Protocetin was injected into the tail vein at a dose of 0.5 µg/g, and the control group was injected with an equal volume of PBS. At 15 min, 0.5 h, and 1 h after injection, blood samples were taken from the abdominal vein of the mice to prepare the serum. The protein levels of D-dimer, t-PA, and PAI-1 in serum samples were measured via ELISA. After blood samples were collected, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation, and then liver, lung, and kidney were sampled for Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining (The detailed method is presented in the Supplementary materials) and qPCR assay.

The mRNA expression levels of TM, TF, t-PA, and PAI-1 in the tissues were measured using qPCR. The primer pairs used for the analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using Prime 9 and SPSS software. Independent sample t-test was used to determine significant differences between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons between multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Purification and physicochemical properties of protocetin

PMV was fractionated on Sephadex G-75, which resulted in multiple protein peaks. Platelet aggregation activity was detected in fractions 48–56 (Fig. 1a). Further purification was performed on Hitrap Heparin HP, and the platelet aggregation activity was concentrated at peak 2 (Fig. 1b). The active fractions were pooled and separated on Hitrap SP (Fig. 1c). Under nonreducing conditions, purified protocetin produced a band at 25.4 kDa on SDS-PAGE (Fig. 1d, original gel is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1). Upon reduction, protocetin exhibited two bands at 16.8 and 15.8 kDa, which were attributed to the α- and β-chains, respectively. The isoelectric points of the α- and β-chains of protocetin were determined to be 8.2 and 9.1, respectively (Fig. 1e, original gel is presented in Supplementary Fig. S2). Through gel filtration, the molecular weight of the protocetin was determined to be 40.9 kDa. Additionally, the molecular mass of protocetin determined by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry was found to be 29.986 kDa (Supplementary Fig. S3). Periodic acid-Schiff reagent staining revealed that both protocetin and its polypeptide chains contained carbohydrates (Supplementary Fig. S4).

N-terminal sequencing

The N-terminal sequences of the polypeptide chains were determined to be DTPSDWSCFTRYTYK (α-chain, 15 residues) and DCPSDWSSFRRYCYQ (β-chain, 15 residues). The similarity between the α- and β-chains was found to be 67%. These N-terminal amino acid sequences also showed high degrees of homology to CLPs such as mucetin, trimecetin and so on (Fig. 2). The β-chain of protocetin is identity with the β-chain of trimecetin (Q5FZI5) in their N-terminal sequence.

Proteolytic activity assay

Protocetin did not show the activities of hydrolyzing fibrinogen, fibrin, arginine ester, and azocase.

Hemorrhagic activity

The hemorrhagic activity of protocetin was evaluated by subcutaneously injecting it into the backs of mice at a dose of 2.5 µg/g. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S6, after 24 h, the bleeding was observed on the inner side of the skin. Significant bleeding was observed under the skin of the PMV, but protocetin did not cause bleeding.

Assays for platelet aggregation

Protocetin induced human PRP aggregation in dose−dependent manner, and 73.42% aggregation occurred at concentration of 60 µg/mL (Fig. 6a). However, protocetin failed to induce rabbit PRP aggregation (Supplementary Fig. S7). Trimecetin16, a CLP from the PMV, also was reported to induce human PRP, but have no activity in inducing rabbit PRP aggregation. The difference of protocetin in inducing platelet aggregation mentioned above maybe due to it ability in recognization and binding molecules from different species.

The PRP aggregation assay also demonstrated the induction of aggregation by protocetin (Fig. 6b). The increase in PRP aggregation induced by protocetin in mice exhibited a dose-dependent manner. The platelet aggregation activity decreased as the concentration of GPIb monoclonal antibodies increased, and at a concentration of 50 µg/mL, the platelet aggregation was almost completely inhibited (Fig. 6e).

Protocetin did not induce aggregation of rat gel filtration platelets, but the plate-let aggregation activity recovered after the addition of vWF. In the presence of vWF, the results of platelet aggregation induced by different doses of protocetin (10 µg, 20 µg and 50 µg) in comparison to ristocetin revealed that GFP aggregation was induced at dose of 50 µg ristocetin, but there was no dose dependence on protocetin (Fig. 6f).

In the competition experiments of in which ristocetin and protocetin bound to vWF, the A1 group did not significantly differ from the control group (P > 0.05). Further comparisons within groups revealed significant differences between A1 and A2 (P < 0.05) and between B1 and B2 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6g). Therefore, the result indicated that the areas of protocetin and ristocetin binding to vWF are partially overlapped.

Binding of protocetin to FIX was assessed by SPR

We investigated the interaction between protocetin and FIX using SPR with a CM5 chip on which FIX was covalently attached. Assay for microthrombus formation

To assess microthrombus formation, we prepared tissues and stained them with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Microthrombosis analysis revealed the absence of microthrombosis in the small and medium sized vasculature of the lung, liver, and kidney (Supplementary Fig. S9) following the administration of 0.5 µg/g protocetin. Moreover, the protein levels of t-PA, PAI-1, and D-dimer in serum were determined via ELISA. The results showed a significant decrease in t-PA content within 0.5 h, while at 1 h, the levels of t-PA, PAI-1, and D-dimer in the serum were significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. S10). For mRNA analysis, we quantified the mRNA levels of relevant proteins in lung, liver, and kidney tissues using qPCR. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S11, the results indicated a significant decrease in PAI-1 expression in lung tissue at 0.5 h and 1 h, additionally, TM, TF, t-PA and PAI-1 gradually decreased in liver tissue within 1 h.

Discussion

In this study, we isolated and purified a novel CLP called protocetin from the venom of Protobothrops mucrosquamatus using a three-step chromatography. We characterized the separation and purification, physicochemical properties, biological functions, and mechanisms of protocetin. Our findings revealed that protocetin exhibits dual functional activities, activating platelet and binding to coagulation factor.

SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that under nonreducing conditions, the molecular weight of protocetin was 25.4 kDa. Under reducing conditions, the molecular weights of α- and β-chains were 16.8 kDa and 15.8 kDa, respectively. The isoelectric points of α- and β-chains were 8.2 and 9.1, respectively. Glycoprotein staining analysis confirmed that protocetin contains glycosyl group. Additionally, the molecular weights of protocetin determined by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and size-exclusion chromatography were 29.9 kDa and 40.9 kDa, respectively. As can be seen, the molecular weights determined by gel filtration and mass spectrometry exists significant different. Protocetin is a glycoprotein, its shape and glycosyl group significantly influence its chromatographic behavior. Consequently, the molecular weight determined by gel filtration is higher than that of other methods. The molecular weight determined by SDS-PAGE is similar to the mass determined by mass spectrometry.

CLPs isolated and purified from different snake venoms typically consist of heterodimers composed of α-chain (14–15 kDa) and β-chain (13–14 kDa) connected by a pair of interchain disulfide bonds. The structure and molecular weight of the α- and β- chains are highly similar, and each chain contains a carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD). These chains form a heterodimer through disulfide bonds and further assemble into αβ, (αβ)2, (αβ)4, and other structures31. Our results revealed that protocetin also functions as a heterodimer.

Protobothrops mucrosquamatus belongs to the genus Protobothrops in the family Viperidae and is primarily found in southern China, including the Taiwan region. To date, three CLPs, namely, TMVA (mucetin), mucrocetin, and trimecetin, have been isolated from the venom of Protobothrops mucrosquamatus. TMVA is a 117 kDa, 88 kDa protein that exists as (αβ)4 and (αβ)2 complexes in its native state. Mucrocetin is 121 kDa in length and is a trimer (αβ)4, while trimecetin is a 26.1 kDa protein that exists as a heterodimer. The molecular weights of protocetin and trimecetin are similar, and peptide fingerprinting analysis revealed some peptide fragments shared between the two proteins16. The similarity of the α-chain and β-chain between protocetin and trimecetin were approximately 34% and 30%, respectively. N-terminal sequence analysis revealed 67% similarity between the α- and β-chains of protocetin. Moreover, the α-chain of protocetin exhibited 73%, 40%, 40%, 80% and 60% identity with the α-chains of trimecetin (Q5FZI6), mucrocetin (Q6TPH0), TMVA (Q8JGT7), TML-3, and AL-B (P81115), respecticely. The β-chain of protocetin exhibited 100%, 53%, 53%, 67% and 73% identity with the β-chains of trimecetin (Q5FZI5), mucrocetin (Q6TPG9), TMVA (Q8JGT6), TML-3, and AL-B (P81116), respectively. Based on this information, we can confidently conclude that protocetin is a new CLP purified from PMV.

After injection of protocetin into the mice, there obtained a significant decrease in platelet count, a considerable prolongation of tail bleeding time, and an increase in plasma levels of β-TG. These findings suggested that platelets were activated and leaded to a decrease in platelet count. Additionally, thromboelastography was used to monitor the blood coagulation process. The results indicated that protocetin had a significant anticoagulant effect. Within 72 h of injecting a dose of 0.5 µg/g protocetin into the tail vein, coagulation factor levels were reduced, platelet function was decreased, and whole blood displayed a hypocoagulation state due to the consumptive coagulation. In vitro experiments demonstrated that protocetin could induce human and mouse PRP aggregation. However, it failed to induce human washed platelet aggregation. Nevertheless, the addition of PPP was able to restore the induced platelet activity. This finding indicates that the induction of platelet aggregation by protocetin requires plasma components, so we speculated that this plasma component is vWF. Inhibition of protocetin induced platelet aggregation was observed when GPIb monoclonal antibodies were used. Furthermore, protocetin did not induce aggregation of rat gel filtration platelets, but the platelet aggregation activity recovered after the addition of vWF. The above experiments clearly demonstrated that protocetin activates platelets through GPIb via vWF, consequently inducing platelet aggregation. Competition experiments between ristocetin and protocetin on vWF further indicated that the areas of protocetin and ristocetin binding to vWF are partially overlapped. The A1 region was identified as the main binding site of vWF for ristocetin32, suggesting that protocetin may also activate vWF by binding to the A1 region of vWF to bind to GPIb. On the other hand, mucetin, mucrocetin, and trimecetin directly activate platelets by binding to the platelet membrane glycoprotein receptor GPIb14–16. Therefore, our results indicate that protocetin, which is a novel CLP from PMV induces platelet aggregation by binding to vWF.

We also observed that the APTT was significant prolonged, and a significant decrease in fibrinogen after protocetin was injected into mice at a dose of 0.5 µg/g or 1.5 µg/g. The significant prolongation of APTT at 0.5 h indicated that coagulation factors have been activated and consumed, which also explained why whole blood failed to form thromboelastography at 0.5 h and 1 h after protocetin injection at a dose of 0.5 µg/g. However, there was no difference in prothrombin time (PT) or thrombin time (TT). In vitro experiments showed that a high concentration of protocetin decreased the APTT and PT, but had no significant effect on the TT and concentration of fibrinogen. This finding suggested that protocetin primarily affects the intrinsic pathway by binding to coagulation factors. Factor-deficient plasma experiments revealed that protocetin prolonged the clotting time of factor- VIII, XI, and XII deficient plasma, but had no effect on the clotting time of factor IX deficient plasma. The activity of coagulation factor IX was almost unaffected, indicating that protocetin specifically targets factor IX. To verify this, a coagulation factor correction experiment was conducted, which showed that the plasma returned to a normal coagulation state only when factor IX was added. Further surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis confirmed that protocetin and factor IX can bind at 1:1 ratio in the presence of calcium ions. Notably, high doses of protocetin shortened the plasma APTT in vitro while prolonging it at low doses. At low doses, protocetin downregulates the activity of thrombin substrates without affecting the activity of coagulation factor Xa substrates. However, at high doses, both substrate activities are upregulated. Therefore, the prolongation of coagulation time at low dose may be attributed to insufficient binding of protocetin to FIX, which fails to change its configuration or activate it. CLPs acting in the coagulation pathway have been isolated from the venoms of Agkistrodonacutus venom, Trimeresurus flavoviridis, etc. Such as ACF I8and ACF II33 from the Agkistrodon acutusvenom, can form a 1:1 complex with activated coagulation factor X (FXa) in the presence of calcium ions, thereby prolonging coagulation time in vitro. Habu IX-bp10, ECLV IX/X-bp11, and AHP IX-bp12can bind to coagulation factors IX or X, inhibiting the conversion of prothrombin into thrombin. Additionally, Agkisacutacin34 exhibit anticoagulant affects by binding to coagulation factor IX/X and GPIb.

By now, all known CLPs only possess the activity of binding to coagulation factor IX/X or platelet, and eventually resulting in anticoagulant effect. However, protocetin, a CLP, is unique in that it exhibits bifunctional activity by activating platelets and binding to coagulation factor IX. These findings prompted us to investigate whether protocetin induces thrombus formation in mice. Have research shown that mucetin can induce platelet aggregation in vitro and cause microvascular thrombosis in multiple organs in vivo35. Therefore, determining whether protocetin also contributes to thrombosis is highly important. Our examination of tissue sections using hematoxylin and eosin staining did not reveal any evidence of microthrombosis. However, we observed a significant increase in the D-dimer level, which suggested fibrinolysis. Additionally, our study demonstrated elevated levels of t-PA and PAI-1 in the serum of mice 1 h after protocetin administration. Compared to those in the normal control group, the ratios of t-PA and PAI-1 were greater, and there was a slight decrease in the mRNA level of PAI-1 in the tissues. These results indicate activation of the fibrinolytic system. Consequently, there was no microthrombus formation after protocetin injection into mice, which can be attributed to either direct activation of the fibrinolytic system by protocetin or the clearance of agglutination. The decrease in the plasma fibrinogen concentration may have also contributed to consumptive coagulation, resulting in the anticoagulant effect observed after protocetin injection in mice.

Conclusion

Protocetin is a novel CLP purified from the venom of Protobothrops mucrosquamatus. Its ability to modulate coagulation function by activating both platelet and coagulation factor IX contributes to a better understanding of the structure and function of snake venom CLP.
