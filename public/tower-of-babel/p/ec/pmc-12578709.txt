The ovarian response to stimulation in patients with lymphoma (Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin); a retrospective age-matched control study
Authors: Toktam Sadeghi, Azar Yahyaei, Maryam Hafezi, Bita Ebrahimi, Firouzeh Ghaffari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fertility preservation has become a critical element in the management of patients with hematologic malignancies. Although fertility in lymphoma survivors has been investigated in numerous studies, the overall quality of evidence remains low. The present investigation was designed to assess ovarian response in patients with lymphoma before oncologic therapy, comparing them with healthy controls and evaluating differences between Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) subgroups. This is a retrospective, age-matched controlled study. All patients diagnosed with HL or NHL who underwent oocyte or embryo cryopreservation between October 2015 and March 2024 were included. The control cohort comprised healthy women who underwent ovarian stimulation during the same period. The primary outcome was total oocytes retrieved. Based on anti-Müllerian hormone values, the study’s statistical power is 80%. A total of 65 patients with lymphoma were enrolled. The control cohort Likewise comprised 65 age-matched individuals. The primary outcome was total oocytes retrieved. The secondary outcome was total number of mature oocytes. The gonadotropin dose and duration of ovarian stimulation were significantly greater in the lymphoma group (P = 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the total oocytes retrieved. The number of metaphase two oocytes and the proportion of mature oocytes to total retrieved oocytes were significantly higher in the lymphoma group (P = 0.013 and P = 0.026, respectively). No significant intergroup differences were observed in the numbers of 2PN, embryos obtained, fertilization rates, or embryo quality. HL and NHL patients exhibit a favorable ovarian response to stimulation prior to gonadotoxic therapy. It is therefore recommended that comprehensive counseling be provided to ensure these patients can maximize their use of fertility preservation services prior to gonadotoxic treatment.

Background

Hematologic malignancies including Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas are among the most prevalent cancers in young women [1]. Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) is divided into two principal subtypes: classical Hodgkin lymphoma and nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma, which differ in morphology, immunophenotype, lymphoma microenvironment, and clinical behavior [2]. According to the World Health Organization classification, non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) comprises more than 60 subtypes with distinct etiologies and clinical behaviors, creating pronounced heterogeneity and complicating prognosis [3–5].

Although mortality has continued to decline, the incidence of various lymphoma types particularly among young women and in Asia has been increasing [4, 6, 7]. In 2020, approximately 627,439 individuals were diagnosed with lymphoma worldwide. Among female patients aged 20–39 years, HL and NHL accounted for 3,304 and 2,639 new cases, respectively [8]. Despite these substantial incidence figures, the reported long-term survival rates remain high at 90% for HL and 75% for NHL [9, 10].

This favorable prognosis is largely attributable to cytotoxic therapies; however, such treatments can markedly impair fertility and, in certain cases, lead to infertility [11, 12]. Notably, fertility impairment may stem from the lymphoma itself as well as from its treatment [13]. Therapeutic interventions can result in oocyte destruction, follicular depletion, and, consequently, reduced ovarian reserve [1, 14]. Moreover, gonadal dysfunction may arise even before oncologic therapy due to the systemic effects of lymphoid neoplasms and unexplained gamete impairment associated with defective DNA repair mechanisms [15].

Because the ability to have biological children is a key determinant of post-cancer quality of life, fertility preservation has become a critical element in the management of patients with hematologic malignancies [14]. Mature oocyte and embryo cryopreservation are the only fertility preservation methods endorsed by most medical societies and are considered the first-line options for post-pubertal women [16].

Research examining ovarian response to stimulation in lymphoma patients is scarce, and many studies evaluate hematologic malignancies without distinguishing specific subtypes. In particular, investigations dedicated to the two principal lymphoma categories [HL and NHL] remain limited. Although fertility in HL survivors has been investigated in numerous studies, the overall quality of evidence remains low because fertility-related markers have not been assessed in a structured manner, underscoring the need for further research. Reproductive patterns in NHL have been explored in only a few studies [11, 13]. As noted earlier, NHL is highly heterogeneous, complicating its prognosis [3, 4]. We therefore hypothesized that ovarian responsiveness might differ between the two subtypes and compared patients with HL and NHL. Accordingly, the present investigation was designed to assess ovarian response in patients with lymphoma before oncologic therapy, comparing them with healthy controls and evaluating differences between HL and NHL subgroups.

Methods

Study design and population

This investigation employed a retrospective, age-matched controlled design. All patients diagnosed with HL or NHL who underwent fertility preservation by oocyte or embryo cryopreservation at the Infertility Clinic of ROYAN Institute, Tehran, Iran, between October 2015 and March 2024 were included. All patients undergo cryopreservation, before the start of first-line therapy. The control cohort comprised healthy women with regular menstrual cycles who underwent ovarian stimulation during the same period owing to male factor infertility and were candidates for IVF-ICSI. Women with polycystic ovary syndrome or diminished ovarian reserve were excluded from the control group. Additional exclusion criteria were a history of ovarian surgery or oophorectomy, relapsed lymphoma, prior in vitro maturation, gonadoprotection with GnRH agonists, ovarian tissue cryopreservation, and any history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy. Age matching between groups was accomplished by individual matching.

Ovarian stimulation

The ovarian stimulation was implemented through random start GnRH antagonist protocol for cancer patients and standard GnRH antagonist protocol for patients in control group.

The optimal dose of gonadotropin for ovarian stimulation was determined based on ovarian reserve markers including anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) levels and antral follicle count.

Early follicular phase: Begins from the start to the middle of the follicular phase (dominant follicle is smaller than 12 mm and progesterone level less than 1.5 ng/ml). Ovarian stimulation is initiated through the administration of gonadotropins. Upon observation of a follicle measuring 12–14 mm, a GnRH antagonist is administered. The triggering final oocyte maturation is performed upon observation of more than three follicles larger than or equal to 17 mm.

Late follicular phase: It is characterized by the presence of a dominant follicle larger than 12 mm and progesterone level less than 1.5 ng/ml. Initially, the patient receives GnRH antagonist for three consecutive days or a single injection of a GnRH agonist is administered to induce the luteal phase. Three days later, ovarian stimulation is initiated through the administration of gonadotropins. Upon observation of a new dominant follicle measuring 12–14 mm, a GnRH antagonist is administered. After observing a follicle measuring 18 mm, ovulation is induced.

Luteal phase: It is characterized by the progesterone level more than 1.5 ng/ml. Ovarian stimulation is initiated similarly to the conventional method through the administration of gonadotropins, and a GnRH antagonist is administered upon observation of a follicle measuring 12–14 mm. After observing a follicle measuring 18 mm, ovulation is induced.

If patient is no risk for ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) triggering is elected. And if the patient is high risk for OHSS, the final oocyte maturation is induced through the administration of a GnRH agonist. Threshold of 19 follicles of ≥ 11 mm on triggering final oocyte maturation day predicted moderate to severe OHSS in GnRH antagonist protocol [17].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was total oocytes retrieved. The secondary outcome was total number of mature oocytes.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative and quantitative data were presented as the frequency and percentage, as well as mean and standard deviation (SD). The normality of data was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables between the two groups were compared using an independent t-test. Comparisons of means across subgroups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when more than 20% of cells with expected counts of less than 5 were observable. All analyses were done using SPSS (version 24, SPSS Inc., Illinois, USA), and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Based on AMH values, the study’s statistical power is 80%.

Results

A total of 65 patients with lymphoma [48 diagnosed with HL and 17 with NHL] were enrolled. The control cohort Likewise comprised 65 age-matched individuals. Ages in both groups ranged from 18 to 39 years, with a mean of 28.2 ± 5.7 years. Body mass index (BMI) was 24.7 ± 4.5 kg/m2 in the lymphoma group and 25.9 ± 3.1 kg/m2 in the control group, showing no statistically significant difference.

Of the 65 lymphoma patients, 43 underwent oocyte cryopreservation, 14 embryo cryopreservation, and 6 both procedures concurrently. Ovarian stimulation was canceled in one NHL patient owing to inadequate response and in one HL patient because no oocytes were retrieved. No cases of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome were observed. In the control cohort, ovarian stimulation uniformly began in the early follicular phase (menstrual cycle days 2–3). By contrast, stimulation in the lymphoma cohort was initiated in the early follicular phase for 34 patients, the late follicular phase for 6, and the luteal phase for 25.

FSH and LH serum levels were compared between 34 individuals from the lymphoma group in early follicular phase and 65 individuals from the control group. Serum luteinizing hormone (LH) concentrations were significantly higher in the lymphoma cohort than in the control cohort (P = 0.019). No statistically significant differences were detected between the groups in serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) or AMH levels. Both the initial and cumulative gonadotropin doses, as well as the duration of ovarian stimulation, were significantly greater in the lymphoma group (P = 0.019, P = 0.001, and P = 0.001, respectively). Ovulation triggering was performed predominantly with a low-dose GnRH agonist in the lymphoma cohort, whereas HCG predominantly was used in the control cohort, a statistically significant distinction (P = 0.001). The groups did not differ significantly in the number of follicles ≥ 12 mm on the trigger day. Although the total number of retrieved oocytes was higher in the lymphoma cohort, these differences was not statistically significant. Nonetheless, the number of metaphase two (MII) oocytes and the proportion of mature oocytes to total retrieved oocytes were significantly higher in the lymphoma group (P = 0.013 and P = 0.026, respectively). No significant intergroup differences were observed in the numbers of 2PN, embryos obtained, fertilization rates, or embryo quality.

Across the three-group comparison, age and BMI did not differ significantly. Serum LH levels were elevated in the NHL cohort relative to the other two cohorts, reaching statistical significance when compared with controls (P = 0.038). Serum FSH and AMH concentrations showed no significant variation among the three groups. Both HL and NHL patients required higher total gonadotropin doses and longer stimulation durations than controls, with the HL–control difference achieving statistical significance (P = 0.005 and P = 0.001, respectively). The number of follicles ≥ 12 mm on the trigger day did not differ among groups. While the total number of retrieved oocytes was comparable across cohorts, the HL group exhibited a significantly greater number of MII oocytes than controls (P = 0.031). Because embryos were cryopreserved for only four NHL patients, a three-way comparison of embryo number and quality was not possible.

Discussion

Research examining ovarian response to stimulation in lymphoma patients is scarce, and many studies evaluate hematologic malignancies without distinguishing specific subtypes. In particular, investigations dedicated to the two principal lymphoma categories [HL and NHL] remain limited.

A 2024 meta-analysis demonstrated that patients with hematologic malignancies, despite their younger age, exhibit lower serum AMH levels than healthy controls. They also received marginally higher FSH doses and showed markedly lower serum estradiol concentrations, yet the number of retrieved oocytes was comparable between groups [15]. AMH, produced by granulosa cells of pre-antral and small growing antral follicles, correlates with the primordial follicle pool and thus serves as an indirect indicator of functional ovarian reserve [18]. The biological basis for reduced ovarian reserve in cancer patients even before gonadotoxic therapy remains unclear, although proposed contributors include heightened catabolism, elevated stress hormones, and compromised general health at diagnosis [19]. Additional hypotheses for the lower AMH levels observed in lymphoma patients relative to healthy individuals encompass hypothalamic–pituitary axis dysfunction, hypopituitarism, intravascular lymphocytosis, and increased cytokine concentrations; a concurrent genetic predisposition to both bone marrow disorders and diminished ovarian reserve has also been suggested [20, 21]. In our cohort, lymphoma patients (both HL and NHL) did not differ from controls in age or serum AMH levels. The divergence between our findings and those of the meta-analysis may stem from the latter’s failure to stratify cases by hematologic malignancy subtype.

Unlike previous investigations, our study compared ovarian stimulation responses not only between lymphoma patients and healthy controls but also between the HL and NHL. As noted earlier, NHL is highly heterogeneous, complicating its prognosis [3, 4]. We therefore hypothesized that ovarian responsiveness might differ between the two subtypes and compared patients with HL and NHL. Our findings indicated that the two groups were comparable in terms of ovarian reserve and stimulation outcomes. Consistently, von Wolff et al. observed no significant difference in the number of oocytes retrieved between these subtypes [22].

Comparison of lymphoma patients, regardless of HL and NHL subtypes, with healthy controls shows that lymphoma patients have lower ovarian reserve and retrieved fewer oocytes before gonadotoxic treatments [20, 23, 24]. Some attribute this to a strong inverse correlation between AMH and circulating cytokines including SIL-2R, IL-6, and IL-8 [24]. Sonigo et al. found that the responsiveness of antral follicles to exogenous FSH varies by malignancy type: The proportion of follicles that responded successfully to FSH was lower in HL than in breast cancer [25]. Di Pietro et al. identified differential expression of 13 microRNAs in the follicular fluid of women with HL compared with healthy controls. These microRNAs are implicated not only in cancer-related pathways but also in gametogenesis and embryogenesis, with roughly half involved in follicular development and oocyte maturation. Although the study did not observe reduced oocyte quality in young women with HL relative to age-matched healthy women, the authors suggested that critical illness may disrupt the follicular microenvironment and, hence, oocyte competence [26]. Some studies hypothesized that inflammation in advanced stages might impair granulosa cell function and diminish ovarian responsiveness, yet they found no significant stage-related differences in serum AMH concentrations, numbers of oocytes retrieved, or oocyte maturation rates [21, 27, 28]; however, elevated lactate dehydrogenase in NHL and low serum albumin in HL markers of more aggressive disease were significantly associated with fewer retrieved and mature oocytes [28].

Overall, ovarian stimulation outcomes in patients with hematologic malignancies remain insufficiently characterized, and many existing studies employ control groups comprising patients with other cancer types [29]. The mechanisms driving fertility impairment in lymphoid malignancies are still unclear. Systemic immune and metabolic alterations linked to the disease’s aggressive nature may impair ovarian function and responsiveness to stimulation [28]. Some investigators propose that inflammation and deteriorated general health might disrupt the follicular microenvironment, compromise granulosa cell integrity, and diminish oocyte quality in young women with HL [21]. Disorders of the hematopoietic system may also exert direct effects on ovarian follicles [30]. Despite these hypotheses, our data show that patients with lymphoma [HL and NHL] exhibit favorable responses to ovarian stimulation.

In our cohort, baseline serum LH concentrations at the initiation of ovarian stimulation were significantly higher in patients with NHL than in controls. Similarly, Lekovich et al. reported markedly elevated LH levels at stimulation onset in lymphoma patients relative to healthy individuals, while noting no difference compared with patients who had other malignancies. They attributed this observation to prior oral contraceptive pill use among the healthy controls and to these individuals’ lower likelihood of initiating stimulation during the luteal phase [20]. We contend, however, that luteal phase initiation cannot account for our finding, as we specifically compared serum LH and FSH levels in patients who began stimulation in the early follicular phase with those of the control group. Few studies have evaluated the effect of LH levels during ovarian stimulation on clinical outcome and these show conflicting results [31]. LH levels and variations in ovarian stimulation present individual differences. The optimal range of LH in ovarian stimulation is not yet well understood, and debates focused on the predictive value of LH levels for treatment outcomes cease. More attention should be paid to LH levels and activities to adjust the medication regimen accordingly and to aim toward LH levels in an optimal range in an attempt to maximize the reproductive outcomes [32]. Its clinical and biological significance remains an open question for future research.

In our cohort, both the absolute number of MII oocytes and the oocyte maturation rate were significantly higher in lymphoma patients than in healthy controls. This outcome is likely attributable to the ovulation trigger method. As noted earlier, most lymphoma patients received a low-dose GnRH agonist trigger, whereas HCG was used more frequently in the control group a statistically significant difference. This is a critical confounding factor. It can strongly suggest that the superior outcome in maturation was not due to a “favorable” biological response inherent to lymphoma patients, but rather to a difference in the clinical protocol. The advantage of a GnRH agonist lies in its capacity to elicit endogenous LH and FSH surges, thereby potentially enhancing oocyte quality [33] and increasing maturation and fertilization rates [34]. Accumulating evidence further suggests that a dual trigger combining GnRH agonist and HCG is the most effective strategy for inducing final oocyte maturation in ovarian stimulation cycles employing a GnRH antagonist protocol [35].

A notable strength of this study is its assessment of ovarian stimulation response in lymphoma patients while explicitly distinguishing HL from NHL subtypes. Its chief limitations are the retrospective design and the limited number of patients across lymphoma types. This limitation severely compromises the reliability of our conclusion that ovarian reserve and stimulation outcomes are “comparable” between HL and NHL. Based on the current data, it is more accurate to state that a difference could not be detected, rather than to conclude that there is no difference. Although we could not be detected significant differences in fertility preservation outcomes between HL and NHL, the marked heterogeneity within lymphoma subtypes underscores the need for multicenter studies with larger sample sizes to determine subtype-specific effects. Lack of access to patients’ clinical and hematological data (e.g., histotypes, characteristics of delivered therapy, and number of treatment lines) is a limitation of this study. Current literature lacks data on the utilization rates of vitrified mature oocytes among patients with hematologic malignancies [14]. There is no follow-up on clinical outcomes such as fertilization rates, implantation, pregnancy, or live birth, which limits the clinical relevance of the finding. Future investigations that report pregnancy outcomes in individuals who have undergone fertility preservation could further clarify the reproductive implications of the various lymphoma subtypes.

Conclusions

In this study, although total oocytes retrieved was considered as the primary outcome, no significant difference was observed between the lymphoma and control groups. However, there was a significant difference in terms of the secondary outcome, total number of mature oocytes. With appropriate stimulation protocols, particularly using a GnRH agonist trigger, a favorable number of mature oocytes can be obtained from lymphoma patients. These findings indicate that both HL and NHL patients exhibit a favorable ovarian response to stimulation prior to gonadotoxic therapy. It is therefore recommended that comprehensive counseling be provided to ensure these patients can maximize their use of fertility preservation services prior to gonadotoxic treatment. Due to the limited of patients, prospective multicenter studies are recommended.
