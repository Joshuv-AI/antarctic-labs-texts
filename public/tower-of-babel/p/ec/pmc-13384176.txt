Continuity as infrastructure: protecting patient outcomes, equity and the rural primary care workforce
Authors: Jesus Ruiz
Published: 2026

Continuity of care has long defined the identity and clinical effectiveness of family medicine and primary care. Longitudinal relationships allow clinicians to understand patients in context and translate that knowledge into better preventive care, chronic disease management and coordinated care. Yet in many contemporary delivery systems, continuity is increasingly treated as optional: desirable when convenient but sacrificed when operational pressures mount.

This issue is increasingly urgent as recent evidence demonstrates that continuity of care remains difficult to achieve despite ongoing primary care reforms, while robust evidence continues to show that continuity is associated with lower mortality and improved patient outcomes.1 2 In this context, protecting continuity should be viewed not simply as a quality improvement goal but as a health system priority. Nowhere is this more visible than in rural safety-net care, where the populations who benefit most from continuity are often those least likely to experience it.

A recent analysis of continuity within Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) documented poor short- and long-term continuity and found no meaningful association between patient-centred medical home recognition and improved continuity outcomes.1 These findings challenge a persistent assumption in primary care reform: that measurement and formal recognition are sufficient to preserve relational care. Instead, they point to a deeper truth—continuity is not an individual clinician trait or a documentation achievement. It is a system property, produced or undermined by organisational design. This perspective argues that continuity should be understood as organisational infrastructure and outlines practical implications for clinic leaders, health system leaders, policy makers and payers responsible for designing and sustaining longitudinal primary care.

Continuity is strongly linked to patient outcomes and survival

The evidence connecting continuity to better outcomes is among the most consistent findings in primary care research. A systematic review found that greater continuity of care is associated with lower mortality across diverse settings, suggesting that continuity is not merely a patient preference but a clinically consequential exposure.2

Continuity also supports prevention, safer prescribing, earlier recognition of deterioration and improved coordination in multimorbidity.

Continuity builds clinical memory and trust over time, enabling shared decision-making, reducing repeated storytelling and strengthening follow-up for patients requiring longitudinal care.

Continuity is an equity intervention, not just a quality feature

Continuity has equity effects because it disproportionately benefits patients facing structural barriers: limited transportation, unstable employment, low health literacy, language barriers and constrained access to specialists. In such contexts, a stable primary clinician can function as the healthcare system’s anchor—translating fragmented recommendations into coherent plans and helping patients navigate complexity.

One patient once told me, “When I see you, I know I won't have to explain everything again.” For patients facing language barriers, chronic illness or difficult life circumstances, continuity is dignity, trust and the reassurance that someone remembers their story.

When continuity collapses, inequities widen. Patients without a usual source of care are less likely to receive preventive services and more likely to experience gaps in routine management.3 For rural and underserved communities, episodic ‘next available’ care can become the norm—high in throughput, low in relational stability—precisely where trust, longitudinal follow-up and contextual knowledge matter most. These inequities are not inevitable. They reflect organisational decisions about how care is delivered and whether continuity is treated as infrastructure or left to individual effort.

Why continuity is hardest where it is most needed: rural FQHC operational design

Rural FQHCs operate under high demand, workforce instability and financial pressures that reward volume over relationships. These pressures often translate into fragmented scheduling, pooled appointments and productivity expectations that undermine continuity.

The FQHC continuity findings described above highlight a critical point: continuity cannot be “incentivised” into existence if the scheduling and staffing systems are built to optimise throughput.1 Continuity indices may improve on paper even as longitudinal relationships gradually erode.

In many safety-net settings, continuity increasingly relies on extraordinary individual effort rather than institutional design. Clinicians often compensate through informal patient panels, scheduling exceptions, double-booking and personal follow-up. This invisible labour temporarily protects continuity but leaves it dependent on individual effort rather than organisational design. Continuity is also workforce infrastructure: relationships protect clinicians

Continuity is not only patient-centred; it is a workforce protection strategy. Primary care derives much of its meaning from long-term relationships with patients. When systems undermine these relationships, clinicians experience moral distress because the care they value becomes structurally difficult to deliver.

The consequences are measurable. When primary care clinicians leave, patient outcomes and utilisation worsen; turnover is associated with increased emergency department use and other disruptions.4 Conversely, meaningful patient–physician relationships are linked with professional fulfilment and may protect against burnout.5 In rural communities—where recruitment is difficult, and turnover reverberates across entire counties—continuity loss and workforce attrition can become a self-reinforcing cycle: fragmentation fuels dissatisfaction, dissatisfaction fuels turnover and turnover further fragments care.

This is the policy and leadership failure at the centre of continuity erosion: systems often treat continuity as a ‘nice to have’ while treating clinician retention as a separate human resources problem. In reality, they are linked. Continuity is a mechanism through which clinicians experience purpose and patients experience trust. If continuity is shaped by organisational design, then health systems must also rethink how they measure success.

Measurement without redesign: why current reforms fall short

Health systems increasingly emphasise dashboards that include access metrics, visit counts and performance indicators. Some of these measures matter, but they can become counterproductive when they displace the infrastructure required for longitudinal care. The ‘quadruple aim’ reminds us that improving population health and patient experience depends on caring for the workforce; clinician well-being is not ancillary to outcomes.6 If continuity is sacrificed to meet short-term productivity targets, systems may achieve ‘access’ while eroding the relational mechanisms that improve outcomes and sustain clinicians.

Better measurement should evaluate whether health systems create the conditions that sustain continuity through panel retention, continuity-first scheduling, relational continuity and patient-reported trust. These metrics shift the focus from simply measuring continuity to designing systems that reliably produce it.

Primary care’s contribution to health systems is well established: stronger primary care is associated with better population health and greater equity.7 Continuity is one of the most important mechanisms through which primary care achieves these effects, supported by decades of conceptual and empirical work.8 These benefits may be especially important in rural communities, where continuity of care is often more difficult to maintain than in urban settings, underscoring the need for organisational strategies that protect longitudinal patient–clinician relationships.9 The lesson is straightforward: continuity cannot be bolted onto a throughput-driven design. It must be built into staffing, scheduling and accountability structures. The next step is translating these principles into operational and policy decisions that make continuity the default rather than the exception.

Reframing continuity as institutional infrastructure: policy and operational levers

If continuity is essential to outcomes, equity and workforce stability, then it should be treated like infrastructure—protected, resourced and monitored in ways that change operations not just documentation.

Health system leaders, policy makers, payers and clinic leaders should ask whether their organisations are truly designed to support continuity. Key questions include:

Conclusion

Continuity of care is central to family medicine because it improves patient outcomes, advances equity and sustains the workforce. Yet the communities that most need continuity—rural and underserved populations—are often those least likely to receive it, particularly in systems that prioritise metrics, money and throughput over relationships.

Continuity should not depend on heroism. Reframing continuity as infrastructure offers a practical path to improve outcomes, narrow disparities and retain the rural primary care workforce. Recognising continuity as infrastructure is only the first step. Policy makers, payers, health system leaders and clinic leaders must build systems where enduring relationships are protected by design rather than preserved through individual sacrifice.
