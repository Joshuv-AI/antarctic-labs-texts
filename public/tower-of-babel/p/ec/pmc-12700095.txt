Substrate-induced remodeling of protein disordered loops activates Csm DNases
Authors: Zhenxiao Yu, Fang Wang, Zixuan Zhang, Yan Zhang, Jianan Xu, Xin Li, Mingqi Liang, Jinshan Guo, Shiqi Ji, Qunxin She
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType III-A Csm systems show two distinct activities: the synthesis of cyclic oligoadenylates by the Csm1 (Cas10) Palm domain and DNA cleavage by the Csm1 histidine–aspartate (HD) domain, both of which are to be activated by the cognate target RNAs (CTR) and regulated in the spatiotemporal fashion. However, it remains elusive how the Csm DNase is activated. Here, we show that the activation occurs at the stage of substrate binding via two main steps: (i) recognition of CTR triggers the Csm effector to develop substrate binding potential, enabling the HD domain of Csm1 subunit to bind single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) substrate; (ii) ssDNA substrate induces the formation of substrate channel where key amino acids within Csm1 HD domain interact with ssDNA substrate, delivering it to the active cavity for cleavage. A tripartite structural element in the Csm1 HD domain, including the catalytic cavity and two disordered loop segments (L1, L2), was found to function in the substrate channel formation. A model is presented to account for the Csm DNase activation in which remodeling of L2 functions as the trigger. The resulting knowledge of this work provides a basis for further optimization of Csm DNase-based nucleic acid detection tools.

Introduction

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) and CRISPR-associated (Cas) systems, consisted of CRISPR arrays and cas gene cassettes, restrict invading genetic elements in a small RNA-guided fashion [1–4]. Seven main types of CRISPR–Cas systems are known [5–7], among which only type III systems exhibit multiple immune activities. Except subtype III-E [8, 9], other type III CRISPR systems, represented by III-A Csm and III-B Cmr systems, contain Cas10 (the large subunit, also named Csm1/Cmr2 in Csm/Cmr systems) as the major functional protein in their multisubunit effector complexes and therefore also known as CRISPR–Cas10 systems [5]. The CRISPR RNA (crRNA) in type III-A Csm effector complex, a typical representative of CRISPR–Cas10 effector complexes, contains a conserved 8 nt 5′-repeat tag (5′-tag) and a downstream spacer region with varied sequence. Once it recognizes cognate target RNA (CTR), which contains a 5′-protospacer region complementary to crRNA’s spacer region and a 3′-protospacer flanking sequence (3′-PFS) mismatching crRNA’s 5′-tag, Csm1 in the effector complex would be activated. Then Csm1 exhibits DNase activity and synthesis of cyclic oligoadenylates (cOAs) via its HD and Palm domains, respectively, to mediate antiviral defense [10]. Furthermore, non-cognate target RNAs (NTRs) showing full complementarity between its 3′-PFS and the 5′-tag of crRNAs fail to activate the immunity. In addition, backbone subunits of Csm effectors (Csm3) exhibit target RNA cleavage and play a central role in the spatiotemporal regulation of the type III-A immunity [11].

To date, most studied type III CRISPR–Cas10 systems, including type III-A Csm systems, mainly rely on CRISPR signaling to mediate antiviral defense, in which the cOA molecules bind to the CRISPR-associated Rossman fold (CARF) domain of CRISPR accessary proteins and activate their effector domain, which are often nucleases [12–18]. These CARF domain proteins facilitate cell dormancy or cell death in a process called CRISPR signaling [13, 16] and protect uninfected bacterial cells in the population via abortive infection. Clearance of invader nucleic acids requires the activity of Csm DNases as suggested from the genetic study of the Staphylococcus epidermidis III-A Csm (SeCsm) [13, 19] and Thermus thermophilius Csm (TtCsm) system [20]. Interestingly, Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus III-A Csm (LdCsm) system lacks the activity of cOA synthesis but is still capable of mediating strong immune responses by degradation of nontemplate DNA strand of accessible transcription bubbles upon activation [21]. Further investigations demonstrated that the LdCsm DNase facilitates abortive infection when the copy number of an invader is high [22], but mediating invader clearance when the invading nucleic acid is in a low abundance in infected cells [23]. Thus, LdCsm provides a good model for investigating the mechanisms of Csm DNases.

The effectors of type I and III CRISPR systems, Cas3 and Cas10, carry the HD nuclease domain of the phosphohydrolase superfamily with the general consensus of H..HD..D [24]. Analysis of the Cas10 proteins from different subtypes of type III CRISPR system further revealed an interesting diversity in their HD motifs, since those of III-A and III-F systems have the HD.HH.D.H motif whereas those from III-B, III-C, and III-D systems have HD.H.D.H, HD.H.H.D, and H.HD.H.H.D motifs, respectively [25]. Nevertheless, structural analyses of several III-A Csm effector complexes, including Streptococcus thermophilus (St) Csm [26], Lactococcus lactis (Ll) Csm [27], SeCsm [28], Thermococcus onnurineus (To) Csm [29], and TtCsm [30], only revealed marginal conformational changes between the structures of the HD domains of the NTR-bound inactive Csm effectors and those of the corresponding CTR-bound active ones. In Type I CRISPR systems, target DNA is captured by the formation of R-loop and delivered to the HD catalytic site from the helicase domain in the same protein for cleavage [31–33]. However, very different challenge is encountered by Csm systems since their effector complexes have to capture the target DNA and deliver that to the catalytic site. It remains elusive how Csm effectors interact with their DNA substrates ever since the spatiotemporal regulation model was proposed in 2016 [34].

In this work, we employed the LdCsm system as a model to study the activation of type III-A Csm DNases. AlphaFold 3 (AF3) structure modeling tool was utilized to predict the putative interaction between LdCsm effector and ssDNA substrate, revealing that a tripartite structural element in the Csm1 HD domain, including disordered loops L1, L2, and the HD catalytic cavity, plays vital roles in DNA substrate binding and DNase regulation. Genetic and biochemical characterization of the tripartite structural element revealed that: (i) L1, L2, and the HD catalytic cavity are important in maintaining Csm DNase immunity; (ii) changes in electric charge level of L1 or L2 finetune the Csm DNase immunity in a regular way. Substrate binding affinity assays further revealed that the activation of the Csm DNase is mainly controlled at the stage of the binding of DNA substrate, involving formation of the substrate binding potential enabled by recognition of CTR, prevention of CTR-independent substrate binding by acidic amino acids in the two loop segments, and formation of the substrate binding channel by L2 and HD catalytic cavity. These factors work in concert to ensure a smooth process of DNA binding, leading to DNA cleavage at the HD catalytic core of the LdCsm DNase.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth conditions

Escherichia coli DH5α was the bacterial host for DNA cloning. Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) was the host for conducting invader clearance assay and for expression and purification of LdCsm effector complexes. Escherichia coli strains were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) or terrific broth (TB) medium. Incubation was at 37°C, 200 rpm. If required, antibiotics were added as the following: ampicillin (Amp) at 100 μg/ml, kanamycin (Kan) at 50 μg/ml, and chloramphenicol (Cm) at 10 μg/ml.

Phylogenetic relationship analysis of type III-A Csm1

Homologs of LdCsm1 were found by protein BLAST (basic local alignment search tool) (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) in NCBI (National center for biotechnology information) website. Amino acid sequences of type III-A Csm1 proteins from 140 different species were obtained (Supplementary Table S1) and analyzed by multiple sequences alignments using the Clustal W matching method in MEGA7.0 [35]. Then the result was used to construct the phylogenetic tree using the maximum likelihood method with the default parameters and drawn with Adobe Illustrator [36].

Structure modeling of LdCsm effectors by AlphaFold 3

AF3 (http://alphafoldserver.com/) [37] was utilized to predict the structure of different conformations of LdCsm effectors and the interaction between LdCsm effectors and ssDNA substrate of 5′-CACA-3′, 5′-CACAC-3′, or 5′-CACACA-3′. Protein sequences of LdCsm1∼LdCsm5 from the complete genome of L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus strain ND04 were used for structure prediction (GenBank: CP016393.1). Structure prediction was conducted with a stoichiometry of LdCsm1123344151. Nucleotide sequences of crRNA or CTR used for structure prediction are listed in Supplementary Table S2. LdCsm1 HD domains in AF3-predicted structures of different LdCsm effectors were selected, compared, and presented by PyMOL (v2.3; http://www.pymol.org).

Construction of ldcsm-expressing plasmids carrying mutations in the HD domain of the L. delbrueckii Csm1

Five loop truncations and 23 single amino acid substitutions were designed for the LdCsm1 HD domain, including d-L1a, d-L1b, d-L1c, d-L2a, d-L2b, E63A, K65A, K66A, K65AK66A, R70A, E73AE76A, D94A, R95A, R96A, R95AR96A, E98AE99A, K102A, D113A, D113N, R125A, H15A, D16N, K19A, H34A, H58A, H59A, D87N, and H206A mutation in the Csm1-encoding sequence. They all were constructed using a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Gibson assembly approach that was briefly described below. Two sets of complementary primers were designed: one was at the mutation position (for a variant csm1 gene with mutation X, the two primers were named X-F and X-R), the other set of complementary primers was picked up from the Csm5 coding sequence (called over-F and over-R) (sequences of primers are listed in Supplementary Table S2). Then, a smaller fragment of pColE-Cas-S1 was amplified from pColE-Cas-S1 by PCR using the X-F and over-R primers while a larger fragment was amplified using over-F and X-R from the same plasmid. Ligation of two fragments by Gibson assembly yielded pColE-Cas-S1 carrying mutation X. In addition, using p15AIE-Cas as the template for the PCR reactions produced the p15AIE-Cas plasmids carrying each mutation designed for the Csm1 protein. The two series of plasmids were to be used in invader clearance assay and for ribonucleoprotein (RNP) purification, respectively.

Purification of LdCsm effector complexes from E. coli

LdCsm purification was conducted as previously described [21]. Expression strains were obtained by transformation of E. coli BL21/DE3 with three plasmids (p15AIE-Cas or its derivative, pET30a-Csm2, and pUCE-S1). Several single colonies were picked up from the selective plates and inoculated into 20 ml LB medium with antibiotics. Cultivation was at 37°C, 220 rpm, overnight. Ten milliliters of the overnight culture was transferred to 1 l TB medium and cultured to a mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.8) under the same growth condition. Then, isopropyl-beta-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to 0.3 mM and the culture was further cultured at 25°C for 16 h to induce the synthesis of LdCsm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and cell pellets were resuspended in 50 ml buffer A (20 mM Tris–HCl, 0.25 M NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, pH8.5). The resulting cell suspension was treated by French press at 4°C and cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 10 000 rpm for 1 h at 4°C. LdCsm complexes were purified in a two-step purification procedure as previously described [21].

The LdCsm complex is captured on the HisTrap affinity column and eluted with buffer B (20 mM Tris–HCl, 0.25 M NaCl, 200 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, pH8.5). The product is further purified by gel filtration with Superdex 200 (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom) using the chromatography buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 0.25 M NaCl, 5% glycerol, pH8.5). Fraction samples are collected and used for further analysis.

Nucleic acid cleavage assay

DNA cleavage reactions (10 μl) were conducted in the cleavage buffer of 50 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 0.1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (pH7.0) containing the amounts of the wild-type (WT) LdCsm or one of its derivative effectors indicated in each assay, 50 nM 5′ FAM-labeled ssDNA substrate, and 500 nM target RNA [sequences of target RNAs, including CTR, NTR, and protospacer target RNA containing no 3′-PFS (PTR) are listed in Supplementary Table S2]. Nucleic acids cleavage was conducted at 37°C for 10 min and stopped by addition of 2× RNA loading dye (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Before loading, samples were heated for 3 min at 95°C and analyzed by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Cleavage products were visualized by fluorescence imaging analysis of the gel.

Fluorescence reporter DNA cleavage assay

The reporter DNA cleavage assay employed the same reaction setup as the DNA cleavage reaction described above except that 1 μM FAM-poly-16T-BHQ1 ssDNA substrate (Azenta Biotechnology Company, Qingdao, China) was used as DNA substrate and the total reaction volume was 20 μl. Reporter reactions were set up in a 384-well black plate (Thermo fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and incubated in an Enspire fluorescence plate reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min at 37°C. Fluorescence was measured in every 5 min (λex: 485 nm; λem: 535 nm). Relative activity was obtained by subtraction of the background fluorescence values generated for the reference sample without any target RNA from the fluorescence values obtained from the reactions containing the CTR.

Invader clearance assay

The assay was first described as the LCI assay in which invader plasmids are to be eliminated by LdCsm upon activation [23]. Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) strain carrying pColE-Cas-S1, a high-copy plasmid expressing the LdCsm effectors (pLdCsm), and p15a-CTR, a low-copy plasmid mimicking invader elements (pTarget), was employed as a positive control. Another strain containing pColE-Cas-S1 and p15a-CK (lacking any target sequence) was used as a reference (CK). In addition, a third strain carrying pColE-Cas-S1 and p15a-NTR (CK NTR) was for testing the influence of NTR on the immunity. Then pColE-Cas-S1 derivatives carrying each of the mutations designed for its Csm1 proteins were constructed and introduced into the p15a-CTR-carrying E. coli BL21(DE3) strain, yielding test strains for functional study of the LdCsm system. Single colonies of test strains and reference strains were inoculated into 5 ml LB medium containing 100 μg/ml Amp and 50 μg/ml Kan and cultured overnight at 37°C 220 rpm. Then, the seed cultures were transferred to 30 ml LB medium with Amp selection and cultured at 37°C 220 rpm for 2 h to reach mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.8). L-arabinose was then added to 0.1% to induce the transcription of CTR, which would then activate the LdCsm immunization. OD600 and pTarget loss rate of the induced cultures were measured every 30 or 45 min for 3.5 h, as conducted in our previous work [23].

Microscale thermophoresis assay of LdCsm substrate binding

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) experiments were carried out at 25°C with a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany) as described previously [38]. LdCsm or mutants were briefly incubated with equal concentration of CTR in buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH8.0), 187.5 mM NaCl, and 50 mM ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid, followed by a two-fold serial dilution. Afterward, an equal volume of 5 nM 5′-FAM labeled ssDNA substrate diluted in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH8.0) buffer was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for ~20 min. Subsequently, the sample was loaded into the Monolith NT.115 Capillary. MST was carried out using 100% LED power and medium MST power. Dissociation constant Kd of the WT LdCsm and its mutated derivatives was calculated using the MO software (MO.Affinity Analysis (x86), NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany) and data fitting was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In vivo assays and biochemical assays were repeated three times, and representative results are shown.

Results

Structure modeling reveals modular organization of tripartite structure elements in the HD domain of III-A Csm systems

AF3 modeling of the LdCsm RNP effector with a stoichiometry of LdCsm1123344151 and a crRNA of 38 nt identified two loop segments in the LdCsm1 HD domain, L1 and L2. The former stretches from N60 to V79, connecting the fourth and the fifth α-helices in the HD domain, and the latter is from D94 to I114, extending away from the fifth helix (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Examination of the published structural data of all available Csm effectors showed that these loop segments are also present in the HD domains of StCsm, LlCsm, and SeCsm (Supplementary Fig. S2). In these HD domains, L1 loop starts immediately after two conserved histidine residues in the HD catalytic core, whereas L2 loop is located on the opposite side of the catalytic cavity, starting with a conserved DRR motif (Supplementary Figs S1B and S2). Interestingly, neither of the above features is seen in the ToCsm HD domain structure, which instead possesses a regulatory loop segment at a similar position as L2, carrying an EREE motif (Supplementary Figs S1B and S2). A phylogenetic tree of 140 representative Csm1 proteins revealed that >80% of Csm1 proteins contain DRR motifs at the corresponding position of L2, whereas <10% contain ERE/EREE motifs (Supplementary Fig. S3). Therefore, Csm systems can be divided into DRR group and ERE/EREE group by different conserved motifs in loop L2. Indeed, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure analysis of ToCsm effectors revealed that the EREE-containing loop in ToCsm1 is partially disordered in CTR–ToCsm while becomes traceable in NTR–ToCsm and binary ToCsm [29]. It was also reported that alanine substitution of the R residue in the EREE motif or replacement of the EREE-containing loop by GGS linker in ToCsm1 inactivated ToCsm DNase, demonstrating the importance of the conserved motif. Intriguingly, substitution of either the first E or the last EE residues in the EREE motif abolished the regulation of the ToCsm DNase, and the resulting mutated ToCsm effector complexes showed low DNase activity in the absence of CTR. Thus, it was suggested that this EREE-containing loop potentially adopts an autoinhibitory conformation to mediate ToCsm DNase regulation, which stays in rigid conformation and utilizes the E residues within EREE motif to prevent access of ssDNA in binary ToCsm and NTR–Csm [29]. However, the ERE/EREE motif is not present in the major group of Csm systems. Thus, it remains elusive how the Csm DNases are to be activated.

To study that, AF3 modeling was employed to predict structures of the above mentioned LdCsm effector complexes in the presence/absence of 4–6 nt ssDNA substrates (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We found that the HD residues K19, H34, and H59 in the catalytic core and R95, R96 residues in the L2 DRR motif interact with ssDNA directly when substrate is positioned in the DNase active center (Fig. 1A and B). We also found, while the topology of L1 remains the same regardless whether ssDNA substrate is present, L2 loop clashes with the ssDNA in the HD domain. Thus, the L2 loop segment has to be remodeled to accommodate the incoming ssDNA substrate (Fig. 1C). Indeed, the conformational change is predicted for L2 such that its outward-shifting brings the conserved R95R96 residues toward the incoming DNA. Consequently, the interactions between ssDNA and the conserved RR motifs stabilize the enzyme–substrate complex via electrostatic interactions (Fig. 1B). This suggests that the two HD loops could function in substrate capture and subsequent binding in the LdCsm DNase.

AF3 modeling of additional Csm tertiary effector complexes belonging to DRR group, including StCsm, SeCsm, and LICsm showed that they all undergo similar conformational changes [Supplementary Figs S4 (top panel) and S5 (left panel)], as observed above with LdCsm. By contrast, the ToCsm and TtCsm effectors featured with the EREE motif do not display prominent displacement in their loops [Supplementary Figs S4 (bottom panel) and S5 (right panel)]. Thus, we hypothesize that during substrate binding process of Csm systems containing DRR motifs, the ssDNA could trigger the conformational change of L2 to activate the substrate binding, leading to DNA cleavage at the HD catalytic core of Csm DNases.

Loop integrity and conserved loop residues are essential for the LdCsm DNase

To investigate the importance of the HD loops in the activation of LdCsm DNase, several loop truncation mutants of LdCsm1 were constructed, including three L1 mutants (d-L1a, d-L1b, d-L1c) and two L2 mutants (d-L2a, d-L2b), and tested for DNA cleavage activity in vitro and immune response by the invader clearance assay (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S6A). The corresponding LdCsm effectors carrying each of the above truncations were all obtained (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Biochemical characterization of their ssDNA cleavage using denaturing PAGE analysis revealed that: (i) in the presence of PTR, NTR, or no target RNA, none of these loop-truncated LdCsm effectors was capable of cleaving ssDNA substrates, same as the WT LdCsm effector; (ii) in the presence of CTR, while LdCsm effector carrying Csm1d-L1a, Csm1d-L1b, or Csm1d-L1c mutation retained residual activity, carrying Csm1d-L2a or Csm1d-L2b truncation showed no DNase activity (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Furthermore, quenched-fluorescence DNA reporter assay [21] was employed for relative quantification of their DNase activity in presence of CTR, revealing that LdCsm effectors carrying Csm1d-L1a, Csm1d-L1b, or Csm1d-L1c mutation each retained 23.4%, 8.5%, or 6.9% of DNase activity relative to the WT LdCsm effector, while those carrying Csm1d-L2a or Csm1d-L2b mutation completely lost it (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig S7C). These results are in agreement with the modeling result where L2 conformational change represents the key step in LdCsm activation. However, none of these truncated mutants are active in mediating LdCsm immunity in the invader clearance assay (Fig. 1E and F, and Supplementary Fig. S7D and E), suggesting their residual activities cannot attain the DNase threshold required for the LdCsm immunity. Nevertheless, these results indicate that the LdCsm HD domain contains three functional elements, the HD catalytic core and two disordered loops, all of which are crucially important for the function of the LdCsm DNase.

The HD disordered loop L2 carries a few conserved amino acids, including the conserved D94R95R96 amino acid residues at the start position and D113 at the loop end, forming a salt bridge with R125 (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S6B). These amino acids in LdCsm1 were mutated by substitution and the resulting LdCsm effectors carrying each of the mutations [Csm1D94A, Csm1R95A, Csm1R96A, Csm1RR (R95AR96A), Csm1D113A, Csm1D113N, and Csm1R125A substitutions] were purified (Supplementary Fig. S7A). The purified effectors were tested for the DNase activity by analyzing DNA cleavage products using denaturing PAGE. We found that all mutated LdCsm effectors are inactivated for in vitro DNA cleavage in the presence of CTR and none of them showed DNase activity in PTR, NTR, or no target RNA-bound forms (Supplementary Fig. S7B), and this is inconsistent with the autoinhibitory function proposed for the EREE motif from ToCsm [29], suggesting LdCsm has adopted a novel mechanism of regulation. Then, the quenched-fluorescence DNA reporter assay was employed to test the DNase activity of these mutated LdCsm effectors in CTR-bound form, further confirming that they all lost DNase activity (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S7F). Testing the in vivo function of these amino acids using invader clearance assay (Fig. 1E) revealed that all the mutations completely abolished the immune response of the LdCsm system (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. S7G and H). Together, these results indicate that these two HD unstructured loops and the conserved residues therein constitute integral parts of the Csm HD domain.

Charged amino acids on the loops finetune the DNase and the immunity of the LdCsm system

Both L1 and L2 are rich in charged amino acid residues, including E63, K65K66, R70, E73, and E76 on L1 and E98E99 and K102 on L2 (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S6B). To assess their functional roles, each of these charged amino acids was subjected to alanine substitution analysis. Testing their DNase activity by PAGE analysis of fluorescence-labeled cleavage products revealed that in CTR-activated forms, LdCsm effectors carrying Csm1E63A, Csm1K66A, Csm1E73AE76A, or Csm12E (E98AE99A) mutations are fully active, those carrying Csm1K65A, Csm1K65AK66A, Csm1R70A, or Csm1K102A substitution retained detectable residual activity, whereas none of their DNase activity can be detected in the presence of PTR, NTR, or in the absence of any target RNA (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B). Their DNase activity was also quantitively tested using the quenched-fluorescence DNA reporter assay. We found that the DNase activity of LdCsm effectors carrying Csm1E73AE76A (in L1 loop) or Csm12E (in L2 loop) is ~1.2- and 2.9-fold higher than the WT, whereas those containing Csm1K65AK66A, Csm1K65A, Csm1R70A, or Csm1K102A retained residual activity of 7%, 9%, 36%, and 60%, respectively (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S8C). Furthermore, invader clearance assay revealed that substitutions of positively charged amino acids reduced the LdCsm immunity, whereas substitutions of negatively charged ones enhanced the immunity (Fig. 2C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S8D–G). Since L1 and L2 are located nearby the HD catalytic pocket, alanine substitutions of nonconserved positively charged amino acids like arginine and lysine in L1 and L2 would reduce their positive charge level, thereby weakening their interaction with negatively charged DNA substrates. Thus, it is reasonable to deduce that these mutations reduced LdCsm immunity via hindering the DNA substrate binding process. Conversely, alanine substitutions of nonconserved negatively charged glutamic acids in L1 and L2 could have enhanced LdCsm immunity by promoting the DNA substrate binding process. Together, these results indicate that L1 and L2 function in DNA substrate binding of the LdCsm effector.

Charged amino acid residues in both loops interact with substrate

MST, a powerful technique for quantitating biomolecular interactions at low concentrations such as DNA–protein interactions [38], was employed to characterize a selected set of mutated LdCsm effectors for their substrate binding using 5′-FAM-labeled ssDNA as the substrate. This revealed that the WT CTR-LdCsm ternary complex binds the substrate with the dissociation constant (Kd) value of 1.64 μM whereas NTR-LdCsm did not show any detectable interaction with ssDNA (Fig. 2E and Table 1). Investigation of the interaction between the ssDNA substrate and LdCsm effector carrying a mutation in the loops, such as d-L1a, d-L1b, E63A, K65A, and K66A (L1 mutants) and d-L2a, d-L2b, D94A, RR, 2E, and D113N (L2 mutants), revealed these amino acids fall into three groups: (i) Seven loop mutations completely abolished the substrate binding of the LdCsm system, including three loop truncations (Csm1d-L1a, Csm1d-L1b, and Csm1d-L2b) and four substitution mutants (Csm1K65A, Csm1D94A, Csm1RR, and Csm1D113N); (ii) two mutants showed impaired activity of substrate binding, including Csm1d-L2a, a 5 AA truncation on L2, and Csm1K66A substitution on L1; and (iii) two additional ones exhibited higher affinity to ssDNA, i.e. Csm1E63A on L1 and Csm12E on L2 (Fig. 2E and Table 1). The loss of DNA binding capability in the first group of loop mutations is consistent with the strongly impaired/inactivation of the in vitro LdCsm DNase and the complete loss of the in vivo immune responses. Furthermore, the strongly impaired substrate binding in the second group also lead to the inactivation of the LdCsm immunity whereas the last group showing increased substrate binding affinity exhibits enhanced in vivo LdCsm immunity, consistent with their elevated DNase activity (Figs 1D–H and 2B–D). The CTR-bound state of LdCsm-Csm12E mutant, for example, showed 4.6-fold decrease in its Kd value compared to the WT LdCsm effector (Table 1), indicative of substantially enhancement in DNA substrate binding ability. As a result, the 2E (E98AE99A)-mutated LdCsm effector exhibited in 2.9-fold increase in the DNase activity in quenched-fluorescence DNA reporter assay (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S8C). These in vitro data are consistent with the enhanced pTarget clearance activity by the corresponding mutated LdCsm system in the invader clearance assay (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. S8F and G), presumably by promoting the DNA substrate binding of the effector. Thus, both the loop integrity and their conserved amino acid residues are crucially important to maintain the DNA substrate binding capability during LdCsm immunity. Moreover, the electric charges of loop L1 and L2 function in finetuning the LdCsm DNase activity via adjusting the DNA substrate binding ability of LdCsm effector.

Functional roles of LdCsm HD residues in DNA cleavage and substrate binding

We also characterized the HD residues in the catalytic core of the LdCsm system. In a previous work, cryo-EM structure analysis revealed that the consensus residues of HD..K..H..HH..D..H of the ToCsm DNase catalytic core are clustered together (Supplementary Figs S1B and S2) [29]. AF3 structure modeling of the apo-form of the LdCsm RNP effector predicts a very similar structure of the HD residues in LdCsm1 subunit, and it further suggests that D16 and D87 coordinate two metal ions along with the side chains of four histidine residues (H15, H34, H58, and H59) (Fig. 3A). These structural features are in full agreement with the HD catalytic sites of Cas3 effectors of type I CRISPR systems although these HD motifs start with the last histidine residue of the Csm motif (H..HD..K..H..HH..D) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S9) [31–33]. To functionally characterize these HD residues in LdCsm, each of them was mutated by alanine or asparagine substitution, and this yielded Csm1H15A, Csm1D16N, Csm1K19A, Csm1H34A, Csm1H58A, Csm1H59A, Csm1D87N, and Csm1H206A mutants. LdCsm effector complexes carrying each of the above mutated HD residues were expressed in, and purified from, E. coli (Supplementary Fig. S10A) and characterized for ssDNA cleavage in the presence of CTR, PTR, NTR, or no target RNA. Analysis of cleavage products of fluorescence-labeled ssDNAs revealed that CTR, but not NTR or PTR, activates the DNase of the WT LdCsm and that of LdCsm-Csm1H59A (Supplementary Fig. S10B). DNA cleavage activity of the above LdCsm effectors was quantitated by the quenched-fluorescence DNA reporter assay, and the two active ones showed very similar activity of DNA cleavage; in contrast, all other HD mutations basically abolished the LdCsm DNase (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S10C).

To investigate how the in vitro DNase activity could reflect the immune responses mediated by LdCsm, the effect of these HD residue mutations on the LdCsm immunity was investigated using the invader clearance assay. Consistent with the in vitro data, plasmid-borne expression of CTR is capable of activating the immunity of the WT LdCsm system, which in turn eliminates pTarget plasmid expressing the CTR (Supplementary Fig. S10D). As a result, viable cell accounts are greatly decreased (Supplementary Fig. S10E) due to lysis of the cells lacking the pTarget plasmid in the ampicillin media. In contrast, plasmid-borne expression of NTRs did not trigger the immune responses (Fig. 3D). In addition, alanine substitution of any of the conserved HD residues tested above completely abolished the LdCsm immunity, including LdCsm-Csm1H59A that is as active as the WT LdCsm effector in the in vitro assay (Fig. 3C). Considering that the local cellular environment for LdCsm DNA cleavage could be very different from the condition in biochemical assay, many reasons may lead to the discrepancy between the results obtained from the in vivo experiments and those from in vitro assay of LdCsm-Csm1H59A. Nevertheless, a plausible explanation is that the DNA substrates of the LdCsm effector in vivo are different from those employed in the in vitro assays. In this regard, H59 could be important in interaction with DNA targets in vivo, e.g. transcription bubbles.

These HD residue-mutated LdCsm effectors were also investigated for ssDNA substrate binding by using MST. MST assays with HD residue substitutions in the presence of CTR or NTR yielded the following results: (i) LdCsm-Csm1H59A did not exhibit any detectable ssDNA binding, suggesting substrate binding facilitated by H59 residue is essential for LdCsm immune system in vivo; (ii) several mutants are inactive in DNA cleavage (LdCsm-Csm1K19A, -Csm1D87N, and -Csm1H206A), but they showed high affinity to ssDNA (Fig. 3E); (iii) although LdCsm-Csm1D87N is inactive, both CTR- and NTR-form of the mutated LdCsm bind the ssDNA substrate (Supplementary Fig. S10F and Table 1). Together, these results show that the DNA-binding by HD catalytic pocket of LdCsm effector is manifold, where conserved residues constituting the HD catalytic pocket play different roles in the DNA-binding and the subsequent DNA cleavage process.

Characterization of factors influencing LdCsm substrate binding

To further characterize substrate binding, the WT LdCsm effector and four mutated ones, i.e. those carrying Csm1E63A, Csm1D87N, or Csm12E substitution and Csm1d-L2a (98–102 aa) deletion, were chosen for comparative studies of their substrate affinity. Four different forms were analyzed for each LdCsm effector, including the apo form (binary LdCsm effector carrying no target RNA) and three ternary LdCsm effector carrying either (i) PTR, (ii) NTR, or (iii) CTR. We found that the apo form of the WT LdCsm does not bind to ssDNA whereas addition of either CTR or PTR activates the substrate binding, and furthermore, inclusion of NTR inhibits the substrate binding (Fig. 4A and Table 1). Since CTR-induced major conformational change has been reported in all tested type III effector complexes [26–29], these results suggest that recognition of CTR by LdCsm effector facilitate its conformational change that enables the DNA substrate binding, but recognition of NTR suppresses the conformational change and prevents DNA substrate binding. These results also demonstrate that the NTR-bound Csm effector does not show the potential to be activated for substrate binding.

We further demonstrate that substitution mutation of Csm1E63A, Csm1D87N, Csm12E, or a five amino acids L2 deletion (Csm1d-L2a) rendered the mutated LdCsm effectors active in substrate binding in the apo form as well as in the presence of NTR (Fig. 4B–E and Table 1), but this type of substrate does not lead to cleavage (ineffective substrate binding). These results suggest that each of these charged amino acids are essential for preventing ineffective substrate binding to LdCsm effectors. Noticeably, the apo- and the NTR-bound LdCsm effectors of the L2 truncation mutant (Csm1d-L2a) showed higher substrate affinity than the corresponding CTR-bound form (Fig. 4F and Table 1), indicating that the L2 loop integrity is crucially important for the substrate-induced substrate channel formation. Thus, the activation of LdCsm DNase involves a series of sequential events to build up the substrate channel for effective substrate binding, leading to the CTR-responsive DNA cleavage in the HD domain of the LdCsm DNase.

Discussion

How type III-A Csm DNase is activated has been a long-standing question in CRISPR research. Structural analysis of apo-, NTR-, and CTR-bound forms of several type III effector complexes has provided the structural basis for the mechanisms of cOA synthesis, but comparisons between the HD catalytic center structures of the active enzymes and those of the inactive ones only show marginal conformational change [26, 27, 29, 39], suggesting that the catalytic core of DNases is probably active. However, these structural analyses have not provided any insightful knowledge about the activation process of Csm DNases. In this work, we demonstrate that the LdCsm DNase activation is primarily controlled at the stage of substrate binding. A tripartite structural element has been identified in the HD domain, including two distinct disordered loops, L1 and L2, and the catalytic pocket. The two loops are positioned on the opposite sides of the catalytic pocket, blocking the entrance of substrate to the catalytic pocket in the inactive state. Upon activation, the two loops function in concert with the HD conserved residues to actively capture DNA substrate and deliver it to the catalytic pocket of Csm DNases for DNA shredding. The entire process probably involves two conformational changes: first, the CTR-induced conformational change that provides the basis for the substrate-induced conformational change of L2; second, L2 remodeling activates the substrate capture and delivering that only operates in the configuration of CTR–Csm effector complexes, leading to DNA cleavage in the catalytic pocket (Fig. 5). This unique architecture of tripartite HD structural elements is well conserved in the HD nuclease domains of several characterized Csm systems (Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting that the revealed mechanisms generally operate in III-A CRISPR–Cas systems.

Model for the molecular mechanism of ssDNA capture and cleavage by the LdCsm antiviral system. Molecular mechanism for activation or inactivation of LdCsm DNase. Paths that lead to successful capture of ssDNA substrate by HD catalytic pocket and ssDNA cleavage are labeled by thick red arrows.

We have then deduced the process of DNA cleavage by the LdCsm DNase. Since the activation process involves L2 remodeling, it is thus likely that effective substrate interaction would start with L1. Here, two amino acids on L1 are implicated in coordinating substrate binding: while K65 initiates proper substrate placement in the substrate channel, E63 rejects the CTR-independent binding. The same principle also applies to the substrate binding at the second step where H59 facilitates the binding whereas D87 rejects the CTR-independent interaction; the incoming substrate will then push L2 aside, inducing the conformational change to expose R95R96 for substrate interaction whereas E98E99 also prevents the CTR-independent binding. The L2 remodeling facilitates the correct placement of the substrate in the substrate channel to ensure DNA cleavage to occur in the catalytic center (Fig. 5). Taken together, all three structural elements are actively involved in the substrate sorting and delivering process since they all contain negatively charged amino acids that ensure substrate binding occurs in the CTR-dependent fashion whereas positively charged amino acids function in capturing DNA substrate.

Using the MST assay, we found that CTR facilitates the substrate-binding-induced L2 remodeling, whereas NTR does not (Fig. 4). These findings are further supported by the following observations: (i) dynamic analysis based on cryo-EM structural data and protein domain labeling experiments indicates that the HD structural domain is more dynamic in the CTR-LlCsm effector compared with the corresponding NTR-LlCsm [27], (ii) single-molecule study has revealed that the HD domain of CTR-SeCsm displays a more dynamic and mobile state than that of NTR-SeCsm [40], and (iii) these disordered loops are either not, or partially, captured by cryo-EM structural analysis [26, 28]. We propose that dynamic change of Csm1 HD domain and its loops is attributed to the distinct binding channels of target RNA’s 3′-PFS in CTR- and NTR-Csm effectors. As reported for StCsm and LlCsm effectors, nucleotides (-2)’ to (-5)’ in 3′-PFS of NTR base pair with 5′-tag of crRNA and bind to a grove closer to Csm4 subunit, while the 3′-PFS of CTR mismatches with 5′-tag of crRNA and is compelled to interact with the linker Zn-finger region between two Palm domains and certain α-helices or loops in Palm1 domain of Csm1 [26, 27]. Interactions with a free 3′-PFS possibly cause higher dynamic of these segments in Csm1, which is then transferred from Palm1 domain to its neighboring N-terminal HD domain. Consistent with this speculation, mutation of conserved amino acid residues within the linker Zn-finger region of LdCsm1 severely impaired LdCsm DNase [21].

In addition to CTR and NTR, a protospacer target RNA carrying no 3′-PFS (PTR) which represents an intermediate state between recognition of CTR and NTR was also tested for its influence on Csm DNase activation in this work. The PAGE analysis and MST assay about PTR revealed that Csm effector’s DNA substrate binding potential could be sequentially regulated during target RNA binding, where base pairing of the protospacer region to crRNA induces a moderate level of DNA substrate binding capability, subsequent recognition of a 3′-PFS mismatching crRNA’s 5′-tag (from CTR) enhances it to enable L2 remodeling and DNA substrate capturing, whereas base pairing of a 3′-PFS to crRNA’s 5′-tag (from NTR) completely abolishes it. This is consistent with a hypothesis about sequential conformational change of LdCsm HD domain triggered by progressive target RNA binding in our previous work [21]. According to the model we presented here, sequential regulation of the Csm effector’s DNA substrate binding potential could be achieved by adjusting the mobility of the HD loops, especially L2.

Taken altogether, the mechanisms of the spatiotemporal regulation of Csm DNases can be deduced as the following. In CTR-Csm effectors, the two disordered loops in HD domains have less restriction and the L2 loop in particular can flip to a point in which the RR residues in the DRR motif are pushed by the incoming substrate into a configuration, which allows the two positively charged amino acids to interact with DNA substrate and stabilize the conformation (L2 remodeling). These interactions lead to the formation of the substrate binding channel where effective substrate-enzyme interactions are stabilized by the tripartite structural elements in the HD domain. DNA cleavage is then immediately followed, and cleavage products are then released from the HD domain. In the meantime, CTR cleavage diminishes the potential for CTR activation and thereby inactivating the Csm DNase.

The revealed mechanisms are very different from those obtained from the type I CRISPR–Cas. In the latter, DNA target is scanned and captured for R-loop formation by the Cascade and then delivered to the HD domain of Cas3 nucleases for DNA shredding [5, 31–33]. This is because type III-A Csm systems mediate unspecific DNA cleavage and their targets are short ssDNA strands in transcriptional bubbles, which can either be a specific one (containing the target sequence) or nonspecific ones. The latter are random transcriptional bubbles that happen to be accessible to activated type III-A effectors. Thus, how nonspecific DNA substrates are captured and delivered to the HD pocket for cleavage represents the main challenge for their DNases. Consistently, in the recently resolved structures of several type III effectors, the catalytic cavity of their DNases is not accessible to DNA substrate. Here we show that these systems have evolved disordered loops on the periphery of the HD domain to gate the HD catalytic cavity. Thus, these loops are at the position to catch incoming substrate. Indeed, we have experimentally demonstrated that the loops function in concert to facilitate DNA shredding at the HD catalytic core.

Intriguingly, the resulting knowledge of this work can guide further optimization of Csm DNase-based RNA detection tools [41, 42]. Featured by ssDNA reporter cleavage after target RNA recognition, Csm DNase-based RNA detection tools have an advantage over other CRISPR-based ones on simplicity and anti-interference ability [42]. Recently, we combined a LdCsm DNase-based RNA detection tool with graphene field-effect transistor (GFET) to produce an amplification-free biosensor named CRISPR–GFET with detection limit at 400 aM [43], making a promising candidate for point-of-care diagnosis. Engineering the LdCsm effector to enhance its DNase activity would be a feasible way to further optimize its detection sensitivity. This work reveals that reduction of charged amino acids in HD loops can influence LdCsm DNase activity in a relatively regular way, which can work as a guideline for construction of DNase-enhanced LdCsm effectors. Same strategy can also be applied to other Csm DNase-based RNA detection tools.

Up to now, the interactions between Csm1 HD domains and their DNA substrates have not been clarified by structural analysis methods [26–30]. In this context, our work combining AF3 modeling and experimental verification provided an alternative approach to give deeper insights into this question, which complemented the previous structural studies on type III-A Csm1 HD domains to a degree. However, given that AF3 modeling has limitation in prediction of flexible loop regions with confidence, we still need to seek for support from structural evidence in the future to further validate our model about L2 remodeling. Inspired by the results in this work, changing the charge level of L1/L2 in LdCsm effector and enhancing its DNA binding affinity could be a putative strategy to help to capture its ssDNA substrate binding conformation by the cryo-EM method.
