How Empathy and Partisanship Affected Attitude Changes Following the Assassination of Shinzo Abe: Evidence from Panel Surveys
Authors: Zeyu Lyu, Susumu Cato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShinzo Abe, the longest-serving prime minister in Japan’s postwar history, was assassinated on July 8, 2022, triggering widespread social reactions and shifts in public opinion. This study investigates the effects of the assassination on attitudes toward Abe using panel data from before and after the assassination. Our results suggest that, overall, attitudes toward Abe significantly improved after the assassination, and that the extent of attitude change varied between groups. Specifically, we find that individuals with strong empathy were more likely to improve their attitude toward Abe, indicating a pattern of emotion-driven attitude change. Our analysis also suggests that partisanship may have shaped individuals’ attitude changes. Specifically, individuals with opposing party preferences were more resistant to attitude change, whereas partisan proximity facilitated positive reassessments. Moreover, the influence of partisanship on attitude change depended on its intensity. Individuals without sustained and strong party preferences were more susceptible to attitude change following the assassination. Overall, this study provides empirical evidence for attitude changes due to political violence and has implications for our understanding of the mechanisms of such attitude changes.

Introduction

Shinzo Abe, the longest-serving prime minister in the postwar history of Japan, was assassinated on July 8, 2022, while delivering a public speech. The assassination of high-profile politicians can have substantial political and societal ramifications (Iqbal and Zorn 2008). For example, the assassination of President Kennedy led to a surge in national unity and public approval (Sheatsley and Feldman 1964); and the assassination of Israeli prime minister Yitzhak Rabin in 1995 led to widespread grief and significant shifts in public attitudes toward him in the immediate aftermath (Raviv et al. 2000). These examples show that political assassinations can have a profound impact on large populations and are a significant factor in shaping public opinion (Sicinski 1969; Esaiasson and Granberg 1996). Similarly, Abe’s assassination caused a shock wave, and the severity and impact of an event like this raises critical questions with regard to its effects on public opinion in Japan.

This study examines how Abe’s assassination affected public opinion in Japan by means of panel data resulting from surveys conducted before and in the immediate aftermath of the assassination. Specifically, we assume that the impact of a political assassination on opinion changes varies across different groups. Building on existing theories and research, we focus on two individual characteristics, empathy and partisanship, which may serve as key factors influencing attitude change.

First, we expect that the impact of an assassination may be linked to individuals’ personality traits (Webster and Albertson 2022). Previous studies have suggested that exposure to political violence, such as an assassination, often evokes empathy and grief for the victims, potentially leading to a positive shift in attitude (Raviv et al. 2000), which is known as the empathy effect (Dinas et al. 2016). Specifically, individuals with high empathy are expected to spontaneously experience profound emotional responses and attitude change (Mehrabian and Epstein 1972). Such changes in attitude in times of crisis can be explained by the terror management theory (TMT; Greenberg et al. 1997). TMT posits that humans’ awareness of the inevitability of death gives rise to mortality salience. To mitigate the anxiety associated with death, individuals tend to adopt cultural worldviews that provide meaning of life and the promise of immortality to those who adhere to culturally prescribed standards of value (Solomon et al. 1991; Pyszczynski et al. 1997). In the context of an assassination event, the sudden and violent nature of the event can starkly heighten mortality salience, thrusting the reality of death into public consciousness and activating the psychological defenses described by TMT, including idealizing the victim in the assassination to mitigate heightened death-related fears (Allison et al. 2009). Such idealization evokes the sense of security derived from upholding the cherished principles of the cultural worldview. Especially, individuals with higher levels of empathy tend to derive a sense of meaning and value from displaying kindness and forgiveness toward others (Schimel et al. 2006), thus their attitudes are more likely to be significantly influenced by the assassination event. Building on these insights, we can assume that empathy is a key factor that may increase approval for the victims of assassinations, leading to the following hypothesis:H1: People with stronger empathy tend to adopt more positive attitudes toward Abe following his assassination.

H1: People with stronger empathy tend to adopt more positive attitudes toward Abe following his assassination.

Furthermore, individuals’ reactions to Abe’s assassination may have varied with partisanship, which is widely acknowledged to be a crucial factor in cognitive processing and attitudes (Bolsen et al. 2014; Van Bavel and Pereira 2018). Typically, as a form of social identity, partisans derive a sense of belonging from their support for a political party (West and Iyengar 2022). This identity can promote affinity for individuals with similar political views while reinforcing bias toward opposing groups, shaping how individuals perceive and interpret information (Iyengar and Krupenkin 2018; Iyengar et al. 2019; West and Iyengar 2022; Lyu and Lyu 2023). Specifically, the influence of partisanship can be thought of in terms of in-group favoritism and out-group hostility. On the one hand, partisanship often promotes thoughts and actions that preserve or heighten the status of in-group members (Ditto et al. 2025). Thus, Abe’s assassination could have been perceived as an attack, not just on Abe as an individual, but on the broader political ideology and identity that he represented. This could have produced a rallying effect among his supporters and greater cohesion among in-group partisans. On the other hand, partisanship can lead to a form of othering characterized by the hostility and negative attitudes toward out-groups, in which opposing partisans are perceived not simply as holding different views, but as fundamentally different and morally inferior (Rathje et al. 2021). In this case, factors contributing to attitude improvement typically diminish when misfortune occurs to members of out-groups (Simas et al. 2020; Allamong and Peterson 2021).

Overall, we can assume an increase in positive attitudes among individuals whose partisanship aligned with Abe’s, and that the strength of this effect is related to individuals’ specific partisanship. In Japanese politics, the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP), of which Abe was a member, has been the dominant political party since 1955, while several other parties exist. Based on alignment with Abe’s party affiliation, individuals can be broadly categorized as LDP supporters or non-LDP supporters (i.e., supporters of other parties). However, there are substantial differences between other parties in regard to their relations with the LDP and their policy positions relative to the LDP—some strongly oppose the LDP, while others share common ground on specific issues or maintain cooperative ties. Therefore, reactions to the assassination may also have varied among non-LDP supporters with differing partisan perspectives. Taken together, we can propose the following hypotheses:H2-1: Individuals whose partisanship aligns with Abe’s tend to adopt more positive attitudes toward him after the assassination.H2-2: Individuals whose partisanship differs from Abe’s are less likely to develop positive attitudes toward him after the assassination.

H2-1: Individuals whose partisanship aligns with Abe’s tend to adopt more positive attitudes toward him after the assassination.

H2-2: Individuals whose partisanship differs from Abe’s are less likely to develop positive attitudes toward him after the assassination.

Another key factor when examining the role of partisanship is its intensity. Specifically, individuals with strong partisanship are more likely to interpret events through the lens of their preexisting political beliefs and affiliations, while those with weaker or unstable party preferences are more influenced by events that shape public opinions and attitudes because their political identity is less rigidly defined (Castro Cornejo 2021). This distinction is particularly relevant in the Japanese political context, where party support tends to be relatively fluid, with some people frequently adjusting their support based on shifting political, economic, and local factors (Kabashima and Ishio 1998). This fluidity suggests that individuals with weaker partisanship are more likely to be influenced by external events than by their current party preference when they form their attitudes. To incorporate this perspective, this study proposes the following hypothesis:H3: Individuals with less stable partisanship are more likely to change their attitudes toward Abe after his assassination.

H3: Individuals with less stable partisanship are more likely to change their attitudes toward Abe after his assassination.

Data and Method

We conducted surveys using the online platform Qualtrics. Respondents were recruited by Cross Marketing Inc., one of Japan’s largest survey companies with about 4.65 million registered members. Our respondents were recruited using quota sampling based on gender, residential location, and age. Thus, the expected distribution of participant characteristics was considered comparable to that of the Japanese population. We conducted multiple survey waves during December 18–21, 2020 (Wave 1, N = 7,080), March 19–22, 2021 (Wave 2, N = 5,304), November 1–10, 2021 (Wave 3, N = 4,808), and July 11–20, 2022 (Wave 4, N = 4,486). The present study is based on surveys that were conducted before the assassination (Waves 1 to 3) and afterward (Wave 4), allowing us to examine changes in attitudes toward Abe. Given that the necessary variables for the analysis were present in different survey waves,1 we integrated them and removed respondents with missing values. As a result, N = 3,308 respondents were included in the analysis.2 Further information about the survey methods and data can be found in Appendix A.

Respondents were required to indicate their attitudes toward Abe (0: negative and 10: positive), and this study employs a fixed-effects regression model to estimate the impact of the event on individuals’ attitudes. This modeling strategy enables us to remove the unobserved heterogeneity by using within-individual changes in attitudes toward Abe that are attributable to the event itself and its interaction with key covariates. Notably, these covariates are estimated based on surveys conducted before the assassination, and therefore are treated as time-invariant variables in this analysis. Specifically, time-invariant covariates include individuals’ empathy, which is measured by asking respondents to evaluate themselves as persons who are kind and thoughtful toward others (1: Absolutely not and 7: Absolutely yes).3 Also, for each respondent, we aggregate respondents’ answers to party support questions from Waves 1 to 3 and define their supported party as the one they most frequently selected across the surveys. When a majority choice does not exist, their response from the latest wave is taken as their supported party. Partisanship includes the LDP, Constitutional Democratic Party of Japan (CDP), Japanese Communist Party (JCP), Reiwa Shinsengumi, Japan Innovation Party (JIP), Democratic Party for the People (DPP), and the Komei Party (Komeito). Support for other, smaller parties is categorized as “Other.” Responses indicating an unwillingness to answer are categorized as “No answer,” and respondents who did not support any party are categorized as “No support.” Here, the reference category is “No support,” indicating individuals without explicit partisan leanings. Moreover, we calculated the number of different parties supported by the same individual in the three survey waves before the assassination to estimate the strength of individuals’ partisanship. In other words, we consider frequent changes in supported parties an indicator of low partisan strength.4

Results

Changes in Attitudes After the Assassination

Figure 1A demonstrates how people’s attitudes toward Abe changed after the assassination. We find that attitudes toward Abe became approximately 31 percent more positive after the assassination (from M = 3.964 to M = 5.199, p < 0.001). To gain further insights into the attitude changes, figure 1B illustrates the shifts in attitude toward Abe following the assassination among individuals with varying initial views. The bar plot shows the differences between attitudes before and after the assassination. Positive values denote an improvement in attitudes. Notably, people who previously felt strong animosity toward Abe exhibited significant improvement in their attitudes after the assassination. These results are consistent with studies suggesting that people tend to boost their evaluations of individuals who have passed away (Allison et al. 2009; Drzewiecka and Cwalina 2020).

Regarding H1, Model 2 indicates that empathy significantly influences the improvement of attitudes toward Abe (0.126, p < 0.001). This result supports the empathy effect, suggesting the improvement in attitudes can be attributed to the emotional response elicited by his death and his role as a victim of political violence.

For H2, Model 3 examines how partisanship affects changes in attitudes toward Abe. With regard to in-group favoritism, although the results indicate that individuals who support the LDP did not significantly improve compared to the attitudes of those who did not explicitly support a political party (−0.038, p = 0.665), we find that individuals who supported the JIP greatly improved their attitudes toward Abe after the assassination (0.368, p = 0.002). This result can be attributed to the policy similarities and cooperative relationship between the JIP and LDP, particularly during Abe’s administration. The close policy alignment likely fostered a sense of affinity between JIP supporters and Abe, which contributed to the observed positive shift in their attitudes driven by in-group favoritism. With regard to out-group hostility, we find that compared to individuals who do not explicitly support a political party, individuals who supported the JCP, a left-wing party with a significant ideological distance from the LDP, were less likely to improve their attitudes toward Abe (−0.479, p = 0.010), indicating that out-group hostility may influence how people interpret events and change their attitudes. However, this mechanism appears to be significant only among supporters of parties that oppose the LDP, such as JCP supporters.6

Regarding H3, Model 4 shows that individuals who frequently changed which party they supported were more likely to develop a more favorable attitude toward Abe after the assassination (0.157, p = 0.003), suggesting that individuals with weaker partisanship are more susceptible to an event’s influence.

Model 5 includes all variables, and we find that these effects remain statistically significant. Specifically, the effect of empathy remains largely unchanged (0.126, p < 0.001), suggesting that the empathy effect is a key psychological mechanism driving attitude change. Moreover, although the effects related to partisanship shift after controlling for empathy, they still play a significant role in explaining changes in attitudes.

Discussion

Our analysis suggests that attitudes toward Abe significantly improved after the assassination, and that the extent of attitude change varied between different groups. Specifically, we find that people’s empathy and partisanship led to variations in attitude change. Overall, this study provides empirical evidence for attitude changes due to political violence and has implications for our understanding of the mechanisms of such attitude changes.

First, our analysis indicates that personality traits can be an important factor in explaining attitude change after an assassination. Individuals with high empathy are more likely to experience profound emotional responses, which translates into more favorable attitudes toward victims. In the context of an assassination, emotions such as grief and shock can spread widely through the public via emotional contagion, reinforcing affective convergence and shaping opinion dynamics in political communication.

Second, our analysis demonstrates the role of individuals’ partisanship in shaping their reactions to the assassination of Abe. While LDP supporters did not significantly increase their support for Abe, JIP supporters—despite not belonging to the LDP—showed a more notable improvement in attitudes, likely due to their partisan proximity to Abe. This response reflects a receptiveness to positive reassessments rooted in in-group favoritism. In contrast, we find that JCP supporters were less likely to improve their attitudes toward Abe, which can be attributed to their ideological opposition to him, highlighting the impact of out-group hostility. Overall, although ideological distinctions among Japanese political parties are generally moderate, the influence of partisanship still emerges among supporters of more ideologically distinct parties.

Additionally, our results suggest that while individuals with strong partisan identities tend to interpret events through their ideological lens, those without stable party preferences may be more open to reassessing their views in response to major political events. Given the high fluidity of party preferences in Japanese politics, critical events have the potential to reshape attitudes among specific groups, leading to substantial realignments within the existing partisan landscape.

Although our analyses provide important insights, they are not without limitations. A significant consideration is the timing of the observed effects. On the one hand, panel data inherently captures information only at specific time points, making it difficult to completely rule out the possibility that factors other than the assassination event may have influenced attitudes toward Abe. On the other hand, while our results suggest that the impact of partisan orientation might be less pronounced in the immediate aftermath of an event, short-term attitude shifts do not necessarily translate into long-term changes (Epifanio et al. 2023). Indeed, previous research indicates that the effects of political violence tend to be ephemeral, as long-standing attitudes and beliefs maintain their relevance and often regain their pre-event roles, a phenomenon referred to as “bounce-back” (Vertzberger 1997; Raviv et al. 2000). Furthermore, although our analysis suggests that the empathy effect appeared consistent across partisan groups in the short term, its influence on attitude change may become increasingly conditional on political alignment over time. Therefore, future research should endeavor to examine the longevity and delayed shifts in attitudes as well as the interplay between the bounce-back effect and individual characteristics such as personality and partisanship. Such investigations will improve our understanding of the enduring impact of political violence on public opinion and contribute to a more nuanced conceptualization of these dynamics.
