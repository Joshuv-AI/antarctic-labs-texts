Central Adiposity and Subclinical Coronary Atherosclerosis in Population-Based Studies
Authors: Mario Cesar Torres-Chávez, Neftali Eduardo Antonio-Villa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Since the first large-scale population studies exploring the association between adiposity and cardiovascular disease (CVD), along with recent Mendelian randomization meta-analyses supporting its causal role, 1 question remains: what is the simplest and most practical way to measure the cardiometabolic risk caused by adiposity?1,2 For decades, this debate has focused on whether body mass index (BMI) can adequately capture the obesity-related risk in its association with incident CVD outcomes. This discussion has led to numerous debates, particularly around concepts such as the “obesity paradox,” “U-shaped” associations, and “metabolically healthy obesity”.3,4 Although BMI has long been considered a widely used metric for assessing obesity-related CVD risk, recent efforts to find a more nuanced measurement have shifted toward a specific body fat compartment: central adiposity, especially visceral fat. As a result, measures such as waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), waist-to-height ratio, and more recent waist-based metrics such as the body roundness index and the body shape index have been studied as better predictors of incident CVD outcomes.5 Nowadays, it is recognized that central obesity plays a major role in the cardiometabolic risk linked to obesity. However, there is still a specific group that remains understudied, which is those individuals who are classified as normal weight by BMI but may have an unfavorable cardiometabolic profile. Among these individuals, the use of CVD proxies, such as subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, offers a practical and accessible approach to studying the impact of central obesity. In this context, a critical question arises: could central adiposity metrics provide additional clinical value in identifying proxies for clinical outcomes, such as subclinical coronary atherosclerosis?

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Khanna et al6 conducted a meta-analysis that aimed to quantify and compare 3 adiposity markers: BMI, WC, and WHR, among individuals with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis (defined as a coronary artery calcification [CAC] >0) recruited in cross-sectional studies and among those who experienced an increase in CAC (defined as CAC progression) in prospective cohorts. In addition, some relevant secondary outcomes that the authors included were differences in visceral fat area (VFA) and subcutaneous fat area among individuals with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, the strength of the association among these anthropometric markers against their evaluated outcomes, their correlation with visceral adiposity measured by image-based methods, stratification by key covariates, and the description of sex-specific differences in anthropometric central adiposity profiles. To conduct their study, the authors collected data from 49 published studies from the early 2000s to 2025, compiling the results from 68,629 participants across 10 countries. The authors provide interesting and insightful results. First, they found in their pooled random-effects model that, on average, those with subclinical atherosclerosis had higher central adiposity metrics, specifically WC, VFA, and WHR, with the first one being the strongest metric correlated with this outcome; however, BMI and subcutaneous fat area metrics did not differ between the 2 groups. Second, among the included cohort studies, the authors found that WC was a stronger predictor of CAC progression compared with BMI alone. Third, WC showed a stronger correlation with VFA than BMI. Fourth, in their stratified analyses, they found that among those with hypertension and increased age, the effect sizes for their 3 adiposity markers were lower. Finally, they reported exploratory sex-specific differences, reporting that women with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis had higher central adiposity metrics despite having lower absolute fat mass than men. Overall, the authors concluded that WC and WHR were considerably higher and strongly correlated with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, supporting the view that these markers may help to improve cardiovascular risk. However, despite these interesting results, the authors acknowledge several limitations, including the use of unadjusted standardized mean differences, which may yield confounded estimates; significant differences in population characteristics of the included studies, CAC definitions, anthropometric thresholds, and measurement techniques for WC; and the absence of sex-stratified models. The authors also recognize that the evaluated central adiposity metrics remain as proxies to quantify visceral fat. As demonstrated by Oyama et al,7 WC often fails to correlate with the actual visceral-to-subcutaneous fat ratio, meaning that even WC can be "blind" to the most metabolically active fat depots that drive coronary inflammation.

The study by Khanna et al6 confirms the limitations of using BMI as a marker of adiposity for assessing cardiometabolic risk associated with visceral fat accumulation. Their findings support the view that we should shift toward waist-based metrics, which are better at identifying adverse CVD outcomes. In a recent position statement endorsed by the International Atherosclerosis Society and the International Chair on Cardiometabolic Risk Working Group, visceral fat is recognized as a key contributor to an adverse cardiometabolic profile and emphasizes the need for simple, accessible, and low-cost metrics to estimate this fat depot.8 However, some gaps still exist in achieving this objective, and more studies are needed to find the best way to measure the cardiometabolic risk linked to visceral fat. Although WC and WHR are simple and accessible methods, using WHR does not necessarily improve visceral fat assessment beyond WC alone. Nevertheless, WC also has important limitations, such as differences in variability across measurement sites, as discussed by Khanna et al.6 An interesting finding from this paper is that WC measurements at the umbilical level showed the strongest association with subclinical coronary calcification, suggesting a preferred measurement location. Another gap is that, although recent waist-based metrics like body shape index and body roundness index have emerged as novel, more nuanced predictors of incident CVD associated with central adiposity, their clinical interpretation is not always straightforward, as they do not directly measure visceral fat. In this context, some specific visceral fat surrogates—such as the visceral adipose index, the estimated visceral adipose tissue area index, and the metabolic score for visceral fat—have been proposed to indirectly estimate visceral adiposity.9 However, their integration into clinical practice and their potential for monitoring changes in visceral fat remain underexplored. In addition, accessible imaging methods such as electrical bioimpedance or ultrasound can provide direct estimates, but they still require specialized equipment and personnel, exhibit moderate variability depending on the algorithm used for estimation, and may introduce operator-dependent errors. Therefore, the ongoing debate now centers on whether we should rely solely on WC or shift to more nuanced metrics, such as waist-derived metrics, visceral fat surrogates, or accessible imaging techniques, to improve the accuracy of assessing cardiometabolic risk driven by visceral fat. This challenge is exacerbated by ethnic differences in fat distribution across populations, sex-specific differences in body composition, potential variations in risk driven by visceral fat across the lifespan, and the logistical and economic challenges of conducting large-scale studies to assess these questions in low- and middle-income countries. All of these gaps could lead to future studies that help improve how we study the cardiometabolic risk associated with central adiposity.

The paper by Khanna et al6 makes a significant contribution to clinicians and public health stakeholders by emphasizing the importance of developing and implementing central adiposity metrics to accelerate the shift away from BMI as the sole measure of obesity in cardiovascular risk screening, including subclinical coronary atherosclerosis. Overall, the future of cardiometabolic research should focus not only on measuring central adiposity but also on developing targeted interventions at both individual and population levels that address visceral fat as the core of cardiometabolic risk, with the goal of reducing cardiovascular outcomes and enhancing overall health.
