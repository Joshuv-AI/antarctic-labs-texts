Vancomycin and phage COP-80B combination therapy for Staphylococcus epidermidis periprosthetic joint infections: a preclinical mouse study
Authors: Vida Štilec, Monika Marušić, Helena Motaln, Klara Gregorič, Urška Draksler, Maša Čater, Jani Izlakar, Nika Janež, Rihard Trebše, Simon Horvat, Matjaž Peterka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing number of joint replacement surgeries has led to an increase in periprosthetic joint infections (PJI). Chronic PJI caused by Staphylococcus epidermidis is very common and challenging to treat, prompting interest in bacteriophage therapy. Preclinical studies are essential for optimizing phage administration routes and dosages. Here, phage replication and bacterial growth inhibition using phage and vancomycin were first assessed in vitro. Then, chronic PJI was induced in mice using biofilm-coated titanium implants inoculated with S. epidermidis COB-SE3 to evaluate the efficacy of phage COP-80B and vancomycin therapy. Mice received one intra-articular and two intraperitoneal phage doses over three days, along with vancomycin administered twice daily for five days. Four weeks after implantation, we quantified and compared bacterial loads in periarticular tissues and implants across four groups: untreated controls, phage monotherapy, vancomycin monotherapy, and phage-vancomycin combination. Infection persistence was lower than expected, limiting the ability to detect treatment effects and assess therapeutic efficacy in this low-virulence model. Intra-articular administration of 1010 PFU of COP-80B did not alter plasma alanine aminotransferase levels, indicating no hepatotoxicity. Bacterial isolates remained susceptible to both phage and vancomycin, while phage-neutralizing antibodies developed during treatment. Overall, this study evaluates phage-vancomycin therapy while also contributing a murine model that advances methodological development for chronic S. epidermidis PJI research, with additional refinement of infection kinetics needed to reliably assess therapeutic efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10096-026-05561-x.Keywords: Phage therapy, Periprosthetic joint infection, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Mouse, Infection models, Vancomycin

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infections (PJI) are rare but serious complications following up to 3% of total joint arthroplasties, imposing a €350 million reimbursement burden on European healthcare systems in 2019 [1, 2]. Coagulase-negative staphylococci predominate as causative pathogens, accounting for approximately 37% of PJI cases, with Staphylococcus epidermidis – a common skin commensal – the most frequent species [3]. This pathogen readily forms biofilms on implanted biomaterials, thereby evading host immunity and resisting mechanical and chemical eradication, often leading to chronic infections [4].

Current PJI management relies on surgical debridement or implant exchange combined with prolonged systemic antibiotic therapy [5]. Even so, recurrence rates can be close to 30% [6, 7]. Phages, which are bacterial viruses that self-replicate at the infection site and possess biofilm-degrading structures, offer a promising alternative or adjunct therapy [8]. Phage therapy has been successfully employed on multiple occasions to treat PJI, albeit mostly as compassionate use when surgical interventions were unfeasible or the bacterial pathogen exhibited multidrug resistance [9–13]. Nevertheless, systematic translational studies and reproducible clinical trials are lacking, leaving key questions unanswered regarding optimal dosing, administration routes, combination with antibiotic therapy, and safety profiles [14, 15]. To date, no serious adverse events have been reported following local or intravenous phage therapy for PJI, despite occasional minor effects such as transaminitis [16, 17].

Translational studies require representative animal models of PJI. Existing models include those in sheep [18], rabbits [19, 20], rats [21, 22] and mice [23, 24], with Staphylococcus aureus as the predominant pathogen [25, 26]. Rodent models are the most prevalent due to their cost-effectiveness, which supports larger cohorts for statistically robust testing of novel therapies, such as phage-antibiotic combinations.

Phages frequently synergize with antibiotics in planktonic cultures, biofilms, and in vivo settings [27–29]. A phage cocktail combined with vancomycin proved superior in treating S. aureus PJI in a rat model [30], while phage K and vancomycin exhibited synergistic action against S. aureus biofilms in vitro and in a murine PJI model [31]. Data for S. epidermidis are limited to a single in vitro study, in which the combination of phage AICAT and vancomycin acted additively against biofilms under multiple conditions [32]. Translating in vitro synergy to in vivo outcomes requires further studies and likely varies by phage-antibiotic pairs. Notably, combining antibiotics with phages may also mitigate bacterial resistance development which is particularly important for managing chronic infections such as PJI [27].

No studies have compared phages, vancomycin, and their combination in a murine model of chronic S. epidermidis PJI, nor have they reported on safety profiling, including assessments of hepatotoxicity, anti-phage immunity, and resistance emergence. To fill this gap, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of previously characterized S. epidermidis phage COP-80B and vancomycin therapy both in vitro and in a newly established murine model of chronic S. epidermidis PJI [33, 34]. We compared the reductions in bacterial burden in vivo following phage and vancomycin monotherapies and their combination, while monitoring liver enzyme elevation, phage-neutralizing antibody development, and the emergence of phage or antibiotic resistance. Importantly, this study not only evaluates phage-vancomycin efficacy, but also addresses the methodological challenge of establishing a chronic S. epidermidis PJI model with sufficient persistence and dynamic range for reliable testing.

Materials and methods

Microorganism and antimicrobial agents

A clinical S. epidermidis strain COB-SE3, isolated from a patient with PJI (Valdoltra Orthopaedic Hospital, Slovenia), was used in this study. The strain has been previously characterized, and its genome sequenced (GenBank accession number: JARGEB000000000) [34]. The strain belongs to sequence type (ST) 278, is a good biofilm producer, and is resistant to erythromycin and tetracycline [34]. COB-SE3 is susceptible to vancomycin as was determined using the microbroth dilution method (minimal inhibitory concentration, MIC, was 2 µg/ml) [35]. The strain was grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB, Merck, Germany) or on tryptic soy agar (Biolife, Italy) at 37 °C.

Previously characterized lytic phage COP-80B, belonging to the Sepunavirus genus, was used in this study [34]. The phage genome sequence is available in GenBank (accession number: OQ448194). Phage was propagated using S. epidermidis strain COB-Sec2, as described previously [33]. Phage lysates were used for in vitro experiments, whereas for the in vivo study, we used a purified phage product with confirmed sterility. Phage lysate was purified via a two-step chromatography process and diafiltration, as described previously [33].

Vancomycin powder (Mylan, USA) was dissolved in sterile water and administered subcutaneously at a dose of 110 mg/kg.

Phage enumeration

Phage quantification was performed either via double agar overlay plaque assay or drop plaque assay on TSB plates with 1.2% (w/v) agar and overlay agar of 0.4% (w/v) as described previously [36, 37]. Briefly, for a double agar overlay plaque assay, 100 µl serial dilutions of the phage lysate in buffer SM (50 mM Tris-Cl, 100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4, 0.01% (w/v) gelatin, pH 7.5) were mixed with 100 µl of the bacterial strain and added to TSB overlay agar, which was poured onto TSB agar plates. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, plaques were counted, and the phage titer was calculated and reported as PFU/ml. Plates yielding 20–200 plaques were deemed within the quantifiable range. For the drop plaque assay, phage serial dilution was prepared in buffer SM, and 10 µl of each phage dilution was spotted onto the overlay agar of the bacterial strain. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, plaques were counted, and the phage titer was calculated and reported as PFU/ml. Drops yielding 10–45 plaques were deemed within the quantifiable range.

Adsorption and one-step growth curve

Adsorption rate constant and one-step growth curve of phage COP-80B and strain COB-SE3 were determined in 1 ml and 25 ml TSB, respectively, supplemented with 10 mM MgSO4 and 1 mM CaCl2 at 37 °C, as described elsewhere with modifications [38].

To determine the adsorption rate constant, a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 was used. Sampling was performed at defined time points (1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, and 25 min) following the start of incubation and samples were titrated to determine the number of non-adsorbed (free) phages at each timepoint. The adsorption rate constant (k) was calculated from the slope of logarithmically transformed relative free phage concentration data over time and concentration of bacteria (B) using the formula k = -slope/B. The adsorption rate constant was determined from two independent experiments.

For burst size and latent period determination, phages were added at an MOI of 0.01 to an exponentially growing bacterial culture and allowed to adsorb for 10 min. Free phages were removed by centrifugation and cell pellets were resuspended in the same volume of medium and transferred to 25 ml medium. Samples were collected at 10 min intervals for 120 min, added to tubes containing buffer SM with chloroform and buffer SM only and phage titer was determined using drop plaque assay. Phage titers were plotted against time, with the latent period determined visually as the interval from adsorption to the first rise in concentration in chloroform-free samples. Burst size was calculated as the difference between the average baseline titer from early plateau points and the average titer from late plateau points, divided by the estimated number of infected cells. Two independent experiments were performed in parallel.

Phage and antibiotic activity against planktonic bacteria

The growth curves of S. epidermidis in the presence of phage COP-80B and/or vancomycin was monitored by measuring optical density (OD600), as described previously [34]. Briefly, 5 × 105 colony-forming units (CFUs) of S. epidermidis subculture were inoculated into each well of a microtiter plate that contained the appropriate concentrations of phage COP-80B (range: MOI 1–100; i.e., 5 × 105, 5 × 106, and 5 × 107 PFU) and vancomycin (range: 2–8 µg/ml) in TSB medium. The OD600 was measured every 30 min at 37 °C for 72 h with continuous shaking at 220 rpm in an Infinite 2000 Pro microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). The experiment was performed in three biological replicates with four technical replicates per biological replicate. To generate a synogram of relative area under the curve (AUC) reduction, absorbance readings from biological replicates were averaged and normalized with the negative control (TSB only). Then, the area under the curve for 72 h was calculated using trapezoidal method and the relative reduction was calculated as: Relative AUC reduction (%) = [(AUCgrowth control – AUCtreatment)/AUCgrowth control] × 100 and plotted on a synogram. To evaluate resistance development, the time to bacterial regrowth was determined for each well. The time to bacterial regrowth was defined as the first timepoint, when the OD600 reading in the well exceeded 0.25. Values were averaged across conditions and plotted on a synogram.

Animals

All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with Directive 2010/63/EU, the Slovenian Animal Protection Act, and approved by the Administration of the Republic of Slovenia for Food Safety, Veterinary and Plant Protection of the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Foods, Republic of Slovenia (Permit Number U34401-22/2022/5).

Male C57BL/6OlaHsd mice (8–11 weeks old) were purchased from Envigo, Italy. The animals were housed under a 12 h dark-light cycle with free access to water and maintenance chow (Standard Diet 4RF21, gamma irradiation sterilized, Mucedola, Italy) in housing units (IVC system, Techniplast, Italy; up to 4 mice per cage) containing environmental enrichment (nesting materials and mouse tunnels). Animals were acclimatized for a minimum of 2 weeks to daily handling before the start of the study.

Implants

The tibial implants were manufactured by three-dimensional metal printing (Ekliptik, Ljubljana, Slovenia) and are chemically composed of Ti6Al4V ELI alloy, as described previously [39]. Dimensions of the implant oval head are 2.00 mm for the major axis and 1.51 mm for the minor axis with a 0.20-mm thickness. The implant stem measures 1.86 mm in length and 0.60 mm in diameter. The stem has a rough surface, which allows for better osseointegration.

Inoculum preparation

Each animal was inoculated with a bacterial suspension and an implant covered with 24-old biofilm. Bacterial suspension for inoculation was prepared on the day of the surgery by culturing COB-SE3 in liquid TSB until an OD600 reached 0.55, corresponding to 5 × 108 CFU/ml. The culture was centrifuged at 7000 x g for 7 min at 4 °C and washed 3 times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Bacterial cells were concentrated by centrifugation and resuspended in PBS to achieve a final concentration of 4.2 ± 0.3 × 109 CFU/ml. The suspension was mixed with 1% (w/v) solution of molten UltraPure™ Low Melting Point Agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in a 1:1 ratio and stored at 37 °C until inoculation during surgery. Each animal received approximately 4.2 ± 0.3 × 106 CFU.

Biofilms on implants were prepared by 24-hour cultivation of sterilized implants in a culture of S. epidermidis COB-SE3 in 0.2 ml TSB at 37 °C with shaking (50 rpm) in a microtiter plate. Implants were rinsed 2 times with sterile PBS before surgical implantation. The mean bacterial density on each implant at the time of surgery was (3.7 ± 0.7) × 105 CFU. Actual implant surface area is 11.95 ± 0.17 mm2 as calculated from true to projected area determined with optical profilometry. Cell density on biofilm-coated implant is therefore (3.1 ± 0.6) × 104 CFU/mm2.

Surgical technique

Unilateral proximal tibial implant insertion surgery was performed on animals under general anesthesia induced with intraperitoneally administered anesthetic mixture consisting of ketamine (140 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg) intraperitoneally. The right leg was shaved and disinfected with Providone-iodine solution. Using sterile technique, a ~ 5 mm medial parapatellar incision was made on the right leg and the tibial plateau was exposed as described previously [39]. Using a 0.6 mm needle, a hole in the medullary canal was made and a pre-seeded implant was press-fit into the hole. Then, 2 µl of bacterial suspension in agarose was pipetted on top of the inserted implant before wound suturing. Mice from uninfected groups received a sterile implant and PBS buffer with agarose. Post-operative analgesic (Buprenorfin, 0.05–0.1 mg/kg) was available and would have been administered if needed according to individual pain scores; however, no animals required analgesic treatment. Clinical signs (body weight, vital signs, weight-bearing activity, wound healing) were monitored every day for 5 days post-surgery.

Treatment study

The study compared treatment outcomes in mice inoculated with S. epidermidis COB-SE3. The primary outcome was the infection rate and quantitative measurement of the bacterial burden (CFU). The study design is displayed in Fig. 1. Animals were randomly allocated to three control groups (n = 5 per group) and four treatment groups (n = 10 per group), and all underwent tibial implant insertion surgery on day 0. Treatment groups included untreated control (group 4), where animals received no treatment agents; phage-treated group (group 5), where animals received phages only; vancomycin-treated group (group 6) and group receiving phage and vancomycin combination treatment (group 7). Establishment and the extent of the infection at the start of treatment were determined with microbiological analyses of the implant and periarticular samples of animals from group 1, which were euthanized at day 7 post-surgery. The sterility of surgical procedures was confirmed by microbiological analyses of samples from uninfected animals in group 2.

Study design and group allocations. In Group 1, we determined the extent of the infection at day 7, when treatment started in the treatment groups. Group 2 was uninfected and served as quality control of the PJI model. In Group 3, uninfected mice received phage intra-articularly on day 7 and intravenously on days 8 and 9. Group 4 was infected and served as untreated control. In Group 5, mice received phage intra-articularly on day 7 and intravenously on days 8 and 9. In Group 6, mice received vancomycin subcutaneously twice daily for 5 days, starting on day 7. In Group 7, mice were treated with both phage and vancomycin. During the wash-out period (27 days for phage-only groups or 29 days for vancomycin groups), no treatment was given to the animals. On day 8, blood was drawn for the analysis of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels. On day 28, X-ray images were taken, bacterial numbers (CFU), cell blood counts (CBC), phage neutralization by antibodies in the plasma and resistance development against phage and vancomycin were determined. D – day

Phages were administered using two routes: on day 7, mice received 1010 PFU intra-articularly and on days 8 to 9, 2 × 109 PFU intraperitoneally. The phage dosing regimen was determined based on published case reports from one of the phage therapy centers, where a combination of intra-articular (intraoperative) and systemic (after surgery) administration was used [10, 17, 40]. Vancomycin was administered subcutaneously every 12 h from day 7 to day 11 at a dose of 110 mg/kg, as this dose was previously shown to approximate the exposure profile for human dosing of 1 g every 12 h [41].

Intra-articular application was performed on day 7 on animals from groups 3, 5 and 7 under general anesthesia, which was induced as described above. Approximately 5 mm skin incision was made adjacent to, but not directly over, the previous surgical incision on the right knee to allow for more precise localization of the knee capsule and 10 µl of phage preparation was then applied with a 27G needle and a 10 µl syringe (Hamilton, USA) into the intra-articular space. For intraperitoneal administration on days 8 and 9, 100 µl of the phage solution was injected into the lower right quadrant of the abdomen using a 27G needle. Mice were gently restrained, and injections followed standard aseptic techniques. Post-injection, animals were monitored for any signs of distress.

Sampling, sample processing, bacterial quantification and identification

Plasma samples were collected from mice tails (tail snip) on day 8 post-surgery to measure alanine aminotransferase levels (ALT, ab282882, Abcam), in order to assess whether phage administration induced elevations in this liver enzyme, as transient transaminitis following phage administration has been reported in multiple case reports [16].

Animals were euthanized on day 28 post-surgery by cervical dislocation, decapitation, and exsanguination into a 50 ml Falcon tube with potassium EDTA. An aliquot of 75 µl of blood was collected separately into potassium EDTA-containing tubes (Sarstedt, Germany) for hematological analysis. Complete blood counts (CBC) were measured with Vetscan HM5 Hematology Analyzer (Zoetis, USA) to obtain white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelet count (PLT), volumetric ratio of platelets (PCT), mean platelet volume (MPV), neutrophil (NEU), lymphocyte (LYM), and monocyte (MON). The remaining blood was used for plasma preparation.

After euthanasia, implants were aseptically removed from the tibiae using sterile forceps. The force required to extract the implant from the tibial canal was rated using an in-house subjective scale from 1 to 3, with 1 representing the easiest harvest and 3 representing the harvest needing additional force. Implants were placed in vials containing 0.5 ml PBS and were subjected to sonication (3 cycles of 5 min with 30-second pauses). Sonicates were serially diluted in TSB medium, plated onto TSB agar and CFUs were counted after 24-hour incubation at 37 °C. The bone with surrounding soft tissue (periarticular tissue) of the right limb was collected aseptically. The collected periarticular tissue was weighed and homogenized in 1 ml PBS with Precellys Minilys Tissue homogenizer (Bertin, France). Homogenization was performed with MK28 hard tissue grinding kits (2 ml) at 5000 rpm for 5 × 60 s with 60 s pauses on ice between cycles. Homogenates were serially diluted in TSB medium, plated onto TSB agar and CFUs were calculated after 24-hour incubation at 37 °C.

The limit of quantification (LOQ) of the CFU method was 250 CFU/ml as determined from quantification of serial two-fold COB-SE3 culture dilutions. The limit of detection (LOD) was 20 CFU/ml (i.e., 1 colony per plate). LOQ for periarticular tissue was calculated as CFU/g tissue from the LOQ of the method, average tissue sample mass (g) and homogenate volume (1 ml). LOQ for the implant sonicate was calculated from the LOQ of the method and sonicate volume (0.5 ml). Bacterial quantification was performed in triplicates. At least 3 colonies per culture positive sample were stored in 50% (v/v) glycerol at −80 °C for further analyses.

To confirm the species identity of isolated bacteria, we amplified 16S rDNA of a random subset using primers fD1 (5’-AGA GTT TGA TCC TGG CTC AG-3’) and RU1406 (5’-ACG GGC GGT GTG TRC-3’) as described previously [42, 43]. Amplicon sequences of 1542 bp length were compared with GenBank reference sequences using blastn to determine species identity and similarity score of > 99% was used to classify the sampled bacteria to the reference species.

Phage susceptibility testing

Bacterial colonies isolated from mice were revitalized on TSB agar. A single colony from each isolate was inoculated into 5 ml TSB and grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm). Overnight cultures were diluted 1:200 in TSB to obtain an initial inoculum of 8 × 105 CFU/ml. For growth curve assays, 8 × 105 CFU was added to the wells of a 96-well microtiter plate. Phage COP-80B (8 × 105 PFU) was added to the cultures, yielding a MOI of 1. The OD600 was measured every 30 min at 37 °C for 48 h with continuous shaking in a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Biotek, USA). Growth curves of mouse isolates were compared to that of a wild-type COB-SE3 under identical conditions.

Vancomycin susceptibility testing

Minimum inhibitory concentrations to vancomycin of bacterial isolates from mice were assessed using the microbroth dilution method and interpreted using the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines [35].

Phage neutralization assay

To determine if phage-neutralizing antibodies developed during the treatment, phage neutralization by plasma was performed using the modified Adams protocol [44]. Plasma samples were diluted 1:100 in PBS. Diluted plasma (200 µl) was mixed with 200 µl of purified phage COP-80B, diluted in PBS to 1 × 107 PFU/ml, and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, serial dilutions were prepared, and phage titration was performed using a drop plaque assay.

Data analysis

Group size for the in vivo treatment study was determined via a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software [45]. Preliminary studies established a baseline infection rate: 80% of untreated control mice remained infected (positive for bacteria via culture) at day 28 post-implantation in the PJI model. Assuming a reduction in the proportion of infected mice to 10% in the phage-treated group, with a two-tailed Fisher’s exact test, α = 0.05 and 80% power, the minimal required sample size was calculated to be 9 animals per group. To account for potential, unforeseeable losses during the experiment, one additional animal per group was included as a reserve, resulting in a final group size of n = 10. The sample size was therefore determined a priori based on the infection persistence observed in preliminary experiments performed under the same inoculation protocol. For the control groups, a smaller number of animals (n = 5) was used, as these groups were intended primarily to confirm infection establishment and procedural sterility rather than to serve as statistical comparators for therapeutic efficacy. This approach reduced animal use in accordance with the principles of the 3Rs.

The results were visualized using Python software packages seaborn and matplotlib [46, 47] or Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM) unless stated otherwise. Data were analyzed using Prism 9.0.0. Data normality was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. In case of parametric data, a one-way ANOVA with post hoc Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare differences between groups and in case of nonparametric data, the Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used. Because a substantial proportion of CFU measurements were below the assay LOD/LOQ and therefore left-censored, CFU counts were not analyzed as continuous variables. Samples were dichotomized as culture-positive or culture-negative, and the incidence of infected implants, peri-articular tissues, and animals was compared between treatment groups and the untreated control using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests. Differences with P < 0.01 were considered statistically significant.

To analyze the synergism between phage COP-80B and vancomycin in vitro, interaction plots and two-way ANOVA were employed to compare the AUCs of each tested combination. Interaction plots showed possible interaction between phage and antibiotic at a given concentration/MOI and two-way ANOVA was used to determine the statistical significance of visually determined interaction, as described previously [27, 48].

Results

We have previously isolated several S. epidermidis phages [34]. Phage COP-80B demonstrated efficacy against a range of clinical isolates in both liquid cultures and biofilms and remained infective in mouse periarticular tissue for several days following intra-articular administration, supporting its suitability for efficacy testing on a mouse model [33]. To further assess the efficacy of phage COP-80B against the clinical PJI isolate S. epidermidis COB-SE3, we characterized key phage growth parameters for this phage-host combination. The adsorption rate constant was measured at 7.57 × 10− 11 ± 2.97 × 10− 11 ml/min, indicating relatively slow and inefficient adsorption. The latent period lasted 45 min, with a burst size of 85.0 ± 4.6 PFU per infected cell (Fig. 2A).

Phage alone inhibited bacterial growth at all tested MOIs, whereas the phage-vancomycin combinations inhibited bacterial growth more effectively than phage alone. Vancomycin showed concentration-dependent activity, with little or no inhibition at lower concentrations. Cell density was measured by OD600 every 30 min over 72 h. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (N = 3). (C) Synogram depicting relative reduction in area under the curve (AUC) from data in (B), expressed as percentage reduction calculated by [(AUCgrowth control – AUCtreatment)/AUCgrowth control] × 100. Values represent the mean relative AUC reduction for each treatment condition. No synergy was observed; additive effects were seen for phage with 2 µg/ml vancomycin at all tested MOIs and for phage at MOI 100 with 4 or 6 µg/ml vancomycin. Our aim was to evaluate phage therapy combined with vancomycin, the standard-of-care antibiotic for methicillin-resistant staphylococcal PJI. First, to identify possible synergy between phage COP-80B and vancomycin, we conducted an optical density-based growth assay, testing four vancomycin and three phage concentrations over 72 h (Fig. 2). To compare the efficacy of each agent, we calculated the relative AUC reduction and the time to bacterial regrowth for each condition from data shown in Fig. 2 (Supplementary Table 1) and presented the results in synograms. The data indicates that phage COP-80B is effective at all three tested MOIs, with relative AUC reductions greater than 82%. Combinations of higher MOIs (10 and 100) and higher vancomycin concentrations (4–8 µg/ml) resulted in the greatest AUC reductions. Time to bacterial regrowth was assessed to evaluate the emergence of vancomycin- and/or phage-resistant populations and to identify antimicrobial doses that prevent regrowth. Consistent with the AUC reduction results, higher MOIs (10 and 100) and higher vancomycin concentrations (4–8 µg/ml) delayed bacterial regrowth. Notably, at MOI 100 combined with 6 µg/ml vancomycin, no bacterial regrowth was observed during the 72-hour testing period. The tested phage suppressed bacterial growth for ≥ 21 h across all tested phage-vancomycin combinations. We further investigated whether phage COP-80B and vancomycin demonstrate synergistic effects against strain COB-SE3 by comparing relative AUC reductions (Supplementary Fig. 1). All combinations of phage with 2 µg/ml vancomycin and phage at MOI 100 with 4 or 6 µg/ml vancomycin showed additive effects (i.e., the combined effect equaled the sum of individual effects, unlike synergy, where the combined effect would exceed this sum). In contrast, the remaining combinations were antagonistic (i.e., the combined effect was lower than expected from the sum of individual effects), indicating no synergy between phage and vancomycin at the tested doses.

In the in vivo study, mice were randomly allocated into seven groups (n = 55) as illustrated in Fig. 1. All mice survived the implant surgery on day 0. Later, on day 7, one mouse from group 5 (Phage) died due to the hypersensitivity to the administered anesthetic. Infection of the knee implant with S. epidermidis COB-SE3 was established at the time of surgical implantation using an implant coated with COB-SE3 biofilm, followed by application of bacterial suspension. Post-surgery, mice rapidly recovered, as evidenced by progressive weight gain (Supplementary Table 2). Postoperatively, mice initially displayed limping and reduced weight bearing on the operated limb, but all animals recovered to a full weight-bearing gait within one week. No CFUs were detected in samples from the uninfected control (group 2), confirming the absence of environmental contamination during the surgical procedures. Additionally, bacterial identity in periarticular tissues and implants at euthanasia was confirmed as S. epidermidis by sequencing of amplified 16 S rDNA (Supplementary Table 3). Radiographic analysis confirmed the presence and maintained positioning of the implant in all surviving animals (n = 54). Implants remained in comparable radiographic position across groups, with trabecular contact and no signs of severe loosening, peri-implant osteolysis, or reactive bone destruction. No differences were observed between groups (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 2).

The primary endpoint of the in vivo study was the infection rate across groups, to determine the therapeutic efficacy of phage treatment. We assessed the effects of phage therapy alone and in combination with vancomycin. The presented PJI model resulted in 100% infection of the periarticular tissue and 100% colonization of the implant at day 7 post-surgery (Fig. 4). By day 28, only 50% of untreated control mice remained infected (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 4), with bacterial loads near or below the quantification limit, indicating spontaneous clearance. Phage treatment started on day 7 with an intra-articular dose followed by two intraperitoneal doses on day 8 and 9, resulted in 56% infection-free mice at day 28, showing minimal improvement over controls. Vancomycin, administered subcutaneously twice daily for 5 days starting on day 7, showed no effect compared with the treatment control. Combined phage and vancomycin treatment yielded 90% infection-free mice on day 28, though not statistically significant compared to uninfected controls (P = 0.1409). The absolute difference in culture-positivity rates between untreated controls (50%) and the combination group (10%) was 40%; however, with n = 10 per group, the 95% confidence interval around this difference was wide, reflecting limited precision under lower-than-expected control persistence. No S. epidermidis was detected on implants from phage-treated groups. In contrast, implants from vancomycin- and control-treated groups tested positive for S. epidermidis, with CFU counts at or below the quantification limit. We have detected S. epidermidis in periarticular tissues of all treatment groups, with four positive samples in control and phage-treated groups, three in vancomycin-treated group and one in the combination group (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables 4, 5 and 6).

During dissection on day 28, no gross morphological differences were observed in knee tissues across groups. However, during implant retrieval, variability in removal difficulty was observed, suggesting differences in osseointegration levels. Using an in-house subjective scale, implants from uninfected controls were significantly harder to harvest (P ≤ 0.01) compared to those from untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. 3), although limited blinding may have introduced bias. No significant differences were found between the untreated control and the treated groups.

To assess potential adverse effects of phage therapy on the liver, plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were measured in all mice on day 8, one day after local phage administration. No significant differences were detected between groups (Fig. 5). Furthermore, hematological parameters (WBC, RBC, HGB, HCT, MCV, MCH, MCHC, PLT, PCT, MPV, NEU, LYM, MON) assessed on day 28 showed no significant differences among uninfected controls, phage application controls and treatment groups (Supplementary Table 7).

(A) The level of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was determined in plasma, collected at day 8, one day after the intra-articular phage dose. The data is plotted on boxplot with the box extending from the first quartile (Q1) to the third quartile (Q3) of the data, with a line at the median. The significant differences between groups are marked with two (P < 0.01) or three (P < 0.001) asterisks. Plasma from all phage-treated animals showed neutralizing activity against phage

To evaluate the development of phage-induced antibodies, we measured the percentage of phage particles neutralized by plasma collected at euthanasia from all groups. A Kruskal-Wallis test indicated a significant overall difference among the six groups (H = 38.8, P < 0.0001). Dunn’s post hoc test indicated that plasma from phage-treated groups showed significantly greater phage neutralization than plasma from groups that did not receive phage, with adjusted P values ranging from 0.0006 to 0.0052. No significant difference was observed between the uninfected phage-treated group and phage-treated groups concurrently infected with S. epidermidis (Fig. 5). The treatment protocol, comprising one intra-articular and two intraperitoneal phage administrations at 24-hour intervals, was sufficient to induce the formation of neutralizing antibodies.

To assess the emergence of phage-resistant mutants, we randomly selected at least 6 colonies per treatment group and evaluated their sensitivity to phage COP-80B using growth curve analysis. Across 5 phage-treated mice where S. epidermidis was isolated on day 28, no phage-resistant colonies were detected, indicating that the treatment protocol employed in this study did not promote phage resistance development (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 8). No vancomycin-resistant colonies were identified (Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

Multiple reports document successful treatment of PJI with phage therapy, yet optimal dosing and efficacy remain undetermined [12, 15, 49]. Animal models play a pivotal role in refining treatment protocols, though reports on phage therapy models for PJI remain scarce [30, 31, 50]. Here, we evaluated the combination of phage COP-80B and vancomycin to treat S. epidermidis PJI in a novel murine model. Phage COP-80B, a strictly lytic phage with therapeutic potential against clinical strains and their biofilms [34], has a favorable pharmacokinetic profile following intra-articular administration, making it a promising PJI treatment candidate [33]. To elucidate infection dynamics for the selected phage-bacteria pair, we quantified the phage adsorption constant and burst size. These parameters may predict in vivo efficacy, as phages with faster adsorption rates, shorter latent periods, and larger burst sizes generate more progeny at the infection site, thereby promoting treatment success [51]. Despite its slow adsorption rate, phage COP-80B demonstrated an extended latent period and larger burst size compared to most reported S. epidermidis phages [52–55].

Growth kinetics assays are valuable for assessing phage susceptibility of clinical strains, aiding phage selection and optimization of phage-antibiotic combinations in clinical settings [56, 57]. Here, an optical density-based assay was employed. To find the most potent antimicrobial conditions for phage COP-80B and vancomycin, a simplified checkerboard assay with three phage concentrations and four vancomycin concentrations was conducted [27]. We extended the assay to 72 h, exceeding standard protocols, to evaluate resistance development; although this can impact method reliability [57]. Treatment efficacy was assessed using relative AUC reduction. Phage COP-80B showed only an additive effect with vancomycin at low concentrations (1xMIC), consistent with previously reported in vitro interactions for staphylococcal phages and further demonstrating that interaction outcomes are concentration-dependent [31, 32, 58]. Time to bacterial regrowth analysis (delay time) [59] revealed phage COP-80B’s capacity to suppress bacterial growth for ≥ 21 h across all tested MOI-vancomycin combinations, comparable to the prolonged inhibition (> 30 h) of staphylococcal phage SaWIQ0488ø1 against a clinical strain [60]. Our in vitro assessment of phage-antibiotic synergy would benefit from endpoint CFU counts, as the optical density methods cannot distinguish live from dead bacteria.

The murine PJI model was established via implantation of biofilm-coated implants and intraoperative injection of bacterial suspension. By day 7 post-infection, all mice were infected; however, infection was maintained in only 50% of untreated control animals on day 28, suggesting substantial spontaneous clearance. This is consistent with other reports indicating that the low virulence of S. epidermidis challenges reliable infection model development [61–65]. The wide confidence intervals observed for binary culture-positivity endpoints further illustrate the limited precision imposed by spontaneous infection clearance in this model. The study was powered a priori based on preliminary experiments indicating 80% culture positivity in untreated controls at day 28, but control persistence was substantially lower (50%) in the present experiment, reducing statistical power. The high rate of spontaneous clearance reduced the dynamic range for detecting treatment effects; while the surgical and microbiological procedures were reproducible and contamination-free, no treatment significantly reduced infection rates. Failure to detect significance in this study cannot be interpreted as evidence of treatment ineffectiveness. Notably, the phage-vancomycin combination resulted in only one culture-positive tissue sample, suggesting potential efficacy that might be proven with larger animal cohorts. Future studies aiming to detect modest treatment effects or robustly evaluate therapeutic efficacy will require either larger cohorts and/or endpoints with greater dynamic range (e.g., earlier timepoints, higher-persistence strains, or quantitative readouts with appropriate handling of values below LOQ). To improve model reliability, strategies to increase infection persistence could include testing alternative clinical strains (as strain selection influences bacterial persistence in infected tissues, though experimental assessment of strain suitability is essential since biofilm-forming ability alone does not ensure reliable infections [62]) and adjusting inoculum or biofilm parameters. Shortening the endpoint may also be considered, although this may reduce fidelity to chronic PJI [61]. In a complementary “stress-test” setting, transient immunomodulation or the use of immunocompromised mice could reduce spontaneous clearance. Importantly, such approaches would need to be interpreted cautiously with respect to clinical relevance, as host immune status can influence both infection kinetics and responses to phage and antibiotic therapy.

Compassionate use cases have reported transient elevations in liver enzymes following phage therapy [10, 17, 60, 66, 67]; however, to our knowledge, no data exists from animal models. Here, plasma alanine aminotransferase levels remained unchanged one day post-intra-articular application. However, interpretation of these findings is limited because only ALT, and not additional markers of hepatic function, was evaluated, and assessment was performed at only a single time point. Further studies with varied phage doses, administration routes, phage species and formulations are required to elucidate the causes of transaminitis observed in humans [16].

Phage-neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) commonly emerge during phage therapy, but their impact on treatment outcome remains unclear. Evidence from clinical reports and case series has not shown a consistent association so far, and favorable outcomes have been reported even when NAbs are present [68–73]. In clinical practice, monitoring NAb development during prolonged therapy is therefore recommended, and phage cocktails can be modified to exclude neutralized phages [14]. Findings from murine studies are similarly mixed, with reports of both preserved and reduced efficacy in the presence of NAbs [74, 75]. Here we show that one intra-articular followed by two intravenous doses induced neutralizing activity irrespective of infection status. Whether this response arises following intra-articular administration alone remains to be verified. Further work should clarify how phage immunogenicity and dosing strategies relate to treatment outcome.

Phage resistance is a well-documented phenomenon in both experimental and clinical settings, with reported frequencies reaching up to 80% in intestinal models and around 50% in sepsis models [76]. In one clinical study, resistance rates varied by pathogen, ranging from ~ 17% in S. aureus up to 86% in Escherichia coli [77]. Importantly, combining phages with antibiotics has been shown to reduce the emergence of resistant variants compared to monotherapy. This is likely due to evolutionary trade-offs, whereby phage resistance can restore antibiotic susceptibility [68, 78]. In this study, no phage- or vancomycin-resistant isolates from day 28 were detected; however, potential mutants may have reverted to wild-type between treatment cessation (day 11) and day 28, when the selection pressure was absent [79]. The extended washout period applied here was intended to reflect chronic, relapsing S. epidermidis infections, where long-term dynamics, including phenotypic adaptation and potential reversion, are clinically relevant [80].

To further advance preclinical studies of phage therapy, several improvements could additionally be implemented. First, PJI treatment typically involves surgical interventions [5], which we omitted for simplicity, despite reports demonstrating feasible surgical procedures in murine models [26]. Second, we examined only one phage-antibiotic combination, whereas clinical protocols would likely employ phage cocktails combined with an antibiotic. To optimize the treatment protocol, future studies should test multiple phage doses and administration routes. Finally, although murine models are practical, using a larger animal model, e.g. rabbit, could reduce sampling errors and better approximate clinical conditions.

In summary, we established a murine model of S. epidermidis PJI where we evaluated vancomycin-phage COP-80B combination therapy. While the infection kinetics limited the dynamic range for detecting treatment effects, the model was reproducible and analytically robust. No adverse events emerged and no resistant bacteria were detected under the sampling conditions used; however, phage-neutralizing antibodies emerged. This model provides a useful platform for studying the safety, immune response, and resistance dynamics of candidate phage therapies in a clinically relevant setting, while also emphasizing the need for further optimization of infection persistence and treatment readouts.
