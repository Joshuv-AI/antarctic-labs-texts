Salary and Support Continuity Influence Medical Students’ Intentions to Pursue Physician-Scientist Careers: Results of an Experimental Survey
Authors: Moo Hyuk Lee, Jong-Il Kim, Young Kyung Do
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite efforts to strengthen the physician-scientist workforce through South Korea’s Physician-Scientist Training Program (PSTP), its impact on medical students’ career intentions remains unclear. This study assessed whether information about the PSTP—specifically salary and support continuity—affects medical students’ willingness to pursue physician-scientist careers. A total of 610 medical students nationwide were randomized to one of four hypothetical information scenarios varying by salary (50 million vs. 75 million KRW/year) and continuity of support. Willingness was measured before and after information provision, alongside perceived adequacy of the support and expected effectiveness of the program. Information provision increased willingness across all groups, with greater increases observed in the “75m KRW/y” and “75m KRW/y plus possible extension” groups. Perceived adequacy and expected effectiveness were likewise higher under enhanced salary scenarios. These findings suggest that PSTP design, particularly higher salary combined with guaranteed continuity, meaningfully shapes career intentions and may guide future policies aimed at strengthening the physician-scientist workforce.Keywords: Physician-Scientist, Physician-Scientist Training Program, Experimental Survey, Medical Students, Career Intentions

The concept of the physician-scientist has gained increasing attention in Republic of Korea (South Korea) as well as globally.1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8 Although there is a broad consensus on the need to cultivate physician-scientists, the number of physicians engaging in basic research remains strikingly low.9 Medical students and early-stage physicians show considerable reluctance to enter this career path, largely due to financial instability, career uncertainty, and limited information on available support.1,3,7,8,10,11

In response, various policies and programs have been introduced to address this shortage and encourage medical students and residents to pursue the physician-scientist pathway. A key initiative is the full-time PhD support program within the national Physician-Scientist Training Program (PSTP), which provides financial support and research funding during doctoral training.1,11 Full-time doctoral students—typically those who have completed medical residency—receive a research grant of 50 million KRW per year and an annual salary of 25–50 million KRW.

Although this program functions as a primary entry point into the physician-scientist career track, it remains unclear whether its salary level and continuity of support shape early-career researchers’ intentions to enter this pathway. Accordingly, this study examined whether providing information about the PSTP influences medical students’ likelihood to pursue physician-scientist careers, and how this effect varies across different levels of salary and support continuity.

We conducted an experimental survey as part of a broader research project on medical students’ perceptions and experiences. The relevant survey items were embedded within a larger questionnaire administered to 610 medical students from 26 medical schools nationwide. Participants were proportionally sampled by region (eight regions) and by school year (3rd–4th and 5th–6th years). Because most students were on leave from school during the 2024 medical crisis, probability-based sampling was not feasible due to instability of the situation. Instead, the research team appointed eight student recruiters with extensive networks to approach students willing to participate. We administered the online survey to the recruited participants from July 29 to 31, 2024, using a platform developed by Research & Research Inc.

The experimental survey consisted of three stages. In the first stage, respondents were provided with a brief overview about the PSTP full-time PhD support program and asked two baseline items: awareness of the program, and willingness to pursue a physician-scientist career. In this study, pursuing a physician-scientist career was defined narrowly to reflect the target population and typical training trajectory—that is, entering a graduate program in basic medical sciences during or after residency training, which represents the standard pathway supported by the full-time PhD program. In the second stage, respondents, randomly assigned to one of the four groups, were shown a short description providing hypothetical information on a combination of two salary levels and support continuity. Groups 1 and 2 were informed of a salary of 50 million KRW per year throughout the PhD program, and Groups 3 and 4 of 75 million KRW; Groups 2 and 4 additionally received information that the support could be extended beyond the doctoral period, indicating guaranteed continuity. The salary levels were selected to reflect the current program cap of 50 million KRW per year and to examine the policy relevance of a 50% increase above the status quo. The four groups were thus defined as “50m KRW/y,” “50m KRW/y plus possible extension,” “75m KRW/y,” and “75m KRW/y plus possible extension.” In the third stage, respondents completed follow-up items assessing perceived adequacy of the support, expected effectiveness of the program, and willingness to pursue a physician-scientist career (measured repetitively). All items were measured on five-point Likert scales, from “Strongly disagree (1)” to “Strongly agree (5).” The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Data 1. A stakeholder review involving one medical student and one recipient of the PhD program informed the survey items, and pilot testing with three medical students (4th–6th years) assessed comprehensibility.

To examine changes in willingness to pursue a physician-scientist career before and after information provision, we assessed three indicators: 1) mean willingness scores, 2) the proportion of respondents who selected “Agree” or “Strongly agree,” and 3) the proportion who selected “Neutral,” “Agree,” or “Strongly agree.” In the third specification, the “Neutral” category was included, as even a neutral response reflects a meaningful level of consideration among medical students given the generally reluctant attitudes towards a physician-scientist career. Paired one-sided t-tests were conducted within each group to assess whether willingness increased following information exposure. In addition, the change in mean willingness scores was modeled as a continuous outcome in a linear regression adjusting for baseline covariates. We further examined whether this change varied across subgroups defined by these covariates.

To evaluate the effect of each condition, we performed logistic regression using a binary outcome indicating whether a respondent was neutral or willing to pursue a physician-scientist career after information provision. Baseline measures were included as covariates to improve precision. The same approach was then applied to two secondary outcomes—perceived adequacy of the support and effectiveness of the program, to assess patterns that may supplement the primary outcome. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata SE 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R 3.6.0. (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Python 3.12.13 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used for visualization purposes.

Baseline characteristics were homogeneous across groups (Supplementary Table 1). Providing information about the PSTP increased respondents’ willingness to pursue a physician-scientist career across all groups, with larger changes generally observed in enhanced salary groups. Mean willingness scores rose by 0.22, 0.10, 0.33, and 0.33 points, respectively; the proportion of respondents who expressed willingness increased by 7.0, 2.9, 5.3, and 7.4 percentage points; when neutral responses were also included, the increases were 7.6, 1.4, 15.1, and 12.2 percentage points (Table 1). Modeling change in willingness scores as the outcome showed that assignment to the “75m KRW/y plus possible extension” group was associated with a 0.16-point increase compared to the “50m KRW/y” group (Supplementary Table 2). Across subgroups defined by baseline characteristics, the magnitude of change following information provision was generally consistent, with minor variations (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Notable group effects were observed in the main outcome (Fig. 1A). The proportion of respondents who were neutral or willing was higher in the enhanced salary groups than in the first two groups (34.9% and 39.2% vs. 29.2% and 25.2%). Interestingly, this difference appeared to be driven primarily by an increase in neutral rather than positive responses. Another notable finding is that, in the “50m KRW/y plus possible extension” group, the proportion of respondents who were neutral or willing was the lowest among the four groups; however, this group had the highest proportion of respondents who were strongly willing (2.9%). Finally, logistic regression analysis yielded odds ratio of 1.75 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.04–2.92) for the “75m KRW/y plus possible extension” group, relative to the “50m KRW/y” group (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes were generally inclined towards neutral or positive responses, with 80.5% for perceived adequacy of the support and 72.3% for expected effectiveness of the program in the “75m KRW/y plus possible extension” group (Fig. 1B and C). Logistic regression further demonstrated significantly higher expected effectiveness in this group, with an odds ratio of 1.74 (95% CI, 1.07–2.84) (Table 2).

These findings highlight three key implications for policies aimed at strengthening the physician-scientist workforce. First, providing clear information on the PSTP can increase medical students’ likelihood of considering physician-scientist careers. The observed increase in the proportion of students willing to pursue such careers was substantial, underscoring its relevance for policy discussions: this is notable because students who initially harbor interest may cease to pursue this pathway due to limited knowledge of available support.11 Given that financial instability is a major source of reluctance towards this career path,3,10 efforts should be made to effectively communicate program details and benefits to influence medical students’ preferences driven by financial considerations. Second, the observed group differences indicate that both the level and continuity of financial support meaningfully shape career intentions. A 50% salary increase above the current 50 million KRW cap, combined with guaranteed continuity, functions as a strong incentive for the physician-scientist pathway. Third, although students were generally reluctant to express willingness to pursue physician-scientist careers, a majority viewed the PSTP support as adequate and effective. Despite hesitancy towards personally pursuing this pathway, respondents still recognized the program as a potentially impactful policy tool.

Future studies could further explore real-world salary distributions from early-career to senior physician-scientists to provide precise estimates of salary ranges. Evidence that salaries exceed 75 million KRW per year at post-graduate stages may further enhance medical students’ willingness to pursue these careers. Salary-related factors can also be examined alongside financial stress, which was positively associated with post-information willingness.

The study has limitations. First, the information provided about the support program may have lacked sufficient detail. Second, stated preferences derived from hypothetical scenarios may differ from real-world career decisions. Third, despite a quota-based sampling strategy, the sample was not fully representative of all medical students. Fourth, transforming the ordinal outcome into a binary measure in the logistic regression analysis may have influenced the analysis; however, this approach was intentional, and the findings suggest that information on salary and continuity primarily reduces students’ reluctance and shifts them towards a neutral stance.

This nationwide study provides the first quantitative evidence on the potential impact of the PSTP on medical students’ career choices, based on data collected directly from medical students. The findings confirm that information on salary level and support continuity increases medical students’ likelihood to pursue physician-scientist careers. The magnitude of this effect depends on program design: both salary levels and continuity of support meaningfully shape career intentions.
