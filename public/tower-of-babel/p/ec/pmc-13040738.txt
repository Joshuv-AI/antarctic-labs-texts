Association between initial norepinephrine dose and minute by minute mean arterial pressure
Authors: Mitsuaki Nishikimi, Shinichiro Ohshimo, Kiyono Nobayashi, Takahiro Kinoshita, Nobuaki Shime
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeither the magnitude nor the timing of mean arterial pressure (MAP) increase after norepinephrine (NE) initiation is well defined, leaving clinicians without clear criteria for adequate starting doses. This study aimed to characterize minute-by-minute MAP responses to different initial NE doses in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with shock.MethodsWe included patients admitted to seven ICUs in Japan between 2013 and 2024 who had hypotension (MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) and initiated NE infusion more than 30 min after ICU admission. Invasive MAP data were collected during the observational period, defined as 30 min before to 120 min after NE initiation. Time-dependent effects were estimated with a generalized additive model adjusting for potential confounders, to evaluate MAP changes with varying starting NE doses.ResultsAmong the 81,829 patients, 5,349 who received NE were analyzed, of whom 2,006 (37.5%) had sepsis. Both the maximum MAP rise and the 15-min rate of increase were higher at an NE infusion rate of 0.100 µg/kg/min, compared with 0.025 and 0.050 µg/kg/min (p < 0.01). Model outputs indicated that NE infusion rates of 0.025 and 0.050 µg/kg/min failed to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min when baseline MAP was below 53.9 and 48.4 mmHg, respectively. Failure to achieve MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min was independently associated with increased ICU mortality (odds ratio: 1.49 [1.27–1.76], p < 0.01). In sepsis, the initial MAP slope at 0.025 µg/kg/min was significantly smaller than in non-sepsis (p < 0.01).ConclusionHigher initial NE doses were associated with both larger and faster MAP increases. Background

Shock is the clinical manifestation of acute circulatory failure characterized by inadequate tissue oxygen delivery that precipitates end-organ dysfunction and carries a high risk of death [1, 2]. Early restoration of perfusion is critical because prolonged hypoperfusion is well known to be associated with increased mortality [2]. Norepinephrine (NE) is therefore widely used in the intensive care unit (ICU) as the first-line vasopressor for shock that is unresponsive to fluid resuscitation. A target mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mmHg is recommended to ensure adequate organ perfusion, a threshold supported by major clinical guidelines [3–5]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of NE infusion across various types of shock, from vasodilatory [6] to hypovolemic [7] and cardiogenic shock [8].

Although the rapid, dose-dependent hemodynamic effects of NE (half-life < 2.5 min) are well documented in animal models of shock [6, 9], its minute-by-minute hemodynamic impact in real-world clinical settings has not been thoroughly investigated [10]. In ICU patients, these effects may differ substantially from preclinical observations because decreased renal and hepatic function, varying degrees of vasodilation, sepsis-related pathophysiology, and receptor down-regulation, all of which are common in critically ill patients, can alter the pharmacodynamic response to norepinephrine [11]. While many previous studies have recommended titrating NE to attain the target MAP level [12, 13], the optimal initial dosing of NE most likely to achieve this target remains undefined. A starting NE dose of approximately 0.05–0.1 µg/kg/min is commonly used in clinical practice based on our study (currently posted as a medRxiv preprint and under review for journal publication) [14], but rigorous evidence supporting this practice is scarce [15–17]. Moreover, neither the magnitude of MAP rise nor the time needed to achieve the target MAP has been clearly defined, leaving clinicians without objective criteria to judge whether the starting dose is sufficient or when to escalate doses to stabilize hemodynamics.

Recently, we developed a retrospective ICU database from seven hospitals in Japan. Apart from most existing resources, it contains time-series vital sign data recorded at 1 min intervals, enabling detailed time-dependent analyses. This high-resolution database allows us to examine the hemodynamic effect of NE infusion in critically ill patients with previously unattainable granularity. Accordingly, this study aims to analyze dynamic blood pressure changes after different starting doses of NE infusion in ICU patients with shock.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the OneICU database, an ongoing nationwide initiative that aggregates multicentre, minute-level physiologic ICU data from tertiary-care hospitals across Japan. The database captures continuous vital signs and clinician-validated spot measurements, each time-stamped and linked to contemporaneous medication-administration records (start/stop times and infusion rates), laboratory values, procedures, and outcomes. Free-text diagnoses are mapped to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision via a natural-language-processing pipeline and validated by intensivists. The database architecture, data quality assurance workflow, and full variable documentation have been described in detail elsewhere [14]. A master protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kansai Medical University authorizes data contribution from 34 hospitals, and several additional centres are finalizing IRB submissions, ensuring the continued expansion of OneICU. For this analysis, we included only the seven ICUs for which data extraction and quality checks had been fully completed. This study was conducted with the approval of the IRB of Hiroshima University Hospital (e2024-0054), which waived the need to obtain informed consent from the patients, to ensure participant anonymity as stipulated in the Japanese government guidelines.

Participants

Adult (aged ≥ 15 years) patients who had hypotension (MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) and initiated NE infusion more than 30 min after ICU admission between February 2013 and February 2024 were included. Patients were excluded, if they had any missing values for the variables needed for our main analysis (age, sex, body weight, vital signs, blood gas, and primary diagnosis documentation for ICU admission), if they had infusion of > 100 ml of sodium bicarbonate during observational period, or if they had extracorporeal membrane oxygenation management during observational period. The reason for excluding patients who received > 100 mL of sodium bicarbonate during the observational period was that acidemia can attenuate the hemodynamic effect of norepinephrine, while bicarbonate administration can restore acid–base balance and enhance norepinephrine responsiveness [18]. We also excluded patients with < 10 invasive MAP measurements in the 30 min preceding NE initiation or < 10 measurements in the post-initiation observation period.

Exposure and outcomes

The exposure variable was the initial NE infusion rate in the ICU, classified into three strata: 0.025, 0.050, and 0.100 µg/kg/min. Each dose was normalized to the molecular weight of NE base, as recommended in current guidelines [19], and calculated using the patient’s admission body weight recorded in the database.

The primary outcome was the time course of invasive MAP following NE initiation. The observation period spanned from 30 min before NE initiation to the earlier of (1) 120 min after initiation or (2) the first change (> 20%) in the infusion rate of NE or any of the following concomitant vasoactive agents: vasopressin and epinephrine. To mitigate high-frequency noise and bridge short measurement gaps, the raw arterial-pressure signal was processed with a low-pass filter before modelling (Fig. 1). We derived two hemodynamic metrics that characterize dynamic blood-pressure responses—magnitude and slope. Magnitude was defined as the largest absolute rise in MAP recorded within the observation window, whereas slope captured the early rate of increase, expressed as the change in the MAP values (mmHg/min) during the first 15 min after NE initiation.

Data collection

From the OneICU database we extracted two sets of variables: (i) admission variables—age, sex, sepsis diagnosis according to the Sepsis-3 definition [20], and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score; and (ii) variables at NE initiation—the initial NE dose (µg/kg/min), heart rate, arterial blood-gas values (pH, bicarbonate, base excess, PaCO₂, lactate), and mechanical-ventilation status (current and prior during the ICU stay). All invasive MAP measurements recorded within the observation window were retrieved. Baseline MAP was defined as the value documented at the moment NE infusion began.

Statistical analysis

Because the MAP response to NE is expected to vary nonlinearly over time (e.g., a rapid early rise followed by a plateau) and may not increase proportionally with dose, we modelled MAP trajectories after NE initiation using multivariable generalized additive models with thin-plate regression splines for main effects and tensor-product cubic regression splines for product terms. This approach extends conventional regression by modelling the outcome as the sum of linear terms for covariates and smooth functions of key continuous predictors, allowing flexible relationships without imposing a specific parametric shape. Covariates adjusted in the model included age, sex, SOFA score at ICU admission, latest heart rate and arterial-blood-gas results before NE initiation, mechanical-ventilation status, renal replacement therapy status, steroid use, use of other vasoactive drugs (vasopressin, and epinephrine), cumulative fluid volume before NE initiation, the presence or absence of sepsis, and initial NE dose. We defined “offset” as time since NE initiation, measured in minutes, and also included in the model. Baseline MAP values at NE initiation and at − 5, −10, − 15, −20, − 25, and − 30 min were also entered in the model. Smooth terms were applied to NE dose, offset, and baseline MAP to account for nonlinear relationships. A smooth product term between NE dose and offset was included in the model to assess dose-dependent effects over time. Predicted MAP curves for a “typical” patient (all covariates fixed at cohort medians) were generated for the three prespecified doses (0.025, 0.050, 0.100 µg/kg/min). These visualizations were based on complete cases without imputations.

From the estimated trajectories, we derived the MAP magnitude change and 15 min slope to quantify time-dependent NE effects across starting doses. Missing pre-NE MAP values were handled with 3 multiple imputations; each imputed set underwent 500 bootstrap iterations. Bootstrap resampling was performed at the patient level rather than at the individual time-point level to account for within-patient correlation of observations. Rubin’s rules combined the results to produce point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup models were fitted separately for three etiologic comparisons: septic vs. non-septic, cardiogenic vs. non-cardiogenic, and hypovolemic vs. non-hypovolemic cohorts (the corresponding etiology indicator was omitted from each respective model). Magnitude and slope estimates across NE doses were compared between cohorts using the same imputation-plus-bootstrap procedure. For the entire cohort, we fitted an additional generalized additive model to characterize MAP over the full 120-min window, fixing the NE dose at the cohort median (0.051 µg/kg/min). Five hundred bootstrap samples provided 95% CIs for the predicted trajectory. We also recalculated the slope by shifting the early-phase window from 0 to 15 min to 2–17 min to account for potential differences in time to pharmacologic effect according to the venous access route.

As sensitivity analyses, we repeated the analysis without censoring patients who underwent ≥ 20% norepinephrine dose escalation or initiation of other vasopressors and analysis including patients who received NE within the first 30 min after ICU admission. We also estimated MAP trajectories using the parametric g-formula to account for time-varying covariates. Detailed methods of parametric g-formula are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

To explore target attainment, we applied the main model to simulated patient profiles with baseline MAP values between 45 and 65 mmHg and initial NE infusion rates from 0.001 to 0.100 µg/kg/min. For each combination, we estimated the time required to reach MAP ≥ 65 mmHg; combinations that failed to reach the target during the 120-min simulation window were coded as “not reach.” Results were displayed as a two-dimensional contour plot.

Before the primary analyses, model performance was assessed in clinical data restricted to patients with at least 60 min of observed MAP after NE initiation. For each patient, we generated a model-predicted MAP trajectory and classified whether the model anticipated achieving MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 120 min. This binary prediction was compared with the actual outcome to compute accuracy metrics. We confirmed that the model correctly classified achievement or failure of MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, with an overall accuracy of 81% (2,438/3,012), sensitivity of 86% (2,239/2,596), and specificity of 48% (199/416) (sFigure 1).

Finally, a multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, SOFA score at ICU admission, latest heart rate, arterial blood gas results before NE initiation, mechanical-ventilation status, and sepsis status was developed to investigate the association between the achievement of MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min and ICU mortality.

MAP trajectories models were fitted using the gam () function from the mgcv package in R. All reported p-values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was regarded as denoting a statistically significant difference. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.0.3) and Python (version 3.12.3). The code can be accessed through the following GitHub repository: https://github.com/takapion/Norepinephrine-Time-Dependent-Effects.

The predicted differences in MAP responses across various NE starting doses are shown in Fig. 2. Across all groups, MAP exhibited a continuous rise following NE initiation, generally reaching a plateau between approximately 45 and 90 min. The time to plateau was shorter with higher NE doses. Specifically, the magnitude and slope of MAP increase were 8.8 mmHg (95% CI, 8.2–9.4) and 0.30 mmHg/min (95% CI, 0.26–0.33) at a starting dose of 0.025 µg/kg/min; 10.6 mmHg (95% CI, 10.0–11.1) and 0.37 mmHg/min (95% CI, 0.34–0.40) at 0.050 µg/kg/min; and 12.4 mmHg (95% CI, 11.5–13.2) and 0.51 mmHg/min (95% CI, 0.46–0.57) at 0.100 µg/kg/min, respectively. Both the magnitude and slope of MAP increase were significantly higher at 0.100 µg/kg/min compared to the lower doses (both p < 0.01). We recalculated the slope after shifting the early-phase window from 0 to 15 min to 2–17 min. The slopes of MAP increase were 0.29 mmHg/min (95% CI, 0.26–0.33) at 0.025 µg/kg/min; 0.36 mmHg/min (95% CI, 0.33–0.39) at 0.050 µg/kg/min; and 0.41 mmHg/min (95% CI, 0.36–0.45) at 0.100 µg/kg/min, indicating that the slope remained significantly higher at 0.050 and 0.100 µg/kg/min than at 0.025 µg/kg/min. We performed sensitivity analyses: (1) without censoring patients who underwent ≥ 20% norepinephrine dose escalation or initiation of other vasopressors, and (2) including patients who received norepinephrine within the first 30 min after ICU admission. In the latter analysis, the number of eligible patients increased from 5,349 to 6,998 by including those who received norepinephrine within 30 min after ICU admission. The results were similar to those of the main analysis (sFigs. 4 and 5). In a further sensitivity analysis that adjusted for time-varying covariates and did not censor at norepinephrine dose changes or initiation of other vasoactive agents, the findings were also similar (sFigure 6).

We further evaluated the time required to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg at different initial NE doses. A scatter plot illustrating the relationship among NE starting dose, baseline MAP, and time to reach the target MAP is shown in Fig. 3A. Cases that failed to reach the target MAP within 60 min (black dots) were predominantly clustered in the lower-left corner, whereas cases that achieved the target within 10 min (white dots) were primarily located in the upper-right corner. The model generated from these plots revealed that lower NE doses failed to achieve the target MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min when the baseline MAP was below certain thresholds, whereas higher starting doses were related to shorter times to reach the target MAP (Fig. 3B). Specifically, NE infusion rates of 0.025 and 0.050 µg/kg/min did not achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min if the baseline MAP was below 53.9 and 48.4 mmHg, respectively (Fig. 3C). Among the analyzed patients, ICU mortality was 24.1% (359/1,488) in those who failed to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min, compared to 14.7% (568/3861) in those who did. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that failure to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min was associated with increased ICU mortality (odds ratio: 1.52 [1.29–1.79], p < 0.01) (sTable 1).

We compared the MAP responses between patients with and without sepsis (Fig. 4). While there was no statistically significant difference in the magnitude of MAP increase between patients with and without sepsis, the slope of MAP was significantly lower in patients with sepsis receiving very low NE doses (0.025 µg/kg/min: 0.21 [95% CI, 0.14–0.27] vs. 0.33 [95% CI, 0.30–0.37], p < 0.01). No significant differences in slope were observed at 0.050 µg/kg/min (0.35 [95% CI, 0.30–0.41] vs. 0.38 [95% CI, 0.34–0.41], p = 0.51) or 0.100 µg/kg/min (0.50 [95% CI, 0.42–0.58] vs. 0.52 [95% CI, 0.45–0.59], p = 0.74). The MAP responses in patients with and without other etiologies (cardiogenic etiology or hypovolemia) are shown in sFigs. 7 and 8.

Discussion

In this study, we found that both the magnitude and slope of MAP increase were greater with higher initial NE infusion doses. We also identified that NE infusion rates of 0.025 and 0.050 µg/kg/min were unlikely to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min when the initial MAP was below 53.9 and 48.4 mmHg, respectively. Furthermore, we observed that among patients receiving very low NE doses, the slope of MAP increase was lower in those with sepsis than in those without sepsis, suggesting a sepsis-specific impairment of vasopressor responsiveness.

A previous large database study indicated that persistent hypotension (> 2 h) was not uncommon in ICU patients with distributive shock and was associated with increased mortality [2]. In this context, a 60-min delay in the onset of the full vasopressor effect risks exposing patients with hypotension to prolonged periods of suboptimal perfusion. In fact, failure to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min was associated with increased ICU mortality in our database, consistent with prior reports [16, 21], although this relationship should still be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Our model demonstrated that lower starting doses carry a risk of failing to achieve a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg within 60 min and are unlikely to restore MAP promptly when baseline blood pressure is severely depressed. Our findings of dose-dependent acceleration in MAP, affecting both the initial rise and the peak, suggest that starting NE at a higher dose (e.g., ≥ 0.1 µg/kg/min) may be more appropriate for patients with marked hypotension, especially when hypotension is driven by sepsis.

We compared the MAP responses between patients with and without sepsis and found that the slope of MAP was lower in patients with sepsis receiving very low NE doses. A previous report has suggested that septic shock may be less responsive to NE compared with other types, possibly due to down-regulation of adrenergic receptors [22]. Additionally, we speculate that an increased volume of distribution, potentially related to increased vascular permeability, and high-volume fluid administration during the resuscitation phase may contribute to the attenuated slope of MAP increase in patients with sepsis.

An estimated MAP increase of approximately 12 mmHg was observed with a starting dose of 0.1 µg/kg/min of NE, suggesting that this dose is feasible for achieving hemodynamic stabilization. In clinical practice, NE infusion is often initiated via a peripheral route, particularly during the early phase of shock resuscitation. Previous studies support the safety of peripheral administration at this dosing level [23, 24]. However, we were unable to directly evaluate its safety or potential adverse effects, such as local ischemic complications. Future studies are warranted to assess the hemodynamic efficacy and safety profile of peripheral NE administration at higher starting doses.

Importantly, there is substantial international variation in the salt formulations of norepinephrine. In this study, we standardized all dosing data by converting them to the molecular weight of the norepinephrine base, as recommended [19]. This approach allows our findings to be generalizable to real-world clinical practice without concern for differences in salt formulations.

One caveat is that patients with more severe shock may have exerted less influence on our primary model because they were more likely to undergo ≥ 20% dose escalation and were therefore censored earlier. To address this potential bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the parametric g-formula that adjusted for time-varying covariates without censoring due to treatment escalation. The trend toward a higher and more rapid increase in MAP with higher starting NE doses persisted even after accounting for changes in heart rate, fluid administration rates, and NE dose, and for the initiation or discontinuation of other therapies, including transfusion, other vasoactive drugs, and sedative drugs. This sensitivity analysis supports our conclusion that lower starting NE doses are associated with a higher risk of failing to achieve MAP ≥ 65 mmHg in patients with severe hypotension.

This study has some limitations. First, we could not exclude residual confounding from unmeasured factors not captured in our database such as unrecorded indicators of illness severity or concomitant treatments. Especially, information on whether norepinephrine was administered via a peripheral or central venous catheter was not available in our database. Second, neither the timing of NE initiation nor the titration strategy was protocolized across institutions. Third, please note that our model is a descriptive/explanatory model rather than a predictive one. Fourth, although modelling within-patient correlation with patient-level random effects is a valid approach, we did not adopt it because it was computationally intensive and could have led to unstable model fitting. Instead, we used patient-level bootstrapping, resampling patients rather than individual time points and refitting the model in each bootstrap replicate. Finally, we could not analyze starting doses of NE far beyond 0.1 µg/kg/min due to the small sample size in that range.

Conclusions

Higher initial NE doses were associated with both larger and faster MAP increases. Lower starting doses appeared insufficient to achieve a target MAP within 60 min in patients with severe hypotension. These results suggest a potential beneficial role for higher starting doses, although prospective studies are needed to confirm optimal dosing strategies.
