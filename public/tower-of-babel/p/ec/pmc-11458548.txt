Impact of Bacille Calmette-Guérin vaccination on the therapeutic schedule of infantile epileptic spasms syndrome: A 25-year Japanese single-center survey
Authors: Hikaru Kobayashi, Hirofumi Inoue, Takeshi Matsushige, Madoka Hoshide, Fumitaka Kohno, Ippei Hidaka, Shunji Hasegawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Early treatment is crucial for improving neurological prognosis in IESS patients.•ACTH therapy for IESS has immunosuppressive effects, posing infection risks.•IESS onset often overlaps with the BCG vaccination period in Japan, creating clinical challenges.•BCG-related adverse events after ACTH therapy were not seen.•Avoidance of ACTH therapy after BCG vaccination limits treatment options.Keywords: BCG, Vaccine, Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, Adrenocorticotropic hormone therapy

Introduction

Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) is a live vaccine administered to prevent tuberculosis infection. In Japan, the BCG vaccination system has changed over the decades [1], [2]. Since 1974, BCG has been inoculated in infants up to four years old and first grade students in elementary and junior high school who tested negative for the tuberculin test. Since 2003, only infants up to four years old has been vaccinated. After 2005, the tuberculin test was abolished, and BCG vaccination was administered to infants up to six months old. Since 2013, infants up to one year old, especially five–eight months old as a standard inoculation period, have been eligible for BCG vaccination because of the increasing number of other vaccines to be inoculated during early infancy and to avoid the risk of BCG-related osteitis/osteomyelitis. The number of children with tuberculosis decreased, whereas there have been several cases of osteitis and osteomyelitis caused by live vaccines annually [1].

Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS), also known as West syndrome for a typical case, is one of the developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, characterized by intractable epileptic spasms, neurodevelopmental regression, and hypsarrhythmia on electroencephalogram (EEG) [3]. Early diagnosis and treatment are essential for improving the developmental outcomes of IESS patients. The age at the onset of IESS usually ranges from four to seven months [4]. In Japan, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) therapy using synthetic ACTH has been administered for IESS patients as one of the most effective treatments. Vigabatrin has been available in Japan since 2016 as a first-line drug, particularly for IESS associated with tuberous sclerosis [5]. However, since ACTH therapy has an immunosuppressive effect, serious infectious diseases such as bacteremia have been reported [6]. Therefore, the administration of live vaccines after ACTH therapy should be postponed for a certain period [7], [8].

The current standard BCG vaccination schedule in Japan overlaps with the peak onset age of IESS, creating clinical challenges. Infants may receive the BCG vaccine shortly before or after the onset of IESS, leading to a dilemma where clinicians must choose between administering ACTH therapy immediately to improve neurological prognosis or delaying treatment to avoid the risk of infection due to its immunosuppressive effects. In 2018, the Medical Safety Committee of the Japanese Society of Child Neurology recommended caution when administering ACTH therapy within eight weeks of BCG vaccination [7]. However, evidence supporting the safety of ACTH therapy immediately after BCG vaccination is limited, and it remains unclear whether ACTH therapy increases the risk of adverse events following BCG vaccination.

This study aims to clarify the relationship between ACTH therapy and the risk of adverse events following BCG vaccination, and to determine whether the timing of BCG vaccination affects treatment decisions and neurological prognosis in IESS patients. We retrospectively investigated the temporal relationship between ACTH therapy and BCG vaccination, its impact on treatment strategy, and the adverse events of BCG vaccination in IESS patients.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yamaguchi University Hospital (2020–177). The study has been carried out in accordance with The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). As this study was a non-invasive investigation, informed consent was obtained in the form of an opt-out on the website. Patients who refused to participate were excluded.

Participants

The participants were IESS patients who had visited our hospital between 1996 and 2020. We retrospectively investigated data from their medical records, including date of birth, sex, underlying disease, date of BCG vaccination, onset of IESS, treatment and its effects, presence of severe infections, and developmental prognosis. Patients diagnosed with “West syndrome,” “infantile spasms,” or “epileptic spasms” were searched from medical records during the target period, and 86 patients were extracted (Fig. 1). Of these, 49 patients were excluded due to missing data on the onset of IESS or the BCG vaccination date, including one patient who received BCG vaccination several years after ACTH therapy but had an unknown exact vaccination date. Of the remaining 37 patients, additional exclusions were made: two patients who did not receive ACTH therapy, two with unknown dates of ACTH therapy, two who received BCG vaccination after ACTH therapy (both more than one year after ACTH therapy), one who received ACTH therapy over one year after the onset of IESS, and four patients who developed late-onset infantile spasms (onset after one year of age).

Flowchart of the current survey.

There were six patients during the period from 1996 to 2004, when BCG vaccination was given to infants up to four years old. Of these, two patients received BCG vaccination numerous days after ACTH therapy, and one patient had late-onset infantile spasms; these three patients were excluded. Another patient also received BCG vaccination after ACTH therapy, but was excluded due to missing vaccination date information, as mentioned above. The remaining two patients received BCG vaccination at 26 and 23 weeks and ACTH therapy at 47 and 120 weeks, respectively.

After these exclusions, 26 patients remained eligible for analysis and were divided into two groups based on the BCG vaccination period:

Group A (1996–2012, N=15): This group includes patients who received BCG when they were 0–4 years old, and those who received BCG when they were 0–6 months old. This group includes two patients who received BCG vaccination prior to 2004 at 26 and 23 weeks, respectively.

Group B (2013–2020, N=11): This group includes patients vaccinated when they were 0–1 year old, following the 2013 change in the BCG vaccination schedule.

Records showed that patients received synthetic ACTH-Z in a median dose of 0.0125 (0.01–0.025) mg/kg/day injection once a day for two weeks, followed by two-week tapering period.

Statistical analysis

Group A and Group B were comparatively analyzed using the χ2, Fisher’s exact, and Mann-Whitney U tests. Statistical significance was set at p-values < 0.05. Analyses and calculations were performed using Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Comparison of ACTH therapy and BCG vaccination timing between two groups

A comparison between Groups A and B is presented in Table 1. The groups did not differ significantly in terms of the age at disease onset or sex. The age of patients at BCG vaccination in Group A was significantly lower than that in Group B (p = 0.0003). The period from BCG vaccination to disease onset was significantly shorter in Group B than in Group A (p = 0.037). The two groups did not differ significantly in terms of age at ACTH therapy administration or in the period from BCG vaccination to the start of ACTH therapy. In Group A, 12 patients (80 %) developed IESS after BCG vaccination, two patients (13 %) received BCG vaccination after the onset of IESS, and IESS onset and BCG vaccination in the remaining one patient occurred at approximately the same time (Fig. 2). In contrast, in Group B, six patients (55 %) developed IESS after BCG vaccination, and five patients (45 %) received BCG vaccination after the onset of IESS.

Adverse events in patients who had received ACTH therapy within eight weeks after BCG vaccination

Six patients received ACTH therapy within eight weeks after BCG vaccination (three patients in each group, Fig. 3), which has not occurred since 2018. Two patients had IESS onset after BCG vaccination (one in each group), three patients had BCG vaccination after the onset of IESS (one in Group A and two in Group B), and one patient had BCG vaccination and the onset of IESS at almost the same time (Group A). No serious adverse events due to BCG vaccination were observed in all of the six patients.

Patients who received adrenocorticotropic hormone therapy within eight weeks after Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination. Case 1 and 6 had infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) onset after BCG vaccination. Case 2, 4, and 5 received BCG vaccination after the onset of IESS. Case 3 received a BCG vaccination and exhibited IESS onset almost simultaneously. All patients experienced IESS onset before 2018. No serious adverse events associated with the BCG vaccination were observed.

Changes in treatment plan due to post-BCG vaccination and their impact on prognosis

Four patients were deferred to or opted out of ACTH therapy because of post-BCG vaccination (Table 2), all of which occurred after 2018. Vigabatrin was selected as an alternative treatment in two cases (cases 7 and 8), both of which were effective and improved the epileptic spasms and EEG findings. The remaining two patients chose anti-epileptic drugs other than vigabatrin: treatment for one patient (case 9, the same patient as case 5 in Fig. 3) was ineffective, and ACTH therapy was performed four weeks after BCG vaccination, followed by a recurrence of IESS. Treatment for another patient (case 10) with cerebral palsy was partially effective; however, after trying multiple anti-epileptic drugs, ACTH therapy was performed after eight weeks. The seizures in the four patients required 2–15 weeks to achieve remission. Three of the four patients had no underlying disease: two patients with symptom duration of two and nine weeks had borderline to mild developmental delays, while one patient with a 12-week symptom duration had normal to borderline motor development and moderately delayed language development at five years old.

Discussion

The present study indicated that IESS onset often overlaps with the period of BCG vaccination in Japan because of the transition in the BCG vaccination schedule, especially after 2013. Problems arise when patients coincidentally develop IESS shortly after receiving BCG vaccination, when patients receive BCG vaccination without recognizing IESS, or when considering the impact on treatment at an early stage of the disease. This overlap creates a clinical dilemma, forcing clinicians to balance the need for immediate ACTH therapy, which is the most powerful treatment option for IESS, to improve neurological prognosis against the potential risk of infection due to its immunosuppressive effects. In fact, this dilemma influenced the treatment choices of four of the eight patients (50 %) after 2018. Fortunately, two of them had no evident impact on prognosis due to vigabatrin efficacy, and one received ACTH therapy four weeks after BCG vaccination without adverse events. Groups A and B did not differ significantly in terms of the period from BCG vaccination to the start of ACTH therapy, which was considered to be the result of intentionally avoiding overlapping timings by not administering ACTH therapy after BCG vaccination. Prior to 2013, three patients also received ACTH therapy within eight weeks of BCG vaccination, but no adverse events were observed.

Several cases of osteitis or osteomyelitis after BCG vaccination are reported annually in Japan as the adverse events of BCG [1]. The median interval between BCG vaccination and the onset of osteitis and osteomyelitis in Taiwan was 13.9 months (range, 3–36 months) when newborns received BCG of the Tokyo-172 vaccine strain [9]. This would mean that more than half of the patients in Groups A and B in this study received ACTH therapy when they were at risk of osteitis or osteomyelitis, but none developed osteitis or osteomyelitis. Children who developed osteitis after BCG vaccination may have a genetic background of gene polymorphisms in Toll-like receptors 1, 2, and 6, mannose-binding lectin, and interleukin-17A in the Finnish cohort [10]. Moreover, when 349 patients with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) were inoculated with BCG, 34 % of these patients developed disseminated lesions and 46 patients had BCG-related death [11]. None of the patients in this study had an underlying immunodeficiency. Thus, while no severe infections were observed in this cohort, the possibility of complications in immunocompromised patients remains a concern. Sepsis, pneumonia, and urinary and gastrointestinal infections are associated with ACTH therapy [12]. Of the 27 patients who received ACTH therapy, bacteremia was observed in 5.3 %, and three patients died [6]. ACTH therapy affects immunity by decreasing the number of lymphocytes and CD4+ T cells [8] and reduce the phagocytic activity of polymorphonuclear leukocyte [13]. As in patients with SCID, T-cell ≤ 250/µL may be associated with a higher risk of complications [11]. Expanded newborn screening for SCID would reduce the possibility of incidental administration of ACTH therapy or BCG vaccination in the future [14]. If IESS patients become infected during ACTH therapy, the infection should be treated immediately after discontinuation of ACTH therapy. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to report on BCG-related adverse events after ACTH or steroid therapy for IESS patients. Moreover, there is no evidence regarding the waiting time for ACTH therapy after BCG vaccination, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

Seizures and EEG abnormalities must be improved with effective treatment as early as possible to improve the neurological prognosis in IESS patients. The short-term remission rate of ACTH therapy for IESS is approximately 70–80 % [15]. In Japan, the approval of vigabatrin was delayed until 2016 because vigabatrin causes irreversible visual field impairment. The spasms-free rate of vigabatrin is 54 % in IESS patients, excluding tuberous sclerosis [16] and 88 % in IESS patients with tuberous sclerosis [17], whereas evidence of other anti-epileptic drugs is insufficient [16]. Therefore, certain patients are at a permanent disadvantage if ACTH therapy is not available.

The present study had some limitations. The main limitation was the small number of cases and the existence of missing data due to the retrospective nature of the study. Because immunocompromised patients were not included in this study, we cannot conclude whether it is truly safe to administer ACTH therapy soon after BCG vaccination to patients with potential underlying diseases. Further evidence is required to clarify the safety of ACTH therapy after BCG vaccination.

Conclusion

The onset of IESS often overlaps with the period of BCG vaccination in Japan, owing to the transition in the BCG vaccination schedule. This overlap presents challenges in determining the optimal timing for ACTH therapy, as clinicians must balance the urgency of treating IESS with the potential risks of immunosuppressive therapy following vaccination. Epidemiological and immunological evidence for setting an appropriate treatment interval, treatment strategies for patients closely after BCG vaccination, and vaccination schedules considering IESS are required in future studies.
