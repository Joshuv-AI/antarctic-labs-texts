STING activates ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis to defend against HSV-1 retinal infection
Authors: Wei Liu, Haifeng Cui, Li Wang, Xiangyu Ge, Xingfei Zhu, Xingmiao Liang, Ziqing Chen, Yuwen Gan, Le Shi, Xuebin Hu, Xiaohu Ding, Zhengjie Xu, Xiaoling Liang, Lili Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) is the most common cause of infectious blindness and is a major cause of acute retinal necrosis (ARN), a severe condition marked by rapid necrosis of the retina. Effective treatments for HSV-1 retinal infection are limited to general antiviral drugs, which do not specifically target the underlying pathophysiology. Here, we investigate the role of stimulator of interferon genes (STING), a key player in innate immunity, in HSV-1 retinal infections. STING knockout (KO) mice are highly susceptible to HSV-1 retinal infection, evidenced by impaired antiviral immune responses, increased ocular virus load and severe retinal necrosis. Mechanistically, STING is required for Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1)-mediated PANoptosis, an inflammatory programmed cell death pathway, in microglia and macrophages. STING forms a complex with ZBP1 and Z-form nuclei acid during HSV-1 infection. Further, activation of STING induced Z-form nuclei acid and subsequent activation of ZBP1. ZBP1 KO mice exhibited a similar defective antiviral phenotype as STING KO mice, whereas treatment with ZBP1 agonist Curaxin CBL0137 rescued the impaired antiviral response in STING-deficient microglia and macrophages and mitigates retinal necrosis in both WT and STING KO mice following HSV-1 infection. Together, our study revealed a STING-ZBP1-PANoptosis signal axis against HSV-1 infection in retina, which should provide new insights for the treatment of retinal virus infection such as ARN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03595-0.

Introduction

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) is a fundamental antiviral innate immunity pathway [1–3]. cGAS senses double-stranded (ds) DNA and generate the second messenger cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), which binds to and activates STING. Consequently, STING activates the transcription factors including interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB), leading to production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and interferons (IFN) that can limit viral replication [2]. In addition, STING exhibits antiviral function through IFN-independent manner, possibly by autophagy and other unknown mechanisms [4–7].

HSV-1 is a neurotropic dsDNA virus which is the most common cause of infectious blindness and fatal encephalitis [8]. The function of STING in HSV-1 retinal infection has not been explored. However, recent studies showed that mice deficient of cGAS or STING are susceptible to acute herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE), and brain microglia confer the antiviral activities in STING-dependent manner [9]. Microglia are resident immune cells of nerve system [10]. Depletion of microglia increased viral titers and disease severity in HSE mouse model [11]. Our recent results revealed that STING is primarily expressed in microglia in both human and mouse retinas [12]. It is currently unknown the response of microglia during HSV-1 retinal infection and the effects of STING in this process.

PANoptosis is an inflammatory and lytic cell death pathway driven by both caspases and receptor interacting protein kinases (RIPKs) [13, 14]. It was initially characterized in response to viral infection, in which Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1) plays crucial roles [15]. ZBP1 is an IFN-inducible protein, contains two binding domains for left-handed DNA (Z-DNA) in the N-terminus and two RHIM domains at the center part that are critical to regulate cell death and inflammatory responses [16]. Upon virus infection, ZBP1 acts as a sensor to assemble the large PANoptosome complex, which are consist of the key molecules of pyroptosis, apoptosis and necroptosis [16]. ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis defend against HSV-1 and influenza A virus (IAV) infection and promotes virus clearance [15, 17]. However, during coronaviruses infection, ZBP1 led to severe tissue damage by promoting cytokine storm [18]. Therefore, an in-depth understanding of ZBP1-drived PANoptosis is crucial for more effective and safe antiviral strategies, especially for neuron infection.

Acute retinal necrosis (ARN) is a rare and severe intraocular inflammatory condition characterized by rapid retinal necrosis, retinal detachment, occlusive vasculopathy, and inflammation in the vitreous and anterior chamber [19]. ARN can lead to blindness and a high rate of recurrent retinal detachments despite aggressive antiviral therapy [20, 21]. The condition is caused by viral infections, including varicella zoster virus, HSV-1 and − 2, and cytomegalovirus [22]. Treatment of ARN typically involves systemic and/or intravitreal (IVT) antivirals such as ganciclovir and acyclovir, but there are no established clinical guidelines for ARN management [23]. Additionally, resistance and adverse effects of antivirals have been reported [23–25]. Therefore, there is urgent need to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the host immune response and inflammation control upon HSV-1 retinal infection, which should provide insights for more effective and targeted treatments of ARN.

Here, we showed for the first time that STING is essential for antivirus defense in retina. Furthermore, we revealed a mechanism through which STING defends HSV-1 infection by activating ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis, and demonstrated the antivirus effect of ZBP1 agonist CBL0137 in HSV-1 retinal infection. The presented study provides new insights for treatment of HSV-1 retinal infections, which may serve as an important complement to broad-spectrum antiviral therapy.

Results

IVT injection of HSV-1 leads to retinal injury and neuroninflammation

Three murine ARN models have been described in which HSV-1 is delivered via the anterior chamber, vitreous cavity or subretinal space [26–28]. Here, we performed intravitreal (IVT) injection of HSV-1 (Fig. 1A), and evaluated the immune response and retinal inflammation, which had not been thoroughly characterized before. Clinical manifestations of ARN include vitreous opacities, scleritis, vitritis, necrotizing retinitis, occlusive vasculitis, and optic disc edema [22]. At 3 days post-injection (dpi) with a lower viral dose (4 × 10^3 Plaque-Forming Unit, PFU), we observed dilation and leakage of retinal vessels, optic disc edema, and retinal whitening on fundus examination (Fig. 1B). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of retinal whole mounts using TER119 (red blood cells) and isolectin B4 (IB4; vasculature) corroborated prominent vascular leakage (Fig. 1C). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) further revealed cellular infiltration into the vitreous cavity (Fig. 1D). At a higher dose (4 × 10^4 PFU), the eyes became completely opaque, precluding in vivo fundus imaging and OCT (Fig. 1E). We therefore assessed retinal morphology by hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, which at 9 dpi showed marked immune cell infiltration into the vitreous, retinal folding and detachment, and iris inflammation; these changes were more severe at higher viral doses (Fig. 1E-F). Viral inclusion bodies were already evident in the retina at 3 dpi after high-dose infection (4 × 10^4 PFU) (Fig. 1G, arrows). By contrast, inactivated HSV-1 (4 × 10^4 PFU) had no detectable effect on overall ocular morphology or retinal architecture (Fig. S1). Across both inoculum levels, HSV-1 DNA (ICP27 and UL43) was elevated at 3 dpi and declined by 9 dpi (Fig. 1H). Plaque assays of vitreous humor after high-dose infection mirrored this pattern: titers were higher at 3 dpi and declined by 9 dpi, whereas low-dose infection produced no detectable plaques (Fig. 1I). We hypothesized that the retinal immune response contributes to the reduction in viral burden at the later time point. To test this, we assessed retinal inflammation by immunostaining for microglia, the retina’s resident immune cells. In control mice, microglia (IBA+) were localized to the inner and outer plexiform layers (IPL and OPL) (Fig. 1J). HSV-1 infection resulted in an increase in microglia and possibly macrophages, as IBA1 also stained macrophages, in both the retina and vitreous cavity (Fig. 1J). HSV-1 infection also induced significant gliosis in Müller cells and astrocytes, as indicated by elevated glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression (Fig. 1K). Additionally, HSV-1 infection caused loss of retinal ganglion cells (Brn3a+) (Fig. 1L) and structural distortion of bipolar cells (PKCα+) (Fig. 1M). STING deficient mice are highly susceptible to HSV-1 retinal infection

HSV-1 infection of mouse retinas induced robust activation of STING and its downstream signaling (Fig. S2). We then compared ocular pathology between WT and STING−/− mice. At the lower virus dose (4 × 10^3 PFU), STING−/− eyes already exhibited more severe retinal destruction (Fig. S3A–B). At the higher dose (4 × 10^4 PFU), STING deficiency led to markedly worsened disease: by 3 dpi, the cornea and lens had become opaque, and by 9 dpi, the eye showed significant damage, with a corneal ulcer clearly visible (Fig. 2A). HE staining revealed pronounced retinal necrosis and progressive pathology in STING−/− mice, with complete loss of retinal lamina by 9 dpi (Fig. 2B-C). These results suggest that STING deficiency increases susceptibility to HSV-1 retinal infection. Consistent with these findings, STING−/− eyes had significantly higher viral titers in the vitreous humor (Fig. 2D) and increased viral protein and DNA levels in the retina compared with WT (Fig. 2E–F). We further assessed viral distribution in WT and STING−/− retinas at 3 dpi, a time point when HSV-1 was sufficiently abundant in WT retinas to enable direct comparisons between genotypes. IF on retinal wholemounts showed that HSV-1 glycoprotein B (gB) signal was largely confined to the inner retina and was consistently more intense in STING−/− retinas (Fig. 2G). Co-immunostaining with retinal cell markers indicated that HSV-1 signal localized primarily to microglia/macrophages (Iba1+) in both genotypes (Fig. 2H). STING is essential for activation of multiple antivirus pathways in mouse retina

We next performed RNA-seq to delineate the mechanisms underlying STING-mediated protection during HSV-1 infection. It showed that HSV-1 infection activated a robust antiviral response in WT retinas, involving innate immune pathways, IFN-β response, and antiviral signaling (Fig. 3A-C). In contrast, this response was significantly diminished in STING−/− mice, highlighting the critical role of STING in the retinal antiviral defense (Fig. 3A-C). In addition to IFN signaling, STING can activate autophagy and/or NF-κB pathways to defend against virus infection [4, 6, 7]. Indeed, besides IFN genes (Oas1g, Zbp1, Nlrc5) and cytosolic DNA-sensing genes (Cxcl10, Zbp1, Irf7), STING deficiency reduced the expression of autophagy genes (Mefv, Trim30a, Igtp) and NF-κB signal components (Cxcl1, Tnf, Bcl2a1a) following HSV-1 retinal infection (Fig. 3D). Tetherin (BST2) has been reported to restrict HSV-1 replication and spread in the cornea in STING-dependent manner [29]. We found that HSV-1 infection robustly induced BST2, whereas STING deficiency blunted this induction (Fig. S4A-B). Because STING is predominantly expressed in myeloid cells, we profiled these populations during HSV-1 retinitis by flow cytometry. The frequency of CD11b+CD45low resident microglia was comparable between WT and STING−/− mice (Fig. 3E–F). In contrast, STING−/− retinas showed a significant increase in CD11b+CD45high infiltrating monocytes/macrophages (Fig. 3E, G) and an expanded CD11blowCD45high leukocyte compartment (Fig. 3E, H). Thus, STING deficiency does not reduce immune cell recruitment; rather, it is associated with augmented myeloid and leukocyte infiltration at later stages of infection. STING is required for PANoptosis activation following HSV-1 retinal infection

RNA-seq analysis revealed that STING depletion globally repressed pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis in mouse retinas (Figs. 3D and 4A). These programmed cell death pathways can converge into a lytic inflammatory cell death process known as PANoptosis, which plays a key antiviral role during HSV-1 infection [17]. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, Western blot (WB) analysis confirmed that STING is essential for PANoptosis activation in the retina during HSV-1 infection, as indicated by reduced activation of pyroptotic markers (caspase-1, GSDMD), apoptotic markers (caspase-3, −7, −8) and necroptotic markers (MLKL) (Fig. 4B-D). IF analysis further showed that IBA1 + microglia/macrophages infiltrating the vitreous cavity and in the retina exhibited clear cell death in WT retinas after HSV-1 infection, whereas IBA1+/TUNEL + cells were reduced in STING−/− retinas (Fig. 4E-F). Analysis of WT retinal flat mounts revealed overlap between HSV-1 and IBA1 signals, indicating active phagocytosis of the virus by microglia/macrophages (Fig. 4G). Additionally, IBA1 levels were reduced in STING−/− retinas even without infection (Fig. 4H), suggesting that STING is essential for microglial homeostasis under normal conditions. Next, we examined PANoptosis in retinal microglia/macrophages. After HSV-1 infection, co-localization of GSDMD, cleaved caspase-3 (CC3), and P-MLKL with IBA1 was evident in WT retinas (Fig. 4I-K). In contrast, STING−/− retinas showed decreased intensity of PANoptosis markers and fewer microglia/macrophages positive for PANoptosis (Fig. 4I-K). Consistent with the findings in retinal myeloid cells, HSV-1 infection induced significant cell death in WT bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) or primary microglia (PMs), which was completely blocked by STING knockout (Fig. S5A, E). HSV-1 infection increased the proportion of PANoptosis marker-positive cells in BMDMs and PMs, whereas STING depletion markedly reduced this population (Fig. S5B-C and F-G). STING activates ZBP1 and PANoptosis through promoting Z-NA accumulation

To identify key molecules in cell death and host response, we overlapped the top 100 genes altered after HSV-1 infection in WT retinas with three gene sets: necroptosis, apoptotic process, and defense response to virus (Fig. 5A). This analysis revealed four genes in common, among which ZBP1 attracted our attention (Fig. 5A). ZBP1 plays a critical role in PANoptosome assembly and PANoptosis activation during HSV-1 infection [17]. Both ZBP1 RNA and protein levels were upregulated in WT retinas after HSV-1 infection, but significantly reduced in STING−/− retinas (Fig. 5B-C). ZBP1 can be induced by the accumulation of Z-type nucleic acids (Z-NA), including Z-DNA and Z-RNA [30]. To investigate this, we used a Z-DNA/RNA antibody to detect Z-NA. HSV-1 infection resulted in an increase proportion of Z-NA-positive PMs and BMDMs, which was diminished by STING deficiency (Fig. 5D-E, Fig. S6). To directly assess the effect of STING on Z-NA production and ZBP1 activation, we treated the BV2 mouse microglia with DMXAA, a potent STING agonist. DMXAA treatment increased Z-NA and ZBP1 signals, which were reduced when DNase I or RNase A were added, preventing Z-NA-ZBP1 speckle formation (Fig. 5F-G). DMXAA-induced cell death was also inhibited by DNase I or RNase A treatment (Fig. 5H-I). Notably, RNase A treatment more effectively suppressed Z-NA and ZBP1 signals, suggesting that STING activation promotes Z-RNA accumulation. It has been reported that ISG mRNAs with a high adenosine-to-inosine (A-I) editing index in the 3’ UTRs can form Z-RNA if not edited by ADAR1, thereby activating ZBP1 [31]. Analysis of our recent scRNA-seq data [32] revealed that activation of STING by cGAMP induced robust expression of ISGs with high editing potential in retinal microglia/macrophages (Fig. 5J), supporting the in vivo formation of Z-RNA. Finally, IF analysis demonstrated that ZBP1, STING, and Z-NA formed clear complexes after HSV-1 infection in BV2 cells (Fig. 5K-L), with co-localization areas ranging from 0.6 to 16 μm² (Fig. 5M). Depletion of ZBP1 renders mice more susceptible to HSV-1 retinal infection

The role of ZBP1 in retinal viral infections has not been previously investigated. We therefore examined the eye morphology of ZBP1−/− mice and found no significant differences in retinal structure under normal conditions (Fig. 6A-D). However, compared to WT mice, ZBP1−/− mice exhibited more severe retinal blood vessel leakage, disruption of retinal structure, and detachment even at lower viral dose (4 × 103 PFU) (Fig. 6A-D). At higher viral doses (4 × 104 PFU), ZBP1−/− mice showed complete corneal opacity, evident retinal necrosis and higher histology score at 3 dpi (Fig. 6E-F). Correspondingly, ZBP1−/− eyes had higher viral titers in the vitreous humor and greater levels of viral DNA in the retina compared with WT eyes (Fig. 6G-H). ZBP1 agonist CBL0137 defends against HSV-1 infection in vitro and in vivo

The obtained results suggest that ZBP1 acts downstream of STING in HSV-1 infection, therefore activating ZBP1 may restore antiviral effects in STING-deficient conditions. To test this, we treated BMDMs with curaxin CBL0137 (CBL), a ZBP1 agonist that promotes Z-DNA formation [31]. CBL treatment strongly suppressed HSV-1 replication in WT BMDMs, however, this suppression was significantly attenuated in Zbp1−/− BMDMs (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 7A), indicating that ZBP1 is essential for CBL’s antiviral effect. In contrast, CBL similarly reduced viral titers in both WT and STING−/− BMDMs (Fig. 7B) and induced comparable cell death post-infection (Fig. 7C), suggesting that CBL acts through ZBP1 and downstream of STING. Similar to BMDMs, in PMs, CBL also reduced viral titers in both WT and STING−/− cells (Fig. S7A-B) and induced cell death post-infection (Fig. S7C-D). Next, we assessed CBL’s antiviral effects in WT and STING−/− mice. In WT mice, IVT injection of CBL improved eye morphology, showing increased corneal opacity and reduced retinal damage after HSV-1 infection (Fig. 7D-F). Consistent with this, CBL reduced viral titers in vitreous humor (Fig. 7G-H). CBL suppressed virus replication in the early stages of infection but had little effect later (Fig. 7I), likely because the virus was already cleared by the immune response. In STING−/− mice, CBL provided more pronounced antiviral protection. Corneal ulcers and retinal necrosis were almost fully reversed (Fig. 7D-F), and viral titers and DNA were significantly reduced in both early and late infection stages (Fig. 7G-I). Finally, we treated HSV-1-infected mice with quinacrine, a structural analog of Curaxin that does not induce Z-DNA [31, 33]. In great contrast to CBL, IVT quinacrine had no protective effect on the retina following HSV-1 injection (Fig. S8). Discussion

Collectively, we identify the critical antiviral role of the STING-ZBP1-PANoptosis signaling pathway in HSV-1 retinal infection and, for the first time, demonstrate that the ZBP1 agonist may offer a potential therapeutic approach to mitigate HSV-1 retinal infection (Fig. 8).Fig. 8STING-ZBP1-PANoptosis signaling defends against HSV-1 retinal infection. Diagram showing that STING activates ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis in microglia and macrophages to inhibit HSV-1 virus load and retinal necrosis

STING-ZBP1-PANoptosis signaling defends against HSV-1 retinal infection. Diagram showing that STING activates ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis in microglia and macrophages to inhibit HSV-1 virus load and retinal necrosis

STING promotes IFN production to limit viral replication. Mice deficient in IFNAR showed increased viral load, reduced chemokines, and more severe retinal necrosis [26, 27]. Additionally, STING has antiviral functions independent of IFNs, possibly through NF-κB signaling [4] or autophagy process [34]. Our RNA-Seq results revealed activation of IFN, NF-κB, and autophagy signaling following HSV-1 retinal infection, which were reversed by STING deficiency, suggesting that STING is central to these pathways. Further studies using STING mutation mice, which abolish STING-mediated IFN production, could clarify whether STING’s antiviral role occurs through an IFN-independent mechanism.

Previous work showed that cGAS–STING signaling is required for spontaneous ZBP1 expression and necroptosis in cells lacking caspase-8 and Fas-associated death domain (FADD) [35]. Here, we identify a distinct role for STING in triggering ZBP1-dependent PANoptosis during viral infection. Based on our data, we propose three non–mutually exclusive mechanisms by which STING activates ZBP1 following HSV-1 infection. First, STING enhances ZBP1 transcription via interferon (IFN) signaling, consistent with ZBP1 being an IFN-inducible gene. Accordingly, Zbp1 mRNA and ZBP1 protein were reduced in STING-/- retinas. Second, STING activation promotes the formation of Z-NA, which then engage and activate ZBP1. Z-NA and ZBP1 can establish a positive feedback loop in which each promotes the other [36, 37]. We found that STING activation by cGAMP injection induced ISGs and likely facilitated Z-RNA formation in the retinas, and STING agonist treatment caused Z-NA accumulation in both microglia and macrophages. Third, STING may activate ZBP1 by forming a complex with ZBP1 and Z-NA. Specks containing ZBP1, Z-NA, and STING were detected after HSV-1 infection, and STING activation alone induced Z-NA-ZBP1 complexes. A recent study showed that cGAS forms a complex with ZBP1 and mitochondrial Z-DNA to enhance type I IFN signaling [38]. However, in contrast to cGAS and ZBP1 co-localized homogeneously in the cytosol, HSV-1-induced distinct speck formation between Z-NA, ZBP1, and STING in our study [38]. Whether STING similarly forms complexes with ZBP1 in response to endogenous cytosolic DNA derived from the nucleus or mitochondria remains to be determined.

We identified a novel signal axis of STING-ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis in HSV-1 retinal infection. PANoptosis, as a lytic inflammatory cells death, is a two-edged sword in virus defense [39]. ZBP1 forms PANoptosome with AIM and pyrin to defend host cells from lethal HSV-1 infection [17]. On the other side, robust secretion of cytokines and other inflammatory factors during PANoptosis can led to cell death and tissue damage [40]. IFN treatment was detrimental rather than beneficial in mice infected with β-coronavirus, whereas deletion of Zbp1 or its Zα domain which is critical for induction of PANoptosis, prevented mice from IFN-mediated lethality during β-coronavirus infection [18]. The effects of ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis on antivirus and cell death should be tightly regulated, and virus-specific immune response awaits further study. STING signaling, as a fundamental sensor of dsDNA, may serve as an upstream regulator of ZBP1, enabling it to distinguish DNA viruses like HSV-1 from RNA viruses such as β-coronaviruses. In this way, STING signaling may fine-tune the outcome of ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis. In addition, cGAS-STING regulates cell fate in HSV-1 viral dose-dependent manner, with higher dosage triggers cGAS-dependent cell apoptosis but lower dosage led to IFN-I responses [41]. Future study using transcriptomic or proteomic analyses could help identify downstream targets of STING-ZBP1 that contribute to the differential immune response to DNA versus RNA viruses, and to various viral dose.

Currently, there is no specific symptomatic treatment for ARN, with broad-spectrum antivirals like ganciclovir and acyclovir being the primary options. In this study, we tested the potential antiviral effects of the ZBP1 antagonist CBL0137 in HSV-1 retinal infection. CBL0137, currently in phase I clinical trials as an antitumor drug (clinical trial #NCT01905228), has shown good tolerance in cancer patients. It was initially identified as an inhibitor of the histone chaperone FACT (Facilitates Chromatin Transcription) by intercalating into DNA and trapping FACT, leading to inhibition of MYC, NF-kB, and HSF1, and activation of p53 [42]. Recently, CBL0137 was found to induce Z-DNA formation, thereby activating ZBP1 [31]. While preparing this manuscript, Liu et al. reported that CBL0137 improved survival in mice with lethal HSV-1 infection by inhibiting FACT, although specific organs were not analyzed [43]. Together with our findings, these results suggest that CBL0137 may be a promising strategy for ARN treatment.

Finally, this study has several limitations. First, IVT HSV-1 inoculation reproduces only a subset of the clinical features of ARN. Although infection induced evident retinitis, vasculitis, and neuroinflammation, the hallmark peripheral, multifocal patches of full-thickness retinal necrosis were not observed, and the retinal whitening seen in our study may not fully correspond to that observed in patients. Moreover, it remains unclear whether the full-thickness necrosis detected in STING−/− or ZBP1−/− mice faithfully recapitulates human ARN. Second, HSV-1 gB immunoreactivity was predominantly detected in the inner retina, which may reflect the injection route rather than intrinsic viral tropism. Unilateral ocular inoculation can lead to retinal necrosis in the contralateral, uninoculated eye [28]. Systematic assessment of contralateral eye involvement in WT, STING−/−, and ZBP1−/− mice should help elucidate the routes of HSV-1 spread within the visual pathway and the contribution of STING-ZBP1 pathways. Finally, the retinal neuronal populations most susceptible to HSV-1 are not yet defined. Single-cell RNA sequencing could help identify the infected cell types and clarify cell-type–specific responses.

Materials and methods

Mice

Tmem173 (Sting)-knockout (KO) mice (C57BL/6J background; Catalogue No. NM-KO-2105967) were obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Zbp1-KO mice (C57BL/6J background; Strain No. T029037) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice were provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-Sen University. Age-matched male and female mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to experimental groups. All mice were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-Sen University (Approval No. Z2024115).

In vivo HSV-1 Infection and drug administration

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (70 µL/10 g body weight in normal saline). Intravitreal (IVT) injections were performed as previously described [32]. Briefly, mouse pupils were dilated with 1–2 drops of tropicamide-phenylephrine eye drops, and corneas were lubricated with hypromellose gel. HSV-1 (KOS strain) or PBS (vehicle control) was injected intravitreally (1 µL/eye) using a Hamilton microsyringe. For the mock infection, HSV-1 was first inactivated by incubating it at 100 °C for 1 h. For combination treatment, 10 mg of CBL0137 (Topscience) or quinacrine (MedChemExpress, USA) was dissolved in 5 mL of PBS via ultrasonication (37 °C, 1 min) and mixed with HSV-1 to achieve a final concentration of 2 mg/mL CBL0137 or quinacrine and 4 × 10⁴ PFU/mL HSV-1. The mixture was co-administered intravitreally (2 µL/eye). Control groups received equivalent volumes of PBS.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus imaging and Slit-lamp examination

After anesthesia and pupil dilation, retinal morphology was assessed using the Heidelberg Spectralis OCT system (Phoenix Micron IV) as previously described [32]. Fundus imaging was performed with the Micron IV retinal imaging system (Phoenix Research Laboratories). For fluorescein angiography, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 2% fluorescein sodium (5 µL/g; Alcon Laboratories), and images were acquired immediately.

For slit-lamp examination, slit-lamp biomicroscope (Topcon Healthcare, SL-D701) equipped with a ×10–×16 magnification objective lens and adjustable slit width (0.1–2.0 mm) was used. A cobalt blue filter and diffuser were applied for enhanced visualization of corneal lesions. Light intensity was reduced to 30% of the maximum output to avoid phototoxicity. The slit beam was angled at 45°–60° to assess epithelial defects, stromal opacity, and neovascularization. Digital images were captured using a slit-lamp-mounted camera with consistent exposure settings (ISO 200, shutter speed 1/60 s). Mice received topical antibiotic ointment (Tobramycin) to prevent infection and were monitored until full recovery from anesthesia.

Single-Cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

The raw data was obtained from our previous results (NCBI SRA database, accession: PRJNA1103951) [32]. Single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10X Genomics Chromium system. Sequencing data were analyzed using Seurat (v4.3.0) with the following parameters. Quality control: Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 9,000 detected genes or >15% mitochondrial reads were excluded. Normalization: the Log Normalize method (scale factor = 10,000) was applied to 2,000 highly variable genes. Doublet removal: doublets were identified and excluded using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3; expected rate = 10%).

Batch correction: principal component analysis (PCA; 20 PCs) and Harmony (v0.1.0) integration were applied. Clustering: the Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.8) was used, followed by UMAP visualization. Annotation: cell identities were assigned using the CellMarker database [44]. Microglia/macrophages were identified by the co-expression of P2ry12, Cx3cr1, Aif1, and Cd68.Interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression (e.g., Slfn5, Igap1, Fndc3a) was analyzed using violin plots (Kruskal-Wallis test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and analysis

Total RNA from paired retinas was extracted using TRIzol™. Library preparation and sequencing were performed on an Illumina platform (Accession: PRJNA1244932). The following pipeline was used for data preprocessing. Adapter trimming: trim Galore (v1.18). Alignment: HISAT2 (v2.2.1; GRCm39 reference genome). Quantification: featureCounts (v2.0.1; FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (|fold change|≥ 2, FDR ≤ 0.05). Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

BMDMs were aseptically isolated from the femurs and tibias of mice by flushing with cold PBS using a 25-gauge needle. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysis buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) for 2 min at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 300 ×g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in complete BMDM medium (DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant M-CSF) and plated in non-tissue culture-treated Petri dishes. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 7 days, with medium replaced on day 3. Differentiated BMDMs were detached with Accutase (Stemcell) on day 7 and re-plated for following experiments.

Isolation and culture of primary mouse microglia

Primary microglia (PMs) were isolated from postnatal day 1–3 (P1–P3) mouse pups. Following euthanasia, the cerebral cortices were dissected, mechanically minced, and digested in 0.125% (w/v) trypsin for 45 min at 37 °C. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. Pelleted cells were resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and seeded into poly-L-lysine-coated T75 flasks. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, the medium was replaced to remove non-adherent cells. Cultures were maintained with medium changes every 3 days until confluency was reached (~ 15 days). Microglia were detached by orbital shaking at 180 rpm for 2 h at 37 °C, collected by centrifugation, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and replated for experiments at 5 × 10⁴ cells/cm².

Cell culture and treatmentThree murine ARN models

BMDMs were seeded in 12-well plates (2 × 10⁵ cells/well) and infected with HSV-1 (strain KOS; MOI = 10) for 24 h. For CBL0137 treatment, BMDMs were infected with HSV-1 (MOI = 10) for 2 h, washed with PBS, and then treated with 1 µM CBL0137 (MedChemExpress) for 6 h. PMs were seeded in 12-well plates at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well and infected with HSV-1 (MOI = 10) for 24 h. For drug treatment, PMs were infected for 2 h, washed with PBS, and subsequently treated with 1 µM CBL0137 for 12 h. BV2 cells were maintained in DMEM (10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were treated with 20 µg/mL STING agonist DMXAA (Selleck) in combination with PBS, 10 µg/mL DNase I, or 100 U/mL RNase A for 6 h. Vero cells were cultured in Vero-specific medium and used for plaque assays to determine viral titers.

Live and dead cell assay

After treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with 1 µM Calcein-AM (live cells, green fluorescence) and 1 µM propidium iodide (PI) (dead cells, red fluorescence) in PBS for 20 min at 37 °C. Cells were gently washed with PBS and immediately imaged using a high-content imaging system (PerkinElmer Opera Phenix) with a 20× objective. Images were acquired for PI, Calcein-AM and bright field.

Plaque assay

Virus titers were determined by plaque assay on Vero E6 cells. Viral stocks were serially diluted 10-fold in DMEM containing 2% FBS. Vitreous humor samples (pooled from 3 to 4 eyes per sample) were collected with a microsyringe, clarified by centrifugation, and 10 µL was brought to 200 µL with DMEM prior adding to Vero cells. Cell culture supernatants were mixed 1:1 with DMEM prior to adding to Vero cells. Confluent Vero monolayers in 12-well plates (2 × 10⁵ cells/well, seeded 24 h prior) were washed with PBS and inoculated with 100 µL of diluted sample supplemented with 150 µL DMEM containing 2% FBS. After adsorption at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 2 h with gentle rocking every 15 min, the inoculum was removed and cells were overlaid with 2 mL plaque medium, prepared as a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of 2× minimum essential medium (MEM) and 2% (w/v) methylcellulose. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, monolayers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (500 µL/well, 1 h, room temperature) and washed three times with distilled water. Plaques were visualized by staining with 0.2% crystal violet in 20% methanol for 5 min at room temperature, followed by gentle rinsing. Distinct plaques were manually counted, and viral titers were expressed as plaque-forming units (PFU) per milliliter.

Immunocytochemistry

Sterile 12-mm round glass coverslips (Biosharp) were pre-placed in 24-well culture plates (Corning). After treatment, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS and fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 10 min at RT. Fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (vol/vol) in PBS (PBST) for 30 min at RT. Non-specific binding were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Boster) in PBST for 30 min at RT. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer (1:200) and applied to coverslips overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After three 5-min PBS washes, cells were incubated Alexa Fluor®-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen) and 1 µg/mL DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) for nuclear counterstaining for 2 h at RT. Images were acquired using an LSM980 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and in situ terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays

The experiments were conducted as previously described [32]. The enucleated eyeballs were fixed in FAS eye fixative overnight. Following fixation, the eyes were dehydrated in ethanol, cleared with xylene and then processed for paraffin embedding. Retinal paraffin sections, cut at a thickness of 10 μm along the superior and inferior retinal meridians, were used for subsequent experiments. The sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and then subjected to antigen retrieval by incubation in sodium citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. The TUNEL assay was performed on the paraffin sections using the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit. Stained retinal sections were imaged using a TissueFAXS microscopy.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining of cryosections and retinal flat mounts

The enucleated eyeballs were dissected in PBS to remove excess connective tissue. A small cut was made in the cornea of each eye prior to fixation in 4% PFA at RT for 30 min. The eyes were then cryoprotected in solutions of 15% sucrose in 0.1 M PBS for 1 h, followed by 30% sucrose at 4℃ overnight. The eyes were subsequently embedded in optimal cutting temperature medium (OCT). Retinal cryosections, cut at 10 μm thickness along the superior and inferior retinal meridians, were subjected to IF analysis as we previously described [12, 32]. Images were acquired using an LSM980 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

IF analysis of retinal flat mounts was conducted as previously described [32]. Briefly, enucleated eye balls were fixed in 4% PFA for 5 min. Then retinas were dissected and fixed in 4% PFA for additional 30 min, and washed with PBS for three times. The retinas were permeabilized by 0.3% PBST (0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 20 min, and blocked with 2.5% BSA (prepared in PBST) for 30 min. The retinas were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4℃ and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. After washing with PBS, the retinas were mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium. Images were acquired using the LSM980 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Fluorescence imaging and intensity adjustment

For each IF image processing, exposure time and detector voltage were first adjusted using the corresponding isotype IgG control, and all experimental sections were subsequently imaged under identical conditions as their matched controls. Image contrast was standardized by adjusting the black and white thresholds in ZEN software (Zeiss, Germany) to ensure consistency across samples. All original ZEN image files have been uploaded to the https://figshare.com/s/f60d58db2ca44f84f7cc.

Flow cytometry analysis of retinal cells

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from freshly isolated mouse retinas by enzymatic–mechanical dissociation. Retinal tissues were digested in 2% (w/v) papain (Worthington Biochemical, USA) dissolved in cell strainer buffer (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] supplemented with 1 mM EDTA and 2% fetal bovine serum [FBS]) for 20 min at 37 °C, with gentle trituration using fire-polished Pasteur pipettes every 5 min. Dissociated cells were filtered through a 100-µm nylon mesh, pelleted by centrifugation (1,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and resuspended in cold PBS. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature (22–25 °C), followed by quenching with 0.1 M glycine for 5 min. After two washes in PBS, cells were resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS and 0.1% sodium azide). Fc receptors were blocked using anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (clone 93; BioLegend, USA) at 1:100 dilution for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (Table S1) diluted in FACS buffer for 1 h at 4 °C under light-protected conditions. Prior to acquisition, cell suspensions were passed through a 40-µm cell strainer to ensure single-cell integrity. Flow cytometry was performed on a calibrated cytometer using compensation beads, acquiring ≥ 300,000 events per sample. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.9.0, BD Biosciences, USA).

Western blot (WB) analysis

Mouse retinas were homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein lysates were processed using a high-speed tissue grinder, sonicated (EpiSonic 2000; amplitude: 60%, 3 s on/off for 1 min), and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were mixed with SDS sample buffer, heated at 95 °C for 10 min to denature the proteins. For WB analysis, 10–15 µg of total protein was used. Antibodies used are listed in the Table S1. The uncropped blots images were shown in Fig. S9.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Genomic DNA was extracted from retinal tissues and cultured cells using the Genomic DNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression was quantified using ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a LightCycler® 480 System II (Roche). Each 10 µL reaction contained 10 ng of template DNA, 0.5 µM forward/reverse primers, and 1× PreMix. Conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 15 min (initial denaturation); 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; followed by melting curve analysis (65–95 °C, 0.5 °C/sec increment). Cycle threshold (Ct) values were normalized to the endogenous reference gene Actb (β-actin) using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate (technical replicates), and results represent three independent biological experiments. The following primer sequences were used: mouse Actb (F: GCTCCTCCTGAGCGCAAG, R: CATCTGCTGGAAGGTGGACA), HSV-1 UL43 (F: TGGTATTGCCCAACACTTTCC, R: GCGCCAGGCACACACAT), HSV-1 ICP27 (F: TCCGACAGCGATCTGGAC, R: TCCGACGAGGAACACTCC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis results are expressed as mean±SD. GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software (GraphPad software, Inc., La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analysis. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Multiple-group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant where, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.
