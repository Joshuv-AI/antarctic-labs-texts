Digital addiction on depression, anxiety, stress and loneliness in healthcare professionals: cross-sectional study
Authors: Kadriye Olğaç Ak, İbrahim Dağ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDigital dependence is an emerging behavioral concern characterized by excessive and uncontrolled use of digital devices, which may adversely affect mental health. This study examined the association of digital addiction with depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness among healthcare professionals.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted between April 15 and July 30, 2025, with 371 healthcare professionals selected via computer-assisted simple random sampling from two hospitals, considering different shift schedules. Data were collected face-to-face using a socio-demographic form, Digital Addiction Scale, DASS-21, and UCLA Loneliness Scale. SPSS 25 was used for analysis, applying descriptive statistics and parametric tests after normality checks.ResultsA total of 371 healthcare professionals participated (57% female; mean daily digital use = 4.44 ± 2.77 h). Digital addiction showed positive correlations with loneliness (r = 0.152; 95% CI: 0.05–0.25), anxiety (r = 0.167; 95% CI: 0.07–0.26), depression (r = 0.168; 95% CI: 0.07–0.26), and stress (r = 0.202; 95% CI: 0.11–0.30) (all p < 0.01). In regression models, loneliness (β = 0.152, p = 0.003) predicted higher digital addiction scores (R² = 0.023), and device-use duration (β = 0.256, p < 0.001) emerged as the strongest correlate of digital addiction (model R² = 0.086). Loneliness and digital addiction together explained 21–23% of variance in anxiety (R² = 0.235), depression (R² = 0.234), and stress (R² = 0.211) symptoms. It has been determined that the 8.6% change in healthcare workers’ digital addiction scores can be explained by the scores of the variables Age, Number of Children, Years in the profession, Years in the unit, and Duration of digital device use. The most significant variable was found to be the duration of digital device.ConclusionsDigital addiction was significantly associated with higher anxiety, depression, and stress among healthcare professionals. Longer device use increased addiction risk. Awareness programs on healthy digital media use and psychosocial support interventions are recommended.LimitationsThe cross-sectional design, self-report measures, and two-hospital sample limit causal inference and generalizability. A potential limitation of the sampling process was the challenge of reaching randomly selected participants across varying shift schedules, although efforts were made to ensure coverage.

Introduction

The rapid proliferation of digital media use has raised growing concerns about its psychological effects on individuals, especially in occupational settings. While digital tools offer convenience and instant access to information, their excessive and uncontrolled use has led to a phenomenon known as digital addiction — a behavioral pattern marked by compulsive engagement with digital devices despite negative consequences [1, 2].

Digital addiction is defined as a way of behaviour characterized by individuals’ using digital devices in an uncontrolled and dysfunctional manner [2]. Uncontrolled digital media use stands out as a global scale problem [3]. According to the “Internet and Social Media User Statistics” report, more than 66% of the world population are internet users, while 62.3% are social media users and 69.4% are mobile device users [4]. Recent surveys show that among healthcare professionals, for instance, 87.9% reported social media use and approximately 1 h/day usage, while in another study 71.2% reported using social media—with 65.7% for both personal and professional purposes [5]. Research reports that when combined with a sedentary life style, prolonged use of digital devices lead to negative consequences on mental health in addition to physical health [6]. With the popularization of the internet and social media, individuals develop digital addiction and this leads to various conduct disorders [7]. Prolonged digital media use is associated with social isolation, low quality of sleep and mental health problems [8–11]. Negative related to digital addiction may also be seen in healthcare professionals.

Digital addiction is increasingly recognized as a public health issue, especially among professionals working in high-stress environments. Healthcare professionals, in particular, face a unique risk for developing digital addiction. Their exposure to occupational stress, emotional exhaustion, and irregular work hours often leads them to rely on digital devices for temporary relief [12, 13]. However, recent studies suggest that this coping strategy may result in increased symptoms of depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness [14–16]. Despite these emerging risks, there remains a lack of research focusing specifically on digital addiction among healthcare professionals. Addressing this gap is crucial for both individual mental health and the overall quality of healthcare services.

Healthcare professionals often turn to digital platforms as a means to alleviate stress, manage information overload, and cope with emotional fatigue caused by demanding workloads and shift-based schedules. Prior studies have shown that this population frequently uses digital media for stress relief, communication, and even clinical decision support, but such patterns may lead to problematic use or digital addiction when boundaries are not maintained [12–14]. Healthcare professionals commonly use platforms such as WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, and YouTube for both personal and professional purposes [17]. While excessive use has been linked to psychosocial risks like loneliness and emotional exhaustion, recent studies also indicate that browsing humorous content or connecting with peers may temporarily reduce stress and improve mood [18, 19]. Therefore, the psychological outcomes of social media use may vary depending on the purpose, duration, and emotional context of engagement.

In a study by Buneviciene & Bunevicius (2021), digital addiction was reported to be associated with increased clinical problems and negative mental health outcomes [13]. In a study by El-Sayed & Goda (2024), it was reported that in addition to being associated with various mental problems, digital addiction may also lead to deficiencies in the provision of health care in healthcare professionals [14]. In this context, examining depression, anxiety, stress and loneliness caused by addiction is very important in terms of increasing the productivity of healthcare professionals and the quality of care [15, 16].

However, there are limited number of studies in literature examining the association of digital addiction specifically on the psychological health of healthcare professionals. The present study aims to examine the association of digital addiction on depression, anxiety, stress and loneliness in healthcare professionals.

Method

Study design

The present study is a descriptive, cross-sectional, and quantitative research conducted to examine the associations between digital addiction and psychological symptoms. The data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software. After testing for normality using skewness and kurtosis values, parametric statistical analyses such as Pearson correlation, multiple linear regression, and path analysis were performed to determine the relationships among variables.

Place and time of research

The study was carried out in two hospitals between 15.04.2025 and 30.06.2025 after ethics committee approval and required institutional permission were received.

Population and sample of research

The study population consisted of a total of 2,399 healthcare professionals actively working in public hospitals under the Ministry of Health, namely Mardin Training and Research Hospital and Kızıltepe State Hospital. A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1). To detect a medium effect size (f² = 0.15) with 80% power and a significance level of 0.05, the required minimum sample size for multiple linear regression with five predictors was calculated as 92 participants.

A total of 220 participants were selected from Mardin Training and Research Hospital and 151 from Kızıltepe State Hospital. Both hospitals are public institutions serving similar socio-demographic populations and employ healthcare professionals with comparable roles and work conditions.

The study was conducted in two hospital settings (Mardin Training and Research Hospital and Kızıltepe State Hospital). To ensure representativeness from both settings, the sampling process was carried out separately within each hospital. Lists of eligible healthcare professionals were obtained from each institution, and participants were selected using computer-assisted simple random sampling proportionally based on the size of each hospital. This approach ensured that respondents from both settings were randomly selected and adequately represented in the final sample. Researchers visited both hospitals during different shifts to ensure accessibility to selected participants. Healthcare professionals are more frequently exposed to stress factors as a result of working in shifts, heavy workload and professional responsibilities that require high level of emotional labour, causing a risk in terms of digital addiction by increasing tendency for digital environment. For these reasons, it was deemed appropriate to form the sample group of the study from healthcare professionals.

Measurement tools

The surveys used in the study were ‘Socio-Demographic Characteristics Form’, ‘Digital Addiction Scale for Healthcare Professionals’, ‘Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale (DASS-21)’ and ‘UCLA Loneliness Scale’ and the total number of questions was 87.

Socio-demographic characteristics form

This form includes 16 questions on socio-demographic characteristics (such as age, gender, marital status, number of children, education level, professional experience, current unit, and type of hospital) and digital device usage patterns (such as duration, purpose, and type of device used) of healthcare professionals.

Digital addiction scale for healthcare professionals

This scale developed by Karaşin et al. (2023) was prepared to find out the digital addiction levels of healthcare professionals. The Digital Addiction Scale for Healthcare Professionals consists of 30 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Total scores range from 30 to 150, with higher scores indicating greater digital addiction tendencies. No established clinical cut-off values exist; therefore, higher continuous scores reflect higher addiction levels. The Turkish validated version was used in this study. The scale has six factors: items 1–8 represent “behaviour change” factor, items 9–12 represent negative side factor, items 13–19 represent digital need factor, items 20–23 represent overuse factor, items 24–27 represent daily impact factor and items 28–30 represent feeling forced factor. The validity of the scale was confirmed through content and construct validity analyses conducted during its development. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) supported the six-factor structure, and item-factor loadings were found to be acceptable. The internal consistency of the developed scale was found to be very high, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.929 [20].

Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale (DASS-21)

DASS-21 was developed originally as 42 items by Lovibond & Lovibond (1995) and then adapted as 21 items by Brown et al. (1997) [21, 22]. Its Turkish adaptation and reliability-validity study was conducted by Yılmaz et al. (2017). The DASS-21 consists of 21 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3). Subscale scores range from 0 to 21 for depression, anxiety, and stress. In the present study, raw DASS-21 scores were used without multiplying by two, following the scoring approach applied in the Turkish adaptation study. Cut-off points recommended for Turkish samples (≥ 5 for depression, ≥ 4 for anxiety, ≥ 8 for stress) were used to indicate clinically relevant symptoms. Higher scores reflect greater symptom severity. The validity of the Turkish version of the scale was confirmed through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), which supported the three-factor model of depression, anxiety, and stress. Factor loadings and fit indices were found to be within acceptable limits. Internal consistency coefficients of the scale were found as 0.819 for depression, 0.808 for anxiety and 0.755 for stress [23].

The DASS-21 is a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic tool; therefore, it was used in this study to assess the severity of psychological symptoms (depression, anxiety, and stress) rather than to establish clinical diagnoses. Its use is appropriate for identifying symptom levels and examining their associations with digital addiction in non-clinical populations such as healthcare professionals.

UCLA loneliness scale

UCLA (University of California, Los Angeles) Loneliness Scale developed originally by Russell, Peplau and Ferguson (1978) is a 4 Likert type and 20 item scale [24]. The scale was revised in 1980 and 1996 [25, 26]. and Turkish adaptation, validity and reliability studies were conducted by Demir (1989). The UCLA Loneliness Scale (Version 3) includes 20 items scored 1–4, with total scores ranging from 20 to 80. Higher scores indicate greater perceived loneliness. This study used the Turkish validated version, and no categorical cut-off was applied; scores were analyzed continuously. Half of the items are positive statements, while the other half are negative. Reversely scored items (1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 15, 16, 19, 20) are reversed while scoring. Directly scored items are 2, 3, 4, 7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17 and 18. Possible total score of the scale is between 20 and 80. The validity of the Turkish version was established through content validation and exploratory factor analysis by Demir (1989), which confirmed the single-factor structure of the scale. Cronbach alpha internal consistency coefficient was found as 0.96, while test-retest reliability coefficient was found as 0.94 [27].

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through the face-to-face survey method. After obtaining the necessary ethical and institutional permissions, the researchers visited the healthcare institutions and explained the study to the participants. Informed consent was obtained, and participants were given printed copies of the survey forms.

Duration and setting

The data collection process was carried out between April 15 and July 30, 2025. The average time required to complete the survey was approximately 20–25 min. Surveys were completed in break rooms or administrative offices to ensure privacy and minimize interruptions.

Statistical analysis

The data included in the study were analysed using SPSS version 25.0. The normality of the data was assessed through skewness and kurtosis values. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used. Since the data met the assumptions for normal distribution, parametric tests were applied. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage) were used to summarize demographic and scale-related variables. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between digital addiction and psychological variables (depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness). In addition, multiple linear regression and path analysis were performed to assess predictive relationships and indirect effects between variables. A PATH analysis was performed for the research. It is detailed in the data analysis section. The model fit indices were found to be acceptable. The model fit indices are listed below. CMIN = 0.011 DF = 1 CMIN/DF = 0.011, RMSEA = 0.084 CFI = 1.000, NFI = 0.967, GFI = 0.925, and TLI = 1.000.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was received from ‘Mardin Artuklu Non-interventional Ethics Committee’ (25.03.2025 dated and 2025/3–14 numbered). Required legal permission was received from the related institution. Informed consent was taken from all participants who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. The present study was prepared in accordance with the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration.

It was found that 57% of the healthcare professionals included in the study were female, 31% were nurses, 24% were doctors and 63% had an undergraduate level of education. It was found that 37% had a level of income lower than expense, 60% were married and 59% were working at Mardin Training and Research Hospital. Of the participants, 22% worked in outpatient clinics, 18% worked in the emergency department and 17% worked in other units. It was found that 64% of the nurses chose their profession willingly, while 23% started working due to economic reasons. It was also found that 54% of the participants had night shifts. It was reported that 96% of the digital devices used were mobile phones and of these devices, 65% were used for social media, 23% were used for communication and 8% were used for watching film/series/video (Table 1).

Among the participants, 96% reported using mobile phones as their primary digital device. Regarding usage purpose, 65% of participants indicated using digital devices primarily for social media platforms, including WhatsApp, Instagram, YouTube, Facebook, and TikTok. These platforms were often accessed for both personal and stress-relief purposes during breaks or after shifts.

Discussion

This study’s findings contribute to a growing body of literature indicating that excessive digital media use among healthcare professionals may have serious psychological consequences. The results found show that digital addiction may have significant association on psychological states of healthcare professionals.

The results that almost all of the participants (96%) used mobile phone and 65% used these devices for social media shows that social media based addiction is dominant. As emphasized in the studies of Peper & Harvey (2018) and Üzgü et al. (2023), this finding shows that the time individuals spend on social media platforms have a direct association on psychological health [6, 11]. The increase in social media use triggers instant gratification, the need for approval and comparison behaviours in individuals and this can lead to loneliness, low self-esteem and stress [28, 29]. In this context, not only total screen time, but also for what purposes the screen is used emerges as an important related to in terms of psychological outcomes. Although this study did not assess which specific social media platforms were used, the high overall prevalence of mobile phone and social media use may reflect attempts at emotional regulation. However, without platform-level analysis, no conclusions can be drawn regarding the psychological effects of specific applications.

The dominance of social media use, particularly via mobile phones, highlights the risk of social media–based digital addiction. Previous studies suggest that excessive use of these platforms may lead to increased symptoms of loneliness, anxiety, depression, and stress. For instance, Elhai et al. (2017) demonstrated that problematic smartphone use was significantly associated with elevated levels of anxiety and depression [30]. Similarly, Boursier et al. (2020) reported that excessive social media use during periods of isolation predicted higher levels of loneliness and psychological distress [31]. Panova and Carbonell (2018) emphasized that screen time, especially on mobile devices, can be a critical marker of behavioral addiction [32]. Naslund et al. (2020) and Twenge et al. (2017) further noted the dual nature of social media: while offering temporary emotional relief, it often exacerbates feelings of isolation and mental health burden when overused [17, 33].

Our findings are in line with this literature, indicating that social media platforms such as WhatsApp, Instagram, and YouTube represent the most frequent and potentially problematic digital use among healthcare professionals. This pattern suggests a risk for maladaptive coping mechanisms in high-stress occupational settings, reinforcing the need for institutional awareness and intervention programs [6, 12, 16].

The results of the study showed positive and significant correlation between digital addiction scores of the participants and their loneliness, anxiety, depression and stress levels. This finding is consistent with previous studies that digital addiction can have a negative association on psychological well-being [11, 29, 30]. El-Sayed & Goda (2024) reported that digital addiction in healthcare professionals may cause not only psychological association in healthcare professionals, but also negative association on the quality of service [14]. Although the shift of healthcare professionals to digital devices is considered to be a relaxing coping method in the short term, it may cause an increase in depression, anxiety and loneliness levels by increasing mental load in the long term [34, 35]. In this context, results of the study suggest that digital addiction should be regarded both individually and organizationally. Although digital addiction is often associated with younger populations, its psychological impact on adults — particularly healthcare professionals — is increasingly recognized [30, 35]. In high-stress clinical environments, healthcare workers may turn to digital devices as a coping mechanism to manage emotional fatigue and professional strain. This repeated reliance can evolve into problematic use, especially when combined with limited offline social engagement and irregular work schedules. These behaviors may intensify feelings of isolation, emotional exhaustion, and poor mental well-being. Therefore, digital dependency in adults may not be a cause in isolation but part of a broader pattern of maladaptive coping in response to occupational stress. In addition to digital addiction, previous research has identified several other factors that may contribute to the development of loneliness, anxiety, worry, and depression in healthcare professionals. These include high workload, frequent night shifts, emotional exhaustion, lack of institutional support, burnout, and limited social relationships [16, 26, 30]. While the present study focused on digital addiction, we acknowledge that these additional factors may interact and influence psychological outcomes. Future research should consider a broader range of occupational and psychosocial variables to build more comprehensive models of mental health in healthcare settings. This recommendation is based on the relatively low explanatory power of the current regression model (R² = 0.086), suggesting that additional factors may influence digital addiction and psychological outcomes. Future studies may include variables such as burnout, job stress, workload intensity, shift patterns, sleep quality, coping strategies, perceived social support, and organizational climate to better understand these relationships.

The result that the level of loneliness predicts digital addiction significantly shows that digital media use is insufficient in meeting the social connection needs of individuals. The predictive role of loneliness in digital addiction reinforces theories of compensatory internet use, where individuals turn to digital platforms to fulfill unmet social needs. However, such use may paradoxically increase feelings of isolation. The significant predictive association of UCLA Loneliness Scale indicates that loneliness is a cycle that feeds digital addiction behaviours. Frequent use of digital media platforms such as social media does not cause permanent social satisfaction in individuals, on the contrary, it may increase the feeling of loneliness [28]. Boursier et al. (2020) showed that using too much social media during the COVID-19 isolation period increased loneliness and anxiety [31]. Similarly, Ime et al. (2024) also found significant relationship between digital addiction and loneliness, being shy and social anxiety [9]. These results highlight the negative association of digital media use styles on mental health. Although some of the referenced studies (e.g., Boursier et al., 2020) were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, the present study was conducted in a post-pandemic context. These references are used to illustrate the broader relationship between digital media use and mental health symptoms, regardless of pandemic conditions.

The observed strong interrelations among depression, anxiety, and stress align with the DASS-21 structure and reflect a cumulative psychological burden commonly seen in high-demand professions. DASS-21 is a measurement tool developed by Lovibond & Lovibond (1995) which assumes that these three structures often occur together [21]. Elhai et al. (2017) reported that digital addiction was effective on all of these symptom clusters and especially intense social media use increased depressive symptoms, anxiety and stress levels [30]. Therefore, digital addiction among healthcare professionals may lead not only to individual psychological disorders, but also to the co-occurrence of these symptoms.

PATH analysis results show that loneliness affects the levels of anxiety, depression and stress both directly and through digital addiction. This result shows that loneliness has indirect association through digital behaviours in addition to its direct association on mental health of individuals. Russell (1996) and Demir (1989) stated that when the feeling of loneliness becomes chronic, individuals may turn to digital devices and search for temporary relief; however, this increases mental symptoms in the long term [26, 27]. Boursier et al. (2020) emphasized that social isolation increased loneliness during the COVID-19 process and this in turn triggered social media addiction [31]. Similarly, Twenge et al. (2017) emphasized that digital media use increased the symptoms of loneliness and depression and this relationship could lead to serious mental consequences especially in young adults [33]. Specifically, Buneviciene & Bunevicius (2021) showed that digital addiction was commonly and frequently correlated with depressive symptoms in healthcare professionals. Work load, intense shifts and emotionally demanding tasks have turned digital environment into an escape, which increases addiction and further exacerbates psychological stress [13]. These pathways should be interpreted as hypothesized mechanisms, rather than confirmed causal processes. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are required to test whether loneliness leads to increased digital use, which in turn exacerbates psychological symptoms.

The finding that usage duration was the strongest predictor of digital addiction aligns with behavioral addiction models, where time spent is a critical marker of dependency. This result is in parallel with the results of Panova & Carbonell (2018) which showed positive correlation between screen time and digital addiction [32]. Alan et al. (2022) emphasized that the level of digital addiction in healthcare professionals could have a direct association on work performance and stress levels [16]. The work load of healthcare professionals and the fact that they work in shifts increases the use of digital device as a way to escape and relax, which may lead to addiction in the long term [12]. The fact that healthcare professionals turn to digital devices during the process of coping with professional stress suggests that they want to relax especially through social media use. However, the negative association of its long term use on mental health shows that this approach is not sustainable [36].

According to regression analysis, the explanatory power of the model that explains digital addiction is relatively low (R²=0.086). This suggests that the factors that affect digital addiction are not limited to only socio-demographic variables, but many other psychological, environmental and organizational variables may also be effective. Factors that are reported to affect digital addiction include psychosocial factors such as low self-esteem, lack of social support, burnout and professional dissatisfaction [29, 35]. Therefore, digital addiction should be addressed as a multidimensional phenomenon and more comprehensive models should be developed. This aligns with behavioral research, in which digital behaviors are influenced by multiple personal, occupational, and environmental variables that extend beyond sociodemographic characteristics. In the present study, the regression model explaining digital addiction demonstrated a modest explanatory power (R² = 0.086). This finding indicates that although age, number of children, years in the profession, years in the unit, and duration of digital device use collectively contribute to digital addiction scores, a substantial portion of the variance remains influenced by other psychosocial and organizational factors not captured in the current model. Such levels of explained variance are common in behavioral and psychosocial research, where complex human behaviors are shaped by multiple interacting determinants. Moreover, multicollinearity analysis showed that the highest VIF value (4.3) remained below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating that the independent variables did not exhibit problematic overlap. Therefore, the model provides meaningful but limited insight into the predictors of digital addiction. Future studies should incorporate broader variables—such as burnout, job stress, coping strategies, shift workload, and organizational climate—as these factors are closely associated with digital addiction behaviors in healthcare professionals. These variables may influence individuals’ tendency to use digital devices as a coping mechanism, thereby contributing to problematic use patterns and affecting psychological outcomes.

As a result of the study, it can be seen that although digital addiction levels of individuals are not low, this creates a risky profile when evaluated with loneliness and psychological symptoms. this creates a risky profile when evaluated with loneliness and psychological symptoms. This pattern points to a concerning psychological risk profile among healthcare workers, suggesting a need for digital wellness programs and proactive mental health monitoring within institutions. In particular, the widespread use of mobile phone (96%) and the prevalence of social media use (65%) indicate that individuals use digital devices intensively to meet their social needs. This is in parallel with the results of Üzgü et al.’s (2023) study that social media based digital addiction may be more common in high stress professions such as healthcare professionals [6]. However, there is strong evidence that the feeling of loneliness increases as the frequency of social media use increases [37]. In addition, it can be seen that most of the participants (54%) have night shifts and a significant part (59%) works in training and research hospital. Night shifts may disrupt sleep patterns and therefore cause an increase in digital media use [34]. In addition, type of hospital and the differences in the workload of the unit may affect using digital devices. For example, healthcare professionals who work in emergency service or intensive care may prefer relaxation by spending time in social media more frequently due to their high levels of stress [16].

In a study by Buneviciene & Bunevicius (2021), it was reported that digital addiction was very common among healthcare professionals and this addiction was usually accompanied by depression, anxiety and burnout [13]. While such studies confirm the psychological risks associated with digital addiction, the present study extends the literature by examining a large, randomly selected sample of healthcare professionals from two hospitals. Furthermore, it is one of the few studies to apply path analysis to explore the mediating role of digital addiction between loneliness and psychological symptoms. This approach allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the indirect pathways through which digital behavior affects mental health. El-Sayed & Goda (2024) emphasized that digital addition may cause not only individual mental health issues, but also a decrease in the quality of patient care and job neglect [14]. These results support the inference in the present study that digital addiction creates a multidimensional risk in terms of both individual mental health and professional performance.

In the present study, although the association of age, number of children and years in the profession on digital addiction were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), indirect relationships between these variables and digital addiction have been reported in literature. For example, it has been reported that with the increase in age, digital media use decreases and young individuals turn to digital environment more [8, 37]. It has also been stated having children may be a limiting factor for using digital device and may increase the need for digital detox especially for female workers [32]. The increase in years in the profession may decrease addiction for digital skills and it is stated that workers with less experience may resort more to digital devices in coping with stress [16]. This indicates that digital addiction is shaped not only with the frequency of technology use, but also with individual life dynamics.

In general, the results of the present study are largely consistent with studies which show the relationship between digital addiction and mental health in both Turkey and international literature. However, while a large number of studies in literature have been conducted on student groups or general population, the present study which focuses on healthcare professionals contributes significantly in terms of both sample and the variety of psychological variables.

Limitations

Cross-sectional design of the study is limited in terms of showing causal relationships. In addition, since the data were collected through self-report, social desirability bias may be an issue. Since the study was conducted with the healthcare professionals in the predetermined hospitals, the fact that the study can be generalized only to a population with similar characteristics is another limitation of the study.

A potential limitation of the sampling process was the difficulty of accessing randomly selected participants across different shift schedules; however, data collection was conducted during both day and night shifts to minimize this limitation.

Finally, recommendations such as digital wellness workshops and screen-time policies are based on emerging evidence in health professions (e.g., El-Sayed & Goda, 2024; Xue et al., 2024) and should be interpreted as expert-informed suggestions rather than definitive evidence-based interventions. Their effectiveness in healthcare professionals warrants further evaluation.

In conclusion, this study reveals that digital addiction is significantly associated with increased symptoms of depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness among healthcare professionals. The findings emphasize the psychological risks of uncontrolled digital media use in high-stress professions. Healthcare institutions should prioritize awareness programs promoting healthy digital behaviors and offer psychological support services tailored to the needs of their staff. While this study identified associations between digital addiction and psychological symptoms, longitudinal research is needed to determine whether digital overuse contributes to the development of anxiety, depression, and stress over time, or whether these symptoms precede problematic use.

The novelty of this study lies in its focus on healthcare professionals, use of random sampling from two distinct hospital populations, and its integration of path analysis to examine digital addiction’s mediating role between loneliness and psychological symptoms. These methodological strengths provide deeper insights into the psychological impact of digital behavior in high-stress occupational settings.

Practical implications and future directions

This study highlights the need for greater awareness of digital addiction among healthcare professionals, given its association with psychological distress. Interventions such as digital detox programs, time management training, and mental health support within hospital settings may help reduce psychological burden, although the present findings reflect associations rather than causal relationships.

Future research should employ longitudinal or experimental designs to explore the causal direction between digital addiction and psychological outcomes. It is also important to include occupational factors—such as burnout, shift patterns, and organizational stressors—to better understand contextual contributors. Future studies should assess platform-specific digital use (e.g., WhatsApp, Instagram, TikTok), as these platforms may have both beneficial and adverse psychological effects. While some forms of use may provide social support, stress relief, and communication opportunities, excessive or maladaptive use may contribute to increased psychological symptoms and digital addiction.

The findings highlight associations between digital device use, loneliness, and mental health symptoms in healthcare professionals. Immediate strategies focusing on digital hygiene (i.e., maintaining controlled and mindful use of digital devices through practices such as limiting screen time and taking regular breaks), awareness, and workplace well-being support may be feasible.

At the same time, rigorous longitudinal and experimental studies with objective usage data (e.g., app-based monitoring) are needed to establish causal pathways and evaluate intervention effectiveness.

Proposed institutional programs (e.g., digital wellness workshops) are supported by limited evidence and expert opinion; thus, they should be implemented cautiously and evaluated systematically (Table 6).
