Loss of Hfe impairs hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell function
Authors: Liliana Arede, Francisco Dias, Bruna Costa, Tiago L Duarte, Janice L Atkins, Graça Porto, Delfim Duarte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Hfe-KO mice have reduced HSPC numbers and function.•HFE C282Y homozygous individuals have reduced blood clonogenic activity and increased incidence of myelodysplastic syndrome.

Introduction

Iron is a fundamental element for maintaining normal steady-state hematopoiesis and a competent immune response. This is well demonstrated by evidence on the essential role of transferrin receptor–mediated iron endocytosis in all dividing cells, particularly in erythropoiesis1,2 and lymphocyte development and proliferation.3 Mechanisms of iron “sensing and signaling” are critical to control the necessary balanced cellular and systemic iron homeostasis. The master regulator of systemic iron homeostasis is the liver peptide hepcidin (encoded by the HAMP gene), which controls serum iron levels through ferroportin-mediated iron export from absorptive enterocytes and recycling macrophages.4 Liver hepcidin is upregulated in conditions of iron overload via the BMP6/SMAD signaling pathway involving HFE, the hemochromatosis-associated protein;5 conversely, hepcidin is downregulated by expanded erythropoiesis through the erythroblast hormone and hepcidin inhibitor erythroferrone, also via BMP6/SMAD signaling.6 In the hematopoietic compartment, however, mechanisms of iron sensing and signaling are still poorly understood. Evidence for a role of HFE in the hematopoietic system was strongly supported by Ramos et al,7 who demonstrated that HFE is expressed in erythroid cells, impairing iron uptake, and that its absence exclusively from the hematopoietic compartment is sufficient to accelerate erythropoiesis. Those findings came to support previous clinical evidence that one of the hallmarks of HFE-related hemochromatosis (HFE-HC), the genetic disorder of iron overload caused by HFE dysfunction, is enhanced erythropoiesis primarily due to increased iron uptake and hemoglobin (Hb) synthesis by immature erythroid cells.7 HFE is a nonclassical major histocompatibility complex class I protein that is involved in the negative regulation of CD8+ T lymphocyte activation both in vitro and in vivo8,9 and whose loss leads to the exhaustion of the most mature effector memory cells.10 More recently, HFE loss was shown to cause γδ T-cell exhaustion and death.11 The role of HFE in earlier stages of hematopoiesis is, however, unknown.

Iron is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) function,12 but the role of HFE remains to be explored. Cellular iron overload in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) due to administration of iron dextran13 and Fbxl5 deficiency14 lead to the generation of reactive oxygen species and to impaired self-renewal in mouse models. The role of iron is further supported by the demonstration that eltrombopag-induced iron chelation restores HSC function15 and that iron removal rejuvenates aged HSPCs.16 The hepatic loss of HFE expression leads to downregulation of hepcidin and iron overload, which are significant confounder when studying the role of HFE in hematopoiesis.

In this study, we used mouse models to uncouple the systemic and hematopoietic roles of Hfe and demonstrate that Hfe loss impairs HSC self-renewal. We also show that HFE-HC patients have subtle but significant differences in blood counts and parameters that likely reflect an impaired HSPC function. This is further supported by the increased incidence of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) in homozygous female carriers of HFE C282Y from UK Biobank. Altogether, our study presents HFE as a new intrinsic regulator of HSPCs.

Methods

Hemochromatosis patients and controls

A group of 14 patients with HFE-HC diagnosed according to well-established criteria17,18 were recruited at Centro Hospitalar Universitário de Santo António (CHUdSA) Hemochromatosis Clinic for the present study. Patients were all homozygous for the HFE p.Cys282Tyr variant and were all under maintenance phlebotomy treatment, with the last phlebotomies performed 3 to 5 months before recruitment. Their serum ferritin values ranged from 50 to 100 ng/mL, as recommended for maintenance treatment.18 In addition, a group of 20 age- and gender-matched apparently healthy blood donors, with at least 3 phlebotomies performed in the last year, were recruited at the CHUdSA Blood Bank as controls (supplemental Table 1). Notably, this is the same regimen followed by patients with HFE-HC in regular maintenance treatment (every 3 months). The patients’ demographic, hematologic, and biochemical parameters at diagnosis were retrieved from the patients’ clinical files. Peripheral blood from patients with HFE-HC and healthy blood donors were prospectively collected to perform flow cytometry and colony-forming cell (CFC) assays after informed consent. Additionally, blood parameters of 189 blood donors and 188 patients with HFE-HC treated in our center were retrospectively analyzed. All patients’ clinical data were anonymized by G.P. (director of the CHUdSA Hemochromatosis Outpatient Clinic), and confidentiality was maintained during the whole process according to the Declaration of Helsinki and Portuguese law. The study received ethical approval from the CHUdSA Ethics Committee(2014.235[173-DEFI 198-CES]).

Mice

Animals were housed at the Instituto de Investigação e Inovação em Saúde (i3S) animal facility under specific pathogen-free conditions, in a temperature- and light-controlled environment, with free access to standard rodent chow (Teklad Global 14% Protein Rodent Maintenance Diet containing 175 mg/kg iron; Harlan Laboratories) and water. C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the i3S animal facility and from Charles River (France). HC (Hfe-knockout [KO]) mice were kindly donated by Helene Coppin (Toulouse, France). Hepcidin-null (Hamp1-KO) mice were kindly provided by Sophie Vaulont (Institut Cochin, Paris, France). CD45.1 mice were acquired from Charles River. All animals in the study were bred in our local animal facility and were exposed to the same diet and environment. All strains used (Hfe-KO, Hamp1-KO, CD45.2 wild-type [WT], CD45.1 WT) were in the C57BL/6J genetic background. All experiments were carried out with young adult (6- to 12-week old) male mice, with the exception of serial transplantation experiments in which both male and female CD45.1 mice were used. Blood was obtained by tail-vein bleeding. All animals received humane care according to the criteria outlined by the Federation of European Laboratory Animal Science Associations for the care and handling of laboratory animals. All experiments were carried out in compliance with the recommendations of the European Directive on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes (2010/63/EU) and Portuguese law (DL 113/2013). Experimental protocols were approved by the i3S Animal Ethics Committee (DD/2019/15) and by the national authority, Direção-Geral de Alimentação e Veterinária.

Murine blood counts

Blood samples (20 μL) were collected from the tail veins of 12-week-old WT, Hamp1-KO and Hfe-KO mice, and mixed with 5 μL EDTA (10%). Complete blood counts were assessed on an Element HT5 (Heska) veterinary hematology analyzer. Peripheral blood parameters are shown in supplemental Figure 1A.

Serial transplantation assay

CD45.2+ whole bone marrow (BM) donor cells (5.0 × 105) were isolated from either Hamp1-KO or Hfe-KO 8- to 12-week-old mice and transplanted together with WT CD45.1+ whole BM cells (5.0 × 105) into lethally irradiated (850 rad, 8.5 Gy) congenic CD45.1+ via IV tail-vein injection. Peripheral blood was collected every 4 weeks and CD45.1/CD45.2 donor chimerism assessed by flow cytometry. Primary recipients were euthanized at 30 weeks after transplantation, and whole BM cells (1.0 × 106 per recipient mouse) were transplanted into a new cohort of lethally irradiated CD45.1+ secondary recipients. Peripheral blood chimerism was assessed every 4 weeks and animals were euthanized at 12 weeks after transplantation, at which time point BM cells were stained for viability (DAPI [(4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole]), lineage, c-Kit, Sca-1, CD45.1, and CD45.2 to evaluate Lineage˗c-Kit+Sca-1+ (LKS) derived from CD45.2+ Hamp1-KO or Hfe-KO donors. CD45.1 female mice (n = 2, 7 weeks old), Hfe-KO males (n = 4, 12 weeks old) and Hamp1-KO males (n = 4, 14 weeks old) were used as donors. Recipient CD45.1 mice (8 males and 2 females) were 7 weeks old.

Flow cytometry

Mouse femurs and tibias were dissected and crushed in phosphate-buffered saline containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). BM cells were transferred through a 40 μm cell strainer into a 50 mL tube and centrifuged at 500g, 4°C for 5 minutes. Staining for cell surface markers was performed for 15 minutes at room temperature using the fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies listed in supplemental Table 2. Flow cytometric analysis of transferrin uptake was performed with transferrin conjugate AF647 (T23366, Invitrogen). Live and dead cells were distinguished using DAPI (NucBlue, R37606, Invitrogen) or Zombie Green (423111, BioLegend). Calibrite beads (349502, BD Biosciences) were used to determine absolute cell counts. Cells were acquired on a BD FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences) and data were analyzed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Tissue iron quantification

Liver nonheme iron content was determined by the bathophenanthroline method, as described.19 Quantification is shown in supplemental Figure 1B.

CFC assays

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with HFE-HC or healthy donors were seeded in methylcellulose-based semisolid medium (StemMACs HSC- colony-forming unit macrophage Media Complete with human erythropoietin; Miltenyi Biotec) for assessment of multilineage colony-forming activity. Cells were counted and resuspended in RPMI with 10% FBS supplemented with penicillin-streptomycin and 2mM L-glutamine prior to addition to the semisolid medium, which was plated onto 35 mm dishes with 6 × 105 cells per dish, in duplicate. For mouse CFC assays, whole BM of WT, Hamp1-KO or Hfe-KO mice was used, and 1 × 104 cells were plated per dish in duplicate in MethoCult GF M3434 (Stemcell Technologies). Colonies were scored by bright-field microscopy at 10 to 12 days after seeding, corresponding to the time point of full colony hemoglobinization.

In vitro EPO stimulation assay

For in vitro erythropoietin (EPO) treatment, mouse LKS from WT, Hamp1-KO and Hfe-KO mice were sorted and cultured in serum-free expansion media with 10% FBS and recombinant murine EPO (200 ng/mL; Peprotech, 100-64-100UG) for 24 hours. Next, flow cytometry analysis of LKS markers (Sca1, c-Kit, CD150, and CD48) and of CD71 and CD135 was performed as described above.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA from fluorescence-activated cell–sorted murine BM LKS and liver was extracted using NZYol (NZYTech). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was prepared from 40 to 400 ng RNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expression levels were quantified using a CFX384 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). All reactions were performed in a total volume of 20 μL with iQ SYBR Green Supermix, 200nM forward and reverse primers, and 1 μL of cDNA. The amplification protocol consisted of denaturation at 95°C for 3 minutes and 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 20 seconds and annealing at 59°C for 30 seconds. The relative quantity of each transcript was estimated using the 2-ΔCt method, after normalization against the average Ct of 2 endogenous control genes: hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (Hprt1) and beta-actin (Actb). Primer sequences (5′-3′) were as follows: Actb, GGCGGACTGTTACTGAGCTGCGTTT (forward) and AAAGCCATGCCAATGTTGTCTCTT (reverse); Hfe, CAACCAACCACTGGATGCCAA (forward) and TCATGGCCTGAGATTGCAAGG (reverse); Hprt1, AGATGGGAGGCCATCACATTGT (forward) and ATGTCCCCCGTTGACTGATCAT (reverse).

UK Biobank

UK Biobank includes ∼500 000 community volunteers aged 39 to 73 years at baseline assessment (2006-2010) from England, Scotland, and Wales. Participants were healthier than the general population,20 but HFE allele frequencies were similar to previous studies from the United Kingdom.21 We included 451 270 participants, genetically similar to the 1000 Genomes project European reference population22 (the categorization of this population is described elsewhere),23 with HFE p.C282Y and HFE p.H63D genotypes from whole exome sequencing.24 Participants gave written informed consent and were informed of relevant health related findings at baseline. However, consent excluded individual notification of subsequent findings including genotypes. North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (research ethics committee reference 11/NW/0382) approved UK Biobank. All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul.

MDS diagnoses included baseline prevalent self-reports and incident diagnoses during follow-up from hospital inpatient data (to October 2022) plus cancer registries (to December 2020 for England and Wales, and November 2021 for Scotland). MDS ascertainment was based on International Classification of Diseases 10th revision code D46. The frequency of MDS in patients with HFE-H carrying C282Y or H63D mutations compared to controls was calculated by logistic regression analysis. Models were stratified by sex, and adjusted for age, assessment center, and 10 genetic principal components (to account for genetic stratification). Analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Statistical analysis

Unless stated otherwise, data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). Statistical significance was assessed by 2-tailed Student t test for 2-group comparison and 1-way analysis of variance with Tukey multiple comparisons for multiple comparison at a P value of <.05 (∗P < .05, ∗∗P < .01, and ∗∗∗P < .001).

Results

Hfe-KO mice have reduced numbers of BM HSPCs

We first analyzed the expression profile of Hfe and Hamp1 in the mouse hematopoietic system using the SPRING tool,25 and verified that although Hfe is expressed in early hematopoietic progenitors, Hamp1 is only expressed at a more differentiated stage (Figure 1A). We confirmed that Hfe was expressed in mouse HSPCs (supplemental Figure 2A-B). To investigate whether Hfe had a cell-autonomous function in the hematopoietic system, we decided to set it apart from its hepatic role in systemic iron regulation. We analyzed at steady state the BM compartments of Hfe-KO (with iron overload and lack of hematopoietic Hfe), Hamp1-KO (with iron overload and normal hematopoietic Hfe), and WT mice (without iron overload and normal hematopoietic Hfe) (Figure 1B; supplemental Figure 1B). We found a significant reduction in the number of LKS HSPCs, including multipotent progenitor populations (MPPs) and nearly significant loss of long-term (LT)-HSCs in Hfe-KO mice compared with WT animals (Figure 1C), a phenotype not observed in mice lacking Hamp1 (Figure 1D). No differences in cell frequencies of HSPCs were observed (supplemental Figure 3A-B). In contrast to HSPCs, we did not observe significant differences in the numbers of BM committed blood progenitors (supplemental Figure 3C) in Hfe-KO (supplemental Figure 3D) or Hamp1-KO animals (supplemental Figure 3E), suggesting a defect at the HSPC level. No differences in apoptosis (supplemental Figure 3F-G) or proliferation (supplemental Figure 3H) were observed between genotypes. Likewise, despite the reduction in LKS HSPCs, no significant hematopoietic defects were observed in the peripheral blood of Hfe-KO mice when compared with WT (supplemental Figure 1A).

Hfe loss impairs hematopoietic reconstitution in vitro and in vivo

Having observed reduced numbers of phenotypic HSPC in Hfe-KO mice, we asked if loss of Hfe affected the function of HSPCs. We first performed in vitro CFC assays with BM cells from WT or Hfe-KO animals (Figure 2A). We found reduced colony-forming ability in Hfe-KO overall, and in myeloid progenitor output in particular, with no changes in erythroid colonies (Figure 2B; supplemental Figure 4A). The reduction in BM clonogenic capacity was less pronounced in Hamp1-KO mice (supplemental Figure 4B). To assess the in vivo HSC function and uncouple iron overload from the hematopoietic expression of Hfe, we performed a LT reconstitution assay by transplanting BM cells from either CD45.2 Hfe-KO or Hamp1-KO mice together with support CD45.1 WT BM cells into lethally irradiated primary and secondary CD45.1 recipients (Figure 2C). Donor cell engraftment (CD45.2) was followed for a period of 30 weeks in primary recipient animals, with no significant differences in hematopoietic reconstitution noted between groups receiving Hfe-KO or Hamp1-KO cells (Figure 2D). In secondary recipients, however, we observed that Hfe loss significantly impaired BM repopulating ability over a period of 12 weeks (Figure 2E). Analysis of secondary recipients revealed reduced numbers of LKS (Figure 2F-G), MPPs-granulocyte macrophage (supplemental Figure 4C), and MPPs with megakaryocytic/erythroid bias (Mk/E) (supplemental Figure 4D) in mice receiving Hfe-KO BM cells. In contrast, mice receiving Hamp1-KO BM cells were able to successfully reconstitute the hematopoietic system. Together, these results demonstrate a defect in HSPC LT self-renewal capacity exclusively upon Hfe loss.

Hfe-KO HSCs have increased in vitro differentiation

To further explore the defect in HSPC fitness upon loss of Hfe, we sorted LKS from Hfe-KO and Hamp1-KO mice, both having significant iron overload, and cultured these cells in vitro for 2 days (Figure 3A). We found a reduced frequency of phenotypic Hfe-KO LT-HSCs, suggesting an accelerated differentiation of these cells (Figure 3B-C). We also confirmed that LKS cells sorted from Hfe-KO mice had reduced HSC numbers and reduced expression of the self-renewal marker CD150 after 24 hours in culture (supplemental Figure 5A-B). We next assessed the expression of transferrin receptor (TfR1/CD71) in cultured LT-HSCs and verified increased CD71 expression in Hfe-KO LT-HSCs (Figure 3D-E). We next evaluated the iron-loading dynamics on the cell surface of Hfe-KO and Hamp1-KO LKS. In agreement with the increased CD71 expression in Hfe-KO cells, there was significantly higher uptake of labeled transferrin in Hfe-KO MPPs (Figure 3F-G). No significant differences were found in LT-HSC (supplemental Figure 5C), which may be due to the slow cycling of these cells. No differences in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, in particular superoxide, were found between cells of both genotypes (supplemental Figure 5D), potentially excluding cellular oxidative stress as the main player in HSC activity due to absence of Hfe. TFR2 interacts with the EPO receptor, and Tfr2-deficient BM cells were shown to have enhanced erythropoiesis and increased EPO sensitivity.26 Considering that HFE interacts with TFR2 in hepatocytes, we decided to evaluate the effect of EPO on cultured LKS sorted from WT, Hamp1-KO, and Hfe-KO mice. After 24 hours incubation with EPO, we assessed the impact of EPO on HSPC numbers. As expected, in WT LKS, EPO only induced the expansion of MPPs-MK/E (Figure 3H). In contrast, EPO induced the expansion of both MPPs-MK/E and HSCs in LKS derived from Hamp1-KO and Hfe-KO (Figure 3H). Interestingly, the stimulating effect of EPO on HSCs was similar for both Hamp1-KO and Hfe-KO, whereas the effect on MPPs-MK/E was more pronounced in Hfe-KO (Figure 3H). These results are in line with the findings of Ramos et al7 and suggest a cell-intrinsic effect of Hfe in response to EPO on a progenitor level rather than on HSCs.

Patients with HFE-HC show impaired HSPC function and have increased risk of MDS

Our data demonstrate that intrinsic hematopoietic Hfe is critical for HSPC activity. To evaluate the functional impact of HFE loss-of-function in human hematopoietic cells, we analyzed the clonogenic capacity of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from patients with HFE-HC (n = 14) and from healthy age- and sex-matched control blood donors (n = 20) (Figure 4A; supplemental Figure 6A). Patients with HFE-HC were in maintenance therapy with phlebotomies performed within 3 to 5 months before blood collection and had serum ferritin levels of 50 to 100 ng/mL (supplemental Figure 6B) and reduced transferrin saturation when compared with their initial diagnosis (supplemental Figure 6C). Patients presented significantly higher Hb levels when compared with controls (supplemental Figure 6D), an observation previously documented by us.27 No other red cell–associated parameters were significantly different between patients and donor volunteers. We found, however, a multilineage impairment in CFC efficiency of PBMCs from patients with HFE-HC, when compared with healthy blood donors (Figure 4B). Although patients with HFE-HC normally do not have clinically significant cytopenias, the evident impact of HFE loss-of-function on HSPCs may translate into discrete changes in differentiated blood counts. To evaluate this possibility, we retrospectively analyzed a larger cohort of patients with HFE-HC (n = 188) from our clinic and used age- and sex-matched blood donors as controls (n = 189). As previously shown,28,29 we observed that patients with HFE-HC had significantly larger red blood cells with increased mean corpuscular volume, likely explained by increased hemoglobinization due to high iron levels, as supported by the increased mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) (Figure 4C). Consistent with the role of HFE in early HSPCs, patients also had reduced numbers of red blood cells and a small but significant reduction in neutrophil counts. Interestingly, patients with HFE-HC had also reduced neutrophil-to-lymphocyte and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios, which are indicative of dysplastic or ineffective hematopoiesis.30 This observation, together with the observed HSC phenotype of Hfe loss, led us to explore the possibility that HFE may be associated with MDS, a clonal hematologic malignancy characterized by genetic alterations of HSCs that cause ineffective hematopoiesis. To do so, we assessed data from UK Biobank involving 451 270 participants. We performed logistic regression analyses to test associations between HFE-genotypes and the odds of MDS. Our analysis showed an increased risk of MDS in HFE C282Y homozygotes, highly significant in females, suggesting that HFE loss-of-function may facilitate HSC transformation in MDS (Figure 4D).

Discussion

In this study, we report a cell-autonomous role of Hfe in the hematopoietic system, independent of its canonical hepatic role in iron homeostasis. Our approach included the use of global Hfe-KO mice, which showed a clear HSPC defect in comparison with Hamp1-KO that also had iron overload but normal expression of Hfe. Furthermore, we performed BM transplantation of Hfe-KO cells into WT recipients. This allowed us to clearly study the impact of hematopoietic-specific loss of Hfe and uncouple it from the presence of iron overload. Phenotypic analysis of mouse BM hematopoietic populations revealed reduced HSPC numbers upon Hfe loss. We also demonstrated functional impairment in self-renewal as well as multilineage clonogenic defects. Importantly, these hematopoietic deviations were not replicated in Hamp1-KO mice (which also display iron overload) or with Hamp1-KO cells, indicating a specific, nonredundant function for Hfe in the hematopoietic system. We observed that upon loss of Hfe, the expression of cell surface transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1, CD71, or TFRC) in HSPCs is higher, which may be due to cellular iron deficiency. This is consistent with the observed unchanged markers of oxidative stress. Alternatively, elevated CD71 levels may be caused by the loss of Hfe itself. HFE competes with cell surface CD71 and inhibits iron uptake.31,32 Consistently, uptake of labeled transferrin is increased in Hfe-KO HSPCs. This observation leads us to speculate that the observed reduction of HSC function in Hfe-KO cells may be due to increased differentiation as a consequence of increased iron uptake. Although Tfr2 deficiency in the hematopoietic system was previously shown to sensitize erythroid progenitors to EPO and to stimulate terminal erythropoiesis,26 Tfr2 expression or its role in HSCs has not been studied. Considering the potential association between HFE and TFR2, one may speculate that this interaction can help explain the HSC effects observed in our studies. We observed that EPO stimulation has a pronounced effect on MPPs-MK/E but not on HSCs derived from Hfe-KO LKS. These results suggest that the HSC defect caused by the loss of Hfe is not driven by Tfr2.

When interpreting these findings, one should also consider that although both Hfe-KO and Hamp1-KO mice have elevated transferrin saturation, ferroportin regulation in the hematopoietic cells of these 2 mutants may differ. In Hamp1-KO mice, hepcidin regulation of ferroportin is eliminated entirely, whereas in Hfe-KO mice, hepcidin still circulates, albeit at a level inappropriately low relative to liver iron stores. On the other hand, having less blocking of ferroportin in the Hamp1-KO potentially increases iron delivery to HSPCs from neighboring niche cells, such as macrophages. Furthermore, the in vivo serial transplantation assays compared the fitness of Hfe-KO (and Hamp1-KO) BM cells directly against WT BM cells, further supporting that the observed HSC phenotype of Hfe-KO mice is dependent on the cell-intrinsic loss of Hfe.

Our analysis of human patients with HFE-HC (HFE C282Y homozygotes), under iron-reduction/maintenance-treatment conditions, revealed parallel defects in progenitor blood cell function. Indeed, patients with HFE-HC have altered erythropoietic parameters (Hb, mean corpuscular value).28,29 Despite these findings, the role of Hfe in the HSPC compartment was never examined. Here, our clinical observations reveal that although most HFE-HC patients do not exhibit severe cytopenias, they have subtle yet significant alterations in blood cell output. We also establish an association between HFE C282Y homozygosity and increased risk of MDS, which particularly significant in women. MDS is a heterogeneous group of BM stem cell disorders characterized by defective hematopoiesis, cytopenias, and clonal evolution.33,34 We and others have previously provided a link between iron dysregulation, defective antioxidant responses and MDS.35, 36, 37 Notably, HFE variants were reported to be frequent in Austrian patients with MDS.38 HFE C282Y homozygosity was, however, rarely detected,38 and its association with MDS is difficult to evaluate outside large population-level studies, as we did in the present study. It is, however, not possible to exclude the potential role of iron overload in the observed association, and future studies should explore this link. Together, our findings define an intrinsic requirement for HFE in maintaining HSPC fitness and hint at a potential relationship between HFE loss-of-function, hematopoietic dysfunction, and MDS.
