Experimental Investigation of Thermally Induced Stress Shadow Effects in Multiwell Hydraulic Fracturing and Neural-Network-Based Fracture Identification
Authors: Shulin Yu, Yang Li, Wenlong Zhang, Yu Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient stimulation of deep high-temperature, low-permeability reservoirs critically depends on the creation of complex fracture networks via multiwell hydraulic fracturing, where thermally induced stress shadow effects modulate fracture propagation and connectivity. In this study, we present a comprehensive experimental investigation using a self-developed high-temperature true triaxial multiwell fracturing apparatus and an artificial dual-well model to systematically characterize the coupled influence of the temperature field, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence on fracture interaction and stress perturbation. A series of fracturing experiments were conducted under varying thermal conditions and fracturing strategies (sequential and synchronous). A postfracturing quantitative analysis of fracture geometry was conducted using CT-derived three-dimensional reconstructions. Furthermore, we developed an improved U-Net neural-network framework to automatically identify fracture connectivity and spatial patterns from low-contrast CT images, achieving improved prediction accuracy over conventional linear models. The integrated “physical experiment–quantitative fracture characterization–neural-network recognition” framework elucidates the competitive and synergistic mechanisms governing the coupled interactions among thermal, geometric, and stress shadow effects in deep reservoirs. The findings demonstrate that increasing perforation spacing weakens stress interference, promoting independent fracture growth. High temperatures reduce peak injection pressure and intensify complex fracture morphologies through thermal microcrack development. The machine-learning-based identification approach reliably discriminates fracture connectivity states and morphological types, offering a robust basis for optimizing multiwell fracturing design and evaluating post-treatment effectiveness in deep high-temperature tight reservoirs.

Introduction

With the progressive depletion of shallow oil and gas resources, hydrocarbon exploration and production have increasingly shifted toward deep, high-temperature tight reservoirs, which have become important targets for ensuring future energy supply. Hydraulic fracturing is one of the most effective stimulation techniques for improving the permeability of low-permeability formations and creating artificial fracture networks that enhance reservoir connectivity. , However, deep reservoirs are commonly subjected to high in situ stresses, large horizontal stress differences, elevated temperatures, and strong geological heterogeneity. − Under such conditions, the injection of relatively low-temperature fracturing fluid into a high-temperature rock mass can induce thermal shock and thermal stress redistribution, thereby modifying rock mechanical properties and fracture propagation behavior. In multiwell hydraulic fracturing, the initiation and extension of one fracture can perturb the surrounding stress field and influence the propagation of adjacent fractures. This stress shadow effect may lead to fracture deflection, arrest, asymmetric propagation, and an uneven fracture-network distribution, ultimately reducing the reservoir stimulation efficiency. , Compared with shallow reservoirs, deep high-temperature reservoirs present more complex thermomechanical responses because temperature differences between the injected fluid and the rock matrix can generate thermal stresses and promote microstructural damage, including intergranular microcracks and lattice distortion. − Therefore, understanding the coupled influence of high temperature, in situ stress difference, and multiwell stress interference is essential for improving fracture-network control and stimulation efficiency in deep tight reservoirs.

Considerable progress has been made in numerical modeling of thermally coupled hydraulic fracturing and fracture interaction. In discrete element modeling, Potyondy et al. simulated the degradation of interparticle bond strength at elevated temperatures and showed that fracture initiation pressure decreases as temperature increases. Dang et al. further developed a discrete element-grain boundary model to investigate thermal damage in defective crystalline rocks, demonstrating that differential thermal expansion among minerals increases microcrack density and weakens rock integrity. Finite-element-based studies have also provided important insights into thermally induced fracturing. Lu et al. showed that thermal stimulation can generate seed fractures and promote subsequent fracture growth in hot dry rock reservoirs, while Guo et al. established a thermohydro-mechanical-damage model and found that temperature gradients can increase fracture deflection and intensify stress interaction among multiple fractures. Extended finite-element and discrete fracture modeling approaches have been used to describe fracture path evolution and stress interference under thermohydro-mechanical coupling. , Phase-field models have further improved the description of crack initiation, branching, and coalescence under thermoelastic loading. , For example, Pang et al. reported that thermal loading accelerates crack propagation and changes fracture connectivity, and Jiang et al. reproduced the coupled evolution of fracture, displacement, and temperature fields during CO2 fracturing in thermo-poroelastic media. Temperature-dependent phase-field formulations have also been developed to account for the interaction between fracture evolution and thermoelastic effects. In addition, discrete fracture network, displacement discontinuity, peridynamic, and machine-learning-based models have been applied to multistage fracturing, fracture conductivity, thermo-poroelastic interaction, and fracture prediction. − Fully coupled thermohydro-mechanical models have also been used to evaluate fracture deformation, natural fracture activation, and heat extraction performance in geothermal and shale reservoirs. , Although these numerical approaches have advanced the theoretical understanding of thermally induced fracture propagation, they still face limitations in representing natural rock heterogeneity, bedding, weak planes, preexisting microfractures, multiphase flow, and wellbore–perforation–fracture coupling at laboratory or field scales.

Laboratory experiments and visualized fracture characterization therefore remain indispensable for revealing the physical evolution of hydraulic fractures in heterogeneous reservoirs. X-ray CT and digital volume correlation have been used to quantify fracture aperture, volume, and connectivity in fractured coal, confirming that in situ stress difference strongly affects fracture complexity and dominant fracture morphology. Thermomechanical experiments on different lithologies have shown that elevated temperature can induce mineral expansion, thermal stress accumulation, and degradation of mechanical properties. , When the temperature exceeds a critical range, the compressive strength of rocks such as granite and shale may decrease markedly, while deformation characteristics and fracture initiation pressure are correspondingly altered. Conventional triaxial hydraulic fracturing experiments on heated granite further indicate that breakdown pressure tends to decrease with increasing temperature under controlled confining pressure conditions. , For shale reservoirs, direct shear and hydraulic fracturing experiments have shown that elevated temperature can enhance anisotropic damage, modify crack deviation from bedding planes, and reduce breakdown pressure by weakening cohesion and frictional resistance. , Thermally induced preexisting microfractures have also been shown to regulate rock strength and failure mode. Nevertheless, most experimental studies still focus on single-well or simplified fracturing conditions, whereas systematic true triaxial experiments on high-temperature multiwell hydraulic fracturing remain limited. , Chen et al. emphasized that many current models remain focused on room-temperature and single-well conditions, thereby neglecting the coupled influence of temperature-field evolution and multiwell stress interference. This limitation suggests that a more fundamental understanding of fracture-induced stress perturbation is still necessary before the coupled thermal and multiwell effects can be properly evaluated. The theoretical basis of stress perturbation around fractures can be traced to Sneddon’s linear elastic fracture mechanics solution, and subsequent experimental–numerical studies have further identified different interaction modes between adjacent fractures. At the same time, fluid–rock interaction and microstructural evolution studies have shown that mesoscale damage processes can further influence fracture conductivity and hydraulic response. , More recent multiwell and multiphysics simulations have improved the description of stress shadow evolution, fracture propagation, and engineering-scale fracturing optimization. − However, these studies have not fully clarified how high temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence jointly control stress shadow intensity, interwell fracture competition, and fracture connectivity under true triaxial boundary conditions.

Although previous studies have advanced the understanding of thermally induced fracture propagation, stress shadow effects, and fracture characterization, the coupled fracture interaction mechanisms in deep high-temperature multiwell hydraulic fracturing remain insufficiently clarified. In particular, systematic physical simulation experiments under true triaxial stress conditions are still limited, and the combined effects of temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence on the interwell stress shadow effect and fracture connectivity have not been fully established. Moreover, the relationship between pressure response, fracture morphology, stress shadow intensity, and U-Net-based fracture identification has not yet been clearly quantified. These limitations restrict the evaluation and optimization of multiwell hydraulic fracturing schemes in deep high-temperature tight reservoirs. To address these issues, this study conducts dual-well hydraulic fracturing experiments using a self-developed true triaxial hydraulic fracturing system under controlled-temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing-sequence conditions. Pressure-response parameters, including breakdown pressure and pressure fluctuation characteristics, are analyzed together with fracture geometric parameters such as the fracture length, branching number, and interwell fracture connectivity. The coupled influence of temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence on the stress shadow intensity and fracture propagation patterns is then evaluated. Furthermore, an improved U-Net model is introduced for U-Net-based fracture identification of interwell fracture connectivity and fracture propagation modes from postfracturing images. By integrating physical simulation, quantitative fracture characterization, and U-Net-based fracture identification, this study provides laboratory-scale evidence for evaluating the interwell stress shadow effect and optimizing perforation spacing and fracturing sequence in deep high-temperature multiwell hydraulic fracturing.

Materials and Methods

This section presents the mix design, fabrication process, and mechanical parameter calibration methods for the artificial rock specimens, ensuring consistency in key physical and mechanical properties, such as elastic modulus and tensile strength. On this basis, a dual-well hydraulic fracturing experimental model incorporating the temperature field is established, in which key variables including the well spacing, injection sequence, and temperature conditions are systematically controlled, thereby providing an experimental basis for the comparative analysis of the fracture propagation behavior and stress interference effects.

Preparation of Artificial Rock Sample

To systematically investigate the effects of temperature and perforation spacing on fracture initiation, propagation, and interaction during dual-well hydraulic fracturing, cement-based artificial rock specimens were employed to minimize the inherent variability of natural rocks in terms of mineral composition, structural features, and initial defect distribution, thereby improving the comparability and repeatability of experimental results under different conditions. The specimens were prepared using quartz sand as the aggregate to form the granular skeleton, while cement and gypsum were used as binding materials to regulate the overall strength, stiffness, and brittle failure characteristics. The mix proportions were determined through a series of preliminary tests and mechanical calibrations, with comprehensive consideration of peak strength, elastic-stage stiffness, postpeak softening behavior, and macroscopic failure patterns, such that the mechanical response of the artificial specimens is comparable in magnitude to that of tight sandstone. This artificial sample not only satisfies the requirements of mechanical similarity and parameter controllability but also exhibits excellent fabrication stability and batch consistency, providing a reliable and consistent material basis for subsequent analyses of fracture initiation pressure, injection pressure response, and fracture spatial evolution in dual-well hydraulic fracturing experiments.

It should be noted that the cement-based artificial specimens were used primarily to improve experimental repeatability and to isolate the effects of temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence. Therefore, the experimental results should be interpreted as revealing the relative regulatory mechanisms of thermally induced stress shadow effects rather than as direct field-scale parameter values.

Uniaxial Compression Test

Uniaxial compression tests were conducted on artificial rock specimens using an electrohydraulic servo-controlled rock mechanics testing system to systematically obtain the fundamental mechanical parameters and the complete stress–strain response of the specimens. The tests were performed under displacement-controlled loading, and the loading rate was kept constant for all of the test groups. To reduce the influence of material discretization on the experimental results, six specimens were tested for each group. During the tests, axial stress and axial strain were recorded simultaneously to obtain continuous and complete stress–strain curves, as shown in Figure . The results indicate that the artificial rock specimens exhibit a typical quasi-brittle mechanical behavior. At the initial stage of loading, the stress–strain relationship is approximately linear, corresponding to a stable elastic deformation stage. As the load increased, the specimens gradually entered a nonlinear damage accumulation stage. Once the peak stress is reached, the stress drops rapidly, accompanied by a pronounced brittle failure. The peak compressive strength of the specimens is mainly distributed within the range of 20–23 MPa, with the strength variability controlled within 10%. The slope of the elastic stage shows only minor discretization, indicating that the prepared artificial rock specimens exhibit good homogeneity and repeatability.

Porosity and Permeability Determination

To provide a mechanical basis for the preparation and calibration of the artificial rock specimens, triaxial mechanical tests were first conducted on natural sandstone cores from four different stratigraphic layers using a high-precision fully digital triaxial hydraulic servo testing system. The tests were performed under confining pressures of 5, 10, 15, and 20 MPa. A total of 16 cylindrical specimens with dimensions of Φ25 mm × 50 mm were tested, with four specimens prepared for each stratigraphic layer. During testing, the specimens were sealed with Teflon material to prevent hydraulic oil from penetrating into the cores, and axial loading was applied at a displacement rate of 0.5 mm/min. The triaxial mechanical parameters, including triaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, cohesion, and internal friction angle, were obtained from these tests. In this study, the mechanical parameters obtained under the 15 MPa confining pressure condition were adopted as the main reference values for the mix design and mechanical calibration of the artificial sandstone specimens because this stress level is close to the boundary stress condition used in the subsequent true triaxial hydraulic fracturing experiments. To further characterize the pore structure and seepage capacity of the artificial rock specimens, porosity and permeability tests were conducted on representative samples. All porosity and permeability tests were conducted at room temperature. The obtained porosity and permeability values were used to describe the initial pore structure and seepage capacity of the artificial specimens, thereby providing a basis for analyzing the formation of fracture flow channels and fracture conductivity in the subsequent dual-well hydraulic fracturing experiments. A self-developed high-temperature true triaxial multiwell hydraulic fracturing experimental platform was used to conduct the physical simulation tests, as shown in Figure . The system consists of an independent three-directional loading frame, a high-pressure servo pumping module, a geothermal simulation and control system, and a multichannel data acquisition unit. CT-Based Fracture Characterization and U-Net Identification Method

In previous researches, fractured specimens were usually broken after hydraulic fracturing to observe fracture morphology and thereby characterize hydraulic fracture behavior. However, this method produces numerous secondary fractures, which can mislead the accurate identification of hydraulic fractures and reduce the reliability of the results. In this study, CT scanning technology combined with machine-learning methods was employed to reconstruct hydraulic fractures in three dimensions, in order to visually present the spatial morphology of the fracture network and analyze its distribution patterns. The three-dimensional fracture reconstruction technique used in this section is based on an X-ray industrial CT inspection system, as shown in Figure . A conventional multislice spiral X-ray CT scanning system was used to perform nondestructive testing of the specimens in this study. The system is capable of scanning cores and artificial specimens of various sizes, with a maximum specimen diameter of approximately 300 mm. During scanning, the X-ray tube voltage was set within a range of 80–140 kV, and the scanning parameters were optimized based on the specimen’s density and internal structural characteristics. The system’s spatial resolution is approximately 0.03–0.05 mm, and the CT imaging density resolution exceeds 1%. Under the scanning conditions of this study, the system was able to reliably identify fractures wider than 0.3 mm, pores with a diameter no less than 0.5 mm, and high-density inclusions of similar scale.

Industrial CT scanning system.

To more clearly display the internal fracture characteristics of the artificial sandstone model after fracturing, the CT scan slices were converted into grayscale images with enhanced grayscale contrast. Subsequently, the grayscale images were processed to enhance contrast and reduce noise, thereby facilitating fracture identification. To accurately extract the geometric information on fractures in the specimens, this section employs image segmentation techniques to extract relevant fracture features and quantitative data from the images. Common image segmentation methods include edge detection, region-based methods, deep-learning-based methods, and thresholding. In this section, image binarization was performed using a grayscale threshold T. Pixels with grayscale values greater than or equal to T were assigned a value of 1, whereas those with values lower than T were assigned a value of 0, thereby converting the image into a binary image, as shown in eq (). A binary image composed of black and white pixels was obtained; black pixels (g = 0) represent the rock matrix, whereas white pixels (g = 1) represent fractures.

While the aforementioned thresholding techniques effectively differentiate the rock matrix, pore spaces, and primary hydraulic fractures in the majority of postfracturing specimens, certain samples exhibit fine-scale, low-aperture fractures that remain nearly indistinguishable from background noise or the matrix via manual inspection. To address this challenge, a U-net convolutional neural-network (CNN) architecture was developed to accurately segment these subtle fracture features from CT slices. The U-Net model is optimized by minimizing a predefined objective function (specifically termed the energy function in this context) through the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm, ensuring high-fidelity fracture extraction even under low-contrast conditions.

A high momentum value (0.99) was used to ensure that the weight update at each iteration was influenced not only by the current gradient but also by the accumulated gradients from previous iterations, thereby reducing update fluctuations caused by the current gradient and helping the neural network converge more stably. To further clarify the implementation and effectiveness of the improved U-Net model, the network structure, training strategy, evaluation metrics, and comparison with conventional image segmentation methods were supplemented in this study. The term “improved U-Net” refers to the adaptation of the conventional U-Net architecture for low-contrast hydraulic fracture segmentation in CT images. The improvement mainly includes three aspects. First, grayscale enhancement and denoising preprocessing were performed to improve the contrast between fractures and the rock matrix. Second, a composite weighted cross-entropy loss function was introduced to reduce the influence of severe class imbalance between fracture pixels and matrix pixels. Third, a boundary-sensitive weight map was used to assign higher weights to narrow fracture boundaries and adjacent separation zones, thereby improving the recognition of fine-aperture fractures, thermally induced microcracks, and disconnected fracture segments.

The network retained the typical encoder–decoder structure of U-Net. In the encoder, convolution and pooling operations were used to extract multiscale fracture features, whereas the decoder gradually restored the spatial resolution of the fracture mask through up-sampling operations. Skip connections were used to fuse shallow boundary information with deep semantic features, which is particularly important for identifying thin fractures with weak grayscale contrast. The model was optimized using stochastic gradient descent with a momentum value of 0.99. The loss function combined a pixel-wise Softmax function with a composite weighted cross-entropy term, as expressed in eqs ()–(4). The initial weights were drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with a variance of 2/N, where N denotes the number of incoming nodes per neuron.

Representative postfracturing CT slices obtained from different experimental groups were selected to construct the image data set. The original CT slices were first processed using grayscale enhancement and threshold-assisted segmentation and then manually corrected to generate reference labels for hydraulic fractures, pores, thermally induced fractures, and microcracks. To improve the model robustness, data augmentation operations, including rotation, flipping, cropping, and grayscale perturbation, were applied during training.

The segmentation performance was evaluated using pixel accuracy, precision, recall, dice coefficient, and intersection over union (IoU), which are commonly used indicators for image segmentation tasks. Physical Model and Experimental Scheme of Dual-Well Hydraulic Fracturing

This section develops a physical experimental model that incorporates both synchronous and sequential injection schemes. This model serves as a scaled representation of the mechanical interactions between adjacent wells in multiwell stimulation scenarios, enabling a systematic comparative analysis of fracture propagation behaviors and interwell disturbance characteristics. To standardize the subsequent description of fracture orientations and stress component, the spatial coordinates x, y, and z are explicitly defined to correspond to the principal stress directions (i.e., the maximum horizontal stress σ H , minimum horizontal stress σ h , and vertical principal stress σ z ).

Synchronous Fracturing

On the basis of differences in injection schemes between the two wells, the dual-well fracturing experiments were categorized into two scenarios: synchronous fracturing and sequential fracturing. Under the synchronous fracturing scenario, both wells initiated fracturing fluid injection simultaneously with identical injection schemes, as summarized in Table . This scenario was designed to simulate multiwell parallel fracturing in field operations and to evaluate the influence of interwell stress shadow effects on fracture initiation and propagation. The internal perspectives of the fracturing model and the cast artificial specimen are presented in Figures and , respectively. Under the synchronous fracturing scenario, to minimize the influence of loading fluctuations on the pressure response, the load was maintained after reaching the target true triaxial stress and synchronous injection was initiated only after the fluctuation amplitude of the true triaxial load stabilized within a narrow range. After injection commenced, the fluid flowed through a single injection wellbore and subsequently entered the branch wellbores via different flow paths. Once the wellbores were filled with fluid, the pressure accumulated, ultimately leading to specimen failure.

Spatial Morphology and Quantitative Analysis of Fractures in Dual-Well Fracturing

Fracture Behavior in Dual-Well Synchronous Fracturing

In deep high-temperature tight reservoirs, the interwell stress interference induced by synchronous dual-well fracturing serves as the primary physical mechanism governing the spatiotemporal evolution of fracture networks. Based on physical simulations of synchronous fluid injection, our findings reveal that varying the perforation spacing directly modulates the magnitude of stress interference between adjacent perforations. Consequently, this modulation dictates the morphological characteristics, 3D spatial geometries, and the dynamic transient pressure response of the induced hydraulic fractures. As illustrated in Figure , at a perforation spacing of 10 mm, the stress interference between adjacent perforations is highly pronounced. Following initiation from the perforations, the hydraulic fractures are driven by the superposition of adjacent induced stress fields, causing them to rapidly coalesce and merge into a single, continuous primary fracture. Furthermore, the fracture plane exhibits significant deflection toward the direction of the maximum horizontal principal stress, representing a typical monolithic fracture geometry. As the perforation spacing increases to 30 mm, the intensity of stress interference between adjacent perforations diminishes. The hydraulic fracture still propagates in a single dominant mode; however, it exhibits a wider spatial footprint and a more expansive geometry. While the 3D fracture morphology retains a fully connected structure, the spatial extension of the fracture plane becomes more regularized and expansive, leading to a marked reduction in morphological complexity. When the perforation spacing is further increased to 50 mm, the interwell stress interference diminishes below a critical threshold. Consequently, each perforation independently initiates a hydraulic fracture that propagates in isolation, forming two distinct fractures without discernible connectivity. The fracture generating from Perforation A extends along the direction of the maximum horizontal principal stress with negligible reorientation. Conversely, the fracture generating from Perforation B, influenced by the stress field of the adjacent fracture, exhibits severe reorientation, deflecting 85° toward the wellbore relative to the x-axis. This transition to a dual-fracture system demonstrates that increased perforation spacing facilitates the independent propagation of hydraulic fractures. From the injection pressure evolution curves, case 1-1 corresponds to a relatively low peak breakdown pressure (17.98 MPa). This is because the superposition effect of the stress fields generated by adjacent fractures effectively reduces the effective fracture initiation stress of the formation, thereby lowering the pressure threshold required for fracture initiation and initial propagation. At the same time, the merged single fracture provides a smoother propagation path, which further reduces the propagation resistance. In case 1-2, the peak breakdown pressure increases to 18.99 MPa, indicating that the weakening of the stress superposition effect increases the formation stress resistance that must be overcome for fracture initiation. In addition, the more extended fracture morphology slightly reduces the efficiency of stress release during propagation, which in turn leads to an increase in the peak pressure. Case 1-3 corresponds to the highest peak breakdown pressure (19.403 MPa). On the one hand, this is attributed to the absence of stress superposition from adjacent fractures when the two fractures initiate independently, meaning that each fracture must overcome the initial in situ stress on its own, which significantly increases the initiation resistance. On the other hand, the reduced interaction between independent fractures weakens the synergistic effects of pressure transmission and stress release, further increasing the peak pressure. Overall, under synchronous fracturing conditions, perforation spacing influences the intensity of stress interference between adjacent perforations, thereby affecting fracture connectivity, spatial distribution, and peak breakdown pressure. As the perforation spacing increases from 10 mm to 50 mm, hydraulic fractures gradually transition from a single merged fracture to two independent fractures. Correspondingly, the three-dimensional morphology evolves from a concentrated integrated structure to a dispersed dual-structure pattern, while the peak breakdown pressure gradually increases. This relationship reveals the intrinsic correlation among perforation spacing, stress interference, and fracture morphology, providing experimental evidence and theoretical support for optimizing perforation parameters and controlling complex fracture networks in hydraulic fracturing.

Fracture Behavior in Dual-Well Sequential Fracturing Under Thermal Effects

At 50 °C, only a single thermal fracture was observed on the specimen surface (Figure (a)). The hydraulic fracture propagated linearly along the direction of the maximum principal stress, and the three-dimensional morphology exhibited a single, smooth fracture surface. The interstage stress interference effect was pronounced. When the temperature increased to 100 °C, thermal fractures appeared on both sides of the specimen (Figure (b)). The thermal microcrack network interacted with the hydraulic fracture, causing deflection and branching during propagation, and the three-dimensional fracture morphology became tortuous and multibranched. When the temperature reached 200 °C, the number of thermal fractures further increased, and fracture propagation gradually shifted toward the radial direction of the wellbore rather than strictly following the maximum principal stress direction. As the temperature increased from 50 to 200 °C, the breakdown pressures of both stages decreased significantly. The first-stage breakdown pressure decreased from 18.118 to 14.38 MPa, a reduction of approximately 20.7%, while the second-stage breakdown pressure decreased from 18.98 to 14.63 MPa, a reduction of approximately 22.9%. This phenomenon can be attributed to the generation of thermal fractures within the specimen at high temperatures, which formed a preexisting defect network and simultaneously weakened the mechanical properties of the rock matrix. The elastic modulus and compressive strength generally decrease as the number of thermal fractures increases, thereby reducing the critical pressure required for fracture initiation. Under all temperature conditions, the second-stage breakdown pressure was slightly higher than that of the first stage, reflecting the typical stress interference effect during sequential fracturing. The hydraulic fractures formed during the first stage reconstructed the internal stress field of the specimen, so the second-stage fracturing had to overcome the residual stress and fracture interaction, resulting in a slight increase in breakdown pressure. Notably, increasing temperature significantly affected the pressure difference between the two stages. At 50 °C, the pressure difference between the two stages was 0.86 MPa; at 100 °C, it increased to 0.94 MPa, whereas at 200 °C, it decreased to only 0.25 MPa. This change is attributed to the full development of thermal fractures at high temperatures. Under the 100 and 200 °C conditions, thermal fractures were more numerous and widely distributed. These thermal fractures acted as weak planes that released part of the stress in advance, weakening the stress interference effect between stages and making the breakdown pressures of the two stages closer. Further observations of fracture morphology indicate that temperature influences the fracturing behavior by regulating the interaction between thermal and hydraulic fractures. At 50 °C, thermal fractures were underdeveloped, and hydraulic fractures dominated the propagation path, resulting in a relatively large interstage pressure difference. At 100 and 200 °C, thermal and hydraulic fractures interwove and propagated synergistically, which not only reduced the overall breakdown pressure but also indirectly weakened interstage stress interference. In summary, the effect of temperature on breakdown pressure is primarily realized through changes in fracture morphology and stress interference. At low temperatures, thermal fractures are underdeveloped, hydraulic fractures dominate propagation, interstage stress interference is strong, and breakdown pressure is relatively high with a clear stage difference. As temperature increases, thermal fractures begin to interact with hydraulic fractures, fracture morphology becomes more complex, and the breakdown pressure difference between stages fluctuates slightly. At 200 °C, thermal fractures are fully developed and interact with hydraulic fractures to form a complex fracture network, which reduces the critical pressure required for fracture initiation and weakens the interstage stress shadow effect.

Fracture Behavior in Dual-Well Sequential Fracturing with Variable Perforation Spacing

Under sequential fracturing conditions, perforation spacing influences the stress interference intensity between adjacent perforations and fracture propagation behavior, thereby affecting the fracture morphology, three-dimensional geometry, and the dynamic evolution of injection pressure. At a perforation spacing of 10 mm, the stress interference between adjacent perforations is extremely strong. The induced stress field generated after the initiation of the first-stage fracture significantly alters the stress state around the subsequent perforation, causing strong stress superposition during the initiation and propagation of the subsequent fracture. As shown in Figure , although the two hydraulic fractures initiate at different stages, their propagation paths differ significantly because of the induced stress from the neighboring fracture. The fracture initiated from perforation A propagates perpendicular to the in situ stress direction, whereas the fracture initiated from perforation B propagates along the direction of the maximum horizontal principal stress. The two fractures exhibit a T-shaped geometry and show a strong tendency toward connectivity. The three-dimensional fracture morphology is characterized by two fracture surfaces that are spatially close and geometrically interactive. Under the 10 mm perforation spacing condition, the experimentally measured breakdown pressure of the first stage is 17.94 MPa while that of the second stage increases to 20.71 MPa, indicating that the subsequent stage breakdown pressure is significantly higher than that of the first stage. This pressure-response characteristic can be attributed to the fact that although the propagation of the first-stage fracture releases part of the in situ stress, the typical small perforation spacing means that when the subsequent fracture initiates, it must overcome both the initial in situ stress and the additional stress induced by the first-stage fracture. Moreover, the stress interference between fractures increases the propagation resistance, resulting in a higher breakdown pressure in the subsequent stages. At the same time, the two-stage pressure response directly reflects the sequential characteristics of fracture initiation and propagation under sequential fracturing, and the higher breakdown pressure in the subsequent stage indicates the amplification effect of stress interference on fracture initiation resistance. When the perforation spacing increases to 30 mm, the stress interference between adjacent perforations is reduced and the influence of the induced stress field from the first-stage fracture on the subsequent fracture decreases, resulting in greater fracture independence. In terms of fracture morphology, although both hydraulic fractures still propagate along the direction of the maximum horizontal principal stress, the spatial angle between the fracture surfaces increases, the distribution range becomes wider, and the morphology becomes more extended, showing a transition from nearly parallel propagation to oblique propagation. The three-dimensional fracture morphology is characterized by two fracture surfaces that are relatively separated in space and less mutually influenced in geometry, and the connectivity tendency between the fractures is obviously reduced. The experimental results show that the breakdown pressure of the first stage is 18.75 MPa and that of the second stage is 20.01 MPa. Although the breakdown pressure of the subsequent stage is still higher than that of the first stage, the pressure difference between the two stages is smaller than that under the 10 mm spacing conditions. The increase in perforation spacing weakens the superposition effect of the induced stress from the first-stage fracture, thereby reducing the additional stress that must be overcome for the initiation of the subsequent fracture. Meanwhile, the more extended fracture distribution slightly improves the stress release efficiency, which further reduces the difference in the breakdown pressure between the two stages. The injection pressure curve still exhibits a two-stage evolution pattern. The peak pressure of each stage increases compared to the 10 mm spacing condition, while the difference between the peak pressures of the two stages decreases, clearly reflecting the sequential fracture propagation behavior under sequential fracturing. When the perforation spacing is further increased to 50 mm, the stress interference between adjacent perforations decreases below a critical level and the influence of the induced stress field from the first-stage fracture on the subsequent fracture is further reduced. The hydraulic fractures induced by each perforation exhibit a completely independent propagation behavior. The experimental results show that the breakdown pressure of the first stage is 19.15 MPa and that of the second stage is 19.58 MPa, with the smallest difference between the two stages. This pressure-response characteristic is attributed to the fact that when two fractures initiate independently, there is no stress superposition assistance from the first-stage fracture, and each fracture must independently overcome the initial in situ stress, which significantly increases the initiation resistance. At the same time, the reduced interaction between independent fractures weakens the synergistic effect of pressure transmission and stress release, leading to a significant increase in the peak pressures of each stage. The reduction in the pressure difference between the two stages indicates that when the perforation spacing is sufficiently large, the stress release effect of the first-stage fracture can no longer influence the initiation resistance of the subsequent fracture, and the initiation behavior of each fracture becomes more independent. From the fracture morphology perspective, the fracture initiated from perforation A propagates perpendicular to the minimum principal stress, while the fracture initiated from perforation B propagates approximately parallel to the fracture generated from perforation A. Because the two hydraulic fractures propagate along their respective preferred directions, the fracture surfaces remain separated without forming a connected structure, resulting in a typical dual-fracture parallel propagation system. The three-dimensional fracture morphology is characterized by two spatially separated and completely independent fracture surfaces. The fracture propagation direction is controlled by the local stress field, which reflects the strengthening effect of an increased perforation spacing on fracture independence. Overall, under staged sequential fracturing conditions, perforation spacing regulates the connectivity of fractures, spatial distribution characteristics, and the two-stage breakdown pressure by controlling the stress interference intensity between adjacent perforations and the influence of the stress field induced by the previously formed fractures. As the perforation spacing increases from 10 mm to 50 mm, hydraulic fractures evolve from two nearly parallel and interacting fractures to a completely independent and separated dual-fracture system. The three-dimensional morphology correspondingly evolves from spatially adjacent and interacting fracture surfaces to spatially separated and independently propagating fracture surfaces. Meanwhile, the first-stage breakdown pressure gradually increases from 17.94 to 19.15 MPa, while the second-stage breakdown pressure gradually decreases from 20.71 to 19.58 MPa, and the difference between the two stages decreases with increasing spacing. Compared with synchronous fracturing, the sequential fracturing process exhibits more pronounced temporal characteristics in fracture propagation. The regulation mechanism of perforation spacing on pressure response is more complex: it not only reflects the influence of stress interference intensity on the breakdown pressure of a single stage but also demonstrates the control effect of the stress field induced by the first fracture on the initiation resistance of the subsequent fracture. This pattern provides important experimental evidence and theoretical support for perforation parameter optimization, fracture propagation sequence control, and construction of complex fracture networks in hydraulic fracturing.

Using the Polygon and Multi-Res modifiers in 3ds Max, vertices were added to the fracture surfaces, as shown in Figure (c), resulting in a total of 272,591 vertices. These vertices form the basic units of the mesh, and their positions determine the flow of the mesh lines, which are then used to define edges and faces. In constructing the mesh, triangular elements were prioritized because they behave more smoothly during dynamic adjustments and refinement, ensuring that the mesh follows the model’s shapes and curves naturally. As shown in Figure (d), this process produced a total of 541,158 faces. With the fracture surface mesh established, the fracture surface area can be obtained using measurement and statistical tools. As illustrated in Figure , the final fracture surface area for the three fracturing processes shows a clear trend: synchronous fracturing generally forms only a single bi-wing fracture, resulting in the lowest total fracture surface area. Sequential fracturing with varying perforation spacing yields a higher fracture surface area, and sequential fracturing considering temperature effects produces the largest fracture surface area due to the development of thermally induced microcracks within the specimen. By summing and comparing the final fracture surface areas across different conditions, an approximate assessment of the fracturing efficiency can be made. Sequential fracturing with varying perforation spacings shows about a 64% increase in fracturing efficiency relative to synchronous fracturing. Moreover, Figure shows that the total fracture surface area increases with both the well spacing and perforation spacing, indicating that the stress shadow effect positively promotes the development of the fracture network.

From an engineering perspective, these results indicate that perforation spacing should be designed by balancing fracture connectivity and stress interference. A small spacing may promote fracture coalescence, but excessive stress interference can restrict fracture extension and reduce the effective fracture surface area. In contrast, increasing the perforation spacing weakens stress interference and favors more independent fracture growth. For high-temperature reservoirs, thermally induced microcracks may reduce breakdown pressure and promote fracture complexity, suggesting that temperature effects should be considered when designing injection pressure and staging strategies. In addition, comparison between synchronous and sequential fracturing indicates that sequential injection may be more favorable for enlarging the fracture propagation range and improving the fracture surface development under the tested conditions.

Conclusion

This study addresses the insufficient experimental understanding of thermally induced stress shadow effects during multiwell hydraulic fracturing in deep high-temperature tight reservoirs. To clarify how the formation temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence jointly regulate fracture interaction, breakdown pressure evolution, and fracture-network morphology, true triaxial dual-well hydraulic fracturing experiments were conducted under controlled synchronous and sequential injection conditions. In addition, CT-based three-dimensional fracture reconstruction and an improved U-Net neural network were integrated to identify the fracture propagation behavior and spatial distribution patterns. Based on the reconstructed fracture morphology, three-dimensional geometric characteristics, and injection pressure responses, the following conclusions were obtained.(1)Under synchronous fracturing conditions, the perforation spacing regulates fracture connectivity by controlling the degree of stress shadow overlap and pressure communication between adjacent perforations. When the perforation spacing was 10 mm, the stress perturbation zones around adjacent perforations strongly overlapped, reducing the effective initiation resistance and promoting the rapid fracture coalescence into a single continuous main fracture. The corresponding peak breakdown pressure was 17.98 MPa. As the spacing increased to 30 and 50 mm, the peak breakdown pressures increased to 18.99 and 19.403 MPa, respectively, representing an overall increase of approximately 7.9% from 10 to 50 mm. This pressure increase indicates that the weakening of stress field superposition forces each fracture to overcome a larger local initiation resistance. Meanwhile, CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction showed that fracture propagation gradually changed from coalesced propagation to spatially separated, independent propagation. Therefore, the effect of perforation spacing is not limited to changing the fracture morphology; it also modifies the local stress redistribution and seepage disturbance between adjacent fractures, thereby controlling the transition from cooperative fracture growth to competitive fracture propagation.(2)Under sequential fracturing conditions, the pressure response and fracture morphology are jointly controlled by the perforation spacing and the stress redistribution induced by the first-stage fracture. At a perforation spacing of 10 mm, the first-stage breakdown pressure was 17.94 MPa, whereas the second-stage breakdown pressure increased to 20.71 MPa with a pressure difference of 2.77 MPa. This indicates that the first-stage fracture reconstructed the local stress field around the subsequent perforation and increased the initiation resistance of the latter fracture through stress shadow interference. When the perforation spacing increased to 30 mm, the pressure difference decreased to 1.26 MPa, and when the spacing further increased to 50 mm, it decreased to 0.43 MPa. Thus, the pressure difference between the two stages decreased by approximately 84.5% from 10 to 50 mm. This reduction demonstrates that increasing perforation spacing weakens the stress shadow imposed by the preexisting fracture, reduces interfracture seepage disturbance, and allows the subsequent fracture to propagate more independently. The CT-derived fracture morphology further confirms this mechanism, showing a transition from interacting and potentially connected fractures to separated dual-fracture propagation.(3)Formation temperature controls fracture propagation by coupling thermal damage, strength degradation, seepage-path activation, and stress shadow modification. As the temperature increased from 50 to 200 °C, the first-stage breakdown pressure decreased from 18.118 to 14.38 MPa, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 20.6%, while the second-stage breakdown pressure decreased from 18.98 to 14.63 MPa, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 22.9%. This reduction is attributed to thermally induced degradation of the artificial rock matrix and the development of microcracks, which reduce local fracture initiation resistance and provide preferential seepage pathways for fluid pressure transmission. In addition, the pressure difference between the two stages decreased to 0.25 MPa at 200 °C, indicating that thermal microcracks partially released local stress concentration and weakened the additional stress shadow effect generated by the first-stage fracture. Therefore, elevated temperature promotes more tortuous and multibranched fracture propagation not simply by reducing rock strength but by coupling thermal damage with stress redistribution and seepage disturbance, which together intensify competitive fracture propagation and increase fracture-network complexity.

Overall, this study demonstrates that the perforation spacing, formation temperature, and fracturing sequence jointly control thermally induced stress shadow effects and fracture-network evolution during dual-well hydraulic fracturing. Increasing perforation spacing weakens interfracture stress interference and promotes the transition from coalesced fracture propagation to independent fracture propagation, thereby enlarging the effective fracture propagation range. Elevated formation temperature reduces the breakdown pressure by weakening the artificial rock matrix and promoting thermally induced microcrack development, which further enhances fracture-network complexity. Compared with synchronous fracturing, sequential fracturing provides a more favorable stress redistribution condition for subsequent fracture propagation and is therefore more conducive to the formation of a larger fracture network under the tested conditions. In addition, the U-Net-based intelligent fracture identification and characterization method, combined with CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction, enables laboratory-scale recognition of pores, microcracks, thermally induced fractures, and hydraulic fractures. The main contribution of this work is to establish an integrated experimental, three-dimensional characterization, and intelligent identification framework for revealing the coupled effects of temperature, perforation spacing, and fracturing sequence on stress shadow-controlled fracture propagation. These findings provide experimental support for perforation design, injection sequence optimization, and fracture-network regulation in deep high-temperature tight reservoirs. Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cement-based artificial specimens used in this study ensured repeatability and controllability, but they cannot fully reproduce the mineral heterogeneity, bedding structures, natural weak planes, and preexisting fracture networks of real reservoir rocks. Second, the experiments were conducted at the laboratory scale using an idealized dual-well geometry and prescribed true triaxial boundary conditions; therefore, the measured perforation spacing and breakdown pressure values should not be directly transferred to field-scale reservoirs without scale correction. Third, several field factors, including proppant transport, fluid viscosity variation with temperature, multiphase flow, long-term leakoff, and wellbore–perforation–fracture coupling, were simplified in the present work. Future studies should combine larger-scale physical experiments, natural rock cores, thermohydro-mechanical numerical simulations, and field monitoring data such as microseismic events or production responses to further validate the applicability of the proposed mechanisms.
