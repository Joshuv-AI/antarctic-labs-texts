Mono-allelic MUTYH mutation as the likely inherited etiology of hereditary breast cancer in a patient from a multi-cancer family- report of a family and literature review
Authors: Akram Sarmadi, Shaghayegh Haghjooy Javanmard, Mehrdad Zeinalian, Majid Hosseinzadeh, Mohammad Amin Tabatabaiefar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer (BC) is the most prevalent cancer globally. Carriers of pathogenic variants in high- or moderate-penetrance genes, have an increased risk of developing hereditary BC (HBC). While, MUTYH is known to be associated with hereditary colonic polyposis and colorectal carcinoma, its role in BC is controversial. This study investigated the genetic cause of HBC in an Iranian family with a history of multiple cancer cases. MethodsClinical examination and exome sequencing (ES) was performed in a patient suffering from invasive ductal carcinoma from a family with several cases of different types of cancer. The pathogenicity of detected variants was done based on American Collage of Medical Genetics (ACMG) and Sanger sequencing was carried out for its validation. Furthermore, we performed a comprehensive review of the literature.ResultsHere, a pathogenic variant (p. A287Pfs*32) was identified in the MUTYH gene in mono-allelic status in four BC patients. However, this variant was previously reported as the cause of MutYH-associated polyposis (MAP) in homozygous status. The review of literature showed that the frequency of MUTYH mutation in BC patients population is in a range of 0.3-5.6%.ConclusionIn this study, a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the MUTYH gene was identified as the possible cause of BC in a multi-cancer family using ES. While the potential association between mono-allelic MUTYH mutations and an elevated risk of BC remains controversial, these findings highlight the necessity for a careful interpretation when assessing the role of MUTYH mutations in BC risk.

Introduction

Cancer is the manifestation of uncontrolled cell proliferation in the body with hundreds of cancer types identified. Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy among women, accounting for about 25% of all new cancer cases [1]. While most BC cases are considered to be sporadic, approximately 15–25% exhibit hereditary clustering [2]. About 20–30% of hereditary breast cancer (HBC) are due to pathogenic variants in the two high-penetrance genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2, especially in women with early-onset BC [3]. In addition to these genes, pathogenic variants in other cancer susceptibility genes would increase the risk of BC in carriers, with an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance [4, 5]. Carriers of pathogenic variants in high-penetrance genes (BRCA1/2, CDH1, PALB2, PTEN, STK11 and TP53) face a more than 5-fold chance of developing BC (> 50% lifetime cancer risk), whereas mutations in moderate-penetrance genes such as ATM, BARD1, BRIP1, CHEK2, NF1 and RAD51C/D, are associated with a 2 to 4-fold elevated risk (conferring 20–50% lifetime cancer risk [6, 7]. Many of these genes are involved in DNA repair system, particularly in homologous recombination (HR) of double-strand DNA breaks (DSBs) [8]. Additionally, mutations in genes which play a role in other DNA damage repair pathways have been implicated in familial BC cases [8].

The mutY homolog (MUTYH) gene, located on chromosome 1p34.3-p32.1, encodes a key enzyme in the base excision repair (BER) pathway [9]. It prevents G: C to T: A transversions, caused by oxidative damage –induced 8-oxo-G: A misspairs [10]. The significance of MUTYH in human disease has been initially associated with colon polyposis and colorectal cancer (CRC), a cancer that ranks as the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality [11]. MutYH-associated polyposis (MAP), a disorder predisposes patient for adenomatous polyps in the colon and rectum, is known to be inherited in an autosomal recessive manner (caused by bi-allelic germline mutations in the MUTYH) [9]. While several studies have explored the association between mono-allelic MUTYH pathogenic variants and BC, the link remains controversial [12–14]. However, emerging evidence indicates that heterozygous MUTYH variants may indeed contribute to BC risk [15–18].

The National Cancer Care Network (NCCN) provides guidelines for genetic testing and clinical management of HBC in patients with early-onset BC (under age 40) and with a strong family history of breast, ovarian and other related cancers. Testing for BRCA1/2 and other BC susceptibility genes has become standard practice for patients suspected to be HBC [19]. Identification of pathogenic variants in these genes, could facilitate the preventive measures such as risk-reducing surgical interventions, which have been shown to improve overall survival [20]. In this study, we performed exome sequencing (ES) in a patient with early-onset BC and a positive family history of breast, ovarian, prostate, liver and lung cancer, who was negative for mutation screening in BRCA1/2 genes.

Materials and methods

Subject and clinical data

A 43-year-old Iranian woman, (proband (III-4) in Fig. 1A), diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) in the right breast, was referred to our center for genetic evaluation. The first presentation of cancer was a palpable right breast mass at the age of 39. Her family history was notable for multiple maternal relatives with breast, prostate, liver, lung and brain cancers. Physical examination, mammography and ultrasound imaging were performed, followed by a biopsy of the mass during a complete mastectomy. Clinical evaluation including cancer staging (0 to 4 used TNM staging), histological grading (grade I, II or III) and Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining were conducted. Since she met the NCCN criteria for BRCA testing, she was first screened for BRCA1/2 genes variants. This study was approved by the Review Board of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: IR.MUI.MED.REC.1402.208). All subjects included in this study gave informed consent for genetic testing.

DNA extraction and molecular testing

The peripheral blood lymphocytes (5 ml) were collected from the patient and genomic DNA was extracted using CTGA PersPure UltraRapid Genomic DNA Extraction kit from blood (Cinna Teb Gene Azma, Iran), according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The quality of DNA extraction was checked using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and Nanodrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, USA) was used for assessing the concentration of it. BRCA1/2 variant screening was performed using the Axen™ BRCA1/2 panel (The amplicon libraries were sequenced by MiSeqDx sequencer).

Exome sequencing (ES) and bioinformatics analyses

About 300 ng of extracted genomic DNA was subjected to exome sequencing (ES) using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, US), with the mean coverage depth of 100X for greater than 92% of targeted regions. These clean reads were aligned to the human genome sequence (GRCh37/hg19) reference, using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) (http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/) program and variants calling were made using the Genomic analyzer tool kit (GATK). The filtration of the variants was performed based on MAF < 1% in dbSNP version 147, exome aggregation consortium (ExAC), 1000 genomes project phase 3 database, Iranome and GTaC (the local databases), and NHLBI GO exome sequencing project (ESP) database. Library preparation, sequencing, bioinformatics, and data analysis were performed as previously described [21, 22].

For assessing the pathogenicity of the candidate variants, several bioinformatics tools were used: Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.0.0) was used for population allele frequency analysis. MutationTaster (http://www.mutationtaster.org/), MutationAssessor (http://mutationassessor.org), MutPred (http://mutpred.mutdb.org/), PROVEAN (PROVEAN scores (v1.1)) and SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org) were used to calculate the pathogenicity score. Next, the variants were investigated in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), ClinVar, Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD) and the published literature to seek the novelty of the variant and its association with cancer.

Sanger sequencing and segregation analysis

To confirm the potential causative variants, direct Sanger sequencing was performed in the family members. Using exon-specific custom primers, we examined the co-segregation of the genotype with cancer phenotypes among cancer-affected family members. PCR amplification was conducted with designed primers (F: GGTGTCTGCAAAGGAGCTCT & R: TCCCTGAGGCTAAGAGCTGT), followed by fragment sequencing. Finally, electropherograms were compared with reference sequence (NM_001128425.2) using SeqMan software version 5.00 © (DNASTAR, Madison, WI, USA).

Results

Clinical findings

A 43-year-old Iranian woman was referred to our center with clinical manifestations of right breast malignancy. She was initially diagnosed with BC at age 39. Her family history revealed two sisters diagnosed with BC at ages 45 and 47, and a brother who developed pancreatic cancer in his 40 s and died several years later. Their mother was also deceased in her 50 s from BC. Additionally, two nieces were affected by BC at ages 38 and 29. Other reported malignancies in the family included liver, lung, and prostate cancers, as well as a brain tumor.

Physical examination revealed a 3.6 cm discrete mass with inflammatory skin changes and no nipple discharge in the right breast. Imaging studies (mammography and ultrasound) confirmed a 3.2 cm mass and pathological examination of the mastectomy specimen showed a 22 × 18 × 6.5 cm breast tissue containing a 3 cm ill-circumscribed, creamy-brown, firm mass located 3 cm from the skin, 2.5 cm from the deep margin, and 4 cm from the medial margin. Twenty lymph nodes were identified in the axillary fatty tissue, with the largest measuring 4 cm.

Neoplastic proliferation of epithelial cells in nests, sheets and rarely tubular patterns were the microscopic description of the specimen. The specimen showed lymphovascular invasion, severe pleomorphism and ductal carcinoma in Situ (DCIS), while the nipple was uninvolved. All 20 lymph nodes demonstrated tumoral involvement. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis showed that the tumor was estrogen receptor (ER) 0%, progesterone receptor (PR) 0%, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) + 1 (negative), consistent with Triple Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC). Her cancer was classified as stage 2 and grade III.

Molecular findings and bioinformatics analyses

BRCA1/2 NGS-based screening on the extracted DNA of the patient showed no mutation. Thus, the DNA sample was subjected to for ES. Analyzing the data obtained from ES identified a heterozygous frameshift variant (c.859delG, p. A287Pfs*32) in exon 10 of MUTYH gene. This one nucleotide deletion causes termination of translation at amino acid 318 of 549, and therefore not catalytically competent. Alanine 287 resides within the iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster loop (FCL) domain, a critical region that mediates both DNA binding and base excision repair functions of the protein. This variant was previously reported in a family with several cases affected with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) in homozygous status [23]. While, herein this variant is the cause of breast cancer in a multi-cancer family in heterozygous status. Frequency of this one-base deletion in heterozygous status is 0.000007 and 0.000008 in gnomAD and ExAC databases, respectively. Furthermore, the pathogenicity effects of this variant was confirmed according to the ACMG guidelines [24] (met PVS1, PS4, PM2 and PP3 criteria) that its evidence is describe below:PVS1: It is a null variant, where loss of function is a known mechanism of disease.PS4: The prevalence of the variant in affected individuals is significantly increased compared to the prevalence in controls (or at extremely low frequency in Exome Sequencing Project, 1000 Genomes Project, ExAC).PM2: Extremely low frequency in gnomAD population databases.PP3: Multiple lines of computational evidence supported the deleterious effect of the variant on the gene or gene product (Table 1).

Discussion

Breast cancer (BC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide, though incidence and mortality rates vary significantly across regions due to economic and lifestyle disparities [11]. While bi-allelic MUTYH pathogenic variants cause MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP), mono-allelic variants have been linked to an increased risk of colorectal cancer (CRC) (OR ~ 11) [25]. Surprisingly, about 2% of individuals in Northern European, U.S. and Australian populations harbor heterozygous for pathogenic MUTYH variants [26]. Emerging evidence suggests that both mono-allelic and bi-allelic MUTYH mutations may elevate BC risk, particularly in BRCA1/2-negative cases [27]. It was for the first time that Nielsen, et al. (2005), suggested the association between BC and MUTYH gene, who found that 18% of women with colonic polyposis and BC carried a pathogenic MUTYH variant [28]. This gene is classified in the low penetrance gene list, causing HBC (Table 2). In our studied patient, we identified a heterozygous allele in the MUTYH gene as potential contributor to cancer development. Previous studies have suggested a role for mono-allelic MUTYH mutations in tumorigenesis, as evidenced by a recent comprehensive analysis of 10,389 cancer patients across 33 tumor types [27]. A distinct mutational signature termed “signature 36”, arises from the defective BER repair system, as a result of excess G: C > T:A transversions, was firstly reported in a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patient with bi-allelic MUTYH germline pathogenic variant [29]. Subsequent studies have observed signature 36 in carriers of mono-allelic MUTYH germline mutations (with gastrointestinal or extra-intestinal tumors), possibly due to somatic inactivation of the remaining wild-type allele [30, 31]. Notably, this signature was also detected in two BC affected women carrying heterozygous MUTYH germline mutations, indicating that signature 36 may emerge in both mono-allelic and bi-allelic MUTYH mutation carriers, potentially contributing to tumorigenesis across different tissues [30].

Chan. et al., in 2018, found that 2.8% of Asian patients with hereditary cancer syndromes (61.8% of whom had hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC)) carried mono-allelic MUTYH mutation [32]. The prevalence of mono-allelic MUTYH pathogenic variants was 3.3% in CRC patients with any family history of cancer, compared to 1.4% in healthy controls [33]. Consequently, the United States National Comprehensive Cancer Network’s (NCCN-2022) recommends that MUTYH carriers with a first-degree relative affected by CRC, should undergo colonoscopy every 5 years starting at age 40, or 10 years before their relative’s CRC diagnosis age [34]. A retrospective study in 2024 involving genetic testing in a large cohort of hereditary cancer patients revealed that 25% of MUTYH mutation-positive patients were BC female patients, most of whom were heterozygous. Additionally, the study found that 7% of MUTYH-associated colonic polyposis patients (without BC) had a family history of BC [35].

To assess MUTYH’s role in BC pathogenesis, we reviewed literature, published between 2005 and 2024. Our analysis of 18 studies involving 21,860 BC, ovarian cancer (OC), and HBOC patients revealed MUTYH pathogenic variant frequencies ranging from 0.3 to 8.7% [18, 35–51] (Fig. 2). The association of MUTYH alteration with BC also was investigated in some studies with complex results: in two studies (sample size: 389 and 503) a positive association of heterozygous variants and BC had been reported (OR 1.02–3.39 and 1.17–17.58, respectively) [13, 43]. Multiple others studies found no association between mono-allelic MUTYH variants and BC risk [10, 12, 14, 16, 33, 47, 52–54]. Notably, Panigoro, et al., identified a positive association between a specific MUTYH intronic variant and early-onset breast cancer. (OR 3.75) [55]. In another study of 303 BRCA-negative Dutch BC patients, no pathogenic variant was found in the MUTYH gene, but 18 patients (5.9%) were heterozygous for 9 different variants of uncertain significant (VUSs) which were suspected to be the cause of the cancer, while family segregation was not performed in this study. Thus, they strongly recommended functional studies to clarify VUS pathogenicity in MUTYH gene [10].

Several studies reporting p.Y179C and p.G382D as the most common pathogenic variants in the MUTYH gene associated with CRC in individuals of European descent, likely due to a founder effect [35, 56]. These two variants are not only predominance in BC in some studies, but are linked to an increased risk of extra-colonic cancers such as breast, ovarian, bladder, duodenal or skin cancers [57]. Different variants exert distinct effects on protein function, leading to varying clinical outcomes. For example, a later age at diagnosis and less severe clinical manifestation are seen in carriers of p.G382D variant, attributed to the variant’s retention of higher adenine glycosylase activity [58]. Moreover, subtle structural differences between variants may influence lifetime cancer risk. Beyond enzyme activity, DNA binding activity, protein purification yield, protein-protein interactions and also post-translational modifications are altered as the results of different variants of MUTYH gene [59]. Given these functional disparities, studying genotype-phenotype correlations is necessary for predicting clinical manifestation, disease severity, and even estimating the age of cancer onset in carriers of different MUTYH pathogenic variants. This study identified variant lies within Fe-S cluster domain of MUTYH, a highly conserved region essential for its repair function, present in nearly all MutY homologs, with the exception of certain bacterial lineages [60]. This domain mediates interaction with SIRT6, an important element in repairing oxidative DNA damages [61]. Studies in overexpression and knockout mouse models have demonstrated that SIRT6 modulates DNA repair capacity, with a two-fold increase in repair efficiency when overexpressed and a 40% reduction when knocked out [61]. Regarding, SIRT6’s vital role in maintaining chromosomal stability, both Mutyh and Sirt mutated mouse have shown chromosomal aberrations [62]. Other than SIRT6, MUTYH have also interaction with some other proteins involved in DNA damage repair, such as PCNA, Hus1, ATR, APE-1, TopBP1, MSH6 and RPA [59]. Therefore, the truncated protein (p.A287Pfs*32) loses its interaction with some important repair proteins, some of which with vital role in other mechanisms of DNA repair such as mismatch repair (MMR). Consequently, MUTYH is involved in the initial steps of MMR and BER pathways, providing key contributions for genomic stability and maintenance.

There are reports of some other genes which are well-known for their causative role in a cancer in bi-allelic form, while mono-allelic variants may predispose carriers to other cancer types. For instance, bi-allelic variants in the PALB2 gene are associated to Fanconi anemia, whereas heterozygous loss-of-function variants increase the risk for breast and pancreas cancer [63]. Similarly, mono-allelic variants in MUTYH may function as low-penetrance predisposing factors for BC. However, in addition to genetic background, non-genetic factors such as age, ethnicity, or environmental influences may modulate clinical manifestations [51]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the contribution of mono-allelic MUTYH variants to cancer risk may be underestimated.

Our study suffers from a series of limitations including: the relatively small sample size of the patients in the co-segregation, the inherent technical limitation of ES, which lacks the capability to detect deep intronic variants. Furthermore, tumor profiling and functional analysis were not performed.

Conclusion

In this study, using exome sequencing, we identified the potential disease-causing variant in a large family with several cases of different types of cancer. While mutations in the MUTYH gene are well-known as the cause of colorectal cancer in bi-allelic form, their association with BC is still controversial. In the current study, we provide evidence that mono-allelic mutations may contribute to the development of cancer, in addition to increasing the risk of several other cancers.
