Emergency Medicine Pharmacist Prescriptive Authority for Resolution of Outpatient Prescription Issues
Authors: Kevin Burnham, Christina Tran, Leandra Garcia, Julian McCreary, Christo Philip, Osama A Shoair
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo describe the implementation and impact of a collaborative practice agreement (CPA) for emergency medicine pharmacists (EMPs) that grants them broad prescriptive authority to resolve outpatient discharge prescription issues independently.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective chart review of all prescriptions written by EMPs under a CPA to resolve discharge prescription issues at a 70-bed emergency department (ED) within a large health system in South Dallas, Texas. This review covered the period from November 1, 2021, to June 30, 2023. EMPs were authorized to independently address prescription problems without a provider’s direct involvement. We collected data on patient demographics, the types of issues encountered, pharmacist interventions, and the rates of patient revisits at 3 and 6 months related to the original prescription issue.ResultsEMPs resolved 295 discharge prescription issues by sending a total of 340 prescriptions, representing 11.6% of the 2919 prescriptions issued by EMPs during the study period. The most common issues addressed were related to insurance or affordability (28.1%) and medication unavailability (22%). EMPs most frequently responded by prescribing therapeutic alternatives (59.7%). Among the 295 patients with resolved prescription issues, 11.2% returned to the ED within 3 months, primarily due to persistent symptoms. At the 6-month mark, 13.2% of the 295 patients had returned to the ED, with all additional revisits citing an inability to follow-up with their provider. Analgesics were the most prescribed medication class (37.3%). No major safety concerns were identified, and all prescriptive actions were retrospectively met with provider agreement.ConclusionBroad EMP prescriptive authority through CPAs enables independent resolution of postdischarge prescription issues, improving care continuity, potentially reducing ED revisits, and sparing provider time to optimally take care of patients. This model can serve as a framework for other institutions aiming to optimize pharmacist-driven transitions of care.Keywords: emergency medicine, pharmacist, transitions of care, pharmacist prescribing, collaborative practice agreements

The Bottom Line

Many patients encounter issues filling prescriptions after leaving the emergency department (ED), leading to delays in their care. This study describes a program where pharmacists, working under a collaborative agreement with physicians, were granted the authority to write or adjust prescriptions directly to address these issues promptly. Among nearly 300 patients whose prescription issues were resolved, very few returned to the ED due to these issues. This model demonstrates that empowering pharmacists to independently resolve prescription issues can enhance access to necessary medications and save physicians’ time.

Introduction

Background

Postdischarge, outpatient pharmacies, and patients frequently contact emergency departments (EDs) to resolve prescription-related issues, reflecting persistent gaps in continuity of care. These issues may include out-of-stock medications, insurance and affordability, inaccurate doses or directions, incorrect formulations, omissions, prior authorization requirements, and ineffective treatment. Previous studies show that inaccuracies in dose or directions, and insurance or affordability are the most common issues.1,2

To tackle discharge prescription issues proactively, several institutions have implemented a prospective review of discharge prescriptions by emergency medicine pharmacists (EMPs).3, 4, 5, 6 Some institutions have resources to allow for the review of all prescriptions by EMPs 24 hours a day, 7 days per week.4 Other facilities have opted for EMPs to review high-alert medications via real-time notification selectively or may opt for retrospective review of discharge prescriptions sent days prior.5,7,8 Despite EMP efforts in these institutions, prescription issues continue to reach outpatient pharmacies, and patient-related medication issues still occur.

Importance

Outpatient pharmacies often provide wide, ambiguous resolution times, leading to care delays. Delays that impact patients’ perception of their overall care from the ED. Pharmacies and patients reach out to EDs about these issues. Unit clerks or automated answering mechanisms can have difficulty resolving or directing the issue. Triage staff will often attempt to locate providers to assist with reported issues. EMPs are particularly well-equipped to handle and prioritize these calls. Prescriptive authority for EMPs can be accomplished using collaborative practice agreements (CPAs). The degree to which this is accomplished varies by state legislation and regulation.9,10 CPAs are formal arrangements between pharmacists and prescribers that authorize pharmacists to initiate, modify, or manage drug therapy according to protocols or under physician delegation. CPAs are typically developed at the institutional or departmental level and require formal approval by hospital leadership and/or medical staff committees. Individual state laws and regulations influence the specifics of CPA implementation. As of 2023, all 50 US states permit pharmacists to engage in CPAs, but authorized services, oversight requirements, and prescribing autonomy differ widely, as described in depth previously.11 In EDs, the prevalence of pharmacist participation under CPAs is not well-documented, but usage is growing, particularly for targeted services such as medication administration, antimicrobial stewardship, and management of delayed cultures.12,13

EMP presence in hospital systems is increasing, and their services vary by practice site.14,15 Pharmacist prescribing is also growing, but this trend is plateauing.16 EMPs, with prescriptive privileges, can improve prescribing practices related to late culture results for discharged patients, but there are no reports of prescribing privileges for other outpatient prescription issues.17 Most prescribing privileges limit pharmacists’ scope to certain medication classes.

Goals of This Investigation

This study aimed to describe an established EMP practice and CPA at a single institution, granting broad scope prescriptive authority to manage discharge prescription issues, specifically, classifying and defining types of issues encountered and prescriptions written. Although other institutions have expanded EMP prescribing through credentialing and privileging pathways, these studies have not described the use of CPAs, management of outpatient pharmacy calls, or types of prescriptions sent by EMPs.5,17 This descriptive study can be a reference for administrators and EMPs looking to expand their service.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective chart review evaluated the use of a CPA that authorized EMPs to resolve discharge prescription issues or errors without direct provider involvement. The CPA was established in 2018 to manage late ED culture results, but later expanded to include resolving outpatient prescription issues. It specified the categories of prescription issues EMPs could manage, defined documentation requirements, and outlined physician oversight. All prescriptions written under the CPA were documented in the electronic health record (EHR) and initially routed to an attending physician for cosignature.

The study was conducted in a 70-bed community ED in South Dallas, Texas. The ED is part of a larger health system but not affiliated with an emergency medicine residency program. During the study period (November 1, 2021, to June 30, 2023), the ED averaged 6626 patient visits per month, totaling 132,522 encounters.

The EMP team consisted of 3 board-certified pharmacy specialists providing coverage for 17 hours per weekday and 10 hours per weekend day. The department also included attending physicians, physician assistants, and nurse practitioners collaborating to support patient flow and care transitions.

Study Sample

All patients receiving a prescription from EMPs to resolve a postdischarge prescription issue during the study period were included. Sex (female or male) was recorded as documented in the medical record and reflects the patient's biological classification. Prescription issues were identified through pulled intervention data on communications from patients or outpatient pharmacies. During EMP coverage hours, issues were escalated to EMPs via secure messaging. EMPs independently reviewed the patient’s chart to determine whether the reported problem warranted a prescription intervention. This analysis did not capture cases where the EMP determined that no intervention was needed, as only interventions resulting in new or modified prescriptions were consistently documented. Outside of coverage hours, prescription issues were rarely directed to physicians.

EMPs documented issue resolutions in notes, which included details about calls, clinical assessment, and actions taken, such as additions, changes, or discontinuations of prescriptions. Notes were routed to physicians for cosignature. EMP prescribing activities were reviewed monthly at division meetings for quality assurance and to identify trends. Prescription problems could be more specifically categorized as either “prescription errors” (eg, inappropriate dose, documented allergy, or contraindication) or “prescription issues” (eg, affordability, unavailability, unclear directions, or patient preference). These classifications were determined by the EMPs based on institutional references, guidelines, and clinical judgment. For the purposes of this study, the umbrella term “prescription issues” refers to all types of prescription-related problems encountered.

“Adverse effects” referred to undesirable side effects experienced by the patient from the originally prescribed medication, prompting a change. “Dose or direction error” involved incorrect, unsafe, or ambiguous dosing instructions, such as dosing outside of guideline-recommended ranges or missing frequency/timing details. “Contraindication” refers to a medication prescribed inappropriately due to a known condition or diagnosis. “Administration difficulty” applied when the dosage form was inappropriate for the patient’s age or ability. “Incorrect product” indicated the wrong medication was selected, whereas “ineffective therapy” was used when the prescribed medication did not meet its therapeutic intent. “New complaint” captured cases, where patients developed additional symptoms after discharge, requiring new treatment. “Medication unavailable” was used when the prescribed medication was not in stock at the receiving pharmacy.

Measurements

Documentation related to relevant EMP progress notes for CPA executions was collected through EPIC (Epic Systems), the EHR system. Charts were identified through filter-based searches, isolating pharmacist-authored progress notes and associated prescriptions between November 1, 2021, and June 30, 2023. Initial abstraction was conducted by one of the study's EMPs (KB) and independently validated by a second EMP (LG). Documentation included prescriber information, prescription issues, findings from EMP review, associated diagnoses, and final actions (eg, new medication prescribed, changes in dosage or directions, medication discontinuation). Issue categories, including dose errors, incorrect directions, or ineffective therapy, were determined based on EMP clinical judgment using institutional guidelines, standard drug references, or accepted prescribing practices. For example, doses exceeding maximum recommended limits or lacking appropriate adjustments were classified as dose errors. Cases of ineffective therapy were identified when prescribed medications did not align with standard treatment recommendations for the patient’s diagnosis or presented a suboptimal choice. Additional information gathered included details on additional patients and ED encounters. All EMP-written prescriptions were included for review.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient demographics, visit characteristics, prescription issues, provider interventions, and patient outcomes. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were reported as means with SD. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 19 (StataCorp LLC) and R Project version 4.5 (The R Foundation).

Ethics

This study was reviewed and determined to be exempt by the Methodist Health System Clinical Research Institute Institutional Review Board review due to no direct patient involvement, minimal risk, and deidentified and aggregately reported data (protocol number 045.PHA.2023.C).

Results

Characteristics of the Study Sample

During the 20-month study period, 165,727 prescriptions were written by ED providers, including 2919 (1.8%) prescriptions issued by EMPs. Of these, 340 prescriptions were explicitly written to resolve 295 outpatient prescription issues following ED discharge. The remaining 2579 EMP-authored prescriptions were for other purposes, including management of late ED culture results, electronic transcription or transfer of paper prescriptions, and verbal or informal requests from ED providers. General classes for all EMP-generated prescriptions are in Figure S1.

The average age of patients with prescription-related issues was 40.8 years (±20.8 years), and two-thirds were female (67%). The original prescribing provider was a physician in 57.6% of cases, whereas 42.4% were mid-level providers. Each of the 3 EMPs in the study handled a comparable volume of discharge prescription issues (EMP 1, 32.2%; EMP 2, 29.2%; EMP 3, 38.6%). Most issues (73.9%) were reported by patients or family members instead of outpatient pharmacies. A complete summary of study sample demographics and visit characteristics is found in Table 1.

EMPs most frequently prescribed therapeutic alternatives (59.7%). Other common interventions included adding medications to existing regimens (17.6%), changing formulations (12.9%), or modifying dosing or directions (9.5%). Notably, 25.8% of the original prescriptions involved controlled substances; due to legal restrictions, EMPs were unable to alter or initiate these medications and instead worked to find alternatives, when appropriate. ED revisits related to the index discharge prescription issue occurred in 11.2% of patients within 3 months, primarily due to persistent symptoms or inability to obtain follow-up. By 6 months, the cumulative ED revisit rate increased to 13.2%, reflecting 6 additional patients (2.0%) who returned to the ED between 3 and 6 months, all citing failure to receive follow-up with the referred provider.

EMP prescribing patterns were consistent across all 3 pharmacists. Analgesics represented the most frequently prescribed class (37.3%), with prescription-strength naproxen accounting for 16.6% of all individual prescriptions sent. The most common associated diagnoses were unspecified acute pain (12.5%), cough (5.8%), cystitis (5.1%), pneumonia or bronchitis (3.4%), and bacterial conjunctivitis (3.1%). Discussion

EMPs resolved 295 discharge prescription problems, primarily related to affordability, medication access, and dosing errors, by writing 340 prescriptions. Most issues were addressed with therapeutic alternatives, often in real-time, with minimal revisits. These findings demonstrate the feasibility and clinical relevance of allowing EMPs to manage discharge prescription issues autonomously, reducing physician workflow interruptions.

EMP services in the ED include direct patient care, code response, drug information, order review, and medication procurement. Although our institution provides extensive EMP coverage during peak hours, pharmacists do not perform a prospective review of discharge prescriptions. As a result, many prescription issues still arise after patients have left the ED. The model described in this study fills that gap by enabling real-time downstream resolution. One additional service highlighted in the 2021 Guidelines on EMP Services is transitions of care (TOC).14 These guidelines state that EMPs “can field (outpatient pharmacy) calls and identify the options to fix the issue with the discharge prescription, discuss with a physician, and communicate the decision to the outpatient pharmacy.”18 Physicians’ time-saving potential is diminished if EMPs only field calls. In contrast, our study supports a more direct model in which EMPs resolve issues independently while maintaining accountability and oversight.

Two primary methods for pharmacists in the United States to prescribe are CPAs and autonomous prescribing. Autonomous prescribing is limited to statewide protocols and class-specific medications, which are not broad enough to solve diverse discharge prescription issues. As described in our study, CPAs are currently one of the only feasible routes for EMPs to establish TOC services, but requirements and opportunities differ by state.9 EMPs should be aware of laws and statutes involving CPAs in their state. Permitted CPA functions to consider by the state include initiating or modifying therapy and ordering or interpreting laboratory tests. In Texas, the location of our study, pharmacists can initiate and modify existing therapy in collaboration with delegating physicians. Delegation of drug therapy management must meet specified conditions.19 One condition is periodic review by delegating physicians, which was accomplished in our setting with real-time notification and progress notes requiring cosignature. Since the service’s inception, no issues or concerns have been identified by participating providers. The cosignature requirement on progress notes was later removed, given the safety of our service. Periodic spot review is still maintained monthly.

Aside from CPAs, institutions in other states have leveraged credentialing and privileging to enhance pharmacists’ practice scope and autonomy.20 In states such as Ohio, EMPs were privileged to assist in the TOC activities of the late ED culture review. A prospective discharge prescription review noted that a pharmacist–physician CPA was not used.5,17 Like CPAs, institutional credentialing also requires periodic review and carries a burden of resource utilization to initiate and maintain. State boards of pharmacy rules and statutes differ regarding this approach, and some states may require a pharmacist–physician CPA despite institutional privileges.20,21 In establishing either or both methods, it is essential to realize pharmacists may be granted broad prescriptive authority for certain medications and disease states and not be confined to protocols deterring clinical judgment.

Discharge prescription issues and errors are well-documented, and their frequency has increased with the widespread adoption of e-prescribing.22 In our study, medication affordability and pharmacy stock shortages accounted for nearly half of all resolved issues. Some electronic prescribing platforms incorporate real-time formulary and inventory checks, but capabilities remain limited and inconsistently implemented.23 Many hospitals limit EMP involvement to postdischarge culture reviews, specific high-alert medications, or serving as an intermediary role, triaging issues before asking for provider assistance.24 We have described methods for enhancing this approach by eliminating the intermediary steps and leveraging EMPs to resolve discharge prescription issues independently. Previous studies identified anticoagulants and anti-infectives as medication classes requiring the most interventions.5 Analgesics and anti-infectives were the most frequent prescription classes requiring changes due to issues in our study.

Nonadherence to medication changes at discharge from the inpatient setting is associated with increased risk of adverse events and readmissions.25 It is essential to ensure smooth TOC at discharge, especially in the ED, where revisits are frequent. One study found that 71.3% of patients’ ED revisits were due to inadequate management and no improvement in symptoms, and 11.7% of system-related causes for revisits were due to medication-related issues.26 Mitigating unnecessary medical costs by preventing revisits for discharge prescription issues is just 1 potential aspect of this service. This service enhances provider efficiency by limiting interruptions. ED physicians average 11.2 to 12.5 interruptions per hour.27,28

Cost justification for EMP services remains a barrier, as many interventions are not directly billable. However, several studies estimate over $300,000 in annual cost avoidance per EMP, based on time saved and adverse events prevented.29,30 EMPs have been shown to save 75 minutes of provider time per shift, with the largest proportions of time saved stemming from fielding general questions from staff (33.8%) and managing critically ill patients (15.4%); 50.8% of time savings directly from CPA-related tasks, such as handling prescription changes and reviewing late results; only 4.1% directly related to pharmacy calls.31 Using national averages, this time saved could equate to an additional 2 patients seen by providers per shift.32, 33, 34

Limitations

This study has several limitations that may affect interpretation and generalizability. First, the analysis was conducted at a single institution with robust EMP coverage of 17 hours per weekday and 10 hours per weekend day. National survey data suggest that approximately one-quarter of EMP practice sites provide less than 8 hours of daily coverage, and over one-third lack weekend services.14 However, some sites may be able to offer expanded 24-hour coverage. Due to inconsistent documentation of the contact time, we could not perform temporal trend analysis to determine when most issues occurred.

Second, the study relied on EMP-authored documentation to identify and characterize interventions. Prescriptions not requiring change were not captured, as these encounters were not consistently documented in a structured format. As a result, our findings may underrepresent the total volume of pharmacist interventions and overrepresent cases involving medication changes. This limits a complete evaluation of the EMP’s impact on TOC and provider time saved. Additionally, categorization of prescription issues, particularly dose errors and ineffective therapy, involved the pharmacist’s clinical judgment. This study included only patients who received an EMP intervention, without a comparator group of patients who did not receive pharmacist support. Given the structure of EMP integration at our institution, where pharmacists were available daily and notified in real time of discharge issues, it was not feasible to define a standard-care cohort for comparison.

Third, the generalizability of study findings may be limited due to variability in state pharmacy laws and collaborative practice regulations. This model’s scalability to other institutions is uncertain, particularly where EMP coverage is limited or has competing priorities. Replicating this service would require careful alignment with staffing resources and departmental priorities.

Finally, although all recorded revisits were related to the original prescription issue, our revisit outcomes remain difficult to interpret in isolation. These revisits could reflect suboptimal resolution of the initial medication problem or represent appropriate follow-up for ongoing care needs. Without a comparator cohort, the significance of these revisit rates remains uncertain.

Future iterations of this program will aim to systematically capture all pharmacist interventions, including those not requiring a prescription change, by integrating standardized documentation templates. These enhancements will allow for more accurate workload assessment and enable future cost-effectiveness analyses of pharmacist-led prescription management.

In summary, this study demonstrates that EMPs, operating under a CPA with broad prescriptive authority, can independently resolve numerous issues related to outpatient discharge prescriptions. This pharmacist-led model may reduce delays in care, prevent ED revisits, and alleviate provider workload. Institutions seeking to enhance TOC should consider integrating EMP-led prescription management.
