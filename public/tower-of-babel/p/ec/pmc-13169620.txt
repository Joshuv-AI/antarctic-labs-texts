A computational framework and software tool for generating complex survival data with predefined censoring rates in simulation studies
Authors: Haichang Chen, Qiang Yao, Yidie Lin, Meijing Hu, Jiao Pei, Cairong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn increasing number of survival analysis methods require validation under complex simulation scenarios. However, existing data generation tools are often constrained in practical applications: they typically simulate simplified censoring mechanisms and lack the flexibility to achieve predefined censoring rates when dealing with complex survival distributions. This study aims to develop a comprehensive computational framework and reproducible software to generate simulated data that meets more diverse needs.MethodsRooted in the concept of numerical inversion, our framework employs automated algorithms to solve closed-form equations for the key parameters of censoring distributions. This enables the precise derivation of parameters required to achieve researcher-specified censoring rates, thereby generating simulated datasets that faithfully mirror complex survival distributions. This tool integrates four core functionalities: constructing complex survival distributions based on finite mixture models; simultaneously handling Type I and Type III censoring data; precisely calibrating user-defined censoring rates; and incorporating covariate effects within survival time distributions. We validated the tool across 64 simulated scenarios, systematically varying survival distributions, censoring distributions, predefined censoring rates, and covariate structures.ResultSimulation results across 64 scenarios demonstrated the robustness and precision of the proposed toolkit. The average observed censoring rates from 1000 replications showed negligible deviation from the predefined targets. The stability of the generation process was confirmed, with the standard deviations of total censoring rates consistently remaining below 1.7% across all simulated settings.ConclusionThe study provides a standardized computational framework by automating the generation of complex survival data with precise censoring control, thereby enabling researchers to conduct simulation studies across diverse survival analysis scenarios and providing essential support for the rigorous validation of statistical methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02843-y.

Introduction

In simulation studies, the generation of simulated data represents the most fundamental and critical step. When comparing the performance of new and old models, the core advantage of simulation research lies in its controllability and repeatability, as presetting all parameters governing data generation allows researchers to make simulated data effectively cover and align with diverse real-world scenarios. Survival time is central to the survival data generation process and is influenced by the complex interplay between survival time distributions, censoring mechanisms, and covariate effects [1]. Although adjusting correlation parameters facilitates flexible simulation design, existing methods lack sophisticated mechanisms for the coordinated control and simultaneous specification of the aforementioned factors. Consequently, researchers often rely on oversimplified configurations that fail to reflect the full complexity of real-world data.

Regarding the survival time distribution, the majority of research only considers simple survival time distributions, such as exponential distribution and Weibull distribution [2, 3]. The former has simple assumptions and poor realistic applicability, and the latter is relatively flexible but cannot fit complex non-monotonic situations. Regarding censoring mechanisms, Type I (administrative censoring) and Type III (random censoring/dropout) right censoring are the two most prevalent forms in medical research [1]. However, existing simulation studies often oversimplify these mechanisms [4, 5]. For instance, some studies focus exclusively on Type I censoring by setting a fixed study duration to achieve a target event rate, thereby neglecting random dropouts that occur during the follow-up [6]. In effect, such approaches fail to account for Type III right censoring, which is a critical component of real-world survival data. Conversely, other studies assume censoring times follow a specific probability distribution to mimic random censoring, but fail to account for the administrative truncation inherent [7, 8]. These oversimplifications fail to capture the variation in effective sample size and information content over time. Consequently, this may lead to inaccurate assessments of statistical power, underestimated standard errors, and ultimately, unreliable methodological evaluations [9, 10]. Moreover, current simulation data generation methods rarely derive censoring parameters through numerical methods or closed-form formulas, resulting in insufficient customization capabilities for simulation scenarios. This limitation hinders the rigorous validation of statistical models across diverse real-world conditions.

In 2022, a study proposed a generalized framework for simulating Type I and Type III right censoring survival data with predefined censoring rates [11]. Although this method performs well in simulating survival data from traditional distributions, its applicability to scenarios involving complex survival time distributions (e.g., non-monotonic hazard or multi-modal distributions) remains expandable. First, a closed-form solution for the parameters under the Weibull distribution cannot be derived without additional assumptions. The restriction undermines the application flexibility of the Weibull distribution, while also increasing computational complexity and creating challenges in censoring rate control [9]. Furthermore, when survival time follows a Weibull distribution, its hazard function is monotonic, which makes its overall survival distribution inflexible and potentially inconsistent with real-world scenarios. If complex survival time distributions are taken into account, it is difficult to include survival time covariates when generating survival times and censored times.

Moreover, an increasing number of survival data analysis methods are beginning to focus on survival data with non-traditional complex distributions in recent years. Flexible parameter models [12, 13], B-spline models [14, 15], and finite mixture models [16] are methods for fitting complex survival time distributions. When researchers aim to explore the performance of novel survival analysis methods under complex distributions via simulation studies, generating simulated survival data that conforms to such distributions is more aligned with the methods’ inherent characteristics and practical application scenarios. However, current survival simulation tools inadequately address these factors, yielding datasets that fail to authentically reflect the core characteristics of real-world data. Specifically, several methods currently exist in R software for generating censored survival data, but they all have limitations in setting Type I right censoring, incorporating covariates, and modeling complex survival time distributions. For example, the SimHaz package can only generate Type III right censoring times with an exponential distribution, while the survsim package allows Type III right censoring times to follow more distributions, but still has limitations in setting Type I right censoring and covariates [17, 18]. The simsurv package, although supporting covariates and complex distributions, cannot preset the censoring rate [19].Therefore, to make the simulated data features more representative of real-world data, these tools still have room for improvement.

Based on this, we developed a comprehensive computational framework and software implementation that simultaneously integrates four key functionalities: modeling complex survival time distributions, supporting two types of right censoring, precisely calibrating to predefined censoring rates, and incorporating survival time-related covariates. Specifically, to overcome the traditional trial-and-error approach in determining the censoring rate, we employ a numerical inversion strategy to precisely derive the key parameters of the censoring distribution, thereby generating simulated datasets that strictly match the predefined target rate. By integrating theoretical rigor with practical utility, this framework provides a standardized and reproducible toolkit. It empowers medical researchers to generate synthetic data that faithfully captures clinical complexity, establishing a robust benchmark for the validation of statistical methodologies.

The probability density function of this sum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\eta_i}\left(\cdot\right)$$\end{document}, simplifying the calculation of the censoring rate. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document} is an upper and lower bound on the integral of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\eta_i}\left(\cdot\right)$$\end{document}.

The covariate vector consists of variables from multiple distributions, making the distribution complex and difficult to parameterize. Fei Wan used kernel density estimation to obtain the probability density function of the covariate effect sum, the method is essentially a non-parametric method and cannot directly derive the domain of the function. Therefore, previous study has employed a plot-based visual inspection approach to approximate the function domain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document} by examining the regions where the probability densities of random variables approach zero in the Gaussian kernel density estimation plot [7]. However, this method has low accuracy, which may lead to deviations in the censoring rate of the generated simulated data.

This study proposes the probability density threshold method to identify \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document}. Mainly, a threshold is set according to the size of the probability density, and the portion of the random variable taking values greater than this threshold is regarded as the valid portion, in which case the minimum and maximum values of the random variable values corresponding to the probability density threshold are the domains of the aforementioned \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\eta_i}\left(\cdot\right)$$\end{document}. In this study, five probability density thresholds were predefined, namely 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001 and 0.00001, and the total censoring rates of survival data generated under these five different thresholds were compared in the subsequent simulation study section to provide a basis for the selection of probability density thresholds.

Censoring parameter estimation with finite mixture models and integrate-uniroot solving

Flexible parameter models [12, 13], B-spline models [14, 15], and finite mixture models [16] are methods for fitting complex survival time distributions. Finite mixture models are a type of survival analysis method that accounts for mixed distributions, and they have been applied in survival modeling [20]. The core idea is to assume that the study population consists of two subgroups, each with its own set of survival time distribution parameters [16, 21]. Compared to other models, the finite mixture model features an intuitive and easy-to-understand structure, with a flexible hazard function shape that facilitates modeling. The model is expressed as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}\left(t\right)=p*{S}_{0A}\left(t\right)+(1-p)*{S}_{0B}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}\left(t\right)=p*exp(-{\lambda\:}_{A}*{t}^{{\gamma\:}_{A}})+(1-p)*exp(-{\lambda\:}_{B}*{t}^{{\gamma\:}_{B}})$$\end{document}

Equation (6) represents the basic form of the model, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is the baseline survival function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0A}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0B}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} denote the baseline survival function for populations A and B, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p$$\end{document} denote the mixed ratio of the two populations. It is common to default that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0A}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0B}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} follow the Weibull distribution, which leads to Equation (7), with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{A},\:{\gamma\:}_{A},\:{\lambda\:}_{B},\:{\gamma\:}_{B}$$\end{document} representing the Weibull parameters for populations A and B.

In 2013, Crowther and Lambert developed a Stata tool based on finite mixture models to generate proportional hazards survival data with complex distributions [22]. By adjusting the parameters of the two Weibull distributions in the finite mixture model, survival data with different hazard function patterns can be generated, including monotonic and non-monotonic forms with one or more extrema. In 2021, Brilleman et al. [19] implemented this method in R via the simsurv package, which will be used to generate the complex survival distributions in this study.

Based on this, it is necessary to determine the key parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} of the Type III right censoring distribution before generating survival data with predefined censoring rates. Assuming the maximum follow-up time (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:maxt$$\end{document}) is fixed to preserve the structural features of the baseline survival distribution, the overall censoring rate can be formulated as a comprehensive integral equation. Given that the selected Type III censoring distributions (exponential and uniform) are governed by a single unknown parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}, i.e., the rate parameter or the interval upper bound), we implemented a numerical solution. Specifically, we utilized the integrate () function for numerical integration and the uniroot () function to iteratively solve for the specific parameter value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} that satisfies the target total censoring rate. The stability of this numerical search is mathematically guaranteed by the existence and monotonicity of the root. Specifically, a valid solution exists only if the target total censoring rate strictly exceeds the baseline Type I censoring rate anchored by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:maxt$$\end{document}. Moreover, the total rate is strictly monotonic with respect to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}: it increases with the exponential rate parameter and decreases as the uniform upper bound increases. The algorithm exploits this monotonicity for efficiency. When the Type III censoring follows a uniform distribution, we first assume the upper bound parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} equals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:maxt$$\end{document}, and compare the calculated total censoring rate with the predefined censoring rate to determine the upper bound of the integral. If the calculated rate is less than or equal to the predefined rate, it mathematically proves the true \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:\le\:\:maxt$$\end{document}. Accordingly, the integral upper limit is dynamically assigned the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}, meaning all Type III censoring occurs before the administrative cutoff, thus reducing the Type I censoring rate to zero. Conversely, if the calculated rate exceeds the predefined rate, the true \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:>maxt$$\end{document}. Here, the integral upper limit is structurally restricted to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:maxt$$\end{document}, and Type I censoring exists (See the Appendix A. Section 4.1.2: Tables A6-A7).

Additionally, it is worth noting that the numerical stability of the root-finding process largely depends on the configuration of search boundaries. While the exponential distribution exhibits high computational tolerance at extreme lower bounds, the uniform distribution is acutely sensitive to this limit. An excessively small lower bound parameter value can trigger severe computational singularities or errors. Therefore, for the uniform distribution, the lower bound was set to a near-zero positive value (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-6}$$\end{document}), while the upper bound was assigned a sufficiently large constant to guarantee opposite signs at the two endpoints. This boundary specification ensured highly robust root-finding, demonstrating stable convergence across all simulation scenarios.

Based on the key parameters of the Type III right censoring distribution derived from the above calculations, survival data with a predefined censoring rate can be generated using the simsurv package [19]. The algorithm for solving the key parameters of censoring distributions is encapsulated in the custom R function thetacal (). This function accommodates four distinct covariate scenarios: (1) null model (no covariates), (2) single continuous (normal), (3) single dichotomous, and (4) mixed covariates (Normal and Binary). And it supports either Uniform or Exponential censoring distributions. Notably, the estimation strategy is adaptive: Gaussian kernel density estimation is employed for complex covariate structures, while standard parametric approaches are used for simpler cases. Code explanations and parameter specifications are provided in Appendix A (Sect. 4: Tables A4-A11).

Methods for generating survival data with predefined censoring rates

The core functionality for generating survival data with predefined censoring rates is encapsulated within the custom R function datagen (). The code explanations and parameter definitions are provided in Appendix A (Sect. 5: Tables A12-A14). The procedure operates as follows: (1) set the number of random seeds; (2) perform covariate sampling to construct a covariate data set; (3) generate a complex survival distribution data considering covariate effects by sampling using the simsurv package (without considering censoring at this stage); (4) sample Type III censoring times using the runif () or rexp () functions in R, according to the specified Type III right censoring distribution. Specifically, the key parameters utilized by the sampling functions (such as “theta” in the expression runif(size,0,theta) shown in row 6 of Appendix A Table A12) are directly obtained from the calculation results of the aforementioned thetacal () function; (5) compare the survival time from Step 3, the Type III censoring time from Step 4, and the maximum follow-up time to determine the outcome type for each individual (event, Type III censoring, or Type I censoring).

Given that different studies may require consideration of distinct types of right censoring (e.g., focusing solely on Type III right censoring), the programme developed in this study retains survival outcomes and survival times for various right censoring types. It also preserves the specific right censoring categories when considering two types concurrently. Furthermore, the key parameter calculation programme and survival data generation programme outlined in the appendix consider two types of covariates: binary categorical variables and normally distributed variables. Future researchers may modify these according to specific study settings, adding, removing, or altering the distribution types of covariates in each section.

Simulation evaluation and analysis

In the simulation study section, we first estimate the hazard functions for data generated from different survival distributions and Type III right censoring distributions without considering covariates. The hazard function estimation is implemented using the muhaz package in R software [23]. The core principle of the muhaz package is to estimate survival hazard functions using kernel density estimation. As kernel density estimation is a non-parametric method, it provides a more accurate description of the hazard functions derived from survival data. After generating data under 64 simulated conditions, we first calculate the total censoring rate, Type I right censoring rate, and Type III right censoring rate of the data under each condition. This serves to verify the correctness of the predefined censoring rate generation method and the accuracy of the total censoring rate. Additionally, considering that both parametric methods and kernel density estimation can theoretically obtain the probability density function of covariate effects when only one binary covariate or only one normally distributed covariate is present, we also compared the time consumed by these two methods in calculating Type III right censoring key parameters, the values of the calculated key parameters, and the overall censoring rate of the simulated data. The two methods were computed 100 times under different simulation conditions, and based on the results, 1000 simulations of survival data were generated. Finally, the probability density threshold method is used to calculate the probability density function domain of the covariate effects from kernel density estimation, and key parameter values and total censoring rates are compared under different thresholds. Notably, the empirical censoring rate for each of the 1000 simulated datasets across all scenarios was calculated using the custom R function censoringcal (). All statistical analyses and simulation studies were performed using R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The complete code for all functions also can be found in the Appendix B.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a survival data generation method and its corresponding R program, aiming to address the challenge of producing accurate data under complex censoring scenarios (concurrent Type I and Type III right censoring) as well as complex survival time distributions (Weibull or finite mixed distribution). Simulation results demonstrated that the method performs robustly across 64 simulation conditions. The average total censoring rate from 1000 simulations showed minimal deviation from the predefined rate, with all standard deviations of total censoring rates below 1.7. This demonstrated the method’s capability to accurately generate survival data aligned with predefined censoring rates and maintain rate accuracy across diverse simulation conditions. In addition, we found that when Type III right censoring followed a uniform distribution and the upper parameter of the uniform distribution was less than the predefined maximum observation time limit, the Type I right censoring rates were all 0, which was the same as the derivation during the theoretical and methodological construction, indicating the correctness of the methodological reasoning process in this study. Therefore, our computational framework and software tool can generate high-quality simulated data, which supports comparative studies on the performance of survival analysis methods under complex survival distributions. Furthermore, it enables the presetting of censoring rates, playing a crucial role in exploring the applicability and performance differences of the same method under varying simulation conditions. Notably, we use numerical integration and root-finding algorithms to obtain censoring parameters, which avoids the complex and manual process of adjusting each censoring parameter in simulated scenarios. Next, we will discuss some considerations for applying this method in practice.

In single-covariate simulation scenarios, parametric methods yielded key parameters of Type III right censoring and total censoring rates comparable to those derived from kernel density estimation, while requiring significantly less computation time. We hypothesize that the elevated computational burden incurred by the kernel density estimation method may result from a combination of Monte Carlo sampling and the smoothing procedure. In Monte Carlo simulations, a sufficient sample size is typically required to mitigate random variation [24]. Given the need for smoothed kernel density estimation in this study, we set a sample size of 10,000 for this phase of the Monte Carlo sampling. Moreover, the smoothing parameter employed for the kernel density estimation was small, which may have further prolonged the computational time [25]. We suggest that researchers customize the sample size and smoothing parameters of Monte Carlo simulation according to their specific study requirements.

In simulation scenarios involving multiple covariates, adopting a lower probability density threshold enhances the accuracy of the data generation process. Under the simulation conditions of this study, setting the probability density threshold below 0.01 yielded a total censoring rate that more closely approximated the predefined target. The probability density threshold is a key indicator for integrating covariate effects, and its value determines the upper and lower bounds of the final integration. Increasing the probability density threshold narrows the resulting upper and lower integration bounds. This narrowing effect reduces the integration area, thereby inducing information loss. In turn, such loss introduces substantial bias into the calculation of key parameters, ultimately causing the total censoring rate of the generated data to deviate significantly form the predefined value. Additionally, we find threshold variations have negligible impact on computation time. Thus, using a lower probability density threshold for key parameter calculations yields more accurate results without added computational cost. The probability density threshold method designed in this study is more precise than the visual inspection method used in previous study [7]. It ensures consistent criteria for determining upper and lower limits when integrating the probability density distributions of covariate effects under different simulation conditions, while also guaranteeing symmetry in handling the tails on both sides of the probability density distribution during integration.

In addition, we recommend exercising caution in using the uniform distribution as the distribution of Type III right censoring time. With an increase in the predefined total censoring rate, the computed upper limit for the uniform distribution of Type III right censoring time declines. This phenomenon is particularly evident when the upper limit is below the maximum observation time and the Type I right censoring rate equals zero. In this case, the simulated data exhibits two key features: no individuals experience Type I right censoring, and all observed survival times fall strictly below the upper limit of the uniform distribution. Setting the maximum observation time can be regarded as a truncation of the hazard function, meaning only the portion of the hazard function where time is less than or equal to the maximum observation time is retained. When the upper limit of the uniform distribution is below the maximum observation time, it can be regarded as a secondary truncation of the hazard function. This secondary truncation is uncontrollable, which makes it hard to ensure the shape of the remaining hazard function aligns with researchers’ requirement. For instance, the hazard function of the WBFMD in this study has two extreme points, located near 0.834 and 3.462. However, the upper limit of the uniform distribution with a predefined censoring rate of 80% is only 1.411, which is less than the second extreme point of 3.462. This triggers a secondary truncation, which in turn results in the hazard function of the generated survival data retaining only one extreme point. Thus, it fails to align with the hazard function shape initially established in this study.

Finally, several aspects of this study warrant further refinement. First, while the probability density threshold method defines the covariate effect domain, it implicitly assumes unimodal and light-tailed distributions. In complex settings, applying a density threshold risks truncating valid secondary peaks or plausible extreme values in multimodal or heavy-tailed covariate distributions. Nevertheless, employing a lower threshold can mitigate the risk to a certain degree without incurring additional computational overhead. Future research could explore computationally tractable approaches for multidimensional joint density functions to further refine this framework. Secondly, regarding numerical implementation, the uniform distribution’s parameter-solving is inherently more sensitive to the lower bound of the root-finding interval than that for the exponential distribution in R’s uniroot () function. Under specific simulated conditions, an excessively narrow bound may trigger computational singularities (due to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1/{\uptheta\:}$$\end{document} density structure), leading to solution failures. This sensitivity is inherently tied to the uniroot () function’s reliance on Brent’s method [26, 27], which utilizes Lagrange interpolation and is highly dependent on interval specifications and tolerance levels [28]. Consequently, future research could explore alternative solvers, such as the rootSolve package to evaluate different root-finding methodologies [29]. Thirdly, while our numerical method calculates exact theoretical parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} independent of sample size, the empirical censoring rate in finite datasets remains subject to random sampling variation [9, 30]. Consequently, although our simulation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{N}=1000$$\end{document}) yielded stable results, smaller datasets will inevitably exhibit wider fluctuations around the target rate. Lastly, to prevent the premature truncation of complex survival distribution features, our framework prioritizes fixing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:maxt$$\end{document}, then deriving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} via numerical inversion to satisfy the target total censoring rate. While the certain research contexts may require independent specification of Type I or Type III censoring rates, our current design ensures the integrity of the observation window. Future methodological research could explore related optimization modules to accommodate these dual-constraint requirements. Nevertheless, we have developed a robust computational framework and tool for generating complex survival data with predefined censoring rates. By incorporating both Type I and Type III right censoring while accommodating complex survival distributions and covariate effects on survival time, this tool provides indispensable support for the diverse simulation scenarios required to rigorously validate emerging statistical methodologies.

Conclusion

The computational framework and software tool proposed in this study effectively integrate Type I and Type III right censoring, complex survival distributions, and survival time covariates. It is capable of accurately generating survival data that aligns with specific research requirements. Furthermore, this study rigorously validates critical technical aspects, including covariate effect handling, probability density threshold settings, and censoring time distributions. Consequently, this research provides a comprehensive and high-performance option for generating simulated data to support future methodological simulation studies in survival analysis.
