Au clusterzymes encapsulated in nanogel activated by alternating magnetic field to promote white fat browning and combat obesity
Authors: Yeying Li, Mengfan Xu, Xiaona Wang, Kexin Zhang, Yu Su, Mengyuan Zhao, Le Li, Jinglei Zhou, Maoqi Luo, Xinyi Gao, Zhenkun Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity, a major global public-health challenge, is a well-established risk factor for numerous metabolic disorders. In this study, we developed a novel nanosystem based on Au clusterzymes (AuCZy) encapsulated in chitosan (CS) and functionalized with an adipose homing peptide (AHP), referred to as AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogel. Specifically, an alternating magnetic field (AMF) was applied to modulate its enzyme-mimicking activity. It was found that AMF stimulation significantly enhanced SOD-like and CAT-like activities, endowing AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels with superior reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity. Furthermore, in vivo experiments demonstrated the efficient targeting of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels to white adipose tissue (WAT). After a 45-day treatment regimen (once every three days) with AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF, obese mice exhibited an 18% reduction in body weight compared to the control group. This combination treatment also suppressed WAT expansion, improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, and ameliorated lipid metabolic disorders. Mechanistic investigations revealed that AMF-enhanced ROS scavenging by AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels activated the AMPK signaling pathway, promoting white fat browning and thermogenesis. This work demonstrates the compelling therapeutic potential of clusterzymes combined with AMF for the treatment of obesity and associated metabolic diseases.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04235-2.Keywords: clusterzyme, nanogels, magnetic hyperthermia therapy, white adipose tissue, obesity

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a significant challenge for public health worldwide. It is characterized by an excessive fat accumulation and/or an abnormal fat distribution within the body, resulting in abnormal weight gain. Obesity is a common cause of a variety of metabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular diseases [1]. The World Obesity Federation (WOF) has recently reported that the global population of obese adults will soar from 524 million in 2010 to 1.13 billion in 2030, with an increase of over 115% [2]. Obesity not only poses a serious threat to human health but also brings about a substantial burden on healthcare and the economy.

Current approaches to obesity management encompass lifestyle modifications, medication, and bariatric surgery, yet are respectively limited by suboptimal efficacy, adverse effects, and surgical risks. Weight-loss medications are widely used in clinical practice, but their use is often accompanied by a series of adverse effects. For instance, pancreatic lipase inhibitor orlistat can cause liver injury and gastrointestinal symptoms [3], whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide and liraglutide) may link to pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and gastroparesis [4]. Thus, there is an urgent need to seek new strategies for obesity treatment with enhanced safety and efficacy.

Metal clusters, a novel type of nanomaterials, are distinguished by their extremely small particle size (usually < 3 nm), high catalytic activity, and good biocompatibility. These characteristics make them promising for biomedical applications [5, 6]. Notably, the ultrasmall size of metal clusters enables renal clearance, thus avoiding potential damage to crucial organs like the liver and kidneys. Au clusters are a representative system of metal clusters, whose core-shell structures resemble natural proteins in terms of size and structural hierarchy [7]. Zhang et al. discovered that Au-based clusters exhibited enzyme-mimicking catalytic activities and demonstrated high efficacy in modulating neuroinflammation [8]. Based on these findings, they proposed the concept of clusterzymes [8].

Iron-based and cerium-based nanozymes are two types of classic nanozymes that have been broadly studied. Despite being the earliest discovered and most studied nanozymes for their peroxidase-like activity, iron-based nanozymes are limited by several critical factors, such as insufficient catalytic selectivity, limited enzyme-mimicking activity, ambiguous catalytic mechanisms, and inadequate biocompatibility evidences [9]. Cerium-based nanozymes are widely recognized as the most effective inorganic nanozymes for mimicking antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT. Nevertheless, their practical applications are constrained by fundamental drawbacks, including the necessity of organic solvents in synthesis which leads to aqueous aggregation and in vivo toxicity [10–12]. Owing to ultrasmall size, Au clusterzymes enable efficient renal clearance, thereby mitigating the risks associated with metal accumulation [13]. Furthermore, their surface enables easy multifunctional modification via Au-S bonds, making them highly suitable for biomedical applications. Recently, a series of studies have confirmed that Au clusters have excellent reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging ability and have been successfully applied to the treatment of related diseases, including acute kidney injury [14, 15], acute lung injury [16], ulcerative colitis [17], sepsis [18], osteoarthritis [19], and acute brain injury [14].

Obesity pathogenesis is closely associated with ROS, which act as a potential driver of metabolic dysfunction. ROS production increases in obesity due to fat accumulation [20]. Obesity is characterized by compromised lipid metabolism, leading to excessive lipid accumulation in adipose tissue and ectopic organs such as liver. This dysregulation is governed by complex signaling networks [21]. Among these, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in controlling adipogenesis, lipid synthesis, and insulin sensitivity [22]. The interplay between ROS and these pathways drives obesity pathogenesis. Previous research has established that Au clusterzymes display robust CAT-like and SOD-like activities, effectively scavenging ROS in white adipocytes and white adipose tissue (WAT) [23, 24]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of clusterzymes in anti-obesity; however, modulation of the activity in vivo remains a critical challenge. Recently, several research teams have made significant progress in enhancing the activities of metal clusterzymes using the single-atom substitution [8, 19]. However, this strategy poses several significant challenges, such as the difficulties in precise synthesis and characterization, poor stability, and high costs [25, 26].

The magnetothermal effect of magnetic nanoparticles in alternating magnetic fields (AMF) enables magnetic hyperthermia therapy (MHT), due to the hysteresis loss and relaxation effect [27, 28]. However, magnetothermal effect is not exclusive to magnetic materials. Non-magnetic metal nanoparticles, which are inherently conductive, can generate Joule heat via the eddy current effect in AMF. Wang et al. demonstrated that Au nanoparticles exhibit significant magnetothermal effects in AMF, enabling controllable drug release from hydrogel [29]. This pioneering work demonstrates the feasibility to modulate the activity of Au clusterzymes by AMF. However, several challenges must be addressed before translating this approach into practical applications, particularly concerning in vivo stability and targeting efficiency [29]. In this study, we designed an adipose homing peptide (AHP)-functionalized nanogel, termed AHP-AuCZy@CS, consisting of Au clusterzymes encapsulated within a chitosan (CS) matrix. Notably, the developed AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogel offer distinct advantages: the AHP moiety enables specific targeting to WAT, the AuCZy exhibits SOD-like and CAT-like enzymatic activities that are tunable by external AMF, and the CS shell ensures high biocompatibility. Especially, AMF is applied to modulate the activity of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels via its magnetothermal properties, enabling targeted ROS scavenging in WAT in vivo, thereby combating obesity (Fig. 1). This work aims to study the therapeutic efficacy of AMF-activated AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels against obesity and its complications, and further explore the potential mechanisms, thereby offering novel insights for the treatment of obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Experimental section

Materials

All chemicals are commercially available and used without further purification unless otherwise stated. Gold (III) chloride tetrahydrate and adipose homing peptide (AHP) were purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Reduced glutathione (GSH), xanthine (X), and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Glycol chitosan, dihydroethidium (DHE), and p-phthalicacid (PTA) were purchased from Macklin. Xanthine oxidase (XO), Triton X-100, 30% gelatinizing solution (29:1), SDS-PAGE separating gel buffer (4×) and SDS-PAGE spacer gel buffer (4×) were purchased from Solarbio. Endogenous peroxidase blocking solution (3% H2O2) was purchased from Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Antifade mounting medium with 4’,6 diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) and PMSF (100 mM) were purchased from Beyotime. Skim milk was purchased from BD. Omni-ECL™ femto light chemiluminescence kit were purchased from NCM Biotech.

Synthesis of AuCZy

AuCZy (GSH-protected Au clusters) was synthesized by the previously reported method [30]. In a typical synthesis, 0.50 mL HAuCl4·4H2O (20 mM), 0.15 mL GSH (100 mM), and 4.35 mL ultrapure water were added into a three-necked flask and then mixed by a gentle stirring at 500 rpm. The mixture was then heated to 70 °C and maintained under gentle stirring for 24 h. Afterward, the product AuCZy was collected and purified by ultrafiltration.

Preparation of AuCZy@CS nanogels

2.0 mL AuCZy, 1.0 mL glycol chitosan (5 mg/mL), and 2.0 mL ultrapure water were added into a three-necked flask and subjected to vigorous stirring to ensure homogeneous mixing. The pH of the mixture was subsequently adjusted to 5.0 using NaOH (1 M). After an additional 30 min of gentle stirring at 500 rpm, the product AuCZy@CS nanogels were collected and purified by ultrafiltration.

Fabrication of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels

To prepare the AHP-functionalized nanogels, 10 mg AuCZy@CS nanogels and 0.2 mg AHP (CKGGRAKDC) were added into a three-necked flask. The mixture was subjected to gentle stirring at 25 °C with a stirring speed of 500 rpm for 6 h, allowing effective conjugation of AHP onto the nanogel surface. After the reaction, the resulting AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels were obtained as the final product.

Characterization

HRTEM images were obtained using a Hitachi HT7800 microscope. For sample preparation, diluted aqueous solution of the AuCZy and AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels were drop-cast onto carbon-coated copper grids and allowed to dry at ambient temperature prior to imaging. Hydrodynamic diameter was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS ZSU3100 system. Prior to DLS analysis, samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to eliminate potential aggregates, and subsequently equilibrated at 25 °C for 3 min within the instrument cell. UV-visible absorption spectra were recorded on a Meipuda UV-6100 S spectrophotometer. The sample was dispersed in ultrapure water to form a suspension at a concentration of 1.3 mg/mL. After ultrasonic dispersion for 10 min, the measurement was carried out. Ultrapure water was used as the reference, and the spectral scan was performed in a 1 cm quartz cuvette within the wavelength range of 350–550 nm. Photoluminescence spectra were measured using a Horiba FluoroMax-4 spectrofluorometer. The sample was dispersed in ultrapure water at a concentration of 1.3 mg/mL. Emission spectra were recorded from 450 to 750 nm with an excitation wavelength of 410 nm, and both the excitation and emission slit widths were set to 5 nm.

Au release assay

Au release from AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels was determined using an Agilent inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) 7800 system. Briefly, the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels were incubated in buffer solutions at different pH values (5.0, 6.0, 7.4, and 8.0) at 37 °C for 72 h. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 30 min using a centrifugal ultrafiltration tube (3 kDa), and the filtrate was collected for Au quantification.

Magnetothermal performance study

To evaluate the magnetothermal conversion efficiency, 2 mL AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersion (1.3 mg/mL in 0.9% NaCl solution) were exposed to AMF with different power (1, 2, and 3 kW). In parallel, nanogel dispersions at concentrations of 0.65, 1.3, and 2.6 mg/mL were prepared and exposed to AMF at a fixed power of 2 kW. The magnetothermal stability was tested by subjecting to cyclic AMF exposure. For each cycle, AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersion was subjected to AMF (2 kW) irradiation for 5 min, followed by cooling for 5 min with the AMF switched off. The on/off cycle was repeated for four consecutive iterations. In these experiments, AMF was generated by a high-frequency induction heating device (SPG-10-II, Shenzhen Shuangping Power Supply Technologies Co. Ltd.), and the temperature of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersion was monitored in real-time using an infrared thermal camera (Testo 869).

SOD-like and CAT-like activities and total antioxidant capacity

The SOD-like and CAT-like activities were assessed by the efficiencies in scavenging O2•− and H2O2, respectively, as described previously [31]. The total antioxidant capacity was determined with an assay kit (Beyotime) according to the ABTS method, and the ABTS•+ absorbance was measured using a Tecan Infinite M200 PRO microplate reader.

MTT assay

The cytotoxicity of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels was assessed using the MTT assay. 3T3-L1 preadipocyte cell line (RRID: CVCL_0123) and L929 cell line (RRID: CVCL_0462) were purchased from ZQXZBio and MeilunBio, respectively. 3T3-L1 preadipocytes were cultured in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, while L929 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with identical supplements. They were both maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. 3T3-L1 preadipocytes and L929 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of ~ 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 µL DMEM medium with 10% FBS. The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersion at various concentrations (0, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL), followed by an additional 48-h culture. 10 µL MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h, and then 100 µL DMSO solution was added and incubated with shaking for 20 min. Finally, the cell viability was quantified by the UV-Vis absorbance at 570 nm using a Tecan Infinite M200 PRO microplate reader.

Hemolysis test

Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from fresh mouse blood via centrifugation. The RBCs were washed until the supernatants became colorless, and then diluted to form a 2% suspension in saline solution. This RBCs suspension was subsequently treated with AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersions at various concentrations (100, 200, 500, 1000, and 2000 µg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The subsequent experimental procedures, including the calculation of the hemolysis rate, were performed as described previously [32].

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Zhejiang Laboratory Animal Center. All mice were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water. The obesity mouse model in this study was established by feeding C57BL/6J mice a high-fat diet (HFD, 60% kcal fat) for 12 weeks.

In vivo biodistribution of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels

HFD-induced obese mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 200 µL AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersion (2 mg in 0.9% NaCl solution). At 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection, mice were sacrificed under anesthesia. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and adipose depots (BAT, EpiWAT, IngWAT, and PerWAT) were harvested. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an in vivo imaging system (VILBER Newton 7.0 FT-400) to assess the biodistribution of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels (autofluorescence) in these organs and tissues. Meanwhile, the Au contents in EpiWAT, IngWAT, and PerWAT were determined using ICP-MS (Thermo ICAP RQ).

AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels + AMF treatment

The mice were divided into five groups, i.e., Normal group (mice fed with standard chow), HFD group (HFD-induced obese mice), AHP-AuCZy@CS group (HFD-induced obese mice treated with AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels), AMF group (HFD-induced obese mice treated with AMF), and AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group (HFD-induced obese mice treated with AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels and AMF). The AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 µL AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels dispersion (2 mg in 0.9% NaCl solution), followed 24 h later by exposure to AMF (2 kW) for 30 min. The AMF group received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 µL 0.9% NaCl solution, and was identically exposed to AMF 24 h post-injection. The AHP-AuCZy@CS group received only the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels injection without subsequent AMF treatment. The Normal group and HFD group received only an intraperitoneal injection of 200 µL 0.9% NaCl solution and did not undergo AMF exposure. Treatments were administered once every three days for a total of 15 rounds. Throughout the treatment period, the Normal group were fed standard chow, while HFD group, AHP-AuCZy@CS group, AMF group, and AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group were fed HFD (60% kcal from fat). At the end of the treatment, mice were sacrificed under anesthesia. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and adipose depots (BAT, EpiWAT, IngWAT, and PerWAT) were harvested, and blood was collected.

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT)

For the IPGTT, mice were fasted overnight before the experiment with free access to water. At the beginning of the experiment, blood was collected from the tail vein to measure the baseline blood glucose level (recorded as 0 min). Then, a glucose solution (2 g/kg body weight) was administered via intraperitoneal injection (i.p.), and the blood samples were collected at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-injection for blood glucose determination. Glucose tolerance curves were plotted based on blood glucose concentrations at different time points, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Regarding the IPITT, the mice were fasted for 4 h before the experiment, after which an insulin solution (1 U/kg body weight) was injected intraperitoneally. The rest of the experimental procedures were identical to that in the IPGTT experiment.

Determination of serum biochemical markers and norepinephrine levels

Lipid metabolism indicators (triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C)), liver function markers (alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), albumin (ALB), total protein (TP)), kidney function markers (creatinine (CRE) and uric acid (UA)) in serum were determined using assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Serum norepinephrine levels were determined using a norepinephrine ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute).

H&E staining

IngWAT, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio) overnight. Following fixation, tissues underwent dehydration and paraffin embedding. Sections were cut at 5-µm thickness, followed by histological staining using a H&E Staining Kit (Solarbio). The stained sections were then examined for pathological features using a digital pathology slide scanner (KFBio KF-PRO-005). Additionally, adipocyte sizes in the IngWAT were measured using ImageJ software.

Sirius Red staining

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (5 μm). Sections were deparaffinized and stained with Sirius Red solution (Wuhan Guge Biological Technology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained sections were examined using both digital slide scanner (KF-PRO-005, KFBio) and polarized light microscope (Eclipse LV100N, Nikon).

DHE fluorescence staining

ROS levels in IngWAT were assessed using DHE fluorescence staining. Fresh tissues were embedded in OCT compound (Sakura) and cryosectioned into 10-µm sections with a Leica CM1950 cryostat. The sections were incubated with DHE at 37 °C for 30 min under light-protected conditions, washed with PBS, and mounted with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI for nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a KEYENCE BZ-X800 automated high-resolution microscope.

Measurement of ROS levels using DCFH-DA probe

Snap-frozen tissues were homogenized in 1 mL ice-cold homogenization buffer. The homogenate was then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. The total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (GLPBIO). Subsequently, 200 µL supernatant was transferred to 96-well plate, and fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime) was added to a final concentration of 10 µM. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min, then measured using a microplate reader (TECAN Infinite M200 pro).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated with RNAiso Plus (GLPBIO), quantified spectrophotometrically (Thermo NanoDrop 2000), and reverse-transcribed with HiScript®III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). qPCR was performed with TOROGreen® qPCR Master Mix (Toroivd) on a RT-qPCR detection system (Bio-rad CFX 96 Touch). Relative mRNA expression was quantified by the delta-delta threshold cycle (Ct) method. The primer sequences for qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting analysis

A lysis buffer containing PMSF (1:100 in RIPA) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail was used to lyse IngWAT for protein extraction. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Cytiva). Membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad). The antibodies for Western blotting analysis are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Tissue fixation, dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning (5 μm) were performed as described for H&E staining in this study. The subsequent experimental procedures were performed as described previously [32].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was processed using GraphPad Prism software. The data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The significance between groups was evaluated using one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, presented as * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01), or *** (p < 0.001).

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels

AuCZy (GSH-protected Au clusters) was synthesized via chemical reduction method [30], and CS was employed as a crosslinker to form AuCZy@CS nanogels. The nanogels were further modified with AHP (CKGGRAKDC) [33], resulting in the formation of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels (Fig. 2A). The transmission electron microscope (TEM) image revealed that AuCZy possessed an ultra-small size (< 2 nm) (Supplementary Figure S1), which was consistent with previous study [34]. TEM imaging further revealed that AuCZy was uniformly embedded within the CS matrix, forming well-dispersed nanogels. The resulting AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels exhibited a regular spherical morphology with a particle size of approximately 40 nm (Fig. 2B) and a hydrodynamic diameter of nearly 290 nm (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels displayed distinct fluorescence characteristics, with excitation and emission peaks centered at 410 nm and 610 nm, respectively (Fig. 2D). Correspondingly, the nanogels in water emitted bright orange luminescence under UV irradiation (Fig. 2D). The excellent fluorescence properties of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels should be attributed to the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) effect of Au clusters impregnated in nanogels [34], thereby establishing a robust basis for in vivo tracking applications. To assess the release behavior of Au clusterzymes under physiologically relevant conditions, we quantified Au release from AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels using ICP-MS over 72 h at pH 5.0, 6.0, 7.4, and 8.0 (Figure S2). Au release exhibited a marked pH-dependent profile, with the highest release observed at pH 7.4 (physiological pH).

Magnetothermal and enzyme-mimicking properties of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels

Benefiting from the eddy current effect of AuCZy (inherently metallic in nature), AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels will be heated when exposed to external AMF. It was found that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels in cell culture dish exhibited sustained temperature increases in a power-dependent manner under AMF exposure (Fig. 2E). For instance, when subjected to 2 kW AMF for 30 min, the temperature of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels increased by nearly 8 °C, which was directly evidenced by infrared thermal imaging (Fig. 2F). In addition, the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels exhibited a concentration-dependent heating profile under AMF exposure at a fixed power of 2 kW (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrate that both nanogel concentration and applied AMF power are critical parameters determining the magnetothermal effect. As non-magnetic conductive nanoparticles, Au clusters generate heat via eddy current-induced Joule heating under AMF. This controlled heating property is essential for modulating the catalytic activities of the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels. Furthermore, no significant decay of temperature increase was observed after four AMF on/off cycles (Fig. 2H), revealing the high magnetothermal stability of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels.

Previous studies have demonstrated that Au clusters show the enzyme- mimicking characteristics, which endowed them with excellent ROS scavenging and antioxidant properties [8, 14]. To further investigate the enzyme-mimicking properties of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels, their SOD-like and CAT-like activities at different concentrations were evaluated both in the absence and presence of AMF exposure (Fig. 2I, J). The SOD-like and CAT-like activities of the nanogels exhibited a clear concentration-dependent increase under both conditions. Notably, AMF exposure significantly enhanced these activities at all tested concentrations. For instance, at a concentration of 20 µg/mL, AMF exposure enhanced SOD-like activity from 23% to 80% and CAT-like activity from 20% to 82%. This result can also be explained by the mechanism that AMF-induced local heating via the eddy current effect accelerates the catalytic kinetics of AuCZy. Furthermore, the total antioxidant capacity of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels was 15 times that of the natural antioxidant trolox (Fig. 2K). When exposed to AMF, its total antioxidant capacity of increased by 54%, reaching 23 times that of trolox. The SOD-like and CAT-like activities of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels were found to be lower than those of previously reported Au clusterzymes [35, 36] possibly because the CS gelation and AHP modification blocked partial catalytic sites. Fortunately, the localized heat generated under AMF exposure markedly improved its catalytic performance, achieving superior ROS scavenging ability and antioxidant capacity.

Targeted performance of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels

To achieve adipose tissue-specific targeting, AuCZy@CS was further functionalized with AHP in this study. Owing to the intrinsic autofluorescence property, the biodistribution of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels in mice can be conveniently tracked. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging showed the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels efficiently accumulated in various WAT depots, including epididymal WAT (EpiWAT), inguinal WAT (IngWAT), and perirenal WAT (PerWAT) (Fig. 3A). The fluorescence images showed that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels displayed a characteristic biphasic accumulation behavior in WAT during the observation period (12–72 h post-injection), with a peak occurring at 48 h followed by gradual clearance. Meanwhile, the liver, lungs, and kidneys also exhibited varying degrees of fluorescence intensity. Previous studies have shown that those organs are the main target organs for Au clusters [14, 24].

Biodistribution of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels in obese mice. (A) Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs and adipose tissues (n = 3). (B) ICP-MS quantification of Au accumulation in EpiWAT, IngWAT, and PerWAT (n = 3)

The biodistribution and clearance kinetics of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels in WAT were also evaluated by Au analysis using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). It was observed that the Au content showed a gradual decline across various WAT within 12–72 h post-injection (Fig. 3B). It was hypothesized that after AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels targeted and accumulated in WAT, decomposition and metabolism occurred, leading to the asynchronous changes observed in fluorescence intensity and Au content. These findings demonstrated that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels can effectively target WAT in vivo, highlighting their potential for scavenging ROS within the WAT microenvironment and thereby treating obesity.

AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF combats obesity and associated complications

The enzyme-mimicking activities of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels have been demonstrated to be significantly enhanced by AMF stimulation. Therefore, AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF were employed to treat high-fat-diet (HFD)-induced obesity in mice (Fig. 4A). After treatment for 45 days (once every three days), mice in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group exhibited a slimmer physique than those in other groups (Fig. 4B), as well as 18% reduction in body weight compared to the HFD group (Fig. 4C). In addition, Lee’s index and organ coefficients also suggested a low degree of obesity in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group (Supplementary Figure S3 and S4). Meanwhile, food intake was monitored throughout the treatment period, and no significant differences were observed (Fig. 4D). This showed that the evident weight loss in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group was not attributable to differences in dietary intake, but rather to other essential mechanisms.

It has been previously established that obesity is closely associated with impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance. As expected, HFD-induced obese mice exhibited significantly higher fasting blood glucose levels compared to the standard chow-feed mice (Normal group) (Fig. 4E). After treatment, the fasting blood glucose levels in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group decreased by 22%, indicating that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF effectively reduced hyperglycemia in obese mice. Furthermore, blood glucose levels in mice were monitored after glucose and insulin injections, respectively. Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) results showed that in the HFD group, blood glucose level rapidly increased after glucose injection, much higher than that in the Normal group (Fig. 4F). It was noted that blood glucose level in the HFD group decreased slowly and failed to return to normal levels 2 h after injection, indicating impaired glucose tolerance in HFD-induced obese mice. However, the IPGTT results for the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group showed a marked difference from the HFD group, yet were most similar to the Normal group. Meanwhile, the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group displayed a 34% lower AUC of IPGTT compared to the HFD group, indicating significantly improved glucose tolerance. Similarly, intraperitoneal insulin tolerance test (IPITT) results showed the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group exhibited improved insulin sensitivity, with a 27% reduction in AUC of IPITT compared to the HFD group (Fig. 4G), indicating partial reversal of insulin resistance. As is well known, obesity triggers insulin resistance, which in turn leads to hyperglycemia; this elevated blood glucose further exacerbates obesity and metabolic dysfunction. This vicious cycle is a core mechanism driving the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Our findings demonstrated that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF can significantly restore impaired glucose tolerance and insulin resistance accompanied by HFD-induced obesity.

Obesity is considered a pathological condition characterized by excessive accumulation or abnormal distribution of fat. It was found that the sizes of EpiWAT, IngWAT, and PerWAT in the HFD group were significantly higher than those in the Normal group (Fig. 5A). AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF effectively inhibited the expansion of WAT, resulting in reduced fat weight in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group (Fig. 5B). Similarly, H&E staining images showed that the size of adipocytes in IngWAT for the HFD group was larger than that for the Normal group (Fig. 5C). However, in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group, the adipocytes in IngWAT were significantly reduced in size by 55% compared to the HFD group (Fig. 5C). The expansion of WAT, through hyperplasia and/or hypertrophy, is a central characteristic of obesity. These findings show that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF can effectively suppress the expansion of WAT, thus combating HFD-induced obesity. Furthermore, the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group exhibited markedly decreased serum levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), along with a significant increase in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), compared to the HFD group (Fig. 5D-G). This revealed that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF effectively ameliorates lipid metabolic disorders, which are closely linked to the development of obesity. To further study the therapeutic effect on obesity-related liver pathology, we performed Sirius Red staining to assess collagen deposition, a key indicator of liver fibrosis. Bright-field images of Sirius Red-stained sections revealed that HFD induced a marked increase in collagen-positive areas in the liver, indicating excessive extracellular matrix deposition and established fibrosis (Fig. 5H). Notably, AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF significantly reduced Sirius Red-positive areas, demonstrating potent anti-fibrotic efficacy. Sirius Red-stained sections under polarized light microscopy further showed that the HFD group exhibited a marked increase in both collagen I (thick and highly birefringent) and collagen III (thin and greenish) fibers (Fig. 5I). Notably, AHP-AuCZy@CS combined with AMF treatment significantly reversed the abnormal accumulation of both collagen subtypes, providing strong evidence for its therapeutic effect on liver fibrosis. These findings demonstrate that AHP-AuCZy@CS combined with AMF effectively alleviates hepatic fibrosis in HFD-induced obesity.

AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF promotes WAT browning and thermogenesis

WAT browning refers to the process in which white adipocytes are transformed into beige adipocytes under specific stimuli. Beige adipocytes, also known as inducible brown adipocytes, share morphological and functional similarities with classical brown adipocytes, such as multilocular lipid droplets, abundant mitochondria, and high UCP1 expression. Conventional obesity interventions primarily target the regulation of energy intake and expenditure balance [37]. In contrast, white adipose tissue (WAT) browning represents a fundamentally distinct strategy that enhances mitochondrial activity and increases energy expenditure by converting energy-storing white adipocytes into thermogenic beige adipocytes, thereby combating obesity and related metabolic disorders [38]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that inducing WAT browning effectively counteracts obesity and associated metabolic dysfunction in HFD-induced obese mice [24, 31, 32]. It was found that the mRNA expression of Ucp1 and Pgc1a in the IngWAT of the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group was significantly upregulated, compared to the HFD group (Fig. 6A). Meanwhile, the levels of UCP1 and PGC-1α proteins in the IngWAT of the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group were significantly higher than those in the HFD group (Fig. 6B), which was further supported by the experimental results of immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Figure S5). It is well known that UCP1 expression is a core indicator of WAT browning. PGC-1α acts as a central co-activator in regulating WAT browning, coordinating mitochondrial biogenesis and the expression of thermogenic genes (such as UCP1) to enhance energy expenditure. Furthermore, immunohistochemical images showed that adipocytes in the IngWAT of the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group exhibited significantly smaller sizes compared to those in the HFD group. This was likely attributable to the formation of beige adipocytes (i.e., WAT browning) with multilocular lipid droplets, rather than large lipid droplets in white adipocytes. These findings showed that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF promoted WAT browning and thermogenesis, thus achieving excellent therapeutic effects on HFD-induce obese mice.

Previous studies have demonstrated that various treatments promote WAT browning and thermogenesis via β-adrenergic receptor-dependent mechanisms. However, in this study, serum norepinephrine levels showed no significant differences among the groups (Supplementary Figure S6). This result suggested that the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF enhances WAT browning and thermogenesis through alternative pathways, independent of β-adrenergic receptors. AMPK functions as a central regulator of cellular energy balance, sensing the cellular energy status through AMP/ATP ratio and maintaining energy balance by modulating cellular metabolism [39, 40]. Previous studies have demonstrated that promoting AMPK and PGC-1α via a UCP1-dependent pathway can enhance fat thermogenesis and combat obesity [41]. It was found that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of Ampk in the IngWAT of obese mice in this study (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, this combined treatment markedly increased p-AMPK protein level but had no significant effect on total AMPK level (Fig. 6E). The ratio of p-AMPK to AMPK in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group was about four times higher than that in the HFD group, indicating a substantial activation of AMPK (Supplementary Figure S7). DHE staining images revealed that the ROS level in IngWAT of the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group was significantly lower than that in the HFD group, AHP-AuCZy@CS group, and AMF group (Fig. 6F). Consistently, ROS levels measured in IngWAT homogenates using a DCFH-DA probe showed that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF reduced ROS levels by approximately 73% compared to the HFD group (Fig. 6G). These results demonstrated that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF can effectively reduce ROS levels in WAT in vivo. This was attributed to the superior ROS scavenging ability of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels when activated by AMF, as described above. It is well known that excessive ROS can damage biological macromolecules and trigger oxidative stress, resulting in cellular impairment and dysfunction. It was reported that high glucose stimulation promotes ROS production in cells, which subsequently leads to AMPK suppression through both its degradation and deactivation [42]. Based on these findings, we propose that the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF scavenge excess ROS in WAT and subsequently activates the AMPK signaling pathway, thereby promoting WAT browning and thermogenesis (Fig. 6H).

The beneficial effects of AMPK activation in obesity are multifaceted. In this study, we provide evidence that AMPK activation by AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF promotes WAT browning and thermogenesis, a key mechanism contributing to reduced fat mass. Interestingly, AMPK is also well-documented as a gatekeeper of adipogenesis. It has been reported that AMPK activation directly inhibits adipocyte differentiation by downregulating key adipogenic transcription factors such as PPARγ and C/EBPα, thereby limiting hyperplasia and excessive fat accumulation [43]. Given that the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF activate AMPK in WAT, it is reasonable that they may similarly suppress adipogenesis. This would contribute to anti-obesity effects through a dual mechanism, promoting energy expenditure and suppressing new fat cell formation.

A recent groundbreaking study has revealed an additional layer of complexity in the regulation of adipocyte thermogenesis [44]. This work identified formyl peptide receptor 2 (FPR2) as a specific receptor for long-chain ceramides and demonstrated that binding of C16:0 ceramide to FPR2 inhibits thermogenesis in brown and beige adipocytes via Gi-cyclic AMP signaling [44]. As ceramide levels are usually elevated in obesity and are linked to oxidative stress [45], it is reasonable that the therapeutic effects of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogel combined with AMF extend beyond direct AMPK activation. By ameliorating oxidative stress and improving the overall metabolic milieu in WAT, AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogel combined with AMF may reduce ceramide accumulation, thereby relieving the FPR2-mediated brake on thermogenesis. This would synergize with AMPK-driven browning to maximize energy expenditure.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a central hub for insulin signal transduction and lipid synthesis, and its dysregulation is closely linked to obesity-related insulin resistance and fatty liver disease [22]. Since oxidative stress can modulate the activity of this pathway [46], the ROS-scavenging effect mediated by the AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogel combined with AMF may influence PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling by improving the cellular redox state, thereby synergizing with the AMPK pathway to restore lipid homeostasis. Future studies need to directly examine the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR in WAT and liver to validate this potential mechanism.

Biosafety evaluation

The biocompatibility of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels was firstly evaluated using the MTT assay. Within the concentration range of 10–200 µg/mL, the nanogels had no significant impact on the viability of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes and L929 cells (Supplementary Figure S8). Additionally, the in vitro hemolysis test showed that the hemolysis rate remained below 2% at concentrations ranging from 100 to 2000 µg/mL (Supplementary Figure S9), showing excellent hemocompatibility.

H&E staining results indicated that after treatment with AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF, no significant pathological changes were observed in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) in mice, including the tissue structure, interstitial spacing, and nuclear morphology (Supplementary Figure S10). A series of serum biochemical markers, including ALT, AST, ALB, TP, CRE and UA, were also detected. No significant differences in these important markers were found among the HFD group, AHP-AuCZy@CS group, AMF group, and AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group (Supplementary Figure S11). Notably, compared to the Normal group, elevated levels of most serum biochemical markers were observed in the HFD group. Fortunately, the upward trend was effectively suppressed in the AHP-AuCZy@CS + AMF group. These findings demonstrated that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF, exhibited excellent biosafety and no adverse effects in mice.

Limitations of the study

First, the study was limited to a short-term safety assessment over 45 days, with no long-term evaluation after treatment cessation. Despite the favorable biocompatibility of clusterzymes in vivo [8], it remains essential to investigate their potential for long-term accumulation and delayed toxicity, particularly in the context of obesity.

Second, functional assessments validating WAT browning and thermogenesis, such as cold tolerance, energy expenditure, oxygen consumption (VO2), and carbon dioxide production (VCO2), were not conducted. These assays are critical for correlating molecular markers of browning with actual physiological outcomes, thereby strengthening the evidence that AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels combined with AMF promotes WAT browning. Moreover, such functional validations are crucial for evaluating the therapeutic potential of this strategy against obesity.

Finally, additional evidence is required to substantiate the proposed mechanism whereby the combination of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels and AMF exposure promotes WAT browning and alleviates obesity via AMPK activation. Specifically, loss-of-function experiments, such as pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockdown, are necessary to functionally establish the involvement of the AMPK/PGC-1α/UCP1 signaling axis.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully fabricated AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels, which demonstrates excellent WAT targeting capability, intrinsic fluorescence properties, and good biocompatibility. We found that external AMF stimulation significantly enhances the SOD-like and CAT-like activities of the nanogels, resulting in superior ROS scavenging capacity. The combination of AHP-AuCZy@CS nanogels and AMF exposure effectively counteracted obesity and its associated complications, including reduced body weight, suppression of WAT expansion, improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, and amelioration of lipid metabolic disorders. Further study revealed that the anti-obesity effect is mediated through scavenging of excess ROS in WAT followed by activation of the AMPK/PGC-1α/UCP1 signaling axis, thereby promoting white fat browning and thermogenesis. This work presents a clusterzyme-based magnetothermal strategy and offers a novel therapeutic approach for treating obesity and associated metabolic diseases.
