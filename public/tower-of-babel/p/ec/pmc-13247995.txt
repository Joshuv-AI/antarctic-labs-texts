Using biofilm-targeting local antibiotics to treat recalcitrant medical device–associated infections: driveline infection as a model
Authors: Yue Qu, David M Kaye, David McGiffin, Anton Y Peleg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDriveline infection, exemplifying medical device–associated infection, is a difficult-to-treat complication of ventricular assist device implantation in which biofilm formation underlies both infection onset and recurrence. Although clinical experience remains limited, treatment success has been reported in cases where local antibiotics at high concentrations were used in combination with surgical debridement and/or device reimplantation. We recently identified the low metabolic state of biofilm cells as the central mechanism underlying the high antimicrobial resistance of staphylococcal biofilms and as a potential therapeutic target for driveline infections. We hypothesize that rationally combining weakly and strongly metabolism-dependent antibiotics at high concentrations for an extended period may eradicate staphylococcal biofilms, including the embedded tolerant and persister cells. If clinically validated, this strategy may hold the key to achieving long-term treatment success in many medical device–associated infections, including the driveline infection.

Introduction

Driveline infection is a frequent complication of ventricular assist device (VAD) implantation, associated with considerable morbidity, mortality and reduced quality of life.1 Systemic antibiotics recommended by the International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT)2 may create an extended asymptomatic period for bridge-to-transplant patients awaiting a donor heart; however, this approach remains suboptimal for patients experiencing a prolonged wait for a donor heart or receiving VADs for destination therapy, as it is unable to completely eradicate the infection and its associated biofilm.3,4 Relapses of driveline infections often occur after cessation of systemic antibiotics.5 Local antibiotics have been used in combination with surgical debridement to manage recalcitrant driveline infections, with successful outcomes reported in several case studies, although the efficacy is likely confounded by publication bias6–11 (see Table 1). The selection of local antibiotic regimens for driveline infections has been based principally on the antimicrobial susceptibilities of the causative pathogen(s), as well as physician experience and preference, and requires further experimental validation. Here, we outline the rationale for using local antibiotics as a therapeutic strategy for medical device–associated infections, with driveline infection serving as a representative model.

Microbial biofilms and tolerant and persister cells in driveline infections

Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci account for at least 50% of all driveline infections.1,12 These species are typical biofilm-producers and can adhere to the surface of drivelines and form biofilms.13 The formation of biofilms is the unifying and defining characteristic of virtually all device-associated infections, including driveline infections, orthopaedic implant infections and pacemaker pocket infections. We examined infected drivelines explanted from VAD patients and found antibiotic-resistant microcolony biofilms in the driveline tissue tunnel.3 Using a tunnel-based biofilm model to mimic the driveline infection microenvironment, we identified the low metabolic states of biofilm cells as the central mechanism underpinning their antimicrobial resistance.14 Tolerant and persister cells represent metabolically quiescent subpopulations within biofilms and are widely regarded as key drivers of long-term therapeutic failure in driveline infections.14,15

Treatment targets: planktonic bacteria, microbial biofilms or tolerant/persister cells?

Although orally or intravenously administered antibiotics are effective in suppressing acute symptoms caused by planktonic cells, systemic antibiotics alone often fail to eradicate biofilms formed on device surfaces or cure device–associated infections.3,12 Indeed, our very recent work demonstrated that most conventional antibiotics achieve 3-log reduction of staphylococcal biofilms only at concentrations attainable through topical administration.16 Inspired by the success in treating prosthetic joint infections and vascular graft infections,17,18 antibiotic beads impregnated with vancomycin, gentamicin or tobramycin have been used to treat recurrent driveline and/or pocket infections6–11 (see Table 1). These beads were designed to directly deliver high concentrations of antibiotics to the infection site and kill biofilms. The key question is what treatment end point should be achieved? It is reasonable to target most metabolically active planktonic and biofilm cells if temporary symptomatic relief is the goal (a short-term success; see Figure 1a). The ultimate goal, however, is to develop effective strategies to minimize the recurrence of device-associated infections. To this end, infection resilience (a mid-term success; see Figure 1b) and complete cure of infection (a long-term success; see Figure 1c) may need to target metabolically less active cells residing in biofilms.

The short-term success focuses on addressing the acute symptoms that patients are experiencing (defined as rapid symptom relief with the patient remaining asymptomatic for 0–3 months). For patients who are eligible for a heart transplant, we aimed at a medium-term success (defined as freedom from symptomatic recurrent infection for 3–12 months) that allows the patient to be bridged to transplantation. For destination therapy patients who are ineligible for transplantation, we target a complete eradication of biofilm cells and a long-term success (free of symptomatic infections for more than 12 months).19

Using population analysis, we identified three subpopulations in staphylococcal biofilms grown on driveline surfaces based on their metabolic status—regular, tolerant and persister cells.14 We also found that vancomycin at high concentrations only killed planktonic cells dispersed from staphylococcal biofilms or cells residing in the periphery of biofilms,20,21 implying suboptimal efficacy of vancomycin alone in treating staphylococcal device–associated infections. An effective antimicrobial regimen for device-associated infections should target metabolically less active biofilm-embedded cells, including tolerant and persister cells.

Selecting biofilm-active combination antibiotics to treat device-associated infections

Rational selection of antibiotics appears to be fundamental for the successful treatment of device-associated infections. Zheng et al. reported that combining weakly and strongly metabolism-dependent antibiotics could more effectively eradicate persister cells.22,23 Gentamicin and tobramycin are weakly metabolism-dependent antibiotics that have concentration-dependent lethality against resource-depleted staphylococcal cells.16,22,23 They also demonstrate adequate penetration through the staphylococcal biofilm matrix and therefore remain viable options for targeting tolerant and persister subpopulations of staphylococcal biofilms.23,24 Vancomycin is a strongly metabolism-dependent antibiotic for Staphylococcus spp.14 Impregnating an aminoglycoside and vancomycin into antibiotic vehicles, or using these antibiotics at high concentrations for irrigation therapy, has been reported to successfully prevent the recurrence of prosthetic joint infections,25 vascular graft infections,17,26 musculoskeletal infections27 and non-S. aureus pacemaker pocket infections.28 It remains to be clarified how vancomycin and aminoglycoside combinations work against biofilm-associated infections. We speculate that including strongly metabolism-dependent antibiotics such as vancomycin in the regimen may allow prompt elimination of planktonic cells and metabolically active cells residing in the periphery of biofilms, thereby reserving aminoglycosides for biofilm-embedded cells (Figure 1). Incorporating vancomycin may reduce the aminoglycoside dose required for biofilm eradication and thereby potentially lower the risk of associated nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity.29,30 It is reasonable to infer bactericidal antibiotics, rather than bacteriostatic agents, should be selected as both the strongly and weakly metabolism-dependent components of the regimen.31 Bactericidal antibiotics often accelerate bacterial cell respiration and bacteriostatic agents decelerate cellular respiration; combining two opposite classes may induce a metabolic trade-off in bacteria and consequently attenuate bacterial lethality of antibiotics.31 Rational selection of local antibiotics for device-associated infections should incorporate pathogen-specific susceptibility profiles, as infection recurrence has been linked to intrinsic resistance of causative pathogens.27 Other key factors that may contribute to the successful eradication of biofilm cells in driveline infections include the concentration of individual antibiotics that is sustained in the tissue tunnel and the treatment duration of such a concentration.20 Vehicles currently used for local antibiotic delivery, such as bone cement, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) and STIMULAN beads, still suffer from burst antibiotic release.32 A biocompatible delivery vehicle with controllable antibiotic release kinetics is therefore needed to facilitate the clinical translation of local combination antibiotic strategies. It has also been reported that ESKAPE pathogens, including S. aureus, rapidly evolve towards high-persister phenotypes during intermittent aminoglycoside therapy in vitro.33 Rational selection of antibiotics for the regimen and sustained maintenance of high local concentrations over prolonged periods are therefore essential. Although challenging, this may be achievable through the use of antibiotic delivery platforms.30

Combining local antibiotics with surgical interventions: other important considerations

Surgical interventions such as radical debridement of infected tissues in the driveline tunnel are crucial for reducing the initial bacterial burden and disrupting biofilms, while rationally selected local antibiotics may further enhance eradication of residual biofilm cells.32 The use of absorbable calcium sulphate antibiotic beads for device-associated infections would be preferable to PMMA beads, which would need to be subsequently removed. Local application of antibiotics at very high concentrations did not appear to induce clinically significant antimicrobial resistance, at least based on the orthopaedic surgical literature,34,35,36 nor was there evidence of systemic absorption or associated toxicity.7,19,30

The future

Although this viewpoint article focuses on driveline infections, high concentrations of local antibiotic therapy may represent a promising strategy for managing a broader range of device-associated infections, including orthopaedic implant infections and pacemaker pocket infections, by more effectively targeting biofilm cells. Experimental and clinical research is needed to further rationalize, optimize and guide the use of this therapeutic strategy for recalcitrant medical device–associated infections.
