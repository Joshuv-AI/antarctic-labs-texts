The Use of Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring in Pediatric Heart Transplant Recipients
Authors: Bushra Anjum, Shannon Oliver, Amira Balbaa, Rae Foshaug, Jennifer Conway, Simon Urschel, Lori J West, Michael Khoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAmbulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) is the gold‐standard non‐invasive test for diagnosing arterial hypertension; however, it remains understudied in pediatric heart transplant recipients (HTRs). We evaluated the role of ABPM in diagnosing and managing hypertension.MethodsWe conducted a single‐center retrospective evaluation of pediatric HTRs transplanted between 2010 and 2023 (inclusive) with ≥ 1 post‐transplant ABPM. Clinic blood pressures (CBPs) temporally linked to each ABPM were used to categorize patients into blood pressure phenotypes: normotensive (including well‐controlled hypertension, if on anti‐hypertensive therapy), sustained, white coat (WCH), and masked hypertension (MH). Demographic, clinical, and echocardiographic characteristics were compared across hypertensive phenotypes, with longitudinal changes evaluated using serial ABPMs.ResultsData were available for 33 pediatric HTRs (61% female [20/33], median age at first ABPM 7.3 [5.3–10.4] years, 0.7 [0.2–2.6] years post‐transplant). At the initial ABPM, 82% (27/33) were normotensive and 18% (6/33) were hypertensive, with 79% (26/33) on ≥ 1 antihypertensive medication. Of those with hypertensive CBPs, 60% (6/10) had a normal ABPM indicating WCH. At subsequent ABPM, 48% (10/21) normotensive patients remained normotensive, while 43% (9/21) developed hypertension. Among the 6 patients with WCH, 5 (83%) developed subsequent hypertension by ABPM. Median left ventricular mass was higher in hypertensive patients (160.9 g, 64.3–172.3 g) compared with WCH (54.4 g, 36.9–100.7 g) and normotensive patients (66.9 g, 45.4–94.3 g) (p = 0.032).ConclusionsABPM provides important hypertension insights for pediatric HTRs, especially with abnormal CBPs. Progression from WCH to manifest hypertension was common. ABPM should therefore be utilized prior to implementing or modifying anti‐hypertensive therapy.Keywords: ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, blood pressure monitoring, cardiovascular risk, hypertension, pediatric, pediatric heart transplant, white coat hypertension

Introduction

Arterial hypertension remains a leading modifiable risk factor in the development of cardiovascular disease [1] and results in cardiac and vascular target organ damage that in turn is associated with increased cardiovascular mortality and poor long‐term outcomes in adulthood [2]. Hypertension occurs commonly in pediatric solid organ transplant recipients, including heart transplant recipients (HTRs) due to pre‐ and post‐transplant renal injury, adverse effects of medications and other factors [3, 4, 5]. Given the significant risk for hypertension development post‐transplant, and the known associations between hypertension and cardiovascular disease development, as well as cardiac and renal target organ damage, make the early identification and treatment of hypertension in pediatric HTRs essential. In routine clinical practice, pediatric blood pressures (BPs) are most commonly assessed during clinic visits (CBP). Relying solely on CBP measurements fails to detect masked hypertension (MH), defined by normal BP in clinical settings but elevated BPs in daily life, which has been found to affect up to 60% of pediatric HTRs [5]. CBP measurements alone also can falsely indicate hypertension when it is not present, termed white coat hypertension (WCH), where there is elevated BP in the clinical settings but normal readings in daily life. CBP are often highly variable, are not representative of everyday variations in BP, and provide no indication of nocturnal BPs [6]. This underscores the importance of ambulatory BP assessments, of which, 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) is typically regarded as the gold‐standard non‐invasive assessment for pediatric hypertension diagnosis [6, 7]. In addition to confirming a hypertension diagnosis, ABPM is also useful in evaluating responses to anti‐hypertensive therapy. However, there is a lack of published evaluation of ABPM in pediatric HTRs. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the incidence of hypertension in pediatric HTRs using ABPM. We hypothesized that hypertension is highly prevalent in this population post‐transplant and that ABPM is effective in detecting otherwise unrecognized hypertension phenotypes, including masked and nocturnal hypertension. A secondary objective was to examine the associations between hypertension phenotypes defined by ABPM and relevant clinical and demographic characteristics, as well as longitudinal changes in hypertension status.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

A retrospective cohort study was conducted involving pediatric HTRs who were transplanted and continuously followed at the Stollery Children's Hospital (Edmonton, Alberta, Canada) between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2023, inclusive. Eligible participants included those who completed at least one ABPM following transplantation at the Stollery Children's Hospital. Per our institutional protocols, ABPMs are typically included annually in post‐transport surveillance starting at 5 years of age. If successive annual ABPM assessments are normal, routine surveillance is often extended to every 2 years. ABPMs at earlier time points (earlier than 1 year post‐transplant and/or before 5 years of age) can be utilized for appropriately sized patients anticipated to tolerate ABPM if there is suspected hypertension based on CBPs. If antihypertensive therapies are initiated or modified, repeat ABPM is typically performed 6–12 weeks following changes to evaluate response to therapy. Exclusion criteria included patients who received combined heart and lung transplantation (although none were performed at our institution during the study period), ABPMs performed after re‐transplantation and patients who received long‐term care or completed ABPMs outside of the Stollery Children's Hospital.

Pertinent clinical and demographic patient information was obtained from electronic patient charts, including pre‐transplant diagnosis, age at transplant, immunologic cross‐match results, induction therapy administered (basiliximab, anti‐thymocyte globulin/thymoglobulin, or none), as well as ABO blood group compatibility. The use of steroid therapy was also recorded and defined as receiving prednisone as part of the standard post‐transplant immunosuppression protocol (typically utilized for the first 4 months post‐transplant for infant transplantations and 6 months for non‐infant transplantations). Rejection history was documented, specifying the number and type of rejections (antibody‐mediated or acute cellular rejection, deemed clinically relevant when resulting in augmentation of immune suppression). Additionally, any history of post‐transplant cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) or post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) was recorded at the time of the first ABPM. We also identified any history of an acute kidney injury (AKI) (pre‐ or post‐transplant) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) as documented in the patient electronic records at the time of the first ABPM.

At the time of each ABPM, the age, weight, height, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) were obtained. BMI, height, and weight percentiles were calculated using World Health Organization normative values [8, 9]. Creatinine and cystatin C values were collected from participants within 3 months of the ABPM assessment, and the CKiD (Chronic Kidney Disease in Children) multivariable Schwartz equation was used to estimate the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [10]. Medications taken at the time of each ABPM measurement were recorded, including anti‐hypertensive agents (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, aldosterone receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, beta‐blockers, and diuretics), immunosuppressive medications (tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, or any other), and steroid therapies.

Echocardiographic data were obtained from the clinical report of the echocardiogram performed on the date closest to that of the ABPM. Left ventricular mass index (LVMi) was measured via M‐mode echocardiogram and indexed to g/m2.7 [11]. The Devereux formula was applied to estimate LVM using M‐Mode measurements preferentially and 2D measurements if M‐Mode was insufficient for reliable chamber size and wall thickness determinations (https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.55.4.613). Systolic function was quantified via the reported ejection fraction, measured from biplane Simpson's technique or alternative techniques as indicated. Diastolic function was evaluated using the transmitral flow velocities to measure early LV filling (E‐wave) and late LV filling (A‐wave). Tissue Doppler analysis was used to evaluate peak early myocardial velocity (e') and late myocardial velocity (a') at both septal and lateral annuli of the mitral valve.

Clinic and Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring

ABPM and CBP measurements post‐transplant were collected. At our center, routine ABPM monitoring typically starts at the age of 5 years and within the first year of transplantation using the Spacelabs healthcare ambulatory monitor (OneTrak model 90227, SpaceLabs Healthcare, Snoqualmie, WA, USA). ABPM parameters collected included the mean systolic and diastolic BP during 24‐h, awake and sleep periods. Clinic systolic and diastolic BP measurements with the closest temporal link to the corresponding ABPM (typically performed during the corresponding clinic visit) were recorded from the electronic medical record. CBP was recorded following standardized conditions using an oscillometer device on the right arm (with left arm or leg BPs utilized if clinically indicated for anatomic reasons) after a dedicated period of rest in the seated position [12]. In circumstances where oscillometric BP's were difficult to obtain or required confirmation, auscultatory measurements were performed.

Blood Pressure Classification

ABPM data was collected and analyzed to assess hypertension status in pediatric HTRs.

For patients whose height was below the minimum value defined in normative value references (120 cm), a height of 120 cm was used to determine hypertensive cut‐points. Hypertension categorization was determined using established definitions from the 2022 American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Statement (Table 1) [6]. CBP measurements were analyzed according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines [12], which define hypertension as systolic and/or diastolic BP at or above the 95th percentile for children < 13 years of age and as ≥ 130/80 for those children ≥ 13 years of age (Table 1).

Each patient was categorized into four BP phenotypes by comparing their initial ABPM readings with the closest temporally linked CBP measurement: sustained normotension (normal ABPM and CBP, including well‐controlled hypertension, if on anti‐hypertensive therapy), sustained hypertension (hypertensive ABPM and CBP), MH (normal CBP but hypertensive ABPM), or WCH (elevated CBP but normal ABPM) (Table 1).

For patients with more than one ABPM during the follow‐up period, serial measurements were evaluated to assess changes in hypertension categorization over time. For this portion of the analysis, given changes in hypertension definitions across sequential guidelines, ABPM measurements performed prior to May 2022 were interpreted using previous AHA ABPM definitions [13], while those from May 2022 or later were assessed according to the updated 2022 AHA Scientific Statement on ABPM in Children and Adolescents (Table 1) [6]. Similarly, CBP readings obtained before October 2017 were evaluated using the 2004 Fourth Report on the Diagnosis, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure in Children and Adolescents [14], whereas CBP measurements from October 2017 and onwards were assessed using the updated AAP Clinical Practice Guidelines [12]. This approach of using the appropriate guidelines at the given time for patients with serial ABPM measurements permitted an assessment of clinician practices in response to ABPM assessments.

Analysis

Categorical data was presented as frequency (percentage) while continuous data was presented as median (25th–75th percentiles). Demographic, clinical, and echocardiographic measures were compared between patients with normotensive and hypertensive ABPM assessments using Fisher exact test (2‐sided) for categorical variables with two variables or Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test for categorical variables with > 2 options and Kruskal–Wallis non‐parametric test for continuous variables. When factoring in the closest temporal CBP, demographic, clinical, and echocardiographic measures were compared across four cohorts: normotensive, WCH, MH and sustained hypertension using chi‐squared test for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis non‐parametric test for continuous variables. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Among 114 pediatric HTRs identified, 6 died prior to undergoing an ABPM, 13 were below the minimum age criterion, 57 were not in active follow‐up at our center (i.e., out‐of‐province patients with outpatient follow‐up at local institutions), 3 did not complete a first ABPM within the inclusion window and 2 had undergone a second transplant. Thus, we identified 33 pediatric HTRs with at least one post‐transplant ABPM (median age at transplant 6.1 (2.4–10.1) years, 61% female, 186 ABPMs performed in total) (Table 2). Dilated cardiomyopathy was the most common pre‐transplant diagnosis (n = 12, 36%), followed by single ventricle congenital heart disease (n = 9, 27%). A history of AKI at the time of transplant was present in 70% of patients, with a diagnosis of CKD found in 55% of patients.

The first post‐transplant ABPM was collected at a median age of 7.3 (5.3–10.4) years old, and a median of 0.7 (0.2–2.6) years post‐heart transplant (Table 2). The CBP was obtained within a median of 0 (0–0.25) days of the ABPM date. At the first ABPM, 79% of patients were on anti‐hypertensive therapy, with 52% on one medication and 27% on two or more anti‐hypertensive therapies. The median BMI was at the 54th (15.9–88.1) percentile. Overweight (BMI 85th to < 95th percentile) was present in 15% and obesity (BMI ≥ 95th percentile) was present in 15% of participants. Post‐transplant steroid therapy was used in 49% of patients (n = 16/33) at the time of the first ABPM. For patients receiving steroid therapy, the first ABPM was performed at a median age of 2.4 (1.2–3.6) months post‐transplant, with 15 of these ABPMs occurring within the first 6 months after transplant.

Hypertension Diagnosis and Classification

Based on the first ABPM measurement, 82% (27/33) of patients were classified as normotensive or as having well‐controlled BP if they were on anti‐hypertensive therapies (Table 2). Among normotensive patients, 81% (22/27) were receiving anti‐hypertensive therapy. Within the normotensive group, when pairing the first ABPM with the corresponding CBP measurements, WCH was identified in 6 patients (18%) (Supplementary Table 1).

The initial ABPM measurement showed hypertension in 6 patients (19%) (Table 2). Specifically, 5 of the 6 patients presented with isolated nocturnal hypertension. The median BMI percentile in patients with a hypertensive initial ABPM was 92 (IQR 53–97), compared with 49 (15–80) in normotensive patients (p = 0.141). Among 6 patients who had hypertension identified by ABPM, 4 had sustained hypertension and 2 had MH. Both patients with MH were taking two anti‐hypertensive medications; among the 4 patients with sustained hypertension, 2 were not taking any anti‐hypertensive medications and 2 patients were on at least one anti‐hypertensive medication. For patients with hypertensive ABPMs, 83% were receiving steroid therapy, compared to 41% of normotensive patients (p = 0.085). All patients who underwent ABPM within 6 months post‐transplant were receiving steroid therapy, including 11 with normotensive ABPMs and 4 with hypertensive ABPMs. There were 17 patients that were not receiving steroids at the time of the first ABPM, including 0 patients with MH, 1 with sustained hypertension, 4 with WCH and 12 with sustained normotension.

The prevalence of CKD was 55% (18/33) overall; a history of resolved CKD was evident in 67% (4/6) of patients with hypertension by ABPM, compared to 52% (14/27) in patients with normotension (p = 0.665) (Table 2). With respect to echocardiographic findings, median LVMi was trending towards significance (p = 0.072), as median LVMi was 36.9 (34.0–49.6, n = 20) in the sustained normotensive group, 39.2 (31.3–44.5) in the WCH group and 49.3 (42.1–88.9) in the masked/sustained hypertension group (Table 3). There was a statistically significant difference in median LVM across blood pressure phenotypes (p = 0.032); the masked/sustained hypertension group demonstrated a significantly higher median LVM of 160.9 (64.3–172.3), compared to 66.9 (45.4–94.3, n = 20) in sustained normotensive patients and 54.4 (36.9–100.7) in the WCH group. In addition, significant differences in systolic shortening fraction were observed (p = 0.032), largely explained by increased shortening fraction in the WCH group compared to other groups. No significant differences in systolic ejection fraction were observed. There were no clinical or statistically significant differences observed regarding diastolic function across the BP cohorts.

Other clinical characteristics, including sex, age at transplant, primary diagnosis for transplant, immunosuppression regimen, positive cross match, history of acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease, PLTD and CAV and graft function, did not differ significantly between normotensive and hypertensive patients (p > 0.1). Several measures, including weight percentile and number of anti‐hypertensive agents, demonstrated trends towards higher values in hypertensive patients but did not reach statistical significance.

Response to Therapy and Longitudinal Trends

Among patients that were classified as sustained normotensive, 48% (10/21) remained normotensive throughout all subsequent ABPMs, while 9 patients (43%) developed hypertension and 2 patients (10%) had no subsequent ABPM available for evaluation within the study period (Figure 1). Furthermore, of the 6 patients who were classified with WCH at the initial ABPM, 5 patients (83%) were found to have hypertension at a subsequent ABPM, and one patient did not have a subsequent ABPM in the study period. Of those 5 patients, 3 (60%) developed isolated nocturnal hypertension.

Among the 6 patients who were hypertensive at their initial ABPM, there were 5 (83%) patients who had their hypertension recognized and documented by the transplant clinician with subsequent modifications to hypertension management implemented, and 3 patients had anti‐hypertensive therapy initiated or adjusted. One patient achieved normal BP on subsequent ABPM, following a change in medication (Figure 1). In contrast, 4 (67%) patients continued to have persistent hypertension despite ongoing therapy. One hypertensive patient did not have any follow‐up ABPM during the study period.

As an exploratory analysis, LVMi was tracked over time in each patient with follow up ABPM and paired echocardiographic data at corresponding time points (n = 28/33) (Figure 2). While there was substantial overlap in LVMi values across the different ABPM phenotypes, some trends were present. The highest initial LVMi values (> 120 g/m2·7) were predominantly observed in patients with persistent hypertension, whereas those with sustained normotension were clustered at lower values, generally below 60 g/m2·7. One patient that transitioned from a hypertensive to normotensive phenotype showed a reduction in LVMi from approximately 100 g/m2·7 to < 60 g/m2·7 over 12 months. While data density was greatest in the first 36 months, long‐term follow‐up beyond 96 months showed late‐term LVMi increases in several hypertensive and normotensive to hypertensive patients.

Discussion

This single‐center retrospective study in pediatric HTRs highlights the importance of early and ongoing hypertension surveillance using ABPM in this high‐risk population. Notably, we found the overall incidence of residual hypertension at the time of the initial ABPM was low (18%), likely reflecting the appropriate detection and management of hypertension in pediatric HTRs at our center. This is further supported by 79% (26/33) of all patients being on anti‐hypertensive therapy at the time of the initial ABPM, including 81% (22/27) of normotensive patients by ABPM, emphasizing the high burden of hypertension in the early post‐transplant period. In contrast, in a single‐center prospective study in pediatric HTRs, the first ABPM was completed a median of 5.5 years post‐transplant and detected masked hypertension in 60% (18/30) of previously undiagnosed patients [5]. The difference in prevalence of hypertension may have been due to the earlier ABPM surveillance in our cohort (median of 0.7 years post‐transplant), resulting in an earlier diagnosis of hypertension. Thus, our patients received earlier treatment, potentially mitigating the development of undiagnosed ambulatory hypertension. Many of the patients were initiated or maintained on antihypertensive therapies during their peri‐transplant inpatient stay, which led to some patients receiving an ABPM earlier than the protocolized 1 year post‐transplant time point. Our findings support the early implementation of ABPM surveillance in pediatric HTRs.

Of the 6 patients with hypertensive initial ABPMs, 5 (83%) had isolated nocturnal hypertension. This is similar to findings from Bansal et al. that identified isolated nocturnal hypertension in 78% of pediatric HTRs with hypertension by ABPM [5]. Nocturnal hypertension cannot be detected by CBP, nor can it be detected via non‐ABPM ambulatory assessments, such as home BP protocols. The high prevalence of nocturnal hypertension may be explained by studies that have shown impaired autonomic regulation of BP after transplantation, contributing to abnormal circadian BP control [15, 16]. Importantly, nocturnal hypertension can contribute to adverse long‐term cardiac effects. For example, in other solid organ transplant populations such as pediatric kidney transplant recipients, children with isolated nocturnal hypertension have significantly higher LVMi compared with normotensive controls [17]. As such, isolated nocturnal hypertension, a challenging hypertensive phenotype to detect with important long‐term consequences, additionally emphasizes the use of ABPM in high risk pediatric populations, such as HTRs. Although the difference in steroid use between hypertensive and normotensive patients did not reach statistical significance, there was a trend towards a higher proportion of patients with hypertensive initial ABPMs receiving steroid therapy compared to patients with normotensive ABPMs. This finding is not surprising given the known association between steroid therapy and hypertension. Our findings align with prior work that included 51 pediatric HTRs where prednisone use was significantly more common in hypertensive (64%) compared with normative patients (23%) [18]. These findings lend support to more contemporary efforts from pediatric heart transplant programs to reduce the magnitude and duration of steroid exposure in the post‐transplant period [19]. In our cohort, most patients on steroid therapy had an ABPM assessment performed closer to the date of transplant. Early after transplant, per protocol, steroids are weaned 0.1 mg/kg/day by day 25 post‐transplant and discontinued by 4–6 months post‐transplant, which coincides with other contributing factors such as proximity to transplant and associated AKI, higher target levels for tacrolimus, and pre‐transplant antihypertensive therapy [12]. Thus, ABPM results should be interpreted within the context of individual patient characteristics and timing of assessment. Even more, the long term impact of steroid discontinuation on blood pressure and cardiac outcomes is difficult to appreciate, as this period also coincides with other concurrent physiological and clinical changes, including improvement in renal function and cardiac adaptations and lower tacrolimus target levels, making it difficult to isolate the independent effect of steroid withdrawal.

In our study population, echocardiographic findings demonstrated structural and functional differences across BP phenotypes, particularly in patients with hypertension. There was a statistically significant difference in median LVM across blood pressure phenotypes, with a trend towards significant changes for indexed LVM (LVMi). This finding is supported by another study that included 28 children more than 6 months after heart transplantation, where researchers found a significant association between mean 24‐h systolic BP and interventricular septal thickness, another marker of cardiac hypertrophy and remodeling (p = 0.01) [20]. Collectively, these results suggest increased cardiac workload and early cardiac hypertrophy associated with hypertension. However, interpretation is limited by the small sample size and potential confounders, including post‐transplant steroid therapy. Our findings are also supported by the Study of High Blood Pressure in Pediatrics: Adult Hypertension Onset in Youth (SHIP‐AHOY) cohort study which showed in 244 healthy adolescents that ABPM phenotypes of WCH, MH, and sustained hypertension have a significantly greater number of subclinical cardiovascular injury markers of left ventricular structure and function, and vascular stiffness compared to normotensive patients even after adjusting for sex, age, and BMI [21]. In addition, although there was a difference in shortening fraction between the groups, it is driven in large part by differences in the WCH group. There is no clear clinical explanation for differences in shortening fraction in the WCH group compared with pediatric HTRs with normotensive or hypertensive phenotypes.

A novel aspect of this study was the trending of LVMi over time across different BP phenotype groups (Figure 2), as part of an exploratory analysis. While sample size limitations prevented an in‐depth statistical analysis, patients with persistent hypertension appeared to have LVMi values that clustered at higher levels across follow‐up, whereas those with sustained normotension were more frequently clustered at lower values. This trend suggests an association between sustained elevated blood pressure and hypertrophic remodeling over time. However, there was variability and overlap in LVMi across BP phenotypes, highlighting that these changes are not uniform and there is a need for ongoing longitudinal surveillance to better characterize patterns of cardiac remodeling, as well as additional contributing factors that influence LVM (including body habitus and donor‐to‐recipient size discrepancies). Prior studies in pediatric HTRs have demonstrated persistent adverse left ventricular functioning/remodeling over time [22]. The impact of post‐transplant remodeling and hypertension on longitudinal LVM in pediatric HTRs requires further study.

Another novel feature of the present study is the serial evaluation of ABPM assessments in our cohort. We found that out of the 21 sustained normotensive patients, 9 (43%) developed hypertension on subsequent ABPMs. A key finding in this study is all WCH patients with a follow up ABPM eventually progressed towards developing hypertension by ABPM. Prior studies have suggested that WCH can be predictive of the development of sustained hypertension in non‐transplant pediatric populations [23]. These findings indicate that WCH is often not a benign entity and may instead represent underlying changes that are not yet manifested into persistent hypertension. Thus, patients with WCH may require closer long‐term surveillance. Among patients that were hypertensive at their initial ABPM, longitudinal ABPM data revealed that four patients remained hypertensive despite treatment, while one patient achieved well controlled BP. These findings underscore the dynamic nature of BP in pediatric HTRs and the challenges in managing it. Moreover, the findings highlight that ABPM is an essential test when evaluating responses to anti‐hypertensive therapies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the study results. A larger sample size would strengthen the study and improve the generalizability of the results. As a referral center performing heart transplants across most of western Canada, many participants underwent routine ambulatory care (including ABPM) at their home institution. Thus, we lack long‐term ABPM data from referral sites. Additionally, the median time of the first ABPM was 0.7 years, earlier than our routine surveillance protocol; therefore, our findings mostly reflect early post‐transplant outcomes and may be biased towards earlier performance in the context of known existing hypertension on therapy or clinical concern of hypertension. Further, donor heart size relative to recipient body size was not available, and therefore donor‐recipient size mismatch may have influenced both absolute LVM and LVMi. Although we aimed to record medications indicated for hypertension, some of these agents may have also been used for other purposes, which may have influenced the interpretation of antihypertensive exposure.

Hypertension diagnostic guidelines are established for heights ≥ 120 cm; however, some patients in our study were below this threshold. In these cases, the closest available reference values for height were used, which aligns with clinical practice. Furthermore, hypertension diagnosis guidelines for interpreting CBP and ABPM changed during the study period. Thus, clinician interpretation may have been based on earlier or newer guidelines, particularly during transition periods. There were key differences in CBP interpretations as the older CBP guidelines use percentile thresholds for all ages and incorporate BP load, which is a portion of the time the BP is above a certain limit, while newer guidelines have removed data from overweight and obese children while also introducing fixed cut‐off points for adolescents. These guideline changes have shown increased sensitivity in hypertension diagnosis, particularly in adolescents, which is a group where hypertension is often underrecognized [24]. Despite these limitations, this study highlights the importance of ABPM in pediatric HTRs and underscores the need for continued refinement of BP measurement methods to improve diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision‐making.

Conclusions

Hypertension, particularly nocturnal hypertension, is common in pediatric HTRs. WCH is important to detect as it can lead to hypertension development, suggesting that isolated CBP elevations may not be entirely benign. As such, ABPM should be incorporated into routine hypertension surveillance in pediatric HTRs as it is a key tool for tracking the progression of hypertension and optimizing anti‐hypertensive therapy in this high‐risk population. Future research should focus on larger, multi‐center evaluations in pediatric HTRs to assess whether the earlier detection and refined treatment of hypertension, through the use of ABPM, can improve long‐term post‐transplant outcomes.
