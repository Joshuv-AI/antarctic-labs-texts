Cognitive impairment and associated factors among adult diabetes mellitus patients in Amhara National Regional State referral hospitals, Ethiopia: a multicenter institution-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Mitku Tessafa, Winta Tesfaye, Yibeltal Yismaw Gela, Baye Ashenef, Amanuel Baye, Mohammed Jemal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCognitive impairment is characterized by compromised cognitive function in one or more domains. Nonetheless, it has devastating health and socioeconomic burdens on diabetic patients worldwide, and studies conducted, especially in Ethiopia, are not large enough. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of cognitive impairment and its associated factors among adult diabetes mellitus patients in Amhara National Regional State Referral Hospitals, Ethiopia.MethodsA multicenter institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted with 415 study participants at referral Hospitals in Amhara National Regional State between May 1 and June 10, 2022. The sample was selected using a simple and systematic random sampling technique. Data were collected using a pretested structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. Cognitive impairment was assessed using standardized Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) tool. Epi data 4.6 and STATA (14.0) were used for data entry and analysis, respectively. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression, odds ratios, and with 95% confidence interval (CI) at a p-value of ≤ 0.05 was used to declare significantly associated variables.ResultsThe magnitude of cognitive impairment among adult diabetes mellitus patients was 46.27%, with 95% CI (41.5%, 51.1%). No formal education (AOR = 4.81, 95% CI:1.54, 15); older age > 60 years (AOR = 3.91, 95% CI:1.6, 7.9), current alcohol drinking (AOR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.13, 6.18), physical activity (AOR = 0.44, 95% CI:0.22, 0.89), fasting blood sugar (FBS) > 130 mg/dl (AOR = 4.69, 95% CI: 2.61, 8.41), hypoglycemia episode (AOR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.62, 5.06), insulin treatment (AOR = 3.76, 95% CI: 1.94, 7.27), body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 (AOR = 3.3, 95% CI:1.03, 10.56) were variables significantly associated with cognitive impairment.Conclusion and recommendationNearly, half of the study participants had cognitive impairment. No formal education, older age > 60 years, current alcohol drinking, physical activity, FBS > 130 mg/dl, hypoglycemia episode, insulin treatment, and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were significantly associated with cognitive impairment. Healthcare providers are recommended to routinely screen diabetic patients during follow-up and, frequently follow cognitively impaired and high-risk groups.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is characterized by compromised cognitive function in one or more domains, which is greater than expected for an individual’s educational level [1–3]. It is a serious and unabated problem of the globe [4]. Both type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) have been associated with reduced performance on multiple domains of cognitive function [5, 6]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a growing metabolic disease that causes damage to multiple systems including the nervous system [4, 7].

The global prevalence of cognitive impairment among T2DM is estimated to be 45% [2]. The prevalence of cognitive impairment among DM patients in the USA [8], Korea [9], China [10], and India [11], was indicated to be 22.2%, 43.5%, 63.33%, and 48%, respectively. In the case of Africa, a 33.4%, 56.8%, and 53.3% prevalence of cognitive impairment among DM patients was reported by a study in Nigeria [12], Egypt [13], and Ethiopia [14], respectively. The majority of individuals with cognitive impairment develop Alzheimer’s disease, which diminishes psychomotor speed, executive function, and verbal and memory processing speed [13].

Sociodemographic characteristics such as sex, age, educational level, occupation, and monthly income are strong factors influencing cognitive impairment in subjects with DM [14–18]. Behavioral factors, such as smoking and heavy alcohol consumption, increase the prevalence of cognitive impairment in patients with diabetes [9, 14, 19–21]. Some studies have suggested that hypertension (HTN), hyperglycemia, obesity, and hyperinsulinemia are determinants of cognitive impairment in DM patients [22, 23]. Chronic renal failure and heart failure also represent new independent risk factors for cognitive decline [20]. Hypotension and hypoglycemia are also associated with it [24].

Cognitive impairment is a concern because it results in disability and increases healthcare costs [25]. It also results in non-adherence to diet, exercise, and diabetic medication [9, 26]. However, therapeutic regimens such as cholinesterase supplementation have been attempted and no curative treatments have been investigated [24].

Although cognitive impairment among patients with DM is a public health problem, studies conducted in Ethiopia are not sufficiently large. Although sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle factors that dynamically change over time significantly affect cognitive decline in patients with DM, no recent study has been conducted in Ethiopia. Nonetheless, cognitive status is significantly affected by educational level, in most studies including the studies conducted in Ethiopia educational level of illiterates is categorized under less than grade 8 attainment prevalence of cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption in Jimma is 11.2%, and 43.4% respectively [14], while in Amhara region prevalence of cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption is 9.3% and 61.6% respectively [27]. Alcohol consumption and cigarette smoking, which affect the cognitive status of patients with DM, show discrepancies in these populations. Overall, information regarding cognitive impairment among patients with DM has not been adequately addressed, especially in Ethiopia.

Early and routine detection of cognitive impairment in patients with DM and provision of possible interventions, such as health education and special glycemic control follow-up, are conducive to mitigating DM-related cognitive impairment. Therefore, this study aimed to assess cognitive impairment and its associated factors among adult DM patients in Amhara National Regional State referral hospitals to fill the gap and provide information about cognitive impairment in diabetic patients for health institutions and other stakeholders, which will be relevant in developing plans to reduce the magnitude of cognitive impairment among diabetic patients. Incorporating this knowledge into health education and training programs will help increase awareness among the community and health professionals, thereby mitigating the burden of cognitive impairment among diabetic patients. This study will also serve as a baseline for future research by revising its limitations.

Materials and methods

Study area and period

This was an institutional-based cross-sectional study conducted from May 1 to June 10, 2022, at selected referral hospitals in the Amhara region. Amhara region is located in the northern region of Ethiopia, the capital city of Bahir Dar, 564 km from Addis Ababa [28]. This region comprises 25.9% of the country’s population [29]. There are eight referral hospitals in the region: the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Referral Hospital, Felege Hiwot Specialized Referral Hospital, Tibebe Gion Specialized Referral Hospital, Debre Markos Specialized Referral Hospital, Dessie Referral Hospital, Debre Berhan referral Hospital, and Woldiya referral Hospital. These Hospitals provide comprehensive inpatient care and support services to more than 30.9 million people. Of these, Debre Markos, Debre Berhan, and Debre Tabor Referral Hospital were the actual randomly selected study sites. Debre Markos Referral Hospital is located in Debre Markos town 256 km from Bahir Dar and 299 km from Addis Ababa. Debre Berhan referral hospital is located at Debre Berhan town, located 518 km from Bahir Dar and 130 km from Addis Ababa. Debre Tabor referral hospital is located in Debre Tabor town, which is 667 km away from Addis Ababa, and 102 km away from Bahir Dar.

Populations and eligibility criteria

The source population was all adult diabetic patients on chronic follow-up at referral Hospitals in Amhara National Regional State. The study population was all adult patients with diabetes who were encountered during the data collection period and were enrolled in the chronic follow-up clinic at Debre Markos referral Hospital, Debre Berhan referral Hospital, and Debre Tabor referral Hospital. All adult patients with diabetes aged > 18 years at the chronic follow-up were included in the study. Patients who were severely ill, had permanent hearing loss, were newly diagnosed with DM, and had a follow-up of less than three months were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined by using a single population proportion formula by considering the prevalence of cognitive impairment among T2DM 53.3% at Jimma University specialized hospitals [14] with the assumption of 95% CI and 5% margin of error (d = 0.05). Then, by considering a contingency rate of 10%, the final sample size was 422. In this study, the first three referral hospitals were randomly selected using the lottery method (Debre Tabor, Debre Berhan, and Debre Markos referral hospitals) and the sampling unit in each hospital was determined based on proportionally allocated numbers. According to a two-month report, there were 2672 DM on follow-up in the three selected referral Hospitals in the Amhara region. A systematic random sampling technique was used to obtain individual samples from each referral hospital. The kth interval for each was determined by dividing the total population size by the total sample size, that is, K = N/n: (672/422 = 6.33 ~ 6). Then, random number 2 was selected using the lottery method as a starting number from 1 to 6. The next participant was selected every 6th interval from the follow-up.

Operational definitions

Cognitive impaired Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of ≤ 21 for the illiterate group [30] and ≤ 22, ≤ 24, and ≤ 26 for grade 8 and less, grade 9–12, and college and university, respectively [31, 32].

For grades 9–12 and higher education, participants with MMSE scores of 20–24 and 20–26 had mild cognitive impairment respectively, 10–19, and 0–9 are, moderate and severe cognitive impairment respectively. MMSE scores of 18–22 for up to grade 8 and 18–21 for those without formal education indicate mild cognitive impairment, and 8–17 and 0–7 indicate moderate and severe cognitive impairment in both groups, respectively [31, 32].

Physically active: The WHO recommendation for weekly physical activity is engaging in at least 150 min of moderate physical activity or 75 min of vigorous physical activity per week [33].

Hypoglycemia episode was assessed by asking patients weather or not they had experienced sign symptoms of hypoglycemia like fainting, sweating, dizziness or lightheadedness, blurred vision [34].

Data collection procedure and tools

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire comprising sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical variables, physical measurements of blood pressure (BP), and body mass index (BMI) adapted from the literature [14–16, 23, 35]. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% (22) participants at Shegaw Motta General Hospital, East Gojam zone of Amhara regional state having similar sociodemographic characteristics to participants in the main study area. Structured questionnaires were translated into the local Amharic language by a language expert and back-translated into English.

Cognitive status measurement

The cognitive status of the participants was screened using the MMSE tool, which was validated in Ethiopia [31] and is a fully structured scale with 19 questions with a total score of 30. It was used to test the cognitive status of participants based on their educational status. They were grouped into seven categories: orientation to the place (state, country, town, hospital, and floor), orientation to time (year, season, month, day, and date), registration (immediately, repeating three words), attention and concentration (serially subtracting 7, beginning with 100, or, alternatively, spelling the word WORLD backward), recall (recalling the previous three words), language (naming two items, repeating a phrase, reading aloud and understanding a sentence, writing a sentence, and following a three-step command), and visual construction (copying a design) [10]. It is widely used in both clinical and research settings, is a quick and easy measure that assesses seven areas of cognitive functioning, and has been shown to have both good test-retest reliability and acceptable sensitivity and specificity to detect mild to moderate stages of dementia [36].

The weight of the study participants was measured using a standard balance, and their height was measured using a standard height measurement instrument. BMI was calculated as BMI = weight in kg/(height in m) 2.

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured using a sphygmomanometer in the sitting position on the left arm by requesting the patient to avoid tight clothing after resting for at least 10 min.

Chronic renal failure, heart failure, retinopathy, type of DM, and related data were extracted from the patient charts. The FBS obtained during the time of data collection was also obtained from the patient’s charts.

Data quality management

The questionnaire was pretested 5 days before the beginning of data collection at Shegaw Motta General Hospital, East on 22 participants (5%) of the sample size, which has similar sociodemographic characteristics with the study area. The principal investigator delivered essential training for one randomly selected nurse as a data collector and one health officer as a supervisor about the data collection procedure and the questionnaire itself for one day at each hospital and the principal investigator and supervisor supervised the study. Questionnaires were checked for completeness and accuracy.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Cognitive impairment (Yes, No).

Independent variables

Sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, educational level, occupation, and monthly income); behavioral factors (cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity); clinical factors (hypertension, duration of diabetes, blood glucose level, antidiabetic and antihypertensive medications, chronic renal failure, retinopathy, and heart failure); and physical measurements (SBP and DBP, and BMI).

Data analysis procedure

The collected data were checked for completeness, entered into Epi data 4.6, and exported to the STATA 14.0 version for analysis. Crude and adjusted odds ratios were used to measure the association between independent variables and cognitive impairment, and the chi-square test was used for categorical independent variables to check the assumptions. The results are presented using a frequency table and a graph. After normality was checked, the mean and standard deviation for normally distributed numerical data and the median and interquartile range (IQR) for asymmetric numerical data were used to present the descriptive statistics. The model had good fitness, as verified by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a p-value of 0.6. There was no multicollinearity checked by the variable inflation factor (VIF) and no omitted variable in the STATA output of the multivariable analysis. Both multivariable and bivariable logistic regression models were used to measure the association between independent variables with dependent variables. Variables with a p-value ≤ 025 in the bivariable logistic regression analysis were selected for the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Variables with a p-value of < 0.05 by multivariable logistic regression analysis were declared statistically significant with cognitive impairment.

Factors associated with cognitive impairment

In bivariate logistic regression analysis, sex, age, residence, educational level, monthly income, occupation, alcohol consumption, physical activity, FBS, DM type, duration of DM, DM drug type, HTN, renal failure, SBP, and BMI were significant (P ≤ 0.25).

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, older age (> 60 years), educational status without formal education, current alcohol drinking, physical activity, FBS (> 130 mg/dl), insulin treatment, hypoglycemic episodes, and BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 were significantly associated with cognitive impairment.

Diabetic Patients who were above the age of 60 years had 3.91 times greater odds of cognitive impairment (AOR = 3.91, 95% CI: 1.6, 7.9) as compared to less than 45 years old respondents. The odds of cognitive impairment among the study participants who had no formal education were 4.81 times (AOR = 4.81, 95% CI: 1.54, 15) greater than those who had higher educational attainment.

Current alcohol drinkers had 2.64 times (AOR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.13, 6.18) greater odds of cognitive impairment than non-drinkers. Being physically active decreased the odds of cognitive impairment by 56% (AOR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.22, 0.89) as compared to physically inactive individuals.

The odds of cognitive impairment among participants who had FBS ≥ 130 mg/dl were 4.69 times (AOR = 4.69, 95% CI: 2.61, 8.41) than good glycemic control (FBS = 80–130 mg/dl) diabetic patients. Insulin-received respondents had 3.76 (AOR = 3.76, 95%, CI 1.94, 7.27) greater odds of cognitive impairment than oral hypoglycemic users. Participants who had hypoglycemic episodes had 2.87 (AOR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.62, 5.06) times more increased risk of cognitive impairment than those who never experienced (see Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, the overall prevalence of cognitive impairment among DM patients was 46.27% with 95% CI (41.5–51.1%), which is in line with the studies conducted in China 44.6%[37], India 48% [11], Korea 43% [8] and Tikur Anebessa Specialized Hospital Ethiopia 45% [16].

However, it was higher than the studies conducted in the USA, at 22.2% [15], Korea 32.7% [9] and Nigeria 33.4% [12]. Owing to this discrepancy, differences in sociodemographic characteristics, type of test, and scoring system can be justified. The prevalence of cognitive impairment among adult DM patients in this study was lower as compared to studies conducted in Jimma, 53.3% [14], Sub-Saharan Africa, 88.5% [3], Egypt, 56.8% [13], Japan, 72.7%[38], and China, 63.33% [10]. This discrepancy could be due to the type of test used; they categorized the illiterate group as those who attained education up to grade 8. An additional possible reason for the discrepancy between this study and the study conducted in Jimma, Ethiopia, might be the small sample size.

This study identified a proportion of T1DM cases at 35.9%, which substantially exceeds the global estimate of 5–10% for T1DM [39]. Patients with T1DM were also significantly younger than those with T2DM, which could confound associations between diabetes type and cognitive impairment. This discrepancy may be due to relatively small sample size and data is only collected from referral hospitals, which may lack generalizability.

Participants who had no formal education had greater odds of cognitive impairment than participants with higher education levels, which is supported by previous studies [15, 35]. The possible reason for this phenomenon might be differences in behavioral and lifestyle factors, higher involvement of diabetic self-care management in higher educational attainment than in those without formal education, and lower educational attainment [16]. Older age (> 60 years) was significantly associated with cognitive impairment, which is in line with the results of other studies [14, 15]. A possible mechanism could be that poor metabolic control in older age groups enhances diabetes-related complications that alter acute mental function [40]. Another possible explanation could be that as the age of patients advances, brain atrophy, white matter lesions, brain volume decrease, oxidative stress, altered vascular function, which decreases mental function, and DM exacerbates degenerative changes in older age groups, including neuronal cells [41, 42].

This study revealed that current alcohol consumption was significantly associated with cognitive impairment; a similar trend has been observed in previous studies in Japan [9, 20, 35, 38]. DM causes neuronal cell death and reduces memory cell formation [12], prevents neuronal regeneration and memory cell formation, and induces brain lesions, which may be a possible reason [43]. Obesity is significantly associated with cognitive impairment, and previous studies have reported a similar trend [44–47]. Obesity increases the likelihood of poor glycemic control since it increases the secretion of non-esterified fatty acid that promotes insulin resistance [48], as explained before poor glycemic control causes cognitive decline. Obesity also induces chronic inflammation that hinders neurogenesis and damages existing cells by compromising BBB integrity, allowing the entry of chemical irritants into the brain, thereby producing inflammatory cytokines [41]. Another possible explanation is that a high BMI in patients with DM increases mental fatigue [40]. However, the relationship between obesity and cognitive impairment remains inconclusive. In some populations, higher BMI has been associated with better cognitive performance [49]. Therefore, this complex and context-dependent association requires further investigation.

This study reported that physical activity was a protective factor, which is supported by a previous study [20]. A possible explanation for this phenomenon might be that engaging in physical activity increases glucose uptake by working muscles, since exercise increases blood flow towards the muscle and eventually increases insulin receptors [48].

Poorly controlled hyperglycemia (FBS > 130) is another significant factor associated with cognitive impairment. This indicates that diabetes treatment improves cognitive function. This finding is supported by those of previous studies [22, 23]. This association could be due to the formation of AGEs and oxidative stress and the direct neurotoxic effect of hyperglycemia that damages neuronal cells and neural blood vessels [12, 42, 50]. It also leads to thickening of the cerebral vascular muscle basement membrane, eventually leading to reduced cerebral blood flow that results in a diminished ability of the brain to understand integrated information. Damage of fibronectin secondary to hyperglycemia that results in the proliferation of capillaries in the brain causing increased permeability of the brain cerebrospinal fluid barrier may be the other possible reason [42]. This study also revealed that insulin treatment is positively associated with cognitive decline. A similar trend was also observed in studies conducted at Tikur Anebessa Hospital, Ethiopia, and Japan [16, 51]. This may be due to the direct effect of insulin, which causes high amyloid deposition [52]. Patients receiving insulin have high blood glucose levels, which may be due to hyperglycemia. The hypoglycemic effect of insulin, which results in sufficient energy being delivered to the brain, may be another reason [51]. Hypoglycemic episodes were a strong factor contributing to cognitive decline in patients with DM, which was also reported in previous studies, including those conducted in India [53, 54]. This might be due to neuronal death and the inability of the brain to perform its task properly, secondary to insufficient energy delivered to the brain, as explained earlier [55].

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of this study was assessing the prevalence of cognitive impairment and associated factors in multi-center settings, which makes it relatively more representative. In the face of this strength, this study has several weaknesses. As the study design was cross-sectional, the cause-and-effect relationship between DM and cognitive impairment could not be determined. Data collection relied on a questionnaire with self-reported variables, which may have led participants to provide desirable responses. This study was conducted in referral hospitals, which may not be fully representative of the general diabetic population, particularly those managed in rural or primary healthcare settings. This study did not include neuroimaging techniques or biomarker analyses to directly assess the underlying neuropathological mechanisms of cognitive impairment. In addition, only the MMSE was used to screen for cognitive status due to financial issues, although brain imaging was required to accurately diagnose cognitive impairment. Furthermore, the lack of a control group is another limitation. Moreover, the overrepresentation of T1DM and age differences among groups should be acknowledged as limitations that may impact the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion and recommendations

The magnitude of cognitive impairment among patients with diabetes is nearly half that required for public health interventions. Sociodemographic characteristics, such as old age (> 60 years), poorly controlled hyperglycemia and hypoglycemic episodes, insulin treatment, obesity, current alcohol drinking, and positive association with it, while physical activity and higher educational attainment were protective factors. Therefore, healthcare providers are recommended to screen for cognitive impairment routinely and frequently follow cognitively impaired and high-risk groups, such as older patients, insulin-treated patients, and those who had more than 10 years of DM follow-up. They are also recommended to provide detailed awareness for diabetic patients about the fact that DM can cause cognitive impairment and different factors that aggravate cognitive impairment and glycemic deviation, such as hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia, through their health education program. Diabetic patients with diabetes are advised to take their medication properly and in a timely manner, avoid alcohol consumption, and perform physical exercise daily. Future researchers should conduct more advanced research to overcome the limitations of this study.
