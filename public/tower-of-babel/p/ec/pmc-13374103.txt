The Efficiencies and Products of Dilute Methane Oxidation in a Chlorine Radical Photoreactor
Authors: Richard Randall, Max I. Kessler, Robert B. Jackson, Arun Majumdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A potential approach for mitigating the climate impact of hard-to-abate, dilute (less than 1000 ppm; ppm) methane (CH4) is gas-phase advanced oxidation (GPAO). Here, we present experimental results from a benchtop GPAO reactor oxidizing CH4 with chlorine radicals (Cl·) produced from the photolysis of chlorine gas (Cl2), a process we term “Cl2-GPAO.” We find that at CH4 concentrations from 2 to 90 ppm, supplying Cl2 in a ratio of 1:1 with CH4 uses photogenerated Cl· efficiently, with 3 Cl· photogenerated for each CH4 oxidized with only 20% of Cl· recombining to Cl2. When more Cl2 is added, the product balance shifts from mainly carbon monoxide to mainly carbon dioxide. Cl2-GPAO performs similarly under relative humidities between 5 and 60%. In the presence of ppm-level ammonia, toluene, nitric oxide, and hydrogen sulfide, CH4 conversion decreases, suggesting that gaseous contaminants compete with CH4 for Cl·. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry revealed that Cl2-GPAO with 30 ppm CH4 and Cl2 produces 1.05 ± 0.32 part per billion (ppb) methyl chloride and 1.25 ± 0.38 ppb chloroform. Methylene chloride and carbon tetrachloride were not detected. We discuss the implications for cost-effective and climate-beneficial Cl2-GPAO at scale.

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is a potent greenhouse gas that has contributed ∼0.5 °C (or ∼30%) to the Earth’s global average temperature rise from preindustrial times. Mitigating ongoing emissions of CH4, which has a global warming potential (GWP) of 120 at the time of its emission and a mean lifetime of 12 years in the atmosphere, is particularly critical to meeting a temperature target below 2 °C and to shaving the peak of the planet’s warming this century. , While cost-effective emissions-avoidance strategies exist for some CH4 sources and emissions-control devices (such as flares, thermal oxidizers, and biofilters) are available for relatively concentrated sources of CH4, most sources of CH4 occur at concentrations so dilute (less than 1000 ppm; ppm) that no commercial technologies exist for mitigating them cost effectively. − Continuously oxidizing all CH4 to carbon dioxide (CO2) that is currently emitted from point sources at 10 to 1000 ppm would avoid ∼0.2 °C of global warming while developing a safe and cost-effective ‘atmospheric methane removal’ process to oxidize 2 ppm CH4 in the ambient air could offset any remaining CH4 emissions, both human and natural. Approaches to cost-effectively oxidize dilute CH4 into CO2, the eventual fate of all CH4 emitted to the atmosphere, could therefore be a valuable tool for climate change mitigation.

Despite this opportunity and the need, cost-effective oxidation of dilute (let alone atmospheric) CH4 remains an open engineering challenge. Very large volumes of air must be processed per unit of CH4 converted, eliminating options for high-temperature oxidation and thus requiring systems to operate at near-ambient temperatures with high reaction rates and low pressure drops. , However, CH4’s chemical stability, low solubility in water (22 mg/L at standard conditions, less than that of many other hydrocarbons), and weak interaction with many sorbents and catalysts make it difficult for such systems to achieve high reaction rates under ambient conditions. , Commonly discussed options for dilute CH4 oxidation in closed systems include thermocatalysis, − photocatalysis, ,− biological methanotrophy, − and gas-phase advanced oxidation. , Each has its own appeals and challenges, and research and development are underway to reveal which, if any, can cost-effectively oxidize dilute CH4 in real-world gas streams.

Gas-phase advanced oxidation (GPAO) stands out as an appealing approach due to its fast reaction chemistry and operation near ambient temperature and pressure. In GPAO, a precursor gas is mixed into an air stream and then photolyzed by ultraviolet (UV) light into oxidizing radicals. This strategy generally mimics the radical chemistry by which CH4 is naturally removed from the atmosphere by the hydroxyl radical (·OH) and the chlorine radical (Cl·). Since radicals oxidize CH4 in the bulk gas, the reaction is not limited by the transport of CH4 onto a surface or into solution, circumventing a major barrier to rapid conversion of dilute CH4 via other approaches. However, radical precursors and artificial light tend to be costly, and highly efficient usage of each is required for a practical process. , Possible precursors include ozone (O3) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which form ·OH, and molecular chlorine (Cl2), which forms Cl·. ,, A process using the moisture in air as a precursor to form ·OH has also been demonstrated.

This study focuses on GPAO with Cl2 as a radical precursor, which we term ‘Cl2-GPAO’ and has elsewhere been referred to as the ‘methane eradication photochemical system’ (MEPS). Compared to ·OH, Cl· is more reactive toward CH4, has fewer quenching mechanisms besides the desired reaction with CH4, and has a lower rate of self-recombination. These features enable high steady-state radical concentrations in the photoreactor, potentially allowing faster CH4 conversion, shorter residence times, and/or smaller reactor volumes than would be expected with ·OH-based systems. However, there are safety concerns related to hydrogen chloride (HCl) and carbon monoxide (CO) that may be produced as reaction products; scrubbing these products would increase the system cost and raise the pressure drop that air-handling equipment would need to overcome. Additionally, chlorine-based aqueous advanced oxidation processes are known to produce halocarbon byproducts such as chloroform, which would be undesirable in a CH4-oxidizing process for reasons of both air quality and global warming potential. ,,

Existing studies have confirmed Cl2-GPAO’s effectiveness on dilute and atmospheric CH4; however, many questions remain unanswered. Polat et al. first explored the process, using ∼50 ppm Cl2 to remove ambient (∼2 ppm) CH4 that would otherwise interfere with spectrometric analysis of nitrous oxide in air. Krogsbo̷ll et al. then proposed applying the same chemistry to control CH4 emissions from point sources, describing the overall system layout for Cl2-GPAO (with an electrolyzer, a UV photolysis chamber, and a caustic scrubber), using ∼100 ppm Cl2 and UV radiation to oxidize ∼50% of 50 ppm CH4, and proposing a reaction mechanism. They also found CH4 conversion to vary approximately linearly with inlet Cl2 concentration. The same study modeled that CH4 would be converted into a combination of CO and CO2 in a roughly 2:1 ratio, with expected minimal (less than 0.01%) conversion of CH4 into chlorinated methanes (methyl chloride (CH3Cl), methylene chloride (CH3Cl2), chloroform (CHCl3), and carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)). The authors recently conducted a field trial at a livestock facility with total flow rates of 120 to 240 L/min, finding the process to effectively convert CH4, ammonia (NH3), and other odorous compounds.

Despite these promising results, numerous uncertainties remain, making it difficult to judge whether and how Cl2-GPAO might be practical at a large scale. For instance, to our knowledge, no experiments have quantified the production of chlorinated methanes, and, to date, the ratio of oxidized CH4 to photogenerated Cl· has not been reported. The inlet Cl2 concentration required to oxidize a certain CH4 concentration at a specified conversion efficiency has also not been studied in detail. Quantifying these values can help to set a lower bound for the input costs of a full-scale photoreactor. Finally, the effects of process parameters such as light intensity, flow rate, CH4 concentration, Cl2 concentration, humidity, and contaminant gases on all of the above metrics have remained largely unexplored.

In this work, we explored the effect of various parameters and operating conditions on Cl2-GPAO’s efficiencies and reaction products. We employed a benchtop reactor with a residence time representative of large-scale applications, in which inlet CH4, Cl2, H2O, NH3, nitric oxide (NO), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and toluene (C6H5CH3) concentrations were finely tuned. Infrared spectroscopy was used to measure the CH4 conversions and major reaction products. For a subset of operating conditions, a flask sample of effluent was collected and analyzed in an off-site gas chromatography–mass spectrometry lab to check for chlorinated methane production at parts per billion (ppb) levels. By measuring the reactor’s total Cl· production rate, we report the process’s efficiency under various conditions in terms of ‘CH4 oxidized per supplied Cl2’ and ‘CH4 oxidized per photogenerated Cl·’. We conclude by discussing these metrics’ implications for the scale-up of Cl2-GPAO.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Setup and Procedure

Cl2-GPAO experiments were conducted in a glass flow photoreactor with an internal volume of ∼600 cm3. The setup and procedures are described in detail in Supporting Information (SI) Note S1. Each experiment used a total of 200 mL/min of flow to obtain a 3 min average residence time, comparable to that used by Krogsbo̷ll et al. Unlike in that study, our small reactor and slow flow rates created a laminar flow regime, resulting in some variability in the residence times experienced by different parcels of gas. This behavior is discussed in Note S2, in which we calculate that around 87% of the gas experienced residence times between 1.5 and 4 min. Although, for cost reasons, large-scale reactors will likely use chemical-resistant plastic walls with internal lighting, a UV-transparent glass reactor with external illumination is more practical at a lab scale. These differences from scaled-up Cl2-GPAO reactors were worthwhile in order to have a small system on which operating conditions could be rapidly changed and parametrically tested.

The gas composition was set by mass flow controllers (MFCs) to be 20% O2, 1% H2O (∼35% relative humidity), various concentrations of CH4 and Cl2 and balanced in N2. A 50 W array of 368 nm UV LEDs (spectrum in Figure S1.3a) and a set of UV reflectors were assembled such that, in the presence of excess CH4, ∼75% of Cl2 was photolyzed during the gas’s residence time in the reactor, as measured by an electrochemical Cl2 sensor (Membrapor Cl/C-200) at the reactor outlet. A UV radiometer probe (UV-Design Germany) measured the incident light flux within the reactor to be 15 mW/cm2. The outlet gas optionally passed through a scrubber system (comprising a bubbler of 1 M sodium hydroxide, a molecular sieve, and an activated carbon filter) to remove Cl2 and HCl, and finally to a pair of infrared spectrometers. CH4 concentrations below 35 ppm were measured with a Picarro g2210-i cavity ringdown spectrometer sampling at 1 Hz with a detection limit of 0.1 ppm. Higher CH4 concentrations and all other gases besides Cl2 were measured with an MKS MultiGas 2000 Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer sampling at 0.08 Hz. For each gas, the FTIR had a detection limit less than 0.5 ppm. Neither instrument had substantial cross-sensitivities among the gases present.

For each experimental condition, a desired inlet gas composition was set, and the UV light was cycled on and off while measuring the change in gas concentrations exiting the photoreactor. While the light was on, the scrubber system was bypassed for 10 min in order to sample the full range of gases exiting the reactor (Table S1.2). Bypassing the scrubber had a negligible effect on the measured CH4 concentration. We report the CH4 conversion (−ΔCH4) and the production of CO and CO2. In no experiment did the FTIR spectrometer detect the C1 products formaldehyde (CH2O) or formic acid (CH2O2) at levels above those gases’ 0.5-ppm detection limit.

To measure the total amount of Cl· produced, we conducted a separate ‘radical-counting’ experiment for each inlet Cl2 concentration used. In these tests, we matched all parameters to the corresponding CH4 conversion experiment but supplied an excess (12,500 ppm) of CH4 as a Cl· scavenger. Due to the much higher concentration of CH4 than of Cl·, each generated Cl· was much more likely to oxidize a C–H bond and become HCl than to recombine into Cl2, unlike the CH4 conversion experiments in which non-negligible Cl· recombination occurred. Two times the Cl2 conversion in a radical-counting experiment is therefore an indirect estimate of the reactor’s total Cl· production at a given flow rate and Cl2 concentration. Note S3 describes the excess-CH4-based radical-counting procedure in greater detail. It also describes how the method was validated by using excess toluene as an alternate radical scavenger. Finally, Figure S3.2 shows the measured Cl· production values at inlet Cl2 concentrations ranging from 1 to 90 ppm for a flow rate of 200 mL/min.

In each experimental condition, we report the number of CH4 molecules oxidized per chlorine radical produced by the reactor, a figure of merit we term the ‘Cl· usage efficiency’ (ηCl· ). This value is the quotient of the CH4 conversion, measured in a CH4 conversion experiment, and the total Cl· production, measured in a radical-counting experiment. Prior studies on Cl2-GPAO have reported the process’s apparent quantum yield (AQY), defined as moles of CH4 oxidized per mole of incident UV photons. , AQY can be determined from measurements of the molecules of CH4 oxidized (n CH4; molecules/s) and the incident light flux (φ; W/m2): AQY=nCH4÷(φ·2rL·λ/hc) 1 where 2r = 0.05 m is the reactor diameter, L = 0.3 m is the reactor length, λ = 368 × 10–9 m is the LED wavelength, h = 6.626 × 10–34 J·s is Planck’s constant, and c = 2.998 × 108 m/s is the speed of light. AQY can also be expressed in terms of ηCl· : AQY=ηCl2−abs·QYCl2−photolysis·2ηCl· 2 where ηCl2‑abs is the fraction of generated photons that are absorbed by a Cl2 molecule (a parameter that depends on the light’s wavelength and flux and the photoreactor’s geometry and reflector setup) and QYCl2‑photolysis is the quantum yield of Cl2 photolysis (the fraction of generated photons that photolyze a Cl2 molecule into 2Cl·). Benchtop reactors have low values of ηCl2‑abs and correspondingly low AQYs due to the long optical paths required for ppm-level Cl2 to absorb large fractions of incident UV light. However, in a well-designed photoreactor and reflector for deployment where ηCl2‑abs ≈ 1, the upper bound on AQY is 2ηCl · .

Values of ηCl· do not depend on photoreactor size except where wall effects are involved. Thus, ηCl· values measured at the bench scale can be used to estimate the efficiencies and costs of scaled-up Cl2-GPAO systems, making ηCl· a useful figure of merit. Though wall effects are more important in small reactors, they did not appear to substantially affect our system’s CH4 oxidation and CO or CO2 yields. This was evidenced by its similar performance across flow rates spanning 75 to 300 mL/min (discussed below in the Results section) since one would expect any present wall effects to affect the measurements more at lower flow rates.

Experiments

A first experimental campaign tested Cl2-GPAO under a ‘base case’ of 30 ppm each of inlet CH4 and Cl2. We chose 30 ppm CH4 as the base case because it is within the range of interest for real-world Cl2-GPAO deployment and, additionally, was the highest CH4 concentration that our most sensitive CH4 analyzer could measure. We chose 30 ppm Cl2 for the base case because a 1:1 Cl2–CH4 ratio was found to give a good CH4 conversion and a good Cl· usage efficiency (discussed below in Section ).

A concern of Cl2-GPAO is the potential production of chlorinated methanes, some of which are toxic and have ppm-level exposure limits. To test for chlorinated methanes, samples of reactor effluent from the base-case experiment were collected in evacuated canisters and sent to Atmospheric Analysis & Consulting Inc. (Ventura, CA) for gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis. The EPA Method TO-15 for organic compound analysis was used. A GC-MS analyzer (Agilent 5890E Series II) was calibrated with certified source standards of 10 ppb. The method’s detection limit was 0.5 or 1.0 ppb for most compounds screened, and the measurement uncertainty was ±30%.

A second experimental campaign explored the effects of changing the base-case parameters of inlet Cl2, CH4, and H2O concentrations. First, the inlet Cl2 concentration was varied for a fixed 30-ppm inlet CH4 concentration. This experiment’s results helped justify the use of a 1:1 Cl2–CH4 ratio in the base case and in most other experiments. Next, the inlet CH4 and Cl2 concentrations were varied from 5 to 90 ppm while maintaining a fixed 1:1 CH4–Cl2 ratio. Last, the inlet gas’s moisture was varied from 5% to 60% relative humidity (RH) to test any effects of humidity on reaction efficiencies.

A third experimental campaign tested the effects of four gaseous contaminants: ammonia (NH3), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), toluene (C6H5CH3), and nitric oxide (NO). The first three may be present at parts per million levels at emission sources on which Cl2-GPAO might be deployed, such as dairy barns and wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs). , Toluene was used as a proxy for volatile organic compounds. NO was of interest for its potential to aid CH4 oxidation through reactions with intermediate hydrocarbons and to promote radical chain reactions. Four CH4 conversion experiments were conducted, each varying the concentration of one contaminant gas while keeping base case values for the inlet Cl2, CH4, and humidity.

A fourth experimental campaign tested Cl2-GPAO using ‘application-based’ gas mixtures representative of three deployment scenarios. Variants of Cl2-GPAO have been discussed as a possible tool for atmospheric methane removal; hence, one experiment used simulated atmospheric air with 1.9 ppm CH4. Additionally, dairy operations and WWTPs release substantial amounts of low-concentration (less than 1000 ppm) CH4 and are, as such, possible candidates for Cl2-GPAO deployment. Two further experiments used CH4 concentrations typical of these applications and included common contaminants at high-but-realistic concentrations to simulate challenging scenarios for Cl2-GPAO to address. Toluene was again used as a representative volatile organic compound. Table shows the gas compositions used in the three experiments, with a 1:1 Cl2–CH4 ratio used throughout. Because Ar and CO2 were supplied from the same tank, their concentrations are coupled, causing the Ar concentrations to be slightly different from the air’s true value of ∼0.9%.

Cost and Emissions Model

To assess Cl2-GPAO’s feasibility, we explored the implications of our results on a full-scale system’s cost and process emissions. We measured cost-effectiveness in $ per metric ton CH4 oxidized, $/tCH4 (or per metric ton CO2 equivalent, $/tCO2e) and emission intensity in metric ton CO2 emitted per metric ton CH4 oxidized, tCO2e/tCH4. We developed a basic cost and emissions model of three process inputs: chlorine precursor, electrical energy for lighting, and UV LED hardware. Embodied carbon, non-LED capital costs, Cl2/HCl scrubbing costs, and other operating and energy costs were excluded; hence, these values are best interpreted as a floor on, rather than an estimate of, deployed Cl2-GPAO costs and emissions. The model is derived in Note S4.

Results and Discussion

Base Case

In the base-case experiment, Cl2-GPAO oxidized more than half of the CH4 in the gas stream; Figure a shows a time series of gas concentrations at the reactor outlet. When the light was turned on, Cl2 and CH4 were removed while CO and CO2 were produced. Figure b shows the mass balance of carbon, whereby CH4 was converted entirely to CO and CO2. 62% (17.1 ppm) of the 30-ppm inlet CH4 was converted during the 3 min average residence time, consistent with Krogsbo̷ll et al., who observed 58% CH4 conversion for the same residence time. The measured product yields, 68% CO and 32% CO2, are close to those predicted by that study’s chemical kinetics model (61% and 39%, respectively). Although the proposed mechanism (see Note S5) predicts the formation of HCl and ClO as byproducts, neither gas was detected in the reactor effluent. Any produced HCl and ClO likely reacted with or adsorbed on plastic pipe fittings and metal analyzer components before they reached the FTIR analyzer’s optical cavity; future study of these acid gas byproducts should therefore be done on higher-flow systems where such effects are minimal.

These measurements, as well as the results from the corresponding radical-counting experiment (see Note S3), allow the computation of several figures of merit for the base case. First, the observed CH4 conversion and light characteristics give an AQY of 0.035% (eq ). For the small size of the photoreactor, a low AQY is expected. Indeed, larger Cl2-GPAO reactors have reported higher AQYs (up to 0.8% in a 30 L/min reactor, 1.5% in a 200 L/min reactor). , Second, based on the radical-counting experiment, the measured Cl· usage efficiency (ηCl· ) was 0.34 oxidized CH4 per photogenerated Cl·, corresponding to a 3:1 ratio of the reactor’s gross Cl· production to its net CH4 conversion. Due to its better transferability to larger reactors, hereafter, we report ηCl· rather than AQY for CH4 conversion experiments. The ratio of the reactor’s net Cl2 conversion to its gross Cl2 photolysis was about 4:5, indicating that about 20% of photogenerated Cl· recombined to Cl2 while the rest ended up in other products such as HCl or ClO.

The GC-MS analysis on the effluent of the base-case experiment measured 1.05 ± 0.32 ppb CH3Cl and 1.25 ± 0.38 ppb CHCl3. Given the 17.1 ppm CH4 converted in the base case, these are yields of 6 × 10–5 CH3Cl and 7 × 10–5 CHCl3 per CH4 oxidized. Dilution from injecting the samples into the instrument resulted in a sample detection limit of 0.8 ppb for both compounds. CH2Cl2 and CCl4 were not detected above their detection limits of 0.8 and 1.6 ppb, respectively. See Note S6 for a report of all compounds detected with the EPA TO-15 Method. The concentrations of all detected compounds were well within safe levels (Table S6.1). For example, CH3Cl and CHCl3 were several orders of magnitude below their 8-h workplace exposure limits of 100 and 50 ppm, respectively. Given the 20-year GWPs of 45 for CH3Cl and 60 for CHCl3, such low concentrations would negligibly affect Cl2-GPAO’s climate benefit. The low or undetected levels of chlorinated methanes despite the known production of halocarbons in chlorine-based aqueous advanced oxidation can be explained by GPAO’s abundance of O2 and absence of the humic substances and halide salts that drive halocarbon production in such applications as wastewater treatment. ,, Future work with an online GC-MS could further elucidate Cl2-GPAO’s byproduct yields.

Effects of Varying CH4, Cl2, Humidity, and Residence Times

As the inlet Cl2 concentration increased in successive experiments with all other parameters held constant, the observed CH4 conversion rose. Beyond a 1:1 inlet Cl2–CH4 ratio, there were diminishing returns of CH4 oxidized per Cl2 supplied (Figure a); correspondingly, the highest ηCl· was observed for inlet Cl2 concentrations of 10 to 30 ppm. The decrease of ηCl· at higher inlet Cl2 concentrations likely occurs because of the competitive reaction of Cl· recombining with itself to form Cl2. Since Cl· recombination is second-order with respect to Cl· concentration, while Cl·’s reactions with CH4 and C1 oxidation products are first-order in Cl· concentration, a greater fraction of Cl· undergoes recombination at elevated Cl· production rates. The consistent observation of Cl2 conversion being 1.2 times the CH4 conversion, regardless of the gross Cl· production, provides further evidence that most Cl· not participating in CH4 oxidation ultimately recombined to Cl2. An analogous phenomenon with ·OH–·OH recombination has been posited to limit the efficacy of hydroxyl radical-based GPAO on CH4. ,

The engineering implication is that increasing the Cl2 feed rate to a 1:1 ratio to CH4 seems to be a downside-free way to boost CH4 conversion, while beyond that ratio, additional conversion comes at the cost of reagent and/or energy efficiency. Applying a cost and emissions model to the efficiency results of this sweep showed that for 30 ppm inlet CH4, a Cl2/CH4 feed ratio up to 1:1 gave the lowest projected costs and emissions per unit of CH4 oxidized (Table S4.1). A 1:1 Cl2/CH4 feed ratio was therefore used for all subsequent experiments.

As the inlet ratio of Cl2 to CH4 increased, the balance of products shifted from CO to CO2 (Figures a and S3.3a). This behavior is consistent with the reaction pathways proposed by Krogsbo̷ll et al., the relevant parts of which are shown in Figure S5.1. Since neither 368 nm light nor Cl· is expected to activate H2O to OH· in substantial quantities, ·OH activity is an unlikely explanation for CO-to-CO2 conversion. , Rather, in the absence of ·OH, one or more pathways via ClCO can transform CO into CO2, consuming and rereleasing Cl· and/or Cl2.

The measured Cl2 conversion was consistently ∼1.2 times the CH4 conversion, regardless of the inlet Cl2 concentration or the Cl· production (Figure S3.2). Based on the proposed reaction mechanism (Figure S5.1), one might expect to observe a Cl2 conversion equal to 1.5 times the CH4 conversion, with Cl· activating CH4, CH3O2 ·, and CH2O and ending up as HCl, ClO, and HCl, respectively. Our observation of at least 3 consumed Cl· per oxidized CH4 (i.e., ηCl· ≤ 0.36) is consistent with Cl· initiating the above three reactions. The lower-than-expected Cl2 conversion could be from self-reactions of ClO to reproduce Cl2 or from other reaction paths proposed by Krogsbo̷ll et al. Further modeling work would help to clarify these phenomena.

Cl2-GPAO appears to work well at a variety of inlet CH4 concentrations and humidities. Varying the inlet CH4 and Cl2 in a 1:1 proportion to one another at concentrations above 10 ppm did not substantially change the percent CH4 conversion, product yields, or Cl· usage efficiency (Figure b). This result is unsurprising given that the main reactions involved are first-order with respect to CH4 and its downstream products. The reduced efficiency at very low CH4 concentrations may be due to Cl· scavenging by sub-ppm, reducing impurities in the gas tanks. A test varying the inlet gas’s moisture from 5 to 60% relative humidity (RH) found humidity to have a minimal effect on the product composition and ηCl· (Figure c).

We additionally found that varying the ultraviolet light intensity and the residence time of gas through the reactor did not greatly affect η Cl• for a fixed budget of photogenerated Cl· (see Note S7). Specifically, ηCl· varied by less than 15% among the experiments with 2-, 3-, and 8-min average residence times with corresponding light fluxes of 21, 15, and 5.2 mW/cm2, respectively (Figure S7.1). Thus, within the range studied, using higher light intensities and shorter residence times appears to be a drawback-free way to intensify the Cl2-GPAO process and improve its economics. However, since about 75% of Cl2 in the reactor already undergoes photolysis (see Note S3), simply increasing the light flux or slowing the residence time has limited potential to increase the gross Cl· production and CH4 conversion. Rather, if substantially higher CH4 conversions than the base case’s 62% are desired, increasing the Cl2-to-CH4 feed ratio is likely the best way to do so.

The 2 min experiment showed an apparent first-order rate constant (k) of 0.40 min–1, and the 3 min base case had k = 0.32 min–1. For comparison, the only other GPAO process that has been demonstrated on dilute CH4 (using 185 nm photolysis of O2 and H2O to form ·OH) achieves 50% conversion in residence times ranging from 10 s to 2 min (k = 0.35 to 4.1 min–1), while thermal catalytic CH4 oxidation requires under 30 s (k ≥ 1.4 min–1) and biofilters require more than 4 min (k ≤ 0.17 min–1). While pressure-drop limitations in the system prevented us from increasing the flow to achieve residence times below 2 min, lower residence times would be desirable in scaled-up systems, and probing the minimum practical residence time for Cl2-GPAO with higher light fluxes is a clear area for future work.

Effect of Contaminants

Increasing the amount of each contaminant gas caused the CH4 conversion to decrease, with toluene causing the sharpest decrease, followed by H2S, NO, and NH3 (Figure ). Although high levels of each contaminant can severely inhibit Cl2-GPAO, sufficiently low levels still allow practically relevant CH4 conversions. The contaminants’ inhibitory effects are likely the result of two mechanisms: first, contaminants scavenging Cl· that might otherwise have reacted with CH4, and second, contaminants reacting with Cl2 in the dark before it could photolyze.

Most of the observed inhibition is likely due to contaminants scavenging Cl·. The sizes of the inhibitory effects correlate somewhat with the contaminants’ reaction rates with Cl·. The second-order rates of Cl· with CH4, NH3, NO, toluene, and H2S are 1.0 × 10–13, 1.2 × 10–13, 2.4 × 10–12, 5.0 × 10–11, and 7.4 × 10–11 cm3/molecule s, respectively. Each contaminant has a higher rate with Cl· than does CH4. NH3 has the lowest rate with respect to Cl· and was observed to have the least effect on CH4 oxidation. Toluene, with a Cl· reaction rate 2 orders of magnitude higher than that with CH4 and NH3, had by far the largest inhibitory effect. Toluene’s larger inhibitory effect than H2S, despite its slightly lower rate, may be because the organic products of the toluene-Cl· reaction have many C–H bonds that can continue to scavenge Cl·. The continued oxidation of these products by Cl· is evidenced by the high CO2 production during the toluene trials (Figure b).

Dark reactions of Cl2 with contaminants may also have contributed to the observed inhibition. NH3, H2S, and NO are known to react with Cl2 in the dark to, respectively, produce ammonium chloride or chloramine, sulfur, and nitrosyl chloride. − However, only in the case of cofed NH3 was there evidence of Cl2 consumption in dark reactions, with 1 to 1.5 ppm Cl2 consumed in the dark per ppm of cofed NH3. It thus seems likely that toluene, NO, and H2S mainly inhibit Cl2-GPAO by competitively scavenging Cl·, while NH3 does so both by scavenging Cl· and Cl2.

Application-Based Experiments

Three application-based experiments simulating real-world deployment scenarios showed substantial CH4 conversion (Figure a). The barn air experiment exhibited modestly poorer performance than the base case, which can be attributed to the combined effects of several contaminants at once. The wastewater plant air’s much poorer performance is likely due in part to contaminants and in part to the effect of chlorine on fittings and reactor components at high inlet Cl2 concentrations (300 ppm, more than three times higher than any other experiment). Finally, the relatively low CH4 conversion in the atmospheric air trial, to which no contaminants were added, agrees with the trend in Figure b of decreasing CH4 conversion and ηCl· at inlet CH4 and Cl2 concentrations below 10 ppm.

Applying the cost model to the results of the base case and application-based experiments resulted in projected input (Cl2 and lighting) costs ranging from $4300/tCH4 for the base case to $8100/tCH4 for the WWTP scenario (Figure b). These values can be considered lower bounds on the system's overall CH4 mitigation costs. Using CH4’s 20-year GWP of 81, that range corresponds to $53 to $100 per equivalent ton of CO2 (tCO2e), while using a 100-year GWP of 28, it corresponds to $154 to $289/tCO2e. Critically, the above cost values are underestimates because they exclude capital costs such as fans, reactor framing, and wall materials, as well as acid-gas scrubbing costs. Accurately projecting the cost of CH4 mitigation via Cl2-GPAO would require a detailed cost model that accounts for those and other expenses. However, the modeled input costs alone are already higher than published social costs of CH4, raising concerns over the economics of the system as a whole. For example, in 2022, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency estimated the social cost of CH4 to be $1600/tCH4 in 2020, $2400/tCH4 in 2030, and $3300/tCH4 in 2040 (all in 2020 dollars). Some estimates have placed the social cost of CH4 to be higher than the EPA’s values.

Estimates of the specific energy required for a full-scale system ranged from 32000 kWh/tCH4 for the base case to 61000 kWh/tCH4 for the WWTP scenario (Table S4.2). These values are an order of magnitude less than the lowest specific energy reported in the literature for the first field-scale prototype of Cl2-GPAO, indicating the potential benefits of optically efficient reactors that minimize the waste of UV photons. On a per tCO2e basis, the values are comparable to the energy usage for the direct air capture of CO2.

However, such large minimum energy demands may be challenging from a lifecycle perspective (Note S4). The base-case Cl2-GPAO system, if powered by the current U.S. electrical grid, would incur 12 tCO2e of lifecycle emissions due to electrical demand alone for each ton of CH4 oxidized. On the contrary, if it were powered entirely by solar energy, only 1.4 tCO2e/tCH4 of electrical lifecycle emissions would be incurred. Table S4.2 contains the corresponding values for the other application-based experiments. Though these values do not preclude climate-beneficial operation of Cl2-GPAO, they suggest that lifecycle emissions should be carefully scrutinized for any proposed Cl2-GPAO deployment, as in some situations, a substantial fraction of the process’s climate benefit might be negated.

Implications

In summary, Cl2-GPAO was able to convert substantial fractions of dilute CH4 streams across a range of operating conditions. After testing a variety of inlet Cl2-to-CH4 ratios, a 1:1 ratio gave a high (62%) CH4 conversion while avoiding Cl· wastage, making that ratio a good starting point for future studies. We made, to our knowledge, the first measurement of Cl· use efficiencies in a Cl2-GPAO reactor, finding that the reactor had to produce a minimum of about 3 photogenerated Cl· for each CH4 that was oxidized. The process behaved similarly at a variety of humidities, residence times, and inlet CH4 concentrations (with equimolar inlet Cl2). Our results were consistent with the reaction mechanism proposed by Krogsbo̷ll et al. and indicated the importance of chlorine-mediated CO-to-CO2 conversion pathways via ClCO. The production of CH3Cl and CHCl3 in the base case was extremely small (∼1 ppb), suggesting that chlorinated methane production may not be a critical barrier to safe deployment.

Even with low production of chlorinated methanes, other byproducts of Cl2-GPAO must be carefully managed. Wet scrubbers will be necessary in any deployment scenario to remove HCl and residual Cl2 from the exiting gas stream. However, these scrubbers do not remove CO, which can constitute more than two-thirds of the CH4 oxidation products and is also toxic to humans and animals. Regulations on CO emissions vary, with occupational exposure limits in the tens of ppm of CO and industrial emission limits in the hundreds of ppm of CO. , In cases where the process’s outlet CO concentration must be reduced, the inlet Cl2-to-CH4 ratio could be increased (lowering the radical usage efficiency) or a downstream thermal or catalytic CO oxidizer could be installed (incurring additional cost).

The Cl2-GPAO process was fairly resilient to the gas-phase contaminants H2S, NH3, NO, and toluene. Low levels of the contaminants did not greatly affect CH4 conversions and Cl· usage efficiencies compared to the base case, although sufficient levels of each contaminant gas caused the CH4 conversion to drop by half or more. This inhibition was due to contaminants either scavenging Cl· or reacting with Cl2 before it could photolyze. We additionally conducted application-based experiments with gas mixtures representing three possible Cl2-GPAO deployment locationsthe open atmosphere, a dairy barn, and a wastewater treatment plantfinding that in each case, the percent CH4 conversion was substantial although lower than in the base case.

Finally, we projected the costs and process emissions of the main inputs to a deployed Cl2-GPAO process (Cl2 production and UV light) to be relatively high, despite excluding fans, reactor hardware, and HCl/Cl2 scrubbers. Using the outputs of the base case and application-based experiments, we estimated costs to range from $4300 to $8100 per ton of CH4 oxidized. These values already exceed the published social costs of methane. Furthermore, powering Cl2-GPAO with today’s grid electricity would produce emissions equivalent to more than half of the abated CH4’s 100-year GWP, though low-carbon electricity could mitigate that effect.

This study’s results do not reveal clear pathways toward substantially lower chlorine and light consumption, besides minimizing inlet contaminant levels. Given the costs associated with these inputs, commercializing Cl2-GPAO may be challenging. While additional, more detailed cost and lifecycle analyses may be informative, they are unlikely to decrease estimates of Cl2-GPAO deployment cost and lifecycle emissions. One useful area of future research would be to quantify and value Cl2-GPAO’s destruction of odorants and other contaminants, providing another value stream to subsidize CH4 destruction. Any process improvements that reduce the Cl· demand to well below 3 Cl· per CH4 oxidized would also be highly valuable. Given the scarcity of chemical processes that might cost-effectively oxidize low-concentration CH4, moving Cl2-GPAO closer to real-world practicality remains a worthy goal.
