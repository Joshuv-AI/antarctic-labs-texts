Connecting with other parents matters: potential buffering role of parents’ group participation in the association between caregiving duration and mental health among 1,358 korean mothers caring for children with developmental disabilities
Authors: Ja Young Kim, Ji-Hwan Kim, Eun-Ji Paeng, Seung-Sup Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is well-known that mothers caring for children with developmental disabilities (DD) commonly suffer from social isolation. Connecting with other parents may play a critical role in the lives of mothers caring for children with DD.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey of 1,358 mothers caring for children with DD who are under 19 in 2024. Caregiving duration was defined as years since diagnosis and categorized into five groups: 0–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, and 13–18 years. Depressive symptoms and burnout were measured by CES-D 11 and Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, respectively. Parents’ group participation was defined as attending disability-related parents’ group activities at least once a month. The association between caregiving duration and mental health was examined, stratified by group participation.ResultsThe association differed by whether mothers participated in parents' groups. Among participants, compared to the 0–3 years group, the prevalence ratios for depressive symptoms were 0.81 (95% CI: 0.68–0.97) for 4–6 years, 0.77 (95% CI: 0.61–0.97) for 7–9 years, 0.77 (95% CI: 0.60–1.00) for 10–12 years, and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.56–0.97) for 13–18 years. However, no association was observed among non-participants. Similar differential patterns were found in the analysis of burnout.Conclusions Participation in parents’ group may play a potential buffering role in the association between caregiving duration and mothers' mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27588-7.

Background

Developmental disabilities (DD) are legally defined in South Korea as including both intellectual disabilities and autism spectrum disorder under the Act on Guarantee of Rights of and Support for Persons with Developmental Disabilities [1]. In 2024, people with disabilities accounted for 5.16% of the total Korean population, of whom individuals with DD comprised 10.7%, corresponding to 0.55% of the overall population [2]. Over the past decade (2015–2024), the population of individuals with DD increased, with a larger increase among children aged 0–9 years (86%, from 13,370 to 24,886) than among those aged 10–19 years (16%, from 44,695 to 51,953).

Because individuals with DD typically experience persistent difficulties in communication, social interaction, and independent daily living, they often require continuous support and supervision across the life course, extending beyond early childhood [3–5]. In Korea, caregiving responsibilities for children with DD are primarily assumed by parents, but within families, these responsibilities are most often undertaken by mothers, who commonly serve as the primary caregivers. As a result, mothers are responsible for a broad range of caregiving tasks, including providing assistance with daily activities, coordinating medical and educational services, and managing behavioral and emotional challenges associated with the child’s condition. Over time, these responsibilities become embedded in their daily routines and roles, shaping everyday life cumulatively [6, 7].

The lifelong caregiving burden experienced by mothers caring for children with DD has been consistently associated with poorer mental health including depression and burnout. A substantial body of evidence indicates that mothers of children with DD experience elevated levels of psychological distress compared with parents of children without disabilities. For example, a systematic review of 19 studies reported higher levels of depression among parents of children with DD, the majority of whom were mothers [8]. Several other studies have also shown that, compared with mothers of typically developing children, mothers of children with DD exhibit higher levels of depressive symptoms as well as a greater prevalence of clinically diagnosed depression [9, 10]. In addition, caregiver burnout has been reported at elevated levels among mothers of children with special needs, including DD, and has been conceptualized as a response to chronic and long-lasting parenting stress [11].

Caregiving duration is one of important components of caregiving burden, as it reflects the time-related dimension of the caregiving experience. However, only a few studies have examined caregiving duration in relation to health outcomes. Santos et al. reported that longer caregiving duration was significantly associated with higher levels of caregiving burden among parents of adults with DD [12]. In contrast, another study found no association between caregiving duration and poorer health outcomes among caregivers of children with DD, suggesting that caregiving burden may emerge early and remain relatively stable over time [13]. Taken together, the limited existing evidence suggests that caregiving duration captures an important temporal aspect of caregiving, while its relationship with mental health outcomes among mothers caring for children with DD remains insufficiently understood.

Peer support among parents of children with DD may be particularly valuable, given the social stigmatization and isolation they often experience. A systematic review of qualitative studies indicates that mothers of children with DD frequently experience felt stigma, characterized by heightened sensitivity to negative judgments, avoidance, or misunderstanding in routine social interactions. In this context, peer connections with others who share similar caregiving experiences may offer a unique source of understanding and support. Previous research suggests that parent-to-parent connections are associated with better mental health among parents of children with DD [14–17]. Qualitative evidence further indicates that peer support helps meet the complex support needs of parents of children with autism spectrum disorder and other DD [18]. Within such peer relationships, parents report reduced feelings of social isolation, increased emotional validation, and access to practical knowledge that supports coping in daily caregiving. In addition, a quantitative systematic review of studies involving parents of children with chronic disabling conditions has reported associations between peer support interventions and reductions in parental stress and psychological distress, including among parents of children with DD [17]. In practice, such peer connections are often formed through organized parent networks, which can serve as one of ways to establish and maintain peer connections among parents.

Despite these benefits, no previous studies have examined the role of parents’ group participation in the relationship between caregiving duration and mental health outcomes among mothers caring for children with DD. Therefore, this study aimed to address following research questions:

Methods

Dataset & study populations

We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional survey as the baseline of a cohort study among parents caring for children with DD in Korea. This survey was conducted as part of the Disability, Social Environment, and Premature Aging (DiSEPA) study, which examines the social environment of people with disabilities and its impact on health.

The survey data were collected through an online survey in December 2024. Participants were recruited through parent networks of children with DD, including the Korean Parents’ Network for People with Disabilities and their affiliated online communities. Participants were eligible if they were primary caregivers of children with DD in Korea. Informed consent for voluntary participation and academic use of data was obtained electronically prior to survey administration. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Seoul National University (IRB No. 2409/002–006) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Of 2,180 participants, after excluding fathers (N = 277), participants whose children were aged 19 years or older (N = 461), and those with missing data in key covariates (N = 84), a total of 1,358 mothers caring for children with DD were included in the final analysis.

Measures

Caregiving duration

Caregiving duration was defined as the number of years since the child's diagnosis of DD and was calculated by subtracting the child’s age at diagnosis from their current age. This reflects the time-related dimension of the caregiving experience and may be associated with caregiving burden. It was categorized into five groups: 0–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, and 13–18 years.

Mental health outcomes

Depressive symptoms over the preceding week were assessed using the 11-item version of the Centers for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (hereafter, CES-D 11). Participants could answer from 0 to 3 (0: rarely- less than 1 day, 1: sometimes- 1 ~ 2 days, 2: often- 3 ~ 4 days, 3: always- 5 ~ 7 days) for each question, and the summed scores of eleven questions ranged from 0 to 33. The standard CES-D of 20 questions uses a cut-off score of 16 points for depressive symptoms, and, accordingly, in CES-D 11, a score of 9 or greater was defined as indicating depressive symptoms [19, 20].

Burnout was assessed using the personal burnout domain of Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, which reflects overall physical and emotional exhaustion. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale and transformed to a standardized score ranging from 0 to 100, with response options of “always” scored as 100, “often” as 75, “sometimes” as 50, “seldom” as 25, and “never” as 0. The personal burnout score was calculated as the mean of the six corresponding items, with higher scores indicating greater level of burnout. High burnout was defined as a mean score of 50 or higher [21, 22].

Parents’ group participation

Parents’ group participation was assessed by asking whether mothers caring for children with DD participated in disability-related parents' group activities at least once a month, which provide opportunities for advocacy and information sharing related to disability rights and services [23]. Responses were categorized as “yes” (participants) or “no” (non-participants).

Covariates

In this study, we included key maternal sociodemographic characteristics, as well as child-related characteristics based on prior literature on caregiving and parental mental health. Age was categorized into three groups (28–39, 40–49, and 50 years old or more). Marital status was classified as married and living with spouse or not living with spouse (including single, divorced, widowed, and separated). Educational attainment was grouped into high school graduate or less and college graduate or more. Monthly household income was categorized into four groups (< 3,000,000; 3,000,000–4,999,999; 5,000,000–6,999,999; and ≥ 7,000,000 KRW).

Child-related covariates included the child’s gender (male or female), age group (0–9 years, and 10–18 years), presence of multiple disabilities other than DD (yes/no), the level of need for daily living assistance (low or high), and the child’s registration status for DD (registered vs. not registered under the national disability registration system, which requires medical evaluation to determine eligibility). These variables were included to account for variations in caregiving demands and disability severity, which are known to be associated with caregivers’ mental health.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of the study population across key covariates was described, and the prevalence of both depressive symptoms and burnout was compared across covariates using chi-square tests.

A modified Poisson regression model with robust variance was applied to investigate the association between caregiving duration and depressive symptoms and burnout among mothers caring for children with DD. Odds ratios from logistic regression would overestimate prevalence ratios when outcome variables were common in the study population (> 10%) [24]. Furthermore, the association was examined using stratified analyses by experience of parents’ group participation. Predicted prevalence of depressive symptoms and burnout was estimated using the post-estimation predict command, and these estimates were plotted to illustrate their pattern across caregiving duration since diagnosis. All covariates were included as categorical variables in the analyses. The results were expressed as prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using STATA/SE version 17.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Most participants were married and living with their spouse (91.5%) and were college graduate or higher (84.8%). Regarding characteristics of children, over two-thirds of children were male (68.3%). The prevalence of both depressive symptoms and burnout was higher among older mothers, those not living with a spouse, and those with lower monthly household income. With respect to child characteristics, higher prevalence of depressive symptoms and burnout was observed among mothers of older or male children, children with multiple disabilities other than DD, higher levels of need for daily living assistance, and those whose children were officially registered with DD under the national disability registration system.

Figure 1 presents the adjusted predicted prevalence of (A) depressive symptoms and (B) burnout by caregiving duration, stratified by parents’ group participation among mothers caring for children with DD. After adjustment for key covariates of mothers and their children, the predicted prevalence of depressive symptoms among mothers who did not participate in parents’ group remained relatively stable as caregiving duration increased, and was generally higher than that among mothers who participated in parents’ group from 4–6 to 13–18 years of caregiving duration. In contrast, among mothers participating in parents’ group, the predicted prevalence of depressive symptoms was highest in the 0–3-year caregiving duration category and showed a decreasing pattern as caregiving duration increased. Burnout also showed different patterns by parents’ group participation. The predicted prevalence of burnout slightly increased with longer caregiving duration among mothers who did not participate in parents’ groups, whereas it decreased among mothers who did.

After stratification, the associations differed by parents’ group participation. Among mothers who did not participate in parents’ group, caregiving duration was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms after full adjustment in Model II, although a modest increase was observed in Model I. In contrast, among mothers who participated in parents’ group, the adjusted PR for depressive symptoms were lower at longer caregiving duration. After full adjustment (Model II), the PRs were 0.81 (95% CI: 0.68–0.97) for 4–6 years, 0.77 (95% CI: 0.61–0.97) for 7–9 years, 0.77 (95% CI: 0.60–1.00) for 10–12 years, and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.56–0.97) for 13–18 years, compared with the 0–3-year group.

Table 3 presents the association between caregiving duration and personal burnout. When stratified by parents’ group participation, longer caregiving duration among mothers who did not participate in parents’ group was associated with higher burnout prevalence in Model I; however, these associations were attenuated and no longer statistically significant after full adjustment in Model II. Among mothers who participated in parents’ group, longer caregiving duration was associated with lower PRs of burnout in Model II, showing a pattern similar to that observed for depressive symptoms.

Discussions

This study is, to our knowledge, the first to examine whether the association between caregiving duration and mental health outcomes, including depressive symptoms and burnout, differ by parents' group participation among mothers caring for children with DD in Korea. Our findings suggest that parents’ group participation may play a potential buffering role in the association between longer caregiving duration and adverse mental health outcomes. When stratified by participation status, mothers who did not participate in parents’ group showed a similar prevalence ratio of depressive symptoms across caregiving duration. In contrast, participants exhibited a significant decline in depressive symptoms as caregiving duration increased. A similar pattern was observed for burnout. Among participants, the prevalence of burnout decreased with longer caregiving duration, whereas it slightly increased among non-participants, although this association was not statistically significant.

Considering these findings, parents’ group participation may be understood as a form of peer-based self-help and social support among parents facing similar caregiving situations. First, parents’ groups provide opportunities for information and resource sharing, including practical knowledge about treatment options, service selection, and daily caregiving strategies, which may reduce perceived caregiving burden [25]. Second, emotional support from other parents facing similar challenges can foster a sense of understanding, and belonging, thereby alleviating emotional distress and social isolation [18]. Furthermore, parents’ groups may serve as networks for collective problem-solving and mutual empowerment, enabling parents to address practical caregiving challenges more effectively [26]. Through these mechanisms, parents’ group participation may mitigate the psychological strain associated with prolonged caregiving demands.

Interestingly, we found that, within the shortest caregiving duration (the 0–3 years group), mothers who participated in parents’ groups exhibited substantially higher levels of depressive symptoms and burnout than non-participating mothers. One possible explanation for these findings is that mothers of children with more severe disabilities may be more likely to seek out and participate in parents’ groups due to greater caregiving demands and increased needs for information, emotional support, and social resources. This interpretation is supported by the distribution of participant characteristics in our study population, which indicates that mothers participating in parents’ groups were more likely to have children formally registered with DD across caregiving duration groups, with the largest difference observed in the 0–3 years group (Supplementary Table 1). In Korea, official registration of DD requires formal assessment of functional limitations and support needs and is therefore often interpreted as an indicator of greater disability severity and caregiving demands. Accordingly, mothers participating in parents’ groups may represent a subgroup with higher baseline caregiving burden, particularly at the early stage of caregiving.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, because this study is based on cross-sectional data, differences in mental health outcome across caregiving duration groups represent between-group differences rather than within-person changes over time. Although caregiving duration was categorized based on the time since the diagnosis of DD, these results should not be interpreted as reflecting caregiving trajectories. Second, caregiving duration was assessed based on a single child, focusing on the child with the more severe condition when parents had more than one child with DD. This approach may not fully capture the overall caregiving experience at the household level. Third, the study sample may not be fully representative of all mothers of children with DD in Korea, as mothers with more severe depressive symptoms or poorer health may have found it difficult to participate in the survey. Fourth, the interpretation regarding the role of parents’ group participation may be influenced by differential discontinuation depending on mental health status. Specifically, we cannot rule out the possibility that mothers with poorer mental health were more likely to discontinue participation over time, potentially contributing to differences between groups. Lastly, information on the duration of parents’ group participation was not available, and thus recent and long-term participants could not be distinguished. This may complicate the interpretation of the findings, as the effects of participation may differ depending on its duration. Nevertheless, the planned longitudinal follow-up, scheduled to begin three years after the baseline survey, provides an important opportunity to examine within-person changes over time and to better understand the temporal dynamics of caregiving burden and mental health outcomes.

These findings may highlight the potential importance of strengthening parent-to-parent connections among caregivers of children with DD. Efforts to reduce barriers to peer support among parents in similar situations and to facilitate diverse forms of connection, including informal networks and community-based support groups, may be beneficial for parents who are socially isolated or face challenges.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that parents’ group participation may play a potential buffering role in the association between caregiving duration and mental health outcomes among mothers caring for children with DD in Korea. Although mothers who participated in parents’ groups exhibited higher levels of depressive symptoms and burnout at the early stage of caregiving—likely reflecting greater baseline caregiving burden—their mental health outcomes showed a declining pattern with longer caregiving duration. In contrast, non-participating mothers did not show comparable change over time. Taken together, these findings suggest that caregiving is a process shaped not only by accumulated caregiving duration but also by differential access to peer-based social support.
