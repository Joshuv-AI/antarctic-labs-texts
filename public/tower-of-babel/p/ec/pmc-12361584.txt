CENcyclopedia: dynamic landscape of kinetochore architecture throughout the cell cycle
Authors: Yu-Chia Chen, Ece Kilic, Evelyn Wang, Will Rossman, Aussie Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe kinetochore, an intricate macromolecular protein complex located on chromosomes, plays a pivotal role in orchestrating chromosome segregation. It functions as a versatile platform for microtubule assembly, diligently monitors microtubule binding fidelity, and acts as a force coupler. Comprising over 100 distinct proteins, many of which exist in multiple copies, the kinetochore’s composition dynamically changes throughout the cell cycle, responding to specific timing and conditions. This dynamicity is important for establishing functional kinetochores, yet the regulatory mechanisms of these dynamics have largely remained elusive. In this study, we employed advanced quantitative immunofluorescence techniques to meticulously chart the dynamics of kinetochore protein levels across the cell cycle. These findings offer a comprehensive view of the dynamic landscape of kinetochore architecture, shedding light on the detailed mechanisms of microtubule interaction and the nuanced characteristics of kinetochore proteins. This study significantly advances our understanding of the molecular coordination underlying chromosome segregation.Subject terms: Kinetochores, Mitosis

Introduction

Chromosome segregation ensures the equal distribution of replicated genomes into daughter cells. Errors in this process can lead to aneuploidy, an abnormal number of chromosomes, and a hallmark of cancer. The kinetochore is a multiprotein complex that assembles on the centromeric chromatin and orchestrates chromosome segregation. Kinetochores not only serve as a structural platform for microtubule assembly but also ensure faithful chromosome segregation by actively monitoring kinetochore-microtubule interactions1.

The kinetochore architecture can be divided into three regions: inner kinetochore, outer kinetochore, and corona2–4. Each region has a unique composition of proteins. The inner kinetochore consists of constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN) proteins, 16 different subunits that directly assemble on the centromeric chromatin and serve as the foundation of kinetochore assembly5,6. The outer kinetochore contains the highly conserved KMN network, which includes the Knl1 complex (Knl1C), the Mis12 complex (Mis12C), and the Ndc80 complex (Ndc80C)7–9. Ndc80C is the primary microtubule-binding site at kinetochores, while Knl1C serves as a major platform for the assembly of spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) proteins to monitor attachment errors. The corona is the outermost layer of kinetochores, where SAC-related and microtubule-associated proteins reside10. The primary function of corona proteins is to form a higher-order assembly that facilitates the capture of chromosomes by spindle microtubules11.

Recent efforts have led to the identification of over 100 different kinetochore-related proteins, which are dynamically regulated throughout the cell cycle to form functional kinetochores12. While some proteins are constitutively assembled at centromeres, others are recruited to kinetochores at specific cell cycle stages13. Previous studies on kinetochore protein dynamics have been often focused on a small subset of proteins or their turnover at kinetochores14–18, failing to fully capture their abundance and the regulation of their recruitment across the entire cell cycle. This limitation hinders the comprehensive understanding of the kinetochore’s dynamic landscape. In this study, we utilized quantitative immunofluorescence (qIF) microscopy to profile the dynamics of 32 kinetochore proteins and 5 mitotic kinase substrates in asynchronous non-transformed RPE1 cells. Our approach provides a holistic view of the kinetochore architecture across all cell cycle phases, including historically less-explored G1, S, and G2 phases. We reveal that CCAN proteins are promptly recruited to kinetochores during S phase when centromeres are replicated. Unexpectedly, we observe that Mis12C localizes to kinetochores as early as in the middle/late G1 phase through CENP-C in an Aurora-B-independent manner, while the recruitment of Knl1C and Ndc80C initiates in late S phase. Additionally, the assembly of SAC-related proteins, motor proteins, and kinases at kinetochores follows a multi-step process, characterized by a subtle yet critical time lag in their association and dissociation. This comprehensive analysis of kinetochore protein dynamics provides new insights into the structural and functional organization of kinetochores, contributing to a more complete understanding of the mechanisms that ensure faithful chromosome segregation.

Results

Strategies for quantifying kinetochore protein abundance across the cell cycle

To quantitatively define kinetochore protein dynamics throughout the cell cycle, we divided the cell cycle into the following stages: G1, S, G2 (late G2), and the sub-stages of M phase. For precise measurement of protein levels at kinetochores in each cell cycle stage, we employed our recently developed immunofluorescence-based method for cell cycle stage identification19 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, and Methods). Specifically, G1 phase cells are defined by the absence of CENP-F signals in the nucleus. S phase cells display distinct nuclear PCNA puncta, CENP-F signals, and brighter CENP-C signals. G2 phase cells exhibit uniform PCNA nuclear staining like G1, along with elevated nuclear CENP-F signals. Replicated sister kinetochore pairs remain within diffraction-limited distances until late G2 phase. In most of our qIF analyses, the G1 and S phases were combined into a single category (termed “G1/S”), representing a random mixture of cells in either G1 or S phase. This categorization was used because most non-CCAN kinetochore proteins are absent from kinetochores during these stages. We also defined G2 phase cells as those containing more than 20 pairs of sister kinetochores. Notably, “G1/S” cells generally lack paired kinetochores (see Methods for details on cell cycle stage identification). Sub-stages of M phase were identified based on their distinct DNA morphologies (Fig. 1a).

As highlighted in this study, the architecture and composition of kinetochores undergo dynamic remodeling throughout cell cycle progression, posing significant challenges to precisely define their temporal organization. Here, we broadly define “the kinetochore” as the assembly of kinetochore architectures observed during mitosis, despite their highly dynamic structural and functional changes from prophase to telophase. In contrast, we refer to its interphase forms from G1 to G2 phase as “pre-kinetochores”, denoting structures that are premature or precede the formation of mitotic kinetochores3,20–22. Notably, many key components of the mitotic kinetochore begin to localize to pre-kinetochores during G2 phase. Moreover, during prophase, kinetochores remain spatially isolated from the cytoplasmic microtubule network due to the intact nuclear envelope. Therefore, our definition of these two terms is not solely based on the presence of specific kinetochore proteins or their accessibility to microtubules, but instead reflects a temporally and functionally defined continuum of kinetochore states throughout the cell cycle.

To prevent artificial dissociation of kinetochore proteins, we utilized either paraformaldehyde (PFA) or methanol fixation without permeabilization prior to or during fixation, for all qIF experiments, except for Bub3 and Ska3 staining (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). For these exceptions, pre-extraction fixation was applied. Detailed information about antibodies and their corresponding fixation methods is listed in Supplementary Data 1–3. Signal intensities for target proteins at individual kinetochores were measured using a local background correction method that we previously developed (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f)23,24. A step-by-step protocol for signal quantification, including an analysis of the impact of bounding box sizes, is provided in Supplementary Figs. 2–4, with a detailed discussion in the Methods section. In the case where target proteins were absent from kinetochores, CENP-C foci served as a reference at the corresponding positions. Notably, we achieved a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio exceeding 10 for most antibodies (27 out of 37) at their peak levels (Supplementary Fig. 1d), enabling highly sensitive and accurate quantifications.

Dynamics of CENP-A and CENP-B

CENP-A, a centromere-specific histone H3 variant, serves as an essential epigenetic marker for kinetochore assembly25–27. Unlike canonical histones, CENP-A is loaded onto centromeres exclusively during early G1 phase28,29. Consequently, the pre-existing CENP-A nucleosomes are evenly distributed between the original and newly synthesized centromeric chromatin during S phase. In support of this, our qIF analysis demonstrated that CENP-A levels at kinetochores peaked during G1/S phase and decreased by approximately half in G2 phase, remaining constant until telophase (Fig. 1b). These results underscore the robustness of our qIF technique, allowing for precise measurement of the dynamics of kinetochore proteins throughout the cell cycle.

CENP-B binds a 17-bp CENP-B box sequence in centromeric repetitive DNA via its N-terminal domain (aa 1–125)30,31. It contributes to the maintenance of CENP-C levels at centromeres and promotes de novo centromere formation32,33. Our findings showed that CENP-B levels increased from G1/S phase to G2 phase (G1/S: 0.37; G2: 0.49), likely due to the synthesis of new centromeric DNA (Fig. 1c). Note that CENP-B signal levels from G2 phase to metaphase represent the combination of two pools of CENP-B proteins residing at a pair of sister kinetochores. We also observed a further increase in CENP-B levels during mitotic progression with a peak at metaphase (Fig. 1c). This could be due to the stability of CENP-B at centromeres. While CENP-B exhibits a highly dynamic exchange rate during G1/S phase, it binds stably to centromeres after G2 phase14.

Dynamics of the CCAN

The CCAN provides a structural platform for outer kinetochore assembly, and thereby serves as a bridging module between centromeric chromatin and spindle microtubules34. CCAN exhibits high structural flexibility, which is critical for controlling SAC activity and the binding affinity between kinetochores and microtubules35,36. Comprising 16 subunits, CCAN is composed of at least five groups: CENP-C, CENP-T-W-S-X, CENP-N-L, CENP-H-I-K-M, and CENP-O-P-Q-U-R. All CCAN proteins are constitutively present at centromeres throughout the cell cycle, though their kinetic profiles vary37,38. To comprehensively analyze the key components of the CCAN complex, we determined the protein dynamics of CENP-C, CENP-N, CENP-I, CENP-K, and CENP-T throughout the cell cycle by qIF (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5).

CENP-C directly interacts with CENP-A nucleosomes through its central domain (aa 426-537)39 and CENP-C motif (aa 736-758)40, enabling one CENP-C molecule to bind to two adjacent CENP-A nucleosomes41. While the N-terminal PEST-rich domain (aa 189-400) allows for the interaction with the CENP-HIKM and CENP-NL subcomplexes42,43, the C-terminal Cupin domain (aa 773-943) facilitates CENP-C dimerization and its recruitment to kinetochores41. We found that CENP-C levels significantly increased from interphase to prometaphase (G1/S: 0.67; PM: 1.00), followed by a gradual decline during mitotic progression (M: 0.87; T: 0.28) (Fig. 2a). A similar kinetic pattern was reported using GFP-fused CENP-C along with live-cell imaging in HeLa cells38. The variation of CENP-C levels during mitosis cannot be attributed to the abundance of its upstream adaptor, CENP-A, as CENP-A levels remained constant throughout mitosis (Fig. 1b). This variation may instead be influenced by the phosphorylation status of CENP-C by Cdk1, as phosphorylation of CENP-C by Cdk1 has been shown to increase CENP-C’s binding affinity to CENP-A in chicken DT40 cells40,44.

CENP-N and CENP-L form a heterodimer through their C-terminal domains43. The N-terminal domain of CENP-N (aa 1–286) is responsible for its binding to CENP-A nucleosomes43,45–47, while the C-terminus of CENP-N (aa 287–339) associates with CENP-C and the CENP-HIKM subcomplexes, contributing to their kinetochore localization48. We found that CENP-N levels peaked during the G1 and S phases. Subsequently, as the cell cycle progressed through mitosis, CENP-N levels exhibited a gradual, albeit slight decrease (G1/S: 1.00; G2: 0.80; P: 0.61; PM: 0.57; M: 0.49; A: 0.47; T: 0.45) (Fig. 2a). The CENP-HIKM subcomplex binds CENP-C, CENP-NL, and CENP-TWSX in vitro48,49, and these interactions are necessary for its kinetochore localization43,48. In line with their reported dependency on kinetochore localization, the kinetics of both CENP-I and CENP-K closely mirrored that of CENP-N (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5), displaying a gradual yet steady decline from interphase through the end of mitosis (G1/S: 1.00; T: 0.58 or 0.55) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5). CENP-N preferentially binds to open CENP-A chromatin rather than to compacted chromatin in vitro50. This property may help explain the observed reduction of CENP-N levels at centromeres during mitotic progression. Given the tight interdependence between CENP-NL and CENP-HIKM subcomplexes in kinetochore localization6,48,51–53, the loss of CENP-N could, in turn, contribute to the diminished presence of CENP-I and CENP-K during mitosis (Fig. 2a).

The CENP-TWSX heterotetramer is formed by the association of a CENP-TW dimer and a CENP-SX dimer54. Although CENP-TW and CENP-SX complexes can independently and directly bind to DNA in vitro, the CENP-TW complex is required for CENP-SX kinetochore localization in cells54. Our qIF showed that CENP-T levels peaked in metaphase (P: 0.57; PM: 0.70; M: 1.00; A: 0.67; T: 0.47) (Fig. 2a). Prior biochemical studies have demonstrated that the CENP-TWSX complex interacts exclusively with the CENP-HIKM subcomplex, but not with CENP-A or other CCAN proteins48,49,55,56. Given that levels of CENP-I and CENP-K do not increase from prophase to metaphase (Fig. 2a), the recruitment of additional CENP-T molecules in early mitosis does not primarily depend on the CENP-HIKM subcomplex. The CENP-T homolog in budding yeast, Cnn1, is recruited to kinetochores during mitosis through Cdk1-mediated phosphorylation57, suggesting that a similar conserved mechanism may exist in human cells for recruiting additional CENP-T during early mitosis.

We noticed there was a high variance of CCAN signal intensity during G1/S phases compared to other stages of the cell cycle. We hypothesized that, unlike CENP-A, CCAN protein levels varied between G1 and S phases because additional CCAN proteins were immediately assembled on the newly synthesized centromeres during S phase50,58,59. To test this hypothesis, we performed qIF for CENP-N, CENP-I, CENP-K, CENP-T, and CENP-C, distinguishing between G1, early S, late S, and G2 phase cells using the method described in Fig. 1a. Our findings revealed that the levels of all examined CCAN proteins gradually increased from G1 to early S phase and peaked during late S phase (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 6). These signals subsequently decreased in G2 phase, coinciding with sister kinetochore separation. The average signal intensity in late S phase increased more than twofold, likely due to kinetochore clustering, in which ~10 kinetochores cluster together during S phase in RPE1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Notably, the levels of CENP-N, CENP-I, CENP-K, and CENP-T in G2 phase returned to values similar to those observed in G1 phase, suggesting that newly synthesized pre-kinetochores recruit comparable copy numbers of these proteins during G1 phase, without substantial additional recruitment in S or G2 phase. In contrast, CENP-C levels in G2 phase were significantly higher than in G1 phase, although they did not increase beyond the expected amount associated with new pre-kinetochore formation during S phase (G1: 1.00; early S: 1.92; late S: 1.99; G2: 1.41) (Supplementary Fig. 6c). This finding suggests that, unlike other CCAN components, CENP-C may undergo selective and additional recruitment to pre-kinetochores during G2 phase, potentially reflecting a distinct regulatory mechanism. In conclusion, CCAN proteins are promptly recruited to newly synthesized pre-kinetochores in nearly equal amounts as the original pre-kinetochores during S phase.

Dynamics of the KMN network

The KMN network has been believed to be recruited to kinetochores during mitosis60,61. Previous studies have demonstrated that the KMN network is assembled at kinetochores through two distinct pathways: the CENP-C and CENP-T pathways62,63. CENP-C recruits an entire KMN network by directly binding to Mis12C through its N-terminus in an Aurora B (AurB)-dependent manner62,64–66. On the other hand, CENP-T can directly recruit up to two Ndc80C and one entire KMN network through Cdk1 phosphorylation23,67.

Mis12C, also known as the MIND complex in budding yeast, consists of four protein subunits: Mis12, Dsn1, Pmf1, and Nsl165,68. X-ray crystallography has revealed that Mis12C is formed by the intertwined C-terminal segments of the four proteins in a 1:1:1:1 stoichiometry65. Mis12C is responsible for the linkage between Knl1C and Ndc80C to CENP-C and CENP-T41,62,65. Nsl1 mediates the interaction with Knl18,9,69,70, and both Dsn1 and Nsl1 are involved in binding to the Spc24-Spc25 subunits of Ndc80C8,9,69. Our qIF analysis demonstrated that all four Mis12C subunits exhibited similar temporal dynamics (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 7a), indicating that the heterotetrameric Mis12C maintained consistent stoichiometry throughout the cell cycle. Unexpectedly, we found that all Mis12C subunits were consistently detected at pre-kinetochores in the majority of G1 and all S phase cells (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6d). Mis12C levels increased and reached a peak during prophase, gradually declined as mitosis progressed, and culminated in mitotic exit. The pronounced increase in Mis12C levels during mitotic entry is attributed to the enhanced kinase activity of AurB and Cdk1, which promotes Mis12C installation on CENP-C and CENP-T, respectively41,62,64. Likewise, the reduction of Mis12C levels at kinetochores during the late stages of mitosis is likely due to the reduced kinase activity of Cdk1 and AurB71–73.

Knl1C is formed by Knl1 and Zwint1 via interactions between Zwint1 and the C-terminal Knl1 (aa 2010-2134)9,74,75. Knl1 is essential for the recruitment of Zwint1 to kinetochores, and Zwint1 also partially contributes to the kinetochore localization of Knl18,76. The C-terminus of Knl1 features two RWD domains: RWDN (aa 2109-2209) and RWDC (aa 2210-2311)70. Knl1 RWDN binds to Nsl1, whereas RWDC interacts with both Dsn1 and Pmf18,9,69,70. Our qIF data revealed that Knl1 and Zwint1 first appeared at pre-kinetochores in a subset of late S phase cells, although both proteins were not uniformly present across all pre-kinetochores (Supplementary Fig. 8). Their signal levels then significantly increased in G2 phase, reaching a peak in prophase (G2: 0.35; P: 1.00 and G2: 0.34; P: 1.00, respectively) (Fig. 3). Following NEBD, levels of both Knl1 and Zwint1 gradually decreased, becoming nearly undetectable by telophase (PM: 0.77; M: 0.54; A: 0.42; T: 0.06 and PM: 0.85; M: 0.61; A: 0.38; T: 0.06, respectively). Notably, the kinetic profile of Knl1C mirrored that of its upstream recruiter, Mis12C (Fig. 3). However, Knl1C was completely undetectable at pre-kinetochores from G1 and through middle-to-late S phases, even in the presence of Mis12C. These findings suggest that Knl1C can enter nucleus during interphase, but additional post-translational modifications (PTMs) may be essential for Knl1’s binding to Mis12C.

Ndc80C consists of four proteins: Hec1 (Ndc80), Nuf2, Spc24, and Spc2577,78. Electron microscopy (EM) images have revealed that Ndc80C forms a long rod with a globular head at each end79. The N-terminal domains of Hec1 directly binds to microtubules78,80, whereas the C-terminus of Spc24-Spc25 directly interacts with either C-terminal Mis12C or N-terminal CENP-T8,9,69. To understand the temporal dynamics of Ndc80C, we chose Hec1 and Spc25 as representative proteins for our qIF assay. In line with Knl1C, Ndc80C was initially detected at a small subset of pre-kinetochores in late S phase (Supplementary Fig. 8). Their abundance significantly increased during G2 and prophase, reaching peak levels in prometaphase (Hec1: G2: 0.11; P: 0.36; PM: 1.00 and Spc25: G2: 0.10; P: 0.37; PM: 1.00) (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Subsequently, their signals diminished as mitosis progressed, becoming nearly undetectable by telophase (M: 0.66; A: 0. 49; T: 0.06 and M: 0.70; A: 0.50; T: 0.05, respectively). Like Knl1C, Ndc80C can also translocate into the interphase nucleus, and PTMs are likely essential for its localization to Mis12C at pre-kinetochores. The increase of Ndc80C levels from G2 phase to prometaphase coincides with the increased levels of its upstream recruiters (i.e., Mis12C and CENP-T) and increased Cdk1 activity81, which facilitates Ndc80C recruitment in a phosphorylation-dependent manner. In conclusion, our qIF results underscore that the KMN network assembles at kinetochores through a multi-step process. Mis12C is recruited to pre-kinetochores during G1 phase, followed by the recruitment of Knl1C and Ndc80C, beginning in late S phase. Furthermore, Ndc80C levels reaches its peak during prometaphase, while Knl1C levels peaks earlier, during prophase. The delayed recruitment of Knl1C and Ndc80C suggests that Mis12C is not merely an adaptor for these complexes, but additional factors are needed after late S phase to facilitate the binding of Knl1C to Mis12 and Ndc80C to Mis12C and CENP-T.

Dynamics of Corona/SAC proteins

The SAC serves as a cellular surveillance system that detects erroneous microtubule attachments during mitosis1,82,83. The mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC), comprising Bub1, BubR1, Mad2, and Cdc20, inhibits the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C)84–86, an E3 ubiquitination ligase, thereby preventing the proteasomal degradation of Securin and Cyclin B87. In the current model of SAC signaling, improper kinetochore-microtubule attachment triggers Mps1-mediated phosphorylation of Knl1, which subsequently facilitates SAC protein recruitment, including Bub3, Bub1, and BubR174,88,89. The N-terminal Knl1 contains 19 repeats of the MELT motif89,90, which are phosphorylated by Mps1 and Plk1 during early mitosis91–93. This phosphorylation enables the docking of Bub1/Bub3 and BubR1/Bub3 complexes89,90,94. However, not all MELT motifs exhibit equal affinity for these proteins90,95, resulting in an average of 6–7 Bub1/BubR1 proteins binding to a single Knl1 molecule95. BubR1 is critical in recruiting Protein Phosphatase 2A (PP2A)-B56 via its C-terminal KARD domain, which contains three phosphorylation sites for Cdk1 and Plk196–98. The BubR1-bound PP2A-B56 complex subsequently decreases the overall phosphorylation on Knl1 MELT repeats, leading to a marginal reduction in Bub1 levels at kinetochores93,96,99,100. Upon chromosome biorientation, PP1 phosphatase is recruited to kinetochores, which further extinguishes SAC activity93,99,101.

Bub1 recruitment to pre-kinetochores began during G2 phase and peaked in prophase (G2: 0.05; P: 1.00) (Fig. 4), matching the kinetics of Knl1 phosphorylation at MELT motifs (hereafter termed pMELT) (G2: 0.01; P: 1.00) (Fig. 4). Notably, BubR1 recruitment exhibited a slight but significant delay, appearing at kinetochores from late prophase (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 9). After NEBD, Bub1 levels decreased slightly (PM: 0.72), while BubR1 levels peaked (PM: 1.00). During metaphase, both Bub1 and BubR1 levels underwent a dramatic reduction (PM: 0.72; M: 0.13 and PM: 1.00; M:0.25, respectively), consistent with the reduction of pMELT levels (PM: 0.45; M: 0.03). Interestingly, BubR1-S670 is highly phosphorylated during late prophase and prometaphase (PM: 1.00), suggesting immediate phosphorylation by Cdk1 upon its kinetochore recruitment. This phosphorylation event likely promotes PP2A-B56 recruitment, leading to a moderate suppression of SAC activity, as evidenced by the reduction in pMELT and Bub1 levels during prometaphase (Fig. 4). We observed that a small fraction of Bub1 and BubR1 persisted at kinetochores from metaphase to anaphase (M: 0.13; A: 0.07 and M: 0.25; A:0.14, respectively), even though the pMELT levels became almost undetectable by metaphase (M: 0.03) (Fig. 4). This observation suggests the presence of two distinct pools of these proteins: one that is stably associated with kinetochores, independent of MELT phosphorylation status, and another whose binding to Knl1 is dependent on SAC activity.

Kinetochore localization of Bub3, a critical binding partner of Bub1 and BubR1, displayed consistent kinetics. Bub3 levels peaked and remained stable from prophase through prometaphase, followed by a significant reduction during metaphase, becoming undetectable by anaphase (P: 0.95; PM: 1.00; M: 0.16; A: 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, the Bub3 binding sites on Knl1 (pMELT) were reduced by half from prophase to prometaphase (P; 1.00; PM: 0.45) (Fig. 4), whereas Bub3 levels remains stable (P: 0.95; PM: 1.00) (Supplementary Fig. 5). This constancy may be attributed to the dissociation of the Bub1-Bub3 complex from prophase to prometaphase (P: 1.00; PM: 0.72) along with the increased recruitment of the BubR1-Bub3 complex to kinetochores (P: 0.27; PM: 1.00), thereby maintaining a constant total copy number of Bub3 at Knl1 during these phases.

Mad2 exists in two distinct conformations: open (O-Mad2) and closed (C-Mad2)102. Upon binding to Mad1, Mad2 undergoes a conformational change from its open to closed form103,104. The formation of the Mad1/C-Mad2 complex at kinetochores creates a platform that facilitates the further recruitment of cytosolic O-Mad2, which is subsequently converted to C-Mad2102,105. We revealed that both Mad1 and Mad2 exhibited similar dynamic profiles at kinetochores, with their recruitment initiating in prophase and reaching their peak in prometaphase (P: 0. 37; PM: 1.00 and P: 0.18; PM: 1.00, respectively) (Fig. 4). Both Mad1 and Mad2 signals at kinetochores immediately became undetectable in metaphase (M: 0.01 and M: 0.02, respectively). Mad1 directly binds to Bub1 through phosphorylation by Cdk1 and Mps1106–109. Besides Bub1, the RZZ complex has also been proposed as an upstream recruiter of Mad1 at kinetochores110–113, however, the direct binding between Mad1 and the RZZ complex has yet to be demonstrated. Collectively, our analysis demonstrated that Mad1 and Mad2 were specifically recruited to kinetochores during prophase and prometaphase. Additionally, consistent with previous observation, both Mad1 and Mad2 were detected on the nuclear pores (NPs) during interphase (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 10)114–116.

The Rod-Zwilch-Zw10 (RZZ) complex assembles as a hexamer with a 2:2:2 stoichiometry in an antiparallel configuration117. This complex is crucial for SAC silencing by recruiting Spindly, the adaptor for Dynein118,119. Dynein, a minus-end directed motor protein, inactivates SAC by stripping SAC proteins from kinetochores upon proper microtubule attachment120. Recent studies have shown that the RZZ complex can self-oligomerize to form a higher-order structure known as the fibrous corona117,121. Zwint1 is essential for recruiting the RZZ complex to kinetochores through a direct interaction with N-terminal Zw10 (aa 1-80), which is regulated by AurB phosphorylation76,122–124. Our qIF demonstrated that both Rod and Zw10 levels peaked during prometaphase, decreased substantially in metaphase, and became barely detectable in anaphase (PM: 1.00; M: 0.21; A: 0.05) (Fig. 5). We noticed that both Zwint1 and AurB were present at pre-kinetochores during G2 phase (Figs. 3 and 6), whereas the RZZ complex did not localize to kinetochores until prometaphase, possibly due to the absence of nuclear localization signals (NLS) in RZZ proteins125.

Spindly, recruited by the RZZ complex, plays a dual role at kinetochores: it promotes corona assembly117 and serves as an adaptor for Dynein, thereby contributing to the inactivation of SAC120,126,127. Consistent with its interaction with the RZZ complex, Spindly predominantly localized to kinetochores during prometaphase followed by a significant loss in metaphase (PM: 1.00; M: 0.06) (Fig. 5). Except for these two mitotic stages, Spindly signals were absent from kinetochores, though it demonstrated distinct nuclear accumulation during G2 and prophase (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 10).

CENP-E, a plus-end directed kinesin-like motor protein, promotes chromosome congression and facilitates the transition from lateral to end-on microtubule attachment128–130. Bub1, BubR1, Mad1, and the RZZ complex have been proposed as upstream recruiters of CENP-E131–134, however, further investigation is required to clarify their specific roles in this process134–136. During prometaphase, CENP-E was promptly recruited to kinetochores, reaching its peak levels (PM: 1.00) (Fig. 5). Following prometaphase, CENP-E levels significantly decreased in metaphase and continued to decline through anaphase (M: 0.24; A: 0.07). This kinetic profile closely matched that of the RZZ complex but differed from those of Bub1, BubR1, and Mad1/2 (Figs. 4 and 5), which were recruited to kinetochores prior to NEBD. Bioinformatic analysis indicates that CENP-E lacks NLS125, suggesting that NEBD may be required for its targeting to kinetochores.

Unlike CENP-E, CENP-F lacks a motor domain but contains two microtubule-binding domains137,138. CENP-F is recruited to kinetochores through its interaction with the kinase domain of Bub1, a process regulated by AurB activity133,135. CENP-F began localizing to kinetochores during prophase, reaching its peak in prometaphase (P: 0.59; PM: 1.00) (Fig. 5). CENP-F levels then gradually declined from metaphase to anaphase and eventually became undetectable during telophase (M: 0.57; A: 0.21; T: 0). We observed that >50% of CENP-F remained at kinetochores during metaphase (Fig. 5), while Bub1 levels markedly reduced (M: 0.13) (Fig. 4). This suggests that Bub1 is either not essential for the retention of CENP-F at kinetochores or that only a small fraction of Bub1 associates with CENP-F in prometaphase. Despite the absence of CENP-F at kinetochores, CENP-F accumulated in the nucleoplasm from S phase to G2 phase (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). The lack of CENP-F at kinetochores during these stages indicates that CENP-F alone is insufficient for its kinetochore targeting, and that Bub1 and AurB activity are likely also required for this process135,139. Collectively, our findings reveal a subtle yet significant temporal gaps in the recruitment and dissociation of SAC-related proteins at kinetochores.

Dynamics of mitotic kinases

Mitotic kinases orchestrate kinetochore functions by controlling the assembly of kinetochore proteins and modulating the binding affinity between kinetochores and microtubules. Understanding the temporal dynamics of these kinases helps us unravel the sequential recruitment events of kinetochore proteins during mitosis. Therefore, we directly or indirectly investigated the dynamics of three major mitotic kinases (AurB, Plk1, and Haspin), along with their related upstream recruitment adaptors, Sgo1 and Sgo2, and the downstream substrate, MCAK, throughout the cell cycle.

The linkage between sister chromatids is established during S phase by the cohesin complex140–142. Upon mitotic entry, most cohesin complexes are removed from chromosomes, except for centromeres, where they are protected by Sgo1143,144. Sgo1 is recruited to kinetochores via Bub1-dependent phosphorylation of histone H2A at Thr120 (pH2A-T120)145–147. Our qIF demonstrated that Sgo1 began accumulating in the nucleus, including, but not limited to, the centromere regions, during the G2 phase (G2: 0.11) (Fig. 6). This initial nuclear accumulation of Sgo1 coincides with the appearance of pH2A-T120 signals throughout the nucleus (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that Sgo1 can immediately associate to H2A histones upon phosphorylation by Bub1. The levels of centromere-bound Sgo1 increased and peaked during prophase and prometaphase (P: 0.90; PM: 1.00). Subsequently, Sgo1 levels decreased rapidly to near-background levels during metaphase (M: 0.07). This substantial loss of Sgo1 is attributed to the marked reduction in pH2A-T120 levels (PM: 0.92; M: 0.34) (Fig. 6), which is likely driven by the recruitment of PP1 and PP2A.

Sgo2 plays a crucial role in protecting chromatid cohesion from premature cleavage during meiosis148,149. In mitosis, Sgo2 localizes to inner centromeres, though its precise function and recruitment mechanism remain largely unexplored. Like Sgo1, Sgo2 began accumulating at centromeres during G2, peaked in prophase, and gradually decreased, becoming nearly undetectable by anaphase (G2: 0.10; P: 1.00; PM: 0.83; M: 0.48; A: 0.03) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Interestingly, unlike Sgo1, ~50% of Sgo2 remained associated with centromeres during metaphase. This suggests that additional factors contribute to its retention at centromeres, consistent with its partial recruitment dependence on Mps1 and Bub1150.

The Chromosomal Passenger Complex (CPC) consists of four subunits: AurB kinase, INCENP, Borealin, and Survivin151. INCENP serves as a scaffold linking the AurB kinase and the localization modules (Borealin and Survivin)151,152. The CPC localizes to the inner centromeres and kinetochores via two distinct pathways153,154. In the first pathway, Haspin kinase phosphorylates histone H3 at Thr3 (pH3-T3), facilitating Survivin binding153,155,156. In the second pathway, Bub1 kinase phosphorylates H2A-T120, which enables Sgo1 binding and the subsequent recruitment of Borealin153,157. During anaphase, the CPC is translocated to the spindle midzone, and in telophase, it relocates to the midbody158–161. We demonstrated that AurB began localizing to pre-kinetochores during G2 phase (G2: 0.11) (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 10), coinciding with the initial association of Sgo1 with histones (G2: 0.11) (Fig. 6). Concurrently, punctate signals of pH3-T3 were detected outside centromeres in the G2 nucleus, but these levels were relatively low compared to its maximum levels (G2: 0) (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Like Sgo1 and pH3-T3, AurB levels substantially increased from prophase to prometaphase (P: 0.61; PM: 1.00). This increase aligns with AurB’s role in destabilizing improper kinetochore-microtubule attachments, facilitating error correction. Supporting this, its substrate, MCAK, a microtubule depolymerizing kinesin essential for correcting erroneous attachments162–164, began localizing to the inner centromere during G2, coinciding with the initial recruitment of AurB to pre-kinetochores (G1/S: 0.00; G2: 0.15) (Supplementary Fig. 5). MCAK levels peaked at prophase, paralleling the rise of kinetochore-associated AurB, and remained elevated through prometaphase, likely contributing to the error correction process prior to the establishment of proper bi-orientation at metaphase (P: 1.00; PM: 0.74). During metaphase, both AurB, Sgo1, and MCAK showed a marked decrease in their kinetochore levels, though they did not completely fall to background levels (M: 0.22, M: 0.07, M: 0.35, respectively) (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, pH3-T3 levels remained high during this stage (M: 1.00) (Fig. 6). Notably, AurB levels at the midzone during anaphase were significantly lower compared to its centromere levels from prophase to metaphase and at the midbody during telophase (Fig. 6). To further assess AurB kinase activity, we examined another downstream substrate CENP-A Ser7 (CENP-A-S7)165–167. Phosphorylation of CENP-A-S7 was detected from G2 phase, peaked during prophase, and sustained through prometaphase (G2: 0.05; P: 1.00; PM: 0.97) (Fig. 6). Notably, the peak of pCENP-A-S7 occurs earlier than that of AurB, suggesting that complete AurB loading to centromeres is not essential for reaching maximal pCENP-A-S7 levels. The pCENP-A-S7 levels gradually declined from metaphase to anaphase (M: 0.69; A: 0.11), likely due to the dissociation of AurB and the recruitment of phosphatases.

Plk1, a serine/threonine protein kinase, governs multiple essential processes throughout mitosis, including centrosome separation and maturation, chromatin condensation, kinetochore-microtubule attachment, spindle assembly, and cytokinesis168. Its dynamic subcellular localization enables interactions with various substrates169,170. The recruitment of Plk1 to kinetochores is proposed to be mediated by Bub1 and CENP-U in a Cdk1-dependent manner171,172. Plk1 was present at pre-kinetochores from G2 phase through telophase with its peak levels in prometaphase (G2: 0.22; PM: 1.00; T: 0.28) (Fig. 6). Notably, although both Bub1 and Plk1 began localizing to pre-kinetochores during G2 phase, Plk1 demonstrated a significant delay in reaching its peak, implying the requirement for additional PTMs for its full recruitment. Consistent with previous studies170,173, Plk1 was also detected at centrosomes from G2 to telophase, at the central spindle in anaphase, and at the midbody in telophase (Fig. 6).

The Ska complex detects microtubule attachments, while Astrin-SKAP acts as a tension sensor

The Ska complex is considered a functional homologue of the yeast Dam1 complex, which is responsible for stable kinetochore-microtubule interactions174,175. It comprises three proteins: Ska1, Ska2, and Ska3176, all of which exhibit interdependence for their recruitment to kinetochores175,177. Prior studies have demonstrated that the presence of Ndc80C and the inhibition of AurB activity are critical for Ska complex recruitment to kinetochores177–179. As anticipated, Ska3 recruitment to kinetochores began in early prometaphase following NEBD (Fig. 7a), indicating that most kinetochores at this stage are already microtubule-bound. Notably, Ska3 levels were saturated from prometaphase to metaphase (PM: 1.00; M: 0.96) (Fig. 7a), suggesting that its recruitment is a binary, all-or-none process. Ska3 levels then decreased during anaphase and became undetectable by telophase (A: 0.58; T: 0.09), likely correlating with a reduction in Ndc80C (Fig. 3). Besides its kinetochore localization, Ska3 was also detected at the spindle poles from prophase to anaphase (Fig. 7a), aligning with previous research175,177,180. In summary, our qIF analysis supports the model in which Ndc80C and microtubule attachments are key drivers for Ska complex recruitment to kinetochores during unperturbed mitosis. However, while the Ska complex is indicative of kinetochore-microtubule attachment, it does not differentiate between lateral or end-on attachment modes.

The Astrin-SKAP complex (hereafter termed Astrin-SKAP) localizes to both kinetochores and mitotic spindles during mitosis due to its capacity to directly interact with Ndc80C and tubulin181–184. The localization of Astrin and SKAP at kinetochores exhibit a reciprocal dependency183. The key function of Astrin-SKAP is to stabilize kinetochore-microtubule attachments, thereby facilitating chromosome congression184–186. Similar to the Ska complex, Astrin-SKAP recruitment to kinetochores requires the presence of Ndc80C and the inhibition of AurB kinase activity181,183. Strikingly, our qIF revealed that Astrin signals were undetectable at kinetochores during early prometaphase (PM: 0.02) (Fig. 7a). Its kinetochore localization then suddenly began and reached its peak levels in metaphase (M: 1.00). Astrin levels then moderately decreased during anaphase and became nearly undetectable during telophase (A: 0.67; T: 0.02).

Our qIF analysis revealed that both Mad1/Mad2 and Ska3 reached their peak levels during early prometaphase (Figs. 4 and 7a). This observation suggests that, at early prometaphase, most kinetochores are associated with microtubules through either lateral or end-on attachments, but the tension is still insufficient to eliminate SAC proteins from kinetochores. To further dissect the mechanism of Astrin-SKAP recruitment to kinetochores, we examined Astrin levels under the following conditions. First, we investigated whether increased tension could promote additional Astrin recruitment. To this end, Astrin levels were assessed in cells treated with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor known to enhance kinetochore tension35. Unexpectedly, Astrin levels in MG132-treated cells were comparable to those in control metaphase cells (Fig. 7b), indicating that Astrin levels were saturated in normal metaphase cells.

Next, we explored the necessity of kinetochore biorientation for Astrin recruitment by using STLC, an Eg5 inhibitor that induces monopolar spindles187. We found that Astrin only localized to a small subset of kinetochores after 2 h of treatment with STLC (Fig. 7b). Additionally, those Astrin-positive kinetochores were the ones close to the center of monopolar spindle within a pair of sister kinetochores (Fig. 7b). Aligned with the previous research183, simultaneous treatment with STLC and ZM447439, an AurB inhibitor, resulted in nearly uniform Astrin levels across all kinetochores with comparable levels to those in control metaphase cells, whereas Astrin signals were completely absent from kinetochores in the presence of both nocodazole and ZM447439 (Fig. 7b). Combined with the qIF data of Ska3, these findings suggest that the AurB activity in early prometaphase kinetochores is still too high to recruit Astrin even though kinetochores are attached by microtubules and potentially generating tension. To further understand the relationship between Astrin recruitment and the SAC activity on the same kinetochore, we co-stained Astrin with SAC proteins, including Mad2 and Bub1. Under the same condition, Astrin-positive kinetochores exhibited negligible Mad2 signals and significantly decreased Bub1 levels compared to Astrin-negative kinetochores (Fig. 7c), indicating that Astrin preferentially localizes to kinetochores with diminished SAC activity. Note that in this experiment, the remaining Bub1 levels at Astrin-positive kinetochores (17%) (Fig. 7c) were similar to those in untreated metaphase cells (M: 0.13) (Fig. 4), indicating that sister kinetochores with Astrin signals in this condition resemble bioriented metaphase kinetochores.

To ascertain the microtubule binding status under these conditions, cells were incubated in cold media for 10 min before fixation (cold-stability assay), followed by co-staining for Astrin, Mad2, and microtubules. As expected, robust Astrin signals were detected on kinetochores with end-on attachments, devoid of Mad2 signals (Fig. 7d). Kinetochores with lateral attachments showed strong Mad2 signals and no Astrin signals (Fig. 7d). Kinetochores exhibiting both Astrin and Mad2 signals were rarely observed, however, these kinetochores were prone to attachment errors, particularly in instances of merotelic attachments (Fig. 7d). In conclusion, while kinetochore biorientation is not essential for Astrin recruitment, the formation of end-on attachments, including syntelic and merotelic, and the generation of force that locally reduces AurB activity within a single kinetochore are crucial. Furthermore, although both the Ska complex and Astrin-SKAP require low local AurB activity for their kinetochore localization, the Ska complex is more sensitive to the subtle decrease of AurB activity than Astrin-SKAP, as only Ska3 is detected in early prometaphase kinetochores (Fig. 7a).

Most kinetochores attach to microtubules in early prometaphase, but generate minimal tension

Our comprehensive analysis of kinetochore protein dynamics provides deeper insights into how microtubule attachment status influences downstream molecular responses. qIF of the SAC, Ska complex, and Astrin-SKAP revealed that most kinetochores are laterally attached to microtubules in early prometaphase, when chromosomes are organized in a characteristic rosette pattern (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 11a). While these lateral attachments efficiently recruit both SAC proteins and the Ska complex, they fail to recruit Astrin-SKAP (Fig. 7e). This suggests that the tension exerted on kinetochores at this stage is insufficient to reduce AurB activity for Astrin-SKAP recruitment and to remove SAC proteins. In other words, microtubule attachment alone is insufficient to inactivate the SAC, and appropriate tension is required. To further test this model, we assessed Astrin localization in MG132-arrested metaphase cells treated with increasing concentrations of paclitaxel (PTX; 0.01–10 µM) (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Although sister kinetochores remained aligned at the metaphase plate, they exhibited reduced tension, as indicated by a dose-dependent decrease in inter-kinetochore distance (Supplementary Fig. 11c–f)188. Under these conditions, Astrin levels at kinetochores declined in parallel with both increased PTX concentrations and reduced inter-kinetochore distances (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d), while the frequency of Mad2-positive kinetochores increased (Supplementary Fig. 11c). These results reinforce the conclusion that Astrin SKAP-recruitment is tension-dependent, rather than solely reliant on end-on attachment or bi-orientation. Consequently, the Astrin-SKAP complex and SAC proteins are largely mutually exclusive in their kinetochore localization. Further supporting this, in cells treated with STLC, a subset of kinetochores within sister pairs could form force-generating end-on attachments without achieving biorientation (Fig. 7b, d). These kinetochores possessed Astrin-SKAP, but lacked SAC proteins. This evidence underscores the critical role of the Ska complex as a marker for kinetochores engaged in microtubule attachment regardless of whether it is lateral or end-on. In contrast, the Astrin-SKAP complex signifies that the kinetochore is under tension, albeit its detection of tension levels is constrained to a relatively narrow dynamic range.

CENP-C functions as the primary adaptor for recruiting the Mis12C to interphase kinetochores

We demonstrated that Mis12C is recruited to pre-kinetochores as early as G1 phase, distinguishing its dynamics from other KMN network components (Fig. 3). Approximately 80% of G1 and 100% of S phase cells, as well as G1 cells arrested with palbociclib for 24 h were Dsn1/Pmf1-positive (Supplementary Fig. 12). Notably, the subset of G1 cells lacking Mis12C corresponded to early G1 cells, immediately following cytokinesis (Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, Mis12C was also recruited in interphase HeLa cells, suggesting a conserved mechanism across cell types (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d). We further dissected the kinetics of Mis12C from G1 to G2 phase using qIF of Dsn1. The profile of Dsn1 closely resembled that of CENP-C (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Notably, Dsn1 levels increased twofold from the G1 to S phase, suggesting that Mis12C is recruited to newly synthesized pre-kinetochores during S phase, similar to other CCAN components. Consistent with CENP-C, but in contrast to CENP-K, CENP-I, CENP-N, and CENP-T, Dsn1 levels were significantly higher in G2 phase compared to G1 phase, indicating that additional Mis12C continues to be recruited to pre-kinetochores during G2 phase. To investigate whether Mis12C forms the same heterotetrametric complex at interphase kinetochores as observed during mitosis, we quantified the population of interphase cells positive for Dsn1 and Pmf1 as well as the signal intensities for these proteins in Dsn1-AID RPE1 cells189. Following 9 h of Auxin treatment, Dsn1-AID cells exhibited a significant reduction (>90%) in the percentage of Dsn1- and Pmf1-positive interphase cells, with a nearly complete loss (~100%) of Dsn1 and Pmf1 signals at pre-kinetochores (Fig. 8a). These findings indicate that Mis12C likely assembles into a heterotetramer complex at interphase kinetochores, akin to its mitotic organization.

Given that both CENP-C and CENP-T independently contribute to Mis12C recruitment to kinetochores during mitosis62, we sought to investigate their roles in Mis12C recruitment during interphase. To this end, we utilized a CENP-C-AID RPE1 cell line40 to quantify the population of interphase cells positive for Dsn1 and Pmf1, as well as the signal intensity of these proteins at kinetochores upon CENP-C depletion (Fig. 8b, c). Depletion of CENP-C by an hour of Auxin treatment resulted in an 85% reduction in the percentage of Dsn1- and Pmf1-positive interphase cells and a 95% loss of their kinetochore levels (Fig. 8b, c). Since CENP-C stabilizes other CCAN proteins42,48, we also quantified the levels of additional CCAN components in CENP-C depleted cells. Notably, we observed no reduction in the percentage of CENP-T, CENP-N, or CENP-I-positive cells upon CENP-C depletion, however, their kinetochore signals were reduced by ~50% compared to control interphase cells (Fig. 8b, c). These results indicate that CENP-C, rather than CENP-T or other CCAN proteins, serves as the primary adaptor for Mis12C recruitment to pre-kinetochores in interphase. As Mis12C binding to CENP-C during mitosis is regulated by AurB kinase activity62–65, which is thought to be minimal in interphase, we next investigated whether AurB kinase activity is required for Mis12C localization to pre-kinetochores in interphase. To address this, we quantified Dsn1 levels at pre-kinetochores during S phase and prometaphase in RPE1 treated with a high concentration of ZM447439 (Supplementary Fig. 14a). After one hour of treatment, AurB activity was completely abolished, as evidenced by the complete loss of pH3-S10 signals in prometaphase cells (Supplementary Fig. 14b, c). Consistent with previous studies62,64, ZM447439 treatment led to a 43% reduction of Dsn1 levels at prometaphase kinetochores (Supplementary Fig. 14c). However, no reduction in Dsn1 levels was observed at interphase kinetochores (Supplementary Fig. 14d), indicating that the mechanism of Mis12C recruitment by CENP-C during interphase is distinct from that in mitosis and independent of AurB kinase activity.

Limitations of the study

In this study, to quantify protein levels at kinetochores, we used antibodies for fluorescent labeling. However, the binding affinity of antibodies to target proteins or potential conformational changes of the target proteins can affect staining quality. To minimize this, we optimized staining protocols for each antibody, including fixation methods and working concentrations, to achieve a high S/N ratio. We also quantified multiple components within the same protein complex to ensure accurate measurements. While fluorescent proteins (FPs) are valuable tools in studying protein dynamics in live cells, qIF offers distinct advantages, including high detection sensitivity and technical feasibility. Reproducing similar experiments using FPs would need a minimum of three colors (i.e., Histone H2B-CFP, a target kinetochore protein-EGFP, and a stable kinetochore marker-mCherry). However, overexpression of kinetochore proteins often causes mislocalization and mitotic defects190–192. This necessitates the generation of cell lines expressing endogenously-tagged proteins via CRISPR-Cas9 or similar techniques, which are time-consuming and impractical for large-scale studies of macromolecular protein complexes, such as kinetochores. Additionally, compared to fluorescence dyes, FPs are significantly dimmer. For example, EGFP is at least 50% dimmer than Alexa 488 based on its extinction coefficient and quantum yield193. These FPs exhibit lower photostability and slower maturation. Furthermore, multiple fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies can bind to a single primary antibody, amplifying the signals far beyond what FPs can achieve. Together, these advantages of qIF enables highly sensitive and precise quantification of protein dynamics.

Discussion

The kinetochore is a highly organized macromolecular protein complex composed of a diverse array of proteins, which are systematically and dynamically assembled throughout the cell cycle. However, a comprehensive understanding of kinetochore architecture during the cell cycle progression has remained elusive. In this study, we utilized qIF to precisely determine the dynamics of 37 different kinetochore proteins/substrates, covering key protein complexes and kinase substrates. Our findings provide a new insight into the dynamic architectural remodeling of kinetochores, shedding light on the assembly and disassembly of kinetochore components. Figure 9 illustrates the proposed model of kinetochore architecture, integrating both our data and previous studies. In G1 phase, CENP-A, CENP-B, and all CCAN proteins are consistently present at kinetochores. Notably, in the middle to late G1 phase, Mis12C is recruited to kinetochores via CENP-C. During the S phase, CCAN and Mis12C are rapidly assembled onto newly synthesized centromeres, while sister kinetochores remain clustered within a subdiffraction-limited distance until G2 phase. As Cyclin B1 levels rise during G2 phase81, Cdk1 becomes active, initiating the phosphorylation of key kinetochore substrates, such as CENP-C and CENP-T. Phosphorylation of CENP-C promotes its binding to CENP-A40,44, reshaping the CCAN architecture, including the dissociation of CENP-NL and CENP-HIKM complexes from kinetochores. Concurrently, phosphorylation of CENP-T facilitates its direct interaction with Ndc80C and Mis12C67. Additionally, the recruitment of Knl1C via Mis12C leads to the phosphorylation of MELT motifs on Knl1 by Mps1, triggering the assembly of the Bub1/Bub3 complex and their downstream proteins, including Sgo1, AurB, and Plk1. Although Mps1 begins accumulating at kinetochores during prophase194, its presence in the nucleus is detected as early as G2 phase195.

Summary of kinetochore landscape changes throughout the cell cycle.

a The relative abundance of individual kinetochore proteins at the kinetochore at each stage of the cell cycle. The data for each protein within the same complex was averaged. b A model of the architecture of the human kinetochore. c A model of the dynamic kinetochore landscape throughout the cell cycle. Individual proteins are outlined in a solid line if they are stably associated to, or a dashed line if they are beginning to appear at or are in the process of significant dissociation from kinetochores.

During prophase, Cdk1, AurB, and Mps1 kinases reach their peak activities194,196,197, leading to the robust recruitment of Mis12C, Knl1C, and the Bub1/Bub3 complex to kinetochores. This creates abundant binding platforms for SAC-related proteins. Simultaneously, Cdk1 and Mps1 cooperatively phosphorylate Bub1106, which facilitates Mad1 recruitment and the assembly of the MCC198,199. The early assembly of the MCC prior to NEBD suggests that pre-loading of SAC components to kinetochores may be critical to prevent premature chromosome segregation when microtubules begin to search for and capture kinetochores immediately upon NEBD. Concurrently, AurB-mediated Bub1 phosphorylation triggers CENP-F recruitment to kinetochores135. After NEBD, proteins lacking NLS, such as the RZZ subunits and CENP-E, gain access to kinetochores. In early prometaphase, AurB reaches peak levels at kinetochores, and its phosphorylation of Zwint1 facilitates RZZ complex recruitment123, which in turn recruits Spindly and CENP-E119,131. In parallel, AurB kinase activity promotes CENP-F recruitment135. Additionally, Mps1 drives the oligomerization of the RZZ complex and Spindly117,119,121, forming a scaffold for the integration of Mad1, Mad2, CENP-E, and CENP-F, a process known as corona expansion. This phenomenon is particularly prominent in unattached kinetochores, as seen in nocodazole-treated mitotic cells24. As a result, most SAC-related and corona proteins reach their peak levels at this stage, increasing the likelihood of kinetochore-microtubule interactions and promoting the transition from lateral to end-on microtubule attachment11,128,200. SAC strength is fine-tuned by the recruitment of BubR1, which peaks during prometaphase and enables the recruitment of the PP2A-B56 phosphatase to counterbalance Mps1 and Plk1 activities96,99.

During metaphase, chromosomes achieve biorientation, creating a physical separation between the outer kinetochore and the inner centromere, where AurB is concentrated. This spatial arrangement facilitates the dephosphorylation of Knl1 by PP2A93, promoting the subsequent recruitment of PP1 to kinetochores. As a result, most of the MELT motifs become dephosphorylated, leading to the dissociation of Bub proteins99. Concurrently, end-on microtubule attachments strip off SAC and corona proteins, as evidenced by a dramatic reduction in the RZZ complex, Spindly, Mad1/2, CENP-E, and CENP-F levels at kinetochores. However, a residual pool of Bub1, BubR1, RZZ, CENP-E, and CENP-F persists on metaphase kinetochores, suggesting the existence of two distinct protein populations: a tension-sensitive pool that dissociates upon chromosome biorientation, and a stable structural pool that likely contributes to the stabilization of kinetochore-microtubule attachments. Meanwhile, the reduction in AurB activity triggers Astrin-SKAP complex recruitment to kinetochores181,183. As cells progress into anaphase, Cdk1 and AurB activity declines further71,73,197,201, leading to a moderate reduction in all KMN network components and their downstream proteins, including the RZZ complex, CENP-E, and CENP-F. In telophase, all outer kinetochore and corona proteins, as well as mitotic kinases, with the exception of Plk1, become nearly undetectable at kinetochores. The remaining Plk1 is responsible for CENP-A deposition onto centromeres during the early G1 phase29.

We found some interesting mismatches in the recruitment timing of kinetochore proteins, diverging from reported recruitment dependencies. These findings suggested the involvement of additional, yet unidentified regulatory mechanisms, potentially related to PTMs, protein synthesis timing, or protein characteristics. Notable examples include the temporal gaps between Mad1 and its upstream adaptor Bub1, as well as Bub1 and BubR1. Despite the fact that both Bub1 and BubR1 bind to pMELT motifs, we observed a clear delay in BubR1 recruitment compared to Bub1. Although Mps1-mediated phosphorylation of Bub1 triggers its binding to Mad1106, a distinct time gap remains. Bub1 levels peak at prophase and moderately decrease in prometaphase. In contrast, Mad1 levels continue to increase from prophase to prometaphase (Fig. 4). Another example is the discrepancy between Zwint1 and the RZZ complex. Zwint1 is recruited to kinetochores as early as prophase, but the RZZ complex is not recruited until prometaphase. Given that Zw10 expression remains stable from G1/S to mitosis202, it is likely that the RZZ complex is prohibited from entering the nucleus until NEBD. In contrast, Spindly can enter the nucleus as early as G2 phase, but does not specifically accumulate at kinetochores until prometaphase, when the RZZ complex is localized to kinetochores. The precise mechanisms underlying the time gaps in kinetochore protein recruitment and dissociation remain unclear and warrant further investigation. In summary, our comprehensive qIF analysis elucidates the dynamic alteration of kinetochore landscape throughout the cell cycle. The insight gained from this study provides a valuable foundation for future research into these complex regulatory mechanisms.

Methods

Cell culture

Human RPE1 and HeLa cells were originally obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). CENP-C AID RPE1 cells40 were a kind gift from Dr. Daniele Fachinetti. Dsn1-AID RPE1 cells189 were a kind gift from Dr. Tatsuo Fukagawa. All aforementioned cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11965092) or DMEM/F12 (Gibco, 11320033) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (Seradigm, 1500–500) under 5% CO2 at 37 °C in an incubator. For inhibitor treatments, cells were treated with 10 μΜ MG-132 (MCE, HY-13259), 3 μΜ nocodazole (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AC358240100), 2.5 or 5 μΜ STLC (Sigma-Aldrich, 164739), 10 μΜ ZM447439 (MCE, HY-10128), or 1 μΜ Palbociclib (MCE, HY-50767). 500 μΜ of Auxin (Sigma-Aldrich, 12886) was used for CENP-C-AID RPE1 cells and Dsn1-AID RPE1 cells. For the cold-stability assay, cells were incubated in cold media at 4 °C for 10 min to depolymerize unstable spindle fibers before fixation.

Drug treatment

To investigate the mechanism for the recruitment of Astrin to kinetochores, we treated cells with different combinations of drugs for 2 h before fixation and IF staining. The tested conditions are listed as follows: untreated; 10 μΜ MG-132 (MCE, HY-13259); 2.5 μΜ STLC (Sigma-Aldrich, 164739); 2.5 μΜ STLC (Sigma-Aldrich, 164739) + 10 µΜ ZM447439 (MCE, HY-10128); 3 µΜ nocodazole (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AC358240100) + 10 µΜ ZM447439 (MCE, HY-10128). To investigate the relationship between Astrin and SAC-related proteins (Mad2 and Bub1), cells were treated with 2.5 μΜ STLC (Sigma-Aldrich, 164739) for 2 h before fixation and IF staining.

Immunofluorescence

Asynchronous RPE1 cells were seeded on #1.5 thickness coverslips at least a day prior to fixation. Cells were fixed by 4% PFA in 250 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5) at 37 °C or cold methanol at −20 °C for 15 min. For Bub3 and Ska3 staining, pre-extraction was performed by incubating cells in 1% Triton X-100 in PHEM buffer at 37 °C for 1 min, followed by fixation with 1% Triton X-100 in 4% PFA/HEPES at 37 °C for 15 min. After fixation, cells were washed with PBS 3 times at room temperature (RT). Only cells fixed with PFA were then permeabilized by 0.5% Nonidet P-40 (Santa Cruz, sc-29102) in PBS at RT for 15 min. After permeabilization, cells were washed with PBS. Blocking was performed by incubating cells in 0.1% Bovine Serum Albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, A2153). Cells fixed with PFA were incubated in primary antibody solution in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 2.5 h followed by incubation in secondary antibody solution in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Cells fixed with methanol were incubated in primary antibody solution in a humidified chamber at RT for 1 h, followed by incubation in secondary antibody solution in a humidified chamber at RT for 1 h. Primary and secondary antibody-related information is listed in Supplementary Data 1-3. After secondary antibody staining, cells were washed with PBS. Cells were incubated in 200 ng/ml DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, 13190309) in PBS at RT for 15 min. The stained coverslips were mounted with homemade mounting media (20 mM Tris (pH 9.0), 90% glycerol, 0.2% n-propyl gallate).

Imaging

Images were acquired using a Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disc confocal and a Hamamatsu Quest qCMOS camera or Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-SoRa W1 spinning disc confocal and Hamamatsu Fusion camera. Both microscopes were equipped with a high-power laser unit (100 mW for 405, 488, 561, and 640 nm wavelength) for excitation. All Z-stack images were acquired at a step of 200 nm controlled by Nikon NIS Elements (version 5.21). A Plan Apo 100x oil objective (NA = 1.45) was used for qIF assay, and a Plan Apo λ 60x oil objective (NA = 1.40) was used for other experiments. Images of all cell cycle stages for one biological replicate were acquired from the same single coverslip on the same day using the same imaging settings. For all qIF assays, the representative images of all cell cycle stages using the same set of antibodies were adjusted to have the same brightness and contrast for the target protein as a fair comparison. All representative images showing a single cell are maximum intensity projections. In contrast, the zoomed-in images highlighting a single kinetochore or a pair of sister kinetochores were obtained either from a single Z-plane, a maximum projection of a few adjacent Z-planes, or from the same projection used for the full-cell representative image. This approach was necessary because kinetochores are distributed along the Z-axis, often leading to overlapping foci in maximum intensity projections. Detailed information on how each zoomed-in image was obtained for the corresponding representative images is provided in Supplementary Data 3. While the qIF for 37 POI shown in the main figures (Figs. 1b, c, 2–6, 7a) display only the merged images of the three channels (POI, kinetochore marker, and DAPI) and POI images, Supplementary Figs. 15, 16 display each channel separately.

Cell cycle determination

For cell cycle identification, we used the ImmunoCellCycle-ID method or a modified version we previously developed19. In all qIF assays, RPE1 cells were stained with antibodies against the target protein, a kinetochore marker (either CENP-C or CENP-T), and DAPI (a DNA dye). Mitotic stages were identified based on DNA morphology. Late G2 phase cells were defined as those containing at least 20 pairs of sister kinetochores, as determined by CENP-C or CENP-T staining. The G1/S phase group includes both G1 and S phase cells, which generally lack paired sister kinetochores. In most figures, “G1/S” refers to this combined group, “G2” indicates late G2 phase, “P” is prophase, “PM” is prometaphase, “M” is metaphase, “A” is anaphase, and “T” is telophase. Progression from telophase to G1 was assessed using co-staining with a tubulin antibody, while prophase onset was determined by chromatin condensation. To define detailed interphase sub-stages (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 6a–d, 8a–c, 12a,b), RPE1 cells were stained with antibodies against the target protein, PCNA (an S-phase marker), CENP-F (a marker present in the nucleus during S and G2 phases), and DAPI, following the ImmunoCellCycle-ID protocol. Briefly, G1 phase cells lack nuclear CENP-F signals. Early and late S phase cells were distinguished based on distinct nuclear PCNA puncta patterns. Both S and G2 phase cells exhibit nuclear CENP-F signals, but G2 phase cells lack PCNA punctate staining. While PCNA alone cannot differentiate between G1 and G2 phases, the presence or absence of nuclear CENP-F enables accurate discrimination. Additionally, late G2 phase cells are characterized by the presence of paired sister kinetochores.

Image analysis

The method for measuring local background-corrected signal intensity at individual kinetochores has been previously described23,24, and a schematic with representative examples is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1e, f. A detailed step-by-step protocol, including example images for CENP-C and Spindly staining, is provided in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3. Signal quantification was performed using MetaMorph (version 7.10). Briefly, two bounding boxes with different sizes were placed on the target kinetochore at its best-focus Z-plane. The smaller box was required to fully encompass the kinetochore signal, and the larger box to fully enclose the smaller one (Supplementary Figs. 1e and 4b). The true signal intensity was obtained by subtracting the local background from the total intensity within the smaller bounding box. The best-focus Z-plane was defined as the optical section with the highest maximum intensity for the given kinetochore (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). In all qIF assays, signal intensities were measured from 25 kinetochores per cell, and in most experiments, 20 cells were analyzed across at least two biological replicates. The number of cells and replicates is indicated in each figure. Because our method incorporates local background correction, the quantification was robust regardless of variations in bounding box size as long as the smaller box fully contained the kinetochore focus (Supplementary Fig. 4). If the smaller bounding box failed to encompass the entire kinetochore, the measured intensity was underestimated. Conversely, without background correction, the integrated intensity increased proportionally with bounding box size due to the inclusion of background signals (Supplementary Fig. 4). When the target proteins did not show detectable signals at kinetochores, the best-focus Z-plane and positions of the kinetochore marker (CENP-C) were used as a reference to align and transfer the corresponding coordinates onto the target protein images (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Because all kinetochore proteins exhibit dynamic changes in abundance throughout cell cycle progression, and due to limitations in co-labeling caused by the host species of antibodies used, most qIF analyses were performed without internal controls for signal normalization. Supplementary Fig. 7 presents qIF measurements of Dsn1 and Spc25 normalized to CENP-A levels at the same kinetochores. The resulting profiles normalized by CENP-A closely matched the original dynamic patterns obtained without internal normalization (shown in Fig. 3). It is important to note that staining, imaging, and quantification for these experiments (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 7) were performed independently. Additionally, Supplementary Fig. 7c shows selected qIF measurement plots of individual cells, suggesting that technical variations from labeling and imaging in our qIF assay have relatively minimal impact on our overall biological conclusions.

To quantify the percentage of cells positive for a specific kinetochore protein, cells were co-stained with antibodies against the target protein, such as Dsn1, Pmf1, CENP-T, CENP-N, or CENP-I, and a kinetochore marker (either CENP-C or ACA). Cells lacking detectable kinetochore-localized signals for the target protein were classified as negative.

Statistics and biological replicates

All quantification plots were made using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5). All statistics in this study were performed with unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test using GraphPad Prism. The p-values between adjacent cell cycle stages are shown in the quantification plot of all qIF assays. The results of statistical comparison between non-adjacent cell cycle stages are listed in Supplementary Data 4. All experiments had 2–3 independent biological replicates performed. Sample size and the number of biological replicates are included in each figure or figure legend. For most of the qIF analyses, each data point represents a single kinetochore, except for some parts of the datasets of inner centromere proteins as well as the qIF dataset of pH2A-T120 and pH3-T3. For proteins that localize at inner centromeres (i.e., CENP-B, Sgo1, Sgo2, AurB, and MCAK), each data point shown from G2 phase to metaphase represents the combined signal intensity of paired sister kinetochores, while each data point shown in G1/S, anaphase, and telophase represents the signals from a single kinetochore dot. In the case of pH2A-T120 and pH3-T3, each data point represents the integrated signal intensity within a single nucleus. Kinetochore signal intensities at each cell cycle stage were normalized to the stage exhibiting the highest mean signal intensity across the cell cycle stages. All error bars represent the standard deviation relative to the mean, and independent biological replicates are distinguished by different colors.
