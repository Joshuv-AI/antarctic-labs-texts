Practical guideline for malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors
Authors: Yale Jiang, Guo Zhao, Zhenglin Zhao, Lei Guo, Shuhang Wang, Ning Li
Published: 2026

Main text

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) are a rare, aggressive, and heterogenous subtype of soft tissue sarcoma that arise from the peripheral nerves or associated sheaths. They account for approximately 5-10% of all soft tissue sarcomas, characterized by rapid growth, a significant propensity for local recurrence, and early metastasis. Nearly 50% of MPNSTs are associated with genetic predisposition of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Their prognosis is worse than sporadic with 5-year overall survival (OS) rate 34%–50%.1 Recent study on a Chinese cohort found malignant transformation rate of 8.2% in NF1 patients, higher than Western and imposing severe economic burden on the domestic medical system.2 Sporadic MPNSTs typically present at around 50 years, later compared to 25–35 years in NF1 patients. Radiation-induced MPNSTs often appear decades after exposure associated with the poorest outcomes. Owing to restricted understanding of the etiology and pathogenesis brought by rarity and heterogeneity, the therapeutic landscape slowly shifts with dismal prognosis. Methods

This guideline was developed by synthesizing peer-reviewed literature, including clinical trials, observational cohorts, and international sarcoma group statements including SARC and EORTC. The formulation of recommendations followed a structured, iterative process of expert deliberation. Recommendations were categorized using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) system. Statements without grading were considered justified standard clinical practice by the authors.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of MPNST is difficult, defined by malignant behavior plus nerve sheath differentiation, rather than a single pathognomonic marker. MPNST is diagnosed by integrating clinical suspicion, morphology, immunophenotype, and molecular alterations, and exclusion among spindle cell sarcomas. NF1 and sporadic MPNST share the same diagnostic criteria, but NF1 requires earlier suspicion, longitudinal assessment, and recognition of intermediate lesions, typically via a stepwise transformation from plexiform neurofibroma to atypical neurofibromatous neoplasm of uncertain biologic potential (ANNUBP).

Clinical presentation

MPNST can manifest in any anatomical site as a painful, rapidly expanding mass with neurological deficits. Anatomical distribution provides critical contextual information that influences diagnostic confidence, differential diagnosis, biopsy strategy, surgical feasibility, and ultimately outcome. Tumors arising along major peripheral nerves or in deep axial locations carry a higher probability, whereas superficially located or visceral tumors require a higher threshold of exclusion of alternative sarcoma entities. Comprehensive neurological, ophthalmological, and audiometric examinations are essential to evaluate multi-site involvement.

Imaging

Imaging is crucial for evaluating the extent and characteristics. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the gold standard. MPNSTs typically appear as heterogeneous masses with areas of necrosis or hemorrhage. Computed Tomography (CT) scans provide additional information when MRI is not feasible or to assess bony involvement. Positron Emission Tomography (PET)-MRI/CT scans are recommended if feasible, to help evaluate the metabolic activity.

Histopathology

The diagnosis relies on histopathological confirmation by biopsy to exclude morphological mimics such as monophasic synovial sarcoma, spindle cell/desmoplastic melanoma, and leiomyosarcoma. Current criteria define high-grade MPNST by a mitotic index of ≥10 mitoses/10 HPFs, or 3-9 mitoses/10 HPFs in the presence of geographic necrosis, thereby distinguishing it from ANNUBP and ANNUBP with increased proliferation, which was formerly termed low-grade MPNST. Immunohistochemically, complete loss of H3K27me3 is a specific surrogate for PRC2 inactivation in high-grade tumors, whereas diffuse S100/SOX10 expression marking benign precursors is often diminished or absent.

Genetic Test

Differentiation between high-grade disease and ANNUBP requires genetic test to discern molecular profiles, distinguishing malignancies characterized by copy number alterations of homozygous CDKN2A/B inactivation, and short structural variants of SUZ12, EED, or TP53 mutation.3

Management

Worldwide guidelines agree that MPNST should be managed as a high-grade soft tissue sarcoma. Complete surgical resection serves as the cornerstone, with radiotherapy for local control, limited and selective use of chemotherapy, and strong encouragement of clinical trial participation.

Surgery

Complete surgical resection offers the only potentially curative intervention; however, meta-analyses report a 5-year OS of 47% after surgery. Local recurrence rate remains high of 40% based on the MONACO study. Margin status is the most powerful and consistently validated prognostic factor. Surgical planning should prioritize oncologic radicality over nerve preservation. Limb-sparing surgery is preferred when negative margins can be reliably obtained, whereas amputation or radical compartmental resection is reserved for unresectable disease, severe neurologic compromise, or intractable pain. In axial, paraspinal, or retroperitoneal locations, surgery should be integrated within a multidisciplinary strategy incorporating radiotherapy and perioperative approaches. Surgery is complicated in NF1, necessitating a lower threshold for radical resection, wider sampling, and close coordination with longitudinal imaging to minimize under-treatment due to intralesional heterogeneity.

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is primarily used to enhance local control in high-grade MPNST. Doses typically exceed 50 Gy, delivered via external‑beam techniques, and are considered most beneficial for patients with high risks of recurrence. Evidence from the MONACO study supports reduced local recurrence risk at 47% in sporadic patients but not in NF1 subgroup, although did not affect OS. Radiotherapy usually commences within 4 to 6 weeks post-operation, contingent upon adequate wound healing. Brachytherapy and intraoperative electron radiotherapy have also been explored, showing 5‑year local control rates of 88%. In metastatic or inoperable disease, radiotherapy provides meaningful palliation. NF1 patients have elevated risk of radiation‑induced second primary malignancies and limited evidence of survival benefit, thus use of radiotherapy warrants careful consideration.

Chemotherapy

The role of perioperative chemotherapy for localized, high-risk MPNST is not definitively established and remains controversial, usually considered on a case-by-case basis in large, high-grade tumors, young patients, or borderline resectable disease. It should be noted that no guideline recommends routine adjuvant chemotherapy. For inoperable or metastatic MPNST, the established first-line treatment consists of anthracycline-based chemotherapy, with response rate of 21%, progression-free survival (PFS) of 17 weeks and OS of 48 weeks. NF1-associated MPNSTs are significantly more chemo-resistant. Performance status is an independent prognostic factor, while chemotherapy regimen of doxorubicin-ifosfamide regimen showed apparent superiority in response and PFS compared with doxorubicin alone. Once progression, treatment options are limited. Regimens such as gemcitabine plus docetaxel commonly employed, albeit with modest response rates of 10-15%.4

Targeted and immunotherapy

Current targeted therapy remains primarily within clinical trials. For cases harbouring BRAF V600E mutations, BRAF inhibitor (e.g., vemurafenib) is recommended. For tumours with PRC2 loss-of-function (e.g., SUZ12/EED mutations), EZH2 inhibitors (tazemetostat) or epigenetic modulators should be considered. Immunotherapy for MPNST is not a standard treatment, although case reports showing long-standing disease control. Checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) show isolated activity, potentially in tumors with positive expression of PD-L1 or high tumor mutation burden.

Palliative care

Quality of life should be preferentially considered. Palliative care should be integrated into the treatment plan to manage symptoms such as pain, functional impairments, and psychological distress.

Future directions

MPNST management is moving from size- and site-based staging toward a combined anatomical and molecular framework. Paired-tissue genomic sequencing is strongly recommended. If conditions permit, universal genetic testing is recommended especially in suspected hereditary cancer syndrome such as NF1/2. Somatic copy-number and methylation profiling of H3K27me3 of cell-free DNA could potentially serve as a biomarker for prognosis and response to immune regulations.5

MPNST patients should be highly encouraged to consider participation in clinical trials, which may provide access to novel treatment strategies. Trial participation is not a last resort but a rational extension of care, with trial selection guided by biological plausibility, disease context, and individualized risk-benefit assessment, particularly in NF1-associated and advanced disease. Referral to specialized sarcoma centers is crucial to facilitate access to these emerging therapies.Trials targeting known MPNST vulnerabilities should be prioritized including MAPK signaling, PRC2 deficiency, CDKN2A loss, YAP/TEAD activation. Combinational targets such as MAPK pathway activated by NF1 loss and compensatory PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling are more encouraging when toxicity is controlable. Epigenetic dysregulation is common in MPNST, indicating that EZH2 inhibitors such as Tazemetostat, and methylation modulators such as cedazuridine plus decitabine, may potentially benefit by complementing PRC2 aberrations. MAT2A inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy particularly in PRC2-deficient tumors, by exploiting synthetic lethality. It should be emphasized in trial selection for MPNST that mechanistic alignment matters more than trial phase alone.

Although checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy showed limited efficacy, neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab combination is explored to overcome the immunosuppressive microenvironment of sarcomas. Pembrolizumab combined with MDM2-p53 modulator such as Alrizomadlin may further enhance its effect. Emerging modalities including oncolytic virus and therapeutic vaccines directed against tumor-specific antigens are promising strategies to potentiate anti-tumor immune activation.

Conclusion

MPNSTs are a rare and aggressive group of soft tissue sarcomas that present significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment modality but often inapplicable. Radiotherapy should not be withheld when necessary, but should be used judiciously with clear indication and risk-adapted planning, particularly in NF1. Given the complexity of MPNST management, treatment should be biology-anchored and delivered in a multidisciplinary setting. Participation in well-designed clinical trials should be considered at any disease stage. Primary therapeutic aim is advocated to be set as durable disease control and symptom alleviation, upon which tumor regression can be further anticipated.
