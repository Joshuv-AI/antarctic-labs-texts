Central Message

Intraoperative molecular imaging (IMI) with pafolacianine can improve oncologic resections by detecting positive margins, identifying occult lesions, and detecting synchronous malignant nodules.

With increasing adoption of lung cancer screening, more than 1 million pulmonary nodules are detected annually. Resection can be curative for those nodules that are early-stage lung cancers, but localization of small nodules—particularly those with a ground-glass component—can be a formidable intraoperative challenge. Intraoperative molecular imaging (IMI) has gained prominence as an adjunct to conventional visual and tactile localization.1 IMI involves systemic delivery of targeted fluorescent contrast agents that accumulate in lung nodules and can be detected using wavelength-specific camera systems.2 Pafolacianine, a folate-receptor alpha−targeted IMI tracer, is the first imaging agent to gain Food and Drug Administration approval.3 There remains a knowledge gap in understanding the patient populations for which pafolacianine-based IMI is most clinically impactful. To elucidate this question, we conducted an analysis of the largest single institutional experience of IMI-guided pulmonary resection.

Methods

Between July 2015 and August 2021, patients with a pulmonary nodule suspicious for malignancy were intravenously administered pafolacianine (0.025 mg/kg) 24 hours before surgery. All patients gave written informed consent for the publication of their study data, and protocols were approved by the University of Pennsylvania IRB (approval numbers: 822,153 [approval date: May 4, 2015], 826,547 [approval date: January 6, 2017], and 842,586 [approval date: April 23, 2020]). During resection, a commercially available wavelength-specific thoracoscope (Medtronic Visionsense3) or robotic scope (da Vinci Firefly; Intuitive Surgical) was used to detect the tracer in malignant cells and guide resection. The primary end point was a clinically significant event (CSE), in which IMI caused a meaningful change in the nature or conduct of the operation. Patient demographic data, tumor histology, and fluorescence characteristics were collected prospectively.

Results

During the study period, 295 patients underwent pafolacianine-based IMI during resection. Infusion with pafolacianine was well tolerated, and there were no serious adverse events. Most patients were female (Table 1; n = 173, 58.6%), middle-aged (mean age, 65.7 years), and former smokers (n = 192 [65%], 39.7 mean pack years). Mean tumor size was 2.2 cm (interquartile range, 1.4-2.8 cm) and most were adenocarcinoma-spectrum lesions (n = 246, 72.6%). Of 339 total lesions, 238 (70.2%) fluoresced with mean tumor-to-background ratio of 3.6 (interquartile range, 2.4-4.3). Ninety-one patients (30.8%) underwent sublobar resection, and 204 patients (69.2%) underwent lobar resection. CSEs occurred in 74 patients (25.1%). The majority of these cases were minimally invasive (video-assisted thoracic surgery: n = 174; robotic assisted: n = 103; thoracotomy: n = 18). Three CSEs occurred in the study: localization of lesions not identifiable by white light thoracoscopy or palpable by minimally invasive techniques (n = 50), identification of a close or positive margin (n = 16), or detection of an occult synchronous nodule confirmed as a malignancy on final pathology (n = 8; Figure 1 for examples of CSEs). The CSE rate for sublobar resection was 38.5% versus 19.1% for lobar resection (P = .0014 by Fisher exact test). Discussion

In this study of the largest single-center experience of pafolacianine-based IMI, we found that the technology yielded meaningful changes in over a quarter of cases. The most common CSE was the detection of lesions not identifiable by white light thoracoscopy, and CSEs were more frequent in sublobar resections. This study adds to the body of literature delineating the benefits of IMI as an adjunct to standard surgical methods in pulmonary resection4,5 and helps identify patient groups for which the technology is most clinically impactful. Although ongoing study is needed for confirmation, our findings support the use of IMI in small, subpleural lesions that appear to border intersegmental planes on preoperative imaging.
