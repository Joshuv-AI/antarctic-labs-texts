Reassessing D-dimer Cut-offs for Pulmonary Embolism: A Path Toward Safer and More Cost-effective Diagnosis
Authors: Amer Abu-Shanab, Montaser Alrjoob, Zain Albdour, Alaa Alkhatib, Apurva Ketkar, Ahmad Al-Alwan, Charmee Vyas, Husam Bader, Sohini Sarkar, Doantrang Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary embolism (PE) is a life-threatening condition requiring early and accurate diagnosis. The Wells and Geneva scores, along with D-dimer testing, are key tools in assessment. However, the standard D-dimer cut-off of 0.5 mg/mL, while highly sensitive, has low specificity, leading to excessive imaging. This study examines whether modifying the D-dimer threshold can improve diagnostic efficiency and reduce unnecessary testing.MethodologyThis retrospective study analyzed 878 patients who underwent computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) at Monmouth Medical Center from January 2022 to December 2023. Statistical analysis, including ROC curve assessment, was conducted using IBM SPSS, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe study cohort had a mean age of 57.2 years (±18.3), with 61.4 % female patients. PE was diagnosed in 7.7 % (n = 68) via CTPA, while 92.3 % had negative results. Dyspnea (59 %) and chest pain (33.9 %) were the most common symptoms. Patients with PE had significantly higher mean D-dimer levels (4.77 mg/mL) than those without PE (1.45 mg/mL, p < 0.001). Raising the D-dimer cut-off from 0.5 mg/mL to 0.65 mg/mL improved specificity from 12 % to 37 % while maintaining 100 % sensitivity.ConclusionThis study highlights the potential to improve the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism (PE) by adjusting the D-dimer cut-off. By increasing the cut-off, we were able to enhance specificity while maintaining sensitivity, leading to fewer unnecessary imaging tests. These results suggest that refining diagnostic thresholds can improve the accuracy of PE detection, reduce patient exposure to unnecessary procedures, and better allocate healthcare resources.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, D-dimer, CTPA, Sensitivity

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a potentially life-threatening condition characterized by the obstruction of one or more pulmonary arteries by a thrombus, often originating from the deep veins.1 The diagnosis of PE remains challenging due to its nonspecific clinical presentation, which can range from mild symptoms such as dyspnea and cough to more severe symptoms like hemodynamic instability and signs of right ventricular strain.1 Accurate and timely diagnosis is crucial for initiating appropriate treatment and reducing mortality rates.

The diagnosis of PE combines clinical assessment, lab tests, and imaging, with tools like the Wells and Geneva scores stratifying patients by risk to guide further diagnostic testing and management strategies. 1 D-dimer, a fibrin degradation product, plays a significant role in the diagnostic algorithm for PE. It has a high sensitivity but low specificity for thrombotic disorders. A negative D-dimer result in patients with low clinical probability can effectively rule out PE without the need for further imaging studies. However, a positive D-dimer result requires additional investigation, as it may be elevated in various conditions, including inflammation, infection, and malignancy.2

Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA) is the gold standard for diagnosing PE, offering high sensitivity and specificity by directly visualizing the emboli in the pulmonary arteries.3 However, CTPA is associated with potential risks that must be carefully considered. Radiation exposure, particularly in young patients and pregnant women, is a significant concern, with a single scan delivering a 10 millisievert (mSV) of effective dose, equivalent to 1–3 years of natural background radiation, potentially increasing lifetime cancer risk with repeated use.4 Additionally, the iodinated contrast media used in CTPA can cause contrast-induced nephropathy, especially in patients with pre-existing renal impairment, diabetes, or dehydration.5

D-dimer testing is a key tool for diagnosing pulmonary embolism (PE), offering high sensitivity but low specificity. The standard cut-off of 0.5 mg/mL often leads to unnecessary imaging with CTPA, exposing patients to risks and increased cost. Some studies have adjusted D-dimer thresholds, such as raising the cut-off for older patients or tailoring it to a patient's likelihood of having PE based on symptoms and history.6,7 However, research into other practical adjustments remains limited. In this study, we analyzed patients who underwent CTPA to rule out PE, assessing whether modifying the D-dimer cut-off could improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce unnecessary imaging.

Methodology

2.1. Study design, population, and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at Monmouth Medical Center collecting data from patients who underwent CTPA to rule out pulmonary embolism between January 2022 until December 2023. The study was approved by the Monmouth Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB). Furthermore, we were adherent to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and rules of Health insurance portability accountability act 1996 (HIPAA) to ensure patient privacy.

2.2. Sample size

The minimum required sample size was calculated using Cochran's formula with a 95 % confidence level and a 5 % margin of error, along with an estimated proportion of 0.5. This resulted in a target sample size of 385 participants. Data was collected from 878 patients, exceeding the minimum sample size requirement.

2.3. Study variables and measurements

Data was collected from patient medical records, including demographics (age, gender), medical history (diabetes, myocardial infarction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), presenting symptoms (dyspnea, chest pain, cough, hemoptysis), laboratory and imaging findings (D-dimer levels, CTPA results), and clinical risk stratification data required to calculate the Geneva score.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS. Qualitative variables were calculated as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were shown as medians and interquartile ranges. To assess the diagnostic performance of different D-dimer cut-offs, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted. The sensitivity and specificity of each D-dimer threshold were calculated, and the optimal cut-off value was determined based on the highest combined sensitivity and specificity. A p-value of <0.05 was considered as significant.

Overall, 137 (15.6 %) of patients had diabetes, 104 (11.8 %) had a history of myocardial infarction (MI), and 163 (18.6 %) had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Dyspnea was the most common chief complaint, reported by 518 patients (59 %), followed by chest pain in 298 cases (33.9 %) and cough in 154 cases (17.5 %). Hemoptysis occurred in 19 patients (2.2 %).

The mean D-dimer level for the entire cohort was 1.81 mg/mL. Patients who tested positive for PE had a significantly higher mean D-dimer level (4.77 mg/mL) compared to those who tested negative (1.45 mg/mL) (p < 0.001). Among patients with confirmed PE, the lowest recorded D-dimer value was 0.65 mg/L.

Based on Geneva score, 1.4 % of patients were categorized as high risk, 60.8 % as intermediate risk, and 37.6 % as low risk. Patients with PE were classified as intermediate risk (72.1 %), while 20.6 % fell into the low-risk category and 7.4 % into the high-risk group. Among those without PE, the majority (59.9 %) were intermediate risk, with 39 % in the low-risk category and less than 1 % in the high-risk group.

Discussion

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a serious and potentially fatal condition, requiring a balance between timely diagnosis and reducing unwarranted interventions. While CTPA is the definitive diagnostic tool for PE, its overuse raises concerns about patient safety, healthcare costs, and resource allocation. Our study suggests a potential to refine the PE diagnostic process by adjusting D-dimer cut-off values to improve specificity while maintaining sensitivity, ultimately reducing reliance on CTPA scans and minimizing the unnecessary imaging and its associated risks.

PE can present with a wide range of symptoms, some more subtle than others, which often overlap with other conditions. Dyspnea was the most frequently reported symptoms in this study, consistent with its role as a key feature of PE, followed by pleuritic chest pain and cough.1 Less commonly, symptoms such as hemoptysis and syncope indicated more severe cases with greater embolic burden. This variability in presentation highlights the challenges of diagnosing PE based on symptoms alone, which is why tools like the Geneva and Wells score are vital.

The geneva score provides a systematic and practical approach to evaluating the probability of PE, categorizing patients into low, intermediate, or high-risk groups based on factors like age, heart rate, recent surgery, and signs of deep vein thrombosis. 8 In our study, the Geneva score demonstrated its effectiveness in stratifying patients, patients with confirmed PE had a higher mean Geneva score (5.52) compared to those without PE (4.36). Among patients with PE, 7.4 % were classified as high risk, and 72.1 % fell into the intermediate-risk category. In comparison, less than 1% of patients without PE were classified as high risk, while 59.9 % were placed in the intermediate-risk category. The large proportion of intermediate-risk patients undergoing CTPA in our study highlights a need for a more deliberate approach to imaging decisions. Greater reliance on the Geneva score combined with D-dimer testing could help better refine risk assessment, ensuring that imaging is reserved for patients with a higher probability of PE.

CTPA is widely used in emergency departments, but its diagnostic yield for PE can be as low as 3 % in some settings, indicating frequent overuse in cases where it provides limited diagnostic value.9 In our study, 330 patients with a low Geneva score underwent CTPA, despite guidelines suggesting that such patients could often be managed without imaging when combined with appropriate D-dimer thresholds.10 This overuse not only strains resources but also exposes patients to risks such as radiation, contrast-induced nephropathy, and incidental findings, which can lead to unnecessary anticoagulation for false positives.11 These findings show the need for careful evaluation of clinical probability and initial non-invasive tests to ensure CTPA is reserved for cases where the benefits outweigh the risks.

The current standard D-dimer cut-off value of 0.5 mg/L, while highly sensitive, has limited specificity, often leading to false-positive results, particularly in those with comorbid conditions such as inflammation, malignancy, or recent surgery.12 These elevated levels, attributable to processes unrelated to PE, create diagnostic challenges and contribute to unnecessary imaging and radiation.

In our study a significant difference in mean D-dimer levels was observed between patients with positive CTPA results those with negative results (p < 0.001). Specifically, the lowers D-dimer observed in patients with confirmed PE was 0.65 mg/L. Increasing the cut-off from 0.5 mg/L to 0.65 mg/L improved specificity from 12 % to 37 % while preserving sensitivity at 100%. Other research has shown that thresholds as high as 0.9 mg/L can achieve a sensitivity of 97 % and reduce CTPA utilization by 27 %.13

It is important to recognize that while clinical scoring systems, D-dimer and imaging play key roles in diagnosing PE, they do not replace the need for clinical judgment. Clinical assessment is essential to interpret these alongside the patient's presentation. Even in low-risk patients, additional testing or imaging may still be necessary if clinical suspicion is high. Our study suggests that modestly increasing the D-dimer cut-off value may help reduce unnecessary imaging while maintaining diagnostic accuracy, but this must be paired with thoughtful bedside assessment to ensure patient safety.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the value of revisiting established diagnostic thresholds, offering a pathway to more precise and efficient care for patients with suspected pulmonary embolism (PE). By raising the D-dimer cut-off we maintained the test's sensitivity while improving specificity, reducing the need for unnecessary imaging. This change helps limit patient exposure to radiation and contrast while easing the demand on healthcare resources. These findings support the potential for more precise diagnostic strategies. Adjusting D-dimer thresholds represents a significant step toward balancing diagnostic accuracy with patient safety, paving the way for a more effective and patient-centered approach to managing PE.

Limitations

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged, including its single-center retrospective design and relatively small sample size of 878 patients with 68 diagnosed with pulmonary embolism, which might affect the generalizability of our findings. Restricting the analysis to patients who underwent CTPA introduces a potential selection bias by excluding those managed without imaging. Additionally, our study population was predominantly white and Hispanic, which could affect the generalizability of our results to more diverse populations.
