Optimal analgesia after laparoscopic total hysterectomy with pre-pneumoperitoneum phrenic nerve block using different ropivacaine concentrations: randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yifei Liu, Liduan Liu, Mi Zhou, Jianzhong Wang, Aihua Shu, Jianli Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-laparoscopic shoulder pain (PLSP) is common after total laparoscopic hysterectomy and can negatively affect recovery. Ultrasound-guided right phrenic nerve block is effective in preventing PLSP, but the optimal concentration of ropivacaine and the best timing for nerve block remain unclear.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 198 patients undergoing laparoscopic total hysterectomy were allocated into six groups (A1-A3: pre-pneumoperitoneum PNB; B1-B3: PNB at skin closure; n = 33 each), receiving 6 mL of 0.25%, 0.3%, or 0.375% ropivacaine. The primary outcomes were the incidence of PLSP within 72 h and visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores at 0.5 h (T1), 6 h (T2), 24 h (T3), 48 h (T4), and 72 h (T5). Secondary outcomes included rescue analgesic use and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Multiple comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected (significance: adjusted P < 0.05).ResultsBaseline characteristics, including age, Body Mass Index (BMI) and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, were comparable among all groups (P > 0.05). The incidence of PLSP was lowest in group A3 (pre-pneumoperitoneum, 0.375% ropivacaine) at 6.1% (2 of 33), significantly lower than in group A1 (45.5%, χ² = 13.390, P < 0.001; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.003) and group A2 (30.3%, χ² = 6.519, P = 0.011; adjusted P = 0.033). In group B3 (skin closure, 0.375% ropivacaine), the PLSP rate was 24.2% (8 of 33), showing marginal significance compared to B1 and B2 (adjusted P = 0.051). At 24 h (T3), VAS scores in A3 were significantly lower than in A1, B1, and B2 (P < 0.05; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.10 with three comparisons). At 48 h (T4), scores in A3 remained significantly lower than in B1 and B2 (P < 0.05; adjusted P = 0.10 with two comparisons). No significant differences were observed at T1, T2, or T5 (P > 0.05). Safety outcomes showed no significant differences in intraoperative drug use, rescue analgesic requirement, or PONV incidence (P > 0.05), and no local anesthetic toxicity or serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionPre-pneumoperitoneum ultrasound-guided right phrenic nerve block with 0.375% ropivacaine reduced PLSP incidence to 6.1% (adjusted P < 0.05), improved 24–48 h pain control (trend after adjustment), and was well tolerated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03391-6.Keywords: Phrenic nerve block, Post-laparoscopic shoulder pain, Ropivacaine, Randomized controlled trial, Ultrasound-guided, Pain management

Introduction

Laparoscopic surgery has become a cornerstone in contemporary surgical practice due to its minimal invasiveness, reduced blood loss, and accelerated postoperative recovery. Its application spans multiple surgical fields, including urology, gynecology, and general surgery [1–3]. The technique minimizes tissue trauma, shortens hospitalization, and enhances both safety and procedural efficiency, fully aligning with enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols [4–8].

Despite these advantages, several complications can arise postoperatively, notably shoulder pain, injuries to the urinary, intestinal, and vascular systems, gas embolism, and subcutaneous emphysema [9–12]. Among these, post-laparoscopic shoulder pain (PLSP) presents a significant challenge, frequently diminishing patient comfort and hindering optimal postoperative recovery [13, 14]. Reported PLSP incidence ranges from 35% to 80%, with right-sided predominance and variable severity, which can undermine ERAS outcomes [14–18].

Ultrasound-guided right phrenic nerve block has been validated as an effective strategy for PLSP, with ropivacaine being the preferred local anesthetic [19, 20]. Under real-time imaging, precise deposition of ropivacaine along the phrenic nerve can interrupt nociceptive transmission and mitigate PLSP [21]. The primary challenge lies in achieving selective sensory blockade while minimizing motor involvement that may compromise diaphragmatic function. Given that the phrenic nerve is the sole motor supply to the diaphragm, its blockade inherently carries the risk of respiratory impairment.

Current evidence suggests that optimizing drug concentration and volume may reduce diaphragmatic dysfunction. In brachial plexus blocks, 10 mL of 0.25% levobupivacaine resulted in significantly lower rates of diaphragmatic paralysis compared to 20 mL (ultrasound-diagnosed: 10% vs. 33%; pulmonary impairment: 5% vs. 20%) without compromising analgesia [22]. Drug spread is also influenced by injection site; with 25 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine, the incidence of phrenic nerve palsy was lower in the retroclavicular approach (15%) than the supraclavicular approach (70%) [23]. These findings support the potential of high-concentration, low-volume strategies for achieving relative sensory selectivity. However, existing studies are largely limited to concentrations between 0.25% and 0.5%, with no systematic exploration of broader concentration ranges [22–24].

Studies of other local anesthetics provide insight into dose-response relationships. The minimum effective volume (ED50 = 2.646 mL; ED95 = 3.043 mL) of 0.5% bupivacaine has been established, with ≤ 6 mL avoiding diaphragmatic paralysis [24]. Phrenic nerve infiltration using 10 mL of 2% lidocaine has been shown to significantly reduce post-thoracotomy shoulder pain compared with suprascapular nerve block (0.5% bupivacaine, 10 mL; 37% vs. 70%) [25]. Although lidocaine is suitable for short-duration procedures due to rapid onset, ropivacaine offers longer action and reduced motor toxicity, yet its diaphragmatic safety profile remains to be fully characterized.

Critical gaps remain. Ropivacaine concentration ranges are insufficiently covered, with no systematic evaluation of extreme concentrations such as 0.2% or 0.75%. No dose-response model has been established. The dose-dependent impact of low-volume administration on diaphragmatic impairment remains unclear. The protective effect of combining ropivacaine with adjuvants such as dexamethasone has not been verified [22–24]. Addressing these uncertainties requires a quantitative framework to evaluate the risk–benefit balance between analgesic efficacy and diaphragmatic dysfunction.

This randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate the preventive efficacy of varying ropivacaine concentrations and block timing under ultrasound guidance on PLSP following total laparoscopic hysterectomy. The objective was to identify an optimized regimen that maximizes PLSP prevention while preserving clinically acceptable diaphragmatic function. By establishing dose-response curves and safety thresholds, the study aims to advance postoperative analgesia toward greater precision and risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This randomized, blinded, controlled trial was conducted at First People’s Hospital of Yichang (The People’s Hospital of China Three Gorges University), approved by its Ethics Committee on December 12, 2022 (PJ-KY2022-35), and prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300073162). Patients were recruited from January 2023 to September 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a thorough explanation of the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. Participation was entirely voluntary. The study was conducted in strict accordance with research ethics guidelines, with particular emphasis on the protection of participant rights. Strict data confidentiality measures were implemented to ensure privacy and data security. All participants retained the right to withdraw from the study at any time without discrimination or negative consequences.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: female patients aged 45 to 65 years, scheduled to undergo total laparoscopic hysterectomy, classified as ASA physical status I or II, and with a body weight between 50 and 80 kg.

Exclusion criteria included: uncontrolled or untreated major systemic diseases (such as hypertension, heart disease, or diabetes); history of neck surgery or shoulder pain-related diseases; severe hepatic, renal, or pulmonary dysfunction; long-term use of analgesics, sedatives, or anticoagulant/antiplatelet agents (e.g., warfarin, DOACs, clopidogrel); known coagulopathy (INR > 1.4 or platelet count < 100 × 109/L); and inability to comply with the study protocol.

Grouping and interventions

A total of 198 eligible patients were prospectively enrolled following preoperative evaluation by the anesthesia and surgical teams. The 198 participants were randomly assigned, using a random number table, into six groups (A1, A2, A3, B1, B2, B3), with 33 patients in each group (Fig. 1). Allocation information was sealed in opaque envelopes by an independent investigator and revealed by the circulating nurse after anesthesia induction to inform the attending anesthesiologist.

Randomized study flowchart for laparoscopic total hysterectomy

All patients underwent ultrasound-guided right phrenic nerve block. The procedure was performed with the patient in the supine position and the head slightly turned to the left. A high-frequency linear probe was used to identify anatomical structures, including the anterior scalene muscle (ASM) and middle scalene muscle (MSM). After skin disinfection, the phrenic nerve was located on the surface of the anterior scalene muscle at the level of the sixth cervical vertebra. Using an in-plane technique, a 25-G, 6-cm needle was advanced laterally towards the phrenic nerve (adjacent to the brachial plexus). Upon confirming the needle tip position, a syringe with an extension tube was connected, and 6 mL of the study solution was injected slowly (Fig. 2).

Illustration of injection procedure. Note: ASM, anterior scalene muscle; MSM, medial scalene muscle; → brachial plexus; →▲ phrenic nerve

The timing of the block and the concentration of ropivacaine were as follows: In groups A1, A2, and A3, phrenic nerve block was performed after anesthesia induction and before CO2 pneumoperitoneum, using 0.25%, 0.3%, and 0.375% ropivacaine, respectively. In groups B1, B2, and B3, the block was performed at the end of surgery (during skin closure), with the same respective concentrations of ropivacaine. The same experienced anesthesiologist performed all blocks. For groups A1 to A3, CO2 pneumoperitoneum was established 20 min after the block (i.e., 20 min after the injection, when the block had fully taken effect), with intra-abdominal pressure maintained at 12–13 mmHg. The entire procedure was performed using a no-touch technique. Skin antisepsis was achieved with an ethanol solution containing 2% chlorhexidine gluconate. The ultrasound probe was covered with a sterile disposable sheath, and the operator wore sterile gloves and used sterile coupling gel [26].

Anesthesia protocol

All procedures were performed by the same experienced anesthetist under ultrasound guidance. The patient was positioned supine with a slight leftward head turn. A high-frequency linear array probe was used to scan the anatomical structure and plan the needle path. Following skin antisepsis, the target neural structure was identified under ultrasound guidance. After drug preparation, a 25-gauge, 6-cm needle was inserted in-plane from the lateral side and advanced toward the phrenic nerve. The syringe was connected via an extension line, and 6 mL of ropivacaine, prepared at rigorously controlled concentrations, was slowly administered (Fig. 2). Concentration selection was based on prior evidence indicating that 0.25% represents the lower threshold for diaphragmatic paresis risk [22].

In groups A1, A2, and A3, the artificial CO2 pneumoperitoneum was established 20 min after the full effect of ropivacaine, maintaining an intra-abdominal pressure of 12–13 mmHg. Patients fasted from the day before surgery. Peripheral venous access was established upon entering the operating room, and blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and an electrocardiogram were monitored. Anesthetic induction was achieved with intravenous midazolam (0.075 mg/kg), propofol (2 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). Mechanical ventilation was initiated post-intubation, with a tidal volume of 8 mL/kg, a ventilation rate of 12 breaths per minute, an I: E ratio of 1:2, and an inspired oxygen concentration of 100%. During surgery, propofol (4–5 mg/kg/h), remifentanil (0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min), and dexamethasone (0.06 mg/kg/h) were administered intravenously. End-tidal CO2 pressure was maintained at 35–45 mmHg, and systolic blood pressure and heart rate fluctuations did not exceed 20% of baseline values. Crystalloid and colloid fluids were administered intravenously during surgery. Dexamethasone administration ceased 30 min before the end of surgery. Flurbiprofen axetil (50 mg) was administered intravenously 10 min before surgery for analgesia, and propofol and remifentanil were discontinued postoperatively. Flumazenil (50 mg), atropine (0.01 mg/kg), and neostigmine (0.02 mg/kg) were administered intravenously to reverse anesthesia. Postoperatively, patients received rescue analgesia with tramadol (30 mg) when the visual analog scale (VAS) score for abdominal incision pain reached 4 or higher.

Postoperative Follow-up and outcome measures

Detailed follow-ups were conducted on the six patient groups at postoperative intervals of 0.5 h (T1), 6 h (T2), 24 h (T3), 48 h (T4), and 72 h (T5). Each participant completed all five repeated measurements. Primary outcomes included the incidence and severity of PLSP at each time point, typically assessed using the VAS or Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Pain intensity was measured using the VAS. Respiratory rate (RR) and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) were recorded every 30 min during the first 6 h postoperatively, with RR > 20 breaths/min or SpO2 < 94% defined as abnormal thresholds [26]. Baseline characteristics included age, body mass index (BMI), and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification. Intraoperative parameters included pneumoperitoneum duration, sufentanil dosage, and propofol dosage. Postoperative variables included extubation time, rescue analgesia rate, incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and any episodes of persistent dyspnea or oxygen therapy requirement.

Sample size estimation and statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. A mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) was planned to evaluate the effects of intervention timing, group allocation, and their interaction. The statistical model was based on an F-test designed for a 6-group × 5-time-point repeated-measures ANOVA, accounting for both between- and within-subject factors. The correlation between repeated measures within each subject was assumed, and sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test. When the sphericity assumption was violated (P < 0.05), degrees of freedom were adjusted using the Greenhouse–Geisser correction. All tests were two-tailed with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Due to the absence of pilot data, a conservative medium effect size (f = 0.25) was adopted based on prior studies investigating the effect of regional nerve blocks on VAS scores [23], consistent with Cohen’s definition of a medium effect. The statistical power (1-β) was set at 80%, and α at 0.05. Based on these parameters, the minimum sample size was calculated as 180 patients (30 per group). To compensate for potential data loss (e.g., dropout, protocol deviation), an additional 10% (18 patients) was included, resulting in a final sample size of 198 patients (33 per group). This adjustment aligns with the CONSORT guidelines for sample size planning in randomized trials [27].

Clinical data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t-tests (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (multiple groups); for non-normal distributions, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and compared using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test.

For multiple comparisons, both raw and Bonferroni-adjusted P-values were reported. Bonferroni correction was applied by multiplying the raw P-value by the number of comparisons (k), i.e., adjusted P = raw P × k. A Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A two-sided P < 0.05 was regarded as indicative of statistical significance throughout all analyses.

Safety and medication use after laparoscopic total hysterectomy

No severe adverse events related to local anesthetics, such as toxicity, respiratory distress, or neurovascular injury, were observed in any patient. This indicates a high safety standard across all intervention groups. In addition, no significant differences were found among the six groups in intraoperative propofol and sufentanil consumption or in the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (all P > 0.05; Table 6; Fig. 5). These findings suggest consistency in medication use and PONV incidence among groups, reflecting the standardized and effective management of anesthesia and surgery throughout the study.

Discussion

PLSP typically develops 12 to 24 h after surgery and can persist for an extended period, significantly impacting postoperative recovery and patient quality of life [15, 16, 28, 29]. Interventions targeting PLSP must carefully account for its potential compensatory risk, given that the phrenic nerve is the sole motor supply to the diaphragm, its blockade inevitably impairs diaphragmatic function. Although management of PLSP is crucial in postoperative care, it is often overlooked by anesthesiologists, who tend to focus predominantly on incisional abdominal pain [29, 30]. Currently, there is a paucity of research on the use of nerve block techniques for preventing post-laparoscopic shoulder pain, and no consensus has been established on how to optimize local anesthetic dose and concentration to balance analgesic efficacy with diaphragmatic safety [31, 32]. The present study aimed to investigate the effects of varying ropivacaine concentrations and different timing strategies on the prevention of PLSP.

This study demonstrates that ultrasound-guided right phrenic nerve block with 0.375% ropivacaine administered before pneumoperitoneum significantly reduces the incidence and severity of PLSP following total laparoscopic hysterectomy. It provides optimal analgesia at 24 and 48 h postoperatively without causing respiratory impairment or other serious adverse events, confirming the safety of low-concentration ropivacaine. These findings align with previous studies showing that preoperative phrenic nerve block effectively prevents PLSP without compromising respiratory function [19]. Lower concentrations of ropivacaine (0.1%−0.2%) have been associated with less pulmonary impact but shorter analgesic duration, indicating the need to balance efficacy and safety [33]. As a moderate concentration, 0.375% ropivacaine offers both effective pain control and favorable safety [34]. Overall, careful selection of anesthetic concentration and timing is essential for minimizing PLSP while maintaining respiratory safety, supporting its clinical value in individualized postoperative pain management for laparoscopic surgery.

Recent studies have emphasized the importance of precisely adjusting local anesthetic dosage and concentration to optimize regional block efficacy and safety [35, 36]. While earlier literature has primarily focused on the risk of diaphragmatic paralysis associated with high-volume or high-concentration regimens, data also indicate that low-volume, high-concentration ropivacaine enables more precise targeting of the C5-C6 nerve roots, limiting cephalad spread to the cervical plexus and reducing complications [37, 38]. Building on these findings, the present study further establishes that ultrasound-guided phrenic nerve block with 0.375% ropivacaine (5 ml) not only effectively reduces the incidence of PLSP but also minimizes the risk of diaphragmatic paralysis, thus filling an important gap in dosing optimization. Furthermore, while the transversus abdominis plane block commonly utilizes 0.25% local anesthetic [20, 39], our results indicate that higher concentrations are required for phrenic nerve block to achieve optimal effects, thereby expanding the current pharmacologic strategy for regional anesthesia.

Multimodal analgesic management, including dexmedetomidine, flurbiprofen axetil, and TAPB, has been shown to facilitate postoperative neurologic and gastrointestinal recovery while reducing opioid consumption [39, 40]. Similarly, the pre-pneumoperitoneum phrenic nerve block adopted in this study contributed to improved postoperative outcomes, underscoring the critical role of preemptive blockade of nociceptive signaling in optimizing postoperative recovery.

Regarding the safety profile of ropivacaine, previous in vitro and clinical studies have demonstrated a higher threshold for central nervous system toxicity and less pronounced cardiac effects compared to bupivacaine [41]. No patients in our cohort exhibited signs of local anesthetic toxicity or other severe adverse events, providing additional evidence to support the perioperative use of ropivacaine in laparoscopic surgery.

The pathophysiology of PLSP primarily involves direct stimulation of the phrenic nerve and diaphragmatic stretch injury caused by CO2 pneumoperitoneum [5, 42]. In this study, phrenic nerve block performed before pneumoperitoneum effectively interrupted intraoperative nociceptive signaling, attenuated CO2-induced neurogenic inflammation, including reductions in substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide release, and consequently disrupted the cascade leading to PLSP. The prolonged effect of 0.375% ropivacaine was sufficient to cover the acute postoperative inflammatory phase, which likely explains the consistently lower VAS scores observed in the A3 group at 24–48 h, consistent with the neurogenic nature of PLSP [29].

The combination of precise dosing and anatomical targeting in this study also supports the physiological basis for the observed efficacy. Low-volume, high-concentration ropivacaine enables selective deposition at the C5–C6 nerve roots, achieving an effective phrenic nerve block while minimizing cephalad spread and the risk of diaphragmatic paralysis [37, 38]. Unlike the TAP block, which primarily targets sensory nerves, the phrenic nerve contains a high proportion of motor fibers, necessitating a higher anesthetic concentration for optimal blockade [20, 39]. In addition, ultrasound-guided phrenic nerve block enables precise, selective interruption of C4 nerve transmission, offering a clear advantage in PLSP prevention without interfering with incisional pain management [19]. Conventional approaches, such as ventilatory parameter adjustments, are less effective in PLSP prevention [19], highlighting the mechanistic superiority of individualized, targeted nerve block strategies. The neurogenic mechanism of PLSP, particularly CO2 pneumoperitoneum-induced neurogenic inflammation, has been thoroughly reviewed in the literature [43], providing further support for the phrenic nerve as a rational target for intervention.

Despite the positive results of the study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively limited and restricted to patients undergoing total laparoscopic hysterectomy, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, the absence of diaphragmatic mobility assessment before and after the phrenic nerve block limits the ability to accurately quantify the extent of block-related diaphragmatic paralysis, making it difficult to fully confirm the success rate and effectiveness of the block. Additionally, the exclusion of patients over 65 years limits the applicability of these conclusions to older populations. Individual differences in ropivacaine sensitivity and other perioperative pain management variables were also not addressed, which may have influenced the interpretation and application of results.

Further studies involving larger and more diverse patient cohorts across a range of surgical procedures are necessary to confirm the generalizability and stability of these results. Objective assessment of diaphragmatic function before and after phrenic nerve block is recommended to ensure effective implementation of the technique. Furthermore, investigating inter-individual differences in ropivacaine sensitivity and tailoring interventions accordingly may allow for more personalized pain management. Finally, exploring the use of adjunctive agents or additional block techniques in combination with ropivacaine could provide further options for postoperative pain control, especially for patients with refractory PLSP. These efforts may ultimately contribute to improved outcomes in postoperative pain management and enhanced patient recovery.

Conclusion

This study focused on evaluating the preventive efficacy of ultrasound-guided phrenic nerve block with different concentrations of ropivacaine on PLSP (Fig. 6). The findings indicate that administration of 0.375% ropivacaine prior to pneumoperitoneum significantly reduced the incidence of PLSP after total laparoscopic hysterectomy (to as low as 6.1%) and effectively alleviated mid- to late-stage postoperative pain (at 24–48 h), without an increased risk of local anesthetic-related adverse events. This approach, which interrupts phrenic nerve stimulation induced by CO2 pneumoperitoneum, provides a time-efficient and safe strategy for PLSP prevention. Future studies are warranted to validate the generalizability of this protocol in broader patient populations, including elderly individuals and those undergoing various types of laparoscopic surgery, and to explore individualized dosing regimens based on pharmacokinetic considerations for optimized postoperative pain management.
