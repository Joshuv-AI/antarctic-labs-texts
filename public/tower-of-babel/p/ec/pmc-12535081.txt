CGRP-targeted therapy fulfilling the treatment gap in medication-underuse setting: a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary headache center in Thailand, a lower-middle-income country
Authors: Sirawit Roongrojwittayakul, Prakit Anukoolwittaya, Akarin Hiransuthikul, Thanakit Pongpitakmetha, Sekh Thanprasertsuk, Wanakorn Rattanawong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigraine preventive treatment is essential to reducing the burden of disease. However, discontinuation of oral migraine preventive medications (OMPMs) remains common due to suboptimal efficacy and tolerability, especially in lower-middle-income countries, which may contribute to migraine progression—a situation that has led to the introduction of the concept of “Medication Underuse Headache”. The calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibody (CGRP mAbs) might close the gap in this situation. This study aimed to investigate the treatment patterns of migraine preventive medications over six months at a tertiary headache center in Thailand and to explore the association between each preventive medication class and discontinuation rates.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted between 2021 and 2023. Adult patients who were diagnosed with migraine and received at least one preventive medication at their first visit to the Headache Clinic at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital were included, with a minimum follow-up period of six months. The primary outcome was the discontinuation of any migraine preventive medication from the baseline regimen during follow-up visits. A multivariable Cox regression model, adjusted for sex, age, and diagnosis, was used to explore baseline factors associated with the discontinuation of preventive migraine medications.ResultsAmong the 100 eligible patients (mean age [SD]: 39.6 [14.4] years; 87.0% female), 41.0% (41/100) discontinued at least one preventive medication, most commonly due to intolerance to side effects (53.7%, 22/41) and lack of treatment effectiveness (34.1%, 14/41). The highest discontinuation rates were observed with serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors at 53.8% (7/13), calcium channel blockers at 37.5% (6/16), and beta-blockers at 25.6% (11/43). Notably, no patients discontinued CGRP mAbs. Patients using CGRP mAbs at baseline had a significantly higher rate of discontinuing at least one other oral preventive medication compared to those who did not use CGRP mAbs: 31.7% vs. 25.4% at month 3, and 55.4% vs. 33.4% at month 6 (p = 0.04). After adjustment, baseline use of CGRP mAbs was significantly associated with an increased risk of discontinuing at least one medication in the regimen (adjusted odds ratio 2.16; 95% CI: 1.16 to 4.03, p = 0.02).ConclusionDiscontinuation of preventive migraine treatment remains a significant issue in Thailand. CGRP mAbs were associated with higher treatment persistence and may help reduce polypharmacy in migraine management.

Background

Migraine is a prevalent neurological disorder, ranking third globally and second in Southeast Asia in terms of disease burden, as reported by the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021 [1]. Treatment strategies for migraine are broadly categorized into two types: acute treatment and preventive treatment. Preventive treatment is essential for individuals who experience frequent attacks that substantially disrupt daily functioning. According to the 2024 International Headache Society (IHS) guidelines [2], preventive treatment should be considered in patients who meet one or more of the following criteria: experiencing four or more monthly headache days; reporting significant personal, social, or occupational impairment due to migraine; achieving inadequate relief from optimized acute therapy; or requiring frequent use of acute medications to manage attacks. Effective preventive treatment should be maintained for a minimum duration of 6 to 12 months for achieving the better control of migraine burden.

In recent years, therapies targeting the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway—specifically, CGRP monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and gepants—have emerged as a novel and specific mechanism-based approach for migraine prevention. These agents have been approved for the preventive treatment of migraine by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and various other regulatory authorities worldwide even in the lower-middle income countries (LMICs) setting, including Thailand [3, 4]. However, in countries where CGRP-targeted therapies are not yet available, the standard of care (SoC) for migraine prevention has relied on oral migraine preventive medications (OMPMs), such as beta-blockers (BBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), antiseizure medications (ASMs), serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs). These medications were not specifically developed for migraine, and many of them have shown only limited or inconclusive evidence regarding their efficacy and safety in migraine prevention. According to data from the International Burden of Migraine Study II (IBMS-II) [5], discontinuation of one or more preventive medications was reported by 24.0% of patients with episodic migraine (EM) and 40.8% of those with chronic migraine (CM), with the most commonly cited reasons being lack of efficacy and adverse side effects. The concept suggests that the inefficacy and intolerability of preventive medications could potentially contribute to migraine progression, a condition referred to as medication underuse headache (MUH) [6].

Due to poor adherence and persistence with OMPMs, along with the availability of CGRP-targeted therapies in Thailand—LMIC—there remains a significant treatment gap [7]. Therefore, this study presents a retrospective analysis involving patients at the tertiary university-based headache clinic in Thailand, focusing on treatment patterns of migraine preventive medications over a six-month period. The primary objective of this study is to assess the prevalence of drug discontinuation within six months after the initial prescription. The secondary objectives are to explore the association between each drug class and discontinuation rates and evaluate the impact of treatment on the number of headache days in the era of emerging CGRP-targeted therapies.

Method

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted on migraine patients who attended the Comprehensive Headache and Orofacial Pain (CHOP) clinic at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), The Thai Red Cross Society, Bangkok, Thailand. The clinic operates two office-hour headache clinic sessions per week, staffed by four neurologists who are headache specialists or have a special interest in headache, and serves an estimated total of over 2,500 visits annually. Our headache clinic provided the CGRP mAbs service, which includes both subcutaneous and intravenous injections, as well as an interventional clinic for headache patients requiring interventions such as onabotulinumtoxin A injection and peripheral nerve blocks. Most patients are referred from other specialists within our hospital and from other public government hospitals across Thailand.

For this study, we performed an electronic medical record (EMR) review of migraine patients who attended the headache clinic at KCMH between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023. Given that the first CGRP mAb, erenumab, was approved by the Thai Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2019–2020 and by KCMH hospital in 2020–2021, 2021 was selected as the start of enrollment.

Study participants

Patients were recruited at their first visit to the headache clinic between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023, and had been diagnosed with migraine. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age over 18 years, a diagnosis of migraine based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) criteria during the study period [8], prescription of at least one preventive medication at the first visit, and continuous follow-up for a minimum of six months. Exclusion criteria included loss to follow-up for more than three consecutive months or having fewer than three total follow-up visits.

Clinical evaluation and outcomes

At the first visit, we collected data on demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and headache characteristics, including the total duration of headache history, baseline number of migraine headache days, headache features, history of medication-overuse headache (MOH), and the definite migraine subtype diagnosis according to the ICHD-3 criteria. We also recorded the history of medication use—specifically, the frequency of acute medication use, as well as both prior and current preventive treatments. At each follow-up visit, data were recorded from the headache diary, including the number of monthly headache days (MHDs), headache severity, total number of abortive pills used per month, and prescribed medications. All headache diaries were reviewed by primary physicians at each visit and uploaded into the EMR. All headache specialists in our clinic used a structured, organized, and pre-planned system for documenting headache patients in each visit.

The primary objective was to describe and characterize treatment patterns of prescribed migraine preventive medications, including both OMPMs and CGRP-based therapy, over a 6-month study period. We aimed to illustrate the proportion of migraine patients who discontinued their preventive medication within six months after initiation, as well as the discontinuation rate of each specific preventive drug within six months of enrollment. The secondary objectives were to determine factors associated with the discontinuation of migraine preventive medications. Additionally, the number of headache days was analyzed over a continuous 6-month follow-up period after enrollment to assess the effectiveness of migraine treatment. A subgroup analysis was also conducted based on migraine type (episodic migraine [EM] vs. chronic migraine [CM]) and treatment regimens containing CGRP mAbs versus those without CGRP mAbs.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board Research Ethics Committee (IRBREC) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand and KCMH in October 2023 (IRB number 1156/2024). The approval of retrieving retrospective data from EMR was made and informed consent was waived by IRBREC. All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

Clinical and demographic data of all patients were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Comparisons between patients with CM and EM were performed using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, and an independent t-test for continuous data. Treatment patterns of migraine preventive drug classes over a six-month period were illustrated using a Sankey diagram. Kaplan-Meier plots were used to show the cumulative incidence of discontinuation of migraine preventive medications, with the log-rank test used to compare the two groups: CM vs. EM, and those with vs. without CGRP mAbs in their baseline regimen.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for sex and age, was used to explore baseline diagnosis as a potential risk factor for discontinuation. Baseline diagnosis was included as a covariate in an additional model to explore whether the use of the CGRP mAbs baseline was associated with discontinuation. Statistical significance was defined as a p of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Participants characteristics

Among the 100 enrolled patients (mean age [SD]: 39.6 [14.4] years; and 87.0% female), 51.0% were diagnosed with EM and 49.0% with CM. A history of MOH was presented in 56 patients (56.0%), and 15 patients (15.0%) had a history of aura. At baseline, the mean (SD) number of monthly headache days was 13.6 (8.0), and the mean number of abortive pills used per month was 17.3 (17.3). The most common comorbidities were myofascial pain syndrome (29.0%), major depressive disorder (15.0%), and anxiety disorder (6.0%) (Table 1).

Compared to those diagnosed with EM, patients with CM had a significantly longer duration of headache history (11.1 [9.1] vs. 5.5 [4.9] years, p < 0.001), a higher proportion of those with MOH history (87.8% vs. 25.5%, p < 0.001), and greater numbers of monthly headache days (18.8 [7.7] vs. 8.6 [4.1] days, p < 0.001) and abortive medication use (24.8 [21.6] vs. 10.3 [6.6] pills per month, p < 0.001).

Treatment pattern

After six months of follow-up, 41.0% (41/100) of patients discontinued at least one medication. When analyzed by medication class, the discontinuation rates were observed with SNRIs at 53.8% (7/13), CCBs at 37.5% (6/16), BBs at 25.6% (11/43), ASMs at 25.0% (7/28), and TCAs at 14.7% (5/34) (Fig. 1). Notably, no patients discontinued CGRP mAbs. The most common reasons for discontinuing preventive treatment were intolerance to side effects (53.7%, 22/41) and lack of treatment effectiveness (34.1%, 14/41). Other reasons included planning for pregnancy and improvement of migraine symptoms (one participant [2.4%] each). Data were missing for two participants.

Rate of discontinuation of any preventive migraine medication by baseline diagnosis

The cumulative incidence of those discontinuing at least one medication increased over time in both patients with CM and EM (Fig. 2). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that patients with EM had a significantly higher rate of discontinuing at least one medication compared to CM: 35.5% vs. 18.4% at month 3 and 51.1% vs. 30.8% at month 6 (p = 0.03). A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model showed EM was significantly associated with higher rates of discontinuing at least one medication (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.16; 95% CI: 1.10 to 4.24, p = 0.02).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective study to characterize migraine preventive treatment patterns and identify factors associated with treatment cessation in the Headache clinic in Thailand. Several key findings emerged from our analysis. First, we observed that nearly half of the patients receiving OMPMs—including SNRIs, CCBs, BBs, and ASMs—discontinued at least one of the agents within six months, mainly due to intolerance to side effects. In contrast, no patients receiving CGRP mAbs discontinued the treatment during the same period. Second, among those receiving CGRP mAbs as part of their regimen, there was a higher likelihood of discontinuing at least one concurrent preventive medication, while maintaining CGRP mAb therapy. Finally, EM demonstrated significantly higher rates of treatment discontinuation at both 3 and 6 months compared to those with CM.

Discontinuation of preventive medication remains a significant challenge in migraine management, an issue commonly referred to as medication underuse [6]. A retrospective cohort analysis using administrative claims data in the United States of America (USA) reported that up to 90% of patients with migraine were non-persistent with their initial OMPMs, with a mean time to discontinuation of approximately six months [9]. Similarly, a retrospective population-based study using an administrative health-related database in Italy found that 73.8% of patients discontinued their initial OMPMs after an average of 3.5 months [10]. Likewise, a retrospective claims database study from Canada reported that 71.1% of patients who received preventive medication were non-persistent, with 50% discontinuing treatment without switching to another preventive therapy [11]. In comparison, our data indicated that 41% of patients discontinued at least one medication.

Each OMPMs has demonstrated varying discontinuation rates across different studies. For instance, a retrospective claims analysis from the USA involving patients with CM revealed that nearly half discontinued their initial OMPMs by day 60, with TCAs associated with the highest likelihood of non-persistence [12]. Similarly, a retrospective claims database study in Canada reported that antidepressants and ASMs had high discontinuation rates, whereas ARBs and CGRP mAbs exhibited high persistence [11]. In contrast, our study found that SNRIs were the most frequently discontinued class, followed by CCBs and BBs, while CGRP mAbs were the only medication class with 100% persistence at 6 months. The high discontinuation rate of SNRIs may be attributable to their relatively greater propensity to induce adverse drug reactions (ADRs), with headache and dizziness being the most frequently reported. Apart from headache, gastrointestinal ADRs (nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and loss of appetite) and sympathetic ADRs (palpitation, agitation, higher heart rate, and higher blood pressure).). These ADRs were also common, leading to high discontinuation during the initiation phase – the first week; whereas the efficacy on mood and pain conditions is required the longer duration around 4–8 weeks, to reach full effect. On the contrary, the TCAs, a multimodal mechanism of action, could demonstrate the efficacy on mood and pain during the first few days of initiation, and the known common anti-cholinergic ADR, including drowsiness, dry mouth, dry eyes, constipation, and difficulty urination, are generally acceptable in most patients during the initiation phase. However, the discontinuation rate of TCAs might be higher in long-term use than observed period in our study due to long-term ADRs such as increased appetite, excessive weight gain, slower cognitive function, etc. The network meta-analysis comparative safety of antidepressants demonstrated a higher rate of discontinuation due to adverse events of SNRI vs. placebo than TCAs vs. placebo [13]. Moreover, evidence from previous studies indicates that the prevalence of headache as an adverse effect is higher with SNRIs than with other classes of antidepressants. This observation may, at least in part, be explained by underlying genetic susceptibility among the Asian population [14]. One of the genetic polymorphisms associated with antidepressant-related side effects is CYP2C19. Studies have shown that CYP2C19 poor metabolizers, carrying homozygous loss-of-function (LOF) alleles (*2 or *3), have higher odds of experiencing side effects (OR = 1.49), whereas ultrarapid metabolizers, carrying at least a gain-of-function allele (*17), have lower odds (OR = 0.83) compared with normal metabolizers [15]. Notably, the prevalence of carriers of the CYP2C19 LOF allele is substantially higher in the Thai and Asian populations, estimated at approximately 60%, compared to 30% in the European Caucasian population. Furthermore, within the CYP2C19 genotype distribution, the proportion of intermediate metabolizers ranges from 40% to 44% in Asians, significantly higher than the 25% observed in Europeans. Similarly, poor metabolizers constitute approximately 6% to 14% of the Asian population, in contrast to only 2% among European Caucasians [16–20]. Another genetic polymorphism that contributes to the higher adverse drug reaction (ADR) and discontinuation rates among the Asian population is CYP2D6, which has a higher rate of reduced-function alleles compared to European Caucasians (50% in Asians vs. 26% in European Caucasians). The reduced function of CYP2D6 can lead to higher levels of active metabolites of many antidepressants, including venlafaxine and amitriptyline; thus, these patients might experience a higher rate of ADRs even with low dose initiation phase [21]. These findings underline the importance of local data, which can inform medication selection tailored to each country’s patient population, which might have different drug efficacy and ADR due to different pharmacogenomic profiles.

The introduction of CGRP mAbs may help address the discontinuation of preventive therapy, as these agents are associated with fewer side effects and improved efficacy compared to OMPMs [22, 23]. Moreover, our findings suggest that CGRP mAbs can facilitate the reduction or discontinuation of other preventive medications. This is supported by network meta-analysis, which shows that OMPMs are associated with significantly higher rates of adverse events and lower efficacy compared to CGRP mAbs [24]. Consequently, in patients who start CGRP mAbs, headache frequency and severity decrease substantially, often eliminating the need to continue OMPMs, thereby allowing patients to discontinue these medications. Therefore, a treatment strategy that incorporates CGRP-targeted therapy can help minimize polypharmacy and reduce the risk of potential drug–drug interactions in migraine management. In contrast, switching between different classes of OMPMs does not effectively address discontinuation of preventive therapy; rather, it only delays the initiation of CGRP-targeted therapy. The more frequently OMPMs are switched, the higher the likelihood of treatment discontinuation and the lower the probability of achieving successful outcomes [25]. Therefore, initiating CGRP-targeted therapy as a first-line preventive treatment may be a reasonable approach, even in LMICs. Furthermore, this strategy may contribute to reducing the overall treatment cost through more effective medication [26, 27].

Our study found that patients with EM had a higher discontinuation rate of at least one drug within their preventive regimen. According to OVERCOME from European countries, the three main reasons patients who required preventive medication did not receive it were (1) the perceived efficacy of treatments other than migraine preventive medications; (2) the belief that their migraines or severe headaches were not serious enough to warrant treatment; (3) concerns about side effects [28]. These factors may help explain why patients with EM discontinued preventive medications more frequently than those with CM. The lower disease burden in EM, combined with more frequent adverse effects and a relatively better response to acute treatments, may contribute to a lower inclination to adhere to or continue preventive therapy.

Our study has several limitations. First, this study was conducted in a tertiary care setting, which may be subject to selection bias because some patients may not have been newly initiated on preventive treatment. It is possible that some patients were referred from primary care settings and may not recall, or may not be aware, that the medications they were taking were prescribed for migraine prevention. The retrospective EMR chart review might create the recall bias; however, we tried to minimize this bias by using a structured, organized, and pre-planned system for documenting headache patients. Additionally, access to care in Thailand is limited, as migraine treatment is not reimbursed. As a result, the study population likely represents individuals who are able to access treatment and may not fully reflect the general migraine population in our country. Second, we strictly required first-time patients with at least 6 months of follow-up and a minimum of three follow-up visits during 2021–2023, and we faced a certain amount of missing data, including the reasons for discontinuation, as several entries in the standard EMR record form did not include this information. Further qualitative or mixed-method studies are needed for a more in-depth evaluation of the factors contributing to treatment discontinuation. Third, the follow-up period was limited to six months, and longer-term adherence patterns remain unknown. Extended follow-up may help to provide a more comprehensive understanding of discontinuation trends over time. Finally, the relatively small sample size may not be representative of the broader migraine population in Thailand. In our country, access to migraine care remains limited because a migraine diagnosis is still associated with stigma. Many patients are unable to take time off work for follow-up visits, and insurance does not cover medical expenses under a migraine diagnosis. These factors result in a high rate of loss to follow-up. Moreover, our inclusion criteria—requiring first-visit patients with at least three follow-up visits within six months to assess discontinuation patterns—further restricted the number of patients who could be enrolled in this study.

Future directions, such as the establishment of a migraine patient registry or analyses using national administrative claims data, could provide a more robust overview of treatment patterns and outcomes at the population level. Another key aspect of our future goals is to ensure alignment with the objectives of Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG3), which aims to “ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages.” Headache disorders are a recognized global health concern, and incorporating access to effective pharmacological therapies, including CGRP-targeted therapies, is an important part of the action plan, particularly in LMICs [29]. However, access to CGRP-targeted therapies remains limited due to a lack of financial resources within public health systems and insufficient access to specialized care [30]. Our findings support that CGRP-targeted therapies can play a valuable role in improving headache care in LMICs, particularly by addressing issues related to preventive treatment discontinuation and reducing unnecessary medication use, ultimately enabling safer and more effective treatment for patients, and providing supporting evidence for advocating reimbursement of migraine treatments in LMICs, particularly in Thailand.

Conclusion

Our study highlights discontinuation of migraine preventive medication as a significant issue observed in Thailand, similar to other developed countries. Treatment with CGRP mAbs demonstrates high persistence rates and comparable efficacy to OMPMs, while potentially facilitating the discontinuation of failed preventive medications including a lack of efficacy or adverse events.
