TCR-pMHC recognition mediates target phagocytosis by T cells☆
Authors: Xiangcheng Chen, Maximilian Wang, Ning Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractT cells are traditionally viewed as non-phagocytic lymphocytes that recognize antigens via the T cell receptor (TCR) and mediate cytotoxicity at the immunological synapse, while phagocytosis is performed by professional phagocytes such as macrophages and dendritic cells. Here we show that peptide–MHC (pMHC) recognition alone is sufficient to drive rapid, antigen-specific phagocytosis by Jurkat T cells. We generated CD8+ Jurkat cells expressing a class I–restricted human TCR (SVAR16) with intermediate-to-high 2-dimensional (2D) affinity for the SARS-CoV-2 epitope HLA∗A2:01–YLQ and used a micropipette system to control and image T cell interaction with pMHC coated beads. Upon contact with cognate YLQ-coated beads, SVAR16 transduced CD8+ Jurkat cells consistently formed phagosomes within minutes and completely internalized beads with consistent kinetics. These results demonstrate that appropriately tuned TCR–pMHC interactions, supported by CD8 co-receptors, can convert a canonical CD4+ T cell line into an antigen-specific phagocyte. This work reports a novel effector function of T cells and suggests that TCR-engineering could convert CD4+ T cells into phagocytes, potentially revealing a new approach to T cell-based cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Innate immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells are classical professional phagocytes that clear apoptotic cells, destroy pathogens, and internalize particular antigens for presentation to adaptive immune cells1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Phagocytosis in these cells is mediated predominantly by receptor-ligand interactions including those by Fc receptors (FcR) and complement receptor 3 (CR3), which recognize specific properties on target surfaces6, 7, 8. Additionally, it has been shown that phagocytic behavior can shift toward trogocytosis, the engulfment of membrane fragments rather than an entire cell, under distinct adhesion conditions and be influenced by target cell cortical tension9,10.

In the adaptive immune system, T cells are key effectors that interact with target cells in an antigen-specific manner through their diverse T cell receptors (TCRs), with the specificity of the TCR–peptide–MHC (pMHC) interactions being critical for T cell function11. While CD8+ is the main driver of cytotoxicity, CD4+ T cells can also engage antigen-presenting cells via TCR–pMHC interactions or CD28 receptors to perform trogocytosis12,13. This contact-dependent behavior begins at the immunological synapse, where TCR microclusters interact with specific pMHC and drive formation of a trogosome14, 15, 16. Trogocytosis allows CD4+ T cells to acquire and present class II pMHC to other CD4+ T cells, upregulating TCR signaling17,18, and to present class I pMHC to CD8 T cells via transphagocytosis19, and skewing responses toward pro-inflammatory Th17 populations20.

Although CD4+ T cells can perform TCR-mediated trogocytosis, whether CD4+ T cells can perform antigen recognition-mediated phagocytosis is not known. This new function, if confirmed, not only expands the functional range known to T cells, but it could also be leveraged in many T cell-based immunotherapies. Earlier work showed that anti-CD3 coated beads can mediate Jurkat cells phagocytosis in a TCR signaling dependent manner16. Thus, we hypothesize that a strong TCR-pMHC interaction, especially facilitated by the expression of co-receptor CD8, should enable T cell to phagocytose pMHC coated beads.

Methods and materials

Packaging of lentivirus

HEK293T cells were seeded in 10 cm tissue-culture treated dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL (6 mL in total) with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PenStrep. On the next day, 3 μg of SVAR16 containing plasmid along with 9 μL of ViraPower Lentiviral Packaging Mix (Thermal Fisher Scientific) were mixed with 36 μL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermal Fisher Scientific) in 6 mL of Opti-MEM (Fisher Scientific) supplied with 10% FBS. After 24 h, media is exchanged to DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PenStrep for virus production. 48 h after the transfection, the supernatant is concentrated using Amicon Ultra −15 (30kDa) to 200 μL. Concentrated virus is then ready for transducing up to 5 × 106 cells.

SVAR16-transduced CD8+ Jurkat cell lines

CD8+ Jurkat cell lines are previously generated and cultured in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PenStrep. 200,000 CD8+ Jurkat cells are resuspended with 200 μL of RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PenStrep along with 50 μL of concentrated virus and 8 μg/mL Polybrene.

Phagocytosis assay

Phagocytosis assay is set-up using Biomembrane Force Probe (BFP) platform21. A SVAR16-transduced CD8+ Jurkat cell was aspirated onto a micropipette. A biotinylated RBC is aspirated on the opposing side micropipette. Biotinylated pMHC (HLA∗A2:01–YLQ and HLA∗A2:01–SL9) were provided by NIH tetramer core facility. Biotinylated pMHC were coated onto 2 μm diameter streptavidin bound glass beads. One pMHC coated bead is then placed onto the apex of the RBC. Upon start, a SVAR16-transduced CD8+ Jurkat cell is brought into contact with a pMHC coated bead and record for 10 min.

Results

MHC class I-restricted TCR transduced CD8+ Jurkat cells can rapidly perform antigen-specific phagocytosis

We used Jurkat T cells previously transduced with human CD8 co-receptors and then introduced the MHC class I–restricted TCR SVAR16 by lentiviral transduction. SVAR16 is a human TCR that specifically recognizes the SARS-CoV-2 spike–derived HLA∗A2:01–YLQ epitope22 with an intermediate-to-high 2D TCR affinity (8.4 × 10−4 μm4) measured by micropipette adhesion frequency assay23 (Fig. S1A). SVAR16 expression was uniform, with site density (SD) of 173 TCR/μm2 and >95% of cells expressing the receptor (Fig. S1B–C). Using Biomembrane Force Probe (BFP) system21, we held a red blood cell (RBC) carrying a pMHC-coated glass bead with one micropipette and an SVAR16-transduced CD8+ Jurkat T cell with the other (Fig. 1A).

Upon contact with HLA∗A2:01–YLQ beads, SVAR16-transduced CD8+ Jurkat cells formed a conjugate with the bead within seconds of contact and phagosome started to form within the first minute, then the cell vigorously started to engulf the bead. By 4 min, the bead was fully internalized, and by 6 min the cell protrusion had disappeared (Fig. 1B). In contrast, no cell-cell conjugation or phagocytosis was observed when SVAR16-transduced CD8+Jurkat cells were put in contact with beads coated with the control pMHC HLA∗A2:01–SL9 from HIV Gag during a 10-min recording (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1D). Across six SVAR16-transduced cells, all formed cell-cell conjugate and phagosomes within 3 min of contact, and three of six completed YLQ-bead internalization, whereas none internalized SL9 beads (Fig. 1C–D). For cells that completed phagocytosis, the total time required was consistently ∼3 min (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these results indicate that CD8+ Jurkat cells transduced with an MHC class I–restricted TCR are able to conduct phagocytosis in an antigen-specific manner, with relatively consistent timing for cell-cell conjugation and phagosome formation and completion of antigen-coated bead internalization.

Antigen site density tunes TCR-driven phagocytosis

Antigen density locally modulates TCR–pMHC interaction strength by increasing the number of available TCR–pMHC bonds and therefore tunes the aggregated signaling output during contact. To test whether antigen density affects T cell phagocytic potential, we modulated pMHC site density on the bead surface and generated additional YLQ-coated beads with site densities of 218, 75, and 27 pMHC/μm2 by adjusting the coating protein concentration (Fig. 2A). We then repeated the phagocytosis assay using the same SVAR16-transduced CD8+ Jurkat cells with these lower-density beads. As antigen site density decreased, not all SVAR16+ cells formed phagosomes within the 10-min contact window (3 of 6 cells when site density = 218 pMHC/μm2, 3 of 6 when = 75, and 4 of 6 when = 27 (Fig. 2B). These data indicate that reducing antigen density decreases the probability of phagosome formation, but SVAR16+ cells can still respond to relatively low antigen levels, likely because this TCR binds cognate HLA∗A2:01–YLQ with intermediate-to-high affinity. We also observed a marked delay in the onset of phagosome formation as site density decreases: the mean time to phagosome was 152 s at 218 pMHC/μm2, 342 s at 75 pMHC/μm2, and 314.5 s at 27 pMHC/μm2 (Fig. 2B–C). Finally, lowering antigen density strongly reduced the efficiency of complete engulfment: while most cells formed phagosomes at 218 pMHC/μm2, only 1 of 6 cells completed phagocytosis at this density, and no cells completed engulfment at 75 pMHC/μm2 or lower (Fig. 2D). Taken together, antigen density on the target surface is a critical parameter for TCR-driven phagocytosis: lower pMHC site density slows the response, reduces the fraction of responding cells, and essentially eliminates complete engulfment, implying that a minimum level of TCR–pMHC engagement is required for efficient bead internalization.

Discussions

We showed that Jurkat T cells engineered with an MHC class I–restricted TCR and CD8 co-receptors can phagocytose pMHC-coated particles through direct, antigen-specific TCR–pMHC engagement. Using the BFP system, these CD8+ Jurkat cells rapidly formed cell conjugation and phagosomes with cognate HLA∗A2:01–YLQ beads but not control HLA∗A2:01–SL9 beads, demonstrating that specific TCR–pMHC interactions are sufficient to drive phagocytosis. Previous work established that professional phagocytes engulf particles via FcR, complement, and pattern-recognition receptors1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, whereas T cells were considered non-phagocytic, acting mainly through synaptic recognition15. Subsequent studies revealed that CD4+ T cells can internalize materials via trogocytosis12,17, 18, 19, 20,24, and Jurkat cells in particular have been shown to internalize anti-CD3 coated beads16. However, prior work had not demonstrated phagocytosis driven by physiological TCR-pMHC recognition, leaving it unclear whether antigen-specific TCR interaction alone can initiate a full phagocytic program. Our data demonstrate that endogenous TCR-pMHC interactions alone can support full engulfment.

Although Jurkat cells are widely used model to study T cell signaling process25, Jurkat cells differ from primary CD4+ T cells in their activation thresholds, metabolic state, and elastic potential of cell membrane26, 27, 28, so the extent to which primary CD4+ T cells can engage the same TCR-driven phagocytic program, and how efficiently different subsets perform engulfment under physiological conditions, remains to be determined. Future work will test class I–restricted TCRs and CARs in primary CD4+ T cells to assess whether similar phagocytic behavior is observed across distinct CD4+ T cell populations.

Mechanistically, TCR engagement is known to signal through LAT–SLP-76–Vav1 complexes to activate Rho family GTPases (Rac1, Cdc42, RhoA), which in turn regulate Arp2/3 mediated actin polymerization at the immunological synapse29. Prior work in Jurkat cells has implicated the small GTPases TC21 and RhoG as key mediators of TCR-dependent phagocytic uptake16, consistent with a model in which TCR–pMHC signaling recruits localized actin nucleation to drive pseudopod extension and bead engulfment. Intracellular Ca2+ flux is tightly coupled to these pathways and is critical for early T cell activation, TCR rearrangements, and dynamic actin remodeling30, 31, 32. Ca2+ signals are integrated into LAT–SLP-76–Vav1–dependent signaling, and perturbations of IRF-4 binding protein (IBP)33,34, RhoH GTPase35,36, VAV137, 38, 39, Rac240,41 all dampen TCR-induced Ca2+ flux and consistently lead to defects in T cell activation, adhesion, and migration, reflecting impaired cytoskeletal reorganization. It is therefore highly plausible that TCR–pMHC–mediated T cell phagocytosis relies on coordinated Ca2+ signaling acting together with these cytoskeletal regulators. Future studies that selectively perturb LAT–SLP-76–Vav1 complexes, TC21/RhoG, and key Ca2+-modulating molecules will be important to pinpoint which nodes in this network govern the transition from transient anchoring to full phagocytic engulfment.

Our findings are particularly relevant to the application of TCR engineering towards developing CAR T cells, specifically in retaining efficacy under the mechanical stresses associated with the solid tumor microenvironment. Although we did not directly quantify the forces experienced by individual TCR–pMHC bonds during engulfment, the transition we observe from transient anchoring to robust engulfment (Fig. 1B) implies a dynamic mechanical landscape at the synapse. At early time points, a small number of TCR–pMHC bonds at the bead–cell interface must support most of the normal load imposed by the micropipette geometry. As pseudopodia extend and cover the bead, both the contact area and the number of engaged TCR–pMHC bonds increase, redistributing the load while introducing lateral components associated with membrane sliding and actin-driven protrusion. We therefore expect that local stress on the TCR complex fluctuates during this process, with higher peak stresses near the leading edge of the phagosome and lower average stresses across the mature phagocytic interface. In this context, TCRs (or CARs) with favorable 2D affinity and enhanced catch-bond stability are likely to be especially effective at maintaining signaling under force fluctuations, suggesting that tuning receptor mechanics could help generate CAR/TCR-T cells that remain functional in the high-shear, high-compression environments of solid tumor microenvironments.

These findings suggest potential functional roles for TCR-mediated phagocytosis in antigen control. CD4+ T cell trogocytosis can protect antigen-expressing targets by reducing surface antigen and limiting cytotoxic attack24, such TCR-mediated trogocytosis may represent a compromised form of phagocytosis when target molecules are clustered locally within or embedded among neighboring cells, making complete internalization difficult under limited cell-cell adhesion. Extending this process to full phagocytic internalization provides CD4+ T cells with a new function—direct target killing via complete engulfment of antigen-bearing cells—which could be particularly useful in anti-cancer therapy and further exploited in CD4+ CAR-T cells. Because antigen acquisition by CD4+ T cells can also influence differentiation of regulatory T cells and Th17 populations20, antigen-phagocytosing T cells may simultaneously shape the tumor microenvironment, modulate bystander T cell phenotypes, and control antigen availability to avoid exhaustion, potentially enhancing local immune activation and immune-cell penetration into tumor sites.

Conclusion

Together, our findings indicate that appropriately tuned TCR–pMHC interactions, supported by CD8 co-receptor engagement, are sufficient to drive antigen-specific phagocytosis by Jurkat T cells, expanding the functional repertoire attributed to T cells and suggesting new strategies to modulate antigen availability and immune responses.
