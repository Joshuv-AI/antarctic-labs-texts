Disseminated paracoccidioidomycosis with a pulmonary-spine fistula: suggestive of contiguous spread
Authors: Michelly Moreira Campos, Frederico de Sottomaior Drumond, Paulo Antônio Ribeiro de Freitas, Raphael Guedes Povoa, Felippe Saad, Marcello Mihailenko Chaves Magri, Wellingson Silva Paiva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paracoccidioidomycosis (PCM) rarely involves the spine. We report a 55-year-old man with disseminated PCM presenting with thoracic spinal cord compression and an unprecedented pulmonary-spine fistula identified intraoperatively. Diagnosis was confirmed by histopathology and mycology. The patient underwent surgical decompression and stabilization followed by itraconazole, achieving complete neurological recovery. This case highlights a unique complication of PCM and supports combined surgical and antifungal management in cases with neurological compromise.

Introduction

Paracoccidioidomycosis (PCM) is a systemic mycosis caused by thermally dimorphic fungi of the genus Paracoccidioides, endemic to Latin America, with Brazil accounting for the majority of reported cases. The disease primarily involves the lungs but may disseminate to multiple organs, particularly in chronic forms affecting individuals with rural exposure [1]. Osteoarticular involvement is uncommon and is usually observed in disseminated disease, particularly in the acute/subacute form of PCM. In a recent systematic review, 60.0% of osteoarticular PCM cases occurred in patients with acute/subacute disease, most commonly presenting as osteomyelitis (82.2%) associated with lytic bone lesions (74.4%) [2]. Vertebral PCM is particularly rare, accounting for approximately 10% of osteoarticular PCM cases in a recent systematic review, and may present with vertebral destruction, epidural extension, spinal cord compression, fistula formation, and neurological deficits, frequently mimicking tuberculosis or neoplastic diseases. In addition, the clinical presentation may be nonspecific, ranging from localized pain to progressive neurological deficits due to spinal cord compression [2,3].

The role of surgery in PCM-related spinal disease remains uncertain, as available evidence is limited to isolated case reports and small case series, with no standardized surgical management strategies currently established [[1], [2], [3]]. Furthermore, complications related to contiguous extension from pulmonary lesions into adjacent thoracic and vertebral structures are still poorly characterized in the literature. We describe a previously unreported presentation of disseminated PCM with thoracic spinal cord compression and a pulmonary-spine fistula, suggesting contiguous spread. A narrative literature review (PubMed, SciELO, and Embase) identified only five prior cases of spinal involvement, none requiring surgical stabilization.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old male from a rural area in Brazil (Pirapora, Minas Gerais) was referred to our center in January 2025 (day 0) with weight loss, hemoptysis, paraparesis, constipation, and urinary incontinence. He worked as a school janitor and had a medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and osteomyelitis treated two decades earlier. He was a smoker and reported alcohol use, with no history of illicit drug use.

Approximately one year prior to admission (day −360), the patient developed pleuritic chest pain, fever, night sweats, and weight loss. Despite inconclusive diagnostic tests, empirical treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis was initiated with isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Serial sputum smears for acid-fast bacilli remained negative throughout treatment. Approximately two months after completing therapy (day −120), he experienced clinical deterioration, including renewed weight loss, weakness, hemoptysis, gait instability, and new-onset constipation and urinary incontinence. Outpatient imaging revealed lytic vertebral lesions with suspected spinal cord compression, and he was subsequently referred to our institution for further evaluation.

On admission (day 0), physical examination revealed sarcopenia and multiple subcutaneous nodules involving the scalp, face, and abdomen (Fig. 1A). Neurological examination revealed grade IV paraparesis, hyperreflexia in the lower limbs, and bilateral Babinski sign, with preserved sensation and cranial nerve function. Investigations for tuberculosis remained negative. Serological testing showed a non-reactive result by immunodiffusion and a reactive result with a titer of 1:2 by counter immuno electrophoresis for Paracoccidioides spp. Imaging studies demonstrated pulmonary involvement adjacent to the thoracic spine (Fig. 1B). Bronchoscopy identified tracheal lesions (Fig. 1C), which were biopsied, confirming infection by Paracoccidioides spp. on histopathological examination. Subsequent mycological examination of sputum revealed yeast-like cells with multiple budding, consistent with the characteristic “pilot wheel” morphology (Fig. 2). Neuroaxis imaging revealed cranial involvement, vertebral osteomyelitis, and spinal cord compression (Fig. 3). The patient underwent thoracic laminectomy with partial vertebral resection and spinal stabilization. A midline incision from T4 to T12 was performed, and exposure of the spinous processes, laminae, and articular facets from T5 to T11 was achieved. During surgical exposure, abundant air leakage was observed due to a fistulous communication between the lung and subcutaneous tissue (Fig. 4). This was evidenced by air leakage from the mechanical ventilation system into the operative field. Temporary control was achieved by applying an absorbable collagen hemostatic sponge at the T8–T9 level, resulting in normalization of ventilatory parameters and cessation of air leakage. The procedure proceeded with placement of pedicle screws at T5, T6, T7, T10, and T11, along with decompression of the spinal canal at T8 and T9. Extensive destruction of the pedicles and vertebral bodies at T8 and T9 was observed, and bone biopsy was performed. Hemostasis was achieved, and the surgical wound was closed in layers. During surgery, specimens obtained from the vertebral lesion and adjacent tissues were collected for histopathological and microbiological evaluation. Histopathological examination subsequently demonstrated chronic granulomatous inflammation with fungal yeasts morphologically compatible with Paracoccidioides spp., confirming vertebral involvement by PCM.Fig. 4After spinal exposure and partial removal of the lesion, there was air leakage from the mechanical ventilation system through a spinal-pulmonary fistula. Postoperatively, a chest radiograph showed no evidence of hemopneumothorax, and the patient was extubated without complications. Postoperative computed tomography confirmed adequate positioning of the pedicle screws (Fig. 5A). The patient demonstrated significant postoperative improvement, with recovery of lower limb strength and gait. Antifungal therapy with itraconazole (ITZ) 200 mg daily was initiated during hospitalization and maintained after discharge. The patient was discharged at day +30 with significant neurological improvement and remains under outpatient follow-up. At follow-up (day +450), corresponding to 15 months of therapy, he remained clinically stable, with a planned total treatment duration of 18 months. Discussion

This report contributes a unique case of disseminated PCM with thoracic vertebral infiltration and spinal cord compression, complicated by a pulmonary-spine fistula, a complication we were unable to identify in the available literature for PCM. While antifungal therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, our case underscores the potential necessity of neurosurgical intervention, particularly in patients with progressive myelopathy, mechanical instability, or inconclusive diagnoses. In endemic areas, PCM should be included among the differential diagnoses of vertebral osteolytic lesions, especially in the presence of other compatible clinical and radiological findings.

According to the literature, bone involvement in PCM is most frequently observed in disseminated disease and may occur in up to 20% of patients, whereas isolated skeletal involvement is uncommon. Osteoarticular manifestations are generally associated with an underlying pulmonary focus [2,4,5]. We identified five published reports describing vertebral involvement in PCM [3,4,[6], [7], [8]], none of which required surgical stabilization. In the study by Lima Júnior et al. [4], bone involvement was identified in seven patients with PCM; the appendicular skeleton was affected in 85.7% of cases, whereas axial skeletal involvement was observed in 42.9%. Only one patient presented lesions confined exclusively to the axial skeleton.

Radiologically, PCM presents well-defined osteolytic lesions [9,10], which may affect vertebral bodies and pedicles, extending towards disc cartilage. Magnetic resonance imaging provides precise details of soft tissues, such as spondylodiscitis, abscesses, and spinal stenosis.

Therefore, differential diagnoses are crucial, particularly with bone neoplasms, metastases, osteomyelitis, tuberculosis, and lymphoproliferative diseases. When PCM affects the spine, clinical presentation can include local pain and spinal cord compression syndrome. Physical and neurological examinations may reveal pain upon palpation of the paravertebral muscles, local bulging, and signs of upper motor neuron involvement [3,7,8,10,11].

Reported cases of spinal PCM in the literature have been treated with ITZ or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) with varying degrees of response depending on the initial degree of destruction. In the study by Alvarenga et al. [3], despite the spinal cord involvement, ITZ was initially used but replaced with TMP-SMX after liver function deterioration, resulting in complete symptom resolution, normalization of inflammatory markers, and radiographic improvement after 36 months. In the study by Milazzo et al. [7], a surgical approach was performed for diagnostic purposes, including curettage of the vertebral bodies and discs and placement of a cortical graft harvested from the iliac bone. Fungal identification was obtained from the lesion material, although no data regarding cure or long-term follow-up were reported. In another report [8], the diagnostic investigation occurred concurrently with sepsis onset, antifungal and antimicrobial treatment was initiated, but the patient progressed to death.

In clinical practice, the most commonly used drugs in the therapeutic arsenal for PCM are ITZ, TMP-SMX, and amphotericin B (AmB). For severe and disseminated forms, D-AmB or lipid formulations are recommended. The induction phase aims at clinical stability and should be conducted for the shortest possible duration. All patients should be treated long enough to reduce symptoms, stabilize body weight, improve radiographic findings, and reduce antibody titers. The main therapeutic challenges in PCM are prolonged use of systemic antifungals, possibility of relapses, and development of sequelae, especially respiratory [1].

There is no information in the literature regarding specific surgical treatment indications for PCM with spinal involvement. In other infectious spondylodiscitis, surgical indication occurs in cases of spinal cord compression, mechanical instability, drainage of large abscesses, or the need for biopsy for diagnosis [2,12].

The substantial post-operative pain reduction observed in our patient strongly corroborates the hypothesis of underlying mechanical instability, underscoring the importance of considering spinal arthrodesis in spondylodiscitis and spinal cord compression associated with instability, and of considering surgical intervention in cases of myelopathy with neurological deficits. A remarkable intraoperative finding was the presence of a fistula, a phenomenon of exceptional rarity in our clinical experience. We successfully addressed this complication through the application of absorbable collagen hemostatic sponge to the affected site, effectively controlling air leakage, normalizing ventilatory parameters, and preventing complications as evidenced by unremarkable postoperative thoracic radiography. We emphasize that evaluation of the anatomical correlation between vertebral or paravertebral lesions and the pleural or pulmonary structures is imperative in cases of extensive discitis. Such assessment facilitates anticipation of potential complications, such as hemopneumothorax, which may significantly impact intraoperative and postoperative management.

In our patient, IDD was negative despite histopathological confirmation of PCM. Although IDD remains a well-established tool for the diagnosis and monitoring of PCM, its sensitivity may be affected by antigenic variability and technical factors. Sanguña et al. (2026), in a multicenter retrospective study evaluating 370 microbiologically confirmed PCM cases over a 10-year period (2013–2022), demonstrated that 52 (14%) sera were initially classified as false-negative by reference centers using the standard P. brasiliensis B-339 reference antigen (RAgPb) [13]. Upon retesting, 34/52 (65%) became positive, highlighting significant operator- and procedure-dependent variability affecting ID sensitivity. The remaining 18 sera (5% of the total cohort) persisted as negative with RAgPb but showed reactivity when tested against autochthonous antigens derived from regional Paracoccidioides isolates, demonstrating that antigenic variability among strains is a critical determinant of test performance. In the same study, dot blot assays using autochthonous antigens demonstrated greater sensitivity, faster turnaround, and operational simplicity compared with ID, supporting their potential utility in routine diagnosis. No statistically significant association was observed between PCM clinical form and ID performance (P > 0.05; odds ratio = 2.69). These findings emphasize the importance of incorporating region-specific antigens and standardized protocols to improve PCM diagnosis and minimize false-negative results in endemic regions, particularly considering the genetic diversity within the Paracoccidioides species complex [13].

PCM with spinal cord involvement constitutes a rare and challenging clinical presentation, posing formidable obstacles in diagnosis and management. This etiology warrants consideration, particularly in endemic regions where spondylodiscitis and pulmonary involvement coexist, and alternative diagnoses have been ruled out. The management of this condition demands meticulous clinical evaluation to ensure therapeutic success. Surgical intervention should be considered in the presence of myelopathy, mechanical instability, and/or when tissue diagnosis is required. Extensive preoperative assessment and interdisciplinary collaboration are fundamental prerequisites, while multidisciplinary management is essential for optimal outcomes.
