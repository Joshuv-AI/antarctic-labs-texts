Biological valorization of methane and nitrogen gas-derived ammonia via methanotrophic bacteria for gut-beneficial nutrients
Authors: Zixi Gao, Yuan Liu, Shanyao Jiao, Rongzhan Fu, Yunhao Chen, Chenyue Zhang, Xuemei Shen, Guidong Yang, Shihui Yang, Xiaoyan Wang, Liang Shi, Qiang Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The in-situ upcycling of decentralized methane and nitrogen gas (N2)-derived ammonia via methanotrophic bacteria is highly attractive. However, the toxic intermediate generated from ammonia oxidation significantly inhibits cell growth, thereby hindering efficient bioproduction. Herein, by integrating transcriptomic analysis, we develop rational metabolic engineering strategies and an optimized fed-batch fermentation to enhance ammonia utilization in a methanotrophic bacterium of Methylotuvimicrobium sanxanigenens. The modified M. sanxanigenens overexpressing hydroxylamine reductase efficiently co-assimilates methane and ammonia for cell protein synthesis, with an 18-fold increase in productivity. The resulting methanotrophic cell protein (MCP) not only exhibits an ideal essential amino acid profile but also contains bioactive nutrients, including polysaccharides and peptides. Oral administration of this nutritional MCP significantly ameliorates colitis symptoms in male mice by attenuating inflammatory progression and restoring the intestinal barrier. Moreover, MCP treatment maintains gut microbiota homeostasis and promotes the excretion of beneficial metabolites, thereby protecting the intestinal microenvironment. Hence, this study provides a promising biological approach for the local bio-valorization of decentralized CH4 and air into functional feed additives. This biotechnology not only facilitates advancements in developing carbon-negative gas-to-value pathways but also drives green transformations in animal husbandry by reducing the use of antibiotics and vaccines.

Introduction

China has abundant decentralized methane (CH4) derived from fossil fuel extraction and organic waste treatment processes1,2. However, it is difficult and costly to collect these CH4 resources via pipelines and upgrade them for natural gas production. Thus, decentralized CH4 is often combusted for low-value heat and power to meet the energy demands of surrounding factories, ultimately resulting in the complete waste of carbon resources. Aerobic methanotrophic bacteria, however, can utilize CH4 to synthesize single-cell protein as an alternative feed additive3. This approach may not only valorize decentralized CH4 but also alleviate protein shortages—a critical issue given that more than 80% of China’s soybean and fishmeal supplies rely on imports, totaling over 100 million tons annually4. This context underscores the urgent need to develop efficient biological approaches for valorizing decentralized CH₄ into protein feed, thus addressing both carbon resource waste and protein supply challenges.

This biological upcycling of decentralized CH4 is technically feasible, as mature technologies exist for supplying key nutrients. As one of the most widely used nitrogen sources in biomanufacturing, ammonia can be produced from nitrogen gas (N2) via an efficient and environmentally friendly photo-electrocatalytic (PEC) process5. Cryogenic distillation, a well-established technology in the chemical industry, separates high-purity N2 from air. Under the assumed conditions (Supplementary Note 1), this process also co-produces high-purity oxygen gas (O2)6. Therefore, simultaneous utilization of O2 generated from both air separation unit (ASU) operations and the PEC process enables maximal O2 utilization, as O2 serves as a crucial substrate for methanotrophic bacteria3. Detailed balanced reactions and corresponding calculations are provided in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Note 3. Moreover, the single-cell protein produced can be processed into microbial nutrients and formulated into animal feed, directly supporting nearby livestock farms. This integrated supply chain—where air-derived O₂ and green ammonia support CH₄-based microbial protein synthesis—lays a solid technical foundation for the sustainable, local upcycling of decentralized CH₄.

However, several vital issues must be addressed to fully realize this prospect. First, methanotrophic bacteria are not natural consumers of ammonia. Notably, it is widely reported that ammonia is partially oxidized by methane monooxygenase (MMO) to form a highly toxic intermediate, hydroxylamine—a compound that significantly impairs the growth of aerobic methanotrophic bacteria7. Given the difficulty in modifying MMO’s selectivity toward ammonia without compromising its methane oxidation activity, there is an urgent need to investigate the mechanisms underlying deficient ammonia utilization by integrating omics analysis. This serves as a prerequisite for designing rational strategies to overcome metabolic bottlenecks in hydroxylamine detoxification. Furthermore, although several studies have shown that methanotrophic bacterium-derived cell protein promotes intestinal health in fish8,9, its biofunctions and associated mechanisms remain underexplored in standardized animal models. This currently restricts its application as a bulk protein substitute—impeding its ability to compete with traditional feeds and gain acceptance from markets and consumers10. These challenges greatly hamper endeavors to establish proof of concept for the in-situ valorization of decentralized CH₄.

In this study, we select Methylotuvimicrobium sanxanigenens NG09 (CGMCC No. 30951)—a methanotrophic bacterium isolated from rice paddies with a high growth rate—as a CH4-utilizing cell factory11. To clarify the mechanism of its growth inhibition by ammonia, we perform a transcriptomic analysis to identify key enzymes involved in hydroxylamine detoxification. Based on these findings, we rationally construct the engineered strain SO01 overexpressing hydroxylamine reductase to enhance its growth with ammonia as the sole nitrogen source (Fig. 1). We further develop a fed-batch fermentation strategy to match the ammonia production capacity of upstream N2 fixation with the downstream growth demands of strain SO01. To expand the applications beyond a mere protein substitute, we investigate the effects of the produced microbial nutrients on attenuating dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in mice. We observe that these microbial nutrients also protect the intestinal microenvironment in mice, as evidenced by the recovery of gut microbiota diversity and the promotion of prebiotic excretion. Results

Uncovering ammonia-induced toxicity mechanism helps to rationally modify methanotrophic chassis

Methanotrophic cultivation typically relies on nitrate as nitrogen source12,13, primarily due to the severe growth inhibition associated with ammonia utilization. Given that the cost of nitrate salts is approximately twice that of ammonia14, it is thus crucial to endow methanotrophic bacterial chassis with enhanced ammonia utilization capacity. Although ammonia is the preferred nitrogen source for protein biosynthesis in most microorganisms, it is partially oxidized by MMO to form toxic hydroxylamine, which significantly hampers cell growth. We firstly tested the growth ability of M. sanxanigenens NG09 with ammonia (for the remainder of this manuscript, molecular ammonia (NH3) and ionic ammonium (NH4+) salt are collectively referred to as “ammonia” for consistency). Similar to other methanotrophic species15, M. sanxanigenens NG09 cultured with ammonia exhibited a 12-h longer lag phase than when grown with nitrate (Fig. 2a)—a nitrogen source widely used in methanotrophic bacteria cultures. This indicated that the cells may experience an adaption period for utilizing ammonia. To clarify the underlying mechanisms of ammonia preference and related growth retardation, we sought to find evidence from whole-genome transcriptional responses of M. sanxanigenens NG09 grown in the exponential phase. This approach enables the identification of key detoxification-related enzymes, which in turn facilitates the development of a rational genetic modification strategy to enhance cellular ammonia utilization. In the PCA analysis, the principal component 1 explained 95% of the variation (Fig. 2b). High correlations among samples within groups, together with weak correlations among different groups (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), indicated that the type of nitrogen source was the prominent cause of changes in gene transcription. Compared with growth with nitrate, 121 and 150 genes were significantly up- and down-regulated in the ammonia culture (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d), respectively.

As ammonia toxicity is proposed to be highly relevant to the formation of hydroxylamine, we then focused on the 45 differentially expressed genes involved in the nitrogen metabolism. The genes encoding nitrate reductase (nar), nitrate transport proteins (nrtABC), and nitrite reductases (nirBD and nir) were all repressed in cultures with ammonia (Fig. 2c). This explained the phenomenon of blocked nitrate uptake when ammonia existed. Additionally, N2 fixation, urea and cyanate assimilation pathways were all inactivated (Supplementary Data 1). These changes were consistent with the fact that ammonia is the preferred nitrogen source with the lowest energy and reducing power demand16. However, when M. sanxanigenens NG09 was cultured with ammonia, the genes encoding hydroxylamine reductase (har) and dehydrogenase subunits (haoAB) were significantly up-regulated by 3.9-, 3.8- and 3.3-fold, respectively. This transcriptional response indicates that the strain was subjected to hydroxylamine-induced toxicity, a conclusion further supported by the detection of 70.5 μM hydroxylamine per unit cell density (OD600) in the ammonia-fed culture (Supplementary Fig. 3). By contrast, no hydroxylamine was detected in the nitrate cultures, which can be attributed to the absence of hydroxylamine-generating steps in the nitrate assimilation pathway17,18. Correspondingly, M. sanxanigenens NG09 accumulates 3.34 mg of NO2−-N L−1 in the ammonia-based cultures, approximately 10-fold higher than that measured in the nitrate-grown cultures (0.33 mg NO2−-N L−1, Supplementary Fig. 4). Together, the transcriptomic analysis results confirmed pronounced impacts on the nitrogen metabolic network of M. sanxanigenens NG09 when the nitrogen source was switched to ammonia.

We further designed metabolic engineering strategies to redirect hydroxylamine flux. The haoAB-deficient strain showed severely impaired growth with ammonia (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Note 5), indicating that hydroxylamine dehydrogenase is important for detoxification. Additionally, as nitrite reductases were all repressed under ammonia culture, nitrite accumulated due to enhanced hydroxylamine oxidation, thus hampering cellular metabolic activities. However, negligible improvement of cell growth was observed by overexpressing hydroxylamine dehydrogenase or two nitrite reductases (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 6). Moreover, in order to alleviate the growth retardation, we also attempted to regulate intracellular ammonia availability by eliminating the transporters. Nonetheless, knockout of amtA and/or amtB did not notably affect the lag phase and growth rate of the engineered strains grown with ammonia. The NH4+-N level exhibits no significant differences (p > 0.05) between the wild type and amt-knockout strains, indicating that the growth of M. sanxanigenens NG09 is not limited by ammonia transport (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Note 7). This result demonstrates that knockout of the putative ammonia transporters (AmtA and AmtB) does not affect the growth of M. sanxanigenens NG09 with ammonia.

Another strategy is to upregulate the transcriptional level of hydroxylamine reductase, which catalyzes the direct hydroxylamine reduction to ammonia. On the basis of per mole hydroxylamine recycled, the reducing power demand is calculated to be only one third of that in the NO2−-to-NH3 reduction. The araC-PBAD regulatory system was established to control the overexpression of har. The inducer arabinose exerts no significant impact on growth at concentrations below 0.5 g L−1 and is not assimilated by methanotrophic bacteria19. Given its superior growth performance with ammonia (Fig. 2d, e), M. sanxanigenens SO01 was selected for subsequent cultivations, with arabinose added at 0.1 g L−1 to induce har expression. When M. sanxanigenens SO01 was cultured with ammonia and supplemented with 0.1 g L−1 arabinose to induce har expression, the hydroxylamine concentration accumulated to 44.6 μM per unit OD600. This level was 37.9% lower than that in the ammonia-fed culture without L-arabinose supplementation (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 8). The significant decreased hydroxylamine accumulation directly supports that har overexpression mitigates hydroxylamine-induced toxicity in methanotroph cells. In contrast, arabinose concentrations of 0.25 and 0.4 g L−1 impose an enhanced metabolic burden, leading to cellular resource reallocation and slowed cell growth as a compensatory adaptation (Fig. 2d)20. Consequently, these higher concentrations fail to yield significant improvements in har expression and even impair the growth of M. sanxanigenens SO01. In summary, the ammonia-based growth capacity of M. sanxanigenens is enhanced by reinforcing direct hydroxylamine reduction.

Developing ammonia fed-batch strategy to boost production of nutritional cell protein

In addition to metabolic engineering strategies, it is necessary to regulate ammonia transport by controlling its concentration, which has been reported to strongly influence the growth of methanotrophic bacteria7. We firstly tested ammonia tolerance capacity of M. sanxanigenens SO01. Pronounced growth inhibition of the har-overexpressing M. sanxanigenens SO01 occurred when ammonia concentration reached 20 mM or higher (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 8). However, more ammonia is required to achieve substantial cell protein production without impairing growth. Subsequently, a fed-batch strategy was established, and the cell density increased by nearly 70% when ammonia concentration did not exceed 10 mM (Supplementary Fig. 10), consistent with the results of ammonia tolerance test. In contrast, a rapid growth decline occurred at 36 h in the high-dose cultivation. Therefore, further fed-batch cultivation was conducted to control the ammonia concentration below 10 mM.

N2-derived ammonia was used in M. sanxanigenens cell protein production. M. sanxanigenens exhibited similar growth on N2-derived ammonia in comparison to a commercial ammonia agent (Supplementary Fig. 11). Additionally, when M. sanxanigenens culture was transferred to grow on 15N2-derived ammonia, 15N-labeled ([m+1]) glutamate, aspartate and leucine accounted for 88%–90% of the corresponding amino acids in cell protein (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 12). This indicates that the protein component of M. sanxanigenens cell protein is synthesized from N2-derived ammonia. In the 3-L fed-batch fermentation (Supplementary Fig. 13), the ammonia stock was derived from the PEC synthesis process, while O2 was continuously supplied via external aeration from an air compressor. By maintaining the ammonia concentration below 140 mg NH4+-N L−1 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 1), pH within 7−7.3 and dissolved oxygen (DO) above 10%, cell growth proceeded without limitations from oxygen transfer or by-product accumulation. Detailed fermentation control parameters including gas flow rate (vvm), CH4:O2 ratio, pressure, and kLa are provided in the Supplementary Information (Methods). It should be noted that no acids or growth-inhibiting by-products were detected in the culture broth of M. sanxanigenens SO01, and rapid cell growth was observed after 12 h (Fig. 3c), with a maximum specific growth rate of 0.156 h−1 achieved (Supplementary Table 2).Fig. 3Development of a fed-batch fermentation for scalable bioconversion of CH4 and N2-derived ammonia to methanotrophic cell protein (MCP).a 15N labeling profiles of L-glutamic acid (Glu), L-aspartic acid (Asp) and L-leucine (Leu) in the cell protein of M. sanxanigenens SO01 cultured on 15NH4Cl. n = 2 biologically independent samples. b Time course of residual nitrogen concentration in M. sanxanigenens SO01 broth in the 3-L fed-batch fermentation. c Time course of M. sanxanigenens SO01 cell density in the 3-L fed-batch fermentation. d Comparison of dry cell weight (DCW), crude protein and polysaccharides contents in MCP obtained from 3-L fed-batch fermentation. The dry cell weight (DCW) and cell protein productivity reached 5.30 g L−1 and 147.2 mg L−1 h−1 (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 14e), representing a 5-fold and 18-fold increase compared to the previously reported values15, respectively. In total, 32.5 mmol of ammonia was consumed per liter of broth, corresponding to the nitrogen demand of the culture. Additionally, cumulative O2 uptake was calculated by integrating the oxygen uptake rate (OUR) over time, reaching 520 mmol per liter of broth (Supplementary Fig. 14c and Supplementary Note 9). Here, the basis and units for normalizing nitrogen source utilization versus O2 uptake are defined as mg of N and mmol of O2 consumed per gram of cell protein produced, respectively. When ammonia and O₂ uptake were normalized to DCW, the results showed that 85.8 mg NH3-N and 98.1 mmol O2 were consumed to produce 1 g of cell protein. The cell protein yield on methane reached 0.97 g g−1 at 36 h, calculated as DCW (5.30 g L−1) divided by methane consumption (5.47 g L−1) (Supplementary Table 3, Eq. (1)), which aligns with previous findings21. The cell protein yield on ammonia-N was 11.65 g g−1 (Supplementary Table 4, Eq. (2)).

To demonstrate the application value of our recombinant strain M. sanxanigenens SO01, a direct comparison was performed between nitrate- and ammonia-utilizing bioprocesses under identical conditions, with the total nitrogen supply uniformly set at 455 mg N per liter of broth for both systems. For the ammonia-utilizing bioprocess, the maximum OD600, peak methane uptake rate (CUR), and highest DCW were 17.68, 33.60 mmol L−1 h−1, and 5.30 g L−1 (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 14a), respectively. During cultivation, the total methane uptake reached 5.47 g L−1 with complete ammonia depletion (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 3). These results corresponded to methane- and nitrogen-based cell protein yields of 0.97 g DCW g−1 CH4 and 11.65 g DCW g−1 N, respectively (Supplementary Tables 3, 4, Eqs. (1) and (2). On the other hand, the peak CUR was higher at 42.37 mmol L−1 h−1 in nitrate-utilizing bioprocess (Supplementary Fig. 14b), accompanied by a total methane uptake of 7.42 g L−1 and a DCW of 6.94 g L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 14f). The corresponding methane- and nitrogen-based yields were 0.94 g DCW g−1 CH4 and 15.25 g DCW g−1 N (Supplementary Table 3).

We further characterized the cell protein of the engineered M. sanxanigenens strain. The protein and polysaccharide contents reached 347 and 429 mg g−1 DCW, respectively, collectively accounting for ~70–80% of the DCW (Fig. 3d). The isoleucine and leucine contents of the methanotrophic cell protein (MCP) were 94.7% and 93.4% of those in whole egg, while the valine content was comparable to that in whole egg and cow milk (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 15)22. The branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) content of MCP was 10.9% higher than that in fishmeal and closely matched that of whole egg (Fig. 3f)23. Additionally, the essential amino acid (EAA) content of MCP reached 107.1% of that in fishmeal. These results indicate that the nutritional value of MCP is comparable to that of fishmeal, whole egg, and cow milk. A minor yet significant intracellular peptide mixture was also identified (inset of Fig. 3f), which was reported to exhibit anti-inflammation effects and protect animal intestinal microbiota24. Furthermore, the safety of this nutritional cell protein was verified, with an endotoxin level below 0.02 EU mL−1. Thus, our methanotrophic cell protein contains diverse nutrients and bioactive peptides, highlighting its potential application as a functional feed additive.

Probiotic effects of nutritional cell protein on gut health

The maintenance of the intestinal barrier integrity is critical for gut health25. MCP treatment mitigated DSS-induced structural integrity disruption of colonic tissue, as evidenced by reducing mucosal ulceration, crypt damage, and inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Data 2). Notably, the tight junction protein Occludin, which is essential for maintaining the intestinal epithelial barrier, was markedly restored upon treatment with the MCP (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 17d). Furthermore, the MCP demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect on the protein expression of pro-inflammatory interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 18a–c), and restored the expression of anti-inflammatory interleukin-10 (IL-10) compared to mice with colitis (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 18d). These findings indicated that administration of MCP helped to alleviate colitis by restoring intestinal barrier and attenuating inflammations in mice.

DSS treatment led to severe dysbiosis of the mice gut microbiota, which was effectively reversed by MCP administration (Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Note 11). MCP gavage remarkably reduced the abundance of Campylobacterota, a phylum associated with inflammation (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 20b). Pathogenic genera, including Klebsiella, Enterocloster, Muribaculum, and Helicobacter, proliferated in mice with DSS-induced colitis, whereas MCP treatment inhibited these pathogens and increased the abundance of short-chain fatty acid-producing probiotics, such as Alloprevotella and Kineothrix (Supplementary Fig. 20d and Supplementary Fig. 21a). At the species level, we observed a proportional rise in the anti-inflammatory bacteria Faecalibaculum rodentium and Odoribacter laneu, while the disease-related Helicobacter ganmani was suppressed following MCP intake (Supplementary Fig. 21b and Supplementary Note 12).

The recovery of the microbiota diversity may improve the distribution of metabolites and thus contribute to the maintenance of the intestinal homeostasis. We conducted further investigations on fecal metabolites in mice (Supplementary Figs. 22, 23 and Supplementary Note 13, 14). Treatment with the MCP resulted in a notable increase in prebiotic excretion including taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA) and suberic acid, compared to the model group (Fig. 5d). TCDCA, previously reported to alleviate intestinal inflammation26, exhibited the highest area under the curve in a receiver operating characteristic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Note 15). Additionally, suberic acid level significantly decreased in mice with colitis, and was markedly elevated after MCP administration (Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Data 3). This compound is highly correlated with enrichments of gut beneficial bacteria27. Therefore, these two metabolites were speculated as the biomarkers for attenuation of mouse colitis through MCP treatment. To sum up, these results highlight that oral administration of MCP assisted in mitigating mouse colitis progression by positively modulating intestinal microbiota and enhancing prebiotics excretion.

Additionally, it is noteworthy that MCP treatment also benefits healthy mice by protecting the gut microenvironment. This was evidenced by a boosted expression of Occludin with MCP administration (Fig. 4e, “−DSS,MCP” group versus “−DSS” group). Furthermore, the abundance of 11 bacterial taxa significantly increased among mice treated with MCP, including the probiotic Lactobacillaceae, Lacticaseibacillus, and Prevotellaceae (Fig. 5e). The MCP also dramatically enhanced the excretion of beneficial metabolites, including isovaleric acid, L-alanine, lithocholic acid acetate, and myristic acid, in comparison to the normal mice (“−DSS” group) (Fig. 5f). Overall, our results suggest the potential use of MCP as a nutritional additive to prevent inflammatory bowel disease in healthy animals.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully demonstrated a feasible biological route to upcycle CH4 and N2-derived ammonia for producing gut health-promoting microbial nutrients. Rational metabolic engineering strategies were employed to endow the methanotrophic bacterium with an enhanced ability to utilize ammonia. An optimized fed-batch fermentation process was further developed to minimize hydroxylamine-induced toxicity and efficiently produce nutritional MCP containing essential amino acids and polysaccharides. Notably, these microbial nutrients exhibit significant benefits in ameliorating colitis progression in mice. This may be attributed to the positive effects of the microbial nutrients on gut microbiota and their ability to enhance the excretion of prebiotic metabolites. Our work highlights the promising prospects of hybrid chemical-biocatalytic processes that harness abundant low-cost gaseous feedstocks to generate value-added products, thereby promoting cross-industry innovation.

Based on transcriptomic analysis, we propose that M. sanxanigenens exhibits a preference for ammonia over nitrate, N2 and urea. This lays a solid foundation for elucidating the mechanism of nitrogen source preference in methanotrophs. Ammonia inhibits methanotrophic growth by triggering the formation of toxic hydroxylamine, which is an intermediate generated via the catalysis of methane monooxygenases. Subsequently, hydroxylamine is oxidized by hydroxylamine dehydrogenase to generate nitrite. Consistent with this metabolic pathway, our experimental results demonstrated the accumulation of both hydroxylamine and nitrite in M. sanxanigenens cultures grown with ammonia, which verifies the occurrence of ammonia-induced toxicity mediated by these toxic intermediates. Additionally, we identified key enzymes involved in hydroxylamine detoxification. By overexpressing hydroxylamine reductase (har) to redirect nitrogen flux and integrating optimized fed-batch fermentation, we established a platform for producing nutritional MCP using ammonia as the sole nitrogen source.

The combined strategies of genetic overexpression and fed-batch fermentation effectively improved DCW and productivity, leading to a 5-fold enhancement compared to unoptimized ammonia-based cultures. Nitrate is widely recognized as a more favorable nitrogen source for aerobic methanotrophs, as evidenced by industrial promising methanotrophic bacteria such as Methylococcus capsulatus and Methylotuvimicrobium buryatense3,12, where nitrate-based cultivation yields higher DCW and productivity than ammonia-based processes. This study, however, provides a viable alternative approach to enable efficient ammonia utilization by methanotrophs. Comparative analysis revealed that the methane-based cell protein yields of the two bioprocesses were nearly equivalent (0.97 g DCW g−1 CH4 for ammonia vs. 0.94 g DCW g−1 CH4 for nitrate). Although the nitrogen-based yield of the ammonia-utilizing bioprocess (11.65 g DCW g−1 N) reached 75% of that of the nitrate-utilizing one (15.25 g DCW g−1 N), the market price of ammonium chloride (the ammonia source we used) was only ~30% of the price of potassium nitrate (USD 220 ton−1 vs. USD 770 ton−1, North America, August 2025)28,29.

To further quantify the economic competitiveness of the ammonia-based system, we calculated the nitrogen source cost per gram of final DCW and per bioreactor run for both processes. Based on the nitrogen-based yields and raw material prices, the nitrogen source cost for the ammonia-utilizing process was determined to be USD 0.072 kg−1 DCW, which is only 19.8% of the cost incurred by the nitrate-utilizing process (USD 0.364 kg−1 DCW). For a typical 1000-L bioreactor run with a final DCW of 5.30 g L−1 (consistent with the data achieved in this study), the total nitrogen source cost for the ammonia-based process was USD 0.38, whereas the nitrate-based process required USD 2.53 (with a final DCW of 6.94 g L−1), representing an 84.9% reduction in nitrogen-related expenditure per batch. In summary, this study demonstrates that the ammonia-utilizing bioprocess with the engineered strain not only achieves competitive cell yields relative to the nitrate-based process but also offers substantial economic benefits. Thus, our work addresses the key technical bottleneck that has long hindered the bio-valorization of decentralized CH4 and N2-derived ammonia into microbial nutrients.

There is an urgent need for functional feed additives that can simultaneously provide dietary nutrition and enhance disease resistance30,31. Enteritis is one of the most prevalent diseases causing severe production losses in animal husbandry32,33. A previous study reported that supplementing M. capsulatus bacterial meal activates the immune response, strengthens the intestinal barrier, and modulates gut flora in fish with soybean meal-induced enteritis34. Building on these findings, we extended the application of MCP to intestinal health protection in mice. Our results indicate that this nutritional MCP may effectively attenuate colitis progression in other monogastric animals, such as swine and poultry. Additionally, MCP administration was accompanied by significantly boosted excretion of TCDCA and suberic acid in the mouse intestine. Previous studies have suggested that elevated levels of the beneficial bacterium Alloprevotella may promote the accumulation of the primary bile acid Chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) in its conjugated form TCDCA by competitively inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria, which suppresses pro-inflammatory factor expression via the bile acid receptor TGR5, thereby alleviating systemic inflammatory responses35. Simultaneously, supplementation with MCP specifically enriched Kineothrix in the gut, whose metabolite butyrate serves as the preferred energy substrate for colonic epithelial cells36. Furthermore, the restoration of suberic acid levels to the normal range indicates significant restoration of the host’s systemic energy metabolism and mitochondrial function. Although further investigations into the prebiotic effects of these beneficial metabolites are needed, our findings identified potential marker metabolites highly associated with colitis attenuation. Notably, methanotrophic bacteria efficiently utilize ammonia, leading to the significant accumulation of polysaccharides as storage compounds. These polysaccharides act as high-quality fermentation substrates preferentially utilized by gut probiotics, enhancing their growth and metabolism. Additionally, they may also directly modulate local immune responses, exerting beneficial effects on the recovery of intestinal inflammation and intestinal barrier. These insights help clarify the gut-beneficial mechanisms underlying the functional properties of MCP.

In summary, our work presents a promising and feasible approach for the in-situ upcycling of low-cost decentralized CH4 and air (N2) into high-value animal nutrients. This technology not only advances the development of sustainable gas-to-value pathway to support carbon neutralization goals but also promotes the green transformation of animal husbandry by providing functional feed ingredients that contribute to improving animal health.

Methods

Animal ethics statement

Mouse experimental protocols in the present study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwestern University (approval number: NWU-AWC-20240803M).

Strain and culture conditions

The bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 8. M. sanxanigenens NG09 was reserved in China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC No. 30951). Wild type and engineered M. sanxanigenens strains were grown in modified nitrate mineral salt (NMS) medium, which comprised 1.000 g L−1 MgSO4 · 7H2O, 0.013 g L−1 CaCl2 · 2H2O, 1.000 g L−1 KNO3, 0.109 g L−1 KH2PO4, 0.215 g L−1 Na2HPO4, and 2 mL L−1 trace element solution37. Frozen stocks of methanotroph strains were stored in −80 °C. For revival of methanotroph strains, stocks were spread on the agar plates (NMS medium containing 1.5% agar powder). The agar plates were then incubated in sealed jars filled with a gas mixture of 20% CH4 and 80% air (v/v). A loop of colonies from the agar plate was inoculated into a 250-mL glass vial containing 50 mL of NMS medium. The vials were sealed with rubber stoppers, followed by the injection of 50 mL of CH4 into each vial. The vials were then incubated at 30 °C with constant shaking at 200 rpm. Sampling was conducted at 12-h intervals, with CH4 freshly injected immediately after each sampling event. When the cell density (reflected by optical density at 600 nm, OD600) reached 1.5−2.0, it was inoculated (10%) to the ammonia mineral salt (AMS) medium to adapt to the change of nitrogen source, in which 1.00 g L−1 nitrate was replaced by 0.55 g L−1 NH4Cl. After OD600 increased to 1.5−2.0, the seed culture was transferred (10%) to another fresh AMS medium to evaluate cell growth. Three biologically independent replicates were performed for each group of methanotrophic bacterium cultivation.

To monitor the extracellular ammonia and nitrite concentrations, cultures were pelleted, and the resulting supernatants were assayed by the Ammonia TNTplus Vial Test Kit (TNT-831, HACH) or Nitrite TNTplus Vial Test (TNT-840, HACH), which can obtain results within 15 min. These samples were measured by the Spectrophotometer DR3900 (HACH). The extracellular hydroxylamine was detected by the colorimetric method38, modified as follows: 1) 200 μL of culture supernatant was mixed with 1.3 mL of 6% sodium acetate, 200 μL of 1% p-aminobenzene sulfonic acid and 100 μL of 1.3% iodine (solubilized by glacial acetic acid); 2) 40 μL of 0.4 M sodium thiosulfate was added to the mixture to react with the excess iodine; 3) 50 μL of 0.6% α-naphthylamine was added to the mixture, and the optical density at the wave length of 520 nm (OD520) was measured; 4) hydroxylamine concentration was determined using a standard curve generated with hydroxylamine hydrochloride over the range of 20−200 μM). The hydroxylamine concentration of samples was measured by Spectrophotometer TU1810 (Beijing Purkinje GENERAL Instrument). For each group of methanotrophic bacterium cultivation, these assays were all performed in three biologically independent replicates.

Transcriptomic analysis

M. sanxanigenens NG09 was grown in 500-mL spinner bottles to obtain sufficient cell mass for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)3. To compare the transcriptional differences of cell growth on ammonia versus nitrate, 1.00 g L−1 nitrate in NMS medium was replaced by 0.55 g L−1 NH4Cl. Cell mass was collected at OD600 2.0−2.5, and the pellets were quickly frozen by liquid nitrogen and stored in −80 °C before RNA-Seq3. Agilent 2100/2200 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), Cutadapt (v 1.9.1) and Bowtie2 (v 2.2.6) were used for quality control. NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), HTSeq (v 0.6.1p1) and GOSeq (v 1.34.1) were used for data analysis. Two biologically independent replicates were performed and analyzed for each group of methanotrophic bacterium cultivation. The whole-genome sequencing data of M. sanxanigenens NG09 have been deposited in National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under accession code JBOBHP000000000. The raw RNA-Seq data of methanotrophic culture grown on ammonia versus on nitrate have been deposited in NCBI under accession code PRJNA1266088 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1266088].

Genetic manipulations

Primers was designed by Snapgene (v 4.3.6.0), and was prepared by Tsingke Biotech. In order to establish a controllable gene overexpression, the araC-PBAD regulatory system was introduced into the plasmid pAWP89 (constructed for methanotrophic cell factories by Puri et al., as described in a previous study)19,39. By replacing the native tac promoter with the araBAD promoter (PCR amplified from pBBR1-MCS5), a new plasmid pAPA24 was constructed (Supplementary Table 8). The pAPA24 backbone was PCR amplified using the primers P89-F and bad89-R (Supplementary Data 4). The target gene was incorporated into the pAPA24 backbone, and the resulting recombinant plasmid was transformed into M. sanxanigenens NG09 according to a standard procedure40. The recombinant strain was selected on NMS plate containing 50 μg mL−1 kanamycin. The gene expression was induced by adding 0−0.5 g L−1 of L-arabinose in the culture media19.

Gene knockout was conducted according to a standard procedure40. Taking deletion of amtB as an example. Upstream and downstream of amtB (both 1000 bp), and gentamycin-resistant gene were PCR amplified by using the primers UpamtB-F, UpamtB-R-t, DoamtB-F-t, DoamtB-R, GmR-F and GmR-R. The knockout complex was PCR assembled and amplified, and was then transformed into M. sanxanigenens by electroporation. The recombinant strain was selected on NMS plate containing 10 μg mL−1 gentamicin.

LC-MS analysis of 15N labeled amino acids

Protein samples (two biologically independent replicates) were subjected to acid hydrolysis (6 N HCl, 110 °C,24 h) to liberate free amino acids. The hydrolysate supernatants (1 mL) were dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen and reconstituted in 50 uL of acetonitrile: water (1:1, v/v). Metabolic flux analysis was performed using a Thermo ScientificQ Exactive HF-X hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer coupled to a UHPLC system. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 m; Waters) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of ammonium acetate buffer (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.4 mL mL–1 as follows: 0–0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5–7 min, 95% to 65% B; 7–8 min, 65% to 50% B; 8–9 min, 50% B; 9–9.1 min 50% to 95% B; and 9.1–12 min, 95% B.

Mass spectrometry data were acquired in electrospray ionization (ESI) mode with the following source parameters: spray voltage, 2.5 kV; capillary temperature, 325 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 300 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 30 arbitrary units; and auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 arbitrary units. MS scans were acquired over a mass range of m/z 70–1050 with a resolution of 60,000, followed by data-dependent MS/MS (Top N = 8) with an isolation window of 1.5 m/z and normalized collision energy of 30. Data acquisition and peak integration were performed using X calibur software (v 4.0.27.19, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Correction for raw data of natural isotope abundance was performed41.

Fed-batch cultivations

Two feeding scenarios were examined in spinner bottle culture. In the high-dose (HD) scenario, when the ammonia concentration decreased to 5−10 mM (70−140 mg-N L−1), 1.50 mL NH4Cl stock solution was fed into the 500-mL spinner bottles. In the middle-dose (MD) scenario, when the ammonia concentration fell below 5 mM (70 mg-N L−1), 0.75 mL NH4Cl stock solution was fed. Three biologically independent replicates were performed for each group of fed-batch cultivations.

The ammonia stream produced from the photo-electrocatalytic ammonia synthesis. The NH4Cl used in the fermentation process was of high purity, and no additional contaminants were introduced after preparation5. To minimize the risk of trace metals or chloride ions, the PEC catalysts and neutralization agents were carefully selected, and gas stripping and vacuum distillation methods were applied to purify the ammonia solution derived from PEC synthesis42, resulting in the production of a pure NH4Cl stock for fed-batch fermentation.

The 3-L fermenters were loaded with 1.8 L AMS medium containing 0.55 g L−1 NH4Cl. After growing for 24−30 h, 200 mL seed culture of M. sanxanigenens SO01 in the spinner bottle was inoculated into each 3-L fermenter. The pH and temperature were controlled at 7−8 and 30 ± 0.1 °C, respectively. The gas flow rate was set at 1 L min−1, resulting in a gas/liquid ratio of 0.5 vvm. Air was supplied by air compressor. CH4 and air was pre-mixed at the volumetric ratio of 1:4 (e.g., CH4:O2 = 1:0.84), and the CH4 and O2 content in the inlet gas were fixed at 20% and 16.8%, respectively. CH4, O2 and CO2 fractions in the off-gas of Fermenter 1 were monitored by gas analyzers, and CH4 and O2 uptake rate (CUR and OUR respectively) were calculated3. The fermenter headspace was controlled at ambient pressure, and antifoam DF-104 was added when the foam level exceeded 6/7 of the fermenter volume to prevent excessive foaming. Under the given conditions (gas/liquid ratio of 0.5 vvm, maximum agitation speed of 1000 rpm), the O2-liquid mass transfer coefficient (kLaO2) was measured to be 109.98 h−1 using the dynamic method43.

During the exponential growth period, the dissolved oxygen was maintained within 5–40% to avoid growth ceasing caused by oxygen depletion or excess oxygen supply, by mildly adjusting the agitation speed (increased by 50−100 rpm when DO drops to 5−10%). Additionally, the purified NH4Cl stock was supplemented into fermenters to support growth demand (Supplementary Fig. 12). When the supernatant ammonia was below 3 mM, 2.5-mL of the purified NH4Cl stock was added to reach a 5-mM increase on NH4+ concentration (Supplementary Table 1), and thereby the NH4+-N concentration was controlled below 140 mg NH4+-N L−1 to minimize ammonia-induced toxicity. After 24 h, dry cell weight (DCW) was measured each 6 h.

Likewise, the nitrate-fed-batch fermentation was performed in comparison to ammonia-fed-batch fermentation. The initial NMS medium contained 1 g L−1 KNO3, and additional 22.5 mM KNO3 was fed into the fermenter to achieve the dose of 455 mg N per liter of broth (the same with ammonia-fed-batch fermentation). The extracellular nitrate was monitored by the colorimetric method3. The supernatant of the fermentation broth was treated with 3 volumes of 95% (v/v) ethanol, and the mixture was then centrifuged to pellet the precipitates. Subsequently, the optical density of the resulting supernatant was measured at 220 and 270 nm, and the nitrate concentration was quantified by referencing a pre-established standard curve.

Determination of nutritional compounds

The Kjeldahl nitrogen content of MCP was measured by the Kjeldahl method, and the crude protein content was calculated by multiplying the Kjeldahl nitrogen content and the factor 6.25. The carbohydrate content of MCP were detected by the glycogen kit (TY-2-Y, Comin Biotechnology)3. To analyze the peptide mixture in MCP lysate, cell protein was resuspended in deionized water (2 g L−1) and then sonicated under ice incubation. The molecular size distribution of peptides was analyzed by 18% SDS-PAGE gel prepared by the ultra-low molecular weight Kit (RTD6122, Real-Times (Beijing)), and was imaged by an automatic in-gel analysis system (ChampGel®6000, SinSage Technology Co., Ltd.). To verify the existence of endotoxins, MCP lysate above was assayed by ToxinSensor™ Chromogenic LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (L00350, Genescript). For each group of methanotrophic bacterium cultivation, these assays were all performed in three biologically independent replicates.

MCP obtained from 3-L fed-batch fermentation was pretreated before analyzing amino acids. 20–50 mg of MCP was mixed with 10 mL of 6 M HCl, and the mixture was transferred into hydrolysis tubes. The tubes were purged with nitrogen gas for 30 s, and they were then sealed and hydrolyzed at 110 °C for 22–24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the hydrolysate was filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane and diluted to 50 mL. A 2 mL aliquot was evaporated to dryness at 85 °C twice to remove residual acid. It was then reconstituted in 1 mL of sodium citrate buffer, and was passed through a 0.22 µm filter prior to analysis. Calibration standards were prepared using an Amino Acid Standard (AAS18, Sigma) diluted by 5-fold with sodium citrate buffer to a final concentration of 0.25 µmol mL–1 for cystine and 0.5 µmol mL–1 for other amino acids.

Amino acid profiles of MCP were detected by a Biochrom 30+ Amino Acid Analyzer (Biochrom, UK) equipped with a Na-type cation exchange column (200 mm × 4.6 mm, 8 µm). The mobile phase consisted of a stepwise gradient of citrate buffers (pH 3.2, 4.25, and 6.45) delivered at a flow rate of 25 mL h–1, with the column temperature programmed from 55 °C to 77 °C. Post-column derivatization was carried out using ninhydrin (10 mL h–1) in a reaction coil at 138 °C, followed by spectrophotometric detection at 570 nm and 440 nm. The instrument limit of detection was determined to be ~2.5 pmol (S/N = 2) with a peak area reproducibility (CV) lower than 1.0%.

Pasteurization treatment

To promote the release of nutritional compounds, 2.5 mg of MCP was resuspended by 200 μL sterile phosphate buffer solution, and was incubated under 70 °C for 30 min35. To simulate mice gastric digestion, 750 μL of the artificial gastric juice (pH 1.0) was added into the pasteurized mixture, and then incubated under 37 °C and 120 rpm for 6 h. The mixtures were pelleted, and the supernatants were assayed by the Total Nitrogen Kit (2714100-CN, HACH). Protein release percentages can be obtained by comparing with the baseline, which contained 2.5 mg MCP, 200 μL PBS and 750 μL artificial gastric juice, and was resuspended and immediately assayed by the kit.

Animal experiment

Male 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice weighing between 20 and 25 g were purchased from Shaanxi Pharmaceutical Medical Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Prior to the commencement of the experiments, the mice were housed for one week in a standard setup consisting of a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, a humidity of 55 ± 5%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle44.

An extensive review of intervention studies involving microbial products in animal models was conducted45,46, and dose-ranging studies were performed. Ultimately, 100 mg kg−1 day−1 of methanotrophic cell protein was selected as the experimental dosage. (Supplementary Fig. 17a). Following a seven-day period of domestication, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 12). The groups were as follows: (1) “−DSS” group: treated with pure water and saline. (2) “+DSS” group: treated with 2.0% DSS for 7 days and saline for 10 days. (3) “+DSS,MCP” group: treated with 2.0% DSS for 7 days and MCP (100 mg kg−1) for 10 days concurrently. (4) “−DSS,MCP” group: treated with MCP (100 mg kg−1) for 10 days. Disease activity index (DAI) of the mice was daily monitored, based on the criteria as mentioned before (Supplementary Table 6)6. The survival rate of four groups were given in Supplementary Fig. 17b.

Histopathological and biochemical analysis

The colon tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin staining. The expression levels of IL-10, IL-6, and TNF-α in serum and colon tissue were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Shanghai, China). The preparation of colonic proteins is conducted using a mixture of radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer and protease inhibitors. For western blotting, primary antibodies against Occludin (Cat No. 27260-1-AP, dilution 1: 35000) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, Cat No. 10494-1-AP, dilution 1: 30000) were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. The second antibody (Cat No. A21020 dilution 1: 20000) was purchased from Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd47,48. The relative expression of Occludin was normalized by GAPDH. For each group of mice, western blotting was performed in six biologically independent replicates.

16S rRNA and metabolomics analysis

Mouse fecal samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C. These samples were transported (protected by dry ice) to BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. for further analyses. Total DNA was extracted from fecal samples using a commercial kit and evaluated using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. PCR amplification was performed using specific primers. The sequencing results are then subjected to a verification process in accordance with the established guidelines for library construction on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Qsep100 (Bioptic), Qubit3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen) and DNBSEQ-G400 (BGI) were used for quality control and sequencing. Cutadapt (v 1.9.1), Bowtie2 (v 2.2.6), HTSeq (v 0.6.1p1), GOSeq (v 1.34.1), iTools Fqtools fqcheck (v.0.25), cutadapt (v.2.6), readfq (v 1.0), Fast Length Adjustment of Short reads (v 1.2.11), USEARCH (v 7.0.1090), UCHIME (v 4.2.40), QIIME (v 1.80), RDP classifer (v 2.2) and mothur (v 1.31.2) were used for data analysis41. The metagenomic sequencing data of mouse fecal samples have been deposited in NCBI under accession code PRJNA1413482 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1413482].

Targeted metabolomics was applied to analyze mouse fecal samples41. Metabolite separation and quantification were conducted using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus (Waters, USA) coupled with a QTRAP6500 Plus high-sensitivity mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA)49. A data matrix containing information on metabolite identification and quantification results was generated using the quantification software Skyline (v 21.1.0.146). This matrix was then subjected to further analysis to extract the desired information. Raw data of the targeted metabolomics analysis of mouse fecal samples are provided in Supplementary Data 5. For each group of mice, assays and analyses of mouse fecal samples were all performed in six biologically independent replicates.

Statistics & reproducibility

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups, with twelve mice housed per cage. Six mice were randomly selected from each group for a full set of subsequent experimental analyses, and the remaining mice were retained as reserve samples for contingency. All mice in each group received the same treatment regimen during the experiment without any difference in feeding and administration. Alternating analyses were performed on each group during the daytime to eliminate any circadian rhythm and/or reagent-related biases. Histological analysis was conducted using a blinded approach, while other experiments were analyzed by the same researcher responsible for mouse treatment. The aforementioned randomization prevents any potential bias.

For the methanotrophic cultivations, monitoring mice during the oral administration experiment and the relevant biochemical assays, statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.6.0. Data were shown as the mean with SD. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test was used to evaluate the differences between variables (p > 0.05 (not significant, NS), *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). For transcriptomic analysis of methanotrophic bacterium cultivation, differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package in R (v 3.4.1). All statistical tests were two-sided (the default setting of DESeq2). The Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at the threshold of adjusted p-value <0.05.

The metagenomic sequencing data of mouse fecal samples was analyzed by the software R (v 3.4.1). For comparisons between two independent groups, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons involving multiple groups with non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. Additionally, UPGMA clustering was performed based on a Weighted Unifrac distance matrix. Details about the statistical tests are provided in the figure legends and the source data file.
