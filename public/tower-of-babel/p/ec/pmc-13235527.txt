Development of fermented dried camel meat using mixed starters and evaluation of physicochemical, textural, microbial, and volatile profiles during storage
Authors: Rendalai Si, Yuanyuan Li, Liang Ming, Jing He, Li Yi, Rimutu Ji, Tungalag Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCamel meat was fermented with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus (F group) and compared against an unfermented control (NF group). Subsequently, it was observed that the lightness value of the F group jerky increased over storage time and remained higher than that of the NF group. Conversely, pH values decreased during storage and were consistently lower in group F. Fermentation significantly reduced thiobarbituric acid and volatile base nitrogen contents. Headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry detected 103 flavor compounds. The main flavors in group F were alcohols, acids, and aldehydes, while group NF was characterized by esters, alcohols, and aldehydes. The relative content of these compounds was higher in group F after storage. Microbial analysis confirmed Lactiplantibacillus and Pediococcus as the dominant, more desirable microbiota in group F. This study provides a theoretical basis for the industrial production of fermented camel meat products.Keywords: Fermented camel jerky, Physicochemical quality, Volatile flavor substances, Microbial diversity

Introduction

Camels are commonly reared in low- and middle-income countries, and their meat is as nutritionally valuable as any other conventional type of meat (Zarrin et al., 2020). In fact, camel meat is an important ethnic food consumed in the arid regions of the Middle East, Northeast Africa, Australia, and China. Given its health benefits and nutritional value, camel meat is an excellent option for sustainable global meat consumption (Djenane & Aider, 2022). Although the technological aspects and quality characteristics of beef, lamb, and pork have been explored extensively, such studies on Bactrian camel meat remain extremely limited.

Fermented meat products are prepared using fresh meat as the primary raw material, which is fermented using microorganisms or enzymes under either natural or controlled conditions (Munekata et al., 2022). Such products are a staple in traditional Chinese cuisine, where they are favored for their distinct fermented flavor (Mao et al., 2024). Common examples of such fermented meat products include Jinhua ham, Xuanwei ham, Harbin red sausage, and Guizhou sour meat. Owing to advancements in modern production technology and equipment, fermentation has become a valuable processing technique in the meat industry, and the application of microbial fermentation to improve the texture, color, and flavor of meat and meat products is increasingly being recognized (Campolina et al., 2023). Studies have also shown that fermenting agents inhibit the growth of harmful microorganisms, enhance the appearance and taste of meat, and extend its shelf life through acid production and competitive inhibition (Ansarian et al., 2022), thereby meeting consumer demands for both quality and safety.

The flavor of fermented meat products is strongly influenced by the microbes involved in the fermentation process. In particular, mixed and multi-strain fermentation processes are often more beneficial than single-strain fermentation (Ansarian et al., 2022). For example, Candogan et al. (2009) found that when fermented beef sausages were inoculated with a combination of three different microorganisms—Latilactobacillus curvatus, Latilactobacillus sakeus, and Streptomyces griseus—the sausages underwent significant changes during fermentation, drying, and maturation. These processes led to the degradation of actin, troponin, and the myosin heavy and light chains and increased the non-protein nitrogen content of the sausages. Similarly, Pavli et al. (2020) found that pH reductions during fermentation inhibited pathogens, spoilage microbes, and other harmful microorganisms, thereby improving the safety and shelf life of meat products. Furthermore, Berardo et al. (2016) reported that nitrite and ascorbic acid reduced lipid oxidation and malondialdehyde formation in dry-fermented sausages, although their combined use increased carbonyl compound production.

In recent years, several advanced techniques have been developed for detecting flavor compounds in foods, including headspace solid-phase microextraction–gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), electronic nose (E-nose) analysis, and gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) (Hu et al., 2022). Among them, HS-SPME-GC–MS is widely used to detect the changes in volatile flavor substances in fermented foods during processing and storage. This method provides theoretical and technical support for the flavor modulation of fermented foods such as sausages and cured meats via the use of microorganisms (Li et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). However, research on the technological aspects and quality characteristics of camel meat in China has been limited so far. Camel meat products are relatively scarce on the market and often lack variety in terms of both texture and flavor. For example, traditional dried camel meat has a long production cycle and a relatively monotonous flavor profile (Zhang et al., 2024). To grow the camel meat industry and improve the utilization of camel meat, fermentation technology must be explored to diversify camel meat products and enhance their flavor.

In this study, we optimized fermentation parameters (time, temperature, and inoculum size) and analyzed the physicochemical, microbial, and flavor profiles of Bactrian camel jerky fermented using Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus. By comparing fermented and traditional jerky, this study sought to diversify camel meat products, enhance their flavor profiles, and promote their utilization in the food industry, providing a foundation for innovative applications of camel meat.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

All the meat samples utilized in this study were procured from the local market (namely, Lifa Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Industry Co., Ltd., in Bayanhot, Inner Mongolia, China). To provide a more detailed and precise account, for the present study, three three-year-old male Alxa Bactrian camels were randomly selected, and the semitendinosus muscles from the thickest parts of their hind legs were collected.

Two commercial standard strains of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum BNCC 336421 and Pediococcus pentosaceus BNCC 193259 were purchased from BeiNa Chuanglian Bio-technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The following reagents were purchased from Suolaibao Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China): MRS broth, MRS agar, and 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA). In addition, sodium hydroxide, n-Heptane (chromatographic grade), and petroleum ether (30 °C–60 °C) were purchased from the Tianjin New Technology Industrial Park Kemao Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Preparation of fermentation starter

Based on the given instructions, the strains were inoculated in MRS broth and cultured at 37 °C. After successive subculturing for three generations (each with a cycle of 18 h), the activated strains were centrifuged to collect bacterial pellets, which were resuspended in 20% glycerol and stored in a refrigerator at −80 °C.

Prior to use, the OD600 value of each bacterial suspension was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) to preliminarily estimate the bacterial concentration. The viable cell count (CFU/mL) was then precisely determined via plate counting. Finally, sterile saline was used to adjust the bacterial concentration to 107 CFU/mL.

Camel jerky production

The camel jerky was prepared using the procedure proposed by Guo et al. (2024), with slight modifications. The key steps were as follows: thawing, cleaning, cutting, marinating, fermenting, drying, and vacuum packaging.

The fascia was removed from the thawed camel meat, and the meat was cleaned and evenly cut into 4 × 4 × 2 cm strips along the direction of the muscle fibers. Then, 2% edible salt, 2% cooking wine, 1% sucrose, 1% glucose, and 0.0035% white pepper were added for seasoning, and the meat was pickled at 4 °C for 12 h. Mixed fermentation with a 1:1 ratio of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus was carried out. The process parameters were as follows: inoculation amount: 107 CFU/g; fermentation time: 12 h; fermentation temperature: 32 °C. After fermentation, the meat was dried in an oven at 50 °C for 6 h, vacuum-packaged (Packaging Sealing Machine, QT-124, China), and stored at 25 °C. The quality changes in the camel jerky were examined at 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, and 30 d of storage.

Two experimental groups were designed for this study: the unfermented group (NF group) refers to samples without starter culture inoculation, corresponding to traditional dried camel jerky prepared without fermentation treatment; the fermented group (F group) refers to samples inoculated with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus, followed by the specified fermentation process.

Measurement of physical and chemical indicators

Determination of color

The method developed by Popp et al. (2013) was adopted to determine the color differences, with slight modifications. A color difference meter was calibrated with black and white sheets. During the color measurements, the samples were rotated 120° in the clockwise direction, each sample was analyzed thrice, and the lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) values of all the samples were subsequently obtained.

Texture profile analysis

A TA-XTC18 texture analyzer was utilized, and the measurement parameters were based on the report by Liu et al. (2024) with slight modifications. The samples were cut into 1 × 1 × 1 cm blocks in the direction of the extended muscle fibers, and the specific parameters were set as follows: target deformation, 50%; pre-test speed, 1 mm/s; test speed, 1 mm/s; post-test speed, 2 mm/s; and trigger point value, 8.00 gf. The cyclic tests were repeated twice.

Determination of water activity (aw)

First, 5 g of each meat sample was homogenized at 20–25 °C and spread evenly to cover the bottom of a small container. Then, an HD-3 A intelligent water activity meter (Jiahang Bochuang Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was employed to measure the water activity (Aw) of the meat samples.

Determination of pH value

The pH value was determined using the method reported by Stegmayer et al. (2023). Specifically, 2 g of dried camel meat sample was made into minced meat, mixed with 10 times the volume of deionized water, homogenized, and then left to stand for 30 min. Subsequently, the pH of the slurry was measured using a pH meter equipped with a glass electrode (FE28 Benchtop pH Meter, Mettler-Toledo, Leicester, UK).

Determination of Total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) content

The TVB-N value was determined using the automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method based on GB 5009.228–2016 (i.e., the National Standard for Food Safety—Determination of Volatile Basic Nitrogen in Food).

Determination of TBA content

Using a slightly modified version of the method reported by Alfaia et al. (2018), 5 g of different camel jerky samples was ground uniformly and placed in a 100 mL conical flask, to which 25 mL of a 7.5% solution containing 0.1% disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and trichloroacetic acid was added. The mixture was homogenized for 10 min before filtration. Then, 5 mL of the filtrate and 5 mL of a 0.02 M TBA solution were mixed evenly and incubated in a boiling water bath. After 40 min, the mixture was removed and cooled to room temperature before centrifugation at 6000 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant was mixed with 5 mL of trichloromethane and shaken well. Proteolysis determination

Myofibrillar protein extraction

Myofibrillar proteins were extracted from the camel jerky based on the method described by Mauriello et al. (2002), with slight modifications. A 2 g sample of camel jerky was mixed with 20 mL of 0.02 mol/L of pH 6.5 phosphate buffer (PB) and homogenized on an ice bath. After centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 4 °C, 20 min), the supernatant was removed to obtain the myoplasmic protein extract. The precipitate was resuspended in 20 mL of 0.03 moL/L pH 7.4 PB containing 0.1% (v/v) TritonX-100, homogenized on an ice bath, and then centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 4 °C, 20 min); the above operation was repeated thrice. The precipitate was resuspended in eight times the volume of 0.1 mol/L pH 6.5 PB containing 0.7 moL/L KI, homogenized on an ice bath, and centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 4 °C, 20 min). The supernatant served as the myofibrillar protein extract. The protein concentration of this extract was determined using the bioconjugate method, with bovine serum protein serving as the standard. The extract was then diluted to a concentration of 2 mg/mL.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

First, 40 μL of myofibrillar protein was obtained from the F and NF group samples collected at different storage time points. Then, 10 μL of 4× sampling buffer (containing DDT) was added, and the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 5 min to denature the protein.

Based on the instructions of the Solepol SDS-PAGE gel preparation kit, a 10% separating gel and 5% concentrated gel were prepared. After the gels solidified, they were placed in the electrophoresis tank along with buffer, and 5 μL of each sample was added to the gel lanes. Subsequently, electrophoresis was performed, first at 80 V for 30 min and then at 120 V after each sample had entered the separation gel. The electrophoresis was continued until the samples crossed the cut-off mark.

The gel was stained by heating it with Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining solution (0.25 g of Kaomas Brilliant Blue, 45 mL of methanol, 10 mL of glacial acetic acid, and 45 mL of ultrapure water), and it was then washed with decolorizing solution (100 mL of glacial acetic acid, 50 mL of anhydrous ethanol, and 850 mL of purified water) until bands appeared. Subsequently, the gel was heated at 45 °C for 20 min in the decolorizing solution, placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C to decolorize it overnight, and then observed on the following day.

High-throughput sequencing

DNA extraction

The DNA extraction was carried out as described by Shao, Wang, Song, Xu, Cai, et al. (2024). Specifically, total genomic DNA was extracted from the microbial communities of the stored camel jerky according to the instructions of the E.Z.N.A.®Soil DNA kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). The quality of the extracted genomic DNA was tested using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration and purity were assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 system.

PCR amplification

The PCR amplification of the extracted DNA was performed using 338F and 806R primers and the following amplification procedure: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 29 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 53 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; followed by stable extension at 72 °C for 10 min; and finally, storage at 4 °C. The 20 μL reaction system contained 2 × ProTaq (10 μL), the upstream primer (5 μM) (0.8 μL), the downstream primer (5 μM) (0.8 μL), and the template DNA (10 ng/μL), and the volume was made up to 20 μL with ddH2O. A 2% agarose gel was used to recover the PCR products.

Sequencing

After recovering the amplified products, high-throughput sequencing was performed to taxonomically analyze the representative amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Various algorithms were applied for the species annotation and assessment, species composition analysis, and species diversity analysis. Finally, intergroup microbial differences were explored via statistical analysis (IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 statistical software, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Volatile flavor substances

As described by Shao, Wang, Song, Xu, Sun, and Xu (2024), with slight modifications, 0.8 g of dried camel meat was added to a 20 mL headspace vial, and 10 μL of 2-octanol was added as the internal standard. Then, GC–MS was performed for the analysis.

The GC conditions were as follows: extraction temperature, 60 °C; preheating time, 15 min; extraction time, 30 min; resolution time, 4 min; mode, splitless; spacer purge flow rate, 3 mL/min; carrier gas, He; chromatographic column, DB-Wax (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm); column flow rate, 1 mL/min; column chamber heating procedure, 40 °C for 4 min, raised to 245 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and held for 5 min; pre-sample inlet temperature, 250 °C; transfer line temperature, 250 °C; ion source temperature, 230 °C; quadrupole temperature, 150 °C; four-stage rod temperature, 150 °C; ionization voltage, −70 eV; mass range, m/z 20–400; scan mode, scan; solvent delay, 2.37 min.

The compound composition was analyzed using Chroma TOF software (V4.3×, LECO), and the NIST libraries were used to obtain the mass spectral data.

Sensory evaluation

All sensory analyses were conducted in accordance with the Principles of Good Practice (Lawless & Heymann, 1999). A sensory panel composed of 10 members from the Teaching Research Section of Food Quality and Safety, College of Food Science and Engineering, Inner Mongolia Agricultural University evaluated the sensory profiles using a descriptive analysis method. All taste-related materials and instruments used in the study were food-grade to ensure participant safety. According to the guidelines of the Institutional Review Board of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University, ethical approval was not required for this research. Nevertheless, all procedures were conducted in accordance with established protocols to safeguard participants' rights and privacy throughout the study. These protocols included voluntary participation, the right to withdraw at any time, and full disclosure of research requirements and risks. In addition, written or oral consent was obtained from the participants, and their data were not released without their consent.

All participants participated voluntarily and signed the informed consent form before sample tasting. The selected panelists had received more than 3 years of specialized training in the sensory characteristics of meat and meat products and had previously participated in multiple sensory evaluations for jerky analysis. All sensory analyses were performed in a dedicated sensory evaluation laboratory. Panelists were only provided with sample codes and were not informed of the specific sample types throughout the evaluation. In addition, room-temperature water and expectoration cups were provided.

This sensory evaluation included five attributes: color, aroma, taste, mouthfeel, and overall acceptability. The overall acceptability score was calculated as follows: (Color score + Aroma score + Taste score + Mouthfeel score) /4. The evaluation criteria for the other four attributes are presented in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and the data were expressed as means ± standard errors. An Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software. Differences were deemed significant at P < 0.05. Origin 2021 software was used to prepare the plots.

pH analysis

As shown in Fig. 1A, the pH of the camel jerky showed an overall decrease during the storage period. In the F group, the pH decreased from 5.1 to 4.5, with a significant decrease (P < 0.05) observed between days 1 and 15. In contrast, the pH in the NF group, which was not inoculated with fermenting agents, showed no significant change (P > 0.05).

Analysis of water activity (aw)

As shown in Fig. 1B, the Aw of the F group showed a significant upward shift between 1 d and 10 d of storage (P < 0.05); however, a downward trend was noted at 10–30 d (P > 0.05), which was not statistically significant. The Aw of the NF group increased between 1 d and 5 d of storage (P < 0.05) and demonstrated no significant change at 5–30 d. The effect of the starter cultures on the Aw in the late storage period was not significant.

TVB-N analysis

As shown in Fig. 2A, the TVB-N contents in both the F and NF groups increased during the storage period. During the first 1–3 days of storage, the F group had slightly higher TVB-N levels (5.92 mg/100 g and 6.19 mg/100 g) than those of the NF group (5.34 mg/100 g and 5.56 mg/100 g). However, by day 5 of storage, the TVB-N content in the F group (6.15 mg/100 g) was lower than that in the NF group (9.19 mg/100 g). At day 30, the F group showed a TVB-N content of 12.76 mg/100 g, which was lower than that of the NF group (13.81 mg/100 g).

TBA analysis

As shown in Fig. 2B, the TBA values in both the F and NF groups showed increasing trends during storage. The TBA levels increased significantly (P < 0.05) between days 1 and 10 and between days 15 and 30 in the F group, while in the NF group, the increase was significant (P < 0.05) from days 3 to 5. Between days 3 and 10, the TBA content in the NF group was higher than that in the F group. At day 30, the TBA value in the F group was 0.36 mg/100 g, while it was 0.42 mg/100 g in the NF group, indicating more pronounced lipid oxidation in the NF group during the storage period.

Protein degradation analysis

The degradation of myofibrillar proteins in the camel jerky during the storage period is shown in Fig. 3. Myofibrillar proteins account for more than 50% of the protein composition of the camel muscle and exhibit characteristic bands corresponding to the myosin light chain (20 kDa), actin (45 kDa), troponin (75 kDa), pro-myosin (80 kDa), α-actinin (94 kDa), and the myosin heavy chain (MHC) (220 kDa). In the protein electrophoresis results from days 1 to 3, the F group showed a gradual weakening of the myosin light chain (20 kDa) bands as the storage time increased, indicating the onset of protein degradation. By day 10, the myosin light chain bands in the F group had completely disappeared, while in the NF group, the bands persisted, although they were less intense. The myosin heavy chain (MHC) (220 kDa) bands in the F group were lighter than those in the NF group between days 1 and 5, further indicating that the fermentation in the F group promoted protein catabolism.

Analysis of bacterial composition at the genus level

Fig. 4A mainly demonstrates the changes in the compositions of the dominant species in the F and NF groups during the storage period. The low-abundance species are categorized as Others. Notably, Lactiplantibacillus and Pediococcus were the dominant genera in the F group on day 1, accounting for relative percentages of 66.5% and 25.5%, respectively. In the F group, Lactiplantibacillus accounted for the highest percentage of all the bacteria (74.5%) at 30 d, and the highest relative abundance of Staphylococcus was 30.8% at 5 d.

Species and bacterial composition during storage: (A) columnar diagram showing the species composition during the storage period; (B) columnar diagram of differences in bacterial composition during the storage period.

In the NF group, on day 1, Acinetobacter was the dominant genera, with a relative abundance of 10.5%. Moreover, at 3–5 d of storage, the relative abundance of Lactiplantibacillus gradually increased to 18.1%, and it thus emerged as the dominant genus. After 10 days of storage, Pseudomonas became the dominant genus in the NF group, with a relative abundance of 13.1%; however, its relative abundance gradually decreased to 4.7% after 30 d of storage. At this time point, Lactiplantibacillus (20.1%) once again emerged as the dominant genus.

Fig. 4B shows the differences in the mean relative abundances of the key microbes among the different groups. Notably, the genera Pediococcus and Lactiplantibacillus showed highly significant differences (P < 0.001) across groups F_1d, NF_1d, F_5d, NF_5d, F_10d, NF_10d, F_15d, and NF_15d. Their abundance in the F group was generally higher than that in the NF group. Notably, Pediococcus showed a relative abundance higher than 60% in the F group throughout the storage period, reaching a maximum value of 70.46% at 30 d and a minimum of 64.7% at 5 d. In the NF group, the relative abundance of this genus at 30 d was 20.11%, which was much lower than the lowest abundance value seen in the F group.

The abundance values of Lactiplantibacillus in both the F and NF groups showed an initial decrease followed by an increase, and the highest value in the NF group was much lower than the lowest value in the F group, which was due to the inoculation of the F group with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus. The abundance of Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter in the NF group was significantly higher than that in the F group at 15–30 d of storage (P < 0.001). Pseudomonas spp. are Gram-negative bacilli with pods and no spores, and they can trigger the spoilage and deterioration of food products. Thus, we can infer that the safety of the camel jerky was higher in the F group than that in the NF group.

Analysis of volatile flavor substances

The volatile metabolites present in the fermented and unfermented camel jerky were analyzed using SPME/GC–MS after 1, 3, 5, 15, and 30 d of storage. A total of 103 volatile flavor compounds were detected in the camel jerky samples from the F and NF groups (as shown in Table S2), including 21 alcohols, 13 alkanes, 7 olefins, 13 ketones, 15 esters, 17 aldehydes, 4 amines, 3 acids, 1 pyrazine, and 9 other compounds.

As shown in Fig. 5A, the relative levels of alcohols, acids, and aldehydes were higher in the F group, and the aldehydes reached a maximum relative level of 2.562 at 30 d. Moreover, the alcohols reached a maximum relative level of 10.654 at 3 d, while the acids reached a maximum relative level of 3.924 at 15 d. In the NF group, esters, aldehydes, and alcohols constituted the highest proportion of flavor compounds (Fig. 5B). The relative level of esters showed an increasing trend from days 1 to 3, peaking at 1.274 after 3 days before plateauing. Alcohols made the most significant contribution to the flavor of the camel jerky in this group, reaching the lowest level of 5.052 at 5 d and the highest level of 10.886 at 30 d. Overall, between 15 and 30 days of storage, the relative levels of the flavor substances were higher in the F group than those in the NF group, demonstrating that fermentation can add flavor to camel jerky.

Analysis of sensory evaluation

Sensory scores of camel jerky at different storage times are shown in Fig. S1. Among the individual sensory indicators, regarding color, the score of the F group exhibited a continuous upward trend with the extension of storage time, stabilizing above 7 points after 10 days and showing a prominent color advantage. In contrast, the score of the NF group remained relatively low without an obvious upward trend, and the gap in color scores between the two groups gradually widened with prolonged storage time. In terms of aroma, the score of the F group remained consistently higher than that of the NF group throughout the period and peaked at 15 days of storage. This phenomenon was closely associated with the abundant aldehydes and acids in the jerky of the F group, which contributed to the formation of a unique fermented aroma. In contrast, the NF group maintained a relatively low aroma score due to its high sulfide content. In terms of taste scores, both groups exhibited an upward trend with prolonged storage time, with the F group showing more significant score fluctuations. The free amino acid content in the F group accumulated continuously, reaching 30.28 mg/100 g at 30 days. Meanwhile, its fatty acid ratio became more optimized, with a PUFA/SFA value of 0.45 at 15 days, when the taste reached the optimal level with a score of 7.5 points, presenting a rich and mellow flavor. In contrast, the NF group maintained a consistently lower taste score due to its persistently low free amino acid content, imbalanced fatty acid composition, and the absence of taste optimization effects induced by fermentation, resulting in a bland overall taste. In terms of mouthfeel, the hardness (286–292 N), springiness (0.94 mm), and water activity (0.82) of the F group all fell within the optimal range at 10–15 days of storage, with the corresponding mouthfeel scores reaching 7.0–7.2 points. In contrast, the NF group had a significantly lower score due to its relatively high water activity, which resulted in a soft texture in the later storage stage.

Discussion

The appearance of meat products is the main factor influencing consumer decisions. The higher the L* and a* values, the better the acceptance of jerky products. The F group showed a higher brightness value than that of the NF group at 30 d of storage. These trends are similar to those reported by Zhang et al. (2020), who observed an increase in the L* values of fermented sour meat. The increase in L* values results from biochemical changes during fermentation, including the breakdown of proteins and lipids into smaller peptides, amino acids, and metabolites that enhance light scattering and reflectance, increasing lightness. Additionally, lactic acid and other organic acids produced during fermentation lower the pH, stabilizing myoglobin and contributing to a brighter appearance, collectively explaining the color improvement in dried camel meat. A gradual decrease in the a* values was detected due to the low moisture content of the jerky and the concentration of pigmented substances within the meat, which led to a deepening of the color. Therefore, the findings show that fermentation can improve the brightness of dried camel meat.

The hardness of jerky is important for its overall taste. According to the results, the hardness of both the F and NF groups gradually increased as the storage time was prolonged, which was due to the drying process, which reduced the moisture content of the jerky, gradually making its structure denser, demonstrating that fermentation increases the hardness of jerky. The results of this experiment were consistent with those reported by Gawborisut and Muengkratok (2024), who studied the increase in the hardness of fermented minced fish sausages. Similarly, this increase could be attributed to the denaturation of proteins in an acidic environment, as well as to the acid-induced increase in the gel hardness (Zhao et al., 2022).

The pH value of the fermented Bactrian camel jerky showed an overall downward trend during the storage period, which was primarily due to the lactic acid produced by the action of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus on the carbohydrates present in the meat. On day 10, there was a slight increase in the pH, likely due to the production of amines from protein breakdown. However, between days 15 and 30, the pH remained relatively stable. These findings are consistent with the results reported by Wang, Hu, et al. (2021), who studied the fermentation of fish protein by the proteolytic bacterium Bacillus subtilis A26. The pH remained lower in the F group than in the NF group, confirming that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus showed a high acid production capacity. A pH below 4.6 inhibits the growth of pathogenic and spoilage microorganisms, which is crucial for ensuring the safety and shelf life of food products (Lv et al., 2023).

The Aw is an important index of the quality of stored fermented meat. The high Aw of the F group could be a consequence of the starter culture addition. The decrease in the water content may be attributed to the fermentation process, during which the pH of the F group dropped to the isoelectric point of the proteins. This reduction caused a decrease in the water-holding capacity of the muscle proteins, which further lowered the water activity (Wang et al., 2022). The reduced water activity likely played a role in the maintenance of the microbial stability and overall quality of the fermented jerky.

The TVB-N is a key indicator of the meat product quality, reflecting the production of basic nitrogen-containing compounds, such as ammonia and amines, which are generated during protein breakdown by enzymes and bacteria during meat spoilage. A higher TVB-N value indicates a greater level of volatile nitrogen and a higher degree of meat spoilage. During the storage period, the TVB-N contents in both the F and NF groups increased. In addition, the TVB-N content in the F group was always lower than that in the NF group for the following reasons: Fermenting microorganisms inhibit harmful bacteria growth, reducing the ammonia and amine production during protein decomposition, and organic acids from fermentation lower the pH, inhibiting the enzymes that produce TVB-N. Moreover, microbial protein decomposition mainly yields non-volatile beneficial substances instead of large amounts of TVB-N, enhancing product safety and stability (Lv et al., 2019).

TBA is an important measure of the malondialdehyde (MDA) content, a primary by-product of lipid oxidation. The lipid oxidation was higher in the NF group than in the F group after 30 d of storage, suggesting that fermentation could have inhibited lipid oxidation as the storage was prolonged. The F group likely showed lower levels of lipid oxidation because Lactiplantibacillus can produce proteases that break down oxidative metabolites and slow down lipid oxidation in camel jerky. These findings align with the findings reported by Gao et al. (2024), who showed that the TBA values in Lacticaseibacillus helveticus UJBH1-inoculated fermented mutton sausages were consistently lower than those in the control samples.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis revealed that fermentation with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus promoted the degradation of the myosin heavy (220 kDa) and light (20 kDa) chains in the camel meat. Similarly, Li et al. (2024) showed that the mixed fermentation of sausages using Lactococcus lactis and Bacillus mycoides can accelerate protein degradation, with the degradation degree increasing as the fermentation time is prolonged. The reasons are as follows: These microbes produce proteases during fermentation to break peptide bonds in proteins, and they also change environmental conditions such as the pH and redox potential, making proteins less stable. In mixed fermentation, there is a synergistic effect where the metabolites of one microbe enhance the enzyme-producing ability of the other, and as the fermentation time is prolonged, more enzymes are produced, increasing the degree of protein degradation.

In this study, we used the Chao1, Shannon, ACE, and Simpson indices to assess the richness and diversity of the bacterial communities in the camel meat samples. The greater the values of the Chao1 and ACE indices, the higher the species richness. Moreover, the greater the value of the Shannon index and the lower the value of the Simpson index, the higher the community diversity (Jia et al., 2020). During storage, non-significant index changes (P > 0.05) were due to a stable microbial community from constant environmental conditions and balanced interactions; the F group had lower species richness (lower Chao1 and ACE indices) as the fermentation screened the community, and the NF group had higher bacterial diversity (a higher Shannon index and lower Simpson index) with a diverse and evenly distributed microbial population. Moreover, in the present study, Lactiplantibacillus and Staphylococcus remained the dominant genera in the F group throughout the storage period, with Lactiplantibacillus showing a continuous increase and Staphylococcus showing an increase followed by a decrease. Similarly, Lactiplantibacillus and Staphylococcus were found to be the most dominant genera in fermented meat rice in a previous study (Wang, Wang, et al., 2021).

The flavor of fermented meat products is derived from various spices and the compounds generated due to carbohydrate metabolism, lipid oxidation, and amino acid catabolism via microbial action (Chen et al., 2021). Alcohols, acids, and aldehydes were the main flavor substances in the F group due to the high amount of lactic acid produced via microbial action during meat fermentation, whereas esters, alcohols, and aldehydes were the main flavor substances in the NF group. Differences in the relative levels of the same flavor compounds at different storage time points impacted the flavor of the camel jerky during storage.

Esters are a class of compounds produced via the esterification of short-chain fatty acids and alcohols, and they have a low threshold value, which promotes the formation of flavor and can give products a fruity aroma (Uzkuc et al., 2020). The main esters detected in the camel jerky during the storage period were ethyl acetate and ethyl lactate. Among them, the ethyl acetate levels in the F group showed an initial increase followed by a decrease, and the relative levels of ethyl acetate peaked at 0.208 after 3 d in the F group and at 0.289 after 5 d in the NF group. The lowest levels were reached after 30 d in both groups (0.043 and 0.042 in the F and NF groups, respectively). The relative level of ethyl lactate was higher in the F group than in the NF group. Aldehydes, which generally have a low threshold value, are an important flavor component of fermented meat products. The main aldehydes detected in the camel jerky were hexanal, nonanal, and heptanal. In the F group, the nonanal levels peaked at 0.211 after 15 d, and the heptanal levels peaked at 0.302 after 30 d, which were higher than the levels in the NF group. A significant change in the n-pentanal levels was detected in the F group. Sun et al. (2023) also found a significant change in the glutaraldehyde levels in fermented sausages, consistent with these findings. Moreover, Wang et al. (2019) discovered that the nonanal levels were higher in fermented sausages than those in unfermented ones. Notably, nonanal, hexanal, octanal, and heptanal are straight-chain aldehydes produced through the degradation of unsaturated fatty acids. Hexanal is a product of lipid oxidation and has a fresh, grassy odor, while nonanal has an herbal odor. Nonanal and hexanal are typically considered the main flavor components of processed meat products. Alcohols are important volatile components produced by lactic acid bacteria during the fermentation process. Small amounts of alcohol impart rose, citrus, and floral flavors to food products and can serve as good solvents for other aromatic substances (Wu et al., 2020). Alcohols are mainly produced by microorganisms that metabolize sugars and prevent the deterioration in the meat quality during processing. In this study, the main alcohols detected in the camel jerky were 1-octen-3-ol and ethanol. Similarly, Lv et al. (2019) detected significant differences in the contents of alcohols, mainly ethanol and 1-octen-3-ol, in traditional fermented sour meat. Notably, 1-octen-3-ol could impart both mushroom and fruit flavors to camel jerky. Ketones are mainly products of fat oxidation and the degradation of amino groups, while acids largely originate from the oxidative degradation of glycerol and phospholipids. The main ketones detected in this study were 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, 2,3-octanedione, and sec-octanone. Moreover, only three types of flavor-producing acids were detected, namely, pentadecanoic acid, α-linolenic acid, and acetic acid, of which acetic acid showed the highest content. Among the other compounds detected, the main olefins were β-stilbene and lauricene, and the main alkanes were decane and dodecane. Only four amines were detected: caprolactam, acetamide, N-methylbutanediamide, and N,N-diethylformamide, with caprolactam showing the highest relative levels. Among the pyrazines, which are products of the Maillard reaction between sugar and free amino acids and impart a distinct roasted nut aroma to food products, only 2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine was detected in the camel jerky.

Additionally, the sensory score comparison results demonstrated that all sensory indicator scores of the jerky in the F group were significantly higher than those in the NF group. This difference may be associated with the variations in protein composition and microbial content between the two groups, confirming that fermentation exerts a significant positive regulatory effect on the sensory quality of camel jerky.

Conclusion

In this study, camel jerky was prepared using Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus as fermenting agents. Fermentation resulted in a brighter color, lower pH, and reduced TVB-N and TBA levels relative to nonfermented samples, and it also improved the safety of camel jerky, promoted the degradation of the myosin heavy and light chains, and enhanced the acidic flavor components. On a microbial level, the fermented jerky was dominated by Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus, while the nonfermented jerky showed higher abundances of Pseudomonas and Fusobacterium. These findings highlight the potential of fermentation for diversifying camel meat products. However, this study was limited to two fermentation agents. Thus, future studies should explore more strains and assess the storage stability, flavor, nutrition, consumer acceptance, and scalability of fermented camel meat products for commercialization.
