Posteromedial papillary muscle perforation with preserved electrical parameters: A novel complication of left bundle branch pacing

Introduction

Left bundle branch pacing (LBBP) has emerged as a more physiological pacing than conventional right ventricular pacing (RVP) for patients with bradycardia, offering low and stable capture thresholds.1,2 Although LBBP-related complications including septal perforation (8%–14%), lead dislodgement (1%–3%), and right bundle branch injury (9%) have been described, papillary muscle perforation represents a previously unreported complication.3

LBBP using a pacing system analyzer (PSA) with modified surface leads has been described and validated when an electrophysiological pacing system (EPS) is unavailable.4,5 However, real-time visualization of subtle QRS and current of injury (COI) changes may be more limited during lead advancement.

Case report

A 78-year-old woman with severe aortic stenosis underwent successful transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), during which she developed left bundle branch (LBB) block, followed by complete atrioventricular block. Previous medication included lisinopril 20 mg for hypertension and atorvastatin 20 mg for dyslipidemia. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a nondilated left ventricle (LV) with a septal thickness of 15 mm, a preserved LV ejection fraction of 56%, and a bioprosthetic percutaneous aortic valve without signs of dysfunction. Electrocardiogram (ECG) immediately after TAVR showed sinus rhythm with LBB block and a QRS duration of 158 ms. In the first hours after TAVR, she developed complete atrioventricular block.

Dual-chamber pacemaker implantation with LBBP was performed. The generator was implanted in the left infraclavicular position, and venous access was obtained via ultrasound-guided puncture of the left axillary vein. A right atrial lead was positioned in the right atrial appendage, and a stylet-driven ventricular lead (UltiPace 58 cm, Abbott Medical, Sylmar, CA) was advanced toward the LBB area with the help of a guiding sheath (CPS Locator 3D Medium, Abbott Medical). The procedure was performed with modified II, III, aVF, V1, and V6 leads connected to a PSA (Merlin 2, Abbott Medical), given that an EPS was not available at the time. Initial implantation site in the midseptum proved unsuccessful to progress the lead, so an inferior position (with negative QRS in leads II and III and a notch in the S wave in V1) was attempted. The lead was screwed in under continuous fluoroscopy with unipolar pacing at 5 V at 0.5 ms and was stopped after the appearance of an r′ in V1 accompanied by a drop in COI. At this point, the R-wave peak time in V6 (V6 RWPT) was 74 ms, the V6–V1 R-wave interpeak time was 35 ms, and the QRS duration was 125 ms, suggestive of conduction system capture. The threshold for loss of capture was 0.25 V at 0.4 ms. However, there was not a change in QRS morphology during threshold testing. The QRS morphology and electrical parameters were stable and similar both before and after sheath slitting. Final device programming was in DDD mode at 60–130 beats/min, with unipolar ventricular pacing at 2.5 V, bipolar ventricular sensing of 14 mV, and a ventricular lead impedance of 380 Ω. Anteroposterior fluoroscopy image at the end of the procedure showed apparently appropriate atrial and ventricular lead positioning (Supplemental Figure 1).

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. Electrical parameters were stable on the day after the implantation, and the chest radiograph showed apparently adequate positioning of the leads, similar to the end of the procedure (Figure 1). However, a TTE performed the next day as part of routine post-TAVR evaluation suggested septal perforation (Figure 2; Supplemental Videos 1 and 2). A 12-lead ECG obtained during ventricular pacing demonstrated a right bundle branch block–like morphology with a superior axis and a prominent Rr′ (“bunny-ear”) configuration in lead V1, a pattern consistent with pacing from the posteromedial papillary muscle (PPM) rather than true LBB capture (Figure 3). Cardiac computed tomography scan definitively confirmed that the lead tip was positioned within the PPM, having traversed the interventricular septum and entered the LV cavity, before anchoring to the PPM (Figure 4).

The lead was successfully repositioned the next day without complications.

Discussion

This case represents the first reported case of an LBBP lead perforating the PPM during implantation, with favorable electrical parameters, a previously undescribed complication of conduction system pacing.

The mechanism likely involves excessive septal penetration during lead deployment, with the lead tip extending beyond the LV septum into the LV cavity and subsequently perforating and anchoring within the PPM. The anatomic proximity of the mid-to-apical interventricular septum to the PPM base makes this trajectory anatomically plausible, particularly if the lead is advanced too deeply or at an unfavorable angle.6

In our case, cardiac computed tomography was essential to confirm lead tip positioning and actually showed septal perforation, with a short intracavitary trajectory before being anchored in the PPM. During lead progression, COI monitoring using the PSA showed an initial rise followed by a decrease, which was interpreted as proximity to the LV endocardium. A sudden COI drop or reversal after septal perforation has been described; however, this was not observed.3 Notably, significant baseline drift and artifacts in the PSA tracings may have limited detection of subtle COI and QRS changes during lead advancement. Theoretically, if the lead were to penetrate the septum right next to the PPM base, it could reach the muscle without any intracavitary trajectory, and therefore, no sudden drop in COI or change in lead impedance is detected.

In the final lead position, COI was still positive and QRS showed an rR′ configuration in the modified lead V1 and an rS in modified lead V6 with V6 RWPT of 74 ms and an interpeak interval of 35 ms. No loss of capture was noted during penetration of the septum. Although no QRS morphology transition during threshold testing was noted, left bundle area pacing was likely and the procedure was terminated.7

Importantly, pacing parameters and QRS morphology were stable before and after sheath slitting, making additional lead advancement at that stage unlikely. Theoretically, torque buildup could result in additional lead advancement after stopping rotations, particularly with stiffer leads. Although we cannot completely exclude minimal delayed progression, we believe that it is unlikely to have occurred, given that no substantial torque buildup was noted and electrical parameters remained stable throughout.

The next day, electrical parameters were stable and the chest radiograph did not show any complications. Without postprocedural TTE and 12-lead ECG, this complication might have gone unrecognized. This highlights the importance of performing a 12-lead ECG after LBBP to confirm adequate pacing morphology, given that electrical parameters alone may be insufficient to detect lead malposition. The stable R-wave amplitude (14 mV), low capture threshold (0.25 V), and reasonable impedance (380 Ω) were all within acceptable ranges for LBBP, demonstrating that the lead tip remained embedded in viable myocardium despite being in an aberrant location.

Pacing from the PPM can produce a right bundle branch block morphology with a superior axis, which could be similar to left posterior fascicle stimulation. However, the prominent Rr′ “bunny-like” configuration seen in the 12-lead ECG in lead V1 (which was not obvious during implantation) suggested an anomalous location of the lead. Although TTE was the first diagnostic clue in our case, echocardiography may overestimate septal perforation and routine postprocedural TTE may not be necessary in all cases.3,8

In addition, the use of a PSA rather than an EPS during implantation likely contributed to failure to recognize the complication intraprocedurally. EPS systems allow continuous, real-time monitoring of unipolar and bipolar electrograms, unipolar impedance, and paced QRS morphology during lead advancement. Beat-to-beat assessment of V6 RWPT, QRS morphology changes, and unipolar impedance can identify the precise moment of LBB capture and detect septal perforation immediately.7,9 PSA systems provide more limited information and may not reliably detect the subtle electrogram changes that herald complications, although recent studies have shown that a PSA can accurately measure LBBP criteria when baseline artifacts are minimal.4,5

Intraprocedural imaging may also play a complementary role. Although intracardiac echocardiography is not routinely used during LBBP owing to the high success rate achieved with fluoroscopy and electrical guidance alone, it may be helpful in anatomically complex cases, in patients with significant structural heart disease, or when a precise LBB implantation site is targeted.10

The clinical significance of this rare complication includes potential for lead instability, although in this case the lead appeared mechanically stable. Theoretical concerns include mitral valve dysfunction from papillary muscle injury, although this was not observed. The thromboembolic risk of a lead traversing the LV cavity, even briefly, should be considered in anticoagulation management decisions. Although there are no specific guidelines for anticoagulation after lead perforation into the LV cavity, we decided to anticoagulate the patient for 1 month after lead reposition, based on the principle that short-term anticoagulation may reduce thromboembolic risk in the setting of endocardial injury and intracavitary lead exposure.11

Conclusion

This case highlights PPM perforation as a possible complication of LBBP. Despite normal electrical parameters, the 12-lead ECG provided critical diagnostic information that identified the lead malposition. Unlike overt septal perforation where lead repositioning is prompted by obvious electrophysiological changes during implantation, PPM perforation may be occult if the lead stays intramyocardially, given that stable electrical parameters can be maintained despite aberrant lead position. This complication underscores the importance of multimodal assessment during LBBP implantation, including careful 12-lead ECG analysis to confirm appropriate pacing morphology. The use of an EPS rather than a PSA should be considered whenever possible during implantation, given that it provides valuable real-time information including continuous unipolar impedance monitoring and detailed electrogram analysis that can detect complications immediately.
