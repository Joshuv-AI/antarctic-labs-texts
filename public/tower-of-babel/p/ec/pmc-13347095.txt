Is breast-conserving surgery feasible for patients with prior breast augmentation?: a single-center retrospective case series
Authors: Mi Young Jang, Jae Bok Lee, Woo Young Kim, Yong Yeup Kim, Jai Hyun Chung, Ji Hye Kim, Ye Ryung Kim, Sang Uk Woo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBreast cancer has the highest incidence among Korean women. With the global rise in breast augmentation, the number of patients diagnosed with breast cancer after augmentation is increasing. Surgical management in this group remains controversial, as breast-conserving surgery (BCS) followed by radiation therapy poses challenges such as capsular contracture, poor cosmetic outcomes, and difficulty obtaining clear margins. This study aimed to assess the feasibility and clinical outcomes of BCS with radiation therapy in breast cancer patients with prior augmentation.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective case series review of 4,568 patients who underwent breast cancer surgery at Korea University Guro Hospital between 2008 and 2023. Seventeen patients had prior breast augmentation. Clinical records, imaging, pathology, and surgical data were reviewed for tumor characteristics, implant placement, treatment, and recurrence. Postoperative mammography was used to assess breast symmetry.ResultsOf the 17 patients (mean age, 46 years), 16 underwent BCS and 1 underwent mastectomy due to extensive microcalcification. Tumor stages ranged from ductal carcinoma in situ to stage IIB. One local recurrence occurred 7 years after surgery. The mean breast volume difference between the treated and untreated breast was 104 mL. No implant rupture or removal occurred during surgery.ConclusionOur findings suggest that BCS with radiation therapy is a feasible treatment option for patients with prior augmentation, as adequate margins were achieved in most cases. However, postoperative breast asymmetry may occur. Given the small sample size and retrospective nature of this study, further large-scale studies are needed to confirm oncological safety and evaluate long-term cosmetic and patient-reported outcomes.Keywords: Breast implants, Breast neoplasms, Mammaplasty, Segmental mastectomy

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer has the highest incidence among Korean women cancer patients. Breast augmentation is one of the most popular plastic surgeries for women worldwide. In 2022, 2,174,616 patients underwent breast augmentation surgery. This represents a 29.0% increase from 2021 [1].

Breast implants do not increase the risk of delayed cancer detection, and patients with augmentation do not have a poorer prognosis compared to non-augmented patients [2]. As the augmented population increases, the number of breast cancer patients with prior breast augmentation will also rise. The surgical treatment of these patients remains contentious.

Patients with breast augmentation are more likely to undergo mastectomy rather than breast-conserving surgery (BCS) [3]. Concerns about capsular contracture, poor cosmetic outcomes, and achieving clear resection margins have impacted the use of BCS and radiation therapy in these patients. Karanas et al. [4] revealed that at the time of breast cancer diagnosis, the breast volume was less than 50% of the original, making it challenging to achieve clear margins; as a result, 4 out of 28 patients underwent mastectomy. However, other studies suggest that BCS is both feasible and safe oncologically, yielding good aesthetic results [5,6].

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the feasibility and clinical outcomes of BCS combined with radiation therapy for breast cancer patients who have previously received breast augmentation.

METHODS

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective case series. We reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent breast cancer surgery at Korea University Guro Hospital between 2008 and 2023.

A total of 4,568 patients underwent breast cancer surgery during the period. Among them, 17 patients had previously undergone breast augmentation (Fig. 1). All information was derived retrospectively from clinical charts, breast imaging, pathology reports, and surgical reports.

Flow chart of patient selection.

Ethics approval was secured from the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (No. 2024GR0330). This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature of study.

Our review encompassed age at cancer diagnosis, initial cancer recognition methods, prior implant placement (i.e., subglandular or subpectoral), cancer stage, histology, immunohistochemistry results, radiation therapy dosage, breast imaging, surgical method, adjuvant treatment, implant complications, and local recurrence.

We used mammography to calculate breast volume to assess symmetry post-cancer treatment, including radiation therapy. The measurements were taken upon the completion of both surgery and radiation therapy, using the cranio-caudal view. The formula used is 1/3 πr2h, based on cone-shaped breasts (Fig. 2) [7].

Breast volume calculation formula.

We assessed the position of the nipple in mammography following cancer treatment, which included BCS, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy.

RESULTS

Seventeen patients who had previously undergone breast augmentation were treated for breast cancer between 2008 and 2023. The mean age at cancer diagnosis was 46 years (range, 33–51 years). Regarding the initial detection of breast lesions, 9 patients presented with a palpable mass, 7 were diagnosed through routine cancer screenings including mammography and breast ultrasonography, and one patient presented with nipple discharge. Regarding implant location, 15 patients had subpectoral implants, while 2 had subglandular implants. Sixteen out of 17 patients (94.1%) underwent BCS, and only 1 patient underwent a mastectomy. The patient who underwent a mastectomy presented with extensive microcalcification, as well as multiple masses and non-mass enhancements on breast imaging. Histological analysis revealed that 5 patients had ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), 11 patients had invasive ductal carcinoma, and one patient had invasive lobular carcinoma. Tumor stages were determined according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer guidelines. Five patients were stage 0 (DCIS), 4 were stage IA, 6 were stage IIA, and 2 were stage IIB (Table 1).

There was only 1 case of cancer recurrence; the primary cancer in this patient was a 3 cm DCIS. She had initially undergone BCS and radiation therapy. Breast cancer recurred 7 years post-surgery. At that time, the patient underwent total mastectomy and implant removal, followed by immediate reconstruction using a tissue expander. All patients, except the one who underwent a mastectomy, received radiation therapy.

The average volumetric difference between the treated and untreated breasts was 104 mL (range, 15.7–247 mL). Most patients exhibited symmetry in nipple position on mammography (Fig. 3). There were no events of implant rupture or unintended removal during the initial cancer surgery.

DISCUSSION

The consideration of surgical methods by surgeons for augmented breast cancer patients focused on ensuring adequate surgical resection margins and achieving optimal aesthetic outcomes. In our study, only one patient had inadequate surgical resection margins due to extensive microcalcification and multiple masses. Except for this patient, 16 patients underwent BCS, and only 1 had a recurrence. Thus, our data suggest that augmented breast cancer patients can achieve adequate surgical resection margins even with BCS. However, BCS caused asymmetry in the size of both breasts.

BCS followed by radiation therapy is standard care but poses challenges related to local complications. Previous studies have reported various complications following breast cancer treatment, such as lymphedema associated with radiation therapy [8].

Similarly, in patients with breast implants, radiation therapy may result in implant-specific complications, including capsular contracture, poor cosmetic outcomes, and lymphedema.

Breast augmentation patients are generally more dissatisfied with their breasts than women who have not undergone augmentation [9]. Consequently, they often pursue cosmetic surgery to enhance their appearance. For this reason, patients who have had augmentations are more sensitive to cosmetic outcomes following BCS and radiation therapy compared to those without augmentations. The cosmetic outcomes discussed in the literature show mixed results based on retrospective studies.

Chu et al. [10] reviewed 37 patients who had undergone breast augmentation before developing breast cancer. Of these, 10 were primary breast cancer patients, with 6 undergoing BCS and radiation therapy. Results indicated cosmetic outcomes were excellent in 4, good in 2, and fair in 1.

Mark et al. [11] reviewed 21 patients who had previously undergone bilateral breast augmentation without complications and later underwent ipsilateral BCS and radiation therapy. Twelve of these patients developed capsular contracture, and 12 reported cosmetic outcomes ranging from fair to poor.

Our study results suggest that BCS combined with radiation therapy is a feasible approach for patients who have previously undergone breast augmentation. However, our study did not address cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction regarding breast appearance post-BCS. Thus, further research on postoperative cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction is warranted. This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective case series, the sample size (n = 17) is small. Second, the absence of a control group prevents a direct statistical comparison regarding oncological outcomes. Third, our assessment of cosmetic outcomes and complications was limited. Due to the retrospective design, standardized data on the severity of capsular contracture (e.g., Baker classification) were not consistently documented. Furthermore, as patient follow-up primarily focused on oncologic surveillance, data regarding long-term cosmetic sequelae were incomplete for some patients due to loss to follow-up.

Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as preliminary data regarding the feasibility of BCS. Future prospective studies with systematic follow-up protocols, robust cosmetic evaluation, and assessment of patient-reported satisfaction are warranted to validate these findings. In summary, BCS combined with radiation therapy appears to be a feasible treatment option for patients with prior breast augmentation, but a concerted effort toward optimizing cosmetic results is essential for this unique patient group.
