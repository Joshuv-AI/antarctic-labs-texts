Study on the formation and characteristics of unstable sheet cavitation on hydrofoils
Authors: Lin Wang, Di Chen, Fangping Tang, Haiyu Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrofoils, as fundamental components of hydraulic machinery, directly influence the performance of such machinery. This study conducted an analysis of the cavitation characteristics of hydrofoils at a + 4° angle of attack under various cavitation numbers using numerical simulation and experimental research methods. The focus of the research was to explore the phenomenon of unstable sheet cavitation and its causes, as well as to reveal the characteristics of the re-entrant jet. The large eddy simulation method was employed to calculate the cavitation morphology under three different cavitation numbers. The method is highly consistent with the experimental results in simulating the small-scale detachment at the tail of unstable sheet cavitation and the large-scale shedding of cloud cavitation. The study found that the detachment of unstable sheet cavitation is closely related to the re-entrant jet, which exhibits transient and abrupt characteristics during the unstable sheet cavitation phase. Furthermore, by applying FFT processing to the distribution of maximum reverse velocity and the spatiotemporal changes of Ux on characteristic lines, eigenfrequency of the detachment of unstable sheet cavitation were identified. The research results indicate that cavitation mainly show as sheet cavitation when the cavitation closure point does not exceed the zero-slope point. Beyond this point, it transitions to cloud cavitation. This study provides new insights into the cavitation phenomenon of hydrofoils and offers quantitative research on the phenomenon of unstable sheet cavitation.Keywords: Hydrofoil, Unstable sheet cavitation, Cavitation shedding, Re-entrant jet, Large eddy simulation

Introduction

Hydrofoils are devices that move through water and generate lift. The working principle of hydrofoils is similar to that of airplane wings, both utilizing Bernoulli's principle in fluid dynamics to create a pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces of the hydrofoil, resulting in lift [1], [2]. Hydrofoils have a wide range of applications in the design of hydraulic machinery [3], [4], [5], and their performance has a critical impact on the performance of hydraulic machinery. Objects moving through water inevitably encounter problems arising from the properties of water, such as cavitation.

Cavitation is a phase change process of water. When the local pressure is lower than the saturated vapor pressure, water will change from liquid phase to vapor phase [6]. Cavitation is widely found in various hydraulic machinery, such as pumps [7], [8], inducers [9], propellers [4] and pump turbines [10]. The generation of cavitation will affect the operating efficiency of hydraulic machinery, accompanied by vibration and noise [11]. The causes of cavitation are very complicated. Since the first discovery of cavitation, researchers have conducted continuous research on cavitation. The cavitation can be divided into bubble cavitation, sheet cavitation, cloud cavitation and super cavitation. There are many studies on sheet cavitation and cloud cavitation. When the cavity attaches to the surface of the hydrofoil, it is called sheet cavitation. When the cavitation reaches a certain length, it will shed which is called cloud cavitation. Partial cavitation means that the cavitation length does not exceed the chord length of the hydrofoil, including sheet cavitation and cloud cavitation [12].

The mechanism of cavitation shedding has been a focus and hot spot for researchers to study. Now there are three mainstream views: re-entrant jet, shock wave and pressure wave. The re-entrant jet was observed in high-speed photography experiments in the 1950 s, and then the research on the re-entrant jet gradually deepened [13], [14]. Kawanami et al. [15] inhibited the generation of effective cloud cavitation through obstacles, indicating that re-entrant jet is one of the causes of cloud cavitation. Callenaere et al. [16] measured the reverse pressure gradient and thickness of the re-entrant jet by LDV and ultrasonic measurement. Coutier et al. [17] measured the internal structure and hydrodynamic characteristics of the re-entrant jet using an endoscope. The formation mechanism of the re-entrant jet is basically clear: the adverse pressure gradient at the closure of the cavity forms a reverse flow, moves forward and cuts off the bubble to form cloud cavitation.

Shock wave is another important mechanism of cloud cavitation formation. Shock wave is also called condensation shock wave. Arndt et al. [18] already realized the disappearance of cavitation at the leading edge and pointed out that it may be the effect of shock waves. Until the use of X-ray technology, this effect was confirmed [19]. Subsequently, the study of shock waves gradually increased [20], [21]. The shock wave acts across the entire cavity, and the obstacles on the suction surface can no longer prevent the generation of cloud cavitation [22]. Shock wave is the main reason for generating of cloud cavitation at low cavitation number [23], [24]. There is still no consensus on the cause of the shock wave.

The third mechanism that causes shedding is pressure wave. The pressure wave mechanism produces collapse from the development of cloud cavitation to the high pressure zone. The literature [25] summarizes the difference between pressure wave and shock wave.

These mechanisms play an important role in the transition from sheet cavitation to cloud cavitation. Unstable sheet cavitation (or quasi-stable sheet cavitation) can induce cloud cavitation. Therefore, it is very important to maintain a stable sheet cavitation, which can greatly avoid the harmful effects associated with cloud shedding [26]. In normal design, the axial flow pump is usually designed to operate before the occurrence of critical cavitation, which means that there is less large-scale cloud cavitation shedding in the pump. However, the erosion of cavitation on the blade still exists, and the small-scale shedding formed by the cavitation of this unstable sheet cavitation may cause serious harm after accumulation. Therefore, it is necessary to study the cavitation of this unstable sheet cavitation, even if it does not have a performance impact.

This paper focuses on the cavitation phenomenon on hydrofoils, with an emphasis on the study of unstable sheet cavitation. It analyzes the cavitation morphology under three different cavitation numbers, the characteristics of the re-entrant jet, and provides a quantitative analysis of the frequency formed by small-scale detachment. The paper offers an approach to delineate the boundary between sheet cavitation and cloud cavitation, explains the causes of the detachment of unstable sheet cavitation, and thus provides new perspectives for the study of cavitation on hydrofoils.

Simulation steps and details

The three-dimensional modelling of the water tunnel test section is carried out by UG NX software, and the model size is shown in Fig. 2. The O-block grid is divided by ICEM CFD. The first layer of grid height is 2 × 10−6 m to ensure that the wall Y+is close to 1. Set the velocity inlet 9 m/s. The pressure outlet is set according to the cavitation number σ=(pout-pv)/(0.5ρlvin2). Other wall conditions are non-slip wall. The numerical simulation is carried out on Ansys CFX. The homogeneous flow assumption is adopted, and the “Couple” algorithm is used for numerical solution. Advection schemes adapt high resolution and transient scheme is second order backward Euler. The total calculation time is 1 s, and the time step is 1 × 10−4 s. Each time step is iterated 15 times. The average Courant number is less than 1.

σ = 1.39 Changes in cavity morphology (left), the distribution of the volume fraction of the cavitation on the middle section (middle), the distribution of streamlines and reverse velocities on the middle section (right).

At this cavitation number, the cavity length is approximately 0.2c. During the development of the cavity, the total length of the cavity remains essentially unchanged, with only small-scale unstable detachment occurring at the tail of the cavity. It is difficult to directly observe a clear periodic phenomenon from this shedding. Looking at the distribution of the cavity volume fraction on the mid-section, unstable detachment mainly occurs in the range of 0.15c to 0.2c. Once formed, the small-scale detachment dissipates rapidly, while the cavity volume fraction in other areas remains essentially stable. The reverse flow mainly exists in the range of 0.15c to 0.25c. The reverse flow is generated at the closure point of the cavity and the wall surface, forming a very small-scale vortex, resulting in only small-scale cavities.

Fig. 10 illustrates the characteristic changes of cavitation and reverse flow at σ = 1.02. Overall, the length and shape of the cavity are stable, with the total cavitation length maintained at about 0.5c. Irregular small-scale detachment occurs at the tail of the cavity, which is slightly larger than the detachment at σ = 1.39. The quasi-periodic time selected is 0.012 s, and the reason for this selection will be given later. According to the cavity volume fraction on the mid-section, the range of small-scale detachment is between 0.25c and 0.6c, with the closure point of the cavity and the wall surface near the zero-slope point. The reverse flow is generated near the closure point, attempting to cut off the cavity. Due to its insufficient strength, it can only follow the cavity backward until it is able to cut off and form small-scale detachment. The small-scale detachment behavior was similar at σ = 1.39, but at σ = 1.02, the cavitation tail was longer, and multiple re-entrant jets were present, and the frequency of shedding was faster. The streamline and reverse velocity distribution show that the intensity and range of the reverse flow have increased significantly, with the reverse velocity range existing in segments, forming multiple discontinuous vortices. This indicates that there are multiple vortex groups coexisting in this process. The reverse flow only exists between the tail of the cavity and the zero-slope point, and it is difficult to continue to develop forward beyond the zero-slope point. This may be the difference in the cause of small-scale shedding and cloud shedding.

Fig. 11 illustrates the characteristic changes of cavitation and reverse flow at σ = 0.74. After the last cloud cavitation, the attached cavitation begins to grow from the leading edge. The cavitation length increases rapidly, and a small-scale detachment forms at the tail position (t = 1/9t3) during the growth process. When the cavitation length reaches its maximum, the tail of the cavity becomes extremely unstable, and after a period of time, it develops into the first cloud shedding (t = 1/9t3 ∼ t = 5/9t3). Subsequently, the cavity grows again, and when the length reaches about 0.6c (t = 6/9t3), the second shedding occurs (t = 7/9t3 ∼ t = 8/9t3). The cavity begins to grow again from the leading edge, entering the next cloud cavitation cycle (t = 9/9t3). The entire process has a period of 0.110 s, with one small detachment and two cloud cavitations occurring.

σ = 0.74 Changes in cavity morphology (left), the distribution of the volume fraction of the cavitation on the middle section (middle), the distribution of streamlines and reverse velocities on the middle section (right).

During the cavitation growth process, the reverse flow originates from the tail of the cavity, partially cutting off the cavity at a position around 0.6c, forming a detachment during the growth process. Then, as the cavitation length reaches its maximum, the reverse flow gathers in the middle and rear part of the cavity, forming a large-scale vortex. At this stage, the reverse flow is mainly behind the zero-slope point. As the cavity develops, the reverse flow moves forward, crosses the zero-slope point, and fills the entire suction surface of the hydrofoil, then forming the first large-scale cloud shedding. After the detachment is formed, the range and size of the reverse flow are greatly reduced. When the second shedding forms, the reverse flow is mainly distributed from the middle and rear part of the cavity to near the zero-slope point. At this time, the thickness of the cavity is obviously thinner than during the first cloud cavitation, which also creates conditions for the formation of the second shedding.

Maximum reverse velocity development characteristics

Fig. 12 illustrates the variation of maximum reverse velocity over time under three different cavitation numbers, reflecting the intensity changes of the reverse flow. At σ = 1.39, the range of maximum reverse velocity is between 2 m/s and 9 m/s. The maximum reverse velocity exhibits many sharp peaks with significant peak-to-peak variations, indicating a more intense and variable reverse flow under this cavitation number. The maximum reverse flow does not show a clear periodicity, but within the red-boxed area marked in the figure, there are four peaks, suggesting a quasi-periodic nature. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysis of the data reveals a dominant frequency of 58 Hz, with an average period of approximately 0.0172 s. At σ = 1.02, the reverse velocity also lacks a clear pattern, with a velocity range of 1.5 m/s to 7 m/s. Both the mean and peak-to-peak values are significantly lower than at σ = 1.39, indicating a weaker reverse flow with less intensity and variability. After FFT processing, no distinct dominant frequency is observed; however, a certain similarity is manually annotated within the red-dashed box, with an average time of about 0.0115 s. At σ = 0.74, the system enters the cloud cavitation phase, exhibiting a clear periodicity, and we focus on the changes within one cycle. Overall, two significant cloud shedding events occur within a cycle, with the first having a lower maximum reverse velocity than the second but lasting longer. During the first shedding, the range of maximum reverse velocity change is smaller than the second. The first shedding forms slowly and gradually, while the second is rapid and intense.

Discussion

This paper demonstrates the evolution of cavitation morphology and the changes in reverse flow at a + 4° angle of attack under different cavitation numbers, elucidating the relationship between the suction surface profile and the behavior of the cavitation.

Fig. 23 shows the average cavitation length of the different cavitation numbers at + 4° in the experiment. The length of the cavity increases more rapidly as the cavitation number decreases. Based on experimental results, a boundary between the unstable sheet cavitation phase and the cloud cavitation phase is proposed. When the cavity length does not exceed 0.55c, it remains in a sheet cavitation state, with the cavity morphology stable within a certain range. Similar conclusions have been observed in other studies, suggesting that a cavity length of around 0.5c serves as a demarcation point between sheet and cloud cavitation. Based on the research presented in this paper, we believe that the boundary between sheet cavitation and cloud cavitation may be where the cavity closure point comes into contact with the location where the slope changes. When the cavity closure point is before or near the zero-slope point, the cavities are in a sheet cavitation state. Once the cavity closure point exceeds the zero-slope point, the cavitation transitions into a cloud cavitation state.

Average length of cavitation at + 4° with different cavitation numbers (Statistics based on the results of the test).

When the cavity closure point is prior to the zero-slope point, an unstable sheet cavitation phenomenon occurs, characterized by the formation of small-scale, irregularly detachment at the tail of the cavity, without a significant change in the overall length. The cause of the unstable sheet cavitation phenomenon is significantly related to the reverse flow. The unstable sheet cavitation does not exhibit a clear periodic behavior. By analyzing its maximum reverse velocity and the spatiotemporal distribution of Ux at different positions, a faint quasi-periodic behavior is still observed. During the unstable sheet cavitation phase, the reverse flow is weak, brief, and abrupt, incapable of forming a large-scale vortex, and thus cannot proceed towards the leading edge but only forms small-scale detachments at the tail.

Upon reaching the cloud cavitation state, although the intensity of the reverse flow does not change significantly, its range and thickness increase, crossing the zero-slope point and continuing forward, resulting in large-scale cloud cavitation.

Some studies have mentioned that the position of the camber affects the cavitation performance of the hydrofoil. Combined with the findings of this paper, the reason might be that the further back the camber, the further back the zero-slope point, and thus the longer the range of sheet cavitation formed, leading to better cavitation performance.

Conclusion

This paper has conducted numerical simulations and experimental studies on the cavitation characteristics of the MHA hydrofoil at + 4° under different cavitation numbers. The main focus was on the changes in cavitation morphology under three cavitation numbers, with particular attention to the phenomenon of unstable sheet cavitation and its causes, and the characteristics of the reverse flow were presented. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1)The large eddy simulation method was used to calculate the cavitation morphology under three cavitation numbers. Verified by experiments, this method can better simulate the small-scale detachment at the tail of unstable sheet cavitation and large-scale cloud cavitation shedding. The lower the cavitation number, the closer the results are to the experimental outcomes.

(2)The detachment of unstable sheet cavitation is highly related to the reverse flow. During the unstable sheet cavitation phase, the reverse flow is brief and abrupt, with intense variations. The formation of small-scale sheet cavitation is mainly due to the weak intensity of the reverse flow, which cannot develop forward to cut off the cavity but instead moves backward at the tail of the cavity as it develops. It continues to move until it cuts off the cavity at a certain location, forming a small-scale detachment.

(3)The small-scale detachment is difficult to observe directly for its pattern. Based on the distribution of maximum reverse velocity and the spatiotemporal changes of Ux on characteristic lines, after FFT processing, its spectral characteristics can barely be obtained. Although its main frequency components are unstable and changeable, they can provide a reference within a certain range. This result offers a quantitative analysis of the detachment of unstable sheet cavitation.
