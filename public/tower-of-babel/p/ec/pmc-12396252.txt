Effects of traditional Chinese exercises on the rehabilitation of patients with chronic heart failure: A meta-analysis
Authors: Yue Wu, Hong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The systematic meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the clinical efficacy of Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCE) in the rehabilitation of patients with chronic heart failure (CHF). The findings provide evidence-based support for the implementation of non-pharmacological interventions in the management of CHF. A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase (up to November 2024) identified RCTs evaluating Traditional Chinese Exercise (TCE) for chronic heart failure rehabilitation. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool assessed study quality. RevMan 5.4 calculated mean differences (MD) with 95 % CIs; heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic (P < 0.05). Fifteen RCTs involving 1,132 patients (573 intervention, 559 control) were included. Meta-analysis revealed that TCE significantly improved CHF outcomes: reduced BNP levels (MD=–38.34, 95 %CI:[–54.88, –21.79], P < 0.00001), increased 6-minute walking distance (MD = 49.75, 95 % CI:[33.86,65.65], P < 0.00001), elevated LVEF (MD = 2.91, 95 % CI:[1.32,4.50], P = 0.0003), and lowered MLHFQ scores (MD = –9.58, 95 % CI:[ –12.75, –6.40], P < 0.00001). NT-pro BNP levels also decreased significantly (MD = –547.14, 95 % CI:[ –698.38, –395.90], P = 0.0001). This meta-analysis demonstrates that traditional Chinese exercises (including Tai Chi, Baduanjin, and Liuzijue) as adjunctive rehabilitation for chronic heart failure patients yield significant clinical benefits compared to conventional treatment alone. The interventions showed marked improvements in cardiac function parameters (BNP, NT-proBNP, and LVEF levels), exercise capacity (6MWT assessment), and quality of life (MLHFQ scores). These findings provide robust evidence for integrating traditional Chinese exercises into comprehensive rehabilitation management for chronic heart failure patients.

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by impaired ventricular pumping and filling capacity due to cardiac structural and functional abnormalities. The primary clinical manifestations include dyspnea, fatigue, and peripheral edema [1,2]. As the terminal stage of various cardiovascular diseases, CHF represents a major global public health challenge and remains one of the leading causes of cardiovascular-related mortality. According to the 2021 ESC Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute and Chronic Heart Failure, exercise rehabilitation demonstrates significant clinical value in improving cardiovascular and respiratory adaptation, as well as enhancing peak oxygen consumption (VO2 max) [3]. With the accelerating global aging population, the prevalence of CHF has exhibited a progressive annual increase, resulting in a growing associated healthcare burden [4,5]. Epidemiological data project that the global population of adult CHF patients will exceed 8 million by 2030 [6]. According to the Global Heart Disease Report [7], heart failure affects approximately 64 million people worldwide, with significantly higher incidence rates observed in elderly populations compared to younger age groups. In China, elderly patients account for over 70 % of heart failure cases. With the progressing population aging, the associated burden of heart failure is projected to increase substantially by 2030 [8]. Despite significant therapeutic advances in recent years, the prognosis of heart failure remains poor, characterized by persistently high mortality and readmission rates. Current data indicate that approximately 83 % of CHF patients require hospitalization at least annually, with 43 % experiencing four or more hospital admissions per year [9]. These findings underscore the critical need for developing more effective therapeutic strategies for chronic heart failure.

Current therapeutic strategies for chronic heart failure (CHF) primarily fall into two categories: pharmacological interventions and non-pharmacological approaches [10]. Pharmacological management of CHF in Western medicine primarily involves vasodilators, cardiotonic-diuretics, and β-blockers. However, these therapeutic approaches demonstrate limited clinical efficacy and are associated with significant adverse effects [11]. However, despite demonstrating significant efficacy in symptom management and prognostic improvement, current pharmacological therapies have failed to fundamentally address the persistently high hospitalization and mortality rates associated with CHF [12]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to explore comprehensive non-pharmacological interventions to optimize CHF management strategies. Both domestic and international studies have demonstrated that various exercise modalities yield significant rehabilitative benefits for CHF patients [[13], [14], [15]]. Traditional Chinese exercises represent a holistic mind–body practice system integrating theories of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), philosophical principles, and health preservation concepts. Their core therapeutic philosophy emphasizes: (1) simultaneous cultivation of physical form and mental spirit;(2) harmonization of qi and blood;(3) maintenance of meridian patency [16]. Traditional Chinese exercises demonstrate measurable improvements across multiple metabolic parameters in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), including glycemic control, lipid profile, and insulin sensitivity indices. Notably, clinical studies have identified Tai Chi as exhibiting targeted therapeutic effects for T2DM management [17]. Traditional Chinese exercises, particularly Baduanjin, may confer multidimensional benefits for stable COPD patients, including improvements in pulmonary function, exercise capacity, health status, psychological well-being, and quality of life [18].

Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCE) have gained increasing attention for their integrated benefits on cardiopulmonary function, psychological status, and quality of life. However, limited evidence exists regarding their therapeutic efficacy specifically for chronic heart failure (CHF) patients. This study employs meta-analysis methodology to systematically evaluate the clinical effects of TCE in CHF rehabilitation, aiming to provide high-quality evidence for comprehensive CHF management and establish scientific foundations for non-pharmacological intervention strategies in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

This study has been prospectively registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews), a database established by the UK National Institute for Health Research Centre for Reviews and Dissemination, under registration number CRD42025639492. The research protocol strictly adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

Search strategy

Three independent reviewers systematically searched all English and Chinese publications reporting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving human subjects, published before November 28, 2024. A comprehensive search strategy combining Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords was implemented across the following databases: CNKI, VIP, Wanfang Database, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, EBSCO, and Embase. Key search terms included: “Tai Chi,” “wuqinxi,” “baduanjin,” “yijinjing,” “liuzijue,” “traditional exercises,” “chronic heart failure,” “heart failure,” and “randomized controlled trial.” The complete search strategy is detailed in Appendix A.

Eligibility criteria

First, a comprehensive literature screening was conducted, involving rigorous evaluation of all retrieved article titles and abstracts based on predefined eligibility criteria. The analysis specifically targeted randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating Traditional Chinese Exercise (TCE) interventions across the full spectrum of chronic heart failure (CHF) classifications, including: Population Characteristics, Intervention Requirements, and Methodological Standards. To maximize the generalizability of findings, this study applied no restrictions regarding: Demographic Factors, Intervention Parameters, and Outcome Measures. To maintain the highest scientific standards, this systematic review implemented stringent exclusion criteria: Non-RCT Designs, Non-Human Research, Limited Evidence Reports, Methodological Concerns, Methodological Concerns, and Secondary Publications.

Data Extraction and management

Two researchers were expected to extract and code data from the included articles, while a third researcher was responsible for a thorough review of these data. The following data were systematically extracted from all included studies:①Study Characteristics: Publication details(Title, publication year, first author), Methodology(Study design, randomization method);②Participant and Intervention Data: Population(Sample size, Baseline demographics, Clinical characteristics); ③Primary and Secondary Outcomes: Functional capacity: 6-minute walk test (6MWT) distance (meters); Biomarkers: B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) (pg/mL) and N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP) (pg/mL); Quality of life: Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ) score; Cardiac function: Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (%).

Quality assessment

Two researchers assessed the risk of bias of the included literature separately using the Cochrane Collaboration tool, and a third researcher scrutinized the results. Information was assessed in six areas, including randomized controlled studies, whether the allocation scheme was concealed, the status of the blinded design, the presence of incomplete data, selective reporting of results, and other biases. The trials were categorized as unclear, high risk of bias, and low risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration). Heterogeneity was analyzed using the χ2 test and I2 statistic. If low heterogeneity (I2 < 50 %) was observed, it was analyzed using a fixed effect model, and if high heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 50 %) was observed, it was analyzed using a random effect model, and subgroup analyses were used to identify potential sources of heterogeneity.

Funnel plots were drawn to detect potential publication bias. Sensitivity analyses were performed by removing each study individually to assess the consistency and quality of results. Inclusion of outcome indicators was continuous variables, and mean difference (MD) and its corresponding 95 % confidence interval (CI) were used to assess effect size.

During the screening process, studies were excluded based on the following predefined criteria: incomplete outcome data, unavailability of full-text articles, conference abstracts, and non-randomized controlled trial designs. Meta-analysis results

Minnesota quality of life Questionnaire (MLHFQ)

Quality of life (QoL) assessments were reported in 10 of the included studies. All trials consistently employed the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ) for standardized evaluation, with lower scores indicating better QoL outcomes. Due to substantial heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 92 %, P < 0.001), a random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis. The pooled results demonstrated statistically significant improvements in QoL scores for the intervention group compared to controls [MD = –9.58, 95 % CI (−12.75, −6.40), P < 0.001]. Specifically, significantly lower MLHFQ scores were observed in the intervention group, indicating that Traditional Chinese Exercise (TCE) may effectively enhance QoL in patients with chronic heart failure (CHF) (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Forest plot of Chinese traditional exercise on MLHFQ scores in patients with chronic heart failure.

Forest plot of Chinese traditional exercise on MLHFQ scores in patients with chronic heart failure.

Given the substantial heterogeneity in MLHFQ outcomes (I2 = 92 %), we performed subgroup analyses stratified by different Traditional Chinese Exercise (TCE) modalities. The analysis included Tai Chi studies (n = 452 patients), 4 Baduanjin studies (n = 275 patients), and 1 Liuzijue study (n = 16 patients). All TCE modalities demonstrated significant reductions in MLHFQ scores: Tai Chi: MD = –12.54 (95 % CI −15.33 to −9.75; P < 0.001); Baduanjin: MD = –5.17 (95 % CI −7.97 to −2.36; P < 0.001); Liuzijue: MD = –20.67 (95 % CI −29.33 to −12.01; P < 0.001). 6-minute walking test (6MWT)

Twelve randomized controlled trials (n = 951 patients) evaluated the impact of Traditional Chinese Exercise (TCE) on 6-minute walk test (6MWT) performance. A random-effects model was utilized for the meta-analysis due to considerable between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 81 %, P < 0.001). The meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in exercise capacity as measured by the 6-minute walk test (6MWT). Compared with control groups, TCE interventions demonstrated a mean increase of 49.75 m (95 % CI: 33.86 to 65.65; P < 0.001) in 6MWT distance, indicating clinically meaningful enhancement of functional capacity in CHF patients (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Forest plot of the effect of Chinese traditional exercise on 6MWT levels in patients with chronic heart failure.

Discussion

Chronic heart failure (CHF) is defined as a clinical syndrome characterized by ventricular ejection or filling impairment due to cardiac structural or functional abnormalities. As the terminal stage of various cardiovascular diseases, CHF is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates, posing a significant threat to global health [34,35]. Although pharmacological therapy can alleviate symptoms, adverse effects, and symptom recurrence may contribute to negative emotional states, reduce treatment adherence, and impair disease prevention [36]. Therefore, investigating non-pharmacological interventions, particularly the role of traditional Chinese exercise in CHF rehabilitation, holds significant clinical relevance.

Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCE) (e.g., Tai Chi, Baduanjin) integrate body posture regulation, breathing control, and mental focus ('tiao-shen, tiao-xi, tiao-xin'). These exercises are hypothesized to improve physiological function by regulating qi and blood flow while balancing yin and yang [37]. Evidence suggests that TCE combined with conventional therapy significantly enhances rehabilitation outcomes in CHF patients. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that TCE improves physiological parameters, cardiopulmonary function, and quality of life, while also alleviating depressive symptoms [38,39]. Furthermore, TCE demonstrates distinct exercise advantages. For instance, Tai Chi exhibits high participation rates and strong adherence [40]while effectively improving symptoms, physiological parameters, and quality of life [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. Optimal Baduanjin training protocols, involving 2–7 sessions per week for 8 weeks, have been shown to enhance cardiac function and oxygen uptake [45], demonstrating suitability for home-based rehabilitation [46]. As a safe and effective non-pharmacological intervention, TCE warrants broader implementation in the management of CHF.

Quality of life (MLHFQ score)

The present meta-analysis demonstrates that Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCEs), including Tai Chi and Baduanjin, significantly mitigate the adverse psychological impacts of CHF while stabilizing physical condition and improving quality of life (as measured by MLHFQ scores). These findings are consistent with the conclusions reported by Yeh et al. [47]. The observed improvement in MLHFQ scores suggests that Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCEs) may effectively alleviate CHF symptoms such as palpitations and fatigue, potentially through modulation of autonomic nervous function and attenuation of inflammatory responses [48]. These physiological modifications ultimately contribute to enhanced quality of life in CHF patients. Subgroup analysis revealed that Tai Chi had superior efficacy compared to Baduanjin in improving MLHFQ scores. This differential effect may be attributed to Tai Chi's unique mind–body integration characteristics: its slow, continuous movements combined with diaphragmatic breathing (tiao-xi) and meditation (tiao-xin) appear more effective in reducing sympathetic tone. In contrast, Baduanjin's static postures may demonstrate weaker immediate psychomodulatory effects [49]. Subgroup analysis by intervention duration revealed significantly enhanced effects in cohorts receiving ≥ 12 weeks of training. These findings align with Taylor's dose–response theory of exercise intervention [50], supporting the existence of cumulative therapeutic effects in TCE-mediated quality-of-life improvement. This temporal pattern may reflect Tai Chi's characteristic 'form-to-mind' training paradigm, which typically requires 8–12 weeks to establish coordinated movement-breath-awareness integration [51]. The included studies demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 92 %), likely attributable to variations in intervention parameters such as exercise modality and frequency. Future investigations should specifically compare the distinct respiratory patterns across TCE modalities (e.g., phonation breathing in Liuzijue versus natural breathing in Tai Chi) to elucidate their differential effects on autonomic regulation [52].

Exercise tolerance (6-Minute walk Test, 6MWT)

The meta-analysis demonstrated that Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCE) significantly improved both peak oxygen consumption (VO2peak) and 6-minute walk test (6MWT) performance in CHF patients. These enhancements indicate clinically meaningful improvements in exercise tolerance and functional capacity for VO2peak and 6MWT. These findings are consistent with prior meta-analyses evaluating TCE efficacy in CHF populations [[53], [54], [55]].

This observed effect may be attributed to the characteristic breathing patterns in TCE modalities such as Yijinjing and Tai Chi. Their standardized diaphragmatic breathing protocols have been shown to modulate parasympathetic activity (evidenced by increased high-frequency heart rate variability [56]. They can also enhance diaphragmatic excursion, thereby increasing maximal ventilatory volume and reducing residual lung capacity. Optimize gas exchange efficiency through Improved V/Q matching and Attenuated exercise-induced hypoxemia. These combined mechanisms collectively contribute to enhanced exercise tolerance in CHF patients by delaying anaerobic threshold onset, reducing perceived exertion (Borg scale), and improving oxygen utilization efficiency. Current evidence demonstrates that structured exercise training induces beneficial adaptations in mitochondrial function among CHF patients, including: Mitochondrial Content Improvement, Enzymatic Activity Enhancement, and Functional Outcomes [57]. As a low-intensity aerobic exercise, Tai Chi demonstrates unique therapeutic advantages through its characteristic “empty-full step transitions”, which increase lower extremity muscle strength, reduce energy expenditure during ambulation, and boost aerobic capacity [43]. The subgroup analysis revealed significantly enhanced treatment effects when the intervention duration exceeded 12 weeks. This finding coincides with the physiological adaptation mechanisms to exercise training proposed in Piepoli's study [58]. The mechanism may be due to the fact that a longer intervention period is more conducive to the formation of exercise compliance, which is conducive to the development of behavioural habits and thus cumulative efficacy. Moreover, a longer intervention period can easily form neuromuscular adaptation, and long-term training can promote slow muscle fiber proliferation and improve muscle oxidative capacity. Meanwhile, 12 weeks may also be the key to the improvement of cardiac diastolic function and the regulation of vascular endothelial function. The meta-analysis revealed substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 81 %, p < 0.001), primarily attributable to Baseline cardiac function variation, Exercise intensity disparities, and Protocol standardization gaps. The Ponikowski study suggested that future studies should use standardised exercise prescriptions and incorporate cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) to further validate the effects of traditional Chinese exercise on exercise tolerance in CHF patients [59].

Indicators of cardiac function (left ventricular ejection fraction, LVEF)

The present meta-analysis demonstrates that Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCE) significantly improve left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in CHF patients. The findings correlate with previous studies [[60], [61], [62]]. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) serves as a key clinical indicator of systolic function and represents an important prognostic marker for sudden cardiac death risk. Enhanced myocardial contractility directly correlates with increased stroke volume, resulting in higher LVEF values. Evidence demonstrates that Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCE) effectively modulate autonomic nervous system activity by suppressing sympathetic tone while augmenting parasympathetic function. Furthermore, TCE promotes significant cardiovascular benefits, including improved coronary collateral circulation, enhanced cardiac output, and increased stroke volume [56]. The intervention effects varied across different types of traditional Chinese exercises (TCEs) due to their distinct exercise intensities. Tai Chi, characterized by low-to-moderate intensity sustained movement, may enhance cardiac adaptation by increasing stroke volume (SV) and cardiac output (CO) [63]. In contrast, Baduanjin's stretch-contraction cycle movements may improve left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) by optimizing venous return and reducing cardiac preload. Qigong's static postures combined with respiratory modulation appear to primarily modulate autonomic nervous activity rather than directly enhance myocardial contractility. Consequently, subgroup analysis revealed that the Liuzijue intervention produced less pronounced improvements in LVEF compared to Tai Chi and Baduanjin. The mind–body interaction of TCEs may attenuate myocardial oxidative stress and fibrosis [64]through dual autonomic modulation: reducing sympathetic excitability (e.g., decreased norepinephrine release) while enhancing parasympathetic tone (increased heart rate variability). Subgroup analysis revealed more significant LVEF improvement after 24-week interventions, potentially mediated by myocardial structural remodeling. Experimental evidence indicates that exercise training lasting ≥ 8–12 weeks can promote myocardial capillary angiogenesis and reduce interstitial fibrosis [58]. Short-term interventions (<12 weeks) may be insufficient to overcome baseline functional limitations in CHF patients, whereas long-term training progressively optimizes exercise tolerance and cardiopulmonary synergy. However, moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 71 %) was observed across included studies, likely attributable to assessment method variability: while most studies employed echocardiography, a minority used cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), which provides superior LVEF measurement precision. Future studies should specify the exercise intensity (e.g., METs), frequency, and respiratory fit to facilitate cross-sectional comparisons. More uniform and precise imaging assessment methods, combined with long-term follow-up, should be used to assess the sustained effect of TCE on LVEF and its impact on the incidence of cardiovascular events [45].

Biomarkers (BNP and NT-proBNP)

The reductions in BNP and NT-proBNP levels observed in this study were comparable to those reported by Yeh et al. [30]and Pan et al. [38]. These results demonstrate that traditional Chinese exercises (TCEs) can significantly decrease both BNP and NT-proBNP levels. TCEs (including Tai Chi, Baduanjin, and Liuzijue) were found to activate skeletal muscle groups, promote meridian and collateral circulation, and modulate qi-blood flow dynamics. The mind–body regulation techniques in Tai Chi (“focusing on the dantian”) and Baduanjin (“breath control and mental concentration”) may modulate myocardial function through hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis regulation, potentially suppressing excessive inflammatory responses (e.g., reduction of NT-proBNP and BNP levels) [65]. The specific breathing pattern of Liuzijue − characterized by nasal inhalation, oral exhalation, prolonged uniform respiration (predominantly diaphragmatic), and silent phonation − may enhance ventricular filling through intrathoracic pressure modulation [66]. Future investigations should examine both the anti-inflammatory properties and cardioprotective mechanisms of traditional Chinese exercises (TCEs). Additionally, patient stratification based on baseline B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels is warranted, with High-BNP patients (>400 pg/mL) preferentially receiving breathing-focused interventions (e.g., Liuzijue). Those with moderate/low BNP levels are more appropriate for multimodal exercises (e.g., Baduanjin or Tai Chi).

Limitations of this study

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, potential biases may have been introduced due to the limited number of available studies, Small sample sizes in most included trials, and Suboptimal study designs. Furthermore, methodological quality was compromised as Allocation concealment was reported in only 2 studies, and Blinding procedures were not implemented in 5 trials. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in pooled analyses, potentially attributable to Variability in intervention frequency across studies, Divergent treatment durations, and Heterogeneity in therapeutic protocols. Literature retrieval constraints were identified, including: Inaccessibility of supplementary materials and conference proceedings, Insufficient reporting of key methodological details, and Unresponsiveness from corresponding authors. To address these limitations, the following are strongly recommended: Large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with standardized protocols, and Comprehensive evidence-based studies.

Conclusions

Traditional Chinese Exercises (TCEs) were demonstrated to be a safe and effective intervention that significantly improved Exercise tolerance, Cardiac function parameters, and Quality of life scores. These clinically meaningful outcomes position TCEs as a valuable component of long-term CHF rehabilitation protocols. While the current study was limited by its modest sample size and analytical constraints, the consistent positive outcomes across multiple endpoints suggest TCEs may offer distinct advantages over conventional exercise regimens. Future studies should compare different groups of patients with different types of CHF and develop standardized intervention protocols based on individual differences to provide clearer guidance on exercise protocols in clinical practice. In addition, more high-quality, large-sample randomized controlled trials are needed to specifically analyze the above outcome metrics for further quantitative clinical promotion of TCE in the rehabilitation of patients with chronic heart failure.
