Age‐Varying Associations of Maternal Knowledge and Adolescents’ Depressive Symptoms in Dyadic and Cultural Contexts
Authors: Tianlu Zhang, Su Yeong Kim, Kennedy Baines, Yang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionExtensive research has documented the influence of parental knowledge on adolescents’ mental health. Although adolescence is marked by rapid developmental changes, how the influence of parental knowledge varies across age remains understudied, particularly within dyadic and culturally informed frameworks that consider adolescents’ disclosure, perceptions of parental knowledge, and cultural values.MethodsData was drawn from a three‐wave longitudinal study of Mexican immigrant families in central Texas (2012–2020; N = 604, 483, 334 across waves), including adolescents aged 11 to 18 years old (M age = 12.9, 13.7, 17.6 across waves; 54.3% female, and 75.3% U.S.‐born at Wave 1). Time‐varying effect models (TVEM) were used to examine age‐varying associations between mother‐reported parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms, as well as the moderating roles of adolescent‐reported disclosure, perceived parental knowledge, and respeto socialization.ResultsHigher mother‐reported parental knowledge was associated with adolescents’ lower depressive symptoms during early adolescence. This association emerged mainly among adolescents who reported lower levels of disclosure or parental knowledge, or experienced lower levels of respeto socialization.ConclusionFindings highlight early adolescence as a sensitive period during which parental awareness is particularly protective, especially when adolescent disclosure is limited and cultural socialization processes are less strongly endorsed. Interventions should therefore move beyond promoting adolescent disclosure alone and support parents in maintaining developmentally appropriate awareness of adolescents’ daily lives.Keywords: age, cultural socialization, disclosure, Mexican‐origin adolescents, parental knowledge

Introduction

Latinx youth comprised approximately 25% of the U.S. youth population (Hispanic Research Center 2024) and experience disproportionate mental health challenges (CDC 2023, 2025). Within the family context, parental knowledge has been identified as an important factor associated with adolescents’ mental health (Racz and McMahon 2011). In Mexican‐origin families, maternal knowledge may be particularly influential, given mothers’ central roles in daily caregiving, monitoring, and communication with adolescents (Blocklin et al. 2011). However, the influence of maternal knowledge may vary across adolescence as youths’ autonomy and independence increase (Eccles et al. 1993). Prior research typically relies on age‐group comparisons that impose arbitrary developmental boundaries or assumes simplified linear patterns of change (Mak et al. 2020), limiting understanding of how associations evolve across adolescence. Time‐varying effect models (TVEM) address these limitations by estimating associations as smooth, continuous functions of age.

Furthermore, parental knowledge is embedded in a dyadic process. It reflects not only adolescents’ disclosure (Kerr and Stattin 2000) but also parents’ perceived knowledge of youths’ whereabouts and adolescents’ perceptions of how much their parents know (Pelham et al. 2022). For Mexican‐origin adolescents, these processes are also shaped by cultural socialization practices, such as respeto (Lopez et al. 2023), yet the role of cultural factors remains relatively underexplored. Examining age‐varying associations between parent‐reported parental knowledge and adolescents’ mental health across levels of adolescent‐reported disclosure, perceived parental knowledge, and cultural socialization can clarify when and for whom parental knowledge is most protective, informing developmentally and culturally responsive interventions.

Parent‐Reported Parental Knowledge on Adolescent Depressive Symptoms

Parental knowledge, defined as parents knowing where the adolescent is, with whom, and when they will be home (Kerr and Stattin 2000), has been widely studied in relation to adolescents’ adjustment. Prior research indicates that mothers are generally more knowledgeable than fathers and are more likely to obtain information through active monitoring and adolescents’ voluntary disclosure (Liu et al. 2020). In Mexican‐origin families, mothers often play a central role in daily caregiving, monitoring, and communication with adolescents (Blocklin et al. 2011; Glass and Owen 2010). As a result, maternal knowledge may be particularly proximal to adolescents’ mental health and warrants focused examination within Mexican‐origin families.

Prior research has linked parental knowledge to adolescents’ depressive symptoms; however, findings in this literature are mixed. For example, empirical studies involving U.S. adolescents in Grades 8 and 10 from diverse ethnic backgrounds (16.8% Latino adolescents; Kreski et al. 2023) have found a negative association between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms, suggesting that parents with greater knowledge of adolescents’ whereabouts may be better positioned to detect risk factors and provide timely support. Similar negative associations have also been reported in studies conducted in other countries (Hamza and Willoughby 2011; Mao et al. 2026). However, other evidence based on adolescents aged 10–14 from ethnically diverse samples, including samples composed of approximately 20% Latinos, has failed to detect a significant relationship (Pelham et al. 2022). Although some of these prior studies have included Latino adolescents within ethnically diverse samples, little research has directly examined the association between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms specifically within Mexican‐origin families. In addition, the inconsistent findings across prior studies may be attributable to differences in the age ranges of adolescent samples, as most studies draw general conclusions about the association between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms based on narrowly defined age groups within adolescence, rather than across the full age range encompassing adolescence (Cutrín et al. 2022; Hamza and Willoughby 2011; Kerr and Stattin 2000). An important issue that has not been adequately explored is whether the association between parental knowledge and depressive symptoms is more likely to emerge at specific ages during adolescence (Hoeve et al. 2009).

Stage‐Environment Fit theory (Eccles et al. 1993) suggests that the family is a key developmental context for adolescents. To achieve a fit between youth's changing developmental needs and the family environment, parental control and youth's autonomy are continuously renegotiated across different periods of adolescence, allowing parents to adjust their parenting strategies. Specifically, as adolescents grow up and age, the desire for autonomy becomes increasingly salient (Keijsers et al. 2016), requiring parents to balance maintaining knowledge of adolescents’ whereabouts while supporting autonomy. Consistent with this perspective, parental knowledge shows a significant decline across adolescence (Lionetti et al. 2019), and higher levels of parental knowledge are more likely to be associated with lower levels of internalizing symptoms among younger adolescents than among older adolescents (Kreski et al. 2023).

Prior evidence on age‐varying associations between parental knowledge and adolescents’ outcomes has largely relied on two approaches: analyses conducted separately for different age groups and models that include interactions between age and parenting variables (Mak et al. 2020; Mao et al. 2026). Although informative, dividing adolescents into discrete age categories is inherently arbitrary, and age cutoffs often vary across studies (Zacher and Froidevaux 2021). In particular, studies that use wave or grade level as proxies for age may obscure actual age‐related developmental patterns during adolescence. Moreover, interaction‐based approaches further impose strong assumptions by specifying age moderation as linear. By using time‐varying effect models (TVEM), this study addresses these limitations by estimating the association between mother‐reported parental knowledge and adolescent depressive symptoms as a smooth function of age. This approach allows the strength and direction of effects to change continuously across the course of adolescence without imposing a priori assumptions such as linearity (Tan et al. 2012). Based on prior research, the present study hypothesizes that the negative association between mother‐reported parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms will be most pronounced during the early period of adolescence.

Moderation Effect of Adolescent‐Reported Disclosure and Parental Knowledge

Following the landmark study by Kerr and Stattin (2000), a large body of research identified adolescent disclosure as the primary source of parental knowledge and as a key predictor of reduced problematic behavior (Liu et al. 2020; Racz and McMahon 2011). Subsequent work has emphasized promoting adolescents’ voluntary disclosure, given its consistent links with better adolescent adjustment (Tokić Milaković et al. 2018; Vijayakumar and Pfeifer 2020). However, focusing on disclosure alone may provide an incomplete account of parental knowledge. It overlooks parents’ active monitoring efforts and their independent awareness of adolescents’ daily lives, as well as adolescents’ perceptions of how much their parents know (Cutrín et al. 2022; Keijsers et al. 2009; Soenens et al. 2006). This limitation may be particularly relevant when studying maternal knowledge in Mexican‐origin families, as mothers in Mexican immigrant families are more likely to actively seek information about their adolescents’ whereabouts rather than rely on adolescents’ voluntary disclosure, whereas fathers tend to rely more on their spouse as a source of knowledge (Blocklin et al. 2011). Moreover, disclosure declines across adolescence as autonomy and privacy needs increase (Lionetti et al. 2019), and its implications for adjustment vary developmentally (Goldstein et al. 2023). Together, these limitations suggest that parental knowledge should be understood within a broader, developmentally sensitive family context.

Stage‐Environment Fit theory (Eccles et al. 1993) provides a useful framework for conceptualizing how maternal knowledge and adolescent disclosure jointly shape adolescents’ mental health across development. This theory posits that adolescent adjustment depends on the alignment between developmental needs and environmental support. From this perspective, low levels of adolescent disclosure may signal heightened vulnerability (Jäggi et al. 2016), because adolescents experiencing elevated depressive symptoms often withdraw socially and become less willing to communicate with parents (Laird and Marrero 2010). In such contexts, higher levels of parental knowledge may be especially important and fit adolescents’ developmental needs, because it enables parents to detect early signs of internalizing difficulties to provide timely support, particularly during early adolescence when youth are developing their autonomy and self‐regulation capacities (Eccles et al. 1993). Thus, parental knowledge and adolescent disclosure may represent distinct yet interrelated processes whose interplay varies across age. Accordingly, the current study tests whether adolescent disclosure moderates the age‐varying association between mother‐reported parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms. The hypothesis is that the promotive effect of mother‐reported parental knowledge on adolescents’ depressive symptoms during early adolescence will emerge when adolescents report lower, rather than higher levels of disclosure.

Beyond disclosure, mother‐ and adolescent‐reported parental knowledge may jointly shape adolescent outcomes. Although Kerr and Stattin (2000) examined parent‐ and adolescent‐reported parental knowledge separately, subsequent studies suggest that discrepancies between these reports may carry developmental meaning (Hou et al. 2020, 2018; Parlette et al. 2022a). When mothers report high knowledge but adolescents perceive lower knowledge, adolescents may perceive greater autonomy and less intrusion, even though parents remain informed and engaged. In such cases, higher mother‐reported knowledge may be experienced as supportive rather than intrusive, thereby benefitting adolescents’ mental health (Vrolijk et al. 2023). Conversely, when adolescents already perceive high levels of maternal knowledge, higher mother‐reported knowledge may be perceived as overly controlling, weakening its protective value on adolescent depressive symptoms.

Importantly, the implications of these discrepancies may vary across adolescence. Increasing gaps between mother and adolescent perceptions during early adolescence may reflect normative autonomy development, whereas similar discrepancies later in adolescence may be less adaptive (De Los Reyes and Epkins 2023; De Los Reyes and Ohannessian 2016). Thus, in early adolescence, a pattern characterized by higher mother‐reported knowledge coupled with lower adolescent‐reported knowledge may signal engaged parenting alongside emerging autonomy of adolescents. In contrast, similar discrepancies after middle adolescence may no longer reflect a normative or adaptive process. Therefore, the present study hypothesizes that higher mother‐reported knowledge would be significantly associated with lower levels of adolescent depressive symptoms when adolescents report relatively low levels of parental knowledge, particularly during early adolescence.

Guided by Stage‐Environment Fit theory (Eccles et al. 1993), this study extends prior work by examining how mother‐reported parental knowledge relates to depressive symptoms as a continuous function of age and by testing whether adolescent disclosure and perceived parental knowledge moderate this association across adolescence. By adopting a developmental and dyadic perspective, this study aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of how parental knowledge operates within parent–child relationships over time.

Moderation Effects of Respeto Socialization

While Stage‐Environment Fit theory (Eccles et al. 1993) helps explain when and for whom parent‐reported parental knowledge may be most beneficial for adolescents, this framework needs to be situated within a broader cultural context. Although extensive research has emphasized that parenting is inherently shaped by cultural values and that the effects of similar parenting strategies vary across cultural contexts (Lansford 2022), limited study has examined the association between parental knowledge and adolescent outcomes within a specific cultural context. This gap is particularly important in research on maternal parenting in Mexican‐origin families. Mothers are often primary agents of ethnic–racial socialization in Latino families (Knight et al. 2017), and their day‐to‐day parenting practices are closely intertwined with the transmission of cultural values. As such, maternal knowledge is not only a monitoring process but is also embedded within broader cultural socialization practices. Understanding the role of maternal knowledge requires consideration of the cultural context and the ways in which cultural processes shape the relationship between parental knowledge and adolescents’ mental health.

Respeto is a distinct Latino cultural value that structures hierarchical relationships between parents and children. It emphasizes that children should show respect to adults and should not interrupt or challenge parents regarding appropriate behavior (Calzada et al. 2010). The process by which parents teach their children respeto values is referred to as respeto socialization. Respeto socialization may be a unique cultural factor in Mexican‐origin families that moderates the association between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms.

The hierarchical relationships embedded in respeto values legitimize parental authority and reinforce the appropriateness of upholding parental knowledge. Through respeto socialization, Mexican‐origin adolescents gradually internalize expectations about how they should behave under conditions of high versus low levels of parental knowledge, which may shape the emotional consequences of parent‐reported parental knowledge on their well‐being. Specifically, respeto socialization is closely tied to the cultural ideal of bien educados (i.e., being well‐mannered, respectful, and well‐behaved), a long‐standing childrearing goal in many Latino families (Lopez et al. 2023). Adolescents who have high respeto socialization, and who place a high value on respecting familial authority figures, may be less likely to challenge parental expectations or rules and may feel greater pressure to regulate their behavior to be “well behaved” (Milan and Wortel 2015). In this context, heightened parent‐reported awareness of adolescents’ whereabouts may be coupled with adolescent concerns about disappointing parents, guilt, or self‐criticism, and create role strain associated with pressures to meet the “good child” expectations, to potentially undermine adolescent mental health.

Accordingly, this study hypothesizes that the protective effect of mother‐reported parental knowledge on adolescents’ depressive symptoms will be evident when adolescents report lower levels of respeto socialization. Because few studies have examined age differences in the influence of cultural values, this study does not propose specific hypotheses regarding when their moderating effects may emerge.

Current Study

Building on Kerr and Stattin's (2000) conceptualization of parental knowledge and Stage‐Environment Fit theory (Eccles et al. 1993), this study extends prior work by using TVEM to examine whether the association between mother‐reported parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms is evident during specific developmental periods across ages 11 to 18 (Research Question 1). The study further examines whether this age‐varying association is moderated by adolescent‐reported disclosure, maternal knowledge, and respeto socialization (Research Question 2). By identifying both the developmental periods and subgroups of adolescents for whom parent‐reported parental knowledge is associated with mental health benefits, this study aims to inform the timing and targeting of culturally responsive prevention and intervention efforts for adolescents’ mental health.

Methods

Participants

The data for this study comes from a three‐wave longitudinal study examining Mexican immigrant families in central Texas (W1: N = 604, W2: N = 483, W3: N = 334). The combined data was structured lengthwise based on participants’ age at each wave, yielding a total of 1393 observations from 604 participants ranging from 11 to 20 years. A small number of observations (N = 26) assessed at ages 19‐20 at Wave 3 were excluded because this group falls outside the commonly defined adolescent range (11–18 years), and the small cell size may yield unstable estimates (Tan et al. 2012).

The final analytic sample included adolescents aged 11 to 15 years at Wave 1 (2012 to 2015, n = 603, M age = 12.42, SD = 0.97) and mothers were 27–61 years old (n = 594, 54% female, M age = 38.89, SD = 5.74); at Wave 2 (1 year later), 80% of adolescents (n = 483, 54% female, M age = 13.23, SD = 0.95) and 81% of mothers (n = 478, M age = 39.80, SD = 5.85) continued participating in the study; at Wave 3 (4 years after Wave 2), 55% of adolescents (n = 305, 56% female; Mage = 16.90, SD = 0.91) and 55% of mothers (n = 300, M age = 54.59, SD = 5.77) continued their participation in the study.

Procedure

Participants were recruited through school presentations, community recruitment, and public records. To be eligible for participation, both parents had to be of Mexican origin and have a middle school–aged child who served as a language broker for at least one parent. Interviews were conducted by bilingual and bicultural interviewers who administered questionnaires to parents and adolescents. Interview questions were presented to parents and adolescents in their preferred language (i.e., Spanish or English). The questionnaires were initially translated into Spanish and then back to English. All procedures received Institutional Review Board approval.

Measures

All variables used in the current study were assessed with the same measures across waves. Maternal knowledge was reported by adolescents and by caregivers who self‐identified as the adolescents’ mothers. Adolescents additionally reported disclosure to mother and maternal respeto socialization.

Maternal knowledge was measured by four items adapted from the Iowa Youth and Families Project across three waves (Conger et al. 1995; Ge et al. 1996). A sample item for adolescents is “During the day, does your mother/father know where you are and what you are doing?” Mothers reported on corresponding items. Responses ranged from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Higher mean scores over four items indicated higher levels of maternal knowledge (Cronbach's α = 0.67–0.72).

Adolescents’ disclosure was measured by three items adapted from a previous study across three waves (Kerr and Stattin 2000). A sample item for adolescents is “Do you tell your mother how your day at school went?” Response ranged from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Higher mean scores over three items indicated higher levels of disclosure (Cronbach's α = 0.78–0.82).

Measures of maternal respeto socialization were derived from Calzada et al. (2010) qualitative study on Latino parenting. Adolescents reported on two items: 1) “My mother teaches me to show respect to elders by addressing them formally”; 2) “My mother teaches to have good manners.” Spearman‐Brown reliability for Wave 1 to Wave 3 measures range from 0.79 to 0.8.

Adolescents’ depressive symptoms were measured using the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D) (Radloff 1977). Adolescents responded to 20 items, such as “I felt people disliked me” ranging from 1 (rarely or none of the time) to 4 (most or all of the time). Higher mean scores indicated higher levels of depressive symptoms. Because the distribution of depressive symptoms was non‐normal, the composite score was log‐transformed prior to analysis (Cronbach's α = 0.83–0.87).

Covariates. Adolescents’ gender, nativity, and mothers’ education levels were also included as covariates in all models. Mothers’ education level was assessed on an 11‐point scale ranging from 1 (no formal schooling) to 11 (finished graduate degree).

Analytical Plan

Time‐varying effect models (TVEM; Tan et al. 2012) using a penalized spline (P‐spline) approach were employed to examine age‐varying associations of mother‐reported parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms. Data was structured in long format based on adolescents’ exact age at each wave. Because adolescents entered the study at different ages (e.g., ages 11–15 at Wave 1) and were followed over unequally spaced intervals (1 year and 4 years later), this design yielded observations spanning ages 11–18 years. TVEM uses participants’ age at each assessment as a continuous time metric rather than wave or time since baseline, allowing age‐specific associations to be estimated by pooling observations across individuals at different ages. This approach is consistent with prior TVEM research using similar data structures (Juang et al. 2018; Juang et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2026). TVEM allows intercept and regression coefficients to vary smoothly over time without imposing parametric assumptions about the functional form of developmental change. Within‐subject correlation due to repeated observations was addressed using robust standard errors (sandwich standard errors; White 1982). Statistical significance at a given age is indicated when the 95% confidence interval for the coefficient does not include zero. All analyses were conducted using a SAS macro (Li et al. 2017), with missing cases excluded listwise from specific models.

For Research Question 1, the TVEM included only mother‐reported parental knowledge to examine the age‐varying association between mother‐reported knowledge and depressive symptoms. For Research Question 2, moderation by adolescent‐reported variables was tested in separate TVEMs. Each model included mother‐reported parental knowledge, one adolescent‐reported variable (disclosure, perceived maternal knowledge, or respeto socialization), and their two‐way interaction. Interactions were examined separately because including all main effects and interaction terms in a single model would substantially increase model complexity and reduce interpretability and stability (Rimpler et al. 2025). When a statistically significant interaction term emerged at any age point, stratified analyses using a median split of the moderator were conducted to facilitate interpretation of moderation patterns. Importantly, the identification of age periods with statistically significant effects was based on the interaction models. The stratified analyses were conducted solely to aid interpretation of the interaction effects, rather than to determine the effects themselves, because the reduced sample size in the stratified models limited statistical precision. Covariates were included in all tested models as time‐invariant effects.

For covariates, the time‐invariant covariates were consistent across all four models (see supplemental Table S2). Specifically, female (b = 0.032‐0.047, SE = 0.009, p < 0.001) and Mexico‐born status (b = 0.024–0.027, SE = 0.012, p = 0.013–0.040) were each associated with significantly higher depressive symptoms, adjusting for time‐varying effects, while maternal education showed a trend‐level association (p = 0.079–0.098) that did not reach significance in any model.

Overall, higher mother‐reported knowledge was significantly related to adolescents’ lower depressive symptoms in early adolescence. This association was moderated by adolescents’ perception of parental knowledge, disclosure, and cultural socialization. Specifically, higher maternal knowledge was more strongly associated with lower depressive symptoms in early adolescence when adolescents reported lower disclosure, perceived their mothers as having less knowledge of their daily activities, or experienced lower levels of respeto socialization from their mothers.

Discussion

Guided by Stage‐Environment Fit theory (Eccles et al. 1993), this study conceptualizes parental knowledge as a developmentally contingent process embedded within the parent–adolescent dyad. Rather than treating parental knowledge as uniformly beneficial or primarily a byproduct of adolescents’ voluntary disclosure (Kerr et al. 2010), study findings suggest that its influence depends on developmental timing and contextual conditions. Using time‐varying effect modeling (TVEM) with longitudinal data collected between 2012 and 2020, this study found that higher levels of mother‐reported parental knowledge was associated with lower depressive symptoms, only during early adolescence (approximately ages 11–13). The protective association of mother‐reported parental knowledge was evident among adolescents who disclosed less, perceived their mothers as less knowledgeable about their daily activities, or experienced lower levels of maternal respeto socialization.

The current study extends prior research by modeling age as a continuous dimension rather than relying on predefined developmental categories. TVEM provides a more precise depiction of when associations are statistically significant and how they evolve across age, offering developmentally specific insight into when parental knowledge may be most beneficial. The age‐varying pattern underscores that the benefits of maternal knowledge are not constant across adolescence. This result is consistent with prior age group‐based findings indicating that parental knowledge is more strongly associated with lower internalizing symptoms among younger than older adolescents (Kreski et al. 2023). One possible explanation for this age‐specific association is adolescents’ increasing need for autonomy as they transition from early to middle and late adolescence, which may reduce the protective value of high parental knowledge in later developmental periods (Keijsers et al. 2016).

Importantly, the findings highlighted early adolescence as a particularly sensitive developmental period during which maternal knowledge was significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms. Early adolescence is characterized by substantial biological, cognitive, and social changes associated with puberty and ongoing brain development, making this period especially vulnerable to the emergence of mental health problems (Whittle et al. 2014). At the same time, parent–child relationships undergo important transitions during the shift from late childhood to early adolescence, including increases in parent–adolescent conflict and decreases in warmth and closeness (Marceau et al. 2015). Adolescents also begin seeking greater autonomy and independence from parents during this developmental period. Together, these developmental changes may make maternal awareness of adolescents’ daily experiences particularly important during early adolescence, while also increasing adolescents’ sensitivity to whether maternal knowledge is experienced as supportive or intrusive, thereby conditioning the effects of maternal knowledge on adolescents’ depressive symptoms. This conditioning process is reflected in the moderating roles of adolescents’ disclosure and perceived parental knowledge.

Moderating Role of Adolescents' Disclosure and Perceived Parental Knowledge

The current findings suggest that maternal knowledge may be most beneficial among adolescents who reported lower disclosure and perceived their mothers as having less knowledge of their daily activities. The moderation effect of adolescent disclosure challenges the assumption that adolescent disclosure is the key factor explaining how parental knowledge influences adolescent mental health and that the two operate synchronously (Kerr and Stattin 2000). Notably, the correlation between mother‐reported knowledge and adolescent disclosure was only small to modest (r = 0.11–0.19, p = < 0.001–0.062), providing preliminary support that these two constructs are related but do not function as a unitary process.

Moreover, if parental knowledge were largely derived from adolescents’ disclosure, mother‐reported knowledge would be unlikely to show a unique association with adolescent depressive symptoms when adolescents report relatively low disclosure. However, this study found that during early adolescence, the negative association between maternal knowledge and depressive symptoms was statistically significant when adolescents reported lower (vs. higher) levels of disclosure. In other words, adolescents who disclosed less are more likely to benefit from high maternal knowledge in terms of reduced depressive symptoms. This pattern implies that parent‐reported parental knowledge and adolescent disclosure do not function as a single, unitary process. Rather, they represent distinct yet interrelated dimensions of parent‐child dynamics that jointly shape adolescents’ mental health. Low adolescent disclosure may signal elevated vulnerability or reduced communication (Keijsers et al. 2009), in which case higher parental knowledge may play a compensatory and protective role in mitigating risk of internalizing symptoms.

The moderation effect of adolescent‐reported parental knowledge reflects the need to balance parental involvement with adolescents’ developing autonomy needs, especially during early adolescence when parent–adolescent conflict tends to increase (Marceau et al. 2015). Specifically, when adolescents perceive relatively low levels of parental knowledge, they are more likely to view themselves as having greater autonomy and to interpret higher mother‐reported maternal knowledge as supportive parental engagement rather than as monitoring or intrusive. Therefore, under conditions of relatively low adolescent‐reported maternal knowledge, higher mother‐reported parental knowledge may be particularly beneficial for adolescents’ mental health (Vrolijk et al. 2023). Also, higher mother‐reported knowledge coupled with lower adolescent‐reported maternal knowledge may indicate that parents maintain awareness of their adolescents’ activities while adopting a more nuanced, nonintrusive approach that is less noticeable to youths (i.e., lower adolescent‐reported parental knowledge; Rote and Smetana 2018) that may relate to better adolescent developmental outcomes. The finding also resonates with prior research on parent‐adolescent discrepancies and underscores the importance of considering both informants’ perspectives (De Los Reyes and Ohannessian 2016).

Moderation Roles of Adolescent‐Reported Respeto Socialization

The current study contributes to the literature by incorporating a cultural perspective and identifying the moderating role of respeto socialization, which has been largely overlooked in research on parental knowledge, particularly in relation to its developmentally varying effects. Respeto socialization may place expectations on adolescents to be bien educado (well behaved; Lopez et al. 2023), and in contexts of high parental knowledge, the internalization of these expectations may increase adolescents’ perceived pressure to behave appropriately, which explains why the negative association between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms does not emerge among adolescents who report high levels of respeto socialization.

Notably, this interaction between parent‐reported parental knowledge and respeto socialization was not observed consistently across adolescence but appeared only during early adolescence, suggesting that parent‐directed cultural processes may exert stronger influence when children are relatively younger adolescents. As adolescents grow older and develop greater judgment regarding which cultural values they wish to endorse and practice, parental cultural socialization may become less influential. This finding also highlights early adolescence as a critical developmental period for identity exploration. During this period, adolescents increasingly explore different social roles and identities and reinterpret earlier self‐perceptions (Erikson 1968). Accordingly, culturally grounded socialization processes such as respeto socialization in this period may become especially influential in shaping how adolescents interpret parental behaviors and parent–adolescent relationships. Together, these findings underscore that cultural contexts are not static attributes but dynamic processes, with distinct implications for parenting effects in different adolescent periods.

It should be noted that this study examined the moderating role of parental respeto socialization rather than adolescents’ respeto endorsement. The internalization of cultural values (i.e., respeto value endorsement) likely requires a longer developmental process, with its influence becoming more evident later in adolescence (Knight et al. 2011, 2018). However, due to lack of data on the construct, this study was unable to directly test the developmental timing of adolescents’ respeto value endorsement, potentially overlooking the gradual internalization of cultural values across adolescence (Lynch 2020). Future research could directly compare parental socialization and adolescents’ value endorsement to further elucidate their distinct developmental timing and contributions to adolescents’ mental health.

Implication

Based on the current findings, relatively high maternal knowledge during early adolescence may be especially important for Mexican‐origin adolescents’ mental health, suggesting that interventions should particularly support mothers’ involvement and awareness during this developmental period when adolescents are navigating increasing autonomy and identity exploration.

The findings also suggest that interventions should move beyond promoting adolescent disclosure alone and instead help parents maintain developmentally appropriate awareness of adolescents’ daily lives. Maternal knowledge was more strongly associated with lower depressive symptoms among adolescents who perceived their mothers as having lower levels of knowledge about their daily activities, suggesting that maternal knowledge may be most beneficial when mothers are able to remain informed and emotionally connected without adolescents perceiving this awareness as overly controlling. Accordingly, family‐based interventions may benefit from helping parents develop nonintrusive monitoring strategies, such as initiating supportive conversations, maintaining involvement in adolescents’ daily routines, and fostering emotionally supportive communication environments, particularly during early adolescence when parent–adolescent conflict tends to increase.

In addition, the moderating role of respeto socialization suggests that culturally responsive interventions may benefit from helping mothers balance the transmission of cultural values emphasizing respeto and family connectedness with adolescents’ increasing developmental needs for autonomy and independence. For example, parenting programs may help mothers maintain awareness and involvement through emotionally supportive communication and collaborative discussions rather than relying primarily on authority or behavioral control.

Limitation and Future Directions

This study has several limitations that point to important directions for future research. First, age‐varying estimates from TVEM should not be interpreted as representing individuals’ average developmental change trajectories; rather, they reflect age‐related trends estimated from the sample as a whole. Also, although TVEM captures how the strength and direction of associations between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms vary across age, the estimated coefficients at specific ages reflect contemporaneous, cross‐sectional associations because parental knowledge and depressive symptoms were measured at the same time points. Therefore, the current study cannot address whether parental knowledge predicts changes in depressive symptoms at later months or years. Future studies should employ longitudinal modeling approaches that explicitly separate within‐person change from between‐person differences and allow for lagged effects, such as dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) (Asparouhov et al. 2018), to more precisely examine how parental knowledge influences adolescents’ mental health over time.

Second, the current study focused exclusively on the association between parental knowledge and adolescents’ depressive symptoms. However, parental knowledge has also been linked to adolescents’ behavioral and social adjustment (Kerr et al. 2012; Miller and Jacobson 2023), and its associations with externalizing outcomes, such as delinquency and behavioral problems, which may differ from those observed for internalizing symptoms (Reitz et al. 2006). Future research should examine whether age‐varying effects and the moderation processes identified in the present study generalize to other domains of adolescent adjustment and whether these associations vary across internalizing and externalizing outcomes.

This study used only two items to measure respeto socialization, as these were the only indicators available in the dataset. Consistent with prior conceptualizations of respeto as a multidimensional construct encompassing obedience, deference to authority, decorum, and appropriate public behavior (Calzada et al. 2010), the current items primarily captured decorum (i.e., good manners) and deference (i.e., formally addressing elders), but not obedience (i.e., following parental directives without questioning) or behavioral regulation in public contexts (i.e., remaining calm and respectful in social settings). Importantly, emerging research suggests that different dimensions of respeto may relate differently to developmental outcomes, with dimensions emphasizing warmth and parental guidance showing more positive associations than those emphasizing strict obedience (Tamis‐LeMonda et al. 2020). Future research should therefore use more comprehensive measures of respeto socialization to examine whether different dimensions show distinct developmental implications across adolescence.

Due to sample size limitations, the current study focused on mother‐adolescent dyads, precluding examination of whether age‐varying effects and moderation processes can be generalized to fathers. Future research with larger samples should investigate whether maternal and paternal knowledge differ in their associations with adolescents’ depressive symptoms, including whether these associations show age‐varying patterns. Such work would help clarify whether the developmental and conditional processes identified in the present study extend across parental roles, with broader implications for understanding parenting practices in adolescence (Waizenhofer et al. 2004).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the link between parent‐reported parental knowledge and adolescent depressive symptoms depends on developmental period, and adolescents’ disclosure behaviors, perceptions of parental knowledge, and cultural socialization. Specifically, higher mother‐reported parental knowledge was significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms among adolescents during early adolescence. This association was stronger when adolescents perceived their mothers as having less knowledge of their daily activities, reported lower levels of disclosure, or experienced lower levels of respeto socialization. Together, these findings extend prior work on parental knowledge and adolescent disclosure by highlighting the importance of developmental timing, family dynamics, and cultural context. More broadly, the results underscore early adolescence as a sensitive developmental window during which parental awareness of adolescents’ daily activities may be particularly protective, especially when adolescents disclose less, report less parental knowledge, and experience lower levels of respeto socialization.
