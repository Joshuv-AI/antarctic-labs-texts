Molecular epidemiology of viral enteric in children in Hangzhou
Authors: Bao-hai Chen, Qin-rui Lai, Ying-ying Chen, Hang-yu Zhou, Duo-te Cai, Hong-qiang Shen, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViral diarrhea is a major global health burden in children. The implementation of unprecedented non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the COVID-19 pandemic, including lockdowns, travel restrictions, school closures, enhanced hand hygiene, and mask-wearing, significantly altered the transmission dynamics of many infectious diseases globally. These measures, primarily targeting respiratory pathogens like SARS-CoV-2, were also anticipated to impact the circulation of enteric viruses, which often spread through the fecal–oral route or via contaminated surfaces and aerosols. However, the extent and nature of this impact on the prevalence, seasonality, and genotype distribution of major pediatric enteric viruses including rotavirus (RV), norovirus (NV), adenovirus (ADV), astrovirus (ASV), and sapovirus (SAV) are key pathogens, their molecular epidemiology during the COVID-19 Pandemic remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to delineate the prevalence, genotype distribution, and genetic features of eight enteric viruses in children with acute diarrhea in Hangzhou, China, from March 2019 to December 2020, encompassing phases before and after COVID-19 emergence.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study analyzed 1,510 stool samples from children with acute diarrhea. Eight viruses (RVA, RVB, RVC, NV GI/GII, ADV, ASV, SAV) were detected using qRT-PCR. Positive samples underwent targeted gene sequencing followed by phylogenetic analysis and amino acid mutation screening against reference strains.ResultsEnteric viruses were detected in 33.38% (504/1,510) of samples. NV GII dominated (48.02%, 242/504), with GII.4 as the predominant genotype (84.62%, 88/104). Temporal shifts were observed: for ASV, HAstV-1 replaced HAstV-5 post-2020, and RV G9 persisted as the dominant genotype (47.06%, 8/17) throughout the study period. ADV 41 remained prevalent (97.37%, 37/38), while SAV exclusively showed GI.1 strains. All amino acid mutations matched known variants.ConclusionThis study offers the first molecular epidemiology data on eight enteric viruses in Hangzhou during a period overlapping with COVID-19. The observed genotype shifts, which highlights the need for targeted surveillance.

Introduction

Viral diarrhea remains a leading cause of under-five mortality globally, with enteric viruses—RV, NV, ADV, ASV, and SAV [1, 2]. RV (Reoviridae, dsRNA) accounts for 20–30% of severe gastroenteritis cases requiring hospitalization in children. It can be divided into 7 subgroups (A-J) based on serological response to VP6 antigen. Infections are mainly reported in groups A, B, and C [2–6]. NV (Caliciviridae, + ssRNA) is associated with 18% of all acute gastroenteritis cases globally and contributes to over 200,000 annual deaths in developing countries. Based on the characteristics of the VP1 gene, there are 10 subgroups of NVs in GI-GX [7], with GI and GII as the main subgroups causing acute gastroenteritis in children [8]. Following the widespread use of RV vaccines, NV has surpassed rotavirus as the most prevalent cause of pediatric acute gastroenteritis in many regions [9]. ADV (Mastadenovirus, dsDNA) type 40 and type 41 in group F are the most common causes of acute diarrhea in children, with 5.18% of diarrheal children testing positive [10, 11]. Most children have mild to moderate dehydration, which may be accompanied by respiratory symptoms [12]. The symptoms of ASV(Astroviridae, + ssRNA) infection in children are mainly diarrhea, and may also present with fever, vomiting, abdominal pain, and anorexia. ASV infection is usually mild, with rare severe dehydration and a good prognosis [13]. SAV (Caliciviridae, + ssRNA) predominantly causes vomiting and diarrhea in children, with vomiting being the most common symptom. The course of disease in children can last for 8–9 days, and the clinical symptoms are less severe than those of RV or NV infection [14].

After the outbreak of the novel coronavirus disease (COVID-19), a series of preventive and control measures were implemented in China, including travel restrictions, social distancing, and mask-wearing. Children, in particular, were asked to routinely wear masks and wash their hands [15]. Recent studies have reported that these measures have led to a decreased incidence of infectious diseases among children in Hangzhou, the capital of Zhejiang Province in East China [16, 17] —a trend that may signal underlying genetic shifts in enteric viruses. However, molecular epidemiological data on enteric viruses in this region, especially genotype distribution and genetic evolution under pandemic-related interventions, remains limited. This observation drove us to explore the molecular epidemiology of enteric viruses in pediatric patients during this period. This hospital-based cross-sectional study characterized the molecular epidemiology of enteric viruses in children with acute diarrhea in Hangzhou during the COVID-19 outbreak, determining their prevalence, genotype distribution, and genetic characteristics compared to pre-pandemic patterns, with implications for clinical diagnosis and prevention.

Methods

Sample collection

From March 2019 to December 2020, 1510 stool samples were collected from all children (Age < 14 years) diagnosed with acute diarrhea in the Children’s Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, including both outpatients and inpatients, to capture real-world prevalence and genotype distribution without selection bias. Samples were stratified into two periods for analysis, based on the timeline of China’s COVID-19 containment measures: pre-COVID-19 (March 2019–December 2019) and during-COVID-19 (January 2020–December 2020).The stool samples were stored in 2.5 ml of normal saline and were preserved under conditions of −70℃. The study (No. 2019-IRB-082) was approved by the Children's Hospital Ethics Committee at Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and all the study participants' parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Detection of viruses

We used qRT-PCR. combined with a melting curve assay to detect RVA, RVB, RVC, NV GI, NV GII, ADV, ASV, and SAV, which was developed in our previous study [18].

Strain selection for sequencing

Among virus-positive samples, strains were selected for sequencing based on the following criteria: (1) sufficient PCR product concentration and quality for successful sequencing, (2) proportional representation of collection time periods (pre-COVID-19 vs. post-COVID-19), and (3) available sample volume. Due to technical constraints and budget limitations, we selected strains randomly from those meeting these criteria using a random number generator, aiming to maintain temporal distribution when possible.

PCR amplification of each virus gene

The positive samples were positive for RVA, ASV, SAV, NV GII, and NV GI, and a two-step method was used to amplify the target gene of the virus-positive samples. First, the viral DNA/RNA was extracted, and the RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using MonScript RTIII Super Mix with dsDNase (Monad Biotech Co., Ltd., China). Second, the cDNA of RVA, ASV, SAV, NV GII, NV GI and the DNA of ADV served as templates for PCR, and the target gene was amplified using 2 × Taq MasterMix (CWBio Co., Ltd., China) under the following conditions: 2 min at 94 °C; 35 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 40 s. Each PCR batch included negative controls (nuclease-free water) and positive controls (transcribed RNA containing target sequences of RVA/ASV/SAV/NV GII/NV GI/ADV in vitro). Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The PCR products were sent to TSingKe Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), for sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) with BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit. Amplicons (≥ 20 ng/μL) underwent capillary electrophoresis under standardized conditions: 50 cm array length, POP-7™ polymer, 1.2 kV injection voltage, and 120-min run time. Raw sequence traces were base-called using Sequencing Analysis v5.4 (Applied Biosystems), followed by bidirectional read alignment in BioEdit v7.2.6 to generate consensus sequences.

Sequence assembly and quality control

Raw sequencing data (forward and reverse reads) were received from TSingKe Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) in FASTA format. The sequence assembly process was performed using Geneious Prime (version 2023.0.4). Forward and reverse chromatogram files were imported into Geneious, and consensus sequences were generated using the following parameters: minimum overlap of 20 bp, minimum overlap identity of 85%, and quality trimming with error probability Limit of 0.05. Poor-quality regions at sequence ends were trimmed based on quality scores, and ambiguous bases were manually inspected and corrected when possible.

Quality assessment was performed by examining chromatogram quality scores, with sequences having average quality scores below Q20 excluded from further analysis. Assembled consensus sequences were then subjected to BLAST analysis against the NCBI nucleotide database to confirm viral identity and rule out contamination.

Phylogenetic analysis

The target gene sequences of each virus acquired in this study were analyzed and compared with public gene sequences using the basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) (available at http://www.ncbi.nlm.gov/BLAST/). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT v7.526 [19]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using IQ-TREE (version 2.1.3) [20] with maximum likelihood (ML) method. The best-fit evolutionary model for each dataset was automatically selected using ModelFinder implemented in IQ-TREE based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Bootstrap support values were calculated using 1000 replicates to assess the statistical reliability of tree topologies. Bootstrap values were assigned to tree nodes if they exceeded 80. All trees were visualized with ggtree v3.16 [21].

Amino acid mutation analysis

For amino acid mutation analysis, the nucleotide sequences of each virus were first translated into amino acid sequences using the Biopython package (version 1.79) with appropriate reading frames and genetic codes for each virus type. Reference strains were selected based on the following criteria: complete genome sequences available in GenBank, well-characterized strains commonly used in phylogenetic studies, geographic and temporal diversity to represent global circulation patterns, and strains representing the predominant genotypes identified in our study. The specific reference strains used were NC_HM565136 (Human adenovirus 41), NC_001943 (Human astrovirus 1), NC_044853 (Norwalk virus GI.3), NC_044046 (Norovirus GII.4), MK111629 (Sapovirus GI.1), and NC_011503 (Rotavirus A, VP7 gene).

Systematic mutation analysis was performed using the snipit package (https://github.com/aineniamh/snipit) to conduct comparative analysis between clinical strains and their corresponding reference sequences, identifying amino acid substitution sites and substitution types. The analysis focused on the target genes used for genotyping: hexon gene for ADV, capsid gene for ASV, capsid genes for NV GI and GII, polyprotein gene for SAV, and VP7 gene for RVA. All identified amino acid substitutions were systematically documented, including their positions, the nature of amino acid changes.

Nucleotide identity analysis

Pairwise nucleotide identity calculations were performed using MEGA version 11.0.13. Identity matrices were generated using the p-distance model with pairwise deletion of gaps. Both intra-study comparisons (between Hangzhou strains) and inter-study comparisons (Hangzhou strains vs. global reference strains) were calculated.

Discussion

Epidemiological characteristics and viral prevalence

Viral infection is often the cause of acute diarrhea in children [22]. RV, NV, ADV, ASV and SAV are all common pathogens leading to viral diarrhea in children [23]. In our previous study, we developed a multi-probe fusion curve technique for the detection of five prevailing viruses responsible for viral enteritis in children [18]. In this study, we found that the percentage of children with acute diarrhea who were positive for these viruses was as high as 33.38%. Foreign studies have shown that the incidence rate of NV is greater than that of RV after RV vaccine introduction [24]. In China, rotavirus vaccines are not yet included in the national vaccination program and their application is dictated by national legal standards [25, 26]. Abiding by principles of informed consent, agreement, and self-expense, vaccination is conducted at specific healthcare units by the parents or guardians of the children. Therefore, it poses considerable difficulty for acquiring precise rotavirus vaccine introduction data. However, despite Limited data on voluntary vaccination coverage, its potential contribution to reducing rotavirus infection rates in our study region should not be ignored. During the period of our study, we discovered that NV GII was the principal pathogen associated with diarrhea in children, accounting for 48.02% (242/504) of the population. Among the positive patients, a significant proportion (5.73%, 29/504) presented as coinfected. Within the 29 dual infected samples, those coinfected with NV GII and ASV had the highest percentage (0.66%, 10/1510). The dominance of NV GII in coinfection cases under COVID-19 interventions may reflect its unique adaptability to NPIs. In addition to norovirus GI infections, all other viral pathogens present as either mono-infections or co-infections, which has also been observed in another study [27]. This may suggest a strong ability of these viruses to co-exist in the host, or they may share common transmission routes and infection mechanisms. Moreover, this reveals that multiple infections should be considered when diagnosing acute diarrhea.

Genotype distribution and comparison with global studies

RVB detection has not been reported in earlier studies from China. Consistent with this finding, no cases of RVB were detected in our article [28]. For RVA, G3 strains accounted for the vast majority of children with acute diarrhea in northeastern Colombia in 2015, Brazil in 2018–2019 and Qatar in 2016–2019 [28–30]. Our previous study identified G9 as the most common genotype causing acute diarrhea among children in Hangzhou, 2017–2018 [31]. Consistent with our previous report, our surveillance also suggested G9 as the most prevalent genotype in Hangzhou during 2019–2020. These results indicate that G9 has solidified its presence as a stable subtype in Hangzhou, China. In our study, AdV-41 was the predominant serotype among children with acute diarrhea. According to former domestic reports, 41 is the most prevalent genotype in China [4, 32–34]. The NV genotypes GII.4, GII.3 were the chief genotypes identified in historical studies [35–40]. An study in China indicated that between the years 2000 and 2018, the genotypes GII.4, GII.17, and GII.2 exhibited the highest positivity rates [41]. A meta-analysis was also conducted, including reports published pre-2017. The most frequently observed genotype was GII.4, which was followed by GII.3 and GII.17. Within the GI genotypes, GI.2, GI.4, and GI.3 were found to be the most prevalent [9]. Our surveillance of 2 strains of NV GI revealed that they belonged to the GI.3 genotype, which was similar to the findings of previous studies in Tibet, China [34]. Notably, the GII.4 genotype, particularly certain strain types, is frequently associated with norovirus pandemics and an elevated disease burden [42]. Although norovirus vaccines remain under development, characterizing locally circulating strains is critical for evaluating the potential coverage and efficacy of candidate vaccines. Our study revealed that the primary genotype was GII.4, with GII.2, GII.3 and GII.6 following in prevalence. For ASV, ASV-1 was the major genotype in Hangzhou, 2019–2020, which is similar to the findings of studies in Chongqing and Shandong, China [33, 43]. ASV-1 represents the predominant astrovirus genotype causing pediatric infections worldwide [44]. Although astrovirus infections typically present with clinically milder manifestations compared to rotavirus or norovirus, the sustained predominance of ASV-1 underscores its stable role within the local spectrum of diarrheal pathogens in children. Continuous epidemiological surveillance remains critical for achieving a comprehensive understanding of the diarrheal disease landscape. Genogroup I was the dominant genogroup of SAV in children with gastroenteritis worldwide [45]. GI.1 was the predominant genotype identified in many reports [33, 46]. In our study, all the SAV strains were of the GⅠ0.1 genotype. The dominance genotype highlights their potential as priority targets for region-specific vaccine development. Amino acids, as core components of antigenic epitopes, are pivotal in infectious disease research, with mutational mechanisms offering multifaceted insights into epidemiological trends, therapeutic design, and vaccine development [47]. While viral evolution typically involves mutation accumulation to evade immunity or confer drug resistance [48], our study detected only known amino acid variants, with no novel mutations identified. This may stem from the NPIs suppressing enteric virus transmission. Reduced viral replication and host-to-host spread likely diminished evolutionary pressure for new mutations. Although no novel mutations were identified, the known amino acid variants detected in this study merit attention due to their potential functional consequences. For instance, specific documented variations in the norovirus VP1 protein (particularly within the P domain) or at certain sites of rotavirus VP7/VP4 proteins have been reported to potentially impact viral receptor binding, antigenicity, immune evasion capabilities, or particle stability [49, 50]. Similarly, established variants in adenovirus hexon protein or astrovirus capsid proteins may be associated with altered tissue tropism or immunogenicity [51, 52]. While functional validation of these variants was beyond the scope of this study, their presence warrants ongoing surveillance of these critical residues to assess potential effects on viral transmissibility, pathogenicity, or the efficacy of future vaccines.

Phylogenetic implications for transmission patterns and evolutionary insights

The phylogenetic analysis provides insights into viral diversity within our study population, though the limited sample sizes for certain viruses (particularly NV GI and SAV) restrict our ability to draw comprehensive conclusions about population-level genetic diversity. The clustering patterns observed should be considered preliminary findings that warrant validation through larger-scale surveillance studies. The temporal clustering observed in ASV strains, particularly the predominance of HAstV-1 after COVID-19, suggests that pandemic control measures may have altered the selective pressure on circulating viral populations, potentially favoring certain genotypes over others. This finding is particularly significant as it demonstrates how public health interventions can influence viral evolution and population dynamics.

The close relationship between Hangzhou strains and global isolates indicates active international transmission networks, likely facilitated by travel and trade connections. For NV GII, the identification of three distinct phylogenetic clusters suggests multiple independent introduction events, possibly reflecting Hangzhou's role as a major economic hub in Eastern China with extensive international connections. The high nucleotide identity (> 95%) observed between Hangzhou strains and global references for most viruses suggests relatively recent divergence times and active gene flow between populations.

The stability of RVA G9 genotype in Hangzhou, as evidenced by its consistent circulation since our previous 2017–2018 surveillance and its clustering with local Chinese strains, indicates successful local adaptation and endemic establishment. This finding has important implications for vaccine strain selection and effectiveness, as regional genetic variations may affect vaccine-induced immunity. The presence of strains similar to Netherlands, Indian, and Thailand isolates suggests multiple introduction events and continued genetic exchange with global RVA populations.

These phylogenetic patterns also reflect the impact of COVID-19 prevention measures on viral circulation. The temporal shift in strain clustering, particularly evident in ASV and NV GII, suggests that public health interventions may have disrupted established transmission chains and altered the competitive landscape among circulating viral variants. However, the presence of virus-specific amino acid mutations, while previously reported, indicates ongoing evolutionary adaptation that may affect viral fitness, transmissibility, or pathogenicity.

Understanding these evolutionary relationships is crucial for developing effective surveillance strategies and predicting future epidemic trends. The genetic similarity between local and international strains underscores the importance of global surveillance networks and the need for continuous monitoring of viral evolution to inform public health preparedness and response strategies.

Study limitations and future perspectives

This study has limitations including potential selection bias from hospital-based sampling in Hangzhou. And the absence of systematic rotavirus vaccination data and incomplete genotyping (e.g., rotavirus P types and norovirus full genomes) could confound interpretations of genotype prevalence. The phylogenetic analyses included strains randomly selected from available positive samples with adequate RNA quality, but the number of strains successfully sequenced for some viruses was relatively low. This Limited sequencing depth reduces the representativeness of our phylogenetic analyses, particularly for less prevalent viruses, and may introduce bias or incompleteness in the conclusions regarding genotype distribution and genetic diversity. Future studies will expand geographic sampling across diverse regions, integrate clinical outcomes and vaccination data, and employ deep sequencing for detecting minority variants, further enhancing the comprehensive Linkage between molecular evolution and epidemiological dynamics. This will inform vaccine strategies and outbreak responses. This study presents an important step in the ongoing studies of pediatric epidemiology in Hangzhou, China. For the first time, eight different types of diarrhea viruses prevalent among children are being simultaneously studied from a molecular epidemiological perspective. In summary, our study investigated the epidemiological and genetic characteristics of RVA, RVB, RVC, NV GI, NV GII, ADV, ASV, and SAV infection among children in Hangzhou 2019–2020. In further studies, we will sequence more clinical enteric virus samples from virus-positive children with diarrhea which would contribute to advanced surveillance of viral diarrhea.
