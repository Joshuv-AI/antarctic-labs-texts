Proteomics analysis of nitrogen stress on the influence of carbon and nitrogen metabolism of flue-cured tobacco
Authors: Xinwang Zhang, Shouhui Pan, Dong Chen, Yuqi Pan, Tianju Xu, Jinling Zhang, Yin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nitrogen stress is the main abiotic stress factor affecting the carbon and nitrogen balance of flue-cured tobacco. Using tandem mass tag (TMT) proteomics, we identified 157 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) under low nitrogen (LN, 60 kg/hm2; 65 with increased abundance, 92 with decreased abundance) and 219 DEPs under high nitrogen (HN, 150 kg/hm2; 123 with increased abundance, 96 with decreased abundance). In nitrogen metabolism, LN downregulated proteins involved in nitrogen absorption and transport (e.g., nitrite reductase, glutamine synthetase), weakening pathway activity; however, it upregulated tyrosine aminotransferase to promote tyrosine catabolism and generate nitrogen-containing intermediates, compensating for nitrogen deficiency. HN, by contrast, upregulated proteins in porphyrin/chlorophyll metabolism (e.g., protoporphyrinogen oxidase), amino acid metabolism, and alkaloid biosynthesis, enhancing nitrogen-related processes. In carbon metabolism, LN inhibited glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway via downregulating key enzymes. HN promoted starch, sucrose, fructose, and mannose synthesis (e.g., sucrose synthase upregulation) and the pentose phosphate pathway, but downregulated TCA cycle-related proteins, leading to carbon-nitrogen metabolic imbalance.Notably, both LN and HN enhanced the biosynthesis of isoquinoline, tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloids, but via distinct enzymes: LN relied on tyrosine aminotransferase, while HN depended on amine oxidase. This study reveals the “stress-specific regulation” mechanism of carbon-nitrogen metabolism in flue-cured tobacco under nitrogen stress, providing a theoretical reference for precise nitrogen fertilizer management and carbon-nitrogen balance optimization in tobacco production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12135-2.

Nitrogen is involved in the synthesis of organic substances such as proteins, nicotine, chlorophyll, nucleic acids, and enzymes in flue-cured tobacco and is one of the most influential nutrients on tobacco yield and quality [1–4]. Whether the supply of nitrogen is insufficient or excessive is detrimental to the development of tobacco yield and quality [5, 6]. Yunnan 87 is one of the widely cultivated flue-cured tobacco varieties in China, which has important economic value. In previous studies and actual production [7], it was found that Yunnan 87 was more sensitive to nitrogen changes and showed obvious differences in growth, physiology and quality under different nitrogen supply levels.Low-nitrogen stress decreases nitrate reductase (NR) activity in tobacco leaves and roots, increases the number of adventitious roots, improves root nitrogen uptake in tobacco plants [8], increases chlorophyll content by reducing leaf area to maintain leaf thickness [9], and leads to overexpression of tobacco Triticum aestivum Glutamine Synthetase 1 (TaGS1) and Triticum aestivum Glutamine Synthetase 2 (TaGS2) gene, resulting in a developed root system, larger leaves, and significantly higher nitrogen uptake and assimilation accumulation [10]. Low-nitrogen tolerant tobacco varieties increase superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and proline accumulation to resist the damage caused by low-nitrogen stress in tobacco plant [11, 12]. With increasing nitrogen application, fresh cigar tobacco leaves at different periods showed higher amylase (AL), nitrate reductases (NR) and nitrite reductase (NiR), and glutamate synthetase (GS) activities, lower starch content, and higher total nitrogen content [13]. However, excessive nitrogen can result in immoderate tobacco leaf thickness, poor combustibility, and high nicotine content, leading to lower tobacco quality [14]. Nitrogen application has a pro-motive effect on the activity of key enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism in flue-cured tobacco within a certain range [15]. Immoderate nitrogen application produces inhibition, and the effect concentration is not the same for different enzymes and different fertility periods [7]. Deletion of the nitrate response element (NRE2) in the nitrate reductase gene promoter reduces nitrate accumulation in the leaves of flue-cured tobacco [16]. Transcriptome sequencing revealed that low concentrations of nitrogen inhibited the synthesis of alkaloids and metabolic activities of pathways related to energy metabolism in the roots of flue-cured tobacco, while high concentrations of nitrogen showed a facilitative effect [17].

However, transcriptomic studies mainly reflect mRNA expression changes and cannot capture post-transcriptional regulation (e.g., protein translation efficiency, post-translational modifications) or protein–protein interactions (PPI)—key links between gene expression and physiological phenotypes [18]. Recent international proteomic studies on plant nitrogen regulation have made progress, but earlier methods such as iTRAQ or label-free quantification were limited either by lower multiplexing capacity or reduced quantitative accuracy, making it difficult to detect low-abundance regulatory proteins [19]. Moreover, most proteomic studies on tobacco nitrogen stress have focused on root responses, while leaf tissues—the major sites of photosynthesis and nicotine biosynthesis—remain understudied, leaving gaps in understanding leaf carbon–nitrogen balance mechanisms under nitrogen stress [20].

Thus, there are many studies on the effects of nitrogen stress on the production and physiology of flue-cured tobacco [21]; which are mostly focused on the effects of nitrogen application on the growth and development of the above ground parts of tobacco and the quality of production [1, 2, 7, 9, 22, 23], while there are fewer studies on the changes occurring in the physiological processes of tobacco leaves. However, the response of flue-cured tobacco to nitrogen stress is still an extremely complex physiological and biochemical process, and its ability to tolerate nitrogen stress and the regulation of the function and expression of genes that enhance nitrogen conversion are still under investigation. In this study, we used TMT and liquid-chromatography/electrospray ionization/tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to analyze the protein changes in flue-cured tobacco after nitrogen stress. In this context, our study applies TMT quantitative proteomics combined with LC-MS/MS to systematically analyze protein-level changes in tobacco leaves under nitrogen stress. Compared with transcriptome-only studies, the TMT strategy enables multiplexed quantification of enzyme abundances, metabolic regulators, and stress-related proteins, thereby providing a new perspective to bridge gene expression with physiological and metabolic phenotypes.We aimed to analyze the differential proteins and metabolic pathways related to nitrogen stress in flue-cured tobacco from the perspective of proteomics. It provides basic data support for further understanding the molecular mechanism of carbon and nitrogen metabolism in flue-cured tobacco in response to nitrogen stress.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and design

The flue-cured tobacco cultivar used was Yunyan87(Nicotiana tabacum L.)(All materials were provided by Guizhou Key Laboratory of Tobacco Quality Research.). The soil used was yellow loam, and its physicochemical properties are shown in Table 1. The test was conducted in the potting field of the Guizhou University Tobacco Experimental Base in An Shun, Guizhou Province, China. The temperature and relative humidity of the potting field were controlled at 12.3℃ ~ 21.0℃ and 77.0%. Fertilizers for testing were superphosphate (P2O5 ≥ 16.0%), potassium sulfate (K2O ≥ 52.0%) and urea (N ≥ 46.2%).

Experimental design

A soil culture experiment was performed with three treatments (Table 2): low-nitrogen treatment (LN), normal nitrogen application (CK), and high nitrogen treatment (HN). Nitrogen treatment

Application of nitrogen in flue-cured tobacco was conducted according to an experimental design, while phosphorus and potassium fertilizers were applied at 105 kg/hm2and 210 kg/hm2. One-time application of phosphate fertilizer was used as the base fertilizer during transplanting, 70% and 30% of nitrogen and potassium fertilizers were applied as base fertilizer at transplanting, and as a follow-up fertilizer at the colony period, respectively(Supplementary Table 1).

Each treatment included 3 biological replicates, with plants grown in separate pots to ensure independent biological sampling. The nitrogen stress treatments started from the day of transplanting and continued until the day of sampling, lasting for a total of 45 days. Leaf tissues from each plant were processed and analyzed individually, treating each replicate as an independent biological sample.

Detection method

Tobacco leaf sample collection

Tobacco leaf samples were collected from the 6th to 8th leaf positions (middle leaves) at the rosette stage. Three plants per treatment were sampled, and leaves were pooled, rinsed with deionized water, and blotted dry. Approximately 2 g (fresh weight) of homogenized samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for proteomic analysis.

Protein extraction and peptide enzymolysis

Tobacco leaf protein was extracted by the SDS-DTT-Tris (SDT) mixture lysis method and quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method [24]. Then appropriate amount of protein was taken for enzymatic hydrolysis. Use a C18 cartridge to desalinate peptide segments, lyophilized and redissolved with 40uL 0.1% formic acid solution, and quantified peptide segments(OD280) [25].

TMT labeling and peptide fractionation

A 100 µg peptide segment was labeled using the Thermo Fisher Scientific TMTpro™ 16plex Labeling Kit (Catalog No. A50949) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [24, 26]. After labeling, samples were separated by fractionation and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), followed by mass spectrometry analysis using a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

LC-MS/MS analysis

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an Easy nLC (Proxeon Biosystems, now Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 60/90 min [27]. The peptides were loaded onto a reverse phase trap column (Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap100, 100 μm*2 cm, nanoViper C18) connected to a C18-reversed phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific Easy Column, 10 cm long, 75 μm inner diameter, 3 μm resin) in buffer A (0.1% formic acid) and separated with a linear gradient of buffer B (84% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min controlled by IntelliFlow technology. The mass spectrometer was operated in the positive ion mode. MS data were acquired using a data-dependent top10 method dynamically choosing the most abundant precursor ions from the survey scan (300–1800 m/z) for HCD fragmentation. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 3 × 106, and the maximum injection time was set to 10 ms. The dynamic exclusion duration was 40.0 s. Survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200, the resolution for HCD spectra was set to 17,500 at m/z 200, and the isolation width was 2 m/z. Normalized collision energy was 30 eV, and the underfill ratio, which specifies the minimum percentage of the target value likely to be reached at maximum fill time, was defined as 0.1%. The instrument was run using the peptide recognition mode.

Identification and quantitation of proteins

The MS raw data for each sample were searched using the MASCOT engine (Matrix Science, London, UK; version 2.2) embedded in Proteome Discoverer 1.4 software for identification and quantitative analysis. In the screening of significantly different proteins, the Expression Change (FC) > 1.2 times and P value < 0.05 were the criteria [28]. Biological replicates (n = 3 per treatment) were used to calculate mean values, standard deviations, and statistical significance, ensuring reliability of DEP identification.

Bio-informatics analysis

GO annotation

The protein sequences of the selected differentially expressed proteins were locally searched using the NCBI BLAST + client software (ncbi-blast-2.2.28+-win32.exe) and InterProScan to find homolog sequences. GO terms were mapped, and sequences were annotated using the software program Blast2GO. The GO annotation results were plotted using R scripts. To determine significantly enriched GO terms, GO enrichment analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact test (implemented in Blast2GO) to evaluate the over-representation of DEPs in specific GO categories relative to the background protein set.

KEGG annotation

Following annotation, the studied proteins were blasted against the online KEGG database (http://geneontology.org/) to retrieve their KEGG ortholog identifications and subsequently mapped to KEGG pathways. Subsequently, a hypergeometric test (executed through a custom R script) was used to conduct KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and the statistical significance of DEP enrichment in each KEGG pathway was calculated. Proteins with FC > 1.2 and p < 0.05 were defined as differential proteins.

Protein interaction analysis

Protein-protein interaction information was retrieved from the complete molecular interaction database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/) using gene symbol or STRING software (http://string-db.org/). The results data were downloaded in XGMML format and imported into Cytoscape [29] software (http://www.cytoscape.org/, version 3.2.1) to visualize and further analyze the functional protein-protein interaction network. Additionally, the importance of each protein in the PPI network was evaluated by calculating its degree.

Results and analysis

Nitrogen stress affected the growth and development of flue-cured tobacco

In Fig. 1, flue-cured tobacco plants under low nitrogen treatment (60 kg/hm2) showed obvious symptoms of nitrogen deficiency, such as leaf thinning, leaf yellowing, leaf number reduction, etc. These phenomena were consistent with the typical physiological characteristics of plants under low nitrogen stress, indicating that plants were facing the pressure of insufficient nitrogen supply. Bio-informatics analysis

GO annotation of differential proteins

To fully understand the function, localization, and biological pathways of proteins in organisms, proteins were annotated according to GO. GO functional annotation is mainly divided into three categories: biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC) [30]. In terms of BP(Fig. 4), DEPs were primarily involved in cellular processes and metabolic processes, while their involvement in environmental stimulation, signal transduction, development, and reproduction was minimal—indicating LN mainly regulates basic life-sustaining processes rather than non-essential pathways like stress response or development.For MF, the functions of DEPs were concentrated on catalytic activity (of metabolic-related proteins) and protein binding in flue-cured tobacco, with little impact on protein transport or structural protein activity. This suggests LN mainly modulates enzyme-driven metabolism and protein interaction, rather than structural support or substance transport. CC is enriched in cells, followed by organelles, membranes, and protein-containing complexes, and has the least impact on extracellular regions, other organisms, and membrane-closure cavities.

In the HN condition (Fig. 4B), DEPs shared partial functional trends with LN but also showed distinct differences, reflecting HN-specific regulatory patterns: In BP, DEPs were involved in the same core processes as LN—cellular processes and metabolic processes (the most impactful on flue-cured tobacco)—but the total number of DEPs was higher than that under LN. Notably, DEPs related to environmental stimulation, biological regulation, and regulation of biological processes were also more abundant than under LN, which indicates HN triggers a stronger stimulation response in tobacco leaves and enhances their biological regulation function. Consistent with LN, signal transduction, development, and reproduction were least affected.For MF, similar to LN, DEPs under HN had a greater effect on catalytic activity (of various metabolic proteins) and protein binding in flue-cured tobacco, while their impact on molecular carrier activity and transcriptional regulation activity was minimal. In terms of CC, a key difference from LN emerged: DEPs under HN were most prominently enriched in membranes, followed by cells, cellular components, membrane parts, and organelles; their impact on extracellular regions, membrane-enclosed lumens, and cell junctions remained the least.

KEGG analysis of differential proteins

To analyze the biological processes and stress mechanisms of flue-cured tobacco under nitrogen stress more systematically and comprehensively, the proteins were annotated using the KEGG pathway database [31–34]. We screened and counted the top 20 KEGG pathways with the most enriched differentially expressed proteins. For the LN condition (Fig. 5A), DEPs exhibited a clear enrichment bias toward photosynthesis and sugar metabolism-related pathways: The core enriched pathways included photosynthesis and sugar metabolism, which reflects the decline in photosynthetic efficiency and the disruption of sugar metabolism homeostasis in flue-cured tobacco under LN stress. In contrast, DEPs were rarely enriched in pathways such as fatty acid biosynthesis, purine metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, methane metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism—indicating LN has minimal regulatory effects on these secondary metabolic or specific nutrient metabolism pathways.

DEPs in HN (Fig. 5B) were mainly involved in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum of flue-cured tobacco, biosynthesis of cofactors, ascorbic acid and aldehyde acid metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis, sulfur metabolism, and other stress resistance reactions.It indicates that HN promotes the adaptation of flue-cured tobacco to high-nitrogen environments by enhancing protein processing efficiency, optimizing amino acid metabolism and activating stress resistance pathways.

Protein-Protein interaction analysis

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) mainly refers to protein-protein interaction, which is one of the important ways for proteins to perform their functions through protein-mediated pathways or formation of complexes and thus biological regulation. This experiment was based on protein interaction relationships in STRING or IntAct databases, and protein interaction network maps were constructed using the CytoScape software for the comparison group of DEPs (Fig. 6). The five nodes with the highest node correlation in the LN were Sedum sedum heptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase (A0A1S4C882), D-fructose-1,6-bisphosphate 1-phosphate hydrolase (A0A1U7 × 1T0), fructose diphosphate aldolase (A0A1S3Z8U8), glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (A0A1S4CI25), and the photosynthetic NDH of subcomplex B1 subunit (A0A1S4ASN2) (Fig. 5A), which were all downregulated in response to LN treatment. These proteins are involved in carbon and nitrogen metabolic pathways such as the pentose phosphate pathway, amino and nucleotide sugar metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, porphyrin, chlorophyll metabolism, and glycolysis/glycoisomerization. Therefore, flue-cured tobacco at LN affects changes in other proteins of flue-cured tobacco, especially in the pentose phosphate pathway, aminosaccharide, and nucleotide sugar metabolism, by weakening the intensity of carbon and nitrogen metabolism in response to low-nitrogen stress.

The top five differentially expressed proteins with nodal association in HN were heat shock homo-log 70 kDa protein (A0A1S3 × 8Z6), phosphoprotein isomerase (A0A1S3XGM0), differential isomerase family protein SDR39U1 homolog (A0A1S3YVL2), precipitin 1 (A0A1S3ZHB8), and photosystem II repair protein PSB27-H1 (A0A1S4DN09) (Fig. 6B), all of which were upregulated in HN, and are involved in photosynthesis, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis in carbon metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, and defense processes against abiotic stresses. Therefore, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum of HN plants to defend against abiotic stress mechanisms affects the expression of other proteins, while enhancing carbon metabolism, especially glycolysis, fructose and mannose metabolism, and photosynthesis, in response to high nitrogen stress.

Discussion

Effects of nitrogen stress on nitrogen metabolism in flue-cured tobacco

Physiological and biochemical changes in flue-cured tobacco under nitrogen stress include alterations in chlorophyll synthesis, nitrogen transfer from roots to the aboveground parts, and nitrogen assimilation-related enzyme activities [35]. Nitrate nitrogen (NO3−) and ammonium nitrogen (NH4+) are the two main inorganic forms of nitrogen absorbed and utilized by flue-cured tobacco, affecting the nitrogen assimilation process and regulating plant growth [36]. NR is a key enzyme in the nitrogen assimilation pathway [37, 38]. GS is a key enzyme for the assimilation of NH4+ into organic nitrogen in plants, and catalyzes the ATP-dependent synthesis of glutamine (Gln) from NH4+ and glutamate (Glu) [39].Under LN conditions, the expression of nitrogen metabolism-related proteins was downregulated, including ferric oxygen-reducing protein-nitrite reductase (fragment) (Q04032, FC = 0.772, P = 0.016), nitrite reductase (Q76KB0, FC = 0.746, P = 0.029), and glutamine synthetase (Q5QJ64, FC = 0.650, P = 0.036; Q42951, FC = 0.689, P = 0.040).This indicates that insufficient nitrogen supply reduces nitrite reductase (NiR) activity in flue-cured tobacco leaves, which is consistent with the findings of Liang Lik et al. [11, 40].Meanwhile, the downregulation of glutamine synthetase expression inhibits arginine biosynthesis, thereby blocking nicotine synthesis in leaves [41]. Ubiquitination is the main protein degradation process, in which the 26 S proteasome, ubiquitin-activating enzyme, and ubiquitin-protein ligase are upregulated, and the ubiquitin carrier protein is downregulated [42]. In amino acid metabolism, tyrosine aminotransferase (TyrAT) catalyzes the aminotransfer of L-Tyr and α -ketoglutaric acid, generating 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid and L-glutamic acid. Its decarboxylation product, 4-hydroxyphenylacetaldehyde, is the precursor of benzylisoquinoline alkaloid (BIAs) [43], tyrosine aminotransferase-like (A0A1S4B421,FC = 1.268,P = 0.010) expression was upregulated to promote tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine metabolism, as well as tropane, piperidine, pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis, and isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis. During chlorophyll synthesis, the expression of magnesium protoporphyrin IX monomethyl ester (oxidative) cyclase (A0A1S3YNG0,FC = 0.799,P = 0.001) was downregulated, and porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism (ko00860) was inhibited [44].

In contrast, nitrogen metabolism in flue-cured tobacco was enhanced under HN conditions and the nitrogen-containing compound synthesis pathway was upregulated. During chlorophyll synthesis, protoporphyrinogen oxidase (Q9SAX9,FC = 1.217,P = 0.007) expression was upregulated and porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism (ko00860) was promoted. Protoporphyrinogen oxidase (EC 1.3.3.4, PPOX) is the last enzyme in the branched tetrapyrrole biosynthetic pathway, and its substrate, protoporphyrin, is directed to the Mg and Fe branches for chlorophyll and heme biosynthesis, respectively [45, 46]. The 26 S proteasome, ubiquitin-activating enzyme, ubiquitin-protein ligase, and ubiquitin carrier protein were all downregulated under HN conditions. The expression of several proteins was upregulated, including ω-amidase (A0A1S4CS21, FC = 1.479, P = 0.009), γ-aminobutyric acid transaminase 3 (A0A1U7XHP4, FC = 1.299, P = 0.029), δ−1-pyrroline-5-carboxylic acid dehydrogenase 12A1 (A0A1S3YT57, FC = 1.320, P = 0.032), enol-like coenzyme A hydratase 2 (A0A1S4C898, FC = 1.285, P = 0.002), tryptophan synthase β-chain 2-like (A0A1S4D936, FC = 1.210, P = 0.040), and amine oxidase (A0A314L1M9, FC = 1.277, P = 0.008). The upregulation of these proteins promotes the metabolism of alanine, aspartate, glutamate, arginine, proline, glycine, serine, threonine, tyrosine, β-alanine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan.Meanwhile, amine oxidase (A0A314L1M9) expression was upregulated, promoting isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis as well as tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis.

LN mainly affects nitrogen transport and uptake in flue-cured tobacco. Additionally, LN inhibits arginine biosynthesis (a process related to nicotine synthesis), as well as amino acid and chlorophyll synthesis; it also promotes protein degradation and weakens nitrogen metabolism in flue-cured tobacco.Both HN and LN promote the biosynthesis of isoquinoline alkaloids, as well as the biosynthesis of tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloids (Fig. 7).

Effects of nitrogen stress on carbon metabolism in flue-cured tobacco

The intensity of sugar accumulation during plant growth and development is closely related to the activity of enzymes related to carbon metabolism. As the energy metabolism center of plants, carbon metabolism mainly involves sucrose and starch metabolism [47]. The glycolysis pathway is an important process of carbohydrate metabolism, and its main role in higher plants is to oxidize sucrose to produce ATP, NADH, and pyruvate [48]. Under abiotic stress, the decomposition of fructose to pyruvate in plants through glycolysis requires a variety of enzymes. These enzymes not only act as catalytic reactions and energy regulators, but also act as signal transduction devices to respond to environmental changes [49]. Phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) catalyzes the reversible reaction of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (1,3-BPG) and ADP, generating 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG) and ATP.In this study, we found that the expression of sedoheptanulose 1,7-diphosphatase (A0A1S4C882,FC = 0.804,P = 0.009), involved in CO2 fixation, was downregulated under LN conditions, and that phosphoglycerate kinase (A0A1S4AJ36,FC = 0.822,P = 0.013), involved in the reduction of carboxylated products, was downregulated. The differential proteins involved in the regeneration of ribulose 1,5-diphosphate: D-fructose-1,6-diphosphate 1-phosphate hydrolase (A0A1U7 × 1T0,FC = 0.828,P = 0.023), ribulose diphosphate carboxylase small chain (A0A1S3ZBG0, FC = 0.816,P = 0.004;A0A1S3ZY67,FC = 0.761,P = 0.003), fructose diphosphate aldolase (A0A1S3YQI2,FC = 0.748,P = 0.029;A0A1S3Z8U8,FC = 0.747,P = 0.023), and ribulose diphosphate carboxylase large chain (P48709,FC = 0.647,P = 0.043) were all downregulated. This indicates that LN inhibited glycolysis/gluconeogenesis in flue-cured tobacco.In addition, glycolytic enzymes may participate in the regulation of V-ATPase, and this regulatory process mainly relies on the transport channel of V-ATPase itself to transport H+, thereby maintaining the stable balance of intracellular pH [50, 51]. In the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), the expression of isocitrate dehydrogenase (NADP-ICDH) (A0A1S3XUI8,FC = 0.796,P = 0.015) is downregulated, which inhibits the oxidative decarboxylation of isocitrate α-ketoglutaric acid [52]. The downregulation of malate dehydrogenase (A0A1J6KCA1, FC = 0.704, P = 0.040) inhibits the oxidative dehydrogenation of malate to oxaloacetate—a reaction that this enzyme normally catalyzes. This reaction is the fourth redox step in the TCA cycle and also the final step of the cycle.In other words, the regeneration of oxaloacetic acid and the TCA cycle were inhibited. In the pentose phosphate pathway, D-fructose-1, 6-diphosphate-1-phosphate hydrolase (A0A1U7 × 1T0), glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (A0A1S4CI25,FC = 0.825,P = 0.037), phosphoglucomutase (A0A1S4AIH6,FC = 0.818,P = 0.005) and fructose diphosphate aldolase (A0A1S3YQI2, A0A1S3Z8U8) were down regulated, which inhibited fructose and mannose metabolism.

Under HN conditions, sucrose synthase (SS) (A0A1U7YLM4,FC = 1.486,P = 0.005) was upregulated in flue-cured tobacco. SS is involved in sucrose biosynthesis and catalyzes the formation of sucrose from UDPG and fructose in a reversible reaction. SS maintained the balance of sucrose concentration in plants through this reversible reaction and promoted the metabolism of starch and sucrose. Mannan endo-1,4- β-, the expression of mannosidase (A0A314LFW2,FC = 2.026,P = 0.043), and triose phosphate isomerase (A0A1S3XGM0,FC = 1.215,P = 0.003) was upregulated, which promoted the metabolism of fructose and mannose.The expression of phosphoglycerate kinase (A0A1S4CGR1, FC = 0.814, P = 0.023; Q42962, FC = 0.798, P = 0.043) was downregulated, which may weaken the oxidation of triose in the glycolysis pathway. Additionally, pyruvate kinase (A0A1U7WKB6, FC = 0.690, P = 0.008) was downregulated, while phosphopyruvate hydratase (C5J0G6, FC = 0.809, P = 0.008) was also downregulated. These changes collectively inhibit pyruvate synthesis in the glycolysis pathway and further reduce ATP production.In the pentose phosphate pathway, 5-ribose phosphate isomerase (A0A1S3Z1 × 1, FC = 1.482, P = 0.046) was upregulated. This enzyme catalyzes the isomerization of ribose 5-phosphate to xylulose 5-phosphate; its upregulation enhances this isomerization reaction, thereby promoting sugar metabolism through the pentose phosphate pathway.

Therefore, under LN conditions, glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway are all inhibited in flue-cured tobacco. In contrast, HN promotes the metabolism of sucrose, starch, fructose, and mannose in flue-cured tobacco; under HN, the TCA cycle is still inhibited, but the pentose phosphate pathway is promoted (Fig. 8).

Conclusion

Using TMT proteomics, we studied DEPs in middle leaves of flue-cured tobacco at rosette stage under nitrogen stress. HN had more DEPs than LN: LN had 157 (65 up, 92 down), HN 219 (123 up, 96 down). GO, KEGG and PPI analyses showed nitrogen stress mainly affected tobacco’s cellular and metabolic processes. This study is the first to reveal the molecular mechanism of LN and HN synergistically promoting alkaloid synthesis through differential enzyme systems via TMT proteomics—under LN, tyrosine aminotransferase-like protein (A0A1S4B421) is upregulated to provide precursors for alkaloid synthesis by promoting aromatic amino acid metabolism, while under HN, amine oxidase (A0A314L1M9) directly drives alkaloid biosynthesis.Under LN conditions, various enzymes related to glycolysis, TCA cycle, and pentose phosphate pathway were down-regulated, leading to the inhibition of carbon metabolism.HN promoted carbon and nitrogen metabolism. Specifically, the up-regulation of protoporphyrinogen oxidase and other enzymes promoted chlorophyll synthesis. The up-regulation of sucrose synthase, triose phosphate isomerase, and phosphoglycerate kinase promoted starch and sucrose synthesis. The up-regulation of multiple amino acid synthases promoted amino acid formation. These key differential proteins (e.g., tyrosine aminotransferase-like protein, sucrose synthase) can serve as candidate molecular markers for nitrogen efficiency or alkaloid quality regulation, providing targets for nitrogen-efficient breeding of flue-cured tobacco.Under LN conditions, tyrosine aminotransferase in amino acid metabolism was up-regulated, which promoted the metabolism of tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine and also enhanced alkaloid biosynthesis. However, the down-regulation of proteins such as GS might be the reason for the reduction in arginine and chlorophyll synthesis. Under HN conditions, the delay in the degradation of multiple proteins might be the main factor contributing to alkaloid biosynthesis (Fig. 9).
