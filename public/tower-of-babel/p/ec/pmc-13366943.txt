Predicting societal risk factors for respiratory co-infections: a cross-sectional study based on multi-source fever clinic data
Authors: Jin Huang, Meiquan Zhang, Haiting Fan, Shengzhong Li, Jianshuai Liu, Xiong Lin, Qing Li, Jiawei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to analyze epidemiological characteristics, clinical manifestations, and socio- environmental risk factors of viral-mycoplasma mixed respiratory infections in children presenting at fever clinics, providing evidence for clinical decision-making.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we analyzed clinical data from 132 pediatric patients with respiratory co-infections confirmed by direct immunofluorescence assay at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (May 1, 2023 – April 30, 2024). Data collection included demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, laboratory results, and pathogen profiles. Social behaviors and environmental factors were also evaluated. An age- and season-matched comparison group of 185 children with single respiratory infections was included. Statistical analyses involved t-tests, χ² tests, and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsThe majority of patients were aged 4–9 years (75.76%), with a male predominance (64.39%). Adenovirus was the most frequently detected pathogen (100 cases) and demonstrated the highest co-infection rate with Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Cases were primarily concentrated in the main urban area of Fuzhou City, notably in Jin’an (38 cases) and Gulou (34 cases) districts. The co-infection group had a significantly longer duration of fever than the single infection group (P < 0.001) and more severe systemic clinical manifestations. Laboratory parameters, including white blood cell, neutrophil, and C-reactive protein levels, were significantly elevated in the co-infection group (P < 0.05). Among environmental factors, both low ambient temperature (P < 0.001) and humidity (P = 0.011) were significantly associated with co-infection. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent risk factors for co-infection: Low ambient temperature (OR = 1.144), poor hand hygiene practices (OR = 3.4691), attendance at childcare facilities (OR = 2.042), recent family member infection history (OR = 1.989), and not having received the influenza vaccine (OR = 2.266).ConclusionsViral-mycoplasma co-infections in pediatric patients demonstrate distinct epidemiological patterns and more severe outcomes. Social behaviors and environmental factors represent pivotal risk factors, warranting targeted public health interventions during high-risk periods and in key locations.

Introduction

Acute respiratory infection (ARI) is a common infectious disease in pediatric fever clinics and a leading cause of global pediatric morbidity. ARI is divided into acute upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) and lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) [1]. It is also a general term for acute inflammation of the nasal cavity, pharynx, or larynx. Viruses are the predominant etiological agents of URTIs, including influenza virus (FLU), parainfluenza virus (PIV), adenovirus (AdV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), rhinovirus, human enterovirus, human metapneumovirus, human coronavirus, and human bocavirus [2]. Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is another important pathogen causing respiratory tract infections in children. MP infection accounts for 10%-40% of community-acquired pneumonia in children aged 5–14 years, though it is less frequent in those under 5 years old [3]. If not treated promptly, URTIs can progress to LRTIs due to factors such as impaired host immunity and co-infections, making them a major reason for childhood hospitalization worldwide. Furthermore, children are particularly susceptible to severe outcomes owing to their narrow bronchial lumens, poor airway ciliary motor function, and inefficient respiratory clearance mechanisms, which often lead to bronchial obstruction [4]. Additionally, children’s lung development is incomplete; their metabolic rates are higher than those of adults, while their respiratory reserve capacity is lower. Consequently, respiratory infections in children often present with more pronounced clinical symptoms and can progress more rapidly [5].

Respiratory tract infections are highly prevalent in children; however, the epidemiology and clinical significance of mixed respiratory infections in pediatric fever clinic settings are not well characterized. The impact of mixed viral infections is not fully understood, although growing evidence links them to increased disease severity, which has important implications for clinical management and patient outcomes [6]. This cross-sectional study was therefore designed to investigate the epidemiology and clinical characteristics of common mixed respiratory infections in a pediatric fever clinic population. The goals of this study were to enhance our understanding of these co-infections and to provide evidence to inform their diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methods

Data collection

We conducted a cross-sectional study using clinical data from 132 pediatric patients with respiratory co-infections. These patients were identified from the fever clinic of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between May 1, 2023, and April 30, 2024. For comparison, 185 patients with single-pathogen infections were selected from the same clinic during the same period. Patients with co-infection with two or more pathogens were confirmed by pathogen detection, and those who visited the hospital for other major diseases were excluded. Clinical data of the enrolled children were collected from the outpatient electronic medical record system of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, including sex, age, season, area, type of disease, clinical symptoms, and auxiliary examination. This study was approved by the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee.

Patient selection

The comparison group (single-infection group, SIG; n = 185) was frequency-matched to the case group by age and season of visit to minimize confounding, ensuring contemporaneous enrollment from the same patient population. It comprised patients with laboratory-confirmed monoinfections, each caused by one of the five target pathogens: influenza A virus (IAV, n = 37), influenza B virus (IBV, n = 37), MP (n = 37), RSV (n = 37), or AdV (n = 37). This design ensured that cases and controls were drawn from the same pathogen panel, providing a matched basis for comparison. This group was limited to monoinfections involving one of the five pathogens detectable by our assay (MP, RSV, IAV, IBV, or AdV). Each pathogen was represented by 37 cases, constituting 20.0% (37/185) of the comparison group. Our primary objective is to elucidate the epidemiological patterns and risk factors associated with viral-mycoplasma co-infections in Fuzhou, China, rather than evaluating bacterial co-infections.

Definition of terms

Co-infection (also called mixed infection): A concurrent infection with two or more distinct pathogen species in a single host. This term emphasizes the diversity of pathogens involved. Co-infections were defined as concurrent infection with two or more distinct pathogen species, limited to five pathogens: MP, RSV, IAV, IBV, and AdV, as detected by direct immunofluorescence assay. The two-pathogen co-infection: A specific type of co-infection characterized by the presence of exactly two different pathogen species. It is a subset of co-infection.

Laboratory indicators and groups

On the day of initial medical consultation, a disposable nasopharyngeal swab (Shaoxing Yingchuang Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Product No. Y1, Zhejiang, China) was inserted through the nostril into the nasal cavity until it reached the nasopharynx. It was then rotated back and forth 2–3 times, promptly withdrawn, and placed into a tube containing viral transport medium (Hangzhou Chuangxin Biological Detection and Control Technology Co., Ltd., Catalog No. 20180019, Zhejiang, China). Samples were stored at 4 °C for no longer than 48 h and transported for analysis as soon as possible. A direct immunofluorescence assay (DFA, Diagnostic Hybrids, USA) was employed to detect five common respiratory pathogens [7]: IAV, IBV, MP, AdV, and RSV. The procedure involved: (1) Fixing the sample on a slide using acetone; (2) Adding fluorescein-labeled monoclonal antibodies specific to IAV, IBV, MP, AdV, and RSV; (3) Incubating at 37 °C for 30 min; (4) Washing with PBS to remove unbound antibodies; (5) Examining under a fluorescence microscope at 400× magnification. Positive results were determined by distinct apple-green fluorescence. The kit’s sensitivity and specificity were validated per manufacturer specifications. Internal quality controls were included in each run. All procedures were performed in strict accordance with standardized laboratory protocols. Positive pathogen results were provided by the Laboratory Department of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, while routine blood test parameters and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were retrieved from the hospital’s inspection system.

Social and environmental factors

To investigate the associations between social and environmental exposures and mixed respiratory infections, we conducted a systematic evaluation of both social behavioral factors and ambient air quality parameters. Social determinants were assessed through structured caregiver interviews and detailed medical record reviews. Specifically, we collected data on hand hygiene practices (handwashing frequency), recent exposure to childcare settings (regular attendance within the past three months), household infection history (any confirmed respiratory infection among cohabiting family members within 14 days prior to the participant’s symptom onset), and influenza vaccination status (based on self-reported receipt of the seasonal influenza vaccine). Poor hand hygiene was defined as a self-reported handwashing frequency of fewer than three times per day. This cutoff was selected based on the median frequency identified in our preliminary survey for key scenarios (e.g., after school/play, before meals, and after using the toilet). Although hand hygiene encompasses other important aspects such as soap use, duration of washing, and thorough drying, these aspects were not captured in the current study due to the challenges of objective measurement via retrospective questionnaires.

Environmental exposure metrics were quantified using real-time air quality data obtained from the Fuzhou Historical Air Quality Analysis Platform, which provided measurements of carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), ozone (O₃), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), temperature (−°C), humidity (%), and wind speed (m/s). To account for potential exposure lag effects, each patient’s symptom onset date was matched with air quality data within a 24-hour temporal window. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS version 27.0 statistical software, and GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 software was also used for data analysis and visualization. The Shapiro-Wilk test will be specified as the method for assessing normality of data distribution. All data were tested for normality, and measurement data following normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. An independent samples t-test was used to compare the two groups. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians with interquartile range (IQR, 25th to 75th percentiles) and compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Count data were expressed as the number of cases (n) or percentages (%). Binary logistic regression was performed using variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis, with enter method for variable inclusion. GraphPad Prism was used for statistical analysis and graph generation, and ArcGIS (version 10.8.2) was used for topographic and standard deviation ellipse (SDE) mapping. The SDE was drawn to understand the direction and range of the distribution of cases in Fuzhou. The major and minor half-axes of the ellipse represent the direction and range of data distribution, respectively. The directionality of the distribution was more visible, and the difference between the major and minor half-axes was more significant. The constituent ratio is used to express the proportion and distribution of each component of the disease in the population, and is generally expressed as a percentage. According to the Seventh National Population Census of China, the permanent resident population of Fuzhou City was 8,291,268 as of November 1, 2020. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic features

Among 132 patients diagnosed with mixed respiratory infections, 100 visited the fever clinic and 32 attended the general clinic. The majority of cases were concentrated in the 4–9-year-old age group, with the highest number observed in the 6-year-old cohort. Gender comparisons showed that the proportion of males (64.39%) was significantly higher than that of females (35.61%). Among 132 patients, AdV was the most common pathogen (100 cases), followed by MP (85 cases), IAV (58 cases), IBV (51 cases), and RSV (4 cases). Among 132 patients with co-infections, infections involving two pathogens were the most frequent (101 cases, 76.52%), followed by three-pathogen infections (28 cases, 21.21%), and four-pathogen infections (3 cases, 2.27%). Among the 132 co-infection cases, a total of 298 pathogen detections were identified. When calculated as a percentage of total detected pathogens, the distribution was as follows: AdV represented 33.6% (100/298) of all pathogens detected, MP 28.5% (85/298), IAV 19.5% (58/298), IBV 17.1% (51/298), and RSV 1.3% (4/298) (Table 1).

Pathogenic characteristics

A total of 14 types of co-infections were identified. The most prevalent combinations were those involving AdV, specifically AdV + MP (28.03%), AdV + IAV(12.88%), and AdV + IBV(12.12%) (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, co-infections involving MP and influenza virus were also common, whereas those involving RSV were uncommon (Fig. 1B). According to the weather characteristics of Fuzhou, March to May is defined as spring, June to August as summer, September to November as autumn, and December to February of the next year as winter. The heatmap analysis (Fig. 1C) demonstrated a clear seasonal pattern: a high incidence of infections occurred in winter, sporadic cases were detected in spring and autumn, and no cases were recorded in summer. This seasonality was further characterized by a shift in the predominant co-infection types; AdV + MP was the most common combination in winter, whereas AdV + IAV was predominant in spring and autumn.

Spatial distribution

The main urban districts of Fuzhou include Gulou District, Jin’an District, Taijiang District, Cangshan District, Mawei District, and Minhou County, and the general patients came from these areas. The regional distribution of 132 mixed cases showed that Jin’an district had the largest number of infections (38 cases), followed by Gulou District (34 cases), Taijiang District (17 cases), Cangshan District (16 cases), Minhou County (15 cases), and Mawei District (12 cases). Cases were primarily concentrated in the two main urban districts of Fuzhou City, Gulou and Jin’an Districts, a distribution pattern associated with the population density in these areas (Fig. 2). The results of SDE show that the distribution of co-infection cases in Fuzhou City has a certain direction. Co-infection cases in this hospital were mainly from the Jin’an and Gulou Districts (Fig. 3A- B).

Clinical characteristics analysis

A total of 317 patients with respiratory tract infections were enrolled in this study, including 132 in the co-infection group and 185 in the single infection group. The single infection group comprised pediatric patients with laboratory-confirmed monoinfections caused by one of the five target pathogens: IAV (n = 37), IBV (n = 37), MP (n = 37), RSV (n = 37), and AdV (n = 37). This design ensured direct comparability with the co-infection group, as both were drawn from an identical pathogen spectrum. Comparison of laboratory indicators showed that the peripheral white blood cell count (WBC, Z=-1.986, P = 0.047), neutrophil count (NEUT, Z=-2.532, P = 0.011), and CRP (, Z=-2.586, P = 0.010) were significantly higher in the co-infection group than in the single infection group. There were no significant differences in lymphocyte (LYMPH), monocyte (MONO), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), and platelet (PLT) counts between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

A comparative analysis of the clinical characteristics between the two groups revealed that the fever duration in the co-infection group was significantly longer than that in the single infection group (Z=-9.492, P < 0.001), with more pronounced systemic symptoms. Specifically, the incidence of fatigue (62.12% vs. 48.11%, P = 0.014) and anorexia (47.73% vs. 36.22%, P = 0.040) was significantly higher in the co-infection group. With regard to respiratory symptoms, the co-infection group exhibited a higher incidence of cough and expectoration (64.39% vs. 52.43%, P = 0.034), pharyngalgia (51.52% vs. 37.84%, P = 0.016), and pneumonia (17.42% vs. 7.03%, P = 0.004) than the single-infection group. Additionally, gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea and vomiting (25.76% vs. 14.60%, P = 0.013) and diarrhea (13.64% vs. 6.49%, P = 0.032), were more frequently observed in the co-infection group. The incidence of chest distress and pain was also significantly higher in the co-infection group (6.82% vs. 2.16%, P = 0.039). However, no statistically significant differences were found in the occurrence of muscle soreness, headache, chills, rhinobyon, runny nose, conjunctival congestion, or dyspnea between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Social and environmental factors

Considering the significant influence of social and environmental factors on infection outcomes, this study further compared the differences in social and environmental exposures between the co-infection group (n = 132) and the single-pathogen infection group (n = 185). Regarding social factors, the proportion of individuals with poor hand hygiene (less than three times per day) was significantly higher in the co-infection group than in the single-infection group (64.39% vs. 34.59%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the co-infection group exhibited higher rates of exposure to childcare institutions (41.67% vs. 28.65%, P = 0.015), recent family infection history (34.09% vs. 23.78%, P = 0.015), and non-vaccination (71.97% vs. 59.46%, P = 0.022). No statistically significant difference was observed in pet contact between the two groups (P = 0.558). With respect to environmental factors, by analyzing the air quality indicators in Fuzhou at the time of disease onset, it was found that the co-infection group was exposed to higher concentrations of CO (P = 0.024) and SO₂ (P = 0.003), as well as significantly low ambient temperatures (LAT, −°C) (P < 0.001) and humidity (H) (P = 0.011). No significant differences were found between the two groups regarding the air quality index (AQI), Particulate Matter 2.5 (PM2.5), Particulate Matter 10 (PM10), NO₂, O₃, or wind scale (WS) (P > 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

Mixed respiratory infections are primarily caused by viruses, bacteria, and atypical pathogens, transmitted via respiratory droplets, aerosols, and contact with contaminated surfaces. These infections, which present with symptoms including fever, fatigue, cough, sputum production, sore throat, and dyspnea, represent a major cause of childhood morbidity, hospitalization, and mortality [8]. Several studies have reported that the influenza virus infection predisposes individuals to secondary Streptococcus pneumoniae (SP) pneumonia. This occurs because viral infection can compromise the respiratory epithelial barrier and impair alveolar macrophage function, thereby increasing susceptibility to secondary bacterial co-infection [9, 10]. The prevalence of viral co-infection in children with ARIs has been reported to be 18% [11]. Furthermore, respiratory co-infections are known to prolong the febrile phase in refractory MP pneumonia, exacerbate systemic inflammatory responses, increase the risk of pulmonary and extrapulmonary complications, and ultimately lead to longer hospital stays [12]. However, contrasting these findings, Wishaupt et al. reported no significant difference in clinical outcomes between single-virus and multiple-virus infections in children with ARIs [13]. This discrepancy may be explained by variations in the specific pathogens involved in the co-infections across different study populations. Consequently, the development and widespread implementation of targeted prevention and control strategies, informed by the specific characteristics of the circulating pathogens, are essential for effective disease management [14]. By analyzing clinical data from pediatric fever clinic patients, this study aimed to characterize the epidemiology, clinical features, and socio-environmental risk factors associated with mixed respiratory infections in children in Fuzhou.

This study found that children aged 4–9 years old in Fuzhou, especially the 6-year-old group, became a high-risk group for mixed respiratory infections. Research by Korean scholars showed that children under the age of 5 years accounted for 76.8% of co-infection cases, with 3-6-year-olds being the peak age group (accounting for 42.1%), which is similar to the results of this study [15]. This age clustering is related to the following reasons: first, the children’s immune system is immature, resulting in insufficient cross-defense capabilities against multiple pathogens; second, the mucosal barrier function is weak, the frequency of respiratory cilia movement is low, and the efficiency of pathogen clearance is weak. In terms of sex distribution, this study observed that the proportion of male children (64.39%) was significantly higher than that of female children (35.61%), indicating that male children are more susceptible, which has also been reported internationally. A study in the United States analyzed blood samples from 125 RSV-infected children and found that children with severe infection had inherent immune abnormalities. Male children were more likely to develop severe symptoms, which were related to delayed viral clearance and uncontrolled inflammation caused by insufficient activation of the interferon pathway [16]. Before puberty, males are at greater risk of severe consequences than females, which may be related to the Th2/Th17 immune deviation and weakened IFN response in prepubertal boys [17]. According to the clinical observation of Wang et al., the above five respiratory pathogens are widely prevalent in children, among which IAV and MP have the highest infection rates, and IBV, AdV, and MP have higher infection rates in male children than in female children [18]. Krishnan A and Shapirod [19–20] found that the detection rate in males was higher than that in females, which is consistent with our results.

This study identified AdV as the most prevalent pathogen in co-infections, accounting for 75.8% of cases. The primary co-infection patterns were AdV with MP, IAV, and IBV. Co-infections involving MP and influenza viruses were also frequent, whereas those with RSV were less common. The clinical significance of AdV is underscored by Zheng L [21], who reported that AdV co-infection in children under five with pneumonia was associated with more pronounced laboratory abnormalities and severe clinical outcomes compared to those without AdV. Our finding of AdV predominance is consistent with a study from Türkiye, where human rhinovirus was the most common virus in co-infections, followed by AdV [22]. The notable increase in AdV circulation observed in 2023 might be a consequence of the non-pharmaceutical interventions during the COVID-19 pandemic, which have altered the epidemiology of common respiratory viruses, leading to more complex circulation patterns in the post-pandemic era [23]. When comparing the spectrum of co-infecting pathogens, previous studies have reported varying prevalence and patterns. For instance, the co-infection rate in children with MP was 31.4%, most frequently with rhinovirus, followed by SP, Haemophilus influenzae (HI), Coronavirus (CoV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Parainfluenza virus (PIV), and AdV [24]. Another study found that 27.0% of MP patients were co-infected with other pathogens, including SP, HI, Staphylococcus aureus (SA), Human bocavirus (HBoV), RV, and RSV [25]. In severe influenza cases, viral co-infections were present in 7.9% of patients, predominantly with rhinovirus and enterovirus [26]. Of note, Drew et al. showed that patients infected with the Flu A/B virus and another virus coinfected were at increased risk of hospitalization [27]. Our results show that the infection rate of RSV is relatively low, but many studies have reported that RSV is a common pathogen in children, especially in infants and young children. This difference in results may be related to region and climate [28–29].

This study found that mixed respiratory infections in children in Fuzhou exhibited marked seasonality, with an incidence peak in winter and a trough in summer. This pattern is likely attributable to climatic factors, particularly temperature and humidity. Several mechanisms may explain this association. First, low winter temperatures can prolong the stability of viral surface proteins, thereby extending viral survival on surfaces [30]. Second, dry air can compromise the respiratory mucosal barrier by reducing mucus secretion from goblet cells, which increases the risk of pathogen colonization [31]. Previous studies have confirmed that temperature affects viral survivability, with respiratory viruses persisting longer in cold, dry environments [32]. A study in Shandong Province, China, identified winter as the peak season for respiratory virus infections, with influenza virus, human rhinovirus (HRV), RSV, and MP being the most common [33]. Similarly, a 3-year surveillance study in Italy found that the primary peaks of viral detection in children with acute respiratory infections occurred in February 2013 (74.81%), February 2014 (62.99%), and January 2015 (68.91%) [34]. Our results further showed that the predominant co-infection pattern in winter was AdV + MP, whereas Adv + IAV co-infections were more common in spring and autumn.

The main urban area of Fuzhou includes Jin’an District, Gulou District, Taijiang District, Cangshan District, Mawei District, and Minhou County. The study showed that among the 132 mixed cases, Jin’an District had the largest number of infections, followed by Gulou District. Sporadic cases have been reported in the Taijiang, Cangshan, Minhou, and Mawei. Most cases were concentrated in Gulou and Jin’an District, which were related to population density. The SDE results showed that the distribution of co-infection cases in Fuzhou City had a certain direction. Co-infection cases in this hospital were mainly from the Jin’an and Gulou Districts. This regional aggregation may be influenced by the hospital’s service area, as these districts are the primary draining areas for the hospital, which could partly explain the case concentration. However, it might also be associated with factors such as population density, transportation, and environmental conditions. Although there is limited research on the specific direction of respiratory infection pathogen transmission, studies on infectious disease transmission in general urban areas have shown that transportation routes and population mobility patterns can influence the direction of pathogen transmission [35]. Research on the spatial distribution of urban infectious diseases also indicates that high population density leads to more frequent contact between individuals, increasing the probability of pathogen transmission [36–37]. Therefore, the observed clustering should be interpreted with caution; it may reflect healthcare access patterns rather than true epidemiological risk factors, representing a limitation of this hospital-based study.

Comparison of laboratory indicators showed that the WBC, NEUT, and CRP levels in the co-infection group were significantly higher than those in the single infection group. Viral co-infection could affect inflammatory states, cardiac function, liver function, and kidney function, possibly due to the virus’s potential to damage tissues, and was associated with high levels of WBC, PCT, LDH, and PLT, indicating that co-infection may affect the immune response, leading to inflammation [38]. Previous studies have reported that multiple pathogen infections have synergistic effects; respiratory virus co-infection can increase the severity of AdV infection, and early diagnosis and treatment can shorten the course of the disease [15]. Lv Z’s al. showed that patients with mixed respiratory pathogens had higher WBC counts, NEUT counts, and CRP levels than those with a single viral infection, indicating a more pronounced inflammatory response [39]. Clarifying clinical implications: The observed statistically significant elevations in WBC, NEUT, and CRP in the co-infection group, with median values remaining within or near normal limits, suggest a modest inflammatory elevation rather than a severe systemic response. This suggests a modest elevation rather than a severe inflammatory response. Assessing the severity of mixed respiratory infections in pediatric patients requires comprehensive consideration of clinical symptoms and imaging findings, in addition to laboratory inflammatory markers.

This study compared the clinical symptoms and signs of the two groups and found that the co-infection group exhibited more pronounced systemic symptoms, such as a significantly longer duration of fever, than the single infection group. Additionally, when comparing symptoms across various systems, the co-infection group also showed more severe manifestations in the respiratory, digestive, and circulatory systems. This is consistent with the Ntagereka PB study, which reported a more complex and severe clinical presentation due to mixed respiratory infections [40]. The coexistence of multiple respiratory pathogens may cause more extensive damage to the respiratory tract and other organs, leading to more severe symptoms [38]. Comparing the imaging results between the two groups, it was found that the MIP group was more likely to develop pneumonia. Metsälä et al. reported that, at least in children, approximately 30–60% of lower respiratory infections have been found to be mixed [41]. Patients with co-infections may have more serious infections and are likely to progress to pneumonia [42–43].

Regarding social determinants, our analysis revealed that the mixed-infection cohort had significantly higher proportions of individuals with poor hand hygiene, exposure to childcare facilities, recent family infection history, and lack of influenza vaccination. A systematic review underscored the critical role of hand hygiene in preventing respiratory infections, as inadequate practices facilitate pathogen transmission [44]. Martin et al. prospectively followed 225 children in daycare settings and observed a high frequency of multiple respiratory virus co-infections, indicating an increased risk in such environments [45]. While a recent study reported that influenza vaccination modestly reduced the odds of other respiratory infections, an earlier investigation noted an elevated incidence of viral co-infections among vaccinated children [46]. Furthermore, children residing with family members who had recent respiratory infections have a higher probability of developing co-infections, highlighting the importance of implementing strategies to mitigate intrafamilial transmission.

Univariate analysis indicated that exposure to higher concentrations of CO and SO₂, as well as LAT (−°C) and H, were associated with an increased risk of co-infections. This is mechanistically plausible, as air pollutants like CO and SO₂ are known to damage the respiratory mucosa, enhancing host susceptibility to pathogens [47]. However, in the multivariate logistic regression model, only lower temperature and poor hand hygiene emerged as the most significant independent risk factors. This suggests that the effects of specific air pollutants may be mediated or confounded by temperature, which serves as a broader proxy for favorable environmental conditions for viral transmission. Our results align with the work of Donzelli et al. [48], reinforcing the importance of integrating environmental monitoring with behavioral interventions into public health strategies to prevent outbreaks. Based on the identified risk factors, the following targeted strategies are proposed: (1) Protect high-risk groups through “winter pre-screening for symptoms” in boys aged 4–9 years; (2) Prioritize families with recent respiratory infections and promote daily hand hygiene supervision in childcare settings; (3) Increase influenza vaccination coverage to > 80% and advance multivalent vaccine development; (4) Establish a “Children’s Respiratory Health Weather Index” to dynamically guide outdoor activities based on temperature thresholds and AQI; (5) Implement multiplex PCR testing for children with fever lasting > 3 days and CRP > 7.51 mg/L, focusing on AdV, MP, and influenza viruses.

This study has several strengths. First, the integration of multi-source data—clinical laboratory records, structured social-behavioral assessments, and real-time environmental metrics—enabled a comprehensive analysis of risk factors. Second, spatial analysis (Standard Deviation Ellipse) effectively visualized the geographic clustering of cases, identifying potential high-transmission zones within the urban environment. Third, the study focused on a pediatric fever clinic population, a critical surveillance point for community-acquired infections, and employed a rigorous age- and season-matched case-control design. Finally, multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent socio-behavioral risk factors while controlling for potential environmental confounders.

A major limitation of this study was its retrospective case analysis design. Therefore, the indications for the samples were not necessarily consistent throughout the population, which may have affected the age, time, and spatial distribution. Information collected from medical records was incomplete. Some symptoms may have been derived from the patients’ subjective description, which may have affected the reliability of the results. A further limitation is the use of DFA rather than multiplex PCR, which precluded detection of rhinovirus, enterovirus, and other common respiratory pathogens. Future studies should employ multiplex molecular assays to provide a more comprehensive assessment of co-infections. In addition, the study was based in tertiary hospitals and did not include patients treated in primary care. This study also had the following limitations: short observation time, small sample size, and single-center design. We agree that the retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability. Therefore, a larger range of epidemiological investigation and clinical research is needed to facilitate the clinical diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of mixed respiratory infection.

Conclusion

This study delineates the epidemiological profile of pediatric respiratory co-infections in Fuzhou, highlighting a predominance in boys aged 5–7 years, winter seasonality, and spatial clustering in urban districts. AdV was the leading pathogen, with AdV + MP and AdV + IAV as prevalent combinations. Co-infections manifested heightened inflammatory responses and clinical severity, necessitating multiplex pathogen testing. Key modifiable risks included low air temperature exposure and inadequate hand hygiene, alongside childcare attendance and non-vaccination as independent factors. These findings advocate for integrated interventions: hand-hygiene promotion, vaccination programs, and environmental monitoring to mitigate outbreaks.
