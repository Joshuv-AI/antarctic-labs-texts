Immunomodulatory Role of Natural Progesterone in Pregnancy: A Review
Authors: Prakash Mehta, Hemant Deshpande, Anjali Deshpande, Reshma Quadros, Shruti Dharmadhikari, Chintan Khandhedia, Gaurav Puppalwar, Amey Mane, Suyog Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimPregnancy complications such as miscarriage, preterm birth (PTB), and luteal phase defects (LPD) are significant reproductive health issues globally, with both physical and psychological implications. This review aims to examine the role of natural progesterone in pregnancy‐related disorders, specifically threatened and recurrent miscarriage, PTB, and LPD. Additionally, it examines how natural progesterone's immune‐modulating actions, including both direct and indirect involvement, contribute to maintaining pregnancy.MethodsThis narrative review was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, and reference screening to identify English‐language studies examining the role of natural progesterone in miscarriage, PTB, and LPD. Emphasis was placed on progesterone's immunomodulatory actions, including cytokine regulation and the activity of the progesterone‐induced blocking factor (PIBF), and their relevance to pregnancy maintenance.ResultsNatural progesterone demonstrates clinical benefit in managing threatened miscarriage, recurrent pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and LPD. Evidence shows that it is well‐tolerated with manageable side effects, reducing miscarriage rates, preventing PTB, and improving LPD outcomes through its effects on the maternal‐fetal interface. This review also highlights progesterone's dual immunomodulatory action, direct cytokine regulation, and indirect influence via PIBF, which together contribute to improved pregnancy maintenance and outcomes.ConclusionNatural progesterone supports pregnancy by preventing miscarriages, preterm labor, and LPD through its direct and indirect immunomodulatory mechanisms. Clinically, it is well‐tolerated with no serious or unexpected adverse events. However, gaps remain in fully understanding its effectiveness, emphasizing the need for further research.Keywords: anti‐inflammatory cytokines, natural progesterone, pregnancy, progesterone‐induced blocking factor, pro‐inflammatory cytokines

Introduction

Globally, miscarriage is a significant reproductive health issue, affecting approximately 10%–15% of recognized pregnancies and accounting for nearly 23 million cases annually [1, 2]. In India, data from the National Family Health Survey (2019–21) indicate that about 7% of pregnancies end in miscarriage, with 23% of these occurring in the first trimester [3, 4]. Additionally, an epidemiology study reported that around 32% of women had a history of at least one spontaneous miscarriage of ≤ 20 weeks of gestation [5]. A threatened miscarriage is characterized by vaginal bleeding and uterine cramping before 20 weeks of gestation in a viable pregnancy [6, 7]. In contrast, recurrent miscarriage is defined as loss of three or more consecutive pregnancies (UK Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists) or two or more failed pregnancies (American Society for Reproductive Medicine) [1, 8].

Miscarriage carries physical risks, such as bleeding and infections, and psychological impact, including post‐traumatic stress, anxiety, and depression [1, 9]. Threatened or recurrent miscarriage also increases the risk of obstetric complications, including preterm birth (PTB) and stillbirth in future pregnancies [1]. PTB, defined as childbirth before 37 completed weeks of gestation, is classified as extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), very preterm (28 to < 32 weeks), and moderate to late preterm (32–37 weeks), and occurs in about half of spontaneous preterm labor cases [9].

Progesterone, produced by the corpus luteum in the ovary, is essential for successfully establishing and maintaining pregnancy [10]. It creates a favorable uterine environment, promotes endometrial changes for implantation, and supports pregnancy maintenance by modulating immune response, reducing uterine contractility, and improving circulation [10, 11]. Additionally, it maintains pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines by promoting a Th2‐dominant immune response [12, 13]. This balance is crucial for preventing maternal immune rejection of the fetus, as it regulates cytokine production and suppresses natural killer (NK) cell activity [12, 13]. Progesterone deficiency disrupts this maternal‐fetal immune tolerance by increasing pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including interferon (IFN)‐γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, negatively impacting pregnancy outcomes [10, 14]. Supporting this, Zwahlen et al. concluded that progesterone promotes a Th2‐like cytokine profile, inhibits mast cell degranulation, and increases vascular permeability [15]. Furthermore, progesterone mediates a “window of vulnerability” after ovulation, during which immune function is suppressed, allowing embryo implantation that might otherwise be prevented with a more active immune system, as the embryo could be recognized as foreign [15]. Hanita et al. reported significantly lower single serum progesterone levels in nonviable compared to viable pregnancies (10.7 ng/mL vs. 45.9 ng/mL), measured at a median gestational age of 10 weeks (up to 13 weeks), suggesting it as a marker for early pregnancy failure [16]. Progesterone deficiency during the luteal phase has also been linked to recurrent miscarriage and preterm labor [17]. A study involving 8.9% of ovulatory cycles with clinical luteal phase deficiency (luteal phase duration of < 10 days) is associated with hormonal imbalances that compromise endometrial stability, potentially causing pregnancy loss [18]. A recent scoping review of 23 studies found a significant correlation between low progesterone levels and first‐trimester miscarriage, with combined hormonal deficiencies further raising the risk [7]. Supplementation of exogenous progesterone is used to overcome the luteal phase deficiency during in vitro fertilization (IVF) cycles and can be administered via different routes [19].

Natural progesterone supports pregnancy maintenance by influencing progesterone‐induced blocking factor (PIBF), indirectly promoting Th2‐type cytokine production [20] and by promoting the expression of human leukocyte antigen‐C (HLA‐C) and HLA‐G, essential for maternal‐fetal immune tolerance [14, 21]. Furthermore, it modulates maternal T‐cell cytokine production, reducing polyfunctional T cells and T‐cell proliferation [14]. Hence, supplementing progestogens during pregnancy may potentially lower miscarriage risk. This review explores how natural progesterone influences pregnancy disorders such as threatened and recurrent miscarriage, PTB, and LPD. It also highlights progesterone's direct role in immune modulation, particularly through cytokine regulation, and its indirect effects via PIBF, which aids pregnancy maintenance. The review also emphasizes progesterone's positive impact on pregnancy outcomes through its immunomodulatory effects. However, it identifies limitations in clinical evidence, including the need for larger, controlled trials and more research on optimal treatment protocols.

Methods

This narrative review is based on literature that came to our attention on or before April 2025. A literature search was conducted in PubMed and Google Scholar for articles. Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional studies. Relevant studies, reviews, and guidelines in English that provided information on the immunomodulatory role of natural progesterone in threatened and recurrent miscarriage, PTB, and LPD were considered. Studies were selected based on their relevance and contribution to understanding the topic. Articles focusing on unrelated populations or interventions were excluded. Key information from the selected articles, including study characteristics, interventions, and main findings, was summarized descriptively (Table 1). No formal critical appraisal or quantitative synthesis was performed.

Progesterone‐Recommendations in Pregnancy

Progesterone, including natural micronized progesterone (NMP), or synthetic progestins like dydrogesterone (DYD), 17a‐hydroxyprogesteronecaproate (17OHP‐C), and medroxyprogesterone acetate, are commonly used for threatened miscarriage, recurrent miscarriage, PTB, and luteal phase defects (LPD) [10, 28]. The routes of administration include oral, vaginal, and intramuscular, with varying regulatory statuses [10]. Vaginal progesterone is commonly recommended for a history of PTB, shortened cervix in singleton gestation, miscarriages, and fertility programs. Research indicates that switching from intramuscular to vaginal progesterone after a positive pregnancy test does not increase miscarriage risk and may provide greater flexibility in treatment options [19]. The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology and the Indian Society for Assisted Reproduction recommend injectable progesterone for luteal phase support (LPS) in women undergoing fertility programs [32, 36]. As per a survey of Indian gynecologists, NMP is commonly used in pregnancy and assisted reproductive technology (ART) cycles [28]. Over 50% of Indian gynecologists reported using progesterone supplementation for managing threatened miscarriage, recurrent miscarriage, PTB, and as LPS [28]. However, a recent meta‐analysis indicates that 17OHP‐C may not significantly reduce PTB or improve neonatal outcomes compared to placebo [37] and may pose risks like congenital heart disease [38]. Due to concerns over its lack of efficacy and safety in reducing spontaneous PTB, the US Food and Drug Administration withdrew the approval of 17OHP‐C in 2023 [39].

Mechanisms of Action of Natural Progesterone in Pregnancy Disorders

Natural progesterone plays a critical role in early pregnancy by facilitating embryo attachment and implantation, inducing secretory transformation in uterine lining, and promoting fetal development [40, 41, 42]. It helps prevent miscarriage, preterm labor, and LPDs by reducing uterine contractility and suppressing premature contractions [10, 40, 41]. This is achieved through time‐dependent stimulation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate and nitric oxide synthase, along with the reduction of prostaglandins, oxytocin, and intracellular calcium, ultimately promoting myometrial relaxation [10, 40, 41]. Progesterone also supports endometrial receptivity by promoting glandular changes and stromal vascularity during the luteal phase [10, 17, 43]. Additionally, it aids successful implantation by promoting cellular proliferation in response to human chorionic gonadotropin [42]. It prevents premature cervical ripening by inhibiting metalloproteinase‐mediated collagen breakdown, preserving cervical integrity, and lowering prostaglandin synthesis [11, 41].

Progesterone's multifaceted actions prevent preterm labor by inhibiting estrogen receptor alpha expression, reducing uterine sensitivity to estrogen, and preventing the formation of connexin 43 gap junctions in the myometrium, which are key for coordinated contractions [41]. It protects against preterm premature rupture of fetal membranes through antiapoptotic, anti‐inflammatory, and antithrombogenic actions, helping preserve membrane integrity [44].

Furthermore, progesterone also modulates the immune system by binding to gamma‐delta T cells, promoting uterine receptivity and immune tolerance for early pregnancy in ART [45]. Direct Action of Natural Progesterone—Modulation of the Cytokine in Pregnancy Disorders

Natural progesterone plays a pivotal role in immune regulation during pregnancy. Pregnancy enhances humoral responses while suppressing cell‐mediated immunity and autoimmune activity, shifting from Th1 to Th2 dominance [46]. Th1 cells produce pro‐inflammatory cytokines like IFN‐γ, TNF‐β, interleukin (IL)‐2, and TNF‐α, which drive cell‐mediated immunity, cytotoxicity, and inflammatory reactions linked to graft rejection and autoimmunity [12]. In contrast, Th2 cells secrete anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐10, and IL‐13, promoting antibody production and humoral immunity [12]. By promoting a Th2‐dominant immune response, progesterone ensures a critical balance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines, essential for a successful pregnancy [12, 13, 47]. This anti‐inflammatory milieu created by progesterone protects the fetus from maternal immune rejection by regulating cytokine production and suppressing NK cell activity [12, 13]. Moreover, the Th1/Th2 ratio is a predictive tool for monitoring pregnancy and managing miscarriage risks [46].

Progesterone also upregulates HLA‐G expression, shielding trophoblasts from NK‐mediated cytotoxicity and maintaining immune homeostasis at the maternal‐fetal interface through dendritic and T regulatory cells [13, 48, 49]. Furthermore, it inhibits inflammation, promoting immune tolerance, successful pregnancy, and fetal development [49]. Additionally, Galectin‐1, regulated by progesterone, induces tolerogenic dendritic cells and IL‐10‐secreting regulatory T cells [50]. IL‐10 counteracts the effects of TNF‐α, which further reduces inflammation, supports trophoblast invasion, and promotes fetal tolerance, critical for embryo implantation [46, 51].

Progesterone also drives uterine decidualization by activating estrogen‐primed stromal cells and regulates key cytokines like IL‐15 and leukemia inhibitory factors for implantation and IL‐11 for further decidualization via autocrine signaling [52]. Progesterone stimulates stromal cells to produce CXCR3 ligands, including CXCL10 and CXCL11, accumulating NK cells at implantation sites [53]. During the luteal phase, when progesterone levels dominate, chemokine receptors such as CXCR1, CCR2b, and CCR5 are significantly upregulated in the decidua [54]. Beyond immunomodulation, progesterone also stimulates the decidua to synthesize critical‐implantation‐supporting factors, such as renin, prolactin, insulin‐like growth factor binding proteins‐1, and extracellular matrix components (laminin, fibronectin) [52]. Through these actions, progesterone plays an essential role in initiating decidualization, recruiting NK cells, and supporting their differentiation via IL‐15 signaling [52]. Threatened Miscarriage

At the maternal–embryonic interface, progesterone promotes immune tolerance to paternal antigens. Low serum progesterone levels predict increased miscarriage risk, with elevated IL‐6 and TNF‐α linked to threatened abortion [6]. A prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) with 221 patients diagnosed with threatened abortion demonstrated that progesterone helps prevent imminent abortion by creating an anti‐inflammatory environment [6]. It reduces IL‐6 and TNF‐α while increasing IL‐10, with the effect proportional to the dose of progesterone [6]. Research comparing the Th1 and Th2 cytokine profiles in women with miscarriages and those with healthy pregnancies found elevated Th1 cytokine levels in the miscarriage group, while Th2 cytokine levels were notably lower [8].

Recurrent Miscarriage

Natural progesterone exhibits immunosuppressive effects by modulating the Th1/Th2 responses. In a study involving 30 women with RPL, 15 of whom were treated with 200 mg progesterone and 15 untreated, alongside 30 healthy pregnancy controls, progesterone therapy led to a marked reduction in Th1 cytokines. Specifically, levels of TNF‐α (37.80 vs. 78.09), IL‐6 (16.93 vs. 81.12), and pentraxin‐3 (PTX‐3) (17.42 vs. 73.53) were significantly lower at all time points in the treated group compared to the untreated group, with a corresponding increase in live birth rates (p < 0.005) [8]. A related study found that while baseline levels of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and PTX3 were comparable across groups, their progressive rise during pregnancy was effectively modulated by oral micronized progesterone treatment [22].

Th17 cytokines, including IL‐17A and IL‐23, are linked to inflammatory responses that can compromise pregnancy. Progesterone downregulates these cytokines while upregulating protective Th2 cytokines [55]. A study on women with unexplained RPL showed progesterone significantly reduced Th1 (IFN‐α, TNF‐γ) and Th17 (IL‐17A, IL‐23) cytokines while increasing anti‐inflammatory IL‐6 [23]. Progesterone's suppression of embryotoxic immune responses further supports its therapeutic role. Studies involving peripheral blood mononuclear cells from women with RPL demonstrated that progesterone inhibits Th1 immunity to trophoblasts and promotes the secretion of transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β [24].

Preterm Labor

Research suggests a Th1 cytokine bias in some women with preterm delivery [56]. A study of 54 women showed higher levels of Th1 cytokines (IFN‐γ, IL‐2) in the preterm group, while the normal pregnancy group had more Th2 cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐10). Additionally, elevated serum levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐12) have been reported in women with PTB compared to those with term births [56]. A preclinical study involving pregnant C57BL/6 mice with preterm delivery examining the effects of vaginal progesterone (1 mg per 200 mL, n = 10) demonstrated a reduction in the proportion of myometrial IFN‐γ positive neutrophils and systemic IL‐1β concentrations [57]. Additionally, vaginal progesterone exhibited local anti‐inflammatory effects at the maternal‐fetal interface and cervix while providing protection against endotoxin‐induced PTB [57].

A recent study on JEG3 trophoblast cells demonstrated that progesterone receptor membrane component 1 (PGRMC1), known for binding and mediating progesterone's actions, mediates progesterone's effects at the maternal‐fetal interface [21]. Specifically, PGRMC1 was shown to enhance immune tolerance markers HLA‐C and HLA‐G, and upregulating ELF3, a transcription factor for HLA‐C [21]. Knockout of PGRMC1 reduced progesterone response, and RU486 inhibited ELF3 expression and invasion, indicating PGRMC1's role in progesterone's immunoregulatory actions [21].

Luteal Phase Defect

The luteal phase combines hormonal, immunomodulatory, and anti‐inflammatory effects essential for implantation and pregnancy maintenance, with progesterone promoting maternal immune tolerance through direct effects on CD4+ and CD8+ cells [14]. Physiological doses of progesterone reduce pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IFN‐γ and TNF‐α), while increasing an anti‐inflammatory cytokine (IL‐4). This cytokine shift, along with reduced T‐cell polyfunctionality and proliferation, supports a pregnancy‐friendly immune environment. These effects are dose‐dependent, with significant changes at concentrations similar to those at the maternal–fetal interface [14]. Clinical studies, including real‐world investigations and prescription event monitoring, have demonstrated its utility in managing conditions like luteal phase insufficiency and bad obstetric history (BOH). In a nationwide study involving 153 women with BOH, prescribing doctors considered oral natural progesterone as a clinically feasible option [58]. Furthermore, progestogens are commonly used in ART to address luteal insufficiency or create a luteal phase in donor cycles [45].

Indirect Action of Natural Progesterone: Progesterone‐Induced Blocking Factor

Natural progesterone regulates key genetic factors like PIBF, NK cells, and HOXA10, which support implantation and reduce uterine contractility. PIBF is necessary for a successful pregnancy as it mediates the favorable Th2‐type activity by producing Th2‐dominant cytokines through an indirect action [13, 59].

PIBF is a protein comprising 757 amino acid residues with a predicted molecular mass of 89 kDa, secreted by lymphocytes in response to progesterone, and plays a pivotal role in immunomodulation during pregnancy [13, 20, 47, 59, 60]. It affects membrane progesterone receptor expression on CD4+ T lymphocytes, which is crucial for pregnancy maintenance, immune response modulation, and inhibition of NK cells, cytotoxic T cells, and arachidonic acid synthesis [13, 14, 60]. Progesterone binds to its receptor on progesterone‐sensitive γδ T cells, activating them upon interaction with fetal or paternal antigens. This triggers the release of PIBF, which binds to its receptors and heterodimerizes with the IL‐4 receptor [13]. This complex activates the Jak1/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) 6 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing production of Th2‐type cytokines [13]. PIBF enhances anti‐inflammatory effects by increasing IL‐4, IL‐6, and IL‐10 and suppresses the STAT4 pathway in Th1 cells by promoting suppressor of cytokine signaling‐3 interaction with the IL‐12 receptor, leading to reduced production of Th1 cytokines like IL‐2, INF‐γ, and TNF‐α, hereby shifting the Th1/Th2 balance to favor pregnancy (Figure 2) [13]. Furthermore, PIBF is suggested to reduce NK cell activity by inhibition of perforin and to promote the production of asymmetric antibodies, exerting an antiabortive effect in vivo [61]. Both progesterone and PIBF are emerging promising biomarkers for evaluating pregnancy viability, as progesterone supports healthy pregnancies and PIBF fosters a favorable immune environment for the semi‐allogeneic fetus [13, 26].

Clinical Implications of PIBF in Pregnancy Disorders

Serum levels of PIBF rise with gestational age during normal pregnancy, while low concentrations are associated with spontaneous pregnancy loss, unexplained miscarriages, and PTB. This underscores PIBF's vital role in maintaining pregnancy and its link to pregnancy‐related complications [13, 47, 62].

Threatened Miscarriage

A study of 32 pregnant women found that PIBF serum levels were significantly higher in those with normal gestational age < 20 weeks (47.153 ± 23.830 ng/mL) compared to women with threatened abortion (11.540 ± 4.892 ng/mL) [63]. Prospective studies on women with threatened spontaneous abortion and threatened preterm delivery consistently showed significantly lower serum PIBF concentrations compared to healthy pregnant women (214.4 ± 120.6 ng/mL vs. 357.3 ± 159.9 ng/mL; p < 0.05, and 171.12 ± 162.06 ng/mL vs. 272.85 ± 114.87 ng/mL; p < 0.05, respectively) [47]. These lower PIBF levels were accompanied by altered cytokine profiles, specifically lower serum levels of anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 (6.7 ± 4.7 pg/mL) and higher levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α (8.4 ± 3.4 pg/mL) and IFN‐γ (9.6 ± 1.8 pg/mL) [47]. Together, these findings suggest that reduced PIBF levels and dysregulated cytokine production may be associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [47]. However, other studies reported that women with threatened abortion do not exhibit a Th1/Th2 cytokine imbalance [64, 65]. Moreover, the immunological protective effect of progesterone in threatened abortion cases appears to be mediated by restoring PIBF levels, rather than directly modulating cytokine production.

Recurrent Pregnancy Loss

Preclinical studies revealed that PIBF treatment led to significantly higher production of IL‐4 and IL‐10 in spleen cells from nonpregnant female mice, supporting its role in enhancing anti‐inflammatory cytokines [66]. A study involving women with a history of unexplained RSM and those presenting with threatened PTD demonstrated that the presence of PIBF was associated with a significant increase in IL‐4, IL‐6 (p < 0.01), and IL‐10 (p < 0.001) levels [59]. A study by Kashyap et al. of 65 RPL cases found lower serum PIBF levels, which correlated with increased IL‐12 and decreased IL‐10 mRNA expression. This suggests that higher PIBF levels may lower miscarriage risk by modulating the immunological state of pregnancy [67].

Preterm Labor

In cases of impending preterm delivery, administration of oral progesterone (200 mg) significantly increased PIBF levels (p = 0.039) and was associated with a prolonged gestational period [Odds ratio (OR): 1.016, 95% Confidence interval (CI): 1.001–1.03; p = 0.033] [25]. A study of 37 threatened PTB symptomatic women and 41 healthy pregnant women (24–28 weeks of gestation) found PIBF to be a strong predictor of PTB, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.956 (95% CI 0.884–0.989; p < 0.0001) [62]. Another study revealed significant differences in immune markers between healthy pregnant women (n = 111) and women at risk of premature pregnancy termination (n = 120) [68]. Healthy pregnant women exhibited significantly higher levels of PIBF (65.05% ± 3.15%) and IL‐10 (28.54% ± 2.06%), and lower IL‐12 expression (6.34% ± 1.05%) [68]. In contrast, women in the at‐risk group showed markedly lower expression levels of PIBF (14.52% ± 2.5%) and IL‐10 (7.06% ± 0.94%), along with elevated IL‐12 expression (30.59% ± 2.92%) on lymphocytes (p < 0.001 for all comparisons) [68]. Another study showed that PIBF concentrations in urine (13.9 ± 2.8 ng/mL) and serum (124.6 ± 46.7 ng/mL) were significantly lower in women who experienced miscarriage compared to those with preterm delivery (18.1 ± 4.4 ng/mL; 180.6 ± 54.4 ng/mL) and term delivery (20.4 ± 8.5 ng/mL; 208.7 ± 114.3 ng/mL), suggesting that PIBF levels may serve as a predictor for successful pregnancy outcomes following IVF [60].

Luteal Phase Support

Exposure to progesterone led to a fourfold increase in PIBF levels in the luteal phase compared to the follicular phase (p = 0.0005) [26]. Supplemental progesterone elevated mid‐luteal phase serum PIBF level compared to endogenous P (547.3 vs. 443.0 ng/mL; nonsignificant) [26]. An in vitro study found PIBF expression in 24.9% of mid‐cycle sera, 49% of luteal phase sera from nonpregnant women, and 75% of luteal phase sera from women who conceived, suggesting PIBF may play a role in early implantation by suppressing NK lymphocyte activity [43]. Measuring PIBF levels can help predict successful pregnancy aiding in early implantation assessment and identifying pregnancy outcomes or potential risks. Pregnancy Outcomes Through Immunomodulatory Effects of Natural Progesterone

Clinical studies support the use of progestogen supplementation in pregnancy maintenance, reducing the risk of miscarriage and improving immunomodulatory balance [6, 8, 22, 25]. It helps prevent imminent abortion by creating an anti‐inflammatory environment, supporting its role in pregnancy maintenance [6]. Furthermore, it results in reduced pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6 and TNF‐α) and increased anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 (with effects proportional to the progesterone dose), thereby preventing imminent abortion [6]. It has been shown that in patients with RPL, exogenous progesterone decreases the Th1 pro‐inflammatory response and efficiently improves pregnancy outcomes by modulating cytokine levels [8]. The results of Kishore et al., 2022 indicated a significant reduction in Th1 cytokines, including TNF‐α (37.80 vs. 78.09), IL‐6 (16.93 vs. 81.12), and PTX‐3 (17.42 vs. 73.53), in the progesterone group compared to normal pregnancy controls [8]. Furthermore, patients treated with oral micronized progesterone demonstrated better pregnancy outcomes, with a significantly higher number of live births (p < 0.005) than the untreated group [8]. Similarly, another RCT involving 60 patients with RPL showed a significant rise in TNF‐α (p = 0.0002) and PTX‐3 (p = 0.028) in the untreated group, while IL‐6 in the oral micronized progesterone‐treated group (p = 0.0001) at 16 weeks [22]. This treatment was associated with a significantly lower abortion rate (3.5% vs. 16.7%) in the progesterone‐treated group compared to untreated RPL patients [22]. Thus, oral micronized progesterone appears to exert an immunomodulatory effect by influencing TNF‐α, PTX‐3, and IL‐6 levels that support pregnancy continuation in RPL [22]. Furthermore, an experimental study demonstrated a significant increase in PIBF (p = 0.039) and IL‐10 levels (p = 0.031) following progesterone administration for a maximum of 7 days [25]. A positive correlation was observed between PIBF and IL‐10 levels (r = 0.549; Spearman rank correlation test), as well as between serum PIBF levels and gestational length (r = 0.428; Point biserial correlation test) [25]. These findings suggest that increased serum PIBF and IL‐10 levels following natural progesterone administration may contribute to prolonged gestational length in patients at risk of preterm delivery [25].

The Safety Profile of Natural Progesterone

The safety and tolerability of progesterone treatment have been extensively evaluated in several clinical studies for different obstetric conditions. Research has found no fatal, serious, or unexpected adverse events with NMP in women with threatened miscarriage [69]. A meta‐analysis revealed minimal or no effect on congenital anomalies or serious adverse pregnancy events with micronized vaginal or oral progesterone [70]. Mild adverse events, including gastritis (2.2%), vomiting (2.2%), drowsiness (4.3%), dizziness (3.2%), and spotting (1.1%) were reported in high‐risk women with PTB factors treated with oral NMP, all managed symptomatically without the need for hospitalization or referral [71]. Vaginal progesterone gel significantly reduced adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with singleton pregnancies and short cervix, lowering neonatal intensive care unit admissions, respiratory distress syndrome, mortality, and hospitalization periods [72, 73]. Further, oral NMP‐sustained release (300 or 400 mg OD) in luteal phase correction after ART cycles showed mild and transient side effects, including drowsiness, hyperemesis, and giddiness [58]. Both combined therapy (oral micronized progesterone and vaginal progesterone gel) and vaginal progesterone gel monotherapy for LPS were well‐tolerated, with high patient satisfaction scores [74].

Multifactorial Etiologies Limiting the Effectiveness of Progesterone

Miscarriage, RPL, and PTB are widely recognized as multifactorial disorders. Although immunological dysregulation represents an important pathogenic mechanism, other etiologies such as chromosomal abnormalities, which account for the majority of first‐trimester losses [75, 76], uterine structural anomalies including septate uterus and fibroids [77] endocrine dysfunction such as thyroid disease and diabetes mellitus [78], thrombophilia including antiphospholipid syndrome and inherited mutations [79], infections of the genital tract or placenta [80], and lifestyle factors such as advanced maternal age, smoking, and obesity [81] also contribute substantially to adverse pregnancy outcomes. While progesterone exerts significant immunomodulatory effects that support pregnancy maintenance, its therapeutic benefit is highly context‐dependent. In pregnancies compromised by chromosomal abnormalities, major uterine anomalies, uncontrolled endocrine disorders, or untreated infections, progesterone supplementation cannot correct the underlying pathology and is therefore unlikely to alter outcomes [82]. These scenarios underscore the importance of individualized clinical evaluation and emphasize that progesterone's role represents only one pathway within the complex and multifactorial pathophysiology of pregnancy disorders.

Current Limitations in Clinical Evidence and Recommendations for Further Research and Clinical Applications

While natural progesterone has demonstrated promising immunomodulatory effects in pregnancy, the current evidence is limited by substantial methodological heterogeneity, including variability in study design, populations, dosing regimens, and administration routes. Many studies lack appropriate control groups, which reduces comparability and the overall reliability of findings. Despite evidence of its potential in reducing pregnancy complications in high‐risk groups, there is insufficient evidence exploring its immunomodulatory effects in threatened or established preterm labor. Though its role in regulating inflammation is suggested, mechanisms remain poorly understood. While generally well tolerated, the long‐term safety profile, particularly regarding maternal and fetal immune effects, is lacking. Furthermore, the lack of standardized progesterone dosage, duration, and timing of progesterone therapy, particularly in IVF pregnancies, underscores the need for evidence‐based protocols.

Future research should address current limitations through well‐designed RCTs employing standardized methodologies and outcome measures. Comparative studies evaluating different progesterone formulations, dosages, and administration routes in conditions such as threatened miscarriage and preterm labor are essential to determine optimal regimens and maximize therapeutic efficacy. Investigations should also assess patient‐specific responses, including distinctions between natural conception and ART pregnancies, to identify subgroups most likely to benefit. Long‐term follow‐up studies are essential to assess maternal and fetal immune outcomes and developmental effects in children exposed to progesterone prenatally. Standardization of study protocols, endpoints, and dosing regimens will improve comparability and strengthen evidence for clinical practice. Additionally, raising awareness about natural progesterone for managing pregnancy complications is crucial for maternal and fetal health. Healthcare organizations should collaborate with local authorities on education programs to ensure equitable access to information and treatment.

Conclusion

Natural progesterone supports pregnancy by preventing threatened miscarriage, RPL, preterm labor, and LPD. Its mechanisms include promoting uterine quiescence, inhibiting cervical ripening, thickening uterine lining, and modulating immune responses. It directly modulates cytokines, shifting the Th1/Th2 balance to favor a Th2‐dominant immune response and indirectly promotes fetal immune tolerance through PIBF, both critical for a successful pregnancy. Although laboratory studies consistently show Th2‐dominance and elevated PIBF levels, translating these immunologic changes into improved clinical outcomes, such as reduced miscarriage rates or higher live birth rates, remains challenging. Variability in study design, progesterone formulations, dosing regimens, and patient characteristics contributes to inconsistent results, highlighting the need for large‐scale, longitudinal studies that integrate immune biomarkers with standardized clinical outcomes and dosing protocols. Both natural and synthetic progesterone formulations are well‐tolerated with manageable side effects, and clinical studies suggest they may enhance pregnancy outcomes through immunomodulation at the maternal‐fetal interface. Future research should clarify optimal therapeutic regimens and confirm whether laboratory immunologic effects reliably correspond to improved clinical outcomes, requiring coordinated efforts among researchers, clinicians, and healthcare organizations.
