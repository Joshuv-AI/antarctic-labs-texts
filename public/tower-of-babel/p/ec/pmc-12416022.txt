Morphology and molecular phylogeny of Cercospora dispori from Disporum smilacinum and Disporum viridescens
Authors: Seo-Yeong Cho, Lamiya Abasova, Ji-Woon Jeong, In-Young Choi, Hyeon-Dong Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main objective of the present study is to compile and comprehensively reevaluate all known records of Cercospora dispori in order to establish a standardized framework for the accurate characterization and identification of this species. Nine isolates of C. dispori obtained from Disporum smilacinum and D. viridescens from various regions of Korea were analyzed. The morphological features of the fungus and isolated colonies were described and illustrated. Nucleotide sequences of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, including the protein-coding genes, such as actin (actA), calmodulin (cmdA), translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1), and histone H3 (his3), were determined, and a phylogenetic tree was generated from a multi-gene dataset of ITS+tef1+act+cmdA+his3. This study provides a more robust taxonomic placement of C. dispori and clarifies its phylogenetic relationships within the genus Cercospora.

Disporum smilacinum A. Gray and D. viridescens (Maxim.) Nakai, belonging to the family Colchicaceae, are monocotyledonous perennial herbs commonly known as fairy bells, primarily distributed in East and Southeast Asia [1]. D. smilacinum was determined as one of several plant species with a positive belowground carbon sequestration capacity, positioning it as a promising nature-based solution for reducing carbon emissions [2]. On the other hand, the bioactive compounds isolated from the leaves of D. viridescens have shown anti-inflammatory properties, indicating their therapeutic potential in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases [3].

Despite their ecological and pharmacological importance, both Disporum species are susceptible to foliar fungal diseases, which may compromise their health and functional value. The causal agent of leaf spot disease associated with hyphomycetes on these plant species is Cercospora dispori Togashi & Y. Maki, which is known from Japan and Korea [4]. This fungus was first described on D. smilacinum var. ramosum Nakai (currently: D. smilacinum) from Japan, based on type material collected on September 22, 1940 [5–7]. In Korea, this fungus was initially reported on D. smilacinum [8], followed by an additional record on D. viridescens [9]. Groenewald et al. clarified its phylogenetic position within the genus Cercospora using an isolate from D. viridescens collected from Korea, thereby confirming its species delimitation and position within the genus [9]. The earlier record by Kim and Shin provided only a morphological description of the fungus [8]. Given the limited publicly available sequence data, restricted to a single isolate originating from the study of Groenewald et al. [9], there is a critical need to expand and verify genetic information for this species. The present study aims to compile and reassess all known records of Cercospora dispori and establish a standardized basis for the accurate characterization and identification of this species by applying an integrative approach that combines detailed morphological examination with multilocus molecular phylogenetic analyses. Additionally, this study contributes to improving the quality and reliability of sequence data available in public databases, thereby supporting future research and diagnostic efforts involving Cercospora species.

Seven samples of D. smilacinum and D. viridescens showing leaf spot symptoms were collected in 2024 from various parts of the Korean peninsula and deposited in the Herbarium of Jeonbuk National University (JBNU-F). These samples, alongside two previously preserved isolates in the Korean Agricul­tural Culture Collection (KACC 43945 and 46410), provided a basis for comprehensive morphological and molecular-phylogenetic investigation. Preliminary microscopy of samples confirmed that the fungus belongs to the genus Cercospora, based on the morphology of conidiophores and conidia. All newly collected samples were subjected to morphological examination using a Zeiss AX10 microscope equipped with an AxioCam MRc5 camera (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). To obtain a single-spore isolate, conidia collected from the young lesions were mounted on a drop of sterile water, then streaked onto 2% water agar (WA; Junsei, Tokyo, Japan) plates supplemented with 100 mg/L streptomycin sulfate and incubated at 25 °C. After two days, the germinating conidia were transferred onto 2% potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco, Detroit, MI, USA) plates to obtain a pure culture. Colony colors were determined using Raynar’s color charts [10].

For molecular-phylogenetic analysis, genomic DNA was extracted from two-week-old colonies grown on PDA using MaglistoTM 5 M kits (Bioneer, Dajeon, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer pairs of ITS5/ITS4, ACT-512F/ACT-783R, CAL-228F/CAL-737R, EF1-728F/EF1-986R and CylH3F/CylH3R were used for amplification and sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, actin (actA), calmodulin (cmdA), translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1) and histone H3 (his3) genes, respectively [11–13]. The obtained forward and reverse sequences were inspected using BioEdit 7.2.5 [14], then assembled sequences were deposited in GenBank (Table 1). The dataset for each gene was generated using 38 closely related sequences of Cercospora spp. retrieved from GenBank, sequences from eight isolates obtained here, and one outgroup. Each dataset was aligned individually in MEGA 11 using the MUSCLE command [15], and later, were combined into a single multigene dataset of ITS+tef1+act+cmdA+his3 using SequenceMatrix [16]. Septoria provencialis (CPC 12226) was chosen as the outgroup [9]. A phylogenetic tree was built through the maximum likelihood (ML) method in raxmlGUI 2.0.14 based on the GAMMA model with GTR substitution [17]. Bootstrap analyses (BS) were performed through 1000 replications to evaluate the robustness of the internal branches. The generated tree was visualized using FigTree v.1.4.4 [18].

Leaf spots were amphigenous, confluent, yellow near the margin, and brown as the disease progressed, eventually spreading along the entire leaf surface and developing into necrotic lesions (Figures 1(A–C) and 2(A–C)). Stromata were lacking or very small, up to 32 µm diam, dark brown, intraepidermal or substomatal (Figures 1(D) and 2(D)). Conidio­phores were solitary, forming in 9–30 loose to dense fascicles, thick-walled, dark brown, paler toward the apex, straight or sinuous-geniculate, almost uniform in width, conically truncate at the apex, 46–107(–184) × 3.3–7.7 μm, width was not uniform, central part was broader than basal and apical parts, 0–4-septate (Figures 1(E,F) and 2(E,F)). Conidiogenous cells were integrated, proliferating sympodially or rarely percurrently, terminal and intercalary; conidial loci are thickened, not protuberant, apical to lateral. Conidia were solitary, hyaline, acicular to cylindrical, slightly curved, obconically truncate or subtruncate, thickened at the base, acute or obtuse at the apex, 46–159(–184) × 3.3–7.7 μm, 2–12-septate, thin-walled, smooth, hila were conspicuously darkened (Figures 1(G) and 2(G,H)).

All isolates obtained in this study showed consistent colony morphology and growth characteristics on PDA. Two-week-old colonies grown on PDA at 25 °C were 25–30 mm in diam., with entire to slightly undulate margin, the obverse surface irregularly folded with prominent network of ridges, white in the margin, becoming pale olivaceous to olivaceous in the center, the reverse of colony was dark olive, with blood red margin (Figures 1(H) and 2(I)). Morphological characteristics of diagnostic features and colonies were consistent with the previous descriptions of C. dispori [5,7–9].

The resulting sequences obtained from eight samples were nearly identical, with the length of 532 bp in the ITS region, 174/226 bp in actA, 268/307 bp in cmdA, 361/401 bp in his3, and 298/307 bp in tef1 genes. A nucleotide BLAST search was applied to the newly obtained sequences to compare their similarity with other reference sequences in GenBank. The results revealed 100% identity to Cercospora chinensis (JX142842, JX143334, JX142596) in the cmdA, his3, and tef1 genes. Whereas, 99.44% similarity to C. capsici (KT193659) for ITS and 97.69% to C. citrullina (MZ437990) for the actA gene was reported.

In the presented phylogenetic tree (Figure 3), our isolates clustered within a single clade with strong support (99% BS value), positioned closer to C. chinensis than to the CBS isolate of C. dispori (CBS 132608), which was located on a separate branch. Notably, a significant variation was observed in the tef1 gene sequence of the D. viridescens-derived isolate (CBS 132608), which differed markedly from sequences obtained from our isolates. Specifically, the tef1 sequence (JX143349) from CBS 132608 is 249 bp long and differs from our sequences by 13 nucleotides. Additionally, there are 6 bp differences in the actA and 2 bp differences in the his3 gene. Despite originating from Korean material, the CBS isolate exhibits considerable genetic variation compared to the isolates obtained in this study. These discrepancies call into question the authenticity of the CBS reference strain and underscore the need for updated sequence data derived from verified isolates. The reason of this inconsistency remains unclear; however, it is possible that the observed discrepancy resulted from a sequencing error or contamination of the original isolate. To address this ambiguity, it is essential that future studies verify the identification of the CBS isolate of Cercospora dispori through both morphological and molecular analyses.

Furthermore, as indicated by the homology search results, C. dispori and C. chinensis share nucleotide sequence identity in the cmdA, his3, and tef1 genes. Consequently, the branch representing C. dispori is closely positioned near that of C. chinensis, which was isolated from Polygonatum humile (CBS 132612), in the current phylogenetic tree. Although both hosts are monocots, the families Colchicaceae (order Liliales) and Asparagaceae (order Asparagales) are neither taxonomically nor phylogenetically related [19]. Morphologically, C. chinensis and C. dispori show no significant differences and share many similar characteristics. In both species, stroma are either absent or very small (to 30 µm vs. up to 32 µm), and the sizes of conidiophores are comparable (61–100 × 5–6 µm vs. 46–107 × 3.3–7.7 μm). However, the conidia of C. chinensis are longer and narrower than C. dispori (60–210 × 3.5–5 µm vs. 46–159 × 3.3–7.7 μm) [9].

The morphological characteristics of key diagnostic features, along with colony morphology, strongly support the identification of our isolates as Cercospora dispori. This identification is further corroborated by the results of multilocus phylogenetic analysis. Although our isolates showed close phylogenetic proximity to C. chinensis, they were clearly distinct from the CBS reference isolate of C. dispori (CBS 132608), which exhibited considerable sequence divergence, particularly in the tef1, actA, and his3 genes. Given the sequence identity among all eight isolates analyzed in this study across five loci, and the morphological conformity to original descriptions, we propose that our isolates represent a reliable and updated reference for the accurate identification of C. dispori. This study not only reinforces the species status of C. dispori as a distinct taxon associated exclusively with Disporum spp. in East Asia but also provides well-validated morphological and molecular data that can serve as a taxonomic and diagnostic benchmark for future studies.
