Resisting Contagious Hopelessness: Therapeutic Alliance and the Limits of Physician‐Assisted Death in Severe and Enduring Anorexia Nervosa
Authors: Elizabeth M. Olson, Fabian M. Saleh, Angela S. Guarda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contemporary psychiatric ethics emphasize autonomy and shared decision‐making, yet these frameworks may be strained in severe mental illnesses characterized by cognitive rigidity, fluctuating decisional capacity, and undue clinician influence. Severe and enduring anorexia nervosa serves as a case example to explore how therapeutic alliance, clinician expectations, and suggestibility may shape expressions of choice in high‐stakes decision‐making, including discussions of physician‐assisted death. Drawing on psychotherapy theory, placebo science, and clinical outcome data, this paper argues that clinician framing and prognostic stance function as treatment variables that may transmit either hope or hopelessness. Ongoing therapeutic engagement focused on improving quality of life and the instillation and preservation of hope should precede any consideration of end‐of‐life interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past decade, psychiatric care has increasingly emphasized patient autonomy, shared decision‐making, and respect for treatment refusal. This shift has advanced patient‐centered care but raises complex questions when applied to severe mental illnesses (SMIs) that alter cognition, values, and future‐oriented judgment. These tensions become especially salient when irreversible or life‐ending interventions are considered, as clinicians must weigh expressed desires against clinical uncertainty, fluctuating capacity, limited access to care, and illness‐driven hopelessness.

Recent proposals to frame severe and enduring anorexia nervosa (SE‐AN) as a “terminal” condition eligible for physician‐assisted death (PAD) have brought these tensions into sharp relief (1). This viewpoint extends this discourse by examining how therapeutic alliance, suggestibility, and contagious hopelessness may complicate autonomy‐centered decision‐making in contemporary psychiatric care.

THERAPEUTIC ALLIANCE AS A TREATMENT TOOL

The therapeutic alliance refers to the influence of a trusting patient–clinician relationship on treatment outcome. Edward Bordin identified three elements: agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and development of a bond (2). He emphasized both the generalizability of the alliance across modalities and the inherent asymmetry of the clinical relationship, in which clinician expertise shapes expectations and direction. Subsequent theorists distinguished early alliance, characterized by the patient's experience of support and credibility, from later collaborative forms marked by shared responsibility and “we‐ness” (3).

Across psychiatric disorders, alliance is among the most consistent predictors of outcome. Stronger early alliance predicts greater symptom reduction, improved adherence, and enhanced functioning in major depressive disorder, schizophrenia spectrum disorders, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, and eating disorders (4, 5). These findings suggest alliance is an active treatment mechanism and not merely a relational backdrop. It shapes engagement, tolerance of distress, openness to cognitive revision, and adherence to medical recommendations. In conditions marked by ambivalence, rigidity, or fluctuating capacity, alliance may determine whether evidence‐based interventions can be implemented at all.

Jerome Frank proposed that therapeutic outcomes are influenced by expectation and suggestion, amplified by clinician credibility and persuasiveness (6). The placebo response is therefore highly relevant to early psychotherapeutic alliance formation. Contemporary placebo research additionally demonstrates that clinician framing and subtle interpersonal cues influence outcomes (7). The therapeutic alliance cannot, however, be reduced to expectancy alone. Beyond anticipation of benefit, it involves the lived experience of being understood and accompanied, which may counteract loneliness and demoralization while shaping future‐oriented judgment.

Relational influence also carries risk. “Nocebo” effects, adverse outcomes shaped by negative expectations or suggestive context, illustrate how clinician framing may unintentionally contribute to harm. The forensic psychiatry literature emphasizes how suggestive interviewing techniques, whether intentional or inadvertent, can systematically bias assessments (e.g., capacity evaluations) by shaping expressed preferences rather than eliciting consistent, illness‐independent values. Repeated questioning, stereotype inducements, guided imagery, peer pressure, selective reinforcement, leading questions, or manipulation of emotional tone can obscure the distinction between relational influence and decisional impairment, undermining self‐determination. These concerns are particularly salient in patients who, by virtue of developmental stage (e.g., children or adolescents), cognitive limitations or illness state (including starvation), may already be especially vulnerable to suggestion (8, 9).

In SMI, clinician stance and expectations function as treatment variables themselves, and awareness of this influence is ethically essential in high‐stakes decision‐making.

INSTILLING HOPE IN PSYCHIATRY

Hope is intrinsic to effective psychiatric treatment and may serve as an antidote to uncertainty and demoralization. In this context, hope is not unfounded optimism but rather, the perceived possibility of achieving a goal (10). It reflects a future orientation that enables patients to imagine alternatives to their current state and to tolerate the discomfort of treatment in service of that possibility. When hopelessness is a symptom, hope must often be actively “held” and projected by the clinician as confidence in the possibility of improvement. Instilling hope helps shape patient expectations and promotes treatment engagement.

Hope and hopelessness operate bidirectionally within the therapeutic relationship. Repeated crises and non‐response may generate demoralization, in both patients and in clinicians. When clinicians relinquish the expectation of improvement, it becomes difficult for patients to sustain it. Clinician commitment to the possibility of recovery helps preserve space for clinical improvement, including in the patient who cannot yet envision change. In this way, hope can be seen as functioning as a clinical variable that can shape both how prognosis is framed and how decisions, including those that may be irreversible for end‐of‐life, are understood.

ANOREXIA NERVOSA AS A CASE EXAMPLE

Therapeutic alliance in anorexia nervosa, as in other psychiatric disorders, correlates with improved outcomes, including weight restoration, reduction in disordered eating behaviors, and cognitive change, particularly among younger patients (11). However, alliance‐building can be challenging because aspects of this behavioral disorder are ego‐syntonic: patients may not view their behaviors as problematic and may overvalue the illness as central to their identity. When seeking help, they may wish to alleviate symptomatic consequences of starvation while remaining reluctant to change eating behaviors essential for recovery. Patient and clinician therefore often hold divergent goals, making medical recommendations feel confrontational. Restriction and compulsive exercise often align with personal values, making behavior change feel threatening. Internalized stigma can further destabilize trust.

In this context, the therapeutic alliance must function as both a platform for change and a buffer against ambivalence, helping patients separate their sense of self from the illness by creating a relational space where recovery becomes thinkable. Ruptures of the alliance through avoidance, non‐adherence, or apparent indifference can provoke frustration in clinicians, risking accommodation to illness‐driven demands or inadvertent collusion with anorectic ideals.

Safety concerns complicate these dynamics. Anorexia nervosa has a mortality rate nearly sixfold that of age‐matched healthy peers due to suicide or to metabolic, renal and cardiopulmonary complications (12, 13). Although weight restoration reverses most effects of starvation, some complications, such as bone density loss or end‐organ damage, may persist. Chronic medical risk heightens clinician urgency. When patients both lack decisional capacity and are medically unstable, coercive interventions including involuntary hospitalization or short‐term nasogastric feeding may be required. Treatment dropout rates remain high relative to other psychiatric disorders, ranging from 20% to 51% in inpatient treatment and 19%–73% in outpatient care (14).

Prognosis adds further ambiguity. In the Massachusetts General Hospital Longitudinal Interval Follow‐Up study, although a majority, 62.8% of individuals with anorexia nervosa, recovered at 22 years, only half this number were recovered at 9 years, highlighting the disorder's frequently protracted time course to recovery (15). Many patients experience multiple relapses or limited response to evidence‐based treatments. Few reliable clinical and demographic factors inform prognosis, and long‐term outcomes remain difficult to predict at the individual level.

At a systemic level, the economic burden of anorexia nervosa is high. A 2024 systematic review demonstrated increasing healthcare utilization across inpatient, outpatient and emergency settings with estimated total healthcare cost in the United States of $12.2 billion USD (16). Shrinking availability of inpatient beds and a systemic shift toward residential over hospital‐based treatment further compromise access and quality of care for the most severe cases (17).

Maintaining hope within this landscape requires that clinicians oppose patient ambivalence about treatment and demoralization about the prospect of recovery. External encouragement may be experienced by the patient as an intrusion in a disorder tied to self‐control and moral worth. Malnutrition exacerbates cognitive rigidity and diminishes the capacity to imagine alternative futures. Under such conditions, a strong alliance may be the clinician's most powerful tool — sustaining potential for change when the patient cannot yet imagine it.

In severe and enduring cases, when intensive treatment or even involuntary specialty care has failed, supportive, palliative or harm‐reduction approaches may become ethically appropriate whilst leaving the door open for complementary curative care. These frameworks balance autonomy, beneficence, and non‐maleficence when cure does not appear immediately achievable, without abandoning hope (18). By shifting goals toward reducing suffering, preserving dignity, improving function and maintaining a relational bridge to future recovery, palliative care approaches should seek to support hope rather than focusing on end of life and death as the inevitable outcome.

Problematically, family members and clinicians themselves are vulnerable to a patient's contagious hopelessness. Repeated care refusals, crises, and emotionally charged interactions can generate burnout. Negative clinician reactions, often grounded in fear, helplessness or frustration, are associated with poorer outcomes (19). Without supervision and support, clinicians may disengage, may adopt pessimistic expectations that reinforce patient hopelessness, or may at the extreme accept or even suggest PAD as a supposed treatment alternative.

CONCLUSION

Discussions of PAD in psychiatric contexts must consider statutory eligibility, formal assessments of decisional capacity, and the relational and clinical processes through which such decisions emerge. Existing frameworks for determining eligibility for PAD, otherwise known as medical aid in dying, appropriately emphasize voluntariness, informed consent, and freedom from external pressure; however, these criteria are more difficult to operationalize in SMIs like longstanding extreme anorexia nervosa, a disorder characterized by fluctuating capacity, entrenched cognitive distortions, and heightened susceptibility to suggestion.

This perspective extends the recent discourse around PAD and SMI by highlighting the role of therapeutic alliance and clinician bias in shaping expressions of choice. In disorders that alter identity and future‐oriented judgment, relational variables are not peripheral to autonomy—they help constitute it. The question is whether PAD requests by patients with SE‐AN can reliably satisfy the foundational requirements of voluntariness and authentic decisional capacity given that clinician stance shapes patient expectations, either reinforcing or counteracting hopelessness, and thereby influencing expressed care preferences. Requests for PAD from individuals with SMI emerging in the context of clinician disengagement, prognostic pessimism, clinician bias toward PAD or contagious demoralization cannot be treated as the expression of stable, illness‐independent patient values. This concern is not hypothetical for SE‐AN: a systematic review of assisted death cases in patients with eating disorders found that the clinical rationales used to affirm eligibility frequently relied on illness duration, treatment resistance, and poor prognosis—precisely the variables most susceptible to clinician framing and systemic failures of care (20).

The absence of reliable individual‐level prognostic markers in SE‐AN further complicates the issue. Treatment futility in chronic anorexia nervosa for example, lacks a clinically, ethically and legally defensible definition sufficient to support irreversible end‐of‐life decisions, a limitation that persists even with careful capacity assessment (21). Major medical organizations, including the American Medical Association and the American College of Physicians, have opposed PAD in psychiatric illness, reflecting similar concerns about decisional integrity and prognostic uncertainty (22, 23).

This viewpoint does not argue against PAD in all contexts, or dismiss the reality of profound and refractory suffering in SE‐AN. Rather, SE‐AN illustrates how specific disease features in SMI can render satisfying PAD eligibility criteria in any clinically or ethically rigorous sense highly subjective. For SE‐AN, malnutrition‐driven cognitive impairment, ego‐syntonic distortion of future‐oriented judgment, and the demonstrated susceptibility of expressed preferences to relational clinician influence impact the ability to reliably determine eligibility for PAD. Current safeguards assess voluntariness and capacity cross‐sectionally as static, and fail to account for the relational and systemic conditions that can influence requests for PAD.

Ongoing therapeutic engagement, access to disorder‐specific expertise, and sustained commitment to preserving hope and improving quality of life should complement existing legal safeguards and preclude consideration of PAD in the presence of SMI.
