The Impact of a Visualized Steerable Sheath on Radiofrequency Catheter Ablation for First‐Time Atrial Fibrillation Ablation: A Prospective, Randomized, Single‐Blind Study
Authors: Chun‐Kai Chen, Chih‐Chieh Yu, Min‐Tsun Liao, Hui‐Chun Huang, Yen‐Bin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA 3D electroanatomical mapping (3D‐EAM) visualized steerable sheath was designed to improve catheter placement and reduce radiation exposure during ablation, an established treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF). However, prospective randomized data comparing the procedural and clinical outcomes of visualized and non‐visualized steerable sheaths are limited.AimsTo compare the impact of 3D‐EAM visualized and nonvisualized steerable sheaths on procedural and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing AF ablation.MethodsThis prospective randomized study included 100 patients who underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation for AF. Patients were randomized (1:1) to either a 3D‐EAM‐visualized steerable sheath (Vizigo) or a conventional non‐visualized steerable sheath (Agilis) group. Key endpoints included procedure and fluoroscopy times, pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) effectiveness, and 12‐month AF recurrence.ResultsAcute PVI was successfully achieved in all patients. Fluoroscopy time was significantly lower in the Vizigo group than that in the Agilis group (11.43 vs. 15.37 min, p = 0.0013), with a zero‐fluoroscopy workflow achievable post‐transseptal puncture. First‐pass PVI success in left‐sided veins was significantly higher in the Vizigo group than that in the Agilis group (84% vs. 64%, p = 0.023). At the 12‐month follow‐up, AF‐free survival was favored in the Vizigo group (91.8% vs. 83.3%; p = 0.203).ConclusionsUsing a 3D‐EAM visualized steerable sheath significantly reduced fluoroscopy time without prolonging the AF ablation procedure, making a zero‐fluoroscopy ablation strategy feasible after transseptal puncture. Furthermore, the improved left‐sided first‐pass PVI success observed with the Vizigo sheath may have implications for procedural efficacy, although its effect on long‐term outcomes warrants further investigation with larger cohorts.

Introduction

The contact force exerted by the catheter on the cardiac tissue is a critical determinant of lesion quality and efficacy of radiofrequency (RF) catheter ablation [1]. Previous studies have reported that steerable sheaths improve catheter stability and average contact force compared with fixed‐curve sheaths, enhancing ablation precision [2, 3, 4]. However, their impact on procedural complications and long‐term atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence remains uncertain [5].

Additionally, a significant limitation of the current catheter ablation techniques is the reliance on fluoroscopy to assess the relationship between the ablation catheter and the steerable sheath, contributing to increased radiation exposure. The recently developed Vizigo steerable sheath, equipped with electrodes that are compatible with electroanatomical mapping (EAM) systems, offers a novel approach for addressing these challenges [6]. Despite the potential benefits of this visualized sheath, its impact on procedure time, fluoroscopy usage, and long‐term AF ablation outcomes has not been fully evaluated in a randomized clinical trial. Therefore, we aimed to conduct a prospective, randomized comparison of the Vizigo visualized steerable sheath and the conventional Agilis steerable sheath to assess their effects on fluoroscopy time, procedural efficiency, and AF ablation outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Study Population

This single‐center, prospective, randomized, single‐blind study was conducted between October 2021 and December 2022 and included consecutive patients who underwent their first radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) for AF enrolled at National Taiwan University Hospital and its Hsinchu Branch. Patients aged 20–100 years, who met Class I indications for catheter ablation for paroxysmal or persistent AF, were considered eligible for the study. The exclusion criteria were an inability to provide informed consent, unwillingness to adhere to follow‐up visits, concurrent enrollment in conflicting studies, or a medical condition likely to result in death within 6 months.

Patients were blinded to the randomized sheath assignment to prevent bias in the self‐reported outcomes, whereas the operating physicians were aware of the sheath type. The long‐term clinical follow‐up and ECG analyses were performed by independent investigators blinded to the group allocation. Randomization was performed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) in a 1:1 ratio, with patients assigned to either the Vizigo or Agilis group. The allocation sequence was concealed until the assignment to ensure blinding of the randomization process. The study data were collected and managed using the REDCap electronic data capture tool hosted at the National Taiwan University Hospital [7, 8]. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital, conforming to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (Central Illustration illustration 1, 1).

In this prospective, randomized, single‐blind trial, 100 patients undergoing first‐time radiofrequency catheter ablaiton for AF were assigned to a 3D‐EAM visualized steerable sheath group (Vizigo; n = 50) or a conventional nonvisualized steerable sheath group (Agilis; n = 50). Use of the visualized sheath reduced fluoroscopy time (11.43 vs 15.37 minutes; p = 0.0013), enabled zero fluoroscopy after transseptal puncture, and inceased the left‐side first‐pass PVI rate (84% vs. 64%; p = 0.023), without prolonging the procedure time. Twelve‐month AF‐free survial was 91.8% versus 83.3% (p = 0.203). AF, atrial fibrillation; EAM, electroanatomical mapping; PVI, pulmonary vein isolation.

Procedures

Preprocedural imaging, including electrocardiogram‐gated computed tomography and transesophageal echocardiography, was performed to evaluate the left atrial (LA) and pulmonary vein (PV) anatomy and to exclude thrombi. The ablation procedures were conducted under conscious sedation with propofol using the CARTO 3 system for 3D mapping. Vascular access was obtained via the five femoral veins and one femoral artery. A 7‐French decapolar catheter was placed in the coronary sinus under fluoroscopic guidance. In the Vizigo group, a contact force‐sensing SmartTouch SF (STSF) catheter was used to create a visualization matrix of the right atrial (RA) septum. An aortogram was created for transseptal puncture guidance according to the operator's preference. A standard double transseptal puncture was performed using a BRK transseptal needle. The first transseptal sheath was an 8 French 63 cm SL0 sheath (Swartz SL0 Transseptal Guiding Introducer; Abbott Laboratories). The second transseptal sheath was either a Vizigo or an Agilis steerable sheath. The mapping catheters used were mainly Lasso mapping catheters. The target‐activated clotting time was 300−350 s throughout the procedure. If the patient had AF, direct‐current cardioversion was performed either before or after the PV isolation procedure based on the operator's judgment.

LA geometry was created using a mapping and ablation catheter. The PV isolation procedure was guided by the VISITAG SURPOINT module with (1) a tag size of 6 mm and > 15% overlap between each tag (i.e., tag distance < 5 mm); (2) a target value of the ablation index of 400 for the posterior wall and 450–500 for the anterior wall of the LA; (3]) a contact force of 5–30 g; and (4) the RF power setting of 40 watts. Both PVs were sequentially targeted. After obtaining the entry blocks, the exit blocks were checked and confirmed. Either adenosine (6 mg/40 kg) injection or isoproterenol challenge (up to 20 μg/min) was used to assess dormant conduction within the PVs. The decision to perform the rapid atrial pacing to test for the inducibility of atrial arrhythmia was made at the discretion of the operator. If typical clinical flutter was documented or typical atrial flutter was induced during the above process, cavotricuspid isthmus isolation was performed. If atypical atrial flutter was induced, 3D mapping was performed, and the index isthmus was ablated. If sustained AF was induced, the necessity of substrate modification was evaluated and implemented.

Procedure Times and Radiation Exposure Analysis

The following procedural time and fluoroscopy data were collected (1) time from the completion of CS placement to the completion of transseptal puncture; (2) LA mapping time from the start of LA mapping to completion; (3) total PVI (pulmonary vein isolation) time: from the start of the first ablation to the completion and validation of PV isolation; (4) skin to PVI time: from vascular puncture to completion of ablation and validation of PV isolation; and (5) total ablation procedure time: from CS placement completion to conclusion of the procedure, including extra‐PV ablation. During each interval, the fluoroscopy time and absorbed radiation dose (dose‐area product, DAP) were recorded.

Contact Force Analysis

CARTONET, Version 14, is a software solution that allows physicians to manage patient CARTO 3 System case information and conduct research. CARTONET maintains patient privacy and security by removing the patient's direct identifiers when sending the data. CARTONET Ablation Analysis allows us to perform retrospective analysis on CARTO 3 System studies. Stability, contact force, and impedance drop were analyzed in each case. An ablation site is composed of N ablation points with (xi, yi, zi) position, i = 1, N, where (xo, yo, zo) are defined as the center of (xi, yi, zi). Stability is defined as the 90th percentile of the Euclidean distances of each one of the points to the center of all points, where di represents the Euclidean distance of ablation point i. Stability was represented in a mini‐meter, where the smaller value indicates a more stable ablation point.

Study End Points and Clinical Follow‐Up

The primary endpoint was the difference in total fluoroscopy time and total fluoroscopy dose between the groups. A pre‐specified step‐by‐step analysis of procedural time and fluoroscopy use for each phase of the procedure (transseptal puncture, LA mapping, and PVI ablation/validation) was also performed. The secondary endpoints included (1) the average contact force of each ablation, (2) first‐pass PVI rate, (3) periprocedural complications (death, stroke, pericardial tamponade, phrenic nerve paralysis, and atrioesophageal fistula), and (4) AF‐free survival at the 12‐month follow‐up.

Periprocedural complications will be registered in medical records. Twenty‐four‐hour Holter ECGs at 3 and 12 months, serial cardiac implantable electronic device interrogation every 3 months, and serial ECG follow‐ups every 3 months were obtained at the outpatient clinic. The blanking period was defined as the first 3 months after ablation. Repeat ablations for symptomatic drug‐refractory recurrence of AF or atrial tachycardia were scheduled after a minimum follow‐up of 3 months. Antiarrhythmic medications were discontinued whenever feasible after ablation, and patients were allowed to receive β‐blockers. In cases of symptomatic arrhythmia recurrence or high risk of recurrence, medication adjustments were allowed at the investigator's discretion. Anticoagulation therapy was discontinued after at least 2 months of follow‐up based on the CHA2DS2‐VASc score.

Statistical Analysis

The t‐test was used to calculate the sample size required to detect a reduction in radiation exposure. Controlling for baseline values with 80% power and a type I error of 0.05, the sample size required to detect a medium‐strength effect size (0.6) between the two groups was 45 participants per group. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the target enrollment was set at 50 participants per group [9].

Analyses of the primary and secondary endpoints were conducted based on the intention‐to‐treat principle. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non‐normally distributed ones are reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Logarithmic transformations were applied to approximate normality for the statistical analyses of non‐normally distributed variables.

Statistical significance between groups was assessed using the Student's t‐test, Fisher's exact test, or chi‐squared test, depending on the nature of the variables. Cox proportional hazards modeling was used to calculate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for time‐to‐event analyses. The incidence of AF‐free survival over time was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/BE 17.0 for Windows (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

In total, 100 consecutive patients were enrolled between October 2021 and December 2022. Of them, 50 were randomly assigned to the Vizigo group (3D‐EAM visualized steerable sheath) and 50 to the Agilis group (conventional steerable sheath). During the 12‐month follow‐up, one patient in the Vizigo group and two patients in the Agilis group were lost to follow‐up, resulting in dropout rates of 2% and 4%, respectively (Figure illustration 1, 1). The baseline characteristics of the two groups are presented in Table 1. The mean patient age was 61.45 ± 11.44 years, and 72% of the patients were men. Most patients had paroxysmal AF (62% in the Vizigo group vs. 68% in the Agilis group), while 34% patients had persistent AF. The average CHA2DS2‐VASc scores were comparable between the groups (1.92 ± 1.12 vs. 1.84 ± 1.40, p = 0.7537). The average LA size was approximately 40 mm (39.59 ± 8.82 vs. 38.50 ± 10.60 mm, p = 0.614). In our patient population, 78% patients in the Vizigo group and 84% in the Agilis group received long‐term NOAC (Novel oral anti‐coagulants) treatment prior to catheter ablation (Table 1). Acute PVI was successfully achieved in all patients. Additionally, 24% of patients in the Vizigo group and 16% in the Agilis group underwent extra‐PV LA ablations. Meanwhile, 42% and 40% patients in the Vizigo and Agilis groups, respectively, underwent additional RA ablations (Table 2).

Primary Endpoint Analysis

The total ablation fluoroscopy time was significantly reduced in the Vizigo group (9.12 ± 4.34 vs. 12.67 ± 6.34 min, p = 0.0018). Additionally, the total DAP tended to be lower in the Vizigo group than that in the Agilis group (21.7 vs. 26.8 Gy·cm2), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.33).

Step‐by‐Step Procedural and Fluoroscopy Characteristics

The total ablation procedural duration was similar between groups (168.97 ± 43.53 vs. 164.95 ± 43.75 min; p = 0.78). A prespecified step‐by‐step analysis of the procedural phases is presented in Table 3. The time required for transseptal puncture was longer in the Vizigo group than that in the Agilis group (26.40 ± 9.02 vs. 20.78 ± 8.18 min; p = 0.0016), reflecting the additional creation of an RA electroanatomical map for guiding the Vizigo sheath, while the fluoroscopy time during transseptal puncture itself was comparable between groups. LA mapping time, total PVI time, and overall skin‐to‐PVI procedural time did not differ significantly between the two groups (Vizigo vs. Agilis group: 15.88 ± 7.26 vs. 16.20 ± 7.44 min for LA mapping; 85.67 ± 34.17 vs. 85.22 ± 26.70 min for total PVI time; 162.14 ± 34.99 vs. 160.30 ± 29.77 min for skin‐to‐PVI time; p = 0.83, 0.94, and 0.78, respectively). Notably, fluoroscopy time and dose during LA mapping and PVI ablation/validation were reduced to zero in the Vizigo group (zero‐fluoroscopy workflow), while the skin‐to‐PVI fluoroscopy time was significantly shortened (11.43 ± 4.32 vs. 15.37 ± 7.00 min; p = 0.0013) (Table 4).

Secondary Endpoint Analysis‐Contact Force, First‐Pass Isolation Rate of PV, and Complications

A significant finding of this study was the higher left‐sided vein (PV) first‐pass isolation rate observed in the Vizigo group than that in the Agilis group (84% vs. 64%, p = 0.023) (Figure 2). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the right‐sided PV first‐pass isolation rate between groups (80% vs. 76%, p = 0.315). Additionally, the contact force measurements showed no differences between the groups, nor did the stability or impedance decrease at the ablation points. Further analysis of contact force at different segments of the left‐sided PV during ablation revealed a trend toward higher forces at the left posterior and inferior segments, the roof, and the ridge, although these differences were not statistically significant. Among the 100 ablation procedures, only one major complication occurred in the Agilis group. A cardiac tamponade event occurred in one patient during cavotricuspid isthmus isolation, caused by a steam pop event that led to hypotension. The patient recovered smoothly after pericardiocentesis. No other major complications such as shock, vascular puncture issues, stroke, or respiratory failure were observed (Table 2).

In the intention‐to‐treat analysis, AF recurrence events occurred in four patients in the Vizigo group and eight patients in the Agilis group during the 12‐month follow‐up, resulting in a 12‐month AF‐free survival rate of 91.8% versus 83.3%, respectively (log‐rank p = 0.203). Discussion

Our study represents the first prospective randomized trial to compare the use of an EAM‐visualized steerable sheath (Vizigo) with that of a conventional steerable sheath (Agilis) in the first‐time AF ablation with comprehensive 12‐month clinical follow‐up. The principal findings were as follows: (1) the use of a visualized sheath significantly reduced fluoroscopy time without compromising the total procedural time or safety; (2) a near‐zero fluoroscopy workflow was consistently achievable after transseptal puncture; and (3) left‐sided first‐pass PVI rates were higher in the visualized sheath group, which might contribute to improved outcomes in AF recurrence.

The most consistent procedural benefit observed in this study was a reduction in fluoroscopic exposure. Although previous studies have suggested that steerable sheath visualization may decrease radiation use [10, 11], our findings extend this observation by demonstrating that fluoroscopy during LA mapping and PVI can be effectively eliminated by using an EAM‐visualized sheath. Practically, this supports a workflow in which fluoroscopy is confined to the transseptal phase, after which mapping and ablation can be performed without radiation exposure. Given the cumulative radiation exposure associated with electrophysiological procedures, this approach may have meaningful implications for patient safety and occupational health [12, 13]. Notably, this finding aligns with emerging data suggesting that zero‐ or near‐zero fluoroscopy AF ablation is feasible across a range of operator experience levels. A recent study demonstrated that even early operators in their adoption of non‐fluoroscopic workflows can achieve successful outcomes when supported by visualization technologies [14]. Thus, by providing real‐time visualization of the sheath orientation and its relationship with the ablation catheter, visualized sheaths may reduce reliance on indirect fluoroscopic cues and facilitate more controlled catheter manipulation. In addition, broader meta‐analytic data indicate that zero‐ or minimal‐fluoroscopy approaches do not compromise the procedural success or safety [15] [16]. Consequently, within this context, the present study provides randomized evidence supporting the procedural feasibility of such workflows in routine clinical practice.

Furthermore, we observed a higher first‐pass isolation rate for the left PVs in the visualized sheath group than that in the conventional steerable sheath group. First‐pass isolation is associated with improved lesion durability and reduced PV reconnection [17]. Additionally, epicardial connections involving the PVs can lead to failure of first‐pass PV isolation and reduced success in the acute and chronic outcomes of AF ablation [18]. Approximately 53.1% of epicardial connections were found in the left PV. While the contact force, stability, and impedance drop were comparable between the groups, we observed a trend toward larger contact forces at several segments of the left PV, including the left PV ridge, which may increase the likelihood of successful ablation of the epicardial connections between the left PV and LA. Thus, the improved first‐pass success may reflect enhanced catheter–sheath coordination and a more precise spatial orientation within the left atrium. In particular, regions such as the LA ridge and posterior wall, where catheter stability can be challenging, may benefit from real‐time visualization of the sheath–catheter relationship. Consequently, these findings suggest that the procedural advantages of sheath visualization extend beyond radiation reduction to lesion delivery.

At the 12‐month follow‐up, AF‐free survival was higher in the Vizigo group than that in the Agilis group, although the difference was not statistically significant (91.8% vs. 83.3%, log‐rank p = 0.203). As the study was not powered to detect differences in clinical outcomes, the numerically higher freedom from AF in the visualized sheath group should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis‐generating. Subsequent larger studies are required to determine whether improvements in procedural efficiency and lesion delivery translate into long‐term clinical benefits.

An apparent discrepancy in our findings was the significant reduction in fluoroscopy time without a corresponding statistically significant reduction in DAP. This may be explained by the disproportionate contribution of the transseptal phase to the total radiation dose, during which the fluoroscopy time was similar between groups. The bulk of the DAP was generated during transseptal puncture and initial catheter placement, which utilized standard fluoroscopic guidance in both groups. In addition, DAP is influenced by multiple factors, including acquisition settings, cine imaging, and beam geometry, which are not directly proportional to the fluoroscopy duration. Procedural steps such as aortography and cine acquisition may also contribute to the radiation dose, independent of fluoroscopy time. Moreover, this study may have been underpowered to detect modest differences in DAP. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted in the context of procedural strategies, in which fluoroscopy was still routinely used during transseptal puncture and initial catheter positioning in both groups.

Compared to previous reports [11], the overall procedure times in our study were longer, likely reflecting the use of ablation index‐guided lesion delivery (35–40 W), comprehensive PVI validation, and a relatively high rate of additional extra‐PV substrate modification. Despite this, the use of a visualized sheath did not prolong the overall procedural duration compared to the use of a conventional steerable sheath, suggesting that the additional mapping steps required for sheath visualization are offset by improved workflow efficiency during LA ablation.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. First, this was a single‐blind study in which patients were blinded to the treatment allocation, whereas operators were not blinded because of the nature of the intervention. However, outcome assessment relies primarily on objective rhythm monitoring, which reduces the risk of assessment bias. Second, the follow‐up period was limited to 12 months, and long‐term outcomes were not evaluated. Our study was not powered to detect differences in clinical endpoints such as arrhythmia recurrence.

Conclusions

Our prospective randomized study provides further evidence that the use of an EAM‐visualized steerable sheath significantly reduces fluoroscopy time and enables a near‐zero fluoroscopy workflow after transseptal puncture without increasing the procedural duration in patients undergoing first‐time AF ablation. The observed improvement in left‐sided first‐pass PVI suggests a potential procedural advantage; however, its impact on long‐term outcomes requires further investigation.

Perspectives

Clinical Competencies

The use of a 3D‐EAM visualized steerable sheath in AF ablation significantly reduces fluoroscopy time and improves first‐pass pulmonary vein isolation for left‐sided veins. This approach facilitates a more efficient procedural workflow and enables the feasibility of near‐zero or zero‐fluoroscopy ablation strategies in clinical practice.

Translational Outlook

Larger studies are needed to determine whether the observed procedural advantages translate into improved long‐term arrhythmia outcomes. Further investigation is warranted to evaluate the role of visualized steerable sheaths in emerging technologies, including pulse field ablation, particularly with respect to optimizing workflow and minimizing radiation exposure.
