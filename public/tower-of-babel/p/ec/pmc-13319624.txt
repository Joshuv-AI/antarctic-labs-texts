Thermal conductivity and viscosity characterization of soybean oil nanofluids dispersed with graphene nanoplatelets, MoS2, TiO2 and Al2O3 nanoparticles for MQL machining
Authors: Anthony Chukwujekwu Okafor, Saidanvar Esanjonovich Valiev, Tobechukwu Kingsley Abor, Ignatius Echezona Ekengwu, Abiodun Saka, Monday U. Okoronkwo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents comparative evaluation of thermal conductivity (TC) and dynamic viscosity enhancement of high-oleic soybean oils (HOSO) by dispersing five nanoparticles—two graphene nanoplatelets (xGnP, GnP), MoS2, TiO2, and Al2O3—to formulate high performance nanofluids for minimum quantity lubrication (MQL) machining. TC was measured for 1–7 wt.% nanoparticle concentrations over 25–75 °C (10 °C increments) using a Transient Hot Wire meter, and viscosity was measured for 1–4 wt.% at 25, 45, and 75 °C using a ViscoQC 300 rotational viscometer. Ultrasonic dispersion ensured uniform nanoparticle distribution. Results show that TC decreases linearly with temperature but increases nonlinearly with nanoparticle wt.% concentration. Viscosity decreases exponentially with increasing shear rate and temperature but rises nonlinearly with nanoparticle wt.% concentration. The highest TC enhancement at 7 wt.% occurred with xGnP/HOSO nanofluids (~ 214% at 25 °C, ~ 196% at 75 °C) followed by GnP/HOSO (~ 37%, ~ 21%), MoS2/HOSO (~ 16%, ~ 9%), TiO2/HOSO (~ 12%, 10%), and Al2O3/HOSO (~ 12%, ~ 7%). xGnP/HOSO achieved TC values of 0.53 W m−1 K−1 (25 °C) and 0.47 W m−1 K−1 (75 °C), approaching conventional emulsion coolants (CEC) levels [0.62, 0.66 W m−1 K−1]. At 4 wt.%, xGnP/HOSO showed the highest viscosity enhancement (622% at 25 °C, 784% at 75 °C), followed by TiO2/HOSO (18.6%; 29.4%), MoS2/HOSO (12.3, 9.9%), GnP/HOSO (11.6, 5.9%), Al2O3/HOSO (8.8, 3.7%). Nanofluid formulation showed that xGnP concentrations above 5 wt.% reduce flowability. xGnP showed superior enhancement of TC and viscosity compared to MoS2 and metal oxides. xGnP/HOSO at 5 wt.% are recommended as sustainable CEC substitute for nanofluid-based MQL machining.

Introduction

In manufacturing industry, problems associated with harmful petroleum oil-based conventional emulsion cooling (CEC) in machining led to the investigation of environmentally friendly vegetable oil base coolants as possible replacement. Environmentally toxic CEC causes not only skin diseases, irritation and health issues but also leads to generation of aerosols at high cutting speeds [1], that proved to expose metals to severe corrosion [2, 3]. Therefore, vegetable oil properties are being enhanced for use in machining applications, which require high thermal conductivity (TC), viscosity and good lubricating properties [4]. The principal authors past work show that heat and friction generated in the cutting zone are predominantly controlled by cutting fluid viscosity and TC, which are affected by temperature. Viscosity of all vegetable oils decreases exponentially with an increase in temperature and is significantly higher than that of CEC, but CEC has higher TC than all vegetables oils [5], therefore CEC is still used as a coolant in machining. Base fluids like vegetable oils impregnated with nanoparticles to increase their TC and viscosity are called nanofluids [6]. Nanofluids are sprayed in mist form to the cutting zone using minimum quantity lubrication (MQL) method. Vegetable oil based nanofluids for MQL machining are environmentally friendly and biodegradable substitute for CEC [7]. Previous investigations have demonstrated that dispersing nanoparticles into vegetable oils improves their cooling and lubrication performance in machining operations.

Several researchers have investigated nanofluid MQL using various nanoparticles and vegetable oils. Al2O3—soybean oil-base nanofluid with nanoparticle wt.% concentration of 0.5, 1 and 1.5 was used in MQL milling of 60Si2Mn. It was reported that cutting forces and surface roughness decreases, and tool life increases with increase of nanoparticle wt.% concentration.[8]. Graphene nanoparticles were dispersed into vegetable oil cutting fluid LB2000 at only 0.1 wt.% concentration for milling of titanium alloy TC4, where it was reported that milling forces were reduced by 37.38%, milling temperature by 29.91%, tool wear by 31.02%, surface roughness by 45.27% compared to dry milling [9].Taguchi method was used to identify optimal parameters for cutting titanium alloy TC4 with graphene—LB2000 vegetable oil based nanofluid with 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2 wt.% concentration of nanoparticle that improved milling forces, milling temperature, surface micro-hardness and surface roughness by 18.13, 13.59, 8.36, and 24.82% respectively. It was reported that optimum parameters are 0.1 wt.% concentration of nanoparticles, 60 mL/h flow rate, and 0.6 MPa gas pressure [10]. Drilling performance of AISI 321 stainless steel was evaluated using graphene impregnated vegetable oil-based cutting fluid at 1.5 wt.% concentration of nanoparticles, where reduction of 27.4, 64.9, 33.8 and 51.7% was reported for thrust force, torque, surface roughness and coefficient of friction respectively [11]. Performance of graphene dispersed at 1.5 wt.% concentration in canola oil-based nanofluid was investigated on Ti-6Al-4V-ELI’s surface grinding, and it was reported that in surface roughness, grinding energy, grinding force, and coefficient of friction were reduced by 16.9, 33.83, 22.1 and 15.1% respectively compared to conventional flood coolant [12]. Comparative evaluation of high oleic soybean oil (HOSO)–based MQL flow rates at 10, 30, 50, 70, and 90 mL/h with a mineral oil–based emulsion flood coolant as a benchmark in high-speed face milling of Inconel 718 using AlTiN/TiN-coated carbide inserts was conducted [13]. It was reported that MQL oil flow rate at 70 mL/h gave the longest tool life comparable to that of mineral oil–based emulsion flood cooling, while 10 mL/h flow rate gave the shortest tool life. Use of conventional MQL (distilled water) and nanofluid based MQL (NF-MQL − 0.2wt.% Cu dispersed in distilled water) for face milling of Inconel 718 [14] revealed that NF-MQL is a better alternative which resulted in 20.1, 14.7 and 13.3% percentage reduction in surface roughness, temperature, and power, respectively. Aluminum Oxide (Al2O3) nanoparticles selected as nano-additives at 0, 2, and 4 wt. % concentration showed reduction in the tool chip contact length and seizure effect [15]. It was reported that TiO2—graphene/rice bran oil hybrid nanofluid reduced tool wear and surface roughness by 38 and 11% respectively compared to TiO2/bran oil based nanofluid, but the individual effect of graphene nanofluid was not reported to ascertain the synergistic effect of the hybrid nanofluid [16]. Cotton seed oil-based fluid impregnated with Al2O3 nanoparticles was used in nanofluid MQL machining of grade 45 steel, better tribological properties than pure cotton seed oil was reported. In the experiment, it was reported that nanoparticles filled the workpiece surface cavities and repaired dents on friction surfaces, thus reducing friction and wear. Polar atoms of the oil easily bonded with nanoparticles creating good lubrication effect [17]. MoS2 nanoparticles were dispersed in soybean vegetable oil for MQL grinding of Grade 45 steel. It was reported that the optimal concentration of MoS2 in the base fluid was 6% that showed the best tribological performance [18]. It was reported that at 1 wt.% concentration of Al2O3 nanoparticles, TC showed the highest enhancement of 6.6, 8.9 and 12.8 for 60:40, 50:50, and 40:60 water-ethylene glycol ratios of the nanofluids [19]. TiO2 nanoparticles of 11nm size were dispersed in aqueous solution over 0.00125–0.1 wt.% concentrations, where 2.5 g PVA polymer was used to modify the surface of TiO2 then the modified TiO2 added into the aqueous solution. It was reported that thermal conductivity of the TiO2-PVA/water nanofluid was enhanced by 6.1% at 0.05 wt.% concentration of the nanoparticle at 40 °C and viscosity was enhanced by 4%. [20]. Reviewed past studies also reveal that vegetable oils dispersed with nanoparticles represent promising alternatives to conventional petroleum oil-based, water-, and ethylene–glycol-based cutting fluids for machining applications. HOSO with higher oleic fatty acid composition showed the most significant viscosity enhancement and oxidation stability and was recommended as alternative to CEC based-cutting fluid for MQL machining. In the authors’ previous work [21], the thermal conductivity (TC) of six high oleic soybean oil (HOSO)- and four high oleic canola oil (HOCO)-based hybrid nanofluids formulated with four types of nanoparticles (Graphene nanoplatelet (xGnP), TiO2, MoS2, and Al2O3 were investigated, where two different combinations of nanoparticles selected from the four nanoparticles at nanoparticle concentrations of 1–7 wt.% were dispersed into the HOSO- and HOCO-base fluids.TC conductivity of the HOSO and HOCO base-fluids were also evaluated for enhancement of TC. Among these 10 formulated hybrid nanofluids, xGnP–TiO2 hybrid increased thermal conductivity of high-oleic soybean oil (HOSO) by 101.36% and 97.52% at 25 °C and 75 °C, respectively. However, in that study, only thermal conductivity of hybrid nanofluids was investigated, while thermal conductivity and viscosity of mono-nanofluids were not examined except for xGnP/HOSO nanofluids which at 25 °C shows TC enhancement for wt.% concentrations at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 are 25, 54, 75, 106, 171, 191, and 214% respectively, while at 75 °C gave an enhancement of 22, 52, 74%, 104, 172, 177, and 196%. No synergistic relationship between graphene nanoplatelet and TiO2, MoS2, and Al2O3 was observed.

An extensive review of the literature indicates that the selection of nanoparticles for nanofluid formulation has been limited and often conducted randomly in machining studies without prior characterization of their effects in the resulting nanofluids. A comparison with previously reported nanofluid studies further highlights the scope and contribution of the present work. A comprehensive review of nanofluid investigations for machining applications [22] has shown that the majority of nanofluid investigations are water and ethylene glycol based, where thermal conductivity enhancement increased significantly with nanoparticle wt.% concentration. However, water and ethylene glycol possess low viscosity, resulting in reduced lubricity and increased susceptibility of machined components to corrosion. For vegetable oil–based hybrid nanofluids, it was reported that Al2O3–TiO2 nanoparticles dispersed at 0.2–0.6 wt.% resulted in a maximum thermal conductivity enhancement of 27.5% at 0.6 wt.% in palm oil [23]. Furthermore, nanoparticle concentrations up to 4 wt.% in HOSO-based nanofluids produced a maximum thermal conductivity enhancement of 55% (MoS2 at 4 wt.% and 70 °C) and viscosity enhancement of 11.5% (TiO2 at 3.5 wt.% and 50 °C) [24]. In contrast, the present study extends nanoparticle loading for thermal conductivity evaluation up to 7 wt.% (in 1% increments). In addition, viscosity is systematically characterized as a function of shear rate, temperature, and nanoparticle wt.% concentration. Unlike previous investigations which focused mostly on water and ethylene glycol based nanofluids and low concentrations ≤ 4 wt.%, the present work evaluates a broader nanoparticle concentration range up to 7 wt.% and provides combined comprehensive characterization of both thermal conductivity and viscosity of formulated nanofluids. This paper, for the first time presents comparative evaluation of thermal conductivity (TC) and dynamic viscosity of five mono-nanofluids formulated using high-oleic soybean oil (HOSO) as base fluids dispersed with five nanoparticles—two commercial graphene nanoplatelets—GnP (UK version) and xGnP (US version), MoS2, and two metal oxides (Al2O3 and TiO2). TC of the formulated nanofluids is investigated at nanoparticle weight percent (wt.%) concentration from 1 to 7% in 1% increment, at temperature range from 25 to 75 °C (10 °C increments). Shear dependent viscosity mapping of the nanofluids is performed at 1, 2, 3, and 4 wt.% concentrations of the nanoparticles at temperature 25, 45, and 75 °C for MQL machining of difficult-to-cut metals. The study establishes practical concentration limits for MQL applications based on combined thermal, viscosity, and flowability considerations.

The experiment is based on thermophysical property (TC and viscosity) characterization and will be a solid foundation for the selection of optimal nanofluid for MQL machining, especially, machining of difficult-to-cut metals such as Inconel 718 and Compacted Graphite Iron (CGI) used in aerospace and automotive industries.

Theoretical background

Thermal conductivity

The two commonly used methods for thermal conductivity measurement are laser flash and transient hot wire methods that use laser beam and current, respectively. Transient hot wire method was used to measure thermal conductivity of the engineered nanofluids. The method is based on ASTM D7896-19 standard, where a hot wire heats the liquid and the temperature change over time is measured. The slope of TC is defined from temperature change over common logarithm of time. A linear trend is fitted in the linear portion of the curve that defines the slope of TC as shown in Fig. 1. Nonlinear curve appearing at the beginning of TC plot is not considered, since the hot wire first heats itself then heats the sample that causes nonlinearity at the beginning of the test. As linear trend follows, the slope of temperature curve is measured for only 1 s. Viscosity

In nanofluid MQL, dynamic viscosity is a crucial thermophysical property influenced by nanoparticle type, size, shape, concentration, temperature, and shear rate, all of which affect the flow behavior, stability, and tribological performance of the nanofluid. The dynamic viscosity of nanofluids can be measured using rheometers and viscometers, which operate on different principles. A rheometer determines viscosity by measuring the relationship between shear stress and shear rate, where the slope of shear stress vs shear rate curve represents viscosity. Among rheometers, the rotational type of concentric cylinder or cone-plate geometries are used in characterizing Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids. A cone-plate geometry was chosen over a Couette (concentric cylinder) configuration. The major advantage of the cone-plate geometry is that the shear rate is constant over the sample gap, meaning that non-Newtonian fluids do not require correction factors [25]. This is especially important for vegetable oils, which may show mild non-Newtonian behavior at lower temperatures and low shear rates [26]. The cone-plate CP40 spindle requires only 0.5 mL of sample per measurement, which is advantageous given the considerable number of fluids (10) and temperature steps (6) involved in this study. The cone-plate geometry is also easier to clean between tests than the Couette configuration [25]. While an enclosed Couette design has advantages for highly volatile samples or very low-viscosity samples where evaporation could be a problem, the CP40 spindle design is well-suited for the viscosities of the studied nanofluids here. The use of cone-plate geometry for vegetable oil viscosity measurement is consistent with existing literature [26].

In contrast, a viscometer applies a predefined torque or rotational speed and directly measures the fluid's resistance, enabling viscosity determination at discrete shear rates. In this study, a cone-and-plate rotational viscometer is used, that operates on the Searle principle, in which a rotating cone-plate shears the fluid over a stationary flat-plate. This setup provides a uniform shear rate and is ideal for low-viscosity fluids. However, in very low-viscosity fluids, high rotational speeds can cause turbulent flow due to centrifugal and inertial effects, which may distort the measurement if not well controlled. Therefore, different cone plate systems are employed for different viscosity level liquids. For low-viscosity liquids like water and vegetable oils, cone plate spindle CP40 is used. Experimental procedure

Materials and method

High Oleic Soybean Oil (HOSO) was selected as base-fluid in MQL for further enhancement of their thermophysical properties (TC and viscosity) by dispersing five nanoparticles (two Graphene nanoplatelets (xGnP, GnP), MoS2, and two metal oxides (TiO2, and Al2O3)) into the base fluid (HOSO) to formulate nanofluids with enhance cooling and lubrication to substitute CEC that is environmentally unfriendly and not biodegradable. The formulated nanofluids are characterized to determine the enhancement of the thermophysical properties of the HOSO base fluid. Because the authors’ earlier investigation demonstrated that high-oleic soybean oil exhibit higher thermal conductivity and viscosity, making it more favorable than other vegetable oils, this oil was selected for the present study. To evaluate the influence of base-oil chemistry, HOSO was therefore used as base fluids, while graphene nanoplatelets (xGnP, GnP), TiO2, MoS2, and Al2O3 were selected, due to graphene’s exceptional thermal conductivity (3000 W m−1 K−1) and the need to ascertain the base nanoparticle that offers the best enhancement of oil thermophysical properties.

HOSO was obtained from Archer Daniels Midland (Chicago, IL). Nanoparticles—Graphene nanoplatelets xGnP was obtained from XG Sciences-USA, while Graphene GnP, Al2O3, MoS2, and TiO2 were obtained from Nanoshell-UK. Preparation of nanofluids

Nanofluids were prepared by dispersing nanoparticles in a base fluid (HOSO) at a given wt.% concentration to achieve a stable cohesive mixture. The nanoparticle calculated weight values (in grams) (Mnp) for a given wt.% concentration (n) was weighed using Torbal AGN120 Analytical Balance scale with a resolution of 0.0001 g with a capacity of 120 g, repeatability of 0.0001 g, linearity of ± 0.0002 g with 1% error in full scale reading. For 1 wt. % xGnP Nanofluid, 1 wt.% of xGnP weighing 0.1654 g was mixed with 20 mL of HOSO Base fluid weighing 16.3831 g. Thermal conductivity measurement

TC of the nanofluids was measured using the Thermtest Transient Hot-Wire (THW-L2) liquid thermal conductivity meter, coupled with an Echotherm chilling/heating dry bath as shown in Fig. 5., in accordance with ASTM D7896-19. This standard describes the transient hot wire method, in which a thin Alumel wire (0.126 mm diameter, 60 mm length) is fully immersed in the sample fluid. The wire serves as both the heating element and temperature sensor, enabling precise TC measurements. The THW-L2 can measure TC from 0.01 to 2 W m−1 K−1 at temperature range of − 50–100 °C with accuracy and repeatability better than 5% and 2%, respectively.Fig. 5Experimental setup of TC measurement

Experimental setup of TC measurement

For TC measurements, 20 mL of each sonicated nanofluid was poured into the THW-L2 meter’ sample cell, and tests were conducted at 25, 35, 45, 55, 65, and 75 °C, with each test running for 7 h. The TC measurement of the base fluid (HOSO) was replicated two times. Temperature rise for all tested nanofluids were similar and varied from 2.37 to 2.87 °C across the temperature range of 25–75 °C that gave accurate and consistent measurement. It was noticed that temperature rise around 3 °C and above leads to error and inconsistent results. In that case the test was repeated.

The heater, by default, sets a measurement temperature of 20 °C and allows it to stabilize for 15 min. Aluminum foil is used to cover the cell to prevent evaporation and prevent cooling of the samples during TC measurement. The TC measurement experiments were started after the THW-L2 temperature sensor stabilized at 21.3 °C. Temperature measurements ranged from 25 to 75 °C at increment of 10 °C and at a power setting of 80 mW in compliance standard ASTM D7896-19 that states maintaining linear conductive behavior. A delay of 10 min was set between tests. The experiment was then initiated, and results retrieved after seven hours, plotted and analyzed in Excel. Thermal conductivity measurements were repeated three times (n = 3) for every nanofluid at each temperature and the average was calculated.

Viscosity measurement

Viscosity measurement was performed using rotational viscometer ViscoQC 300 L configuration coupled with cone-plate temperature-controlled device PTD 100 equipped with spindle CP40. The temperature-controlled device was installed in the viscometer, and the spindle was inserted into the temperature-controlled device. An empty measuring cup of the device was inserted, and the gap was set as per manufacturer instructions, which can vary from 0.048 to 0.052 mm for the PTD100 temperature device with CP40 (cone-plate with 40 mm diameter) spindle. Then, the cup was removed, and 0.5 mL of sonicated HOSO vegetable oil-based nanofluid was poured into the measuring cup as shown in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Experimental setup for viscosity measurement

Experimental setup for viscosity measurement

Measurements were programmed to run at varying shear rates for each sample across the temperature range. Viscosity of the nanofluid was then measured at 25 , 45 , and 75 °C. Acquired measurements were saved in the viscometer and later processed and plotted using MATLAB and Excel. The highest shear rate was selected when the torque values are within 50–90% of its maximum value. For the L configuration of the ViscoQC 300, the torque range is from 0.00673 mNm to 0.0673 mNm, corresponding to 10–100%. Accuracy and repeatability of ViscoQC 300 L is 1% (that is 0.673 μNm) and 0.2%, respectively.

The viscometer ViscoQC 300 L uses predefined torque and speed to determine viscosity based on Eq. 7 under Sect. 2.2.7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \eta = \frac{TK \cdot SMC \cdot 10,000}{\omega } \cdot \frac{Torque\% }{{100}} $$\end{document}where TK denotes torque constant, that is 0.0673 mNm for rotational viscometer ViscoQC L configuration with spindle multiplier constant (SMC) of 0.331, and ω is spindle speed, where ω varies from 0.01 to 250 rpm. Viscosity measurements are provided by the viscometer that uses Eq. 5 to determine the viscosity of the fluid. Torque% is recommended to maintain at least 10%, which is automatically predefined in the viscometer ViscoQC 300, that does not start taking measurements unless the torque exceeds 10% of its value. The accuracy of viscosity measurements depends on the torque value: at 90% torque, the relative error is 1.1%; at 50%, it is 2%; at 20%, it is 5%; and at 10%, it is 10%.

Cone-plate and parallel-plate systems [31] operate based on standards ISO 3219|1993 for cone-plate and ISO 6721–10|1999 for parallel-plate, where the bob shears the sample under test in a defined gap between the fixed flat-plate and the cone or plate shaped bob as shown in Fig. 2. Cone-plate measuring system CP40 with cone angle of 0.8° is used in the experiment. Due to the narrow gaps, only a small amount of sample is required.

The Cone-Plate Temperature-Controlled Device PTD100 was temperature-calibrated by the manufacturer in our lab prior to testing. The calibrated PTD100 was mounted to the bottom of the ViscoQC 300 rotational viscometer. The viscometer was calibrated at each temperature range using reference standard fluid N14 and the spindle calibration factor was adjusted accordingly.

The viscometer starts data acquisition only after the temperature stabilized and the torque reached at least 10% of its capacity. However, it ensured that each measurement was recorded as the torque value was above 50% of its capacity. For each shear rate step, data was recorded every 20 s once these conditions were met, resulting in four data points collected over a total duration of 1 min and 20 s. Each test for one nanofluid at a time included more than one step. After each measurement step, the viscometer paused to adjust the shear rate, allowing the temperature to stabilize, and verify that the torque again exceeded 10%, before proceeding with the next step. If the torque exceeded 100%, the viscometer issued a warning and halted the measurement, prompting to select a lower shear rate. To eliminate systematic bias, the measurement steps were randomized. All data were saved in.csv format and subsequently processed using MATLAB and Excel. Viscosity measurements were repeated four times (n = 4) for each nanofluid at each shear rate within the specified temperature range. At each shear rate, data were recorded every 20 s, resulting in four data points over a total duration of 80 s.

Results and discussion

Results section presents sequential nanofluid TC and viscosity characterization followed by cross comparison of the tested nanofluids.

Thermal conductivity results

TC of the five formulated mono-nanofluids (xGnP/HOSO, GnP/HOSO, TiO2/HOSO, MoS2/HOSO, and Al2O3/HOSO) were measured and characterized at 6 temperature values of 25, 35, 45, 55, 65, and 75 °C and at weight percentage concentrations (wt.%) of the nanoparticles ranging from 1 to 7% with increment of 1%. Thermal conductivity percentage (%) enhancements were calculated relative to the TC of HOSO base fluid. The TC of HOSO base fluids were determined to be 0.171 W m−1 K−1 at 25 °C and 0.1603 W m−1 K−1 at 75 °C. The thermal conductivity of the five nanofluids are presented below.

xGnP/HOSO

The TC of xGnP/HOSO nanofluid as a function of temperature and wt. % concentration is shown in Fig. 7a and b, respectively. TC of xGnP/HOSO decreases linearly with increasing temperature and increases nonlinearly with nanoparticle wt.% concentration. A gradual linear increase in TC is observed within nanoparticle concentration range of 1–4 wt.%, followed by a rapid nonlinear increase from 4 to 5 wt.% xGnP concentrations. Above 5 wt.%, the rate of increase in TC becomes more gradual. A comprehensive review of literature [31] has shown that MWCNT, when used with coconut oil, improves surface quality, that nano-graphite used with water-soluble oil reduced surface roughness by 3.1%, while carbon nanotubes decreased surface roughness by 34%, and graphite, when used with water-soluble oil at 2 wt.% concentration produced the best surface quality. GnP, when used with water oil, improved the surface roughness by reducing the tool flank wear by up to 10–15% and improved tool life by up to 10–15%. In addition, GnP, when used with vegetable oil, improved surface quality by reducing the tool flank wear by up to 10–15%. High thermal conductivity (TC) of xGnP-H5 nanoplatelet (3000 W m−1 K−1) is attributed to its multilayered structure, small thickness of 15 nm, large specific surface area (SSA = 50–80 m2/g), and low density of 0.03 g/cm3, which allows the nanoparticles to remain stable and well-dispersed. When dispersed in HOSO base-fluid, xGnP nanoplatelets significantly increase the base-fluid's TC—approaching that of CEC—while enhancing the viscosity of the HOSO base-fluid. This combination makes it an ideal candidate to replace CEC.

At 7 wt.% concentration of xGnP, TC of the xGnP/HOSO nanofluid is 0.53 W m−1 K−1 at 25 °C and 0.47 W m−1 K−1 at 75 °C. At a 1 wt.% concentration, these values are 0.21 W m−1 K−1 and 0.19 W m−1 K−1, respectively. Notably, TC at 7 wt.% concentration closely approaches that of CEC (0.62 W m−1 K−1 at 25 °C and 0.66 W m−1 K−1 at 75 °C). Overall, the TC of the xGnP/HOSO nanofluid is enhanced significantly as the wt.% of the xGnP nanoparticles increases, as shown in Fig. 8.

Enhancement values of approximately 22.4, 52, 74, 104, and 172% were recorded for the 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 wt.% concentrations, respectively, staying stable across all six measured temperatures. In contrast, at 6 and 7 wt.% concentration, TC enhancement began to diverge across the temperature range, where the maximum enhancements of 191 and 214% were observed at 25 °C, while the values decreased to 186 and 196% at 75 °C for the 6 and 7 wt.% samples, respectively.

Viscosity results

It was observed that at concentrations exceeding 5 wt.%, the nanofluids became excessively dense, which could lead to nozzle clogging during machining. Therefore, concentrations beyond 5 wt.% were not considered in this study. Viscosity of the nanofluids decreases exponentially with increasing shear rate and temperature but increases nonlinearly with increase in wt.% concentration of nanoparticles. Percentage viscosity enhancements were calculated relative to the viscosity of HOSO base fluid, where viscosity of HOSO base fluid was determined to be 52.13, 24.63, and 10.87 mPa s at 25, 45, and 75 °C, respectively.

Nanofluid formulated by dispersion of nanoparticles in vegetable oil combine the properties of the two leading to an increase in viscosity and TC. Moreover, nanofluid behavior becomes non-Newtonian as the relationship between shear stress and shear rate at temperatures 25, 45, and 75 °C for different nanofluids as shown in Fig. 17. Shear-rate ranges from 1 to 200 s−1 were selected depending on nanofluid viscosity and to ensure reliable measurements. The applied ranges maintained the viscometer torque within 50–90% of its operational capacity while also covering shear conditions relevant to MQL operation. At lower temperatures, where viscosity was higher, lower shear rates in the range of 1–50 s−1 were sufficient to generate measurable torque. As temperature increased and viscosity decreased, higher shear rates up to 200 s−1 were required.Fig. 17Shear stress change over shear rate for the nanofluids at 25, 45, and 75 °C

Shear stress change over shear rate for the nanofluids at 25, 45, and 75 °C

In Fig. 17a nonlinear relationship is observed between shear stress and shear rate across all nanofluid types and concentrations, demonstrating non-Newtonian shear thinning behavior, especially at 25 °C. Al2O3/HOSO nanofluids showed shear stresses from approximately 0.5–1.5 N/m2 at lower shear rates (10–40 1/s), increasing with nanoparticle concentration. GnP, MoS2, and TiO2 exhibited intermediate shear stresses between 1 and 2 N/m2 across shear rates 20–100 s−1. xGnP nanofluids presented the highest shear stress values, reaching about 2 N/m2 at lower shear rates. At higher temperatures (blue curves at 75 °C), the slopes are lower indicating lower viscosity compared to that of at 45 and 25 °C. Some curves, especially at 25 °C, appear to have non-zero intercept if extended, that indicate shear thinning behavior particularly at higher wt.% concentrations of nanofluids. At higher shear rates, relationship between shear stress and shear rate becomes more linear showing that the nanofluids behavior is approaching Newtonian at higher shear rates and temperature.

With increasing temperature, the rheological behavior shifted toward Newtonian. In contrast, increasing nanoparticle concentration enhanced non-Newtonian behavior due to stronger internal structural interactions and network formation within the fluid. The observed shear-thinning behavior is beneficial for MQL applications. At low shear conditions, relatively higher viscosity reduces the risk of nozzle dripping, while at elevated shear rates the viscosity decreases, facilitating improved atomization and effective spray formation.

Uncertainty analysis

Experimental measurements inherently contain uncertainty. Overall uncertainties were evaluated based on measurement repeatability, and uncertainties were determined using the root mean square (RMS) method, which squares individual uncertainty values, calculates their mean, and then applies the square root. This approach provides a statistically robust representation of combined uncertainties by appropriately weighing larger uncertainty values while avoiding cancellation effects between positive and negative deviations.

Conclusion

This study investigated thermal conductivity (TC) and viscosity of nanofluids formulated using high oleic soybean oil (HOSO) HOSO) as base fluid, dispersed with one of five types of nanoparticles—graphene nanoplatelets (GnP), exfoliated graphene nanoplatelets (xGnP), TiO2, MoS2, and Al2O3 for potential use in Minimum Quantity Lubrication (MQL) machining of difficult-to-cut metals. The formulated nanofluids used for comparative TC and viscosity tests are xGnP/HOSO, GnP/HOSO, TiO2/HOSO, MoS2/HOSO, and Al2O3/HOSO. TC was measured at temperatures from 25 to 75 °C (10 °C increments) for nanoparticle concentration of 1–7wt.% (1%. increments), while viscosity was measured at 1–4 wt.% and at 25, 45, and 75°C. From the results, the following conclusions are made.All nanofluids exhibited increased TC and viscosity with increase of nanoparticle wt.% concentration; TC followed a second-order polynomial trend, while viscosity increased exponentially.xGnP/HOSO achieved the highest TC enhancement (up to 214% at 7 wt.%), approaching the TC value of CEC.At 1–5 wt.% concentrations, thermal conductivity enhancements were ~ 22.4%,~ 52, ~ 74, ~ 104, and ~ 172% respectively. At 7 wt.% concentration of xGnP, TC reached 0.53 W m−1 K−1 (25 °C) and 0.47 W m−1·K−1 (75 °C), close to that of CEC levels [0.62,0.66 W m−1 K−1].xGnP/HOSO showed the highest viscosity increases: ~ 32–622% (1–4 wt.%) at 25 °C and ~ 47–784% at 75 °C. TiO2/HOSO ranked second (~ 2–18.6%; ~ 10–29.4%), followed by MoS2/HOSO (~ 5.6–12.3%; ~ 2–9.9%), GnP/HOSO (~ 2–11.6%; ~ 0.8–5.9%), and Al2O3/HOSO (~ 2–8.8% at 25 °C; ~ 0.7–3.7% at 75 °C). xGnP/HOSO at 25 °C, viscosity at 1 and 4 wt.% concentrations of xGnP are around 68.8 mPa·s and 376.5 mPa s, respectively.
