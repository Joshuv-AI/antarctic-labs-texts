Comparative environmental and economic assessment of greenhouse cucumber and opuntia ficus-indica cultivation in arid regions
Authors: Hajar Abyar, Sahel Pakzad-Toochaei, Fatemeh Einollahipeer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change and the ongoing water crisis necessitate the adoption of efficient agricultural production systems that are adapted to arid and semi-arid regions. Insisting on cultivating water-intensive products, especially cucumber, in areas facing severe water shortages has led to environmental and ecological challenges. Therefore, moving towards water-resistant species and encouraging and informing farmers are imperative to fulfill the sustainable agriculture goals. Hence, this study was conducted to assess the environmental and economic burdens of cucumber compared to Opuntia ficus-indica cultivation, a drought-tolerant plant, in an arid region of eastern Iran. The life cycle assessment (LCA) was performed on a functional unit of one ton of product, encompassing every stage from greenhouse construction through to harvest. The cost analysis was also carried out over a 25-year cultivation horizon. The results indicated that cucumber cultivation had 1.8 to 8.3 times higher environmental impacts across nearly all impact categories due to intensive operational inputs. Cucumber showed 4.5 times higher CO₂ emissions (2443 kg CO2 eq) and over 8 times more water demand (95.42 m3) than O. ficus-indica. For the cucumber, the operational phase was dominated by diesel and electricity consumption, which influenced global warming (90.8%), ozone formation (88.5%), and ecotoxicity potential (59–78%). Both crops showed significant impacts during the greenhouse construction phase (> 95% contribution), linked mainly to materials such as concrete and cables. The integrated analysis confirmed that the cultivation of O. ficus-indica reduced total environmental impacts by 75% compared to cucumber. Although the economic analysis highlighted a higher internal rate of return (21.49%) and a shorter payback period (6.44 years) for cucumber, its cultivation was not economically justifiable due to the nearly 10 times higher water and fuel consumption. This study suggests O. ficus-indica cultivation as a sustainable low-input alternative with a more balanced footprint in water-scarce and climate-sensitive regions.

Introduction

Climate change, water scarcity, and the increasing demand for safe and sustainable agricultural products are the persistent concerns in recent decades. Arid and semi-arid climatic conditions worldwide have led to recurrent droughts, declining groundwater levels, and severe environmental stresses1. These conditions have not only reduced the efficiency of natural-resource utilization, but they have also affected food security and the economic sustainability of communities that rely heavily on agriculture2. Hence, the adoption of efficient production systems, particularly those that reduce water and energy consumption while enhancing productivity and product quality, has become a strategic priority in natural-resource management. Indeed, selecting and developing cropping patterns that are adapted to the climatic conditions of a region is imperative not only for the economic well-being of farmers and rural households but also for minimizing pressure on natural resources and preventing ecosystem degradation3.

Greenhouse cultivation systems have received considerable attention due to their ability to create a controlled environment for crop growth and reduce water consumption4,5. These systems, especially in arid and semi-arid regions, entail high energy. For instance, greenhouse cucumber cultivation in Iran, as an important economic crop with the increased area under cultivation, has significant energy consumption, mainly related to diesel fuel6. Alternative hurdles are the reduction in soil quality due to cultivation and harvesting, the increase in soil-borne diseases, and soil salinization, which diminishes crop productivity in greenhouses7. Due to the lack of fresh water resources and the use of mineral fertilizers, greenhouse crop production, such as cucumber, has imposed groundwater pollution and ecological challenges8.

Greenhouse cucumber cultivation is highly water-intensive, requiring irrigation almost every other day9. On the other hand, Opuntia ficus-indica, as a drought-tolerant plant, requires minimal irrigation, enabling grow in poor soils, and represents a valuable option for sustainable agriculture in water-scarce regions10,11. Beyond their nutritional benefits, Opuntia spp. offer diverse applications in food, pharmaceutical, livestock feed, and industrial units. The adaptation of food-production systems and the increase in biomass availability in climate-affected regions through Opuntia spp. are not only theoretical concepts; they are already being implemented worldwide12,13.

Opuntia spp. protect against erosion, contribute to soil health, and thrive in hot climates14. Although the nutritional composition of the cladodes varies with species and cultivation conditions, they generally contain 92% water, 1.5% protein, 5.5% carbohydrates, 1.15% ash, minerals, and 13 mg of vitamin C per 100 g of cladodes15. However, complete substitution of animal feed with Opuntia spp. may trigger diarrhea, reduced milk fat, and weight loss; hence, partial substitution is recommended to enhance sustainability and reduce the carbon footprint13. All parts of Opuntia spp., including fruits, cladodes, and flowers, are edible, providing digestible fiber and antioxidants16. Moreover, their seed oil is high in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly linoleic acid, with health benefits for coronary heart disease and rheumatoid arthritis17. However, the choice of the beneficial crop for cultivation in water-scarce and arid regions is not fulfilled without assessing the economic and environmental aspects.

The environmental impacts of agricultural products become evident when assessed across their entire life cycle assessment (LCA). LCA is a strategy to evaluate the environmental repercussions of the production cycle, from resource extraction and land preparation to cultivation, harvesting, and ultimately waste management or recycling18. This approach identifies environmental hotspots and assists in selecting more sustainable agricultural systems19. In parallel, life cycle cost (LCC) analysis determines the total cost of a product, system, or service over its entire life cycle. It quantifies indicators such as initial investment costs, operational expenses, economic returns, profitability, and payback period, ensuring the economic feasibility of sustainable farming practices20.

Few studies have focused on LCA and LCC assessment of crops. The available literature consistently showed that greenhouse cucumber production emitted markedly more greenhouse gases (GHG) than an open-field production system by 98.78%21. Another study referred to diesel fuel as an environmental hotspot of the cucumber cultivation, with significant contributions to human health, climate change, and resource damage8. Zamani, et al.22 evaluated environmental repercussions and water footprint of horticultural crops cultivation and underscored the human health impact stemming from the chemicals used and their associated emissions. Other investigations addressed the environmental impacts of cucumber and tomato production in greenhouse and open-field23, the optimization of energy flow in greenhouse cucumber production24, the environmental burdens and energy use25, and GHG emissions26 in cucumber cultivation. In contrast, the LCA of Opuntia spp. remains underexplored. Recent literature indicates that current fertilization practices, involving the use of chemical fertilizers and fresh cow manure, can affect global warming, ozone depletion, acidification, and water consumption27. The direct impact of the transportation phase, generated wastewater, and packaging on water consumption and mineral depletion has also been referred to27.

Despite the widespread expansion of greenhouse cultivation in arid and semi-arid regions, cropping patterns within these systems remain largely dominated by water and energy-intensive vegetables, such as cucumber. Most existing studies have focused on improving the efficiency of conventional greenhouses, while the fundamental question of crop choice under severe water scarcity has received limited attention. To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive eco-environmental assessment of agricultural products under the hot and arid climate has yet been conducted. This gap limits the ability of policymakers and farmers to make informed decisions regarding sustainable land use, energy, and water allocation in climate-vulnerable regions. Addressing this gap, the present study was designed as a problem-oriented assessment of the environmental and economic dimensions of O. ficus-indica cultivation, compared with greenhouse cucumber cultivation. The purpose of this study was not to imply direct substitution between cucumber and O. ficus-indica. Rather, the rationale was based on substitution at the cropping-system and resource-use level under conditions of severe water scarcity, where greenhouses often become idle due to water shortages and energy constraints. Hence, there is a critical need for alternative crops to assist farmers and livestock‑breeders living in these regions. O. ficus-indica, as a drought-tolerant species, is capable of being cultivated within similar protected infrastructures, requiring lower energy and water input. Therefore, the main goals of this research were as follows: (1) identify and compare the environmental impacts of the two cropping systems using LCA, (2) determine their contribution to GHG emissions, (3) analyze and evaluate economic indicators, (4) quantify the results’ reliability through uncertainty analysis, and (5) propose the optimal cropping pattern based on natural resource limitations, climatic conditions, and the socio-economic needs of the region. Overall, the results of this study can serve as a valuable model for other arid and semi-arid regions. This integrated approach can provide guiding cropping patterns toward sustainability and efficiency and determine their compatibility with sustainable development principles.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the farms of the Research Institute of Zabol, located in Zahak (about 30 km from Zabol city) in southeastern Iran, on the border with Afghanistan. Due to the drying up of the Helmand River, which originates in Afghanistan, and the Hamoun wetland, this region is particularly faced with limited water resources, successive droughts, and declining groundwater levels28,29. Low precipitation, high temperature, intense evaporation, and transpiration accompanied by various synoptic atmospheric positions are the main reasons that have turned the region into a critical climatic center30. Due to the recent twenty-year droughts in the region, farmers face water shortages in the spring, and their crops are destroyed by drought. Hence, the Research Institute of Zabol, Iran, has been focused on the cultivation and production chain of O. ficus-indica, as a drought-resistant plant (Fig. 1). In this study, two greenhouse farms cultivating cucumber and O. ficus-indica within the same agro-climatic zone were considered. The primary data was collected from on-site measurements and direct observations at each farm.

Antioxidant activity assessment

Antioxidant activity is an effective factor that helps the plant to withstand water shortage and harsh conditions. Hence, this assessment was conducted to highlight the physiological resilience of O. ficus-indica under arid conditions and to support the interpretation of LCA results, rather than as a direct environmental impact indicator. The antioxidant content of the cactus pad extract of O. ficus-indica was evaluated using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) method at concentrations of 5, 10, 25, 50, 75, 100, and 200 mg/mL. DPPH solution was prepared at a concentration of 100 µmol per sample, and one ml of sample and one ml of DPPH solution were added to each microtube. The control sample also contained ethanol instead of DPPH. The microtubes were incubated in the dark for 30 min, after which the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer (Photonix Ar 2015)31. Life cycle assessment

LCA is a systematic and standardized method for examining the environmental impacts of a product or process based on ISO 14,040 and ISO 14,044 standards32.

Goal and scope

The main goal of the research was to compare the environmental performance of greenhouse cucumber and O. ficus-indica cultivation. A functional unit was the production of one ton of product from cradle to gate22. The system boundary was considered from the stage of greenhouse construction to the product harvest. As shown in Fig. 2, the considered steps are land-preparation (drainage, plowing, and plotting), greenhouse construction (plastic covering), planting, cultivation, and harvesting.

Life cycle inventory

The raw materials and their quantities were estimated for land preparation, such as plowing the land surface, creating a gentle slope for drainage, and positioning the foundation for the construction of the 540 m2 greenhouse structure. It should be noted that due to the regional climate during the hot season, cucumber is not cultivated, and O. ficus-indica also does not require cooling. For heating, a single central heater installed in the middle of the greenhouses was used to warm the space. Since cucumber is highly sensitive to low temperatures, the five‑month growing period requires the heater to be switched on from approximately 3–4 p.m. until 6 a.m. Lighting in the cucumber greenhouse was provided at night for a total of 12 h using twenty 20‑W low‑consumption lamps. These lamps served only to illuminate the interior, not to supply a photoperiod for crop production. For the O. ficus-indica, only one lamp was installed for controlling greenhouse equipment such as the heater. The cow manure was added to both greenhouses at the beginning of soil preparation (before planting). For cucumber, potassium sulfate, superphosphate, and ammonium nitrate were applied when preparing the planting substrate. Additional fertilizers, including fertilizer type 1 and type 2, humic acid, calcium nitrate, and iron fertilizer, were used throughout the cucumber growth and harvest phases during the six months. Metasodium pesticide was also incorporated into the cucumber production system for soil preparation. For O. ficus-indica, fertilizer was applied every six months for spring and autumn. Moreover, the cucumber yield was 8 tons per cropping cycle, whereas the minimum annual cactus pad production was 3.4 tons for the first year. A list of materials, chemicals, and equipment required per functional unit for each product is presented in Tables 1 and 2. The whole data was gathered from on-site evaluation, field measurements, and direct observations at greenhouse facilities. Complementary information was achieved from expert interviews and local operators. The materials required for the truss system, plastic covering, cabling, and piping of the greenhouse structure were also quantified. The amounts of electricity used for lighting and heating, fuel and water consumption, pesticides applied, and cucumber seeds and cactus pads were also estimated.

Life cycle impact assessment

Environmental impact assessment was carried out using SimaPro software (v. 6.9). For the assessment, the ReCiPe method was employed at both midpoint and endpoint levels under the hierarchist perspective, which focuses on environmental challenges in the cause-and-effect chain and integrates intermediate impacts into the ultimate impact categories33. The following impact categories were assessed at the midpoint level: global warming potential (GWP), stratospheric ozone depletion, ionizing radiation, ozone formation-human health and terrestrial ecosystem, fine particulate formation, terrestrial acidification, freshwater and marine eutrophication, terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecotoxicity, human carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic toxicity, land use, mineral and fossil resource scarcity, and water consumption8. Moreover, three damage categories, including human health, ecosystem, and resources, were evaluated at the endpoint level based on the experimental data and background data from the Ecoinvent 3 database.

The impacts of the construction and operation of both greenhouses were compared based on the production of one ton of product. The relative importance of each impact category was determined using normalization methodology, providing a comparable scale for impact categories. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) method was employed to calculate GWP, expressed in kg of CO₂ for 100 years34. The total freshwater consumed in the entire production of a product was assessed based on the AWARE method35. This approach aggregates all water scarcity impacts in the region.

Results interpretation

Uncertainty analysis is an applicable strategy to evaluate the confidence level of the results and highlight the influence of data errors or variations36. Model uncertainty was apprised by the Monte Carlo method in SimaPro software based on 10,000 simulations with a 95% confidence interval37.

Economic analysis

The capital expenditure (CAPEX) includes the costs of greenhouse components, equipment setup, and installation, which were calculated based on current market prices. The cost of chemical fertilizers, equipment repair or replacement, fuel consumption, energy and water consumption, and labor were considered as an operational expenditure (OPEX). The energy consumption for lighting and temperature control was estimated at $0.0003/kWh (equivalent to about 330 Iranian Rials/kWh at the time of data collection), based on the highly subsidized electricity tariff applied to the agricultural sector in Iran to support farm productivity. All primary data were collected directly from the operational site. Results and discussion

Antioxidant activity of O. ficus-indica

Based on initial sampling in O. ficus-indica greenhouses, the average fresh biomass production per pad (average fresh weight) was estimated as 1000 ± 45 g. The dry biomass of each pad was also about 5% of the fresh biomass. These findings indicate the significant potential of O. ficus-indica for biomass production in the climatic conditions of the Sistan region. In addition, an increasing trend of antioxidant activity proportional to the concentration of the extract was observed. The DPPH inhibitory percentage at concentrations of 5, 10, 25, 50, 75, 100, and 200 mg/ml was 15.06%, 20.14%, 23.60%, 27.42%, 31.12%, 45.87%, and 61.28%, respectively. The findings were compatible with Zourgui, et al.40, who reported the antioxidant potential of Opuntia streptacantha. Overall, the IC50 value in the pad of O. ficus-indica was estimated as 130.21 mg/mL.

Midpoint impacts

The environmental burdens of construction and operational phases of cucumber and O. ficus-indica cultivation are illustrated in Table 3. The construction phase dominated the whole impact categories, while cucumber cultivation was a pioneer for the operational phase. For GWP, the CO2 emitted during the greenhouse construction was relatively 16,629 kg CO2 eq, mostly associated with concrete utilization (85.95%), as shown in Fig. 3. Regarding the literature41, the materials used in the infrastructure for cucumber, lettuce, and tomato production such as iron, plastic film, and stamping wire contributed 45.6% and 54.5% of GHG release in intercropping and monocultures systems, respectively. Notably, O. ficus-indica significantly exhibited 4.5 times lower CO2 emissions than cucumber due to savings of 1540 L of diesel consumption. The GWP of O. ficus-indica was 0.535 kg CO2 eq/kg crop cultivation, which was aligned with Vergel-Rangel, et al.42, reporting 0.562 kg CO2 eq emission from cultivating one kg of nopal. This reflects the energy-intensive greenhouse infrastructure required for cucumber cultivation. The need for artificial heating/cooling, pumps, and mechanized farming in greenhouse systems explains this high burden43. The associated literature also pointed out the substantial role of diesel fuel (> 60% contribution) in GHG emissions from greenhouse cucumber production4,26. Another study expressed a 27.38% and 22.02% contribution of chemical fertilizer to total GWP for sunflower and cucumber production, respectively44, while it was lower than 3% for cucumber and O. ficus-indica in this study.

The ozone depletion, terrestrial acidification, and fine particulate matter formation derived from cucumber cultivation were four times higher than those for O. ficus-indica, which was related to considerable diesel and electricity usage. Ahmadbeyki, et al.8 also identified the largest contribution of diesel fuel, electricity, nitrogen, and human labor for cucumber cultivation, accounting for 62.4%, 11.92%, 11.46%, and 8.2%, respectively. Moreover, the cucumber cultivation incurred the release of 6.91 kg P eq and 0.52 kg N eq, with the largest share of 77% to 95% for the construction phase, while it was 62.5% to 83.33% lower for O. ficus-indica cultivation. This is linked to fuel consumption as well as nutrient leakage and fertilizer runoff.

Terrestrial ecotoxicity was extremely higher as 293,377 kg 1,4-DCB for the construction phase, followed by 3763 and 2905 kg 1,4-DCB for marine and freshwater ecotoxicity, respectively. For the operational phase, the O. ficus-indica cultivation reduced the total ecotoxicity impacts by > 51% because of lower energy demand. The overall higher ecotoxicity and non-carcinogenic burden for cucumber suggests greater operational reliance on inputs such as pesticides or other auxiliary materials that contain non-carcinogenic toxic substances that are then released into soil and water systems. The same trend was observed for human toxicity and mineral resource scarcity, which were 44% to 51% lower for O. ficus-indica cultivation. Notable differences were also detected in fossil resource scarcity and water consumption categories, which increased for cucumber cultivation by 85.13% and 87.91%, respectively, mainly from greenhouse materials and fossil-fuel-based energy use25,45. The combustion of fossil fuels for electricity generation and supplying the diesel demand, accompanied by volatilization from nitrogen-based fertilizers, potentially aligns with the higher resource scarcity46,47. However, O. ficus-indica is drought-tolerant and a crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) species, making it a water-efficient crop to grow in areas with less water availability48.

The share of each input parameter to midpoint impacts is depicted in Fig. 3. As can be seen, for cucumber cultivation, electricity consumption dramatically contributed to GWP (43.38%), ionizing radiation (56.92%), ecotoxicity potential (> 45%), and human non-carcinogenic toxicity (30.98%). Similarly, Fathelrahman, et al.49 highlighted the contributions of electricity usage for cooling, desalination, and pumps alongside materials including galvanized steel, concrete, and high-density polyethylene to human toxicity, ecotoxicity, and eutrophication impacts. The striking diesel utilization for cucumber cultivation influenced ozone formation, fine particulate matter formation, terrestrial acidification, eutrophication potential, GWP, and land use, with a share of 43.38% to 84.11%. Diesel was the main driver due to machinery and fuel use in greenhouse operations. The release of CO2 from cucumber cultivation could be linked to diesel fuel consumption8. Vergel-Rangel, et al.27 expressed that the substitution of the gasoline car for an electric vehicle resulted in a 4.35% to 20.49% reduction in terrestrial ecotoxicity, GWP, and fossil resource depletion due to low CO2 and NOx emissions. Another study also found the determining effect of electricity on eutrophication, GWP, and human toxicity43.

The eutrophication potential arises from fertilizer and nutrient runoff because cucumber needs 75% higher fertilizer, despite also needing calcium nitrate and humic acid. Furthermore, the elevated levels of acidification and eutrophication strongly suggest that intensive fertilization and potentially higher use of chemicals characterize the cucumber operational phase. This intensive management also requires a significantly greater area of land, compounding the terrestrial impacts. Rashidi, et al.25 also identified nitrogen fertilizer used for cucumber cultivation as a key contributor to most impact categories.

Diesel‑fuel emissions from cucumber cultivation were far higher than those of O. ficus-indica because cucumber needs frequent operations (heating, irrigation, and spraying), while O. ficus-indica is a low-input crop. The diesel usage and energy consumption for the heating of the cucumber greenhouse were also highlighted by Ahmadbeyki, et al.8 and Hou, et al.50. Hence, the use of efficient heaters, a ventilation system, and a thermostat controller was suggested to diminish the electricity consumption43. For O. ficus-indica cultivation, the impacts of electricity and diesel utilization were observed in GWP (41.46%) and marine eutrophication (52.84%), respectively. The role of irrigation drip tape to land use (32.92%), freshwater eutrophication (28.4%), and ionizing radiation (25.18%) categories was ascertained for O. ficus-indica cultivation. It is notable that the installation of an electrical panel in a greenhouse substantially contributed to human toxicity, mineral resource scarcity, and freshwater eutrophication, likely due to metals used in production. The humic acid required for cucumber cultivation also affected freshwater eutrophication (27.5%). In addition, the use of nitrogen fertilizer in O. ficus-indica cultivation was associated with stratospheric ozone depletion (39.74%), reflecting emissions of N₂O and related compounds27. Regarding the literature42,51, fertilizing nopal with manure intensifies CH₄ emissions; however, in the present study, the impact of manure on overall environmental burdens was negligible.

In terms of the construction phase, concrete, cable, and galvanized pipe were impactful parameters to environmental burdens with a share of 16–85.95.95%, 3.46–81.62%, and 0.52–27.96%, respectively. Concrete dominated the construction impacts because cement manufacture releases ozone-depleting precursors. The contribution of cables to ecotoxicity and non-carcinogenic toxicity (> 74%) can also be due to heavy-metal mining and processing. Indeed, the construction phase had a greater toxicity impact than the cultivation phase, which was associated with the metals (copper and aluminium) and plastics used in the cables. Overall, cultivation impacts were tied to electricity dependency, while the construction phase was linked to material processing. Intensive cucumber farming uses more resources per functional unit, while O. ficus-indica is land- and resource-efficient, and its environmental burdens were mostly relevant to the construction phase, showing the importance of infrastructure.

The normalized data in Fig. 4 offer a comprehensive comparison of the environmental burden attributed to the operational phase of cultivating cucumber versus O. ficus-indica, framed against the substantial initial impacts of the construction phase. The integrated operational-construction analysis illustrated the determining role of the construction phase in the whole impact categories accounted for > 95% contribution. Human carcinogenic toxicity stood in the first rank with a share of 43.87%, followed by freshwater (26.07%), marine (19.56%), and terrestrial (4.36%) ecotoxicity. The main contributors were concrete for human toxicity and the used cable for ecotoxicity impacts. Construction activities are inherently resource and energy-intensive due to the extensive use of fossil fuels for excavators and trucks, alongside construction materials such as cement and steel52. This also involves mining, high-temperature processes, and chemical treatments, which contribute heavily to GWP, toxicity, and resource scarcity impacts. This underscores that construction activities also consume significant amounts of water, possibly for mixing concrete, dust suppression, or site preparation53.

The comparison of two crop functions demonstrated the lower environmental burdens of O. ficus-indica cultivation against cucumber, accounting for 44.29% (human carcinogenic toxicity) to 87.9% (water consumption) impact reductions. This suggests that the cultivation and processing of O. ficus-indica are less resource-intensive than cucumber cultivation. O. ficus-indica is extremely drought-tolerant, requiring far less irrigation than most conventional crops, thrives in marginal lands with low soil fertility, decreasing the need for fertilizers, and has low susceptibility to diseases, lowering the use of pesticides54,55. Although the antioxidant activity of O. ficus-indica was not an LCA parameter, its role in increasing stress tolerance explained the reduced reliance on water and energy inputs, which is reflected in the lower environmental impacts. The results underlined that the choice of low-impact bio-based material, such as O. ficus-indica, can lead to substantial environmental benefits. When aiming for sustainability, efforts should focus on reducing the impacts of the construction phase through material selection, energy efficiency, and waste minimization.

Endpoint impacts

The damage assessment of crop cultivation is shown in Table 4, accompanied by the parameters’ contribution in Fig. 5. The human health value for cucumber was 0.005 DALY, relatively similar to that for Ahmadbeyki, et al.8, who accounted for 0.012 DALY, originating from the on-greenhouse emissions. The endpoint impacts of both phases were observed in human health impacts (83.66% to 95.37%), followed by resources (1.94% to 13.21%) and the ecosystem (2.6% to 3.12%). Approximately 60% of the construction phase impacts were related to concrete utilization, while 50% of the total impacts for cucumber cultivation were attributed to diesel consumption. For O. ficus-indica, the electrical panel and electricity were the crucial contributors with a share of 31.33% and 25.68%, respectively. Although the construction phase led to significant environmental impacts (660.5 Pt), the cultivation of O. ficus-indica (18.5 Pt) reduced the total impacts by 75% (Fig. 6). In the resource category, diesel and electricity consumption accounted for the largest share, which was in accordance with Ali, et al.56 and Ahmadbeyki, et al.8, who reported that diesel fuel contributed about 65% of the impact, followed by electricity and nitrogen consumption in the resource-damage category.

As shown in Fig. 5, diesel and electricity consumption accounted for 33.14%−44.91% of the impact on human health, 33.66%−45.08% on ecosystems, and 15.35%−81.95% on resources.

This was in line with the findings of Fathelrahman, et al.49, who presented the critical role of electricity consumption and chemical use to endpoint impacts of cucumber and tomato production. They also suggested the efficient utilization of electricity and fertilizers using advanced control systems to restrict raw material usage.

Carbon footprint

The results of the carbon footprint are shown in Fig. 7a, which highlights that the greenhouse construction phase contributed 87.5% of total GHG emissions, accounting for cucumber and 97.2% for O. ficus-indica. The remaining emissions originated from the cultivation phase, dominated by diesel (47%) and electricity (44%) for cucumber (Fig. 7b). O. ficus-indica cultivation phase contributed much less because it is naturally adapted to arid climates, requiring less energy input. Its impacts came mainly from electricity (42%) and diesel (18%). Andreu-Coll, et al.57 also expressed that one ha of O. ficus-indica during its productive life can take ~ 462 tons of CO2. In terms of the construction phase, greenhouse construction was dominated by concrete (∼85%), which induced a high carbon footprint due to cement production. The substantial role of electricity usage in GWP, accompanied by high-density polyethylene, concrete, and steel used in the cucumber greenhouse structure, was underlined by Fathelrahman, et al.49, confirming the results of this study. Notably, the carbon footprint for cucumber and O. ficus-indica cultivation was estimated at 2333 and 517.8 kg CO2 eq for one ton crop production, respectively. The difference in carbon footprint evaluation of the IPCC and ReCiPe methods is attributed to the different considered criteria and emission factors58. However, the substantial CO2 emissions of cucumber cultivation were also reported, largely associated with the use of fertilizers59. Another study linked the CO2 emission to the consumed electricity, nitrogen fertilizer, and diesel fuel, accounting for 4997.75 kg/ha44. They also referred to the GHG emissions from electricity usage in the irrigation system44.

Water footprint

Regarding Fig. 7c, within the cultivation phase, irrigation water (93%) was the overwhelming contributor, showing high water dependency in greenhouse-grown cucumber. In contrast, concrete production dominated (70%) the construction phase water consumption, reflecting water use in cement production. Although the cultivation of both crops resulted in water consumption, the gap was much greater for cucumber (4011.14 m3) than for O. ficus-indica (475.85 m3), which was due to the high irrigation requirements of cucumber60, whereas O. ficus-indica naturally minimizes water needs54. Over eight times greater water demand for cucumber is a key driver for subsequent energy-related impacts, as water treatment, pumping, and delivery require significant power.

The results of uncertainty analysis for one ton of crop production are shown in Fig. 8. The mean of normalized value, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV) were reported for greenhouse construction and crop cultivation for midpoint impact categories. As can be seen, the CV value for the construction phase varied from 2.1% for mineral resource scarcity to 29.8% for freshwater ecotoxicity. The largest uncertainty was relevant to ecotoxicity potential categories, which was less than 30%. For the cucumber, the CV values were lower than 23%, which was mostly related to marine ecotoxicity, followed by land use and ionizing radiation. Moreover, the CV values for O. ficus-indica cultivation were lower than 15% in most impact categories, indicating the data reliability. The largest CV was associated with stratospheric ozone depletion (24.2%), mineral resource scarcity (23.9%), and land use (23.2%). Despite a high CV value in some impact categories, the results were of high confidence for human toxicity, water consumption, eutrophication, and GWP.

Economic analysis

The required costs for greenhouse construction up to product harvest per one ton of greenhouse cucumber and O. ficus-indica are revealed in Tables 1 and 2. The total investment costs for greenhouse cucumber and O. ficus-indica over 25 years were estimated at $32,686 and $28,269, accounting for 60% and 79% of the total costs, respectively (Table 5). Galvanized pipes, concrete, and plastic used in greenhouse construction exhibited the highest shares of CAPEX, estimated at 36.2–42.9%, 25.6–30.3%, and 14.2–21.6%, respectively. A key difference between the two products was that the heater required for cucumber greenhouses imposed almost twice the financial burden compared to the O. ficus-indica greenhouse. In addition, the plastic cost for the cucumber greenhouses was 1.8 times that of the O. ficus-indica greenhouse. This was related to annual plastic replacement for the cucumber greenhouse, against once every two years for the O. ficus-indica greenhouse.

The operational costs for cucumber and O. ficus-indica production over 25 years were determined to be $22,144 and $7385.4, respectively. Labor for bed preparation and plastic covering (33.2%) and labor for maintenance and harvesting (23.8%) had the largest contributions to operational costs of the cucumber cultivation. However, for O. ficus-indica, labor for maintenance and harvesting (38.5%) and fertilizers (29.1%) played substantial roles. A notable difference in the operational costs of the two products was attributed to the labor hours required for cucumber maintenance and harvesting, which were more than twice those needed for O. ficus-indica cultivation. The results were in agreement with Azizpanah, et al.44, who declared that human labor and chemical fertilizers were the main reasons for the total cucumber cultivation cost. It is worth noting that the diesel consumption cost for the cucumber greenhouse was about 1.4 times higher than that estimated for the O. ficus-indica greenhouse. Moreover, the diversity and cost of fertilizers required for cucumber cultivation were much higher than for O. ficus-indica, even though fertilizers accounted for the largest share of O. ficus-indica operational costs.

The cultivation of greenhouse cucumber illustrated a higher IRR (21.49%) compared to O. ficus-indica (14.19%) and a shorter payback period (6.44 years versus 12.4 years). Considering the lower initial CAPEX for O. ficus-indica cultivation, its longer payback period is entirely expected. On the other hand, the water consumption cost for cucumber cultivation was estimated to be nearly 10 times that of O. ficus-indica. Given the critical water shortage and the environmental conditions in the Sistan region, the cultivation of greenhouse cucumber is not economically justifiable. Notably, water with a low electrical conductivity (EC) content is used for cucumber cultivation, and the increase of EC up to 2.6 dS/m may cause salinity stress in the plant61. On the other hand, the cucumber cultivation typically begins between September 11 and March 11 and sometimes extends to April 9. However, due to a lack of access to sufficient water and the rising EC levels in local wells, many regional greenhouses remain idle and unused. Therefore, cultivating Opuntia spp. in these greenhouses can not only tolerate a high EC level but also partly compensate for the initial investment costs.

Social implications

A key determinant of whether the environmental and economic benefits of Opuntia ficus-indica can be realized is its social acceptability among producers and consumers. In arid regions, adoption is primarily driven by the cactus’s negligible water requirement, low-cost propagation from cuttings, and its multifunctionality, such as fresh fruit, pads for feed, and its food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic applications. However, the principal barriers are the lack of mechanised harvesting equipment and limited market information, especially for the fresh-fruit segment, which is still perceived as a niche product. Labor analyses in this study indicated that cucumber greenhouse cultivation requires substantially more person-hours than O. ficus-indica, thereby freeing labor for ancillary activities such as fruit processing or value-added product development. Notably, the cactus’s ability to generate additional revenue streams, e.g., animal feed, improves the resilience of farm household incomes to climate-induced production risks. Governmental support can also lower entry barriers and signal market confidence, encouraging wider farmer participation. Overall, the farmer’s willingness to adopt, labor reallocation, market development, and supportive policies strengthen the case for O. ficus-indica as a socially sustainable alternative to water-intensive greenhouse cucumber in arid environments.

Conclusion

This study focused on developing a solution-oriented approach for cultivating resistant crops to address the challenge of water scarcity in arid regions. The scrunizing of the environmental and economic impacts of greenhouse cucumber and O. ficus-indica cultivation revealed the significant environmental burdens of cucumber cultivation. This was primarily due to its heavy reliance on diesel and electricity for heating and operation, frequent use of fertilizers and chemicals, and high irrigation demands. Conversely, O. ficus-indica cultivation proved much less demanding, indicating lower emissions and inputs because of its natural adaptation as a drought-tolerant species. This adaptability mitigates the need for water, fertilizer, and energy, enabling impact reductions up to 87.9% in water consumption compared to the cucumber. Nonetheless, the greenhouse construction significantly overshadowed the operational phase impacts in toxicity (1999 kg 1,4-DCB), resource scarcity (3430.15 kg oil eq), and land use (794.3 m2a crop eq). The economic assessment over 25 years illustrated lower investment and operational inputs for O. ficus-indica cultivation, resulting in a total LCC nearly 35% lower than cucumber cultivation. However, the payback period for O. ficus-indica was twice the greenhouse cucumber, which can be attributed to the limited system boundary and lack of data for the fruit-bearing stage. Overall, O. ficus-indica exhibited a long-term economic alternative that reduced financial risk under conditions of energy limitation and water scarcity. Moreover, for sustainability achievement, the reduction of operational input for cucumber and the decrease of environmental burdens of the construction phase are imperative. However, idle greenhouses for O. ficus-indica cultivation offer a practical approach to partially compensate for initial investment costs and promote resilience in arid agriculture.
