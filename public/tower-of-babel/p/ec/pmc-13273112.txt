Ligand-dependent reprogramming of HNF4A expression and function suppresses multistep hepatocarcinogenesis
Authors: Hikari Okada, Kouki Nio, Naoki Ohno, Yusuke Masuo, Takatsugu Hirokawa, Tetsuro Shimakami, Tosiaki Miyati, Hiroyuki Takamura, Tetsuo Ohta, Azusa Kitao, Satoshi Kobayashi, Toshifumi Gabata, Osamu Matsui, Yukio Kato, Motoharu Seiki, Masao Honda, Shuichi Kaneko, Taro Yamashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Activation of tumor suppressors represents an attractive strategy for cancer treatment. Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4A) functions as a tumor suppressor in the liver by inhibiting hepatocyte proliferation; however, no effective agonists have been identified. Here, we aimed to identify novel ligands for HNF4A and evaluate their role in hepatocarcinogenesis. We identified polyprenoic acid (PA, peretinoin) as the first ligand capable of directly binding HNF4A. Reanalysis of prior clinical trial data revealed that PA suppressed the progression of dysplastic nodules (DNs) to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), while showing no effect on local HCC recurrence after initial treatment. Consistently, PA inhibited DN growth but not established HCC in Pdgf-C transgenic mice. Mechanistically, PA selectively bound to the HNF4A P1 isoform, enhancing its transcriptional activity and upregulating hepatocyte maturation markers (ALB, TTR, and SLCO1B3), while suppressing alpha-fetoprotein expression driven by the HNF4A P2 isoform. Importantly, hepatocyte-specific Hnf4a knockdown or lipid-nanoparticle-mediated Hnf4a siRNA abrogated the protective effects of PA. These findings establish HNF4A as a pharmacologically controllable tumor suppressor and highlight PA-like compounds as promising agents for preventing liver carcinogenesis.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the fifth most common human cancer worldwide, associated with a poor prognosis,1 and its incidence is increasing in Western countries.2 HCC develops through a multistep process, progressing from regenerative nodules to dysplastic nodules (DNs) and eventually to HCC.3 Throughout these processes, the accumulation of gene mutations can activate oncogenes or inactivate tumor suppressor genes, leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation, motility, angiogenesis, and metastasis.4

Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4A) is an orphan nuclear receptor recognized as the master regulator of hepatocyte development, nutrient transport, and metabolism.5 It also serves as a tumor suppressor in the liver, inhibiting the proliferation of hepatocytes.6 Fatty acids, such as linoleic acid (LA), have been identified as endogenous ligands for HNF4A7,8,9,10; however, supplementation with these fatty acids has minimal impact on HNF4A activation. Structural analysis demonstrated that fatty acids bind to both the open (inactive) and closed (active) conformations of HNF4A,11 suggesting that ligand binding is required but not sufficient for HNF4A activation. Although HNF4A plays a crucial role in liver metabolism, regeneration, and carcinogenesis, no effective HNF4A agonists have been identified.5

Polyprenoic acid (PA), previously known as peretinoin, is a small synthetic molecule that structurally resembles fatty acids. Notably, its potential to prevent HCC recurrence following radical treatment was initially reported in 1996.12 However, a phase II/III randomized placebo-controlled study later suggested marginal effects of treatment with PA on HCC prevention.13 As the name peretinoin indicates, PA was initially considered an acyclic retinoid owing to its binding to retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα),14 and evidence suggests that aberrant retinoic acid (RA) signaling may contribute to oncogenesis.15 However, no reports have conclusively demonstrated the effects of PA on RA signaling. Despite the unknown mechanisms and molecular targets of PA in HCC, its utility as a chemopreventive drug in HCC is currently being evaluated in phase III clinical trials.

Given the lack of clear therapeutic effects of PA, precise identification of its molecular targets through reverse translational research is essential to develop more effective clinical trials and treatments. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to investigate whether PA is a potential HNF4A ligand and its novel physiological function in inhibiting HCC carcinogenesis and recurrence. To this end, we selected platelet-derived growth factor C (PDGF-C) transgenic (Tg) mice, the only animal model that develops HCC from precancerous DNs against a background of liver fibrosis.16 These mice are ideal for evaluating the effects of PA, as their HCC does not originate from hepatocytes with specific transgenic activation of oncogenes such as c-Myc, β-catenin, or transforming growth factor α,17 thereby mimicking the heterogeneous nature of HCC.

Results

To investigate the recurrence patterns, we used computed tomography (CT) scans to monitor the same patients over time. Of the total cohort, we analyzed 110 placebo recipients and 111 PA-treated patients (600 mg) and observed no significant differences in their clinical characteristics (Table S1). During follow-up, 55 patients in the placebo group and 38 patients treated with PA-treated group exhibited recurrence, with PA demonstrating a marginal, non-significant trend toward reducing recurrence (p = 0.12; Figure 1B). A total of 24 placebo-treated and 21 PA-treated patients developed de novo HCCs, whereas 13 placebo and 10 PA patients exhibited local recurrence of previously treated tumors, with no significant effect of PA in either context (Figures 1C and 1D). However, we noted a marked reduction in HCC recurrence from DN in PA-treated patients. Specifically, only 7 patients in the PA group exhibited DN-associated recurrence, compared with 16 in the placebo group (p = 0.037). Notably, PA-treated patients exhibited no further recurrence for at least 2 years post-enrollment (Figure 1E). The hazard ratio for DN-associated HCC recurrence in the PA-treated group was 0.41 (95% confidence interval, 0.17–0.98; p = 0.046; Figure 1F), suggesting a significant protective effect of PA against HCC progression from DNs.

MRI analysis visualizes multistep HCC progression in PDGF-C Tg mice

PDGF-C Tg mice progressively develop liver fibrosis and cirrhosis (LC) with age, forming DNs at 32–40 weeks of age. Subsequently, they develop HCC at a high frequency of approximately 70%–90% after 40 weeks. To noninvasively and longitudinally evaluate the progression of liver disease in these mice, we performed MRI using the liver-specific contrast agent Gd-EOB-DTPA (Figure 2A). This contrast agent targets mature hepatocyte function through drug transporter OATP1B3 activity,18 which is useful for the detection of preneoplastic DNs and HCC in humans.19 In 32-week-old mice, MRI revealed small hypointensity nodules without arterial enhancement, corresponding to DNs. In 52-week-old mice, nodules exhibited hypo-intensity with arterial enhancement, consistent with the transition to HCC (Figure 2B). Consistent with our MRI findings, histological observations revealed a gradual increase of Ki-67-positive S-phase cells and loss of the mouse Gd-EOB-DTPA transporter OATP1 as disease progressed from liver cirrhosis to DNs and ultimately HCC (Figure 2C). We further analyzed tumor tissues at 32 weeks (DNs) and 60 weeks (HCC) of age, as well as non-cancerous liver tissues from mice aged 32 weeks (LC). The expression of Afp, a tumor marker, increased from LC to DNs and HCC, whereas that of Slco1a1 (the murine equivalent to human OATP1B3) decreased (Figure 2E). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a gradual loss of membrane OATP1 expression over time (Figure 2F), with AFP detected exclusively in HCC tissues. Whole-exome sequencing analysis revealed random and significant accumulation of gene mutations in tumor tissues (Figure 2G; Table S2). Network analysis of these mutated genes suggested an abrogation of tumor suppressor genes, including transformation-related protein 53 (Trp53) and tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (Tsc2), and antigen-presenting molecules, such as H2-B1, H2-K1, H2-M3, and H2-T3, in 60-week-old HCC mice (Figure 2H). These findings suggest that the molecular subtype of HCC in PDGF-C Tg mice resembles the tumor protein 53 (TP53) cluster recognized in human HCC subtypes.20 In contrast, no specific networks were mutated in DNs in mice aged 32–40 weeks, consistent with HCC and DN exome data.21

PA inhibits the growth of DNs and reduces the accumulation of mutations in PDGF-C Tg mice

We administered PA (80 mg/kg) to 32-week-old PDGF-C Tg mice for 8 weeks (Figure 3A), resulting in a clear regression or loss of tumors (Figures 3B and 3C). In contrast, when treatment with PA was initiated at 52 weeks of age (Figure 3A), we observed no effect on liver tumors developed in PDGF-C Tg mice (Figures 3D and 3E). Transcriptome analysis of liver tissue from 40- and 60-week-old PA-treated mice revealed two gene clusters that were differentially expressed in tumor and non-tumor tissues. We identified 604 significantly activated (307 genes in cluster 1) or inactivated (297 genes in cluster 2) genes in tumor tissue compared to non-tumor tissue (p < 0.05; Figure 3F; Table S3). The EOB-AFP classification system,22 which combines Gd-EOB-DTPA-enhanced MRI (EOB-MRI) and serum AFP status, reflecting the stem and mature status of HCC, clearly classifies the malignancy of HCC based on the SLCO1B3 (OATP1B3) gene signature. We observed that the degree of anaplastic lesions according to the EOB-AFP classification was largely controlled by HNF4A and FOXM1.22 Transcription factor analysis revealed activation of cell growth factors, such as SP1 and FOXM1, and inactivation of several nuclear receptors, including HNF4A, in liver tumor tissues of PDGF-C Tg mice (Figure 3G).

Cluster 1 genes were suppressed, whereas cluster 2 genes were reactivated in PA-treated DNs (Figure 3F), suggesting that nuclear receptors, such as HNF4A, might be reactivated following treatment with PA. HNF4A nuclear expression was maintained in 32-week DNs, whereas it was lost in 52-week HCC (Figure 3H, left panel). Moreover, HNF4A nuclear expression was evident in reversible DN + PA tumors but not in irreversible HCC+PA tumors (Figure 3H, right panel). These findings suggest that the antitumor activity of PA may be mediated through the preservation of HNF4A in DNs, which is lost in the transition to HCC.

PA induces hepatocyte maturation in PDGF-C Tg mice by downregulating HNF4A P2 and upregulating HNF4A P1 expression

We have previously demonstrated that HNF4A expression decreases with the progression of HCC and that decreased HNF4A expression promotes cancer progression.22 In the present study, using immunostaining, we demonstrated that HNF4A expression is higher in DN sites of precancerous lesions than in HCC (Figure 4A). HNF4A has 12 isoforms, which are roughly divided into two types, P1 and P2, with P1 being involved in maturation and P2 in undifferentiation.23 In 32-week-old mice, DNs exhibited expression of both HNF4A P1 and P2 isoforms, whereas their expression levels were significantly reduced in HCC tissues of 52-week-old mice (Figure 4B). In the liver tissue of 7-day-old mice, the expression of HNF4A P2 was high, whereas that of HNF4A P1 was low (Figure 4C). In contrast, the liver tissue of 20-week-old adult mice exhibited low HNF4A P2 expression but high HNF4A P1 expression (Figure 4C). Treatment with PA at the DN stage in PDGF-C transgenic mice significantly increased the expression of HNF4A P1 in liver tissue, while simultaneously decreasing the expression of HNF4A P2 (Figures 4D and 4E). In DNs, treatment with PA significantly increased the mRNA expression of Alb, Ttr, Slco1a1, Atf7, Stat6, Otc1, and Pck1, which are markers of mature cells (Figure 4F). It also resulted in decreased mRNA expression levels of Afp, Vim, and Sall1, which are markers of undifferentiated cells (Figure 4G). However, treatment with PA did not affect the expression of these genes in the HCC stage. Furthermore, treatment with PA inhibited the p-AKT (Ser473)/total AKT and p-Erk1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) signaling pathways, which are involved in DN survival and proliferation and required for progression to HCC (Figure 4H). PA mediates the maturation of hepatocytes in vitro via HNF4A P1

As our results indicated that PA activates HNF4A in mouse DNs, we subsequently explored the direct impact of PA on hepatocytes. To this end, we assessed HNF4A activation in vitro in a cell line established by treating human operation specimens with collagenase and transplanting them into NOD-SCID mice. These DN-like primary HCC cells exhibited higher HNF4A P1 and P2 expressions than other cells, similar to the mouse DNs (Figure S1A). The human HCC cell line Huh7 also demonstrated a similar HNF4A expression pattern. PXB cells, which are human primary hepatocytes, expressed only HNF4A P1 and not P2. Moreover, HLF, a highly malignant and undifferentiated HCC cell line, did not express any isoform of HNF4A (Figure S1A).24 Treatment with PA in DN-like primary HCC cells increased HNF4A-driven transcriptional activity in a dose-dependent manner, as assessed using the luciferase assay (Figure S1B). This resulted in increased dose-dependent expression of TTR and SLCO1B3, which are downstream targets of HNF4A P1 (Figures S1C and S1D). In contrast, treatment with ATRA in DN-like primary HCC cells suppressed the expression of SLCO1B3, indicating that PA activates HNF4A, whereas ATRA does not. Treatment with PA also suppressed cell proliferation in DN-like primary HCC cells in a dose-dependent manner, with this effect being lost upon HNF4A knockdown (Figure S1E). It also upregulated the transcriptional activity of HNF4A through its DNA-binding site, DR1. However, when the DNA-binding site was mutated, this transcriptional activity was lost (Figures S1F and S1G). In DN-like primary HCC cells, HNF4A P1 expression was significantly dominant compared with HNF4A P2 (Figure S1H). Therefore, although P1-specific siRNA treatment reduced the significant amount of all HNF4A and P1, P2-specific siRNA treatment only reduced HNF4A P2 and did not affect the total amount of HNF4A. Consequently, knockdown of HNF4A P1 blocked the PA-mediated enhancement of HNF4A signaling, whereas knockdown of HNF4A P2 did not alter the PA response (Figures S1H–S1K). Moreover, treatment with PA in PHH cells expressing only HNF4A P1 resulted in a similar enhancement of HNF4A signaling activity, which was abolished by suppressing P1 expression (Figures S1L–S1N). PHH cells also exhibited increased HNF4A transcriptional activity through DR1 in response to PA treatment, whereas this activity was lost when the DR1 sequence was mutated (Figure S1O). We obtained similar findings with primary hepatocytes from TAM-inducible HNF4A-repressed mice, where PA enhanced HNF4A signaling only when HNF4A expression was high (Figures S1P–S1R). Collectively, these results suggest that PA might act specifically as a ligand for HNF4A P1, promoting the maturation and normalization of hepatocytes.

PA is the first discovered synthetic ligand functionally modulating HNF4A

The administration of PA significantly activated HNF4A signaling in HLF cells expressing HNF4A2 P1, but not in cells overexpressing HNF4A8, the P2 isoform (Figures 5A–5D). Overexpression of HNF4A P1 in HLF cells markedly enhanced PA-induced HNF4A signaling, as evidenced by increased H4 luciferase activity, suppression of cell proliferation, and upregulation of SLCO1B3, ALB, and TTR expression. Moreover, treatment with PA in HLF cells expressing HNF4A2 inhibited cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 5E). To clarify whether PA and HNF4A bind to each other, we performed a docking simulation. We found that PA fits into the hydrophobic pocket of the LBD in both open (docking score: −11.938 kcal/mol) and closed (docking score: −12.053 kcal/mol) conformations of HNF4A, interacting via a hydrogen bond/salt bridge to Thr181 and Arg226 (Figure 5F). Binding in vitro studies using recombinant HNF4A or RXRα and mass spectrometry confirmed that PA and LA preferentially bind to recombinant HNF4A rat than to RXRα (Figure 5G). We separately introduced three mutations into HNF4A (V255M, E285Q, and I314F) and found that only the V255M mutation disrupted PA binding to HNF4A, promoting conformational changes in the LBD of HNF4A in both open and closed conformations (Figures 5F and 5H).

We further investigated whether PA enhances the DNA-binding activity of HNF4A by binding to V255 using H4 and DR1 luc (Figures 5J and 5K). Our results revealed that PA significantly increased the DNA-binding activity of HNF4A2 wild-type (WT), whereas the DNA-binding activity remained low with HNF4A2 V255M. Notably, a mutation in the HNF4A2 binding sequence attenuated the DNA-binding activity induced by PA compared to the WT. These results indicate that PA enhances DNA binding of HNF4A.

PA specifically binds to HNF4A P1 and modulates transcriptional program

PA selectively activated HNF4A P1, increasing DNA element activity and downstream target gene expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 6A). In Huh7 human HCC cells, PA significantly increased HNF4A binding to its DNA element sites (Figure 6A), whereas supplementation with the endogenous ligand LA7 or retinoids, such as ATRA and 9-cis RA, exhibited no effect (Figure 6B). These results suggest that PA, but not LA or ATRA, exerts an agonistic effect on HNF4A. Although PA has long been recognized as a synthetic acyclic retinoid, our results indicate that PA is a ligand of HNF4A. Therefore, to further assess the functional role of V255, we evaluated the V255M mutant using the same experimental system (Figure 6C). Although the V255M mutant retained basal binding to the HBE, the PA-induced increase in DNA binding observed in the WT protein was abolished. To evaluate whether the effect of PA depends on vitamin A signaling or HNF4A, we knocked down the expression of HNF4A or RXRα in DN-like primary HCC cells and assessed cell proliferation. Undifferentiated HCC (HLE and HLF) and Huh7 cells exhibited similar expression of RXRα, RXRα (ΔN197), and RARα proteins, which are involved in vitamin A signaling (Figure 6D). HNF4A knockdown decreased RXRα expression, whereas RXRα knockdown had no significant effect on HNF4A expression, suggesting partial control of RXRα expression by HNF4A (Figure 6E). HNF4A silencing abolished the PA-induced reduction in cell proliferation, whereas RXRα knockdown had no significant impact (Figure 6F). Treatment with PA for 6–12 h led to a time-dependent increase in the expression of HNF4A P1, whereas HNF4A P2 expression tended to decrease at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures 6G and 6H). Consistently, the expression of ALB and TTR was also elevated in a time-dependent manner. Furthermore, as early as 12 h after PA treatment, H4-luciferase activity was markedly enhanced by PA (Figure 6I). These findings suggest that the antitumor effects of PA depend on HNF4A rather than RA signaling.

PA directly acts on HNF4A and activates the transcription of its target genes

Variants of HNF4A arise from differences in the A/B (N-terminal residues 1–38) and F domains (C-terminal residues 378–474) (Figure S2A). The ligand activity of PA induces transcriptional activation by binding to the 255th amino acid residue of HNF4A2. The nucleotide sequence of the E domain (LBD), which serves as the PA-binding site, is conserved across all HNF4A isoforms. To clarify the roles of the variable domains in PA-mediated transcription, we generated HNF4A2 mutants lacking either the A/B domain or the F domain and overexpressed them in HLF cells, which lack endogenous HNF4A expression (Figure S2B). Luciferase reporter assays using H4 and DR1 sequences revealed that deletion of the A/B domain did not abolish PA-induced transcription, whereas deletion of the F domain completely eliminated it (Figures S2C and S2D). Furthermore, HNF4A8, which shares the F domain with HNF4A2 but harbors a different A/B domain, exhibited markedly reduced transcriptional activity (Figure 5B). These results indicate that both the A/B and F domains are essential for PA-mediated transcriptional activation of HNF4A. Notably, the P1 isoform, which contains the AF-1 transactivation motif,25,26,27,28 may enhance the function of the F domain, whereas the P2 isoform may reciprocally suppress F-domain-mediated transcriptional activity.

We subsequently treated DN-like primary HCC cells with PA and analyzed transcriptional changes using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). A heatmap of the top 100 differentially expressed genes (p < 0.05) is presented in Figure S3A (Table S4). Several malignancy-associated genes, including MYCN, DKK1, FOXC2, ITGA6, and TGFB2, were significantly downregulated in response to treatment with PA. Gene Ontology analysis revealed enrichment of vitamin-A-related biological processes, consistent with the fact that PA is a vitamin A derivative (Figure S3B). Pathways related to cholesterol metabolism, fatty acid transport, and stem cell differentiation were also significantly enriched. Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis with an anti-HNF4A antibody, performed 12 h after treatment with PA, revealed that the total number of HNF4A genomic binding sites was comparable between PA-treated and control cells (Figure 6J; Table S5). However, treatment with PA induced a redistribution of HNF4A-binding sites to distinct genomic regions. Pathway enrichment analysis based on integrated RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data revealed that PA acts through HNF4A to activate the transcription of estrogen-responsive genes, while repressing genes associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), KRAS signaling, interleukin (IL)-2/STAT5 signaling, and inflammatory responses, as shown in the Hallmark pathway analysis (Figure 6K). In addition, Reactome pathway analysis suggested activation of interleukin- and cytokine-mediated immune responses and potential suppression of metabolic pathways (Figure S3C). Collectively, these findings suggest that PA-mediated activation of HNF4A relies on coordinated regulation between the A/B and F domains, where the P1 isoform supports and the P2 isoform dampens F domain activity. Through this regulation, PA reinforces HNF4A-driven transcription to preserve hepatocytic differentiation and inhibit oncogenic and inflammatory signaling.

Suppression of HNF4A P1 and P2 expression alters signaling in hepatocytes

Transcriptome analysis following siRNA-mediated knockdown of HNF4A P1 and P2 in PXB cells and DN-like primary HCC cells revealed distinct gene expression profiles (Figures S4A and S4B). A heatmap analysis highlighted 3,573 differentially expressed genes (Figure S4C; Table S6). Genes involved in ribosome, immune, and metabolic cascades were significantly different between PXB cells and DN-like primary HCC cells (Figure S4D). In PXB cells, HNF4A P1 suppression primarily affected inflammatory pathways and nuclear receptor regulation (Figure S4E). In DN-like primary HCC cells, gene expression profiles showed significant differences compared with those in PXB, and the gene expression cascade was similar to that illustrated in Figure S4F. PXB cells expressed only HNF4A P1 at high levels, whereas DN-like primary HCC cells expressed both HNF4A P1 and P2 at high levels. Moreover, the knockdown of HNF4A P2 significantly altered the focal adhesion pathway in DN-like primary HCC cell types (Figures S4E and S4F). In vitro RNA-seq data identified ATF7 and STAT6 as new candidates for HNF4A P1, in addition to OTC1 and PCK1, which were previously reported.29 The analysis also revealed VIM and SALL1 as new candidates downstream of P2 (Figures 4F, 4G, and S4F). In PDGF-C Tg mice treated with PA during the DN stage, the expression of ATF7, STAT6, OTC1, and PCK1 was upregulated, whereas that of VIM and SALL1 was downregulated. However, the administration of PA to HCC eliminated these changes in gene expression. Overall, the changes in gene expression induced by siRNA indicate that the physiological effects of HNF4A are likely influenced by the relative levels of HNF4A P1 and P2.

Hnf4a knockdown suppresses the inhibitory effect of PA on tumor development

In both humans and mice, the expression of HNF4A P1 and P2 is diminished during the progression from DNs to HCC. This phenomenon nullifies the anticancer effect of PA as the malignancy of HCC increases. PA reactivates HNF4A P1 signaling and suppresses P2 function, which is compromised during the DN stage. We hypothesized that silencing both HNF4A P1 and P2 would reduce the physiological effects of PA. To test this, we used LNP-siRNA targeting total Hnf4a expression and administered it to PDGF-C Tg mice at 32 weeks of age, followed by 8 weeks of oral treatment with PA to examine the effect on DNs (Figure 7A). Analysis of Hnf4a mRNA revealed that transfection with siRNA targeting Hnf4a reduced its expression levels by over 50% in the liver of PDGF-C Tg mice compared with siRNA control (Figure 7D). Under an identical treatment schedule, knockdown of Hnf4a expression maintained approximately 50% suppression in the liver irrespective of PA administration (Figure 7E) and accelerated HCC development at 40 weeks. These findings suggest that HNF4A is a critical molecular target of PA in inhibiting the progression of DNs to HCC.

Genetic loss of endogenous HNF4A exacerbates hepatocellular carcinoma in PDGF-C Tg mice

The suppression of Hnf4a expression by siRNA counteracted the effects of PA and enhanced the malignancy of HCC. To further explore this aspect, we generated PDGF-C Tg/Alb-creERT2/Hnf4a flox/flox homozygous mice with a genetic deletion of endogenous Hnf4a (Figures S5A and S5B). Upon intraperitoneal administration of tamoxifen (TAM), mice died within approximately 20 days (Figure S5C). Histological analysis confirmed that the liver tissue exhibited extensive necrosis consistent with liver failure (Figure S5D), suggesting that the expression of HNF4A is essential for maintaining liver homeostasis.

In a parallel experiment, we transitioned from a genetic homozygous deletion to a heterozygous deletion. Unlike homozygous mice, these mice survived TAM administration, allowing observation for 2 months (Figure S5E). TAM-treated mice exhibited an approximately 70% reduction in HNF4A expression compared with those without TAM treatment (Figure S5F). Liver tissue from TAM-treated mice demonstrated increased tumor size and significantly increased weight (Figure S5G). Analysis of tumors revealed increased expression of Afp, indicative of immaturity of hepatocytes, and decreased expression of the maturation markers Alb, Ttr, and Slco1a1 (Figure S5H). Furthermore, genetic suppression of HNF4A inhibited the nuclear expression of HNF4A P1 and the expression and localization of OATP1 to the cell membrane, while promoting an increase in the number of Ki67-positive hepatoma cells, indicative of cell proliferation (Figure S5I). These findings highlight the importance of the expression of HNF4A in hepatocytes as a critical mechanism for hepatocyte function and maturation, with the potential to suppress hepatocarcinogenesis from DNs to HCC.

Discussion

Several risk factors, including hepatitis B and C virus infection, alcohol consumption, diabetes, and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, contribute to the development of HCC.30 Individuals with risk factors are recommended to undergo regular hepatic surveillance through ultrasonography and serum AFP measurement to enable the diagnosis of HCC at early stages.31 In addition to established biomarkers, such as liver fibrosis markers, detectable DNs through imaging,32 and elevated serum AFP levels, we recently identified laminin γ2 monomer as a novel serum biomarker, representing a strong risk factor for the development of HCC.33,34 Despite the identification of these risk factors, no therapeutic options are currently available to prevent the development of HCC in high-risk patients, owing to the lack of effective prophylactic treatments. In the present study, we identified PA as the first HNF4α agonist and used it as a chemical probe to demonstrate that HNF4A activation contributes to the prevention of HCC development, even after DN formation. Our study highlights the critical need to establish appropriate eligibility criteria for HCC prevention trials involving PA, as its molecular target, HNF4A, is already lost in 40% of advanced HCC cases,22 rendering it unlikely to effectively inhibit tumor proliferation in these patients.

Re-analysis of a phase II/III clinical trial, alongside our preclinical findings, suggests that treatment with PA should be considered for patients with a high risk for HCC development without prior history of the disease. These patients include individuals with liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, or those in early stages of carcinogenesis, such as when DNs are detected, often due to hepatitis infections, alcohol abuse, or lifestyle-related liver diseases. Future clinical studies are required to evaluate the effect of PA on the prevention of HCC in these high-risk patients.

Although this retrospective clinical analysis relied on contrast-enhanced CT, our preclinical data indicated that Gd-EOB-DTPA MRI enables non-invasive monitoring of OATP1B3 downregulation during the transition from DNs to HCC. These findings suggest the potential of EOB-MRI as a non-invasive imaging biomarker for premalignant-to-malignant progression and its potential utility in prospective clinical trials evaluating chemopreventive or recurrence-preventive agents such as PA. Furthermore, as loss of HNF4A expression accompanies the progression of DNs into HCC, additional preclinical studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms underlying the inhibition and possible reactivation of HNF4α expression.

HNF4A is an important nuclear receptor involved in the regulation of lipid, carbohydrate, and drug metabolism and a determinant for liver regeneration and carcinogenesis.6,8,9,35 Potential ligands that bind to HNF4A have been extensively explored, and only fatty acids, such as LA, have been identified as endogenous ligands.7,8,9,10 However, in the present study, in vitro supplementation with LA exhibited little effect on HNF4A activation. This suggests that while ligand binding may be necessary, it may not be sufficient to activate HNF4A. In addition, HNF4A has often been regarded as constitutively active, raising questions about its potential as a drug target. Despite its critical role in liver homeostasis, no synthetic agonists have been identified to date.5

Our findings demonstrate that PA is the first synthetic ligand capable of binding to and activating HNF4A. We confirmed this interaction through docking simulations, recombinant protein binding assays, transcriptional activity reporter assays, target gene expression, and direct detection of HNF4A bound to its DNA-binding element. HNF4A has 12 splicing isoforms, which can be roughly divided into two subtypes, P1 and P2.23 PA activates HNF4A signaling by binding to HNF4A P1 to promote the maturation and normalization of hepatocytes, while simultaneously suppressing the expression and signaling of HNF4A P2, which is linked to immaturity of hepatocytes. This dual mechanism is important as a physiological mechanism through which PA suppresses DNs, suggesting that imbalances in the expression of HNF4A P1 and P2 may accelerate the multistep process of HCC.

In poorly differentiated HCC, HNF4A expression almost disappears.36 However, it remains unclear why PA, but not LA, robustly activated the HNF4A transcriptional program, warranting further investigation. In the present study, we demonstrated that PA selectively activates the HNF4A P1 isoform while suppressing P2-dependent transcription. Despite sharing a common LBD, HNF4A P1 and P2 exhibit opposite transcriptional responses. This suggests that the isoform specificity of this isoform is not determined solely by the LBD but rather by isoform-specific regulatory mechanisms involving other domains, such as the A/B and F domains.23,25,37 The balance between AF-1-dependent cofactor recruitment mediated by the A/B domain and transcriptional repression mediated by the F domain may contribute to the distinct transcriptional outputs of P1 and P2. Nuclear receptor transcriptional activity is regulated by co-activators, such as p300/CBP and SRC family members (NCOA1, NCOA2, and NCOA3), and co-repressors, including NCoR (NCOR1) and SMRT (NCOR2); differences in interactions with these cofactors may contribute to the differential regulation of P1 and P2.38,39 In addition, HNF4A P1 and P2 promoter activity is regulated by liver-enriched transcription factors, such as HNF1A and FOXA2.40 However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the differential regulation of P1 and P2 remain unclear and require further investigation using domain-mutant analyses, cofactor interaction studies, and structural approaches.

In the present study, the effect of PA on HNF4A was sustained in the presence of endogenous fatty acids. This finding suggests that nutrients, metabolites, and fatty acids are required to maintain the physiological activity of HNF4A P1 and that maintaining high HNF4A P1 expression and sustaining PA-mediated downstream signaling may be regulated by distinct physiological pathways. Nevertheless, additional studies are required to explore the molecular mechanisms through which PA influences HNF4A and other nuclear receptors.41 A detailed analysis of the structure-activity relationship of PA-related compounds may inform the development of more potent synthetic agonists of HNF4A for therapeutic use in the future.

Materials and methods

Reagents

PA was acquired from Kowa Company Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). LA, all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and 9-cis retinoic acid (9c-RA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Cell culture

The HCC cell lines Huh7, HLE, and HLF were obtained from the JCRB Cell Bank. DN-like HCC cells were established from primary HCC tissues exhibiting Gd-EOB-DTPA uptake at the hepatobiliary (HB) stage, as previously described.18,42 These DN-like HCC cells were confirmed to be p53 WT. Mouse primary hepatocytes were isolated from liver tissues of 8-week-old Alb-creERT2/HNF4A flox/flox mice through collagenase perfusion and Percoll density gradient. All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% FBS. Human primary hepatocytes, PXB cells, and the specialized culture medium were purchased from Phenix Bio (Hiroshima, Japan).

Mouse model

All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Kanazawa University, Takara-machi Campus. The present study adhered to the ARRIVE Guideline 2.0. PDGF-C Tg mice were generated by transfecting fertilized eggs with DNA containing the human PDGF-C open reading frame (ORF) and human growth hormone (hGH) poly(A) signal downstream of the albumin promoter (the Laboratory Animal Resource Center, University of Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan). Alb-creERT2 mice were kindly provided by Professor Pierre Chambon (University of Strasbourg), and HNF4A flox/flox mice were kindly provided by Professor Frank Gonzalez (NIH/NCI). Alb-creERT2/hPDGF-C Tg/Hnf4a homozygous and heterozygous floxed mice or Alb-creERT2/Hnf4a homozygous floxed mice were generated by pairing mice.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using a 0.4 tesla MRI system (Hitachi Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) with a 3D gradient-echo T1-weighted sequence (TR/TE = 66.5/4.0 ms, flip angle 40°, field of view 10 cm, matrix 224 × 192, slice thickness 1.0 mm) at the indicated age to assess liver carcinogenesis. For dynamic imaging, a thin catheter was generated and surgically placed in the internal jugular vein of mice. A dose of 0.025 mmol/kg Gd-EOB-DTPA (Bayer Schering Pharma, Berlin, Germany) was injected via the catheter, and arterial phase images were immediately acquired. For HB phase imaging, 0.025 mmol/kg Gd-EOB-DTPA was injected into the tail vein, with images obtained 12–20 min after injection. Mice presenting liver nodules on HB images were used for subsequent studies. PA was dissolved in corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich) and administered orally (80 mg/kg/day) 5 days per week for 8 weeks. The human equivalent dose (HED) was calculated using body surface area normalization. The mouse dose of 80 mg/kg/day was converted to the human equivalent dose using Km factors (mouse = 3, human = 37), resulting in an HED of 6.49 mg/kg/day, which corresponds to approximately 390–450 mg/day for a 60–70 kg adult. Tumor size was calculated from MRI images using the formula: L × W × H/2. In vivo delivery of siRNAs (50 μg/mouse) targeting HNF4A or control was performed using Invivofectamine 3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) through the tail vein every 2 weeks. The Hnf4a-specific siRNA sequence (s67634; Ambion, Waltham, MA, USA) was selected for its effectiveness in silencing expression in vivo. WT mice on a C57BL/6J background, PDGF-C Tg mice, and Alb-creERT2/hPDGF-C Tg/Hnf4a floxed mice were housed in pathogen-free animal housing facilities under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. Male mice were utilized for animal experiments.

Gene expression modulation

Stable HNF4A knockdown in DN-like HCC cells was achieved using pGFP-V-RS vectors (TF320382; OriGene Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA), as previously described.42 Briefly, plasmids targeting HNF4A2 (OHu25290) and HNF4A8 (OHu25149) were purchased from GenScript Biotech (Piscataway, NJ, USA). PCR primers for human HNF4A2 Forward: CGGTGAATTCCTCGAGCCGCCATGCGACTCTCC, human HNF4A8 Forward: CGGTGAATTCCTCGAGCCGCCATGTCGGACTGG, and HNF4A Common Reverse: CGGTAGAATTGGATCCTACTTGTCATCGTCAT were used to amplify flagged ORF DNA. The resulting DNA was ligated into Xho/BamH1-cleaved pLenti X-puro vector using In-Fusion cloning (TAKARA Bio, Shiga, Japan) and propagated in Escherichia coli. Lentiviral High Titer Packaging Mix (TAKARA Bio) was used for viral production. Transient knockdown of RXRA was achieved using Silencer Select siRNA (s12384; Ambion) and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Human HNF4A P1-specific siRNA was generated with the following sequences: forward: AGAATGTGCAGGTGTTGACG and reverse: CTCGAGGCACCGTAGTGTTT by JbioS (Saitama, Japan). Human HNF4A P2-specific siRNA was generated with forward: GGCCATGGTCAGCGTGAACG and reverse: GCCCTTGCAGCCGTCACAGC by JbioS. Control siRNA (SI03650318) was obtained from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany).

Site-directed mutagenesis

The HNF4A2 vector was PCR-amplified using primers (forward 5′- TAACTCGAGGCCGCCATGCGACTCTCCAAAACCCT-3′ and reverse 5′- AATGGATCCCTACTTGTCATCGTCATCCTTGTAGTCGATAACTTCCTGCTTGGTGAT-3′). Inserts were digested and introduced into the cloning site of pcDNA3.1 (−) using Xho1 and BamH1 digestion to generate pcDNA3.1(−)-HNF4A2. V255M, E285Q, I314F, ΔN, and ΔC mutations were introduced using the PrimeSTAR Mutagensis Basal Kit (TAKARA Bio) with the following primers: V255M forward 5′-ATGAGCCGGATGTCCATACGCATCCTTGAC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCGTATGGACATCCGGCTCATCTCCGCC-3′; E285Q forward 5′-GATGACAATCAGTATGCCTACCTCAAAGCC-3′ and reverse 5′-GTAGGCATACTGATTGTCATCGATCTGCAG-3′; I314F forward 5′-GAGGACTACTTCAACGACCGCCAGTATGAC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCGGTCGTTGAAGTAGTCCTCCAAGCTCAC-3′; ΔN forward 5′-GCCACCATGACGTCCCCATCAGAAGGCA-3′ and reverse 5′-TGGGGACGTCATGGTGGCGGATCCGAGCT-3′, and ΔC forward 5′-AGGGTCCGATTACAAGGATGACGACGA-3′ and reverse 5′-TGTAATCGGACCCTCCCAGCAGCATCT-3′.

Microarray analysis

Gene expression profiles were obtained using the GeneChip Mouse Genome 430 2.0 Array (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA). PDGF-C Tg mice were treated with vehicle (soybean oil) or PA for 8 weeks. Tumor and non-tumor liver tissues from mice at 40 or 60 weeks were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Class comparison analysis was conducted using univariate t tests and random permutation test using the BRB-Array Tools v.4.5.0. Heatmaps were generated with Genesis v.1.7.7 software. Transcription factor analysis was performed using MeraCore software (http://www.genego.com).

RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells transfected with HNF4A P1 or P2 siRNA and from PA-treated cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA library preparation for siRNA-treated samples was performed at Riken Genesis (Tokyo, Japan) using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, CA, USA). RNA library preparation for PA-treated samples was carried out at Rhelixa (Tokyo, Japan) using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). The resulting libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA). RNA-seq reads were mapped to the human reference genome (hg38) using TopHat v.2.1.1 (Center for Computational Biology, Johns Hopkins University). Transcript assembly and expression quantification were performed with Cufflinks v.2.2.1 and Cuffquant v.2.2.1 (Johns Hopkins University). Hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization were conducted using the R package Heatplus (v.2.34.0; Bioconductor Project). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using iPathway Guide (Advaita Bioinformatics, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). The results obtained from RNA-seq and ChIP-seq were subsequently integrated.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC was performed using the Envision+ kit (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA, USA). Mouse monoclonal anti-human Ki-67 (DAKO); mouse monoclonal anti-human organic anion transporter 1B3 (OATP1B3) MDQ/5F260 (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO); rabbit monoclonal anti-human HNF4A (clone C11F12, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); an antibody detecting all splicing variants of HNF4A (HNF4A All) mouse monoclonal anti-HNF4A P1 K9218 (Perseus Proteomics Inc., Tokyo, Japan); goat polyclonal anti-human alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) C-19 (Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA); and mouse monoclonal anti-CD31 JC/70A (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) antibodies were used to detect Ki-67, OATP1B3, HNF4A, AFP, and CD31, respectively.

Whole-exome sequencing

DNA was extracted from frozen tissues using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen). Whole-exome capture was performed using the SureSelect Mouse All Exon Kit (Agilent Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The HiSeq 2500 Sequencing System (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for massive parallel sequencing. Sequence reads were mapped against the mm10 reference genome (University of California, Santa Cruz Genome Browser [http://hgdownload.cse.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/0010/chromosomes/]). Sequence variations, including single-nucleotide polymorphisms and insertions/deletions, were identified using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v.3.8; Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA). Genes with variant allele frequencies exceeding 0.1 were considered mutated. Whole-exome sequencing and data analysis were performed by Riken Genesis Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis

Total RNA was extracted using High Pure RNA Isolation Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Gene expression of selected genes was determined in triplicate using the 7900 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and the results were normalized relative to ACTB expression. Specific TaqMan probes for human RXRα, OATP1B3, transthyretin (TTR), and ALB and mouse Slco1a1, Ttr, Alb, and Afp were purchased from Applied Biosystems. The primers were as follows: specific TaqMan probe for human HNF4A P1, forward: AGAATGTGCAGGTGTTGACG, reverse: CTCGAGGCACCGTAGTGTTT, probe: TGGGCAATGACACGTC-FAM and for human HNF4A P2, forward: GGCCATGGTCAGCGTGAACG, reverse: GCCCTTGCAGCCGTCACAGC, probe: TTCTTACGACACGTCCC-FAM. The TaqMan probe for mouse Hnf4a P1 was forward: ATGGGCAATGACACGTCC, reverse: CTCACGCTCCTCCTGAAGAA, probe: CAACAGCCTGGGCG-FAM and for mice Hnf4a P2, forward: GCGAGTCCTTATGCCCTCA, reverse: TGAATTGAGGTTGGCACCTT, probe: ACGTGTCTGATGTGATCT-FAM.

Immunoblots

Whole-cell lysates were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA; Millipore, Burlington, MA) lysis buffer, as previously described.42 Nuclear extracts were prepared using NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Antibodies used included anti-HNF4A P1 K9218 (Perseus Proteomics Inc., Tokyo, Japan), anti-HNF4A P2 H6939 (Perseus Proteomics Inc.), anti-HNF4A All H1415 (Perseus Proteomics Inc.), anti RXRα (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-RXRα ΔN197 (Santa Cruz), anti-RARα C-20 (Santa Cruz), anti-Lamin A/C (Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-ACTB (Santa Cruz). Immune complexes were visualized using ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Amersham Biosciences, Little Chalfont, UK). Western blot band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (v.1.53).

Luciferase assay

Human and mouse HCC cell lines and normal liver cells were transfected with HNF4-responsive firefly luciferase (H4-luciferase) and constitutively expressed Renilla luciferase, both of which are contained in the Cignal HNF4 Reporter (luc) Kit (SABiosciences, Frederick, MD, USA). The plasmid containing the HNF4A DNA binding site (D, GSE25021) in pGL4.23 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was amplified using the following forward and reverse primers: AGGTCAAAGTCACACTCGAGGATATCAAGATCTGGGCCT and ACTTTGACCTCTGGCTAGCGAGCTCAGGTACCGGGCCA, respectively. Amplification was performed with PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase (TAKARA Bio), and the product was circularized via self-ligation. Mutant DR1 was generated using the following forward and reverse primers: AGGTAACATTCACACTCGAGGATATCAAGATCTGGGCCT and AATGTTACCTCTGGCTAGCGAGCTCAGGTACCGGCCA, respectively. pRL-SV40 (Promega) was used as a control. PXB cells and other cell lines were transfected with firefly luciferase reporter and Renilla luciferase control plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, cells were treated with PA for 24 h, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 103/well in 96-well plates, and cell density was measured in quadruplicate after 72 h using Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitation of HNF4A bound to hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 binding elements

HNF4α bound to the hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 binding elements (HBEs) was measured using the TransAM HNF Family kit (Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, nuclear extracts (10 μg) were incubated in a 96-well chamber coated with oligonucleotides coding HBEs. HNF4A was detected using an anti-HNF4A antibody and HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG), allowing the colorimetric visualization of the binding of HNF4A to HBEs. Bound HNF4A was measured in triplicate for each experiment.

Quantitation of PA and LA from recombinant proteins in vitro

HLF cells were transfected with pCMV6-HNF4A2 plasmid OHu25290 (GenScript Biotech) or pCMV6-RXRα plasmid RC210311 (OriGene Technologies) and immunoprecipitated using anti-DYKDDDDK Tag (M2) monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) and Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each recombinant protein (1 mg/experiment) bound to the beads was incubated with LA (100 μM) or PA (100 μM) for 30 min, followed by five washes with phosphate-buffered saline. Proteins were eluted in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline at 65°C for 30 min. Lipids attached to the eluted protein in the supernatant were transferred to new tubes, extracted by performing three washes with 300 μL of hexane, dried under N2 gas (organic fractions), and derivatized with methanol. They were subsequently analyzed using a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with electrospray ionization (LCMS-8040; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled to a liquid chromatography system (Nexera, Shimadzu, Japan).

Analysis of HCC recurrence in a clinical trial

Contrast-enhanced CT scan data were collected from patients enrolled in a phase II/III randomized placebo-controlled study conducted from 2005 to 2009.13 MRI data were not available, as Gd-EOB-DTPA was only introduced in Japan in 2009. Eligibility criteria have been previously described.13 Patients underwent curative surgery or radiofrequency ablation for HCC before enrollment, with CT scans performed every 3 months after enrollment. An independent, blinded imaging committee evaluated HCC recurrence based on multiphasic contrast CT. Recurrence was assessed independently by two abdominal imaging radiologists (O.M. and S.K., with 26 and 43 years of experience, respectively), without information on clinical and pathologic results. Among 116 patients treated with 600 mg/day PA and 113 patients with placebo, five patients who received PA and three patients who received placebo were excluded owing to short follow-up periods (<3 months). Local recurrence of initially treated HCC was diagnosed in 10 PA and 13 placebo patients. HCC recurrence from DNs was diagnosed in 18 placebo and 7 PA patients. De novo HCC recurrence was identified in 24 PA and 21 placebo patients based on CT data revealing no prior DNs.

Docking simulation of PA

The X-ray structure of the ligand-binding domain (LBD) of mouse HNF4A with two conformational states within the homodimer (an open and a closed form; PDB ID: 1M7W)9 was refined for docking simulations using the Protein Preparation Wizard Script of Maestro (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, USA). PA ionization and energy minimization were performed with the OPLS3 force field in the LigPrep Script in the Maestro. Docking simulations were conducted using the Glide XP docking program with default parameters (Schrödinger, LLC).19,20 The grid center for docking was defined using the reference position of lauric acid on 1M7W.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing

ChIP-seq analysis was performed using the ChIP-IT High Sensitivity Kit (Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol for fixation, chromatin conditioning, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and purification. Cellular DNA was fragmented using the EpiShear Probe Sonicator (Active Motif). The antibody used for ChIP was anti-HNF4A All H1415 (Perseus Proteomics Inc.). The library-derived DNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus to 40.0 M reads. Signal aggregation plots and signal heat maps were generated for the regions obtained from the extraction of variable peak regions using edgeR (v.3.26.8), with visualization performed using the software deeptools (v.3.2.1).

Statistical analysis

Paired t tests were used to assess tumor volume changes before and after PA treatment within groups. Unpaired t tests were applied to compare differences between two groups for luciferase data, gene expression data, and cell proliferation data. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with the log rank test was used to compare HCC recurrence. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software v.10 (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The hazard ratio of PA treatment versus placebo was calculated with an univariate Cox proportional hazards model using SPSS software (v.29).
