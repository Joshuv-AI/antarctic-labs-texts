Establishing the UK clubfoot research priorities
Authors: Amanda Trees, Emily Jane Baird, Dawn Lockey, Christine Douglas, Elizabeth Ashby, Donald M. Campbell, Yael Gelfer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsClubfoot is the most common congenital foot deformity. Although the overall effectiveness of the Ponseti method is well established, it has traditionally been taught as a unified doctrine, and individual components, particularly those related to the maintenance of correction, have not been evaluated with the same level of rigour. Consequently, high-quality evidence to support clinical decision-making remains limited. This study used a consensus-based approach to identify research priorities for clinical trials in clubfoot management.MethodsThis study followed a previously published protocol. A modified Delphi technique was used, which involved an initial scoping survey, a two-round modified Delphi consensus survey, and consensus meetings that included the UK Clubfoot Network Research Committee. The survey was distributed among key stakeholders in the management of clubfoot as well as patients and carers in the UK. Reporting followed the ACCORD checklist.ResultsA total of 103 and 93 participants contributed to rounds one and two of the Delphi process, respectively, scoring research priorities from one (low priority) to five (high priority). Stakeholders identified nine priorities in research across four themes of Primary Treatment, Bracing, Relapse, and Physiotherapy. The highest scoring themes were Relapse and Bracing, and the highest scoring priority was regarding the effectiveness of unilateral versus bilateral bracing.ConclusionIdentification of research priorities in clubfoot through this study will guide multicentre clinical trials to improve evidence-based clubfoot treatment.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(7):845–851.

Introduction

Clubfoot is the most common congenital limb deformity, with an estimated incidence of one to two in 1,000 live births.1-3 Clubfoot can present with varying degrees of severity and has multifactorial aetiology, including genetic predisposition, aberrant embryological signalling pathways, vascular dysgenesis, and environmental influences.4 The goal of treatment of clubfoot is to provide long-term correction of foot position to optimize function and eliminate pain. The Ponseti method of correction is the gold standard for treating clubfoot and is used worldwide.5 Ponseti treatment involves a series of foot manipulations and above-knee casting, followed by Achilles tenotomy and maintenance of the corrected foot position using a foot abduction brace. Consensus guidelines for the treatment of clubfoot in the UK were published in 2022.6 They standardize the management of clubfoot up to walking age. However, variation persists in other aspects of clubfoot management,7-10 including delivery of antenatal counselling, bracing protocols, and management of recurrence of deformity. Furthermore, success of the Ponseti method is heavily reliant on engagement of caregivers, particularly during the maintenance phase of treatment.

The Ponseti method has traditionally been taught and implemented as a complete, unified doctrine, where its overall effectiveness has been well established. However individual components, particularly those relating to maintenance of correction following initial success, have not been subjected to the same level of rigorous evaluation. Few studies have captured the opinions of the caregivers along the Ponseti journey. The 2019 James Lind Alliance Priority-Setting Partnership agreement with the British Society of Children’s Orthopaedic Surgeons (BSCOS) in Children’s Lower Limb Surgery highlighted variations in practice, geographical variation, and lack of good evidence to guide clinical decision-making.11

One of the challenges to undertaking clinical research is access to funding. Similarly, the challenge faced by funding institutions is how to identify the most important unanswered clinical questions.

Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT)12 recommended the creation of the UK Clubfoot Network (UKCN). A research subcommittee within UKCN was formed with the primary aim to establish research priorities in clubfoot.

Consensus methods offer a systematic approach to synthesizing information from multiple stakeholders, including patients and carers. The Delphi technique is a structured process using ‘rounds’ of questionnaires to gather opinion and ultimately, reach consensus.13

This paper aims to describe the process undertaken and reports the identified priorities for future research in clubfoot in the UK.

Methods

The consensus study protocol was published in September 2025,14 including the methodology and timeline. The study followed a four-stage process: 1) establishment of a steering group; 2) steering group meetings, which generated statements with input of multiple stakeholders, patients, and carers and checking these against the published literature; 3) a two-round modified Delphi survey; and 4) a final consensus meeting in which participants were asked to rate each statement using a likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1 indicating lowest priority and 5 indicating highest priority). An additional option of not applicable (N/A) was provided for each statement. The consensus criteria are presented in Table I.

Selection of steering group and panellists

Members of the steering group were recruited from the UKCN research committee, consisting of consultant paediatric orthopaedic surgeons and physiotherapists with interest and expertise in clubfoot management (AT, EJB, DMC, DL, CD, EA, and YG). The steering group meetings were led by a chair (YG) who was selected by the UKCN. None of the steering committee had any competing interests.

The recruitment process

The modified Delphi survey was open to all members of the UKCN, BSCOS members with interest and expertise in clubfoot treatment, and a cohort of parents/carers of children with clubfoot. Parents/carers were recruited from clubfoot clinics across the UK. The number and distribution of the participants in the Delphi survey are presented in Table II. An invitation and link to an individual online survey (JISC Surveys, UK) was distributed to all participants via email.

Delphi survey respondent background.

The initial steering group meeting collated a list of statements related to gaps in knowledge requiring further research. As per the James Lind Alliance guidelines,15 interviews with parents, patients, carers, and other stakeholders informed the generation of statements. Finally, further research priority statements were suggested by the research committee members for consideration. The statements were divided into themes: Diagnosis, Primary Treatment, Cost, Carbon Footprint, Bracing, Relapse, Nonidiopathic Clubfoot, Physiotherapy, Sport and Physical Activity, Basic Science, and Training.

Participants had three weeks to complete each round of the survey and were given the opportunity to add further statements that they considered important. Reminder emails were sent to all participants who did not respond initially. Data were collated and analyzed and the results for each statement were presented as an overall score (median and IQR) and subdivided by stakeholder group: ‘consultant/fellows’, ‘physiotherapists and orthotists’, and ‘parents/guardians and patients’.

In the steering group meeting between the two rounds of the survey, the anonymized results from round one were discussed and processed. Statements were deemed ‘consensus in’ if they achieved a median score of 5 (automatically through) or 4, with an IQR of 1 or 2 (low variability). If one stakeholder group felt the statement was of high priority, consideration was given to statements that reached a median score of 4, with higher variability or 3 with low variability. Graphical representation of responses from round one accompanied the survey for round two, and an indication of any new statements. The final consensus meeting followed round two of the Delphi survey. Statements that achieved consensus were approved. Statements that did not reach consensus were discussed in depth to determine whether they had merit for inclusion or amalgamation with statements of similar themes.

Consultation with institutional research and development offices deemed that this study did not require ethical approval. The study followed the reporting of the Accurate Consensus Reporting Document (ACCORD) checklist.16

Results

Four steering group meetings were held throughout the consensus process. Two meetings were held prior to the first round of the Delphi. One was held between round one and two, and the final meeting after round two. During the initial sessions, ten research themes were proposed and accepted. The generated statements were allocated to those themes. The third meeting took place following Delphi round one. In this meeting, statements that achieved ‘consensus in’ were approved. Statements that were ‘consensus out’ were discussed and the individual stakeholder scores analyzed in greater details for consideration of inclusion in round two. Additional proposed statements from round one were discussed for quality and inclusion. The final meeting took place after the second round of the Delphi. The statements to be included in the final consensus were determined and finalized.

Round one Delphi survey

In total, 103 participants completed round one of the survey. A total of 32 statements were included in round one, allocated across ten themes: Diagnosis, Primary Treatment, Cost, Bracing, Relapse, Nonidiopathic Clubfoot, Physiotherapy, Sports and Physical Activity, Basic Science, and Training (Appendix 1). Overall,24 statements reached ‘consensus in’ and were included in round two (median scores of 5 or 4 with low variability). Eight statements did not reach ‘consensus in’ and were rejected after discussion among the steering group, as per protocol. An additional six statements, proposed by participants of round one, were considered worthy of inclusion in round two. A total of 30 statements were then progressed to round two. The final consensus meeting was held virtually, and all participants were able to review the scores of the outcomes from the survey prior to the meeting. A group discussion was undertaken and each outcome considered in turn. Each of the remaining 23 statements and their scores were considered individually and voted on. The final consensus identified nine research priorities across four key themes: Primary Treatment, Bracing, Relapse, and Physiotherapy. Within Primary Treatment, the highest priority related to understanding challenges during Ponseti casting and barriers to adherence from the family perspective. The Bracing theme contained the greatest number of priorities, reflecting ongoing uncertainty, including the comparative effectiveness of unilateral versus bilateral bracing and the optimal bracing regimen in terms of duration and daily wear. Within Relapse, priorities focused on identifying patients at risk of recurrence and determining optimal management strategies at different ages, including the timing of tibialis anterior tendon transfer. The Physiotherapy theme highlighted the need to evaluate interventions that maintain correction and prevent relapse throughout childhood (Table IV).

Discussion

This is the first study to establish clinical research priorities in clubfoot management. Opinions were sought from a broad range of stakeholders: paediatric orthopaedic surgeons, allied health professionals (AHPs), and parents/carers of children with clubfoot. Stakeholders were spread across the UK and can therefore be assumed to be representative of national opinion. The top priorities reflect gaps in knowledge in the literature, and areas where there is no clear pathway for clinical decision-making.

An initial set of themes were refined through prioritization, with four themes deemed as most important: Primary Treatment, Bracing, Relapse, and Physiotherapy.

The primary treatment pathway of the Ponseti method is well established and has previously been described by consensus opinion in the UK.6 However, challenges during the casting process and barriers to adherence with bracing were determined as high-priority across all stakeholders, but of particular importance to AHPs. Qualitative research exploring the family perspective during the initial casting period, as well as the challenges associated with adherence to a bracing regimen, were identified as priorities within the primary correction theme. Addressing these topics may help identify strategies to overcome barriers and ultimately improve outcomes.

A ‘relapse or recurrence’ is defined as “Any change in the clinical presentation of the fully corrected foot (following initial management) that requires further treatment.”6,8 Foot abduction brace use is a principal factor linked to deformity recurrence, yet the bracing regime has not been systematically investigated. Consequently, the effects of reduced wear time and the minimum effective threshold of use are not established. Multiple systematic reviews, and single-centre studies have studied compliance with the foot abduction brace in relation to recurrence of deformity.17-24 Longitudinal studies have demonstrated that reduced evertor activity can be a predictor of recurrence, but it is unknown whether this is an independent risk factor.25-27 Within the established priorities in this study, seven relate to bracing and relapse. This highlights the gap in knowledge and the level of challenge it represents.

Ponseti28 originally advocated the use of a tibilalis anterior tendon transfer for treating dynamic supination and relapse. However, there is no consensus on when this should be performed. Hosseinzadeh et al29 presented results of a survey of Paediatric Orthopaedic Surgeons of North America regarding management of the relapsed clubfoot, without consensus among the 321 participants as to optimal intervention or timing. This is echoed by Eidelman et al,30 Masrouha et al,31 and Desai et al,32 with over 300 publications discussing groups at risk for relapse and subsequent management with variable opinions. All stakeholders participating in this consensus study deemed this an important topic for future research.

Despite physiotherapists being key clubfoot practitioners who play a major role in Ponseti casting and bracing,25,33,34 their potential contribution to strengthening and conditioning is largely unexplored. No studies have evaluated physiotherapy interventions beyond walking age, nor examined their role in the long-term outcomes of children with clubfoot. Across all stakeholders in this study, research in physiotherapy interventions was rated as high-priority. Within the Physiotherapy theme, stakeholders prioritized research focusing on the role of activity, stretching, and structured physiotherapy programmes in maintaining correction and preventing relapse throughout childhood. Key areas of uncertainty include the optimal type, intensity, and duration of physiotherapy interventions, as well as the most appropriate age groups for targeted intervention. Stakeholders also highlighted the need for studies incorporating both patient-reported outcomes and objective functional measures, to better understand the long-term impact of physiotherapy on function, participation, and recurrence of deformity.

The strength of this study is its vigorous design, implementation, and interpretation of the results in accordance with the published protocol.14 When evaluated against the DCAT (Delphi Critical Appraisal Tool) checklist used to assess the quality of the modified Delphi process, this study scores highly across both the core and additional criteria.35

A limitation of the study’s methodology relates to the recruitment process. Invitations to participate were sent to members of the UKCN, all practising Ponseti users within BSCOS, and a group of parents, resulting in a relatively small participant cohort. It is acknowledged that a larger population of Ponseti practitioners exists beyond BSCOS and the UKCN; nevertheless, the survey yielded responses from 103 participants and over 90% retention for round two.

The panellists’ expertise, geographical distribution, and retention percentage were appropriate to represent the Ponseti community in the UK.

It is important to emphasize that this priority-setting exercise is UK-focused and intended primarily for UK funders. However, international collaborations and shared platforms remain essential for broader knowledge exchange and the development of future research. A separate priority-setting exercise for low- and middle-income countries, along with mechanisms for sharing priorities globally, should follow from this work. Addressing the caveat associated with the four bracing priorities is essential for proposing high-quality research. In particular, the ability to accurately quantify brace wear remains a key limitation, as caregiver-reported adherence is known to be unreliable, and sensor-based technologies are not yet widely available in the market.36,37 Future studies should incorporate pragmatic approaches to overcome these limitations, which may include embedding sensor-based monitoring within sub-studies of larger trials, as well as developing and validating standardized caregiver-reported adherence tools. Integration of remote monitoring and device development into trial design offers an opportunity to generate more robust adherence data, which are critical to evaluating the effectiveness of different bracing strategies. Such approaches would enable meaningful progress in this area while technological solutions continue to evolve. Further consideration must be given to the position of equipoise ahead of developing randomized controlled trials. Freedman38 suggested that if there was “genuine uncertainty in the expert medical community” over a preferred treatment, regardless of whether the individual practitioner had a treatment preference, then this was sufficient to describe a trial as having “clinical equipoise”. With this in mind, it will be possible to devise methodologies to address the research priorities in clubfoot.

In conclusion, the priority statements derived from this consensus study will lead to the development of large multicentre randomized controlled trials, providing better evidence for clinical decision making and reducing variations in care in children with clubfoot.

Take home message

- This UK-based clubfoot research priority-setting excercise presents nine research priorities across four themes, using a consensus based approach drawn from the UK Clubfoot Network, BSCOS members, and a cohort of parents/carers.

- The priority statements derived from this consensus study will lead to the development of large multicentre randomized controlled trials, providing better evidence for clinical decision-making and reducing variations in care in children with clubfoot.
