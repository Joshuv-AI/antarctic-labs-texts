Are RAS inhibitors necessary during intensive immunosuppression for lupus nephritis? Real-world evidence from the STAR cohort
Authors: Xiaohui Zhang, Xiuli Zhang, Juan Zhao, Yong Fan, Shan Song, Hong Huang, Dai Gao, Lanlan Ji, Zhuoli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWhile guidelines advocate renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) for renoprotection in lupus nephritis (LN), evidence is largely extrapolated from non-lupus populations. Evidence specifically evaluating RASi efficacy during the intensive induction phase of active LN remains scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the real-world impact of adding RASi to standard induction therapy on renal remission and glucocorticoid tapering in LN.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the STAR (Treat-SLE-to-Target) registry. Patients receiving ≥3 months of continuous RASi during induction were classified as RASi, while those without RASi exposure during follow-up were classified as non-RASi. Propensity score overlap weighting was applied to balance baseline characteristics. The primary endpoints were rates of complete renal remission (CRR) at 12 months. Secondary endpoints included total renal remission (TRR), the magnitude and rate of proteinuria reduction and glucocorticoid dosage.ResultsAmong 314 patients, 220 received RASi and 94 did not. The RASi group had higher baseline 24-hour urinary protein and hypertension prevalence. After weighting, characteristics were balanced. Weighted analyses showed no significant difference in CRR at 12 months (58.7% vs 64.7%, p=0.428) or 6 months (43.1% vs 53.4%, p=0.169). Conversely, 6-month TRR was significantly lower in the RASi group (59.0% vs 76.8%, p=0.026). Proteinuria percentage reduction was comparable between groups, and RASi use was not associated with accelerated glucocorticoid tapering or more rapid decline in disease activity. Multivariable analysis identified LN at SLE onset and baseline pyuria as independent predictors of CRR, but not RASi usage.ConclusionAdding RASi to standard induction therapy provided no additive benefit for renal remission or proteinuria reduction within the first 12 months nor did it facilitate glucocorticoid tapering. The haemodynamic antiproteinuric effects of RASi appear overshadowed by potent anti-inflammatory treatment during active LN.

Introduction

Lupus nephritis (LN) is a major manifestation of SLE, affecting approximately 50% of patients and contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality.1 Persistent proteinuria predisposes to chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal disease, cardiovascular diseases and even death in patients with LN.2

An early significant drop in proteinuria is a predictor of a favourable long-term renal outcome. Moreover, the earlier the LN remission is achieved, the better the long-term renal prognosis.35 Therefore, LN therapy should aim at complete renal remission (CRR) or at least partial renal remission by 6–12 months.6

The renin-angiotensin system (RAS) plays a crucial role in regulating renal function. RAS is involved in blood pressure, sodium and fluid regulation and ultimately affects renal functions.7 8 It has been shown that RAS inhibitors (RASi), including ACE inhibitors (ACEi) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), can reduce urinary protein excretion and protect from renal injury in both diabetic and some nondiabetic nephropathies.913 RASi are widely recommended for the management of proteinuria and hypertension as well as for renal protection.1416 However, the evidence supporting these recommendations is predominantly derived from non-lupus populations and few studies have specifically evaluated RASi use in SLE.

More importantly, while the long-term renoprotective effects of RAS inhibitors are well established,913 high-quality evidence remains limited regarding whether their use during the active inflammatory phase of LN provides additional benefit in accelerating early clinical remission. Accordingly, this study was designed to evaluate the induction-phase clinical utility of RASi in real-world patients with LN.

Methods

Study population

This study was a retrospective analysis of STAR (Treat-SLE-to-Target) cohort, a prospective longitudinal registry established at Peking University First Hospital in 2007. All participants met the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) or 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics classification criteria or 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR)/ACR classification criteria for SLE. Follow-up visits were conducted every 1–3 months during active states and every 3–6 months during stable periods. Detailed cohort methodology has been published previously.17 Inclusion criteria were defined as: (1) evidence of active renal involvement characterised by persistent proteinuria (>0.5 g/24 hours) with or without active urinary sediment (specifically red blood cell casts) or an unexplained decline in renal function; (2) active LN (either new-onset or relapsing) requiring induction therapy. Active LN was preferentially confirmed by kidney biopsy when available; in patients without biopsy, the diagnosis was adjudicated by two independent senior rheumatologists based on standardised clinical and serological criteria.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following: (1) age <18 years; (2) pregnancy; (3) baseline proteinuria <0.5 g/24 hours at the initiation of induction therapy; (4) concurrent thrombotic microangiopathy or other coexisting renal pathologies; (5) received transient or inconsistent RASi therapy for <3 months during the induction phase.

Data collection

Demographics, clinical and serological characteristics and treatment were recorded. Laboratory tests included complete blood counts, serum creatinine (SCr), albumin, complements (C3 and C4), ANA, anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), anti-extractable nuclear antigen, anti-phospholipid antibodies, 24-hour urinary protein (UTP) and urine sediments. The medication regimens, including glucocorticoids (GC), hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), immunosuppressive agents and RASi, were recorded. The GC dosage was checked at each visit and converted to prednisone equivalent dose. The disease activity of SLE was assessed at each visit using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K). The time point of initiating induction therapy for LN was defined as the baseline of this study.

Patients were categorised into the RASi group if they received continuous RASi therapy for ≥3 months during the induction phase, whereas those without RASi exposure during follow-up were classified as the non-RASi group. The mean duration of RASi exposure in the RASi group was 9.7±3.0 months.

Outcomes of the study

The primary outcome of the study was the proportion of CRR at 12 months, which required (1) proteinuria <0.5 g/24 hours and (2) SCr within 15% change from baseline or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2. The secondary outcomes included (1) the proportion of CRR at 6 months; (2) the rates of total renal remission (TRR) at 6 months and 12 months, representing the composite of patients achieving either CRR or PR, the latter defined as a ≥50% reduction in proteinuria to subnephrotic levels (<3.5 g/24 hours) with stable renal function (SCr within 15% change from baseline or eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2); (3) the absolute and percentage reduction of UTP at 6 months and 12 months from baseline; (4) changes in serological markers (anti-dsDNA, C3/C4 levels) and daily GC dosage.

Statistical methods

Categorical variables were described as the number of cases (percentage). Normally distributed data were presented as means±SD, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as medians with IQRs. Comparisons of continuous variables were made using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test where appropriate. Categorical variables were compared by the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability test. To rigorously address potential confounding factors and selection bias, we employed propensity score-based overlap weighting (OW) rather than conventional matching. The independent prognostic factors of CRR at 12 months were determined by logistic regression. Subsequently, weighted logistic regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors for CRR at 12 months, presented as ORs with 95% CIs. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, V.4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Graphs were made using GraphPad Prism V.8.0.2. All testing analyses were two-sided, with p value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 314 LN patients were included in this study. Baseline clinical characteristics are detailed in table 1. The cohort had a mean age of 36.0 years and was predominantly female (267, 85.0%). The median SLE duration was 2.5 years. Of these patients, a definitive diagnosis of active LN was established via kidney biopsy in 170 (54.1%), while the remaining were diagnosed based on strict clinical and laboratory surrogate criteria. All patients were ANA positive, and 83.2% patients were anti-dsDNA positive. Other autoantibodies observed included anti-SSA (57.3%), anti-nRNP (42.0%), anti-Sm (33.1%), anti-rRNP (28.9%), anti-SSB (16.2%) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (14.4%). Regarding antiphospholipid antibodies, 49 (20.3%) patients were positive for lupus anticoagulant, 33 (12.3%) for anticardiolipin antibodies and 47 (20.4%) for anti-β2-glycoprotein I.

Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the RASi and non-RASi groups

Of the 314 enrolled patients, 220 were categorised into the RASi group and 94 into the non-RASi group. The mean duration of RASi exposure was 9.7±3.0 months. Compared with the non-RASi group, patients treated with RASi were significantly older and more likely to have hypertension (24.1% vs 10.6%, p=0.01, standardised mean difference (SMD)=0.361), higher UTP and lower serum albumin levels. During the induction therapy phase, 42 (13.4%) patients received GC pulse therapy, and the median initial dose of prednisone was 58.4±17.8 mg/day. Mycophenolate mofetil (140, 44.6%) and cyclophosphamide (118, 37.5%) were the most prescribed immunosuppressants. The initial dose of oral GC was comparable between the two groups, yet the categories of immunosuppressants differed significantly. HCQ was administered to 261 (83.1%) patients with balanced usage between the two groups (table 2, left columns).

To mitigate baseline disparities and selection bias, we applied propensity score-based OW. In the weighted cohort, all baseline covariates, including demographics, laboratory parameters and treatment regimens, achieved excellent balance. As illustrated in table 2 and online supplemental figure 1, the absolute SMDs for all variables were <0.01 (table 2, right columns), indicating negligible differences between the RASi and non-RASi groups.

The comparison of renal remission between the two groups

Regarding the primary endpoint, the unweighted CRR and TRR rates at 12 months were comparable between the RASi and non-RASi groups in the unweighted analysis (table 3, left columns). Crucially, following propensity score-based OW to adjust for baseline disparities, no significant differences were observed in the weighted 12-month CRR (44.6% vs 51.5%, p=0.428) or TRR (70.6% vs 81.3%, p=0.170) between the two groups (table 3, right columns).

For the secondary endpoints at 6 months, the unweighted CRR rate was significantly lower in the RASi group compared with the non-RASi group (41.9% vs 57.3%, p=0.002), while the TRR rate did not differ significantly (p=0.110). However, after OW to balance baseline characteristics, the disparity in 6-month CRR lost statistical significance (43.1% vs 53.4%, p=0.169), although the weighted 6-month TRR rate remained lower in the RASi group (59.0% vs 76.8%, p=0.026).

Additionally, we compared treatment outcomes between patients with initial-onset LN and those with relapsed disease. Subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences in baseline renal pathological Activity Index (8 (5–11) vs 7 (5–10), p=0.339) or Chronicity Index (2 (0–3) vs 2 (1–4), p=0.235) between initial and relapsed episodes. Correspondingly, there were no significant differences in CRR or TRR achievement at either 6 months or 12 months between the two subgroups (p>0.05 for all comparisons, data not shown), suggesting that the impact of RASi therapy remained consistent regardless of the clinical episode type.

Regarding overall disease control, the achievement of Lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS) was similar between the RASi and non-RASi groups at both 6 months (3.6% overall) and 12 months (20.3% overall; p>0.05 for all comparisons; data not shown).

Longitudinal disease activity and GC dosage

During follow-up, overall disease activity declined substantially. The median SLEDAI-2K score decreased from 17 (IQR: 13–21) at baseline to 6 (IQR: 2–8) at 6 months and 4 (IQR: 2–6) at 12 months. No significant differences in SLEDAI-2K scores were observed between the two groups at any time point.

Serological improvements paralleled these clinical trends, characterised by a marked reduction in the proportion of anti-dsDNA positivity and significant increases in C3 and C4 levels. Although C4 and platelet levels were statistically higher in the RASi group at 6 months, values in both groups remained within the normal reference range.

Concurrently, GC doses were tapered. The median daily dose of prednisone decreased to 15.0 (IQR: 12.5–20.0) mg/d at 6 months and 10.0 (IQR: 7.5–10.0) mg/day at 12 months. Comparison of tapering schedules revealed no significant differences between the RASi and non-RASi groups (online supplemental tables 1 and 2).

In the multivariable analysis, LN at SLE onset exhibited a significantly higher likelihood of achieving CRR compared with those with relapsing or delayed renal involvement (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.2 to 5.2; p=0.002). Baseline pyuria was also positively associated with CRR attainment (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.2 to 5.3; p=0.015).

Conversely, elevated baseline SCr was inversely associated with the probability of CRR (OR 0.65 per mg/dL increase, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.91; p=0.017). Notably, the administration of GC pulse therapy during the induction phase was associated with a lower probability of achieving CRR (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.75; p=0.012). Importantly, the use of RAS inhibitors showed no significant association with CRR attainment at 12 months.

Discussion

The RAS is pivotal in the progression of chronic renal disease of both diabetic and non-diabetic origin. Beyond its role in regulating blood pressure, sodium and fluid balance, RAS is also involved in inflammation, immune responses and oxidative stress.7 18 RASi has been shown to reduce urinary protein excretion and protect from renal injury by lowering blood pressure and independent antihypertensive action.19 20 These pleiotropic effects include decreasing efferent arteriolar resistance, improving glomerular perm selectivity, promoting compensatory growth of residual nephrons and attenuating tubulointerstitial fibrosis.102125

Preclinical studies consistently support the renal protective utility of RASi in murine lupus models. Early research by Herlitz and Tarkowsky demonstrated that captopril significantly ameliorated proteinuria, glomerular histopathology, lymphoid hyperplasia, dermatitis and hypergammaglobulinemia, thereby improving survival in MRL/lpr mice.20 26 27 Subsequent studies in NZW/NZB and MRL/lpr models confirmed that RASi delayed proteinuria onset, reduced glomerular hypercellularity, retarded nephritis progression and even reversed established proteinuria in advanced disease stages. These were further confirmed in NZW/NZB and MRL/lpr mice by Perez De Lema G and Maira Soto. They found that RASi delayed the onset of proteinuria, reduced glomerular expansion and mesangial proliferation, retarded the progression of early nephritis and even reversed proteinuria in advanced disease.2830

Supported by these mechanistic rationales and, more importantly, by robust evidence from diabetic and non-diabetic chronic kidney diseases, RASi is widely recommended by EULAR, ACR and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines for managing hypertension and proteinuria, as well as for preserving renal function.61416 However, it is critical to recognise that these recommendations are largely extrapolated from data in diabetic nephropathy, hypertension and other glomerulonephritides such as IgA nephropathy.913 Clinical evidence specifically evaluating the efficacy of RASi in the distinct inflammatory context of LN remains scarce.

Historical data are limited to case reports and small series. Shapira et al first described an adult LN patient who responded to captopril, resulting in a substantial reduction in proteinuria from 28 g to 11.5 g over 10 weeks.31 Later, Tse et al reported resolution of proteinuria and improved serum albumin in a small cohort treated with ACEi (n=12) or ARB (n=2).32 Similarly, Kitamura et al observed significant proteinuria reduction (62.7% at 6 months and 84.8% at 12 months) and stable serum SCr in seven patients receiving ARB, notably without changes to their concomitant immunosuppressive regimen.33 Furthermore, a post-hoc analysis of the LUMINA cohort suggested that ACEi use was associated with a lower risk of developing renal involvement and improved renal survival.34 However, these studies are constrained by significant limitations, including small sample sizes, lack of control groups and retrospective designs. Most critically, the confounding effect of concurrent GC and immunosuppressants makes it difficult to dissect whether the observed proteinuria reduction was attributable to RASi or standard induction therapy.

The present study provides real-world evidence on the impact of RASi during LN induction therapy. In contrast to prior expectations, we found that the addition of RASi did not confer a significant benefit on renal remission. Although unadjusted analyses showed a greater absolute magnitude of UTP decrease in the RASi group, this was driven by higher baseline proteinuria in these patients. Crucially, there were no significant differences in the percentage of UTP reduction between the two groups. Following adjustment for baseline characteristics via OW, the disparity in UTP reduction remained insignificant. While the non-RASi group achieved a higher TRR rate at 6 months, this initial clinical advantage disappeared by 12 months. This temporal trend likely reflects the baseline pathological heterogeneity, as the RASi group contained a substantially higher proportion of mixed lesions (Class III/IV+V; 37.5% vs 14.7%). It is well-recognised that the membranous component in mixed LN typically necessitates a more protracted course for proteinuria resolution compared with pure proliferative lesions. However, even after achieving balanced pathological distributions through OW to account for this physiological delay, our 12-month data confirm that the RASi group still did not achieve a superior reduction in UTP or a higher rate of CRR compared with standard induction therapy alone.

Furthermore, no additional benefits were observed regarding secondary outcomes. Disease activity index and serological improvement were comparable between the RASi and non-RASi groups. While a study in paediatric lupus suggested that early RASi initiation was associated with faster GC tapering (adjusted subdistribution HR 1.81),35 our data in adult LN did not support this finding, showing no significant difference in GC tapering speed over 12 months.

While we fully recognise the well-established long-term renoprotective and cardiovascular benefits of RASi in chronic kidney disease management, the lack of additional benefit observed with RASi during the intensive induction phase of active LN likely reflects the potency of standard care. The typical induction regimen for LN comprises high-dose GC, HCQ and potent immunosuppressive agents (mycophenolate mofetil or cyclophosphamide). The profound anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects of this combination may overshadow the haemodynamic and antiproteinuric effects of RASi. Consequently, the contribution of RASi to the reduction of proteinuria appears marginal in the context of active immune-mediated inflammation. This contrasts with its pivotal role in diabetic or hypertensive nephropathy, where haemodynamic factors predominate. Our findings suggest that during LN’s initial period of peak inflammation, the clinical priority resides in the rapid control of immune-mediated injury through standard induction therapy. Furthermore, with the increasing integration of biological agents such as belimumab into LN management, the specific adjunctive benefit of RASi may be further diluted. Therefore, a prospective randomised controlled trial is warranted to validate these real-world observations.

Multivariable analysis identified LN at SLE onset and baseline pyuria as independent favourable predictors of 12-month CRR. Notably, concurrent LN at SLE diagnosis was significantly more likely to achieve renal remission compared with others. This observation may be attributed to the intensified surveillance protocol for patients with established disease; regular monitoring of urinalysis and renal function facilitates the early recognition of renal flares, thereby enabling prompt therapeutic intervention before severe renal injury occurs. Conversely, elevated baseline SCr and GC pulse therapy were inversely associated with CRR attainment. The negative association with pulse therapy likely reflects ‘confounding by indication,’ as this intensive regimen is typically reserved for patients with more severe clinical presentations or higher disease activity.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a real-world observational study, the decision to prescribe RASi was at the discretion of the treating rheumatologist. The higher baseline UTP and prevalence of hypertension in the RASi group suggest an indication bias, where RASi was preferentially prescribed to patients with more severe renal phenotypes. While we used OW to rigorously balance these baseline disparities, we recognise the inherent constraints of retrospective designs in establishing causal efficacy. Second, we analysed RASi use as a binary variable without stratifying by specific dosage, which may mask dose-dependent effects. Third, although OW was employed to balance baseline characteristics rigorously, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, the rate of biopsy-verified diagnosis of active LN in our cohort was suboptimal (54.1%). While this pragmatic inclusion reflects a well-recognised logistical challenge and variation in real-world clinical practice, the lack of histological data for all patients remains an inherent limitation, though our findings appeared robust across the biopsy-defined subgroups. Finally, the primary endpoints were restricted to renal remission rates at 12 months. While our data demonstrate that RASi does not confer a significant additive benefit for proteinuria reduction or renal remission within the first year of induction therapy, this evidence serves to inform clinical prioritisation in the context of potent standard care. The potential renal protective role of RASi in delaying long-term CKD progression requires investigation in future studies with extended follow-up.

Conclusion

This study indicates that adding RASi to standard induction therapy for LN patients offers no significant benefit for renal remission or GC sparing. While RASi remains a cornerstone for non-inflammatory kidney diseases, its additive value appears marginal in the setting of acute immune-mediated inflammation. In this context, clinical outcomes are primarily driven by potent immunosuppression. These findings challenge the direct extrapolation of guidelines from diabetic nephropathy to the induction therapy for LN. Future trials are warranted to assess long-term protection of RASi against the progression of chronic kidney disease.
