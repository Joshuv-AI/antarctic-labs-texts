Thyroid disease and breast cancer, benign breast neoplasm: a two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Bowen Wang, Yue Ling, Hui Zhang, Yang Liu, Jiacheng Yuan, Yinuo Zhang, Ming Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer (BC) is a prevalent and significant health issue and a major contributor to global cancer incidence, accounting for 31% of all reported cases in women. Benign breast neoplasm, as a benign tumor with a high incidence in women, may play an important role in the development of BC. Previous studies have shown that thyroid dysfunction and thyroid cancer (TC) can lead to the occurrence of many cancers. Therefore, we conduct Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore the causality of thyroid dysfunctions, TC, and breast neoplasm.MethodsThe data of the analysis from the genome-wide association study (GWAS) dataset. The exposure includes FT4, TSH, hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and TC. Meanwhile, the outcome consists of BC, HER2-enriched BC, HER2-negative BC, and benign breast neoplasm. We used five methods (inverse variance weighted (IVW) random effects model, IVW fixed effects model, MR-Egger method, median weighted method, and the weighted mode method). We used the MR-PRESSO test and MR-Egger intercept test to detect horizontal pleiotropy and Cochran’s Q test to detect heterogeneity.ResultsThe IVW method showed a positive relationship between high FT4 levels and BC (OR = 1.210 p = 0.008) and an inverse association between TSH levels (OR IVW = 0.908 p = 0.007), hypothyroidism (OR IVW = 0.959, p = 0.014) and BC. For HER2-positive BC, an elevated FT4 level was associated with an increased risk (OR IVW = 1.314, p = 0.001). Genetically predicted high TSH levels (OR IVW = 0.899, p = 0.02) and hypothyroidism (OR IVW = 0.944, p = 0.003) were associated with a decreased risk of HER2-positive BC. Meanwhile, individuals with TC (OR = 1.003, p = 0.048), and hyperthyroidism (OR IVW = 1.127, p = 0.006) were associated with an increasing risk of development of benign breast neoplasm. Hyperthyroidism was associated with an elevated risk of benign breast neoplasm.ConclusionsThe present MR study explains the association between thyroid diseases and BC (mainly in HER2-positive BC). Furthermore, it demonstrates that hyperthyroidism, low levels of TSH, and TC may contribute to the development of benign breast neoplasm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04046-x.Keywords: Breast cancer, Thyroid cancer, Mendelian randomization, Thyroid dysfunction, Benign breast neoplasm

Introduction

Currently, female breast cancer is the most diagnosed malignant tumor globally. In 2020, breast cancer (BC) incidence was approximately 2,261,419 cases, accounting for 11.7% of all cancers, with a total of 684,966 deaths, representing 6.9% of all cancer deaths [1]. The report indicated that female breast cancer was the most frequently diagnosed malignancy (11.7% of total cases), followed by lung cancer (11.4%), colorectal cancer (10.0%), prostate cancer (7.3%), and stomach cancer (5.6%). In 2024, Siegel et al. estimated that 310,720 women and 2,790 men in the United States would be diagnosed with breast cancer, and 42,250 women and 530 men would die from the disease [2]. Therefore, early detection and diagnosis of breast cancer are of significant importance.

BC has many risk factors, mainly divided into genetic and non-genetic factors [3]. In previous cohort studies, benign breast neoplasm has been shown to be an important risk factor in the development of BC [4–6]. A meta-analysis published in 2015, demonstrated that either breast proliferative diseases with atypia or not associated with an elevated risk of the development of BC. Therefore, finding the risk factors of benign breast neoplasm is an important step in preventing BC.

Thyroid hormone (TH) has many important functions such as growing, differentiating, developing, and metabolizing. Thyroid dysfunction is a common endocrine disorder, with its incidence showing an increasing trend globally [7]. Thyroid dysfunction primarily includes hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, and their prevalence rates vary significantly among different populations [7]. Epidemiological studies indicate that women, the elderly, and individuals with a family history are high-risk groups for thyroid disorders [8]. The incidence of hyperthyroidism is notably higher in women than in men, while hypothyroidism is more common in the elderly. In a prospective population study of 66,140 participants, using the form of a questionnaire, they found that hyperthyroidism is associated with a high risk of lung cancer and prostate cancer among the 21 cancers, but not a link to the BC [9]. Another prospective study involving 10,318 individuals in which TH including free thyroxine (FT4) and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) were available indicated individuals with high levels of FT4 are more likely to get BC (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.42; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.12–1.79) [9]. The relationship between thyroid dysfunction and BC is still inconsistent in the current cohort study. TC may be a potential risk factor in the occurrence of BC. The latest study of patients first diagnosed with BC or with TC reviewed that the risk of papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) elevated 1.3-fold in the patients with BC, and the risk of BC increased 1.2-fold in individuals with TC [10]. However, this finding indicated that BC and TC may be a potential risk factor for each other, while the presence of common etiology can’t be ruled out. A study using nine US surveillance epidemiology and end results (SEER) cancer registries, demonstrated that the risk of BC (risk ratio [RR] = 1.46; 95% CI:1.10 to 1.95) increased in individuals with differentiated TC and needing the use of radioactive iodine treatment after at least 20 years of survival.

Based on the findings of this research, we want to explore two hypotheses. Firstly, whether thyroid dysfunction including FT4, TSH, hypothyroidism, and hyperthyroidism have a causal association with BC. Secondly, whether TC is associated with a high risk of BC. In addition, the causal relation between these factors (FT4, TSH, hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and TC) and benign breast neoplasm may exist, because BC and benign breast neoplasm have a common risk factor (estrogen). As an important etiology of BC, it is necessary to explore the association between benign breast neoplasm and thyroid dysfunction, TC.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a statistical method that leverages genetic variation to investigate the causal relationship between an exposure or risk factor and an outcome of interest. The key advantage of MR analysis lies in its ability to address issues of confounding and reverse causation, which are common challenges in observational studies. While, the causal relation between TC and BC had been proved in the previous two MR studies [11, 12], which support the causal link between BC and an increasing risk of TC, but not in reverse. Refer to existing research results which are inconsistent. To genetically illustrate the relationship between thyroid dysfunction, TC, and BC, benign breast neoplasm, our MR analysis is conducted. We choose the FT4 levels, TSH levels, hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, TC as the exposures, and BC, benign breast neoplasm as the outcome to detect the relationship between thyroid diseases and breast neoplasms.

Materials and methods

Assumptions of MR study

MR research needs to satisfy three core assumptions: relevance assumption, independent assumption, and exclusion restriction assumption [13]. Firstly, instrumental variables must be strongly correlated with exposure factors at the genome-wide level of significance (p < 5 × 10 − 8) (Relevance assumption). Secondly, instrumental variables could not be associated with any confounders associated with the outcome (Independent assumption). Lastly, instrumental variables can only affect outcome variables through exposure, not other pathways (p > 5 × 10 − 5) (Exclusion restriction assumption). We designed an MR study to explore the causal association between thyroid disease and breast neoplasm (Fig. 1).

Model of Mendelian Randomization and three assumptions (relevance assumption, exclusion restriction assumption, independent assumption)

Study population and data sources

The data of exposure including FT4, TSH, and hyperthyroidism used in the MR study was obtained from the ThyroidOmics Consortium, for a total of 72,167 individuals [14]. In addition, the MR study employed summary-level data from the IEU Open GWAS database, namely thyroid cancer including 649 cases and 431 controls (GWAS ID: ieu-a-1082), hypothyroidism including 405,357 individuals (GWAS ID: ebi-a-GCST90013893), and benign neoplasm of the thyroid including 2,079 cases and 101,074 controls (GWAS ID: finn-b-CD2_BENIGN_BREAST_EXALLC). Similarly, data on BC, HER2-positive BC, and HER2-negative BC were acquired from the FinnGen R9 alliance [15]. In this MR analysis, the phenotype was “Malignant neoplasm of breast (controls excluding all cancers)”, “Malignant neoplasm of breast, HER2-negative (controls excluding all cancers)” and “Malignant neoplasm of breast, HER2-positive (controls excluding all cancers)”. On the one hand, the malignant neoplasm of breast GWAS included 182,869 Finnish adult subjects, including 15,680 cases and 167,189 controls. In contrast, the HER2-positive BC GWAS consists of 176,715 Finnish adult individuals, with 9,698 cases and 167,017 controls. The HER2-negative-like BC GWAS involved 172,982 Finnish adult individuals, with 5,965 cases and 167,017 controls. The data all came from European populations.

Selection of genetic instrumental variables

Referring to previous MR studies, we implemented a rigorous selection process [16]. Using the selection of instrumental variants (IVs) of thyroid cancer as an example, the same criteria were used for the following studies. For continuous exposures (FT4 and TSH levels), all odds ratios are reported per one standard deviation (SD) increase in the exposure. To satisfy the first MR assumption that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) must be strongly associated with thyroid cancer, SNPs associated with thyroid cancer were selected at the genome-wide level (p < 5 × 10 − 8). To ensure the independence of the instrumental variables, we used strict criteria (r2 ≤ 0.001; clumping window, 10 000 kb) and finally selected 347 SNPs associated with thyroid cancer. These selected SNPs were then aligned with the outcome of BC GWAS summary results. Find SNPs with palindrome sequences and remove them: rs4142879, after that only left 346 SNPs. Then we retrieved these SNPs on PhenoScanner (www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk) [17, 18]), a website that facilitates the identification of genetic variants associated with various phenotypes or traits, to examine which SNP might be associated with risk factors of outcome, including exogenous female hormone use, age, dense breast tissue, early onset of menstruation, radiation exposure, alcohol, high body mass index and late onset of menopause [3].

We found 5 SNPs that could be risk factors for malignant neoplasm of breast: rs461599 was associated with alcohol intake frequency, rs10031777 was related to age at menopause, while rs10493096, rs1577026, and rs4567782 were linked to BMI. Then only 341 SNPs were left. From the GWAS database of outcomes, 335 SNPs (Supplementary Table 1) were left as instrumental variables. After rigorous screening, SNPs related to FT4 (n = 14), TSH (n = 41), hyperthyroidism (n = 15), hypothyroidism (n = 99), and thyroid cancer (n = 341) were identified from the above standard. Characteristics of these SNPs used as instruments were presented in Supplementary Table S1-S4. The F statistic [19], ) of the SNPs calculated in the analysis was larger than 10, indicating that there were no weak instrumental variables [16].

statistical analysis

In this MR study, we used five methods including the inverse variance weighted (IVW) random effects model [20], IVW fixed effects model [20], MR-Egger method [21], median weighted method [22], and the weighted mode method [23].

The IVW method is a commonly used method for combining the causal estimates obtained from multiple genetic instruments. IVW method calculates a weighted average of the causal estimates, with the weights being proportional to the inverse of the variance of each estimate. It assumes that all genetic instruments are valid and have a consistent causal effect.

The MR-Egger regression method allows for the possibility of unbalanced pleiotropy (when genetic instruments affect the outcome through pathways other than the exposure of interest). It estimates the causal effect and tests for directional pleiotropy by assessing the intercept of the regression.

The weighted median method is robust to the presence of invalid instruments. It calculates the median of the causal estimates from individual instruments, with weights assigned based on the inverse of their variances. It provides valid causal estimates as long as at least 50% of the weight comes from valid instruments.

The weighted mode method combines information from multiple genetic instruments and aims to improve precision in estimating causal effects by considering the mode (most frequent value) of the individual causal estimates.

To guarantee the reliability of this trial, we performed horizontal pleiotropy, heterogeneity, and sensitivity analysis. The presence of horizontal pleiotropy was assessed utilizing the MR-PRESSO test [24] and the MR-Egger intercept test [25]. Furthermore, Cochran’s Q-test was also utilized to identify any cases of heterogeneity [20]. The Cochran’s Q-test is a statistical method used to test the variability of multiple related samples. When the p value is less than 0.05, we use IVW random effects model, otherwise, we use IVW fixed effects model.

Moreover, we also performed a leave-one-out analysis to evaluate whether a single SNP accounted for a significant association. In addition, we also use the forest plot, funnel spot, and scatter plot for the initial assessment of bias and publication preference of study results. The results of these tests are listed in Supplementary Figure S1-S20.

All analyses were carried out in R software 4.3.2 (https://www.R-project.org) with the TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.8), MendelianRandomization (version 0.8.0), and MRPRESSO (1.0) packages.

Results

For each exposure-outcome pair, the IVW random effects model was primarily used when significant heterogeneity was detected (Cochran’s Q test p < 0.05); otherwise, the fixed effects model was applied. Complementary analyses using MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode methods were performed to assess the robustness of causal estimates and detect potential pleiotropy, as detailed in the Methods section.

FT4 levels, TSH levels, and hypothyroidism have a causal association with BC

Genetic prediction has shown that FT4 levels are associated with an increased risk of the development of BC (odds ratio [OR] IVW = 1.210 p = 0.008; OR weighted median = 1.256, p = 0.0009; OR weighted mode = 1.291, p = 0.008) and HER2-enriched BC (OR IVW = 1.314 p = 0.001; OR weighted median = 1.308, p = 0.003; OR weighted mode = 1.378, p = 0.005). The results of IVW indicate that people with high FT4 levels can experience an average of 21.0% increased risk of BC and a 31.4% increased risk of HER2-positive BC, suggesting high FT4 levels may contribute to the development of BC (mainly in HER2-positive BC). We discovered that TSH levels were associated with a decreasing risk of development of BC (OR IVW = 0.908, p = 0.007; OR MR Egger = 0.799, p = 0.020; OR weighted median = 0.869, p = 0.006; OR weighted mode = 0.87, p = 0.086) and HER2-positive BC (OR IVW = 0.899, p = 0.02; OR MR-Egger = 0.760, p = 0.029; OR weighted median = 0.866, p = 0.029; OR weighted mode = 0.817, p = 0.029). The MR study finding indicated that TSH levels may protect against the development of BC (mainly in HER2-positive BC). In individuals with hypothyroidism, a greatly decreased risk of BC had been found in four methods (OR IVW = 0.959, p = 0.014; OR MR-Egger = 0.925, p = 0.043; OR weighted median = 0.932, p = 0.003; OR weighted mode = 0.938, p = 0.008), as was the risk of HER2-positive BC (OR IVW = 0.944, p = 0.003; OR MR-Egger = 0.891, p = 0.043; OR weighted median = 0.919, p = 0.031; OR weighted mode = 0.915, p = 0.018). The MR results of BC and its subtypes are in Fig. 2A and B, and Fig. 2C.

C The causal effect estimates of thyroid diseases on HER2-negative breast cancer. D The causal effect estimates of thyroid diseases on benign breast neoplasm

TC, hyperthyroidism associated with increased risk of benign breast neoplasm, and TSH levels associated with a reduced risk

After analyzing the relationship between TC and benign breast neoplasm using the four above methods independently. TC linked to an elevated risk of benign breast neoplasm by IVW analysis (OR = 1.003, p = 0.048). In addition, hyperthyroidism also had a positive causal relationship with the occurrence of benign breast neoplasm, and the causal effect was estimated to be 1.127 (95% CI, 1.034–1.228; p = 0.006) by the IVW method. However, we discovered that TSH levels were associated with a decreasing risk of the development of benign breast neoplasm by the IVW method (OR IVW = 0.777, p = 0.004). The MR results of benign breast neoplasm are in Fig. 2D.

Discussion

Our Mendelian randomized study included five exposures (FT4 levels, TSH levels, hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, and TC) and four outcomes (BC, HER2-positive BC, HER2-negative BC, and benign breast neoplasm) investigated the causal link between thyroid disease and BC, benign breast neoplasm.

Thyroxine is a crucial hormone produced by the thyroid gland that influences energy production, body temperature, and the functioning of organs and tissues. Several previous studies have indicated an association between high FT4 levels and BC [9, 26, 27]. A cohort study that included 10,318 participants found high levels of FT4 associated with an elevated risk of BC (HR: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.10 − 2.84). In addition, increasing FT4 levels were observed in individuals with BC in a prospective case–control study [26]. Notably, these investigations have demonstrated the potential of FT3 and FT4 to stimulate the proliferation of BC cells [28–30]. Some studies suggested triiodothyronine (T3) and 17β-estradiol (E2) could promote the proliferation of BC cells [28]. A meta-analysis published in 2022 has found that elevated FT4 levels increase the risk of BC [31]. On the contrary, a previous MR study on the association between thyroid function and 21 cancers showed that FT4 levels had a weak association with BC [32]. We think the difference is due to too small sample sizes. Compared to previous MR studies, we more focused on individuals with HER2-positive BC [11, 32]. Also, our study concluded that elevated levels of FT4 increase the risk of BC (mainly in HER2-positive BC), but was not associated with HER2-negative BC. A notable discrepancy was observed between the causal effects of genetically predicted FT4 levels and diagnosed hyperthyroidism on HER2-positive breast cancer. While elevated FT4 showed a significant increased risk (OR = 1.314), diagnosed hyperthyroidism exhibited a weaker, non-significant trend (OR = 1.08, p = 0.374). This divergence is clinically meaningful and likely reflects the inherent differences in the genetic instruments used for a continuous quantitative trait (FT4 levels) versus a complex binary disease diagnosis (hyperthyroidism). GWAS for diagnosed hyperthyroidism may capture genetic variants associated with various underlying etiologies, including autoimmune conditions such as Graves’ disease, which might have distinct biological pathways and clinical manifestations compared to the direct physiological effects of elevated FT4. While a sensitivity analysis using overlapping SNPs was considered, we believe the fundamental difference in phenotypic definitions is the primary driver of the observed discrepancy, and our current analysis captures the distinct genetic influences of these two related but not identical thyroid traits.

In our present MR study, the TSH level has an inverse causal relationship with BC (mainly in HER2-positive BC). Similarly, some previous observational studies are in line with our findings. A previous 29,691 participants cohort study indicated that low levels of TSH are linked with an elevated risk of cancer in people who have never had thyroid disease, which is consistent with our MR results [33]. A cohort study of 62,546 Korean women over 40 years of age who never had BC showed that high levels of TSH were associated with a decreased risk of BC than low levels of TSH in individuals in the euthyroid range [34]. However, two previous studies differed from our results, which showed a weak causal relationship between TSH and risk of BC [27, 35]. Differences between studies may be due to reverse causation bias in case-control studies, measurement biases leading to weakened effects or insufficient statistical power.

Hypothyroidism may play a protective role in the occurrence of BC (mainly in HER2-positive BC). In previous years, most observational studies have found that hypothyroidism is linked to low BC risk [36, 37]. Patients with hypothyroidism had a decreased risk of BC in a cohort study of 61,873 participants and 4.9 years follow-up [36].

Another study of 1,136 patients with primary breast cancer and 1,088 controls found that individuals with primary hypothyroidism had a low BC risk compared to those who are euthyroid [37]. An MR analysis verified that hypothyroidism has an inverse association with BC in line with our findings [32]. However, none of them were analyzed according to the commonly used clinical classification of BC, according to the expression of ER, PR, and HER2. Our study divided breasts into two groups according to the expression of HER2 and found that hypothyroidism not only reduces the risk of BC but especially in HER2-positive BC.

However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. The suggested protective effects of genetically predicted TSH and hypothyroidism on BC may be explained by the following mechanisms. Firstly, the thyroid receptor is expressed in the normal mammary epithelial cells, and T3 will mimic estrogen binding to ER and thereby stimulate BC cell growth [38]. Gonzalez-Sancho et al. studied the association between thyroid hormone and the proliferation of BC cells. They found that T3 inhibits the expression of T1 genes and cyclin D and thus inhibits the proliferation of mammary epithelial cells [39]. TSH is more sensitive to changes of thyroid function than T3 and T4. When thyroid dysfunction occurs, TSH levels are often one of the earliest abnormal indicators. Hence, both hypothyroidism and elevated TSH levels lead to decreased T3 concentrations in vivo. This may be one of the reasons for the reduced risk of BC. Benign breast neoplasm is a high-incidence tumor and a major etiology of BC among the female. After the MR study, we found that individuals with hyperthyroidism, low TSH levels, and TC have an elevated risk of the development of benign breast neoplasm.

Our study observed protective effects for both genetically predicted high TSH levels and hypothyroidism on breast cancer risk. Given that elevated TSH is a primary diagnostic marker for primary hypothyroidism, it is important to consider the nature of this relationship. The strong biological and clinical association between TSH levels and hypothyroidism suggests a shared underlying genetic architecture. This raises the possibility of vertical pleiotropy, where genetic variants primarily influence TSH levels, which in turn causally leads to hypothyroidism, and the observed protective effect of hypothyroidism is largely mediated through TSH. This would imply a single causal pathway (genetic predisposition to higher TSH leading to hypothyroidism, which then confers protection against BC) rather than two entirely independent protective factors. While our MR methods are designed to address horizontal pleiotropy (where instruments affect the outcome via pathways independent of the exposure), interpreting the effects of highly correlated exposures like TSH and hypothyroidism requires caution, especially in the presence of potential vertical pleiotropy. Future studies exploring the precise causal chain within the thyroid axis, potentially utilizing advanced methods to quantify genetic correlation and overlapping instruments, would be valuable.”

Previous studies indicated that physiological levels of T3 can enhance the ability of estradiol (Est) to stimulate the proliferation of BC cell lines by estradiol-mediated gene transcription [40]. Thyroid hormones can stimulate normal mammary epithelial cells and BC cells by mimicking estrogen binding to the ER. Therefore, monitoring abnormal concentrations of thyroid hormones is very important to prevent breast disease. In this MR analysis, we found that thyroid dysfunction and thyroid disease affect BC, especially in HER2-positive BC, a highly heterogeneous BC type that accounts for 25% of BC. Second, we found that hyperthyroidism and thyroid cancer are risk factors for benign breast neoplasm at the genetic level. More research is needed to clarify the relationship between hyperthyroidism, TC, and benign breast neoplasm, possibly from findings of larger clinical studies or more available GWAS data. The observed causal association between genetically predicted TC and benign breast neoplasm (OR = 1.003, p = 0.048) warrants careful interpretation due to its extremely small effect size and borderline statistical significance. While our primary MR analysis suggests a causal link, we acknowledge its fragility and the need to consider alternative explanations beyond traditional confounding. A detailed biological plausibility check of the instrumental variables for TC revealed. More critically, we must consider the potential for ‘genetic surveillance bias.’ Individuals with a genetic predisposition to TC might undergo more frequent medical examinations, including general health screenings or specific imaging, which could lead to an increased detection rate of benign breast neoplasms, rather than a direct causal effect on their incidence. While Mendelian randomization is robust against many forms of confounding, biases related to differential detection or diagnosis driven by genetic predispositions remain a challenge. Therefore, despite the statistical significance, the clinical relevance of this minute effect is limited, and this finding should be interpreted with caution, pending further validation from studies specifically designed to address such detection biases. Therefore, patients with thyroid diseases should continue to receive regular physician examinations and monitoring of the breast, as well as active management of thyroid disease and control of associated risk factors.

However, there were several limitations to this study. A large GWAS sample size is needed in an MR analysis. Small sample sizes can lead to instability in the results. We may get inconsistent results when experiments are repeated. This can lead to inaccuracies in the reliability and robustness of the experiment. We chose the largest GWAS data available from the FinnGen R9 alliance, IEU database and ThyroidOmics Consortium to avoid the overlap of the data, but some sample sizes of exposure are relatively small. Furthermore, while we employed methods to detect general horizontal pleiotropy, we acknowledge the potential for unmeasured confounding through specific biological pathways, such as the sex hormone axis (e.g., estrogen and SHBG levels). Thyroid function and breast cancer are both influenced by hormonal regulation, and it is plausible that some genetic instruments for thyroid traits could exert pleiotropic effects on sex hormone levels, thereby influencing breast cancer risk independently of thyroid function. Future studies, ideally utilizing multivariable Mendelian randomization with genetically predicted sex hormone levels as covariates, would be beneficial to further disentangle these complex relationships. Our findings suggest differential causal effects of thyroid function on breast cancer subtypes, particularly for HER2-positive BC. However, we acknowledge that the HER2-negative breast cancer cohort has a considerably smaller number of cases compared to the HER2-positive cohort (N cases = 5,965 vs. 9,698). This disparity in sample size implies a potential for insufficient statistical power to detect effects of similar magnitude in the HER2-negative stratum. Therefore, while our results point towards a stronger association with HER2-positive BC, the definitive claim of subtype specificity requires further validation from studies with larger HER2-negative cohorts to ensure adequate statistical power and to allow for formal statistical comparisons between subtypes. Thyroid hormones play crucial roles in regulating various biological processes in the body such as Metabolism Regulation, Development and Growth, Reproductive Function, etc. Although we have removed the known causes of BC and benign breast neoplasm, there may still be potential confounding factors that could interfere with our results. Another limitation of our study is its one-sided Mendelian randomization design, focusing solely on the causal effect of thyroid traits on breast neoplasms. We acknowledge that the literature, including other MR studies, suggests a possible bidirectional relationship where breast cancer or its associated hormonal environment could influence thyroid function. While exploring this reverse causality would provide a more comprehensive picture, performing such bidirectional analysis requires specific data availability and advanced analytical capabilities that are currently beyond the scope of this study. Future research should endeavor to investigate this potential bidirectional relationship to fully elucidate the complex interplay between thyroid health and breast cancer. Our study was primarily based on GWAS data from European populations. This limits the generalizability of our findings to other ancestries, as genetic architecture, including linkage disequilibrium structure and allele frequencies, can vary significantly across different populations. Furthermore, gene-environment interactions, which may influence the causal effects, could differ in non-European populations. Future Mendelian randomization studies utilizing diverse populations are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

The present MR study indicates the association between thyroid dysfunction and BC. TSH levels and hypothyroidism may play a protective role in the development of BC, and FT4 levels may be a risk factor. Furthermore, it demonstrates that hyperthyroidism, low levels of TSH, and TC may contribute to the development of benign breast neoplasm. Therefore, we should strengthen the monitoring of the breast in the population with thyroid dysfunction and TC to avoid breast diseases.
