Transcranial Ultrasound in the Helicopter Emergency Medical Services—A Feasibility Study
Authors: Ingrid N. Rognes, Pål C. W. Morberg, Henriette S. Jæger, Marius Rehn, Kristine Stø, Lars Alteheld, Else Charlotte Sandset, Maren R. Hov, Karianne Larsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and PurposeTranscranial color‐coded Doppler (TCCD) is a noninvasive, widely available diagnostic method that may identify large vessel occlusion (LVO) in acute stroke patients. This study aimed to explore whether helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) physicians could use TCCD in a prehospital setting to identify the proximal segment of the middle cerebral artery (M1‐MCA).MethodsIn a feasibility study following brief training, three HEMS physicians and a single‐reference examiner performed transtemporal TCCD examinations of four healthy volunteers, on ground and in flight a cruising helicopter. One color and one pulsed‐wave Doppler image of M1‐MCA were stored per examination, and time was recorded. TCCD images were retrospectively assessed by two raters for M1‐MCA identification success and image quality, using a 5‐point Likert scale.ResultsParticipants performed a total of 32 bilateral M1‐MCA examinations. The HEMS physicians identified M1‐MCA in 83% of color Doppler examinations, with median (interquartile range [IQR]) time of 83 (63–130) s. Substantial inter‐examiner variability was observed. The single‐reference examiner identified M1‐MCA in 88% of color Doppler examinations using 58 (49–101) s. Median (IQR) Likert scores for successful images were high: HEMS physicians 4.5 (4.5–5.0) and single‐reference examiner 5.0 (4.5–5.0), with no significant difference between on‐ground and in‐flight examinations.ConclusionFollowing brief training, HEMS physicians were able to perform TCCD field examinations of M1‐MCA in healthy volunteers, with identification rates and image quality comparable to a single‐reference examiner. Substantial inter‐examiner variability was observed, highlighting the importance of tailored training and competency assessment. Research is required to evaluate TCCD use by HEMS physicians under real‐world prehospital conditions.

Introduction

Management of acute ischemic stroke, including large vessel occlusion (LVO), depends critically on rapid diagnostics. Effective reperfusion therapies with intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy are highly time‐dependent constrained by narrow therapeutic windows [1, 2]. Stroke patients in rural and remote areas are disadvantaged with longer prehospital times, lower treatment rates, and increased disability and mortality compared to patients in more densely populated areas [3, 4, 5]. The European Stroke Organisation (ESO) emphasizes structured, regionalized stroke systems that aim toward fast identification of stroke patients and correct attribution of etiology, thus offering a critical role of prehospital diagnostics [6, 7].

Prehospital services, including the helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS), play a key role in delivering a potential neuroprotective bundle of care, including early physiological optimization and time‐critical interventions. In cardiology, prehospital thrombolysis administered by ambulance personnel is strongly recommended in guidelines for ST‐elevation myocardial infarction in patients with long transport times to an intervention center [8, 9]. This is transferrable to acute stroke care but currently limited by the absence of reliable prehospital diagnostic tools in air ambulances to safely initiate reperfusion therapy. In Norway, HEMS physicians already administer thrombolytic agents and antihypertensive therapies in non‐stroke conditions; highlighting that the primary barrier to prehospital stroke treatment initiation is diagnostic certainty rather than therapeutic capability. The present study therefore focuses on the feasibility of adding prehospital neurosonology to support future diagnostic assessment in acute stroke care.

Prehospital point‐of‐care ultrasound examinations performed by prehospital personnel can inform diagnostic decision‐making, patient management, and triage across trauma, respiratory, and cardiac emergencies [10, 11, 12, 13]. Ultrasound is therefore an important diagnostic tool in HEMS for acute cardiovascular and abdominal conditions and for guiding interventions. Transcranial color‐coded Doppler (TCCD) sonography is a rapid and accurate method to assess intracranial vessel patency in hospital settings and has shown promising diagnostic accuracy for LVO detection in acute stroke patients, including in prehospital settings [14, 15, 16]. Feasibility of TCCD performed by neurosonologists has been demonstrated both in ambulances and rotor‐wing air ambulances more than a decade ago [17, 18]. Logistic and economic challenges with the use of in‐hospital specialists complicate integration into the prehospital work environment. Since these initial studies, substantial technological advances in portable ultrasound systems have improved image quality, size, usability, and robustness, making them more accessible for prehospital deployment. Whether HEMS personnel can be trained to perform TCCD with an acceptable accuracy and quality is unknown. In the prehospital environment, ultrasound identification of LVO in the first segment of the middle cerebral artery (M1‐MCA) in combination with clinical assessment may improve prehospital triage [14, 18].

Introducing TCCD to the prehospital environment may represent an adjunctive tool in acute stroke assessment, particularly in rural or resource‐limited regions where delays to definitive care can be substantial [19, 20]. TCCD might reduce time to definitive treatment through prehospital detection of LVO and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), by directly routing to Comprehensive Stroke Centers (CSC) [21, 22]. However, TCCD should not be considered a stand‐alone tool and would need to be integrated with structured clinical assessment using validated prehospital stroke scales, combined with telemedical support from in‐hospital stroke specialists [23]. In this context, TCCD may provide complementary physiological and vascular information beyond clinical assessment alone.

This study aimed to explore the feasibility of prehospital TCCD in an HEMS simulation setting by measuring accuracy and quality of M1‐MCA identification performed by HEMS physicians after a short training program. To our knowledge, this is the first feasibility study of TCCD performed by trained prehospital personnel in a HEMS setting and may serve as a basis for future studies in prehospital acute stroke care.

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Design

This feasibility study was performed at Oslo University Hospital, Ullevål (OUH‐U), Oslo, Norway, and the Norwegian Air Ambulance Foundation (NAAF), Training and Development Center, Ås, Norway. OUH‐U is a CSC where acute stroke assessment in Oslo is centralized, and the hospital evaluates around 600 confirmed stroke patients per year [24]. The sample size of participants and number of examinations were small and pragmatically defined; therefore, no formal power calculation was performed, and all between‐group comparisons were exploratory.

In a simulated HEMS environment, the study explored whether three HEMS physicians could utilize TCCD to identify bilateral M1‐MCA in healthy volunteers both on ground and in flight of an Airbus H145D3 after a short training program. One neurologist with special TCCD training in stroke patients served as the single‐reference examiner. The study setup is illustrated in Figure 1. The three HEMS physicians and the single‐reference examiner attended a local, study‐specific training program developed by two European Society of Neurosonology and Cerebral Hemodynamics (ESNCH) certified neurosonologists at OUH‐U, both with extensive experience of performing TCCD in acute stroke patients.

Illustration of the study setup. TCCD was performed by three HEMS physicians and one single‐reference examiner on four healthy volunteers, both on ground and in flight. The scans were interpreted by two blinded neurosonologists. HEMS, helicopter emergency medical services; TCCD, transcranial color‐coded Doppler.

The primary objective was to measure the number of successful identifications of M1‐MCA in color Doppler mode by the HEMS physicians. Secondary objectives were examination time and successful velocity measurements with pulsed‐wave (PW) Doppler. Exploratory analyses were performed for comparison of success rate, quality, and time between ground and air examinations, and further between the HEMS physicians and the single‐reference examiner.

Study Participants and Healthy Volunteers

The nationwide Norwegian HEMS is physician‐staffed, with anesthesiologists with specialized training and experience in prehospital critical care [25]. The HEMS physicians in this study were recruited from the Division of Prehospital Services at OUH by invitational letters to the department. The single‐reference examiner was recruited from the Department of Neurology at Akershus University Hospital, also by invitational letter. Eight healthy volunteers with no known history of cerebrovascular disease participated in the study: four in the practical training session, and four were examined on the study day. For volunteers participating on the study day, the presence of an adequate transtemporal acoustic window for TCCD was required and confirmed in advance by a neurosonologist. All participants volunteered, and participation was based upon written, informed consent.

Training Program

The training program consisted of an introductory theoretical part where digital study material containing relevant information (neuroanatomy, physiology, ultrasound device (Vscan Air SL) [26], and examination technique) was distributed to the study participants. The second part of the TCCD training program was a 3‐h in‐person practical training session, August 25, 2024, at the Department of Neurology, OUH‐U, led by two ESNCH‐certified neurosonologists specialized in cerebrovascular ultrasound 6/5/26 at 3:58:00 p.m. The training was performed in accordance with current local practice at OUH‐U and included active and focused hands‐on TCCD training on healthy individuals. A final test of acquired TCCD skills (standardized observational test [SOT]) was performed at the end of the training session. The SOT tested technical skills and examination skills controlling the ability to visualize M1‐MCA, measure flow velocities, freeze and store images, and complete the examination within the time limit of 4 min per side. The participants were also required to complete and pass the same SOT at the start of the experiment day.

Experiment

During the experiment on September 5, 2024, the HEMS physicians and the single‐reference examiner utilized TCCD to identify M1‐MCA in four healthy volunteers on ground and in flight, Figure 1. An Airbus H145D3 rotor wing, dedicated to research and development purposes, was used for the in‐flight exams. The helicopter is staffed and equipped according to Norwegian standards for rotor‐wing ambulance operations, in addition to the study participant, a healthy volunteer (marker), and a study observer.

The four healthy volunteers were examined in supine position on a stretcher both on‐ground and during in‐flight cruising, Figure 1. The participants performed the on‐ground and in‐flight examinations in an alternating matter following a preplanned schedule, Table 1. A maximum time accepted for examination on the right and the left side, respectively, was set to 4 min (240 s) per side, and the examination was halted by the study observer if the time limit was exceeded.

Each TCCD examination was performed as a targeted assessment of the M1‐MCA segment of the MCA by a transtemporal insonation in the axial plane [27]. The examination started with identification of the temporal bone window on the right side and, thereafter, visualization of intracranial arteries in color mode. An image of the anticipated M1‐MCA in color Doppler mode was captured, and the time used was registered. Next, peak systolic velocity (PSV) was measured in the proximal M1‐MCA using PW Doppler mode without angle corrections. Velocity was recorded and PSV measured in cm/s. Total examination time per M1‐MCA was defined as the time from initiation of examination (probe touching skin) until acquired PW image and the participant signaling end of examination (probe off skin). The procedure was thereafter repeated on the left side.

Equipment and Technical Setup

Four identical Vscan Air SL (GE HealthCare) handheld, wireless ultrasound devices with prespecified TCCD settings were each paired with an individual iPad (Apple Inc.) which was strapped to the examiner's thigh during in‐flight examinations with a kneeboard mount (RAM Tab‐Tite, RAM Snap‐Link), Figure 2. The ultrasound devices were turned off and charged after every examination. A cooling station was also prepared for the devices to mitigate potential overheating during periods of intensive use. Time was registered for every examination using a stopwatch. Images were stored in the default file format on the hard drive of the respective iPad and thereafter converted to JPEG (.jpg) to reduce file size. Encoding used the JPEG standard with a “High” quality setting; dimensions and content were unchanged. As the Vscan Air SL is not standard equipment in Norwegian HEMS, and approval for in‐flight use was obtained from the Continuing Airworthiness Management Organization, Norwegian Air Ambulance Helicopter (approval report no. LEQ‐04062024).

Evaluation

Two blinded ESNCH‐certified neurosonologists (hereafter referred to as blinded raters) independently evaluated each examination for successful color Doppler identification of M1‐MCA and image quality using a 5‐point Likert scale (1–5) [28]. A Likert score of 1 indicated very poor quality and a score of 5 very good quality, Table 2. Acceptable image quality was defined as a Likert score of ≥3. The criteria for each Likert score were predefined by the blinded raters.

For PW Doppler measurements, the quality of the flow curves was classified in the same manner, Table 2, also with a Likert score ≥3 as the cutoff for acceptable image quality. PSV was obtained retrospectively from the flow curves. The neurosonologists performed the assessments independently of each other and were blinded to any identifiable TCCD information, including examiner and scan location (ground, air).

In cases of discordant evaluations between the two blinded raters regarding M1‐MCA identification success (Likert score ≥3 vs. ≤2), the examination was classified according to the lower score and considered unsuccessful and was excluded from further analyses. Image quality analyses were performed only for examinations classified as successful (Likert score ≥3).

Demographic Variables

Demographic variables of the participating physicians included age, sex, medical specialty, years of work experience (in‐hospital, prehospital (HEMS)), years of experience with general ultrasound examinations, and experience with TCCD and were recorded for each participant. Data collected from the healthy volunteers were age, sex, height (cm), and body weight (kg).

Statistical Analysis

In exploratory analyses, success rate, examination time, image quality, and flow measurements were compared between on‐ground and in‐flight examinations, and between the HEMS physicians and the single‐reference examiner.

Descriptive and summary statistics were non‐normally distributed and thus reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Interrater agreement for the predefined dichotomized Likert score categories for successful and unsuccessful imaging (Likert score ≥3 vs. ≤2) was analyzed using Cohen's kappa. A kappa value of ≤0.20 represents poor agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 good agreement, and 0.81–1.00 very good agreement [29]. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used. Data were recorded manually on paper during the training and experiment days and later transposed to a spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 (version 2409; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Data analyses were performed in JMP Pro 18 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (version 4.5.0). Figures were produced in R using the ggplot2 package [30].

Ethics

The study was assessed by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics Southeast Norway (REK Southeast, ref 744206) and found to be outside REK's mandate; therefore, formal ethical approval was not required. The study was approved by the data protection officer at Oslo University Hospital (ref. 24/11221).

Results

The HEMS physicians were median (IQR) 50 (48–51) years old and had median (IQR) 20 (16–25) years of experience in anesthesiology, and 14 (11–22) years as prehospital care providers. All had experience in general ultrasonography procedures but had no previous experience with TCCD. The single‐reference examiner had 26 years of clinical experience and performed approximately 200 in‐hospital TCCD per year but had no prehospital experience. The four healthy volunteers were median (IQR) 38 (34–43) years old. All HEMS physicians and the single‐reference examiner passed the SOT within the time limit after the training program and again at the beginning of the experiment day.

The study participants performed a total of 32 bilateral M1‐MCA examinations producing a total of 64 color Doppler mode and 64 PW Doppler mode M1‐MCA images: 48 of each mode by the HEMS physicians and 16 of each mode by the single‐reference examiner. All examinations were performed within the time limit of 4 min.

Color Doppler Mode

The three HEMS physicians identified M1‐MCA in 83% (40/48) of color Doppler examinations with no differences between in‐flight and on‐ground examinations. The median (IQR) examination time was 83 (63–130, n = 40) s; 92 (65–157, n = 20) s on ground, and 78 (59–110, n = 20) s in flight, p = 0.36, Figure 3A. PW Doppler

Among the HEMS physicians, PW imaging was successful in 63% (30/48) of examinations; 58% (14/24) on ground and 67% (16/24) in flight, with a Likert score of median (IQR) of 4.5 (4.4–5.0, n = 30). The distribution of PW Doppler Likert scores per examiner is shown in Figure 4B.

For the single‐reference examiner, PW imaging was successful in 88% (14/16) examinations; 100% (8/8) on ground and 75% (6/8) in flight, with a Likert score of median (IQR) of 4.5 (4–4.5, n = 14), Figure 4B.

Inter‐Examiner Variability

Considerable inter‐examiner variability was observed among the HEMS physicians. Examination duration varied between physicians, with differences observed in both color and PW Doppler assessments under on‐ground and in‐flight conditions, Figure 3B. The single‐reference examiner demonstrated more consistent image quality ratings and examination times across assessments. For M1‐MCA identification using color Doppler, Likert score distributions differed between physicians, although most assessments were rated as acceptable to very good (Likert 3–5), Figure 4A. Variability was more pronounced for PW Doppler assessment, where one HEMS physician (HEMS 1) consistently achieved higher Likert scores and a greater proportion of successful examinations than HEMS 2 and HEMS 3, Figure 4B.

Agreement Between Blinded Raters

The interrater agreement between the blinded raters, for the two Likert score categories ≥3 (successful) and ≤2 (unsuccessful), was kappa 0.93 for color Doppler and 0.64 for PW Doppler images; indicating very good and good agreement. Discussion

In a high‐fidelity, simulated prehospital rotor‐wing setting, we found that HEMS physicians were able to use TCCD to accurately identify the M1‐MCA in healthy volunteers, achieving identification rates above 80% and approaching the performance of a single‐reference examiner. Image quality was acceptable across examinations, demonstrating that prehospital physicians without prior neurosonology experience can achieve clinically meaningful performance after a short, focused training program, even during in‐flight air ambulance operations.

Compared with the single‐reference examiner, HEMS physicians required a median of 25 s longer to complete the examinations. Examination times appeared somewhat shorter in flight than on ground. However, this difference was small and should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size. The examination order was not fixed, and the finding may reflect random variation, increasing familiarity with the procedure, or differences in participant focus during the in‐flight setting.

For the PW Doppler assessments, HEMS physicians achieved a success rate just above 60%, whereas the single‐reference examiner approached 90%. This is a clinically relevant difference in real‐world acute stroke settings, as flow measurements are crucial to identify stenosis or occlusion in intracerebral arteries. An important finding was the substantial inter‐examiner variability observed among the HEMS physicians despite identical theoretical and practical TCCD training, Figure 4B. This was particularly evident for PW Doppler assessment, which showed marked differences in image quality ratings and success rates between physicians. These findings may suggest that additional training is needed for the HEMS physicians, as PW Doppler assessment is technically complex and operator‐dependent. Future studies should therefore consider more individualized training pathways, competency‐based assessment, and predefined proficiency criteria before study participation. Such measures may reduce inter‐examiner variability and improve the reliability and reproducibility of prehospital neurosonology performed by nonexpert operators. PSV measurements remained within the expected normal range in both examination settings [31]. Although numerically higher values were observed during in‐flight and left M1 examinations, the study was not designed to evaluate differences in velocity measurements, and these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Nevertheless, the results suggest that clinically useful prehospital TCCD examinations may be achievable after relatively limited training. Given the substantial inter‐examiner variability observed, strategies to support less experienced operators warrant further investigation. In a planned follow‐up study (UltraHEMS‐video), we will evaluate whether real‐time TCCD with screen sharing and remote expert guiding during in‐flight examinations can improve image quality, increase diagnostic success, and reduce operator‐dependent variability.

Our findings align with previous literature showing that focused training allows even novice operators to achieve satisfactory vessel visualization suitable for expert interpretation [15]. Additionally, TCCD examinations have been shown feasible outside traditional neurosonology settings with focused training, consistent with broader trends in point‐of‐care ultrasound education [32, 33].

In a real‐life HEMS setting, TCCD could be a valuable diagnostic tool for acute stroke patients enabling examination and accurate triage of the patient on‐scene, but also examinations and monitoring in flight [10, 17, 18]. This is especially true for patients with severe symptoms and suspected LVO, and for inter‐hospital transfer of confirmed LVO cases. In this study, the participants were trained to identify M1‐MCA in healthy volunteers. Detecting pathology in acute stroke patients would require more extensive experience and training. Up to 30% of ischemic strokes are caused by an LVO [34], where MCA occlusions represent around 70%. Additional knowledge and training in general TCCD detection of LVO in other cerebral arteries would be necessary for a thorough prehospital LVO screening. Telemedicine, remote expert guiding, and AI guided examinations could be solutions for integrating TCCD in a real‐life HEMS setting [35]. In a planned follow‐up study, we will explore if real‐time screen share and guiding by an in‐hospital specialist is feasible and has the potential to increase in‐flight TCCD success rate and quality. A combination of clinical examination, standardized stroke severity scoring such as the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [36], and TCCD could inform on stroke subtypes and improve field triage by accurately routing eligible patients directly to thrombectomy capable centers. Transcranial B‐mode ultrasound imaging has the potential to visualize large parenchymal hemorrhages, especially in the basal ganglia or thalamus [21, 37]. The future integration of blood‐based biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity for ICH may further mitigate the risk of misclassification and improve prehospital diagnostic accuracy. Importantly, ultrasound alone cannot rule out cerebral hemorrhage, which excludes field‐initiated thrombolysis. In this context, TCCD should be viewed as a supplementary triage tool.

CT or magnetic resonance (MR) imaging is still needed to definitively exclude intracranial hemorrhage before starting acute treatment. Currently, no radiological tool for acute stroke diagnostics is implemented into HEMS in Norway. Hence, accurate prehospital triage and initiation of acute treatment are unavailable for stroke patients assessed by HEMS care providers. This could also explain why HEMS is rarely involved in primary dispatch to acute stroke cases other than patients with symptoms severely affecting vital functions and patients in regions far from acute stroke centers [38, 39, 40, 41]. Although current CT and MR technology are not compatible with air transport, TCCD is readily available as HEMS is already equipped with ultrasound devices. When in the hands of trained personnel, TCCD is an inexpensive, promising, and validated method for detecting LVO in suspected acute stroke patients [14, 17, 18], also when tested in prehospital settings. Importantly, our study demonstrates that TCCD can be performed in flight, allowing diagnostic assessment without prolonging on‐scene time.

Despite these promising reports, TCCD for the acute stroke patient is, to our knowledge, not implemented in ground ambulances or HEMS. A contributing factor for this paradox is likely the logistical, economic, and operational challenges in deploying in‐hospital specialists into the prehospital field for evaluation of suspected stroke patients [14, 17, 18]. TCCD is also not a point of care ultrasound technique commonly used by emergency care providers [10]. Furthermore, the prehospital use of ultrasound is limited to a few modalities in selected medical conditions, and prehospital personnel need special training to achieve sufficient skills for TCCD use in acute stroke evaluations. TCCD could in the future be combined with other diagnostic tools like blood biomarkers, near infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) or other innovative devices, which could increase diagnostic accuracy, inform on stroke subtype, and potentially enable acute stroke treatment [42, 43, 44, 45].

This study has some important limitations. We tested feasibility of TCCD in a high‐fidelity simulated prehospital setting with young, healthy volunteers with pre‐study confirmed sufficient temporal windows. The results cannot be fully extrapolated to a prehospital reality typically involving elderly, acute stroke patients with a higher probability of poor acoustic windows due to increased ossification in the temporal skull [46]. Acute stroke patients may also have agitation, reduced cooperation, abnormal anatomy, or ongoing treatment that could influence the examination. Furthermore, some of the heterogeneity in both times and quality observed among the HEMS physicians might be influenced by previous experience with ultrasound and the short training program. The Vscan Air SL required formal airworthiness approval for use in this study, highlighting potential logistical considerations for future prehospital implementation. However, other portable ultrasound systems also support TCCD, and future integration may be feasible through adaptation of existing prehospital ultrasound platforms. Finally, a complete TCCD examination was not conducted, and a study of the M1‐MCA in healthy volunteers does not fully reflect the technical and diagnostic challenges encountered when examining pathological vessels. Future studies should explore strategies for standardizing TCCD training of non‐neurologists and prehospital personnel to enhance performance consistency and explore the potential for TCCD in a real‐life, prehospital acute stroke setting; all of which were outside the scope of this study.

In conclusion, after a short training program, HEMS physicians were able to accurately perform prehospital TCCD and identify the M1‐MCA segment with acceptable image quality in healthy volunteers within a rotor‐wing study setting. Substantial inter‐operator variability was observed, particularly for PW Doppler assessment, highlighting the importance of training optimization, competency‐based assessment, and exploration of remote support strategies. Further research is required to evaluate TCCD use by HEMS physicians under real‐world prehospital conditions involving acute stroke patients.
