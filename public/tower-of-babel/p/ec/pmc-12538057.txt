Association of standard dietary inflammatory index with diabetic retinopathy in diabetes: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Zihan Xu, Feipeng Jiang, Yun Zhang, Jier Ma, Jianjun Ren, Meixia Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is a major cause of vision impairment, and its development is linked to chronic inflammation. Investigating the role of dietary patterns, specifically the standard dietary inflammatory index (DII), may provide insights into modifiable risk factors for DR.ObjectiveTo quantify the impact of dietary inflammatory potential (assessed via the standard DII) on both risk of DR and structural alterations across individual retinal layers.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study using data from the UK Biobank. Cox proportional hazards models and restricted cubic splines were used to examine the correlation between the standard DII and DR in three adjusted models. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the outcomes. Linear regression models were employed to explore the associations between the standard DII and retinal structural parameters.ResultsData from 16,224 participants with diabetes from the UK Biobank were analyzed, including 1,257 (7.75%) who developed DR during the follow-up period. Higher standard DII scores were associated with an increased risk of DR in all adjusted models [Model 3: HR = 1.51, 95% CI: (1.15, 1.99), p = 0.003]. A J-shaped association between standard DII scores and DR was observed, and the relationship was significant only when the standard DII score was greater than −2.5 [HR = 1.59, 95% CI: (1.44, 2.04), p = 0.002]. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent results across different stratified groups, indicating increased DR risk. Image analysis revealed that standard DII scores were associated with the thinning of the retinal pigment epithelium layer.ConclusionsOur findings suggest a J-shaped association between standard DII score and DR risk in a multiracial sample from the UK Biobank. These findings indicate that an anti-inflammatory diet may be promising for mitigating the onset of DR.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), remains the leading cause of preventable blindness among working-age adults [1,2], affects millions globally, with cases number predicted to reach 160 million by 2045 [1]. These findings underscore the urgent need for effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Among various risk factors of DR, inflammation plays a crucial role in disease progression, affecting both early and advanced stages. Characterized by dysregulated levels of key mediators (cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and adhesion molecules), inflammation drives retinal damage via angiogenesis, endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular permeability [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Concurrently, structural change in retina has been identified prior to as well as at the time of DR diagnosis [[8], [9], [10]] and continue throughout DR progression [11].

Modifiable risk factors, particularly diet habits represent promising on DR intervention [12]. Diet pattern influence chronic systemic inflammation [13], and anti-inflammatory Mediterranean pattern corelates with reduced DR risk [14]. However, nutritional research faces methodological challenges, including recall bias in diet assessment approaches (food frequency questionnaires, 24-h recalls), high interviewer burden, and overreliance on cross-sectional designs. The complexity of nutrient composition, ingredients interactions, and temporal variations necessitates more precise and objective assessment [15].

To address these limitations, our study utilized dietary inflammatory index (DII), an objective scoring tool designed to quantify inflammatory potential of different diet system based on causal link between food parameters and systemic biomarkers [16,17]. Deprived from 927 peer-reviewed articles, DII has been consistently connected with inflammatory diseases, such as cardiovascular disease and obesity [18,19]. In DM population, higher DII is associated with increased T2DM risk, insulin resistance and fasting blood glucose level [18]. Although sensitivity may vary across cohorts, DII is valid and robust in studying diabetes and its microvascular comlications, including diabetic retinopathy which remains insuffient explored.

This study aims to evaluate the association between pro-inflammatory diets and DR prevalence, and further assess impact of diet-induced inflammation on retinal structural integrity through layer-specific optical coherence tomography.

Design and methods

Study sample

Registrations and patient consents: The UK biobank is approved by the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee, the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care and the Community Health Index Advisory Group. All participants provided written informed consent. The use of data was facilitated through UK Biobank Applications 80787.

Sample population: The UK Biobank is a population-based cohort study in which more than half a million participants aged 40–69 years were recruited between 2006 and 2010 from 22 assessment centers in England, Scotland and Wales [20]. All patients provided their health information and biological samples to the assessment centers at the baseline survey. The follow-up period lasted until the date of DR diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or termination of follow-up. The flow chart describes the selection process (Fig. 1). Participants who had DM but not had DR at baseline were included. We removed patients who lacked covariates and dietary data that could be used in calculating DII scores (Fig. 1). In the final analyses, data from a cohort of 16,224 participants were collected to explore the connection of DII score with DR development. Additionally, 7151 participants in this cohort were chosen for imaging analyses with full good-quality data on OCT images.Fig. 1Study design. Dietary intake assessment

We used data from UK Biobank, which collected the 24-h diet questionnaire through 70,000 recruiters. The UK Biobank subsequently distributed email-based questionnaires at four different periods between February 2011 and April 2012 [21]. The questionnaire covered approximately 200 common foods and drinks in 11 categories: [1] hot and cold beverages [2]; alcoholic beverages [3]; cereals [4]; milk, eggs and cheese [5]; bread, pasta and rice [6]; soups, snacks and pastries [7]; meat and fish [8]; vegetarian foods [9]; spreads, sauces and cooking oils [10]; fruits and vegetables [11]; vitamin and mineral supplements [22].

Definitions of the DM and DR

Individuals who had a physician diagnosis, insulin therapy, or met any of the following standards were considered to have DM: (1) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥7 mmol/L; (2) oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) ≥11.1 mmol/L; (3) HbA1c ≥6.5%; and (4) classic symptoms of hyperglycemia or hyperglycemic crisis and random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L.

DR was defined via a combination of main and secondary ICD-10 (Field IDs 41202, 41204: Code H36.0) and ICD-9 (Field IDs 41203, 41205: Code 3620) diagnoses for macular degeneration, self-reported macular degeneration (Field ID 20002: Code 1276).

Assessment of DII

As an indicator used to estimate the inflammatory potential of an individual's diet, a higher DII indicates greater pro-inflammatory potential [23]. The DII assesses the combined effects of 45 food parameters on inflammation via effects (+1 means increase, 1 means decrease, 0 means no effect) that each nutrient has on six inflammatory biomarkers, including IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and CRP [17]. Ultimately, 28 eligible nutrient parameters that recorded in UK Biobank were chosen: alcohol, vitamin A, C, D, E, B-6, and B12; carbohydrate; cholesterol; ω-3 fatty acids; ω-6 fatty; energy; total fat; fiber; folic acid; Fe; Mg; Zn; Se; protein; saturated fat; monounsaturated fatty acids; polyunsaturated fatty acids; trans fat; β-carotene; niacin; thiamin; riboflavin.

We used the method proposed by Shivappa et al., who offered a world-representative composite database that provides a standard mean and deviation of 45 food parameters [17]. Z score of each parameter was achieved by subtracting the standard mean from reported intake and dividing by its standard deviation. Next, the Z score was translated to a distribution between −1 and 1. The percentile also settles the argument of measurement unit. The overall food parameter-specific inflammatory effect score was subsequently multiplied by the center percentile value of each nutrient to obtain the food parameter-specific DII. Eventually, all singular food parameter-specific DII scores were summed into the overall DII score. The overall DII score was calculated for each participant. To account for total energy intake, this score was divided by the individual's daily energy intake (in kilocalories) and multiplied by 1000 to derive the standard DII per 1000 kcal, which was used as the primary exposure variable in all analyses.

Retinal optical coherence tomography (OCT), retinal layer segmentation and retinal subfield segmentation

We utilized retinal layer thickness obtained from retinal OCT images to explore the diet-induced inflammatory damage to the retinal structure in DR pathology. Topcon 3D OCT 1000 Mk2 (Topcon, Inc., Japan) was used for spectral domain OCT scans. 512 horizontal A-scans or B-scans and 128 B-scans in a 6 × 6-mm raster pattern was used for macular volume. The internal limiting membrane (ILM), nerve fiber layer (NFL), ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), external limiting membrane (ELM), photoreceptor inner and outer segment (ISOS) and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) were used to segment the retina. The subfields of the retina were divided on the basis of their relative position to the macula: central, inner inferior (II), inner nasal (IN), inner superior (IS), inner temporal (IT), outer inferior (OI), outer nasal (ON), outer superior (OS), and outer temporal (OT) regions.

Quality control measures included the image quality score, the ILM indicator, a validity count, and motion indicators. Patients were excluded from the analysis if they withdrew consent, had poor SD OCT signal strength and missing thickness values from any Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study subfield, image quality score less than 45, poor centration certainty, or poor segmentation certainty using TABS software (poorest 20% of images excluded based on each of the segmentation indicators). This led to the identification of the subset of patients with good-quality, well-centered images and central, stable fixation during the OCT scan.

Covariates

We included the following items for analysis to control for potential bias resulting from covariates. Sociodemographic status: age, sex, ethnicity, total household income before tax (low income: income < £18000, middle income: £18000 ≤ income < £52000, high income: income ≥ £52000), educational attainment (less than high school, high school or above) and employment. Lifestyle factors included alcohol consumption status (never, former or current), smoking status (never, former or current) and physical activity (the summed metabolic equivalent time score per week for all activities). Physical measures: Body mass index (BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) and HbA1c. Medical information: Diabetes duration, medication for diabetes, hypertension, medication for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, medication for hyperlipidemia and chronic kidney disease (CKD). A polygenic risk score (PRS) for DR, constructed using the clumping and thresholding (C + T) method implemented in PRSice software [24], was included to assess genetic susceptibility.

Statistical analysis

Baseline analysis: Baseline characteristics were presented as the means and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables normally distributed and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The median and interquartile range were calculated for skewed distributed variables. Differences were compared between groups via analysis of variance and the chi-square test.

Longitudinal analysis

A Cox proportional hazards model was conducted with proportional hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the prospective relationship between standard DII and DR. The proportional hazards assumption for the primary exposure variable (standard DII per 1000 kcal) and key covariates was formally assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and tested statistically using the cox.zph function in the R survival package. Global tests for the model and specific tests for the standard DII exposure variable indicated no significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption (p > 0.05 for standard DII and global test). The follow-up time was calculated from the date of baseline assessment to the date that DR was diagnosed, death, or end of the follow-up (censored availability of record data in UKB on February 28, 2018, for England; July 31, 2021, for Scotland; September 30, 2021, for Wales), whichever occurred first. Participants were categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4) based on their standard DII scores, with the specific cut-off points provided in Table S1. And we subsequently adjusted segmental and whole covariates into models to further identify their interference on the relationship in standard DII and DR risk: Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for the Sociodemographic and lifestyles factors (age, sex, ethnicity, education, household income, employment, physical activity, smoking status, drinking status and BMI). Model 3 was further adjusted for Diabetes duration, hypertension, hyperlipidemia and CKD.

Restricted cubic splines analysis based on Cox proportional hazards models was performed to characterize the dose-response associations in Model 3. The restricted cubic splines analysis was particularly effective at visualizing potential nonlinear correlations by conducting a piecewise linear regression model that estimates the threshold effect through a smoothing curve [25].

Sensitivity analyses

We performed the following sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings:

(1) Further Covariates Adjustment: We further adjusted models for the medication for diabetes, medication for hypertension, medication for hyperlipidemia and PRS for diabetic retinopathy DR. (2) Subgroup Analyses: To examine potential effect modification, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by: age (<65 or ≥65 years), sex (male or female), race (White or other), income group (low, middle, high), employment status (unemployed or employed), alcohol consumption status (never, former, or current), smoking status (never, former, or current), BMI (<25 or ≥25 kg/m2), hypertension (yes or no), hyperlipidemia (yes or no), and CKD (yes or no). The p-values for the interaction terms between the standard DII exposure and each subgroup variable were adjusted for the False Discovery Rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. (3) Exclusion of Recent Ophthalmic Conditions/Surgeries: To mitigate potential confounding from acute ocular issues, we excluded participants who reported eye infections or underwent ophthalmic surgeries within 4 weeks prior to the baseline assessment and repeated all primary analyses.

Image analysis

We used a linear regression model with the false discovery rate (FDR) method to further explore the associations between standard DII and retinal structural alterations layer by layer in Model 3.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

We selected 48,979 diabetes patients without DR at baseline from an initial cohort of 502,369 participants in the UK Biobank. Of these, 16,224 patients had complete data for calculating the standard DII and were included in further analyses. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between participants included in the dietary analysis and those excluded due to missing dietary data were provided in Table S2. The prevalence of DR did not differ significantly between groups (7.7% vs 8.2%, p = 0.075). For included participants, during the follow-up period of 14.67 years (SD: 2.22), 7.75% (1,257 patients) developed DR. The distribution of standard DII scores in the overall study population is presented in Figure S1.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized as follows: the average age of the participants was 60.00 years (SD: 7.23), and they had been living with diabetes for an average of 9.58 years (SD: 6.75). DR was more prevalent among older patients (p < 0.001), males (p = 0.014), nonwhite individuals (p = 0.009), and former smokers (p = 0.023). Patients with middle incomes were also more likely to develop DR (p = 0.015). Additionally, patients with higher levels of HbA1c (p < 0.001), longer diabetes durations (p < 0.001), hyperlipidemia (p < 0.001), hypertension (p < 0.001), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) (p < 0.001) had more proportion of DR than those without these conditions. Other characteristics were similar between two groups. Most participants in both groups were employed, well educated (high school or above), and current drinkers. Over 50% of participants in both groups had a history of hypertension.

Association between standard DII and DR

The results indicated a significant association between the standard DII and the risk of DR (Table 2). In the fully adjusted Model 3, higher standard DII scores were associated with an increased risk of DR, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.51, 95% CI: (1.15, 1.99), p = 0.003. This suggested that a more pro-inflammatory diet was linked to a greater risk of DR. Sensitivity analysis

After additional adjustment for diabetes medications, hypertension medications, hyperlipidemia medications, and PRS for DR, higher standard DII scores remained significantly associated with increased DR risk (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.10–1.91; p = 0.008; Table S3). A significant dose-response relationship was observed across standard DII quartiles (p for trend = 0.026), with the highest quartile showing a trend toward elevated risk (HR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.02–1.28). These findings were consistent with the primary analysis.

To further assess robustness, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, income, employment status, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI, hypertension status, hyperlipidemia status, and CKD history. The association between standard DII scores and DR risk persisted consistently across all subgroups (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Subgroup analysis. Additionally, after excluding participants with recent eye infections or ophthalmic surgeries (within 4 weeks prior to baseline), the association strengthened: higher standard DII scores predicted increased DR risk (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.12–1.91; p = 0.006; Table S4). The dose-response relationship across quartiles also remained significant (p for trend = 0.037; HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.32), aligning with the main findings.

Image analysis

The participants with higher standard DII scores presented a thinner RPE layer across all subfields, with the inner inferior region showing the most significant thinning, followed by the inner nasal, central, and inner temporal regions (Fig. 4C, Table S5). While a thinner whole RPE layer was also observed in patients with higher standard DII scores, this finding was not statistically significant (Fig. 4A, Table S5). Additionally, we observed disproportionate changes in macular thickness. Specifically, the central, outer nasal, and outer inferior regions were thicker, whereas the inner, outer temporal, and outer superior regions were thinner (Fig. 4C, Table S5). Discussion

Our study revealed a significant association between a higher standard DII score and the risk of DR. We also found a J-shaped relationship between the standard DII and DR prevalence, with DR incidence progressively increasing when standard DII score was greater than −2.5. Image analysis further revealed that participants with higher standard DII scores had a thinner RPE layer across all subfields.

Our investigation provides robust evidence demonstrating a significant association between elevated standard DII scores and increased risk of DR. This finding aligns with and extends the current body of scientific literature, emphasizing the pivotal role of diet-induced inflammation in the pathogenesis and progression of DR. Chen et al. analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005–2008 and found that higher standard DII scores were associated with increased DR risk, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 1.38 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.06–1.81, p < 0.001) [26]. Similarly, Pan et al. reported that among diabetic patients, each unit increase in standard DII corresponded to a 14% increase in DR prevalence (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.02–1.26) [27]. These findings collectively corroborate our conclusion that pro-inflammatory dietary patterns may promote DR development by exacerbating inflammatory responses. Interestingly, above studies used data from NHANES database, UK Biobank validation remains limited. Study with UK Biobank consistently shows higher standard DII and standard DII predicts increased DM mortality [28,29]. These findings align with our DR results, jointly supporting standard DII's clinical relevance for diabetic adverse outcome.

A noteworthy finding from our study is the J-shaped relationship observed between standard DII scores and DR prevalence, indicating that DR risk gradually increases when standard DII scores exceed −2.5. This non-linear association with a turning point suggests that the mild anti-inflammatory diets may be insufficient to prevent diabetic retinopathy (DR) development, necessiating more radical anti-inflammatory dietary patterns. Though randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are required to validate this observation.

This pattern resonates with findings from Pan et al., who identified a non-linear relationship between standard DII and DR prevalence in diabetic patients, with a significant positive correlation when standard DII <−0.87 and a plateau effect at higher values [27]. In contrast, Zhang et al. reported a U-shaped relationship between standard DII and DR risk, where both extremely low and high standard DII scores were associated with elevated DR prevalence, with minimal risk occurring in the middle range [30]. The discrepancy between our J-shaped finding and the U-shaped relationship reported by Zhang et al. may be attributed to differences in study populations, methodological approaches, or the influence of unmeasured confounders. Interestingly, Tabung et al. proposed that extremely low DII scores might reflect dietary patterns lacking essential nutrients despite being anti-inflammatory, potentially explaining the increased risk at very low DII values in some studies [31]. While our findings differ slightly in curve morphology, these studies collectively highlight the complex non-linear association between dietary inflammation and DR risk, warranting further investigation to elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Our imaging analysis revealed that higher standard DII scores were associated with thinning of the RPE layer across all subregions. The RPE plays a crucial role in maintaining retinal homeostasis, and its thinning may represent early structural damage induced by dietary inflammation. This finding provides novel insights into the pathophysiological link between dietary inflammation and DR. Although existing literature has not directly examined the relationship between standard DII and RPE thickness, the role of inflammation in retinal damage is well-established.

Experimental studies by Rangasamy et al. demonstrated that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, which are elevated in pro-inflammatory dietary patterns, directly impair RPE function and integrity through disruption of tight junctions and increased oxidative stress [32]. The anatomic proximity of RPE cells to systemic circulation enhances their vulnerability to inflammatory factors. Additionally, Rowan et al. showed in animal models that high-fat, pro-inflammatory diets lead to RPE thinning and dysfunction prior to overt retinopathy, supporting our observation that RPE changes may represent early manifestations of diet-induced retinal damage [33]. Meanwhile, subsequently activated signaling pathway (such as NF-κB pathway), extracellular mitochondria and inflammatory cell (macroglia) permit propelling RPE damage and possibly drive consequent RPE thinning [[34], [35], [36]]. Our study extends these findings by establishing a direct correlation between standard DII scores and RPE thinning in humans, suggesting that dietary inflammation may promote DR development through compromising retinal structural integrity.

From clinical and public health perspectives, these findings carry significant implications. Given the strong association between elevated standard DII scores and increased DR risk, interventions targeting dietary inflammation may constitute effective strategies for DR prevention and management. Encouraging diabetic patients to adopt anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy fats, may help reduce DR risk. A systematic review by Cheung et al. found that dietary patterns rich in antioxidants and anti-inflammatory nutrients (like the Mediterranean diet) exhibited protective effects against DR development [37]. Díaz-López et al. showed adhering anti-inflammatory diet (Mediterranean diet) with supplemented nutrients (virgin olive oil or nuts) undergoes 44% and 37% lower DR risk in a 6 years median follow-up of randomized controlled trial analysis [38]. Another large scale longitudinal study with over 20000 participants shared similar results that anti-inflammatory food was associated with reduced DR threat [39]. Potential of diets could offer actionable guidance for clinicians in formulating personalized nutritional recommendations. Furthermore, foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids (such as fatty fish) have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties and are associated with reduced DR risk, as evidenced by the PREDIMED trial, which reported a 48% reduction in sight-threatening DR among participants with type 2 diabetes who regularly consumed at least 500 mg/day of omega-3 fatty acids [40]. Jiang et al. further demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial that a 12-week anti-inflammatory dietary intervention significantly reduced serum inflammatory markers and improved retinal microvascular parameters in patients with early-stage DR [41]. Moreover, the positive effect of food originate from contained bioactive molecule, some of which were found suppressing inflammation in DR that further support the potential benefits of anti-inflammatory dietary approaches, including phlorizin, biochanin A, citri reticulatae pericarpium [42,43].

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our analysis excluded 66.8% of eligible UK Biobank participants with diabetes due to missing dietary data, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by "healthy volunteer" bias (healthier, higher socioeconomic status, more health-conscious participants) in UK biobank and the data-driven DII threshold derived from restricted cubic splines that requires external validation to mitigate overfitting concerns. Third, while we employed energy-standardized DII scores to adjust for total energy intake, our focus on dietary inflammatory potential does not account for other dietary patterns (e.g., Mediterranean diet) that may influence DR risk and could correlate with standard DII. Residual confounding by overall dietary quality remains possible. Fourth, DR identification relied on composite administrative codes and self-report without retinal imaging validation. While this approach is well-established in cohort studies, it may undercapture early-stage DR or misclassify non-diabetic eye conditions. Future studies with standardized retinal assessments are needed to confirm our findings. Fifth, reliance on a single baseline 24-h dietary assessment from the UK Biobank is susceptible to recall bias and may not capture long-term dietary patterns or temporal changes. Sixth, despite comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, comorbidities, medications, and genetic susceptibility (PRS), residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly quantified factors (e.g., detailed longitudinal glycemic control, family history, or environmental exposures) cannot be excluded. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the association between standard DII and DR.

Future key consideration warrants further validation through standardized detection of inflammatory biomarkers, immunohistochemical markerscell morphology, index adjusted by energy, retinal function assessment (electrophysiology). Regarding the high burden of follow-up and record, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with small samples is of great importance.

Additionally, our findings regarding RPE thinning require further investigation to validate its potential as an early marker of DR and elucidate its specific pathological connection to dietary inflammation.

Furthermore, Intervention studies assessing the impact of anti-inflammatory dietary modifications on retinal structure and function would provide valuable insights into the potential reversibility of these changes. And the exploration of gene-diet interactions may help identify individuals particularly susceptible to inflammation-mediated retinal damage, enabling more targeted preventive strategies.

Conclusions

Overall, our study revealed that higher standard DII scores increase DR risk and induce retinal structural remodeling, characterized by RPE layer thinning, with the inner inferior region exhibits the pronounced alterations.
