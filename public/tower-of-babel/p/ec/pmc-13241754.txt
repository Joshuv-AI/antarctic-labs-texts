These Boots Are Made for Walking: Sex‐Specific Physiological and Metabolomic Strategies Reflect Male‐Skewed Vulnerability to Ocean Warming in a Keystone Amphipod
Authors: Joana Filipa Fernandes, Lauric Feugere, Mário S Diniz, Piero Calosi, Diana Madeira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSex‐specific variation can critically shape species' physiological responses to environmental change, with potentially strong implications during reproduction. Yet this source of variation is often overlooked. To help address this paucity, we investigated fitness and whole‐organism traits and metabolomic profiles to elevated temperature in males and females of Gammarus locusta , a keystone intertidal amphipod inhabiting thermally dynamic coastal environments. Individuals were gradually acclimated to four ecologically relevant temperatures (16°C, 21°C, 26°C and 31°C) and maintained at these conditions for 21 d under controlled laboratory settings. Survival declined at 26°C and 31°C in both sexes, and females exhibited consistently higher upper thermal limits, broader thermal safety margins, but lower thermal acclimation capacity compared to males. Whilst reproductive output was lower in elevated temperatures (26°C and 31°C), juveniles' body length increased, indicating a “quality‐over‐quantity” reproduction strategy. At the metabolomic level, females exhibited greater plasticity, with enhanced pathways linked to energy metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and cellular stress defence, suggesting adaptive activation rather than heightened vulnerability, consistent with their higher thermal tolerance and survival. Elevated temperatures also impaired amphipods' energetic status, with both glucose and ATP:ADP ratio decreasing, particularly at 31°C. Overall, we show that sex‐specific metabolomic strategies define different sex thermal tolerance levels in G. locusta . As ocean warming intensifies, males' greater vulnerability could skew population sex ratios toward females. This imbalance may limit mating opportunities and alter population operational sex ratio and therefore dynamics, if the number of males is insufficient to fertilise all females. Our study highlights the importance of considering sex‐specific physiological responses by integrating them with life‐history and fitness measures to build a mechanistic understanding of how thermal stress may shape reproductive dynamics and, ultimately, affect population viability. By linking cellular metabolism with organismal fitness, we provide the cross‐scale insight needed to more accurately forecast marine invertebrates' responses to global change.Keywords: acclimation capacity, climate change, energy metabolism, global change, invertebrate, metabolomics, plasticity, temperature, thermal tolerance

Introduction

Biological variation between sexes is a deeply rooted feature of animal life, influencing not only individual traits but also population structures, dynamics and ecological functions (Edmands 2021; Gissi et al. 2023). Males and females often differ in their morphology, endocrine function, and physiological abilities (Berns 2013; Valencak et al. 2017). Particularly, there is growing evidence that females and males show distinct responses to environmental challenges across taxa (Gissi et al. 2023; Pérez‐Cerezales et al. 2018). For instance, females typically allocate more energy toward somatic maintenance and reproductive investment, while males often exhibit a greater investment in growth, development, and mating behaviours (Hinojosa and Thiel 2003; Merrill and Collins 2015; Raymond et al. 2004). Despite these fundamental differences, to date, only a few ecological and physiological studies considered sex differences when characterising species' tolerance and adaptability to environmental stressors (Ellis et al. 2017; Iwasa and Yamaguchi 2023), thus potentially obscuring a key source of biological variation. Understanding the extent and direction of sex‐specific responses, as well as the mechanisms underpinning such differences, is therefore essential for predicting population‐level tolerance and resilience, identifying the mechanisms underlying vulnerability, and anticipating demographic or reproductive shifts under future warming scenarios.

Ocean warming represents one of the most pervasive and multifaceted stressors threatening marine ecosystems (Bopp et al. 2013; Venegas et al. 2023). It is characterised not only by a chronic increase in baseline seawater temperatures, but also by the increasing frequency, intensity, and duration of acute thermal anomalies, such as marine heatwaves (MHWs; Hobday et al. 2016; IPCC 2022). MHWs also reduce oxygen solubility (Shepherd et al. 2017), alter the water chemistry (Orr 2011), disrupt seasonal thermal cues (Bennett et al. 2019), and shift metabolic baselines in marine organisms. Therefore, the effects of MHWs may ripple across all levels of biological organisation, from molecular pathways to whole‐organism physiological traits, species interactions and population dynamics (Sokolova et al. 2011; Venegas et al. 2023). Moreover, a chronic increase in temperature can drive long‐term shifts in habitat suitability and alter phenology, whereas episodic MHWs can rapidly push organisms beyond their physiological limits, triggering mortality events, reproductive failure, and community collapse (Smith et al. 2023; Venegas et al. 2023). This particularly applies to marine ectotherms, which often present narrower thermal safety margins than terrestrial ectotherms (Villeneuve and White 2024). Because thermal stress affects energetic balance, development, and reproductive success, sex‐specific physiological strategies may modulate how well males and females can cope with warming. Therefore, disentangling sex differences becomes fundamental to forecast population trajectories under climate change. In this context, thermal plasticity, defined as the capacity of an individual to adjust its thermal limits through acclimation (Ghalambor et al. 2007), emerges as a crucial adaptive mechanism (Bennett et al. 2021). By shifting their thermal limits upward, organisms can expand their thermal tolerance window to alleviate the risk of acute thermal injury during sudden or sustained heat exposure (Magozzi and Calosi 2015; Whiteley and Mackenzie 2016). Growing scientific evidence suggests that thermal plasticity may differ between sexes. Despite this, a large proportion of studies (ranging from 75% to 90% – Edmands 2021; Pottier et al. 2021) still do not report or consider sex, making it difficult to assess the role of sex‐specific differences in species' responses to global changes. Therefore, identifying when, why, and how sexes differ in their thermal responses is critical for understanding population tolerance and for generating mechanistic insights that can inform broader, cross‐species predictions.

The few studies that analysed sex reported differences in energy allocation strategies, reproductive roles, and life‐history pacing, evidenced that males and females exhibit distinct physiological responses to elevated temperatures (Tarka et al. 2018). While consistent sex‐specific heat tolerance patterns remain elusive, several studies report that females exhibit greater thermal plasticity, and may possess inherently higher heat tolerance across taxa (Edmands 2021; Pottier et al. 2021). This enhanced plasticity in females may stem from physiological adaptations enabling them to sustain core functions under elevated temperatures. This is particularly true not only during the energetically‐demanding reproductive phase, where oocyte formation substantially shapes female metabolic allocation and influences other physiological processes (Edmands 2021; Hayward and Gillooly 2011; Lipaeva et al. 2025), but also during post‐extrusion, as an important fraction of their energy metabolism is diverted to eggs' ventilation and/or parental cares (Marochi et al. 2021). Conversely, males often show a more limited thermal performance capacity (Sasaki et al. 2019; Vermandele et al. 2024), suggesting that they are less plastic and more vulnerable to ocean warming. However, the cellular physiological adaptations explaining these differences are still poorly understood. Most notably, the disproportional vulnerability to increased temperature reported between sexes may disrupt sex ratios, and reduce reproductive output, brood size and egg viability (Donelson et al. 2010; Edmands 2021). Ultimately, this can have dramatic demographic effects (Brunel and Boucher 2007). Furthermore, the presence of sex‐specific responses to warming may cause chronic warming to exacerbate existing sex‐dependent differences in developmental timing (Sasaki et al. 2019), moult cycles (Nelson 1990), or reproductive maturation (Spinks et al. 2021), which could lead to population‐level mismatches.

Whole‐organism thermal tolerance and plasticity are underlied by complex molecular mechanisms. For instance, elevated temperatures can impair enzymes, destabilise cellular membranes, and trigger the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), all of which compromise homeostasis (Benedetti et al. 2021; Lesser 2006; Sokolova et al. 2011). Organisms may counteract these effects by adjusting biochemical pathways, for example by producing antioxidant defences (Vinagre et al. 2014), reshaping energy metabolism (Beaudreau et al. 2024; Guscelli et al. 2023; Lemieux and Blier 2022; Li et al. 2017), modulating osmolytes production (Madeira et al. 2021), and inducing chaperones (e.g., heat‐shock proteins) (Madeira et al. 2014; Shatilina et al. 2011). These molecular fingerprints reflect marine ectotherms' ability for cellular rewiring and can be harnessed to describe their thermal tolerance. Although several studies have examined isolated temperatures or specific physiological traits (e.g., Neuparth et al. 2002; Rastrick and Whiteley 2013), a mechanistic, integrated multi‐level sex‐specific perspective on molecular, physiological, and reproductive thermal tolerance is surprisingly missing.

To address such a gap, we investigated the sex‐specific fitness response, thermal tolerance and metabolomic plasticity across a broad thermal window in females and males of a keystone intertidal amphipod, Gammarus locusta , used as a model species. Estuarine and intertidal amphipods inhabit some of the most thermally variable environments (Bozinovic et al. 2011), undergoing daily cycles of desiccation, high diurnal heating, and temperature fluctuations (Eckersley and Scrosati 2012; Vaz et al. 2005). In this context, amphipods must cope with a wide range of temperatures, including both marine extreme events and chronic ocean warming trends. We hypothesise that sex‐specific differences will emerge across multiple biological levels of G. locusta in response to thermal stress. Based on existing literature (Gissi et al. 2024; Vermandele et al. 2024), we expect males to be more vulnerable than females to elevated temperatures because their faster growth rates and larger body size may impose higher energetic demands and a lesser scope for energy reallocating toward heat‐stress defences. Conversely, we expect females to maintain performance under high temperatures through enhanced plasticity and/or energy production mechanisms.

Material and Methods

Study Area and Field Environmental Data Collection

We selected as a study area the coastal lagoon of the Ria de Aveiro in Portugal (Mira channel, Costa Nova, 40°36′ 59.3″ N 8°44′ 25.3″ W) (Figure S1). Here, to characterise the thermal environment of the habitat of G. locusta , intertidal temperature data was collected every 30 min using data loggers (with a resolution of 0.1°C; v.2.4 27 mm, Envlogger ElectricBlue, Porto, Portugal). Temperature data was acquired during summer months, i.e., between May 30th and October 17th, 2022. Data loggers (n = 3) were deployed, ensuring that they were always submerged at low tide, in three tide pools at the collection site. After that, descriptive statistics (means and standard deviation—SD, minimum and maximum values) were computed and data were plotted using the R package macleish vR 4.4.0. These data were used to inform and to calculate thermal safety margins (TSM).

Specimens' Collection, Transport and Husbandry

Gammarus locusta individuals were collected in the Mira channel in July 2022 (n total = 858 adults including ovigerous females and males; females 0.021 ± 0.003 g and males 0.038 ± 0.012 g of weight measured on a subsample of 50 indiv.). At the time of collection, environmental conditions were: seawater temperature = 20.4°C ± 0.5°C, salinity = 30.7 ± 0.3, dissolved oxygen = 8.96 ± 0.11 mg L−1, and pH = 7.98 ± 0.28. Specimens were transported (over a 7 min drive) to the ECOMARE Laboratory for Innovation and Sustainability of Marine Biological Resources, Gafanha da Nazaré, Portugal (40°38′17.3″ N 8°43′43.8″ W) inside 20 L buckets filled with local, constantly aerated, seawater. After arriving at the facilities, individuals were haphazardly placed inside forty 20 L plastic tanks (n = 40 amphipods per tank). Tanks contained artificial seawater (made with reverse osmosis water and artificial sea salt; Pro‐Reef, Tropic Marin, Lisboa, Portugal), and a 40‐mm layer of sand (grain size ±0.3 mm) collected from a nearby beach (Praia de Biarritz, Costa Nova, Ria de Aveiro; 40°62′87.47″ N, 8°74′58.88″ W) to mimic habitat conditions. In addition, constant aeration was provided, and the photoperiod was set to 14 L:10 D to mimic summer season light exposure. Seawater temperature and salinity were measured daily with a multi‐parameter probe (MU 6100H, VWR, Leuven, Belgium). Mean ± SD conditions were: temperature 20°C ± 1°C, salinity 30 ± 3. Other seawater parameters were measured twice weekly: pH at 8.1 ± 0.1, total ammonia < 0.1 mg L−1, nitrites < 0.05 mg L−1, nitrates < 0.1 mg L−1, and dissolved oxygen > 8.5 ± 0.3 mg L−1. To ensure suitable water quality, we conducted partial water changes (50% of the total volume) every 2 days. Amphipods were kept under these conditions for 15 days prior to the beginning of the experiment to allow them to adjust to laboratory conditions and recover from any possible stress due to collection and transportation. Mortality during this acclimation period was low, with only six individuals (< 1% of total n = 858) dying across all tanks. Only females carrying unfertilised, dark‐coloured eggs were used for posteriori experiment; egg colour was monitored daily during the husbandry period to ensure synchronised reproductive status. Individuals were fed ad libitum with Ulva spp. collected at the sampling site.

Experimental Design, System and Protocol

To investigate sex‐specific thermal responses, as well as fitness, in the amphipod G. locusta , adult females and males (n = 400 of each sex) were exposed for 21 d to one of four temperatures: 16°C, 21°C, 26°C and 31°C. These temperatures were selected to reflect a thermal gradient including optimal (16°C), supra‐optimal (21°C and 26°C), and near‐critical (31°C), based on natural temperature recordings (Figure 1) and future climatic predictions for the end of the century (IPCC 2022). These temperatures span the full thermal range experienced in the species' intertidal habitat—from typical summer conditions (~16°C–21°C) to the upper extremes recorded during low tide in warm days (up to ~28°C–29°C), with 31°C representing projected near‐future summer maxima under warming scenarios (Figure 1).

Daily seawater temperature in Ria de Aveiro, collected every 30 min, from June to October 2022. Each point represents an individual temperature measurement. The black line shows a smoothed trend (LOESS). The maximum recorded temperature (28.6°C) and minimum (14.9°C) are highlighted with circled markers and labelled.

Temperature treatments were achieved independently in individual closed plastic aquariums, with a total of 20 replicate tanks (five replicates per temperature treatment; box size = 300 × 200 × 170 mm, 6.5 L), set up on two shelves in a climatised 20°C room. Briefly, five replicate tanks per treatment were set up and randomly distributed on a bench, with approx. 20 male and 20 female individuals per tank. Each tank was individually regulated by a submersible heater (Thermocontrol 3612 50 W, Eheim, Deizisau, Germany) or chiller (HC‐300A, Hailea, Guangdoung, China), depending on the experimental temperature group. Tank water parameters were monitored daily using a multiparameter probe (MU 6100H, VWR, Leuven, Belgium) to measure temperature, pH, salinity, and oxygen. Additionally, ammonia, nitrates, and nitrites were measured with colorimetric test kits twice a week. Artificial seawater (prepared as described above) was supplied to each tank at a constant salinity of 30. The experimental bench was equipped with fluorescent lamps (V165 LED, ViparSpectra, CA, USA), and the photoperiod was set to a 14 L:10 D (as described above). Each tank included approximately 40 mm of sand (as described above) and one oyster shell (to create and mimic tide pool refuges), and amphipods were fed ad libitum with Ulva spp. to simulate natural habitat conditions as closely as possible during the 21 d exposure period. To prevent thermal shock, temperature in each tank was gradually adjusted from a starting point of 20°C to the target experimental temperature by 1°C h−1 increments or decreases (according to Fernandes et al. 2023; Missionário et al. 2022). Oxygen levels in each tank were maintained through continuous aeration provided by an air stone connected via silicone tubing to an air compressor (LP‐100, Resun, Guangdong, China). After 21 d of exposure (D21), one female and one male per tank (n = five tanks per temperature treatment) were collected, quickly weighed, and immediately flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored in a −80°C freezer for metabolomic analyses (see details below). Additionally, three individuals per tank (n = 15 per temperature treatment) were haphazardly collected for Critical Thermal Maximum (CTmax) assays. No feeding occurred 24 h prior to the sampling for metabolomics and physiological assays to avoid the effect of specific dynamic action (SDA) and reduce variability due to recent food intake.

Survival

Mortality was observed at the end of the experiment and calculated as the proportion of surviving individuals relative to the initial number placed in each tank. Sex was not included as a factor in survival models because, at the end of the exposure period, the sex of some deceased individuals (especially at higher temperatures) could not be reliably identified due to rapid deterioration. An individual was considered dead when it showed no movement, no response to gentle stimulation, no pleopod or appendage activity, and typically remained motionless at the bottom of the tank, consistent with standard criteria for mortality assessment in amphipods well established for this species (Costa and Costa 1999).

Upper Thermal Tolerance Limits, Heat Tolerance Plasticity and Thermal Safety Margins

Thermal tolerance was determined using a dynamic temperature assay to define specimens' Critical Thermal Maximum (CTmax), which is commonly used in ectothermic animals (Madeira, Narciso, Cabral, and Vinagre 2012; Vinagre et al. 2016). CTmax was determined using a temperature‐controlled water bath, where individuals were exposed to a constant increase of seawater temperature at a rate of 2°C h−1 (Fernandes et al. 2023) until they reached the pre‐defined endpoint of loss of locomotor performance. This was assessed by gently tapping or tilting the beaker to elicit a swimming response, allowing us to measure individuals' CTmax, defined as the temperature at which the individual failed to respond and passively sank to the bottom. Three individuals from the same tank were carefully transferred from the tanks to 250 mL glass beakers filled with artificial seawater (prepared as described above) and a standardised 20‐mm thin layer of sand. These beakers (five per temperature, 20 in total, each with the three individuals from the same tank) were then placed in the temperature‐controlled water bath (Optima T100, Grant Instruments, Cambridge, UK). The CTmax trial for each group began at its respective acclimation temperature, ensuring continuity between pre‐exposure and testing conditions. This procedure follows the latest recommendations to avoid differential thermal shock and injury that would otherwise occur if CTmax trials all started at a common temperature (Raby et al. 2025).

A total of 15 individuals per exposure temperature (n = 7–8 females and 7–8 males per temperature treatment) were tested. To accurately track water temperature during the assay without disturbing the amphipods, we used a multiparameter probe (MU 6100H, VWR).

At the end of the experimental period, reproductive output was assessed by counting all offspring present in each tank. Juveniles were preserved in 96% ethanol for later measurements. A representative subsample of juveniles (30 per tank, five tanks per temperature, n total = 600) was used to assess mean juvenile size per temperature using a stereomicroscope (DMS 300, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) in conjunction with Leica LasX software, following the method described in Costa and Costa (1999). Characterisation of Metabolomic Profiles

The metabolomics profiles of female and male amphipods exposed to different temperatures were characterised using a targeted method developed by Les Laboratoires Iso‐ BioKem Inc. (modified from Beaudreau et al. 2024; Guscelli et al. 2023). Briefly, for metabolite extraction, samples were freeze‐dried for 24 h, weighed on a microscale (XPR6UD5, Columbus, Mettler Toledo, OH, USA) before being homogenised in a bead‐resistant tube with 2.8 mm glass beads (Fisherbrand Bulk Beads, cat. number 15–340‐160, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 6000 rpm for 30 s (Precellys 24, Bertin corporation, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France). Once the extraction solution (v/v mixture of 2,2,2‐Trifluoroethanol/LC–MS grade water) was added, samples were vortexed for 10 s and centrifuged at 30,130 × g for 5 min at 5°C to collect and transfer 250 mL of supernatant in an amber HPLC vial with insert (Wheaton, NJ, USA) to analyse the dried extract. Finally, 10 μL of the supernatant were injected in a high‐performance liquid chromatography system (HPLC 1260 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to a mass spectrometer (6420 Triple Quad, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) to detect targeted metabolites (metabolite list provided in Table S1). The absolute quantification of these metabolites (in ng mL−1) was assessed with the MassHunter QQQ quantitative (Quant‐my‐Way) software (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) using a calibration curve previously created using Valine‐d8 and Pyruvate‐d3 standards (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Tewksbury, MA, USA). Raw data matrices were normalised by dry weight and final concentration values were expressed as ng of metabolite mg−1 dry weight.

Data Analyses and Bioinformatics

All analyses were performed in the R software (R Core Team 2024) (v4.4.2) and plots produced with the ggplot2 package and edited on Adobe Illustrator (29.2.1). Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were validated using the Shapiro–Wilk test shapiro.test and Levene's test leveneTest from the car package, respectively.

Survival

To test whether elevated temperatures negatively impact amphipods' survival, the proportion of surviving individuals at the end of the exposure time was analysed using a one‐way ANOVA test with Temperature as a fixed factor using the aov function from the stats package. When significant effects were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons between temperature groups were conducted using the Tukey's HSD test via the TukeyHSD function.

CTmax

To determine if the CTmax data followed a normal distribution, a histogram, a QQ‐plot, and Shapiro–Wilk tests were carried out using the package stats. CTmax was analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model (LMM; package lme4), with Temperature and Sex as fixed factors, wet weight as a covariate and Tank as a random effect. The models were ranked according to the Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC), and the model with the lowest AIC was reported. Quality of fit was determined considering the Null deviance and Residual deviance values. Random effects and covariate (wet weight) were found not to have a significant effect (p = 0.532 and p = 0.101 respectively) on CTmax and were removed from analyses.

Reproductive Output and Juveniles Body Length

The effect of temperature on reproductive output was assessed using a generalised linear model (GLM, using the glm function from the stat R package) with a negative binomial distribution to account for overdispersion in count data. Temperature was included as a fixed factor and the final number of females in each tank as a covariate. Juveniles' body length was analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model (lmer function from the lme4 package), with Temperature as a fixed factor and Tank as a random effect. Post hoc comparisons were performed, where appropriate, using estimated marginal means via the emmeans function from the emmeans package.

Targeted Metabolomics Data

As metabolomics datasets can contain a high number of missing values, the matrices were first screened and processed for missing data imputation. Across the 49 metabolites analysed (27 amino acids, 9 organic acids, 3 sugars and alcohols, 1 fatty acyl and 9 other metabolites; see for the full list and details Table S1) in 40 specimens, a total of 70 values were missing due to concentrations falling below the limit of quantification (LOQ), corresponding to approximately 3.6% of the dataset. Given the nature of these missing values—classified as ‘Missing Not at Random’ (MNAR)—we followed the protocol proposed by (Wei et al. 2018) using the online web‐tool (https://metabolomics.cc.hawaii.edu/software/MetImp/; last accessed April 2025). Briefly, we applied the QRILC algorithm (Quantile Regression Imputation of Left‐Censored Data) after filtering out features with more than 30% group‐wise missing data (seed = 1234). This procedure led to the exclusion of two metabolites out of the 49 originally targeted. Finally, the database was imported into Metaboanalyst 6.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/, accessed in April 2025) (Pang et al. 2024) to be cube‐root transformed, to follow normality and homoscedasticity assumptions, and pareto‐scaled, to adjust for differences in abundances between metabolites. These pre‐processing steps are commonly used transformations for metabolomic data (Pang et al. 2021; van den Berg et al. 2006).

Metabolomics data were analysed in multiple steps using a suite of multivariate, univariate and functional analysis tools. First, transformed metabolite data were analysed by principal component analysis (PCA) using singular value decomposition with the prcomp base function. The screeplot R function helped evaluate the proportion of variation among temperature and sex groups explained by each component and selected the first two PCA components. The first two PCA components explained most of the variation (> 50% of total) and were plotted using the ggplot2 package as a function of Temperature‐ and/or Sex–groupings. A permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was applied to all 40 samples to test for the effects of Temperature, Sex and the interaction between Temperature × Sex on the whole metabolome (response of 47 targeted metabolites) using the adonis2 function (number of permutations = 9999, distance method = Euclidean) from the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2020). The fixed independent variables were assumed to be categorical for this analysis (Temperature and Sex). The temperature treatment was considered as a categorical variable to facilitate multivariate analysis and for ease of interpretation. Individual dry weight was used as a proxy for body size and included as a covariate to account for the sexual dimorphism in size, females being smaller than males. To further investigate differences in metabolomic profiles across experimental temperatures and between sex, a supervised sparsed partial least squares discriminant analysis (sPLS‐DA) was conducted using the splsda function from the mixOmics R package (Rohart et al. 2017). Separate sPLS‐DA models were constructed for: (i) all samples grouped by treatment Temperature, (ii) all samples grouped by Sex, and (iii) within‐sex subsets (females and males separately), with treatment temperature as the grouping factor. For each model, individual sample scores were visualised in the first two latent dimensions (components) using plotIndiv, with ellipses (95% confidence intervals) overlaid to indicate group separation. Component 1 (PLS1) scores were extracted for downstream visualisation and linear modelling. The discriminatory performance of the sPLS‐DA models was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) metrics. The auroc function from mixOmics was used to compute AUC values for all pairwise group comparisons within the first two sPLS‐DA components. Associated p‐values were also computed to assess statistical significance (Table S4). To identify differentially abundant metabolites (DAM) between Temperature and Sex groups, we applied Limma (Linear Models for Microarray Data), using the limma package (Ritchie et al. 2015). The modelling was performed with the lmFit, eBayes, and topTable functions. Contrasts between two groups were compared with makeContrasts. Metabolites with adjusted p‐values (considering a conservative false discovery rate of FDR < 0.05 and a more exploratory FDR < 0.1) and absolute log2 fold‐change > 1.5 were considered differentially abundant. Results from the Limma analysis were visualised using volcano plots, generated with the EnhancedVolcano package (Blighe et al. 2024), which displays significance (−log10 p‐value) versus magnitude (log2 fold‐change) of change across conditions.

Pathway analyses were performed using the FELLA package, a diffusion‐based algorithm that considers a list of DAM and places them into knowledge‐based biological networks that allow for pathway crosstalk (Picart‐Armada et al. 2018). The algorithm was set to 10,000 iterations and P‐scores and the number of nodes were limited to 0.05 and 1000, respectively. Daphnia pulex , a model crustacean available in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; Kanehisa and Goto 2000), was used to build the reference network. In addition to housing the original input metabolites, the resulting network generated a list of significantly affected metabolites, enzymes, reactions, modules, and pathways. The visual representation of this network was trimmed according to the elements of interest, namely the input metabolites and pathways, and then improved aesthetically using Cytoscape 3.10.3 version (Java 17.0.5) (Shannon et al. 2003).

Finally, ATP:ADP ratios calculated a posteriori as an indicator of cellular energetic status (Feidantsis et al. 2020) were analysed using linear models with Temperature (treated as a continuous predictor) and Sex as fixed factors. Both the full model, including the Temperature × Sex interaction, and a simplified additive model were fitted to evaluate the effects of Temperature and Sex on ATP:ADP ratios.

Results

Survival

Survival at the end of the 21‐d exposure period decreased significantly with increasing temperature (F3,16 = 17.27, p < 0.0001). Post hoc comparisons revealed that mean survival at 16°C (66.9%) and 21°C (62.3%) was comparable, both being significantly higher than survival at 26°C (49.1 A%) and 31°C (38.9%), with the lowest survival observed at 31°C (Figure 2).

The bar graphs represent the mean and the vertical lines their associated SD. Different letters denote significant differences (p‐adj < 0.05) between means.

Upper Thermal Tolerance Limits, Heat Tolerance Plasticity and Thermal Safety Margins

The minimum CTmax value observed was 32.6°C for males exposed to 16°C, while the highest one was 35.1°C for females exposed to 31°C. CTmax increased non‐linearly with increasing exposure temperature, but differently for females and for males, as indicated by the presence of a significant interaction between the terms Sex and Temperature (χ 2 = 15.367, df = 3, p < 0.0015, Table 1). In more detail, the increase in mean CTmax between 16°C and 21°C was greater in males than in females: 2.73% versus 1.49%, respectively. Further, females displayed higher mean CTmax values when compared to males at 16°C and 31°C (2.12% and 1.16% higher, at 16°C and 31°C, respectively, Figure 3A). Moreover, variation within each group was low, as shown by coefficients of variation (CV) ranging from 0.64% to 1.37% (Table S3). ARR values decreased with increasing temperature, in females and males, ranging from [0.11–0.18] at 21°C, to [0.10–0.14] at 26°C and [0.08–0.10] at 31°C, respectively, with males generally showing slightly higher values than females (Table S3).

Reproductive Output and Juveniles Body Length

Reproductive output significantly decreased non‐linearly with increasing exposure temperature, from 470.4 hatched juveniles at 16°C to 11.2 at 31°C (Figure 3C). Juvenile body length significantly increased with increasing exposure temperature, visibly in a non‐linear fashion, reaching a plateau at 26°C. At all elevated temperatures, juveniles were significantly longer when compared to the optimum (16°C). The non‐linearity of the thermal response was supported by the fact that juvenile mean size increased linearly from 16°C to 26°C (from 3.50 to 8.98 mm, respectively) but plateaued at the highest temperature treatment (31°C, 8.05 mm, being comparable to that at 26°C; Figure 3D).

Metabolomic Profiles in Response to Sex and Temperature

The amphipods' whole targeted metabolome (i.e., 47 metabolites) primarily differed based on Sex and Temperature, but not their interaction (Table 2), which was visually confirmed through the sPLS‐DA (Figure 4A,B). Despite the interaction between Temperature and Sex was not significant in the PERMANOVA of the whole targeted metabolome (Table 2), as males and females exhibited different thermal tolerance levels across the tested temperature gradient, and metabolomic responses were shown to strongly relate to changes at the whole‐organism level (e.g., Calosi et al. 2017), we dissected the thermal response per sex. To do so, we used specific contrasts to identify possible differences in metabolite concentrations underlying males and females' responses to different temperatures. Accordingly, when Sex had a significant effect (main or interactive), traits were re‐analysed separately for each sex to avoid masking temperature effects (Vermandele et al. 2025). These within‐sex analyses revealed that females and males' whole metabolome clustered based on temperature (Figure 4C,D).

Discussion

Our study uncovers pronounced sex‐specific variation in thermal sensitivity and physiological responses in the amphipod G. locusta , a keystone species in coastal marine ecosystems. Females display a higher upper thermal tolerance, but lower plasticity for heat tolerance, when compared to males. This was coupled with subtle metabolite‐level sex‐specific patterns, with differences in metabolite abundance between the sexes increasing along the temperature gradient, before converging close to the species' near‐critical upper thermal limits. This indicates that near‐critical conditions may override or suppress sex‐specific metabolomic variation. Particularly, females consistently showed higher abundance of metabolites associated with cellular energy balance and redox regulation (such as NAD, FAD, ATP, ADP Glutamate, Aspartate, Betaine, Cystine and Serine) when compared to males. This highlights females' greater ability to maintain physiological functions and cellular metabolism under moderate warming conditions, but less so when extreme temperatures occur. Exposure to intermediate and high temperatures imposes marked negative effects on G. locusta , reducing their survival rates, reproductive output and cellular energy status. By combining the characterisation of fitness, juveniles' growth, physiological performances and molecular mechanisms, our study provides a unique perspective on the thermal abilities of females and males of a keystone marine ectotherm exposed to a thermal gradient. Overall, we evidence a complex interaction between sex and exposure to ocean warming, underscoring the need to consider sex as a fundamental source of phenotypic variation.

In fact, for key traits (i.e., CTmax and Thermal Safety Margins) we show sex‐specific thermal responses. However, this pattern is not ubiquitous, with traits such as ATP:ADP ratio not differing or showing just a marginal difference between females and males. Altogether our results highlight that sex effects are ecologically extremely relevant, despite being trait specific. We also show that integrating molecular approaches, such as metabolomics, to the characterisation of life history and whole organisms' responses provides valuable mechanistic insight into species' sensitivity to global change drivers. We call for the wide implementation of sex as a biological variable (Clayton 2018; Farrukh and Becker 2025; Shansky and Murphy 2021), as well as the characterisation of the mechanisms underpinning such differences, as critical steps toward improving future biodiversity projections under global changes.

Sexual Dimorphism: From Metabolites to Physiology

Here, we show that spent females and males exhibit distinct metabolomic profiles, with nine pathways differentially utilised between sexes. Notably, males under‐utilise amino acid biosynthesis and metabolism, which are central for energy production, as well as folate transport and metabolism, which are key for anabolic reactions (DNA, RNA and amino acid synthesis) (Zarou et al. 2021). Additionally, males show the de‐activation of two key pathways when compared to females. First, males rely less on ATP‐binding Cassette (ABC) transporters, which are essential for molecule and ion transport across membranes (Dean et al. 2001; Jeong et al. 2017) and possibly heat stress tolerance (Duan et al. 2025). ABC transporters also play a key role in regulating cellular homeostasis under stress by mediating the active export of metabolites, osmolytes, and xenobiotics, and their lower expression has been associated with reduced stress tolerance in invertebrates under thermal challenge (Duan et al. 2025). Second, males rely less on glutathione metabolism, a pathway important for managing oxidative stress and contributing to cellular protection during thermal exposure (Cabral et al. 2025). These results suggest that males rely on a more energy‐conserving cellular metabolic strategy to sustain basal functions when compared to spent females, with males also displaying lower baseline levels of adenylates (including AMP, ADP and ATP) when compared to females. However, weak differences in females and males' ATP:ADP ratio are found, with males displaying a slightly higher ratio than females. This suggests that despite overall adenylate levels being lower, males have abundant energy and remain in a state of low activation of ATP‐generating pathways. This energetic profile points to a strategy of maintenance, rather than energetically costly biosynthetic processes. A lower ATP:ADP ratio in females may suggest constitutive higher energy turnover and higher activation of ATP producing pathways. This divergence in metabolomic profiles is likely underpinned by the distinct biological roles of each sex within the species life‐history strategy and populations dynamics, with females needing to allocate resources for processes like egg production, accumulation of egg's reserves to fuel embryogenesis, and brooding, processes that are energetically demanding (Hayward and Gillooly 2011). Given that G. locusta has a short generation time and reproduces continuously across seasons (Costa and Costa 1999), females likely maintain a sustained reproductive investment year‐round. This could amplify sex‐specific differences in energetic demands and help explain the consistent metabolic differences we report. By contrast, males often invest more in mate searching and guarding or clasping females, which may require intense but less constant energetic costs. These different behavioural demands between sexes align well with the metabolomic patterns we observe, suggesting that males rely more on immediate energy‐mobilising pathways, whereas females channel resources into biosynthetic and antioxidant processes associated with egg production and brooding (Alonzo and Warner 2000; Rotimi et al. 2024). As the females used in our experiment were ovigerous at the time of collection from the field but had released their eggs prior to experimental sampling, the observed differences likely reflect post‐reproductive physiological states. This recent investment in reproduction may help explain the enhanced biosynthetic and antioxidant activity seen in females, in contrast to males' reliance on more transient energy pathways. Also, in our study, we observed clear sexual dimorphism in CTmax, with females showing higher thermal tolerance than males. As expected, body mass also differed between sexes, with males being larger, a factor often linked to reduced heat tolerance in ectotherms (Brans et al. 2017; Leiva et al. 2019). However, our analyses showed that body mass did not significantly explain variation in CTmax, indicating that the observed sex differences in thermal tolerance are not attributable to size but instead reflect genuine physiological dimorphism.

From Divergence to Convergence: How Temperature Modulates Sex‐Based Physiological Strategies

Besides the general metabolome differences between sexes, when investigating molecular sexual dimorphism at each exposure temperature, the key finding of our study is that there are metabolome‐level and functional‐level differences in the thermal response of males and females. At the optimal temperature (16°C), only alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism is lower in males when compared to females. This indicates that, compared to males, females may maintain higher baseline levels of amino acid pools for energy production or biosynthesis to fuel their higher reproductive energetic demand (Raymond et al. 2004). However, as temperature increases, the differences between males and females become more marked, being maximum at 26°C. At 21°C, three pathways are lower in males than females, including glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, as well as one‐carbon pool by folate and folate transport and metabolism. This suggests a progressive decline in the ability of males to perform anabolic reactions (biosynthesis and turnover of proteins, DNA, RNA) as temperature increases. Folate metabolism is essential for anabolic processes, cell division and embryogenesis (Wangensteen et al. 2016), and differs between males and females at 21°C, in line with a prior study on crustaceans (Jiang et al. 2023). At 26°C, males have lower β‐alanine metabolism, a key process in cell volume regulation and stress responses (Kasschau et al. 1984), and lower ABC transporters, indicating reduced translocation of molecules and ions across membranes, potentially impairing cellular homeostasis (Thomas and Tampé 2020). Concomitantly, males show a reduction in metabolites associated with arginine and proline metabolism, which support cellular energy production (Saibi et al. 2015), as well as in pathways linked to redox regulation (Jefferies et al. 2003) and non‐homologous end‐joining, a vital DNA repair mechanism (Barnes 2001). At 31°C, these sex‐specific metabolomic differences largely disappear, with males and females displaying overlapping metabolomic profiles. This convergence likely reflects a breakdown in sex‐dependent physiological strategies under extreme thermal stress. At a high near‐critical temperature, both sexes may face comparable energetic and cellular constraints, leading to a shared metabolic suppression or collapse in regulation. This could result from the failure of aerobic metabolism and mitochondrial function, leading to a loss of ATP production capacity and forcing both sexes to rely on similar anaerobic or emergency metabolic pathways. Moreover, essential regulatory mechanisms (such as redox balance, ion transport, and protein repair) may become saturated or impaired, narrowing the scope for sex‐specific metabolic adjustments. Such convergence has been proposed as a hallmark of approaching upper thermal limits, where organismal systems lose the capacity for differentiated responses (Bawa et al. 2021). This loss of metabolomic sexual dimorphism at high temperature represents a key finding, underscoring that sex‐specific resilience is most evident under moderate stress but may vanish when survival thresholds are exceeded (Weaving et al. 2022).

Thermal tolerance limits show sex‐specific differences, with females consistently exhibiting higher upper thermal limits but lower ARR than males across treatments. This supports growing evidence of sex‐specific tolerance to heat in marine arthropods (Gissi et al. 2023; Lam et al. 2022; Mills and Fish 1980; Missionário et al. 2022; Vermandele et al. 2024). Altogether, this suggests that females can have higher baseline tolerance but lower plasticity for this trait. Interindividual variation in tolerance to heat is greater among males at the control temperature. However, this variation diminishes at higher temperatures, and approaches that of females, suggesting that thermal stress narrows phenotypic differences as both males and females reach shared physiological limits. A similar trend of decreased phenotypic variability at extreme temperatures has also been found for other intertidal organisms (Madeira et al. 2021). This said, the selective death of the most thermally sensitive phenotypes cannot be entirely excluded. The progressive increase and eventual plateau of CTmax with acclimation temperature is consistent with the ‘Concrete Ceiling’ hypothesis (Sandblom et al. 2016; Stillman 2002; Schulte et al. 2011), which proposes that acclimation capacity diminishes at higher temperatures, even if plasticity is evident at moderate ranges. Together, these patterns suggest that both plasticity and selective filtering shape thermal responses under chronic warming.

In the present study, thermal safety margins (TSM) are also broader in females, suggesting that they may cope better with marine heatwaves (Vermandele et al. 2024), whereas males may face greater mortality risk. Such risk could be mitigated (at least in part) by the males' greater heat tolerance plasticity. However, the fact that their tolerance reaches a plateau at 26°C and does not further improve at 31°C in both sexes may indicate a limited window for thermal damage risk mitigation if warming exceeds these limits. The maximum habitat temperature (MHT) used in our TSM estimates was recorded in 2022. However, ocean temperatures are very likely to have increased since then (Monteiro et al. 2025), potentially narrowing G. locusta 's thermal safety margins under real‐world conditions. Still, estimating interannual variability in thermal limits and MHT could be an important avenue of research to refine TSM predictions. Beyond thermal limits and fitness, temperature also affects other physiological processes, with elevated temperatures disrupting immune regulation in gammarids (Labaude et al. 2017), hampering chemosensory mate detection across generations (Pereira et al. 2024) and increasing metabolic rates (Aarset and Aunaas 1990). Thermal tolerance is also species‐specific (Bedulina et al. 2017; Magozzi and Calosi 2015; Vermandele et al. 2024), which as we show here depends on sexes' differential capacity to modulate cellular stress responses and metabolism when faced with heat stress (Foley et al. 2019; Madeira, Narciso, Cabral, Diniz, and Vinagre 2012).

Linking Sex‐Specific Thermal and Metabolomic Plasticity With Fitness Outcomes

The sex‐specific divergence in metabolomic strategies we report here highlights that sex and associated reproductive features significantly influence thermal stress tolerance, with females generally demonstrating a greater metabolic plasticity, higher energetic capacity and greater ability to keep homeostasis. In G. locusta , elevated temperatures reduced both survival and reproductive output, corroborating the strong negative effects of warming on amphipod fitness (Tsoi et al. 2005; Xue et al. 2018). Although we could not estimate sex‐specific survival, other studies also report that differential survival between sexes may alter populations sex ratio, thereby affecting reproductive success and ultimately populations' dynamics, especially as warming trends continue to intensify (Edmands 2021). Concomitant to a decline in survival, fitness also decreases at elevated temperatures, suggesting that ocean warming may severely compromise G. locusta 's reproductive capacity and thus its persistence locally, as shown in other species (Bai et al. 2023; Gissi et al. 2023). Crucially, while male‐biased vulnerability to heat has been reported before (Pottier et al. 2021; Vermandele et al. 2024), our study is the first to identify the molecular and physiological mechanisms underpinning this pattern, specifically in G. locusta , and link them to fitness. By combining organismal, physiological and metabolomic analyses within a single experimental framework, we move beyond descriptive comparisons and provide mechanistic insight into the processes driving sex‐specific thermal tolerance. This includes the identification of key metabolic pathways (e.g., glutathione and ABC transporters) that are differentially regulated between sexes and directly linked to their performance under heat stress. Interestingly, more numerous but smaller juveniles are produced at lower temperatures, whereas at higher temperatures, fewer but larger juveniles are recorded. This shift may reflect a temperature‐induced adaptive trade‐off in brooding strategy, wherein females prioritise investment in egg quality over quantity to increase survival under stressful conditions, a pattern observed in other invertebrates (Gibbin et al. 2017; Maranhão and Marques 2003; Massamba‐N'Siala et al. 2014). Such thermal impacts on fitness may carry population‐ and ecosystem‐level consequences, particularly for coastal species, such as G. locusta , playing key trophic roles in such transitional ecosystems. However, the sex‐specific responses we report here may not be ubiquitous being potentially dependent on taxa, life‐stages, populations and generations (Ascione et al. 2024; Cachet et al. 2015; Guscelli et al. 2023; Marcellin‐Gros et al. 2020; Verberk et al. 2013). However, Gissi et al. (2023) report that sex‐specific differences were detected in 89.7% of the studies accounting for sex. Nevertheless, our integrative design, linking thermal tolerance, reproduction and metabolism, provides a transferable approach to uncover sex‐dependent vulnerabilities in other ectotherms. We provide an empirical demonstration of how sex shapes thermal physiology and metabolism to influence fitness outcomes, underscoring the importance of incorporating sex as a fundamental axis of biological variation to improve predictions of thermal sensitivity under climate change. Further, we show the importance of acquiring a critical understanding on the molecular underpinnings of females and males' responses to ocean warming. While integrative approaches such as metabolomics, lipidomics, and even de novo transcriptomics are increasingly accessible and applicable beyond traditional model species, they still face challenges (particularly regarding annotation depth and pathway resolution). Nonetheless, these tools offer valuable mechanistic insight when applied thoughtfully, even in non‐model organisms. We evidence that different metabolomic strategies emerge between sexes, driving differences in their thermal tolerance and overall fitness under heat stress. For estuarine species, which inhabit extremely dynamic thermal environments (Costa and Costa 1999), and act as key nodes in coastal food webs (Vinagre and Gastón 2024), such integrative approaches are highly relevant. We highlight that when ATP production is compromised under heat stress, organisms are left with insufficient resources to maintain vital cellular processes, eventually leading to their death. Moreover, we report a strong male‐skewed vulnerability to ocean warming, underpinned by their lower ability to maintain homeostasis, energy production or activate stress mitigation pathways when compared to females. We call for a greater effort in defining sex‐specific patterns in thermal biology experiments, to be able to draw where possible and applicable generalities (or lack of) which can help drive our understanding of the ecology and evolution of females and males of the same species. In addition, integrating sex as a key biological variable will strengthen predictions on marine ectotherms' tolerance and ability to adapt to an increasingly variable and harsh environment, particularly when the molecular underpinnings of sexual variation are elucidated.
