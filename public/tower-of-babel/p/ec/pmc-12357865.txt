AI-assisted multi-modal information for the screening of depression: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Luyao Wang, Chenhan Wang, Chenyang Li, Toshiya Murai, Yicai Bai, Ziyan Song, Shuoyan Zhang, Qi Zhang, Yu Huang, Xiaoying Bi, Jiehui Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Depression is a prevalent and costly mental disorder across all ages. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted physiological and behavioral information—such as electroencephalography (EEG), eye movement, video or audio monitoring, and gait analysis—offers a promising tool for depression screening. We systematically reviewed the classification performance of these AI-assisted measures in depression screening. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Google Scholar, Web of Science, and IEEE Xplore, with the search date up to June 7, 2025. The reported AUC values are pooled estimates calculated from all results of eligible studies. AI-assisted multi-modal methods achieved a pooled AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.92–0.96), outperforming uni-modal methods (pooled AUC: 0.84–0.92). Subgroup analysis indicated deep learning models showed higher performance, with an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97). These findings highlight the potential of AI-based multi-modal information in depression screening and emphasize the need to establish standardized databases and improve research design.

Introduction

Depression is a leading global mental health disorder and a major contributor to the worldwide burden of disability1. The potential pathogenesis of depression remains unclear, and reliable screening in clinical practice is challenging, as current assessments rely primarily on patient history and self-reports2. In addition, this makes large-scale community screening difficult and increases the risk of missed diagnoses. Therefore, effective and accurate methods for depression diagnosis are essential for early intervention. Recently, with advances in medical technology, cost-effective, non-invasive, and easy to collect techniques—such as EEG, eye movement, video or audio monitoring, and gait analysis—have been increasingly implemented in depression screening3–6. These methods provide objective indicators for physiological and behavioral information.

Depression is a clinically and etiologically heterogeneous disorder7, and this heterogeneity is reflected in the variability of objectively measurable biological markers detected through physiological and behavioral assessments. Based on EEG facial recognition task or attention task, researchers detected abnormal brain activation in emotional networks in depression patients, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and amygdala8–11. In addition, researches of eye movement tracking reveal that depression patients tend to focus more on negative stimuli while ignoring positive interactions when viewing emotional images12–14. This may be accompanied by changes in the frontal eye field (FEF), parietal cortex, and occipital cortex. Additionally, depression patients exhibit slow audio rates and increased negative facial expressions15. Furthermore, there is a bidirectional interaction between sensorimotor system and brain networks related to emotions and higher cognitive function16,17. Depression may result in severely debilitating conditions companied with abnormal gait18. We summarized underlying brain areas related to different physiological and behavioral information in Fig. 1. Current research shows that multi-modal information can lead to more personalized and accurate screening20. However, the challenge of exploring the correlations and complementarity between multi-modal information is an urgent problem to be solved. With the development of artificial intelligence (AI), researchers are trying to use more automated, objective, efficient, and real-time methods to depression screening. The AI technology especially deep learning (DL), promises to enhance the accuracy of multi-modal feature integration, thereby enhancing classification performance and reducing screening errors. Although numerous studies have employed various AI algorithms20–22, comprehensive meta-analyses using multiple modalities for depression screening within digital healthcare is still insufficient.

This study systematically examines whether integrating AI-assisted (i.e., employing artificial intelligence algorithms, including machine learning and deep learning, for feature extraction and classification) multi-modal physiological and behavioral information can improve the classification performance for depression screening. Multi-modal AI-assisted screening methods have already achieved significant breakthroughs in other areas of medicine23,24, which suggests promising potential for mental health applications as well. Although prior meta-analyses has explored digital signals from wearable devices(such as physical activity data, location data, etc.)21 and social media platforms (including tweets and communication logs)25,26 as potential indicators of depression. In contrast, our meta-analyses concentrate on objectively measurable physiological and behavioral information that are commonly used in fusion frameworks, such as EEG, eye movement, facial expressions, speech, and gait. By conducting a systematic meta-analysis of the modeling results derived from published data, we seek to highlight the unique value of multi-modal integration and outline future directions for more effective and objective depression screening.

Results

Study selection and characteristics of eligible studies

A total of 1564 records were identified in the initial search, of which 981 were duplicates. After screening the titles and abstracts, 108 studies were excluded as they were clearly unrelated to the research topic, leaving 475 articles for full-text eligibility assessment. Among these, 397 were excluded after reviewing the content of the full-text manuscripts, resulting in 80 studies included in the qualitative synthesis (comprising 33 EEG studies, 6 eye movement studies, 6 video studies, 16 audio studies, 6 gait studies, and 13 multi-modal studies). These 80 studies27–106 provided sufficient data to meet the inclusion criteria for the meta-analysis (Fig. 2). Characteristics of all included studies (n = 80)

71 of 80 studies (89%) explicitly reported that the ground truth assessment of their classification labels came from scales (e.g., DSM-IV and -V, PHQ-8, and -9, etc). The remaining nine studies did not specify any depression diagnosis or screening criteria. Furthermore, data were sourced from public datasets in 38 out of 80 studies (47.5%), while the remaining studies involved the collection of proprietary datasets (42/80, 52.5%). The predominant ground truth assessments utilized were the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fourth/fifth edition (25/80, 31.3%), and the Nine/Eight-item Patient Health Questionnaire (26/80, 32.5%). Tasks assigned to subjects under each modality (including both uni-modal studies and multi-modal studies containing the corresponding modality) were eye open/closure for EEG (24/38, 63.1%), clinical interviews for video (8/16, 50.0%) and audio (23/30, 76.7%), free viewing for eye movement (7/7, 100.0%), and free walking for gait (4/6, 66.7%). The studies collectively employed 19 algorithms, with the most frequently utilized ones being support vector machine (SVM) (36/80, 45.0%), k-nearest neighbor (15/80, 18.8%), and convolutional neural network (CNN) (17/80, 21.3%).

In the multi-modal depression screening research, 13 studies provided a total of 15 investigations on different types of modal fusion methods and four results. Four of investigations used EEG and audio information (4/15, 26.7%), one used EEG and eye movement (1/15, 6.7%), and the remaining 8 investigations used audio, video, and text information (10/15, 66.7%).

Discussion

In recent years, automatic depression screening methods have become increasingly diverse, especially combining with multi-modal physiological and behavioral information. However, most existing studies have focused on single-modality approaches. For example, depression screening using wearable devices and AI has achieved classification accuracy ranging from 70% to 89%21, which is similar to the performance of single-modality approaches in our meta-analysis. Nevertheless, there is a significant disparity in data formats across different modalities for depression screening, and various AI technologies still face challenges in integrating multi-modal inputs effectively. Therefore, we aim to explore whether AI-assisted multi-modal information could enhance the classification performance for depression screening. Given these challenges, we aimed to explore whether AI-assisted multi-modal information could further enhance the classification performance for depression screening. By adhering strictly to screen review guidelines, we ensured the integrity of our study. While our research demonstrates that uni-modal analysis using either voice or EEG alone achieves satisfactory diagnostic performance, the integration of multiple modalities yields superior classification accuracy. Notably, AI-assisted multiple modalities can classify patients with and without depression in up to 96% of cases (Table 2).

Due to decades of rapid development, EEG and audio signals have become a crucial method for screening and treating mental disorders107. The availability of relatively sufficient data now allows meta-analyses to validate some EEG research findings. Several EEG studies have identified a link between hemispheric asymmetry in the cortical frontal regions and depressive symptoms108. The current research on left and right frontal lobe electrodes consistently shows that, during eye-open/eye-closed task29,41,56, the EEG collected by electrodes in the right frontal lobe contains more depression-related information than the electrodes in the left frontal lobe. This suggests that patients with depression may exhibit abnormally increased right frontal activity at rest, providing important insights for future research in this area109. In addition, there are two main approaches for audio-based depression screening: extracting acoustic features for traditional machine learning or using end-to-end deep learning on raw timing audio signals or spectrograms. Our subgroup meta-analysis showed that machine learning with prosodic features achieved similar classification performance to deep learning methods (AUC = 0.92 and 0.94), with 17% higher specificity but 8% lower sensitivity (Fig. 3). However, the reduction in sensitivity increases the risk of missing depression cases. Therefore, achieving an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity is crucial for developing effective depression screening tools, as is the case for all modalities. Despite the promise of EEG and audio signals, other single modalities have not performed as well, leading to the exploration of multi-modal approaches.

For screening methods using uni-modal information, feature extraction-based algorithms outperformed other algorithms in subgroup analyses (Table 3). This discrepancy may be attributed to the rapid advancements in the field of feature extraction, particularly concerning EEG107 and voice data108. In this study, we compiled statistics on the frequency and selection rates of specific features used across modalities based on the literature (Table 1). Through this statistical analysis, we can identify frequently used features, such as power spectral density and Lempel–Ziv complexity from EEG data, and fixation duration from eye movement data, and assess their importance in both single-modality and multi-modal screening. This provides valuable reference data for optimizing feature selection strategies and enhancing the accuracy of depression screening in future research.

There are two main multi-modal fusion methods currently prevalent in current research. The first method combines EEG and audio, which discriminates depression from NC with a pooled SE of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.83–0.92), a pooled SP of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91), and an AUC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.91–0.95). The second method integrates video, audio, and text, which discriminates depression from NC with a pooled SE of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.84–0.92), a pooled SP of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.93), and an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97) (Table 3). This observation underscores that AI-based multi-modal fusion is a promising tool for screening depression. The improvements observed may be attributed to several factors: AI-assisted multi-modal physiological and behavioral information can effectively integrate diverse data formats, thereby reducing the noise and variability inherent in uni-modal data. In addition, the standardized and stable screening process of AI can minimize subjective errors. Moreover, while patients might try to conceal their conditions during a clinical evaluation, they cannot easily hide physiological signals (e.g., EEG) and behavioral reactions (e.g., micro-expressions).

Despite the progress, there are still several technical improvements needed in current research. Many studies on ML methods continue to rely on classic models such as SVM and KNN, which primarily concatenate features from different modalities. Exploring more advanced models could be beneficial. For instance, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and its derivatives (e.g., LightGBM, CatBoost) not only often outperform traditional classifiers in various tasks but also offer visualization of feature importance scores or decision trees, enhancing interpretability in multi-modal data analysis110–112. This transparency is crucial, as understanding the contribution of each modality can lead to better model interpretation and refinement. Multi-kernel learning allows the integration of multiple kernels113, each tailored to a specific modality, to better synthesize heterogeneous data114. This approach captures the unique features of each modality more effectively, thereby improving overall model performance.

Furthermore, methods using DL have improved overall screening accuracy by 3% compared to those using traditional ML methods (Table 2). Despite this improvement, current research still faces several challenges. For instance, due to the significant differences in data formats between input modalities, 89% of the multi-modal DL investigations included employ a late fusion strategy (n = 9). This strategy involves concatenating features at the decision-making layer. While late fusion requires less data and allows for easier integration of multiple sensing strategies and algorithms, it may not fully leverage the complementary strengths of different modalities115. Additionally, this approach is more susceptible to overfitting, especially with small datasets116. To address these limitations, we suggest exploring more sophisticated fusion and alignment techniques in multi-modal DL screening model, especially in small sample size117.

For future work in multi-modal AI depression screening, several areas need focus. Integrating advanced DL techniques is crucial, such as implementing cross-modal attention mechanisms, which allow models to dynamically concentrate on the most relevant features of each information117 (Fig. 4). Additionally, techniques like cross-task and cross-dataset transfer learning could be used to help enhance the generalization capability of models, ensuring robustness and stability in practical applications118. Moreover, integrating multi-modal frameworks with large language models (LLMs) may help leverage rich contextual semantics, leading to more personalized depression screening. Developing more tools for interpretability is also essential for understanding and improving AI systems. In addition to the model framework, proper standardization and preprocessing of data are also important to remove noise and format differences between datasets and modalities120,121. For instance, in this study, all literature involving EEG preprocessed the data to remove artifacts such as EMG, ECG, and frequency signals outside the EEG band, including power frequency signals. Similarly, due to factors like aliasing, high-order harmonic distortion, and high frequencies from the human vocal apparatus and audio collection equipment, audio signals require preprocessing such as pre-emphasis, framing, and windowing. Video-based studies also performed face detection and alignment between adjacent frames, with some extracting 68 facial landmarks and action units for further feature extraction. Finally, gait-based studies using Kinect devices processed the three-dimensional coordinate stream of 25 joints generated by the Kinect. This involved discarding incorrectly estimated coordinates or frames with deformed skeletons and performing coordinate transformations. Most studies in this review have considered these steps; however, the lack of a unified standard for preprocessing certain modalities (e.g., gait, eye movement) may lead to bias. Future research should focus on developing more robust and automated preprocessing pipelines to efficiently handle various formats.

Despite the promising performance of existing models, the challenge of data availability remains significant. Depression data is sensitive, making it difficult to collect diverse datasets for estimating the severity of depression. Among the studies included in this meta-analysis (the dataset used in each study is listed in Supplementary Table 1), six public datasets are highlighted: the PRED + CT dataset (providing EEG data)122; the AVEC 2011–2021 datasets (e.g., DAIC-WOZ and E-DAIC, featuring video, audio, and text modalities mainly in English)123–130; the MODMA dataset (offering EEG and audio modalities in Chinese)131; the BlackDog database (providing video and audio modalities in English); the CMDC dataset (including video, audio, and text modalities in Chinese)98; and the EATD-Corpus dataset (focusing on audio modalities in Chinese)106. However, each of these public datasets, as well as the private datasets used in included studies, contains no more than 280 subjects (with sample sizes ranging from 24 to 275), which hampers the ability to identify clinical biomarkers universally and reliably, especially with DL approaches132,133. We advocate for the establishment of a larger, more diverse, and standardized database of depressed patients, with multi-modal data collected under consistent conditions, equipment, and configurations.

While data-driven depression screening using modalities such as EEG, eye movements, facial expressions, speech, and gait has the potential to relieve clinical workloads and enable early intervention, it also raises ethical challenges. There is a risk of misuse by third parties, such as employers or insurers, especially if data are used without explicit patient consent or understanding. Researchers and clinicians must ensure transparent communication regarding data use, secure robust data protection measures, and guarantee that participation is voluntary and withdrawable at any time. Continuous ethical oversight is essential to safeguard individuals’ rights and promote the responsible application of these technologies134.

This study has several notable limitations. Firstly, we focused on English-language articles, which may overlook valuable findings from non-English studies. Secondly, as most pooled estimates for the algorithms were based on a limited number of studies and limited sample sizes within these studies, the results may be subject to bias. Additionally, the subgroup analysis in this study still exhibited certain heterogeneity. Further research should examine the classification performance of AI algorithms in more homogeneous patient groups. Moreover, few of the studies involved cross-ethnic or cross-age research, making it challenging to verify whether models trained on one specific ethnic group or age group are equally effective for others. Additionally, we excluded studies that only reported continuous metrics such as MAE or RMSE to maintain analytic consistency, as such assessments have not been conducted for all modalities in the existing literature. Future research could further explore the use of continuous outcome measures to broaden current insights. Some relevant studies, such as Audiface135 and Audibert5, which only reported F1 scores without providing sufficient data (e.g., TP, TN, FP, FN) required for meta-analysis, could not be included, which may have introduced inclusion bias. Finally, potentially due to the influence of public datasets, most of the multi-modal fusion methods in the literature focused on two modalities, preventing further investigation into the effectiveness of each modality.

This study highlights the significant potential of artificial intelligence algorithms in multi-modal depression screening and outlines the promising prospects and development directions for each modality. Despite challenges such as the risk of false positives and false negatives, data privacy and security concerns, and regulatory approval requirements, AI remains a valuable tool for assisting doctors with diagnosis. Additionally, we emphasize the need to enhance the study design of AI-based depression screening systems and advocate for the creation of data-sufficient standardized databases to support further research.

Methods

The systematic review and meta-analysis were performed according to the standard PRISMA (preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses)136 (Supplementary Table 7). The study was registered in the PROSPERO (CRD420251049107).

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

Two researchers with experience in computer science and biomedical engineering independently conducted literature searches and records. The agreement was then achieved by a third reviewer with the expertise of data analysis. All these searches were performed on Google Scholar, Web of Science Core Collection, and IEEE Xplore database for publications up to June 7, 2025. Only English-language articles were included. To investigate the strategies of automated clinically informative depression screening with both single and multiple modalities, we conducted a literature search using a combination of key terms from three categories: (1) depression-related disorders (“depression” OR “major depressive disorder” OR “mood disorder”), (2) screening processes (“detection” OR “diagnosis” OR “screening”), and (3) modalities (“EEG” OR “eye movement” OR “gait” OR “speech” OR “vocal” OR “audio” OR “audiovisual” OR “facial” OR “visual” OR “video” OR “multi-modal”). Search queries were constructed by combining at least one term from each category with the AND operator, while terms within each category were combined using OR. First pre-screen by looking at abstracts and titles to filter out irrelevant articles. Eligibility is then checked against our criteria (Fig. 2) and unqualified full-text review entries are removed. Eligible studies that reported AI-assisted each modality for the screening of Depression with classification outcomes such as sensitivity (SE) and specificity (SP) were then used to calculate the 2 × 2 contingency tables.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) the main focus was on depression (excluding studies centered on other disorders such as schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease, brain trauma, or cancer); (2) only technical research was included (excluding reviews and non-technical papers); (3) the study purposed and evaluated an approach for depression diagnosis that used machine learning or deep learning models to classify depression; (4) comprehensive classification performance results were reported, specifically accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, recall, precision, or an explicit confusion matrix, enabling direct extraction or calculation of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN).

Data analysis

Details of papers for quality review were manually summarized in a spreadsheet, including title, modality, year of publication, source of data, type of patient’s depression, number of patients and normal control, types of features, algorithm model, and classification performance data.

Classification performance data for included studies were extracted, and contingency tables were created. These data included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, recall, precision, or an explicit confusion matrix, which enabled direct extraction or calculation of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). Contingency tables were used to construct SROC curves and forest plots, as well as to calculate pooled sensitivity and specificity. If a study provided multiple contingency tables for different AI algorithms, the contingency tables for different AI algorithms were used independently.

Risk of bias and applicability appraisal

The risk of bias and applicability of all selected studies were assessed by using the QUADAS-2 criteria137. It provides researchers with a standardized framework for assessing the risk of bias and applicability when conducting reviews that evaluate the accuracy of AI-assisted screening tests.

Data synthesis

We performed a meta-analysis of studies using contingency tables to estimate the classification performance of AI methods, including machine learning (ML) or DL algorithms. As differences between studies were assumed, a random effects model was performed. We conducted a meta-analysis of all single-modal and multi-modal research literature data; number of research literature for all modalities was greater than 5, which meets the requirements of random-effect analysis138. We used contingency tables to construct stratified summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves and calculated summary sensitivity and specificity for predicting high heterogeneity. The combined curve is the corresponding 95% confidence region and 95% prediction region plotted around the average estimate of SE, SP, and AUC in the SROC plot. Heterogeneity was assessed, using the I2 statistic. Meanwhile, all SROC curves were summarized on one graph to facilitate comparison and calculated using STATA statistical software (version 17.0) (Midas and Metandi modules; StataCorp). Statistical significance is shown when the P < 0.05.
