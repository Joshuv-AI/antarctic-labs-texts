The Prognostic Impact of Early ctDNA Kinetics in Metastatic Pancreatic Cancer Using the ctDNA-RECIST
Authors: Mette M. Steiniche, Sidsel C. Lindgaard, Inna M. Chen, Julia S. Johansen, Rikke F. Andersen, Torben F. Hansen, Lars H. Jensen, Louise S. Rasmussen, Malene W. Johansen, Morten Ladekarl, Anders K.M. Jakobsen, Karen-Lise G. Spindler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:Changes in ctDNA levels during systemic treatment may predict treatment efficacy in patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), but quantitative response criteria are not yet established. This study evaluates our recently proposed ctDNA-RECIST.Experimental Design:Blood samples were collected before treatment, before the second treatment cycle, and at the time of the first CT evaluation from 220 patients with metastatic PDAC receiving first-line palliative chemotherapy. Plasma ctDNA levels were measured using Droplet Digital PCR with HOXA9 methylation assays. ctDNA response was determined according to ctDNA-RECIST and correlated with overall survival (OS).Results:ctDNA was positive before treatment in 71% of the patients and was related to OS [HR = 1.61; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.19–2.19; P = 0.002]. Among ctDNA-positive patients, ctDNA maximal response (MR; n = 41) and ctDNA disease control (DC; n = 107) before the second treatment cycle had longer OS compared with ctDNA progressive disease (PD; n = 5; median OS: MR 11.9 months, DC 7.2 months, PD 3.6 months; P = 0.002). In Cox regression, ctDNA DC (HR = 1.55; 95% CI, 1.07–2.26; P = 0.021) and ctDNA PD (HR = 4.50; 95% CI, 1.74–11.6; P = 0.002) showed shorter OS compared with ctDNA MR. The same applied to ctDNA response at the time of the first CT evaluation assessed from the second treatment cycle (P < 0.001) and from treatment start (P < 0.001).Conclusions:ctDNA-RECIST applied to liquid biopsies holds potential for early evaluation of treatment benefit in patients with metastatic PDAC, offering a novel, minimally invasive method to guide early clinical decision-making. Future studies should validate ctDNA-RECIST prospectively, preferably in randomized controlled trials.

Translational Relevance

Despite growing evidence of the prognostic impact of ctDNA kinetics in patients with solid tumors, the lack of standardized response evaluation criteria has hindered progress toward clinical implementation. Current studies are characterized by methodologic heterogeneity, including variations in ctDNA quantification techniques, ctDNA markers, and ctDNA response definitions. Our study applies, for the first time, the proposed ctDNA-RECIST based on Droplet Digital PCR analysis of methylated HOXA9, providing a reproducible and clinically applicable approach for assessing ctDNA kinetics. Our findings support the clinical validity of ctDNA-RECIST as an emerging tool to guide treatment decisions in patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, allowing for earlier evaluations of overall treatment benefit and individualized treatment strategies.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) has a 5-year survival rate of only 13%, and more than 80% of patients are diagnosed with unresectable disease, resulting in a median overall survival (OS) of less than 1 year (1–3). Systemic chemotherapy is the cornerstone in the treatment of unresectable PDAC, with the aim of relieving cancer-related symptoms and prolonging survival. Combination chemotherapy regimens, FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, have improved OS compared with gemcitabine alone but are also associated with higher toxicity, and only a minority of patients receive second-line chemotherapy due to rapid clinical deterioration (1, 2, 4, 5). American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines acknowledge concerns about the balance between treatment efficacy and toxicity by emphasizing the importance of discussing patients’ personal preferences (6), underlining that a reliable measure of overall treatment benefit is essential. Response evaluation according to RECIST has notable drawbacks in PDAC (7). Treatment efficacy is typically assessed after 8 to 12 weeks, and the objective response rate according to RECIST is a poor surrogate marker for OS (8). Imaging is further challenged by dense fibrotic stroma and inflammation surrounding the primary tumor, resulting in low reproducibility even when standardized RECIST is applied (9, 10). Consequently, there is an urgent need for reliable biomarkers not only for early treatment evaluation but also for benefit in terms of OS.

Currently, CA19-9 is the only biomarker approved by the FDA for pancreatic cancer (11, 12). It has shown promise as a predictor of outcomes in some studies (13, 14). Other studies have failed to support its predictive value, and CA19-9 has shortcomings as other conditions, such as pancreatitis, cirrhosis, diabetes, and cholestasis, can cause elevated levels (15–17). Furthermore, 5% to 10% of the general population do not express high levels of CA19-9 due to a lack of Lewis antigen expression (15–17).

Liquid biopsies, particularly ctDNA, offer a promising alternative for treatment monitoring. Several studies have investigated changes in ctDNA during palliative chemotherapy with respect to clinical outcomes, associating ctDNA increase or persistence with shorter OS and progression-free survival (PFS; refs. 18–22). Some findings are evident as early as 2 weeks after initiating the first cycle of chemotherapy (23, 24). Despite consistent evidence linking ctDNA kinetics to survival outcomes, methodologic heterogeneity—such as varying analytic methods, ctDNA markers, timing, and thresholds—hinders clinical implementation. In PDAC, KRAS mutation is a commonly used ctDNA biomarker (19, 23, 24), but studies investigating methylation patterns as ctDNA biomarkers are emerging (25). Aberrant expression of HOXA9 found in a majority of malignancies (26, 27) has shown promise as a ctDNA biomarker across several tumor types, and is relevant to explore in PDAC (28–31).

Standardized frameworks, such as the recently proposed ctDNA-RECIST, could address these challenges and enable widespread adoption of ctDNA as a response biomarker (32).

The aim of the present study was to investigate the prognostic impact of early ctDNA kinetics of HOXA9 in patients with metastatic PDAC based on the ctDNA-RECIST response classification. To our knowledge, this is the first and largest study to investigate a ctDNA-based response evaluation tool in this patient population.

Materials and Methods

Study population

The study included 220 patients with metastatic PDAC selected from the Danish study “Biomarkers in Patients with Pancreatic Cancer (BIOPAC)—can they provide new information of the disease and improve diagnosis and prognosis of the patients?” (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03311776; www.herlevhospital.dk/BIOPAC/). The BIOPAC study is a prospective multicenter open cohort study with ongoing enrollment until 5,000 patients are included (33). Inclusion criteria for the present cohort comprised age >18 years, histologically confirmed diagnosis of PDAC, disease stage IV, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) ≤2, no previous chemotherapy, and eligibility for standard first-line palliative treatment. Plasma samples were available before the first palliative treatment, before the start of the second chemotherapy cycle, and at the time of the first CT evaluation, and patients with samples available from baseline and at least one of the on-treatment sampling time points were selected. Furthermore, the patients were selected to approximate a balanced distribution across standard first-line chemotherapy: (i) gemcitabine (n = 73), (ii) gemcitabine in combination with nab-paclitaxel (n = 75), and (iii) FOLFIRINOX (5-fluorouracil, irinotecan, oxaliplatin; n = 72). Patients were included upon referral to the Departments of Oncology at Copenhagen University Hospital, Herlev and Gentofte; Aalborg University Hospital; or Vejle Hospital, Denmark. The patients were included between November 2010 and February 2024 and were followed until November 7, 2024, or death. At the time of follow-up, 11 patients (5%) were still alive. All included patients had complete follow-up for the clinical endpoints. However, attrition in longitudinal sample availability was observed over time (Fig. 1).

Blood sampling

Blood samples were obtained prior to the first cycle of palliative chemotherapy (time point A), prior to the second cycle (time point B, 2–4 weeks after time point A, depending on the chemotherapy regimen), and at the time of the first CT evaluation (time point C, 2–3 months after time point A, depending on the hospital). Blood samples were drawn into 9 mL EDTA tubes, and plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 2,300 g for 10 minutes within 2 hours of sampling. Plasma was aliquoted into Greiner tubes (Cryo.s Freezing Tubes, 2 mL, GR-121280, Greiner Bio-One GmbH) and stored at −80°C until analysis (33).

Analysis of HOXA9-methylated DNA

After thawing, plasma was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 minutes, and ∼35,000 copies of CPP1 exogenous DNA (soybean-derived fragments used as an internal control) were added to each sample (34). Cell-free DNA was purified from 4 mL plasma (1.4–4 mL) using the DSP Circulating DNA kit (QIAGEN, RRID:SCR_008539) on the QIAsymphony platform, as previously described (35). DNA was eluted in 60 µL and diluted to 200 µL. Quality control of the extracted DNA was performed as described in the supplementary material (Methodological Appendix 1).

Residual DNA (194 µL) was concentrated to 20 µL, and bisulfite conversion was performed with the EZ DNA Methylation-Lightning Kit (Zymo Research, RRID:SCR_008968). Converted DNA was stored at −20°C if not immediately used in Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR). The converted DNA was analyzed using an in-house HOXA9 methylation–specific ddPCR assay (35, 36) with albumin as a reference gene (37). It was performed in duplicate, using 5 µL of DNA per well in a 20 µL reaction volume. Droplets were generated on the automated droplet generator with 15,000–20,000 accepted droplets per well (Bio-Rad, RRID:SCR_019714). Droplets were read on the QX200 Droplet Digital Reader (Bio-Rad, RRID:SCR_019714) with FAM/VIC detection, and data were exported as a percentage of methylated HOXA9 (HOXA9 copies/mL divided by albumin copies/mL), including a 95% confidence interval (CI) derived from a Poisson distribution. Five positive droplets defined positivity. The cutoff was previously established based on plasma from healthy donors (38). If the CI included 0, HOXA9 was considered negative. HOXA9 methylation analysis was performed blinded to clinical endpoints.

ctDNA-RECIST classification of ctDNA results

Patients were categorized into ctDNA response groups based on ctDNA-RECIST (32). These criteria classify ctDNA kinetics into the following categories: progressive disease (PD), defined as an increase in ctDNA above the previous value with no overlap of the two CIs; stable disease, defined as a ctDNA value within the CI of the previous value; partial response, defined as a decrease in ctDNA below the previous value with no overlap of the two CIs; and maximal response (MR), which includes both complete response, defined as a decrease in ctDNA to an undetectable level, and near complete response, defined as a ctDNA decrease with the lower CI overlapping 0. The category ctDNA disease control (DC) was introduced, comprising ctDNA stable disease and ctDNA partial response. This classification provides a standardized framework for assessing treatment response using ctDNA.

Statistical method

Associations between baseline ctDNA levels and clinical characteristics were assessed using the t test, rank-sum Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Scatter plots with fitted regression lines were used to visualize correlations between log-transformed ctDNA and log-transformed CA19-9, and log-transformed ctDNA and age. A Sankey plot was used to visualize the shift in patient distribution from log-transformed ctDNA quartiles at time point A (prior to treatment) to ctDNA-RECIST response classifications at time point B, with arrow width reflecting the number of patients in each group. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves, with differences evaluated by the log-rank test. OS was defined as the time from day 1 in the first cycle of chemotherapy to death or the date of censoring (November 7, 2024), and PFS was defined as the time from day 1 in the first cycle of chemotherapy to PD, death, or censoring, whichever came first. PD was determined by the treating physician and could be based on either radiologic or clinical assessment. CT scans were assessed locally by radiologists experienced in pancreatic cancer imaging but were not centrally reviewed, and RECIST evaluations or detailed CT scan descriptions were not available for this analysis. Survival analyses were conducted based on ctDNA positivity at time point A, log-transformed ctDNA level at time point A, and according to ctDNA-RECIST response classification based on the change in HOXA9% from time point A to time point B, from time point A to time point C, and from time point B to time point C. In survival analyses based on ctDNA response from time point B to time point C, OS was defined as the time from time point B to death or the date of censoring, and PFS was defined as the time from time point B to PD, death, or censoring, whichever occurred first. In analyses of OS and PFS, patients were categorized into the clinically relevant response groups: ctDNA MR, ctDNA DC, and ctDNA PD. Additionally, survival analyses were conducted based on ctDNA positivity at time points B and C, serving as landmark analyses, in which OS was defined as the time from time points B and C to death or the date of censoring, and PFS was defined as the time from time point B or C to PD, death, or censoring, whichever occurred first. The same applied to log-transformed ctDNA levels at time points B and C. HRs with 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Statistical significance was defined as a P value below 0.05. Univariate Cox regression identified predictors of OS. Variables that were significant at a 5% significance level and/or found to be clinically relevant were included in a multivariate Cox regression [sex, age, ECOG PS, body mass index (BMI), liver metastases, and CA19-9] to adjust for confounding factors. Multivariate analyses were conducted with and without the exclusion of CA19-9 nonexpressors (CA19-9 <37 U/mL). In cases with a limited number of events in Cox regressions for outcomes according to ctDNA response categories, analyses were repeated with the aggregation of groups to increase the statistical robustness of the model. Cox proportional hazards were visually inspected on log–log survival plots with no violated assumptions. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (RRID:SCR_012763) version 18.5 (StataCorp, 2023) for Windows.

Results

A total of 617 blood samples were obtained at the predefined time points from 220 patients diagnosed with metastatic PDAC across three study sites (Fig. 1: Study flow and ctDNA-RECIST schedule).

Liver metastases were present at the time of inclusion in 139 (63%) patients. Eighty-one (37%) patients were categorized as ECOG PS 0, 105 (48%) as ECOG PS 1, and 26 (12%) as ECOG PS 2. In eight patients (4%), ECOG PS was not available.

Across all included patients, the median OS was 9.6 months (95% CI, 8.1–11.1), the median PFS was 5.4 months (95% CI, 4.9–6.4), and the median number of cycles in the first line of treatment was 6 [interquartile range (IQR): 4–10].

Prior to treatment (time point A), the ctDNA positivity rate was 71% (156 of 220) based on the predefined cutoff of 5 positive droplets, whereas 92% (203 of 220) of patients had a measurable ctDNA signal, including those with fewer than 5 positive droplets. Among the 64 (29%) initially ctDNA-negative patients, 22 (34%) later converted to ctDNA-positive. The median time from baseline sampling (time point A) to treatment start was 2 days (IQR: 0–4 days), and samples for early ctDNA response evaluation (time point B) were collected after a median of 28 days (IQR: 18–35) from treatment start. The median time from baseline sampling (time point A) to the time of blood sampling close to the first CT/treatment evaluation (time point C) was 64 days (IQR: 58–97 days) for patients included at Aalborg Hospital and Vejle Hospital, and it was 84 days (IQR: 72–92 days) for patients included at Herlev Hospital.

The median pretherapeutic ctDNA level was 0.385% methylated HOXA9 (range: 0%–23.2%) across all included patients. In univariate analysis of each baseline characteristic, ctDNA levels were significantly associated with liver metastases (P < 0.001) and with CA19-9 levels above the median (P = 0.002; Table 1). Levels of ctDNA and CA19-9 were normally distributed on a log scale, and the correlation between log-ctDNA and log-CA19-9 was low, with coefficients of 0.16 (P = 0.050; Supplementary Fig. S1). Neither ctDNA positivity rate (P = 0.969), ctDNA level (P = 0.749), nor total cell-free DNA (P = 0.900) was associated with the year of sampling when EDTA plasma was stored at −80°C.

Prognostic impact of positive ctDNA

Patients with positive ctDNA (≥5 positive droplets) prior to treatment (time point A; n = 156) had a significantly shorter median OS of 8.5 months (95% CI, 7.3–10.0) compared with 13.8 months (95% CI, 9.3–16.3) for those with negative ctDNA (<5 positive droplets; n = 64; log-rank P = 0.002, HR = 1.61, 95% CI, 1.19–2.19; P = 0.002; Table 2; Supplementary Fig. S2A). In univariate analysis, age above 65 years, presence of liver metastases, ECOG PS ≥1, CA19-9 level above median, and positive ctDNA were significantly associated with OS (Table 2), but not BMI above 25 kg/m2 nor below 18.5 kg/m2. In a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, positive ctDNA, age above 65 years, presence of liver metastases, and ECOG PS ≥1 remained predictors of shorter OS (Table 2). Adjusting for the type of chemotherapy regimen, age was no longer prognostic (Supplementary Table S1A).

The prognostic value of early ctDNA response

Among patients with positive ctDNA prior to treatment (time point A), 153 had an available blood sample at time point B and were classified into three groups based on the change in ctDNA from time point A to B: ctDNA MR (n = 41, 27%), ctDNA DC (n = 107, 70%), and ctDNA PD (n = 5, 3%). The relationship between log ctDNA levels prior to treatment and ctDNA-RECIST response category is illustrated by the Sankey plot, with ctDNA MR and ctDNA DC, including patients from all four baseline ctDNA quartile levels (Fig. 2). The median OS was significantly different across the three response categories of ctDNA MR, DC, and PD, with a median OS of 11.9 months (95% CI, 8.6–14.2) for ctDNA MR, 7.2 months (95% CI, 5.6–9.2) for ctDNA DC, and 3.6 months (2.06–NA, log-rank P = 0.002) for ctDNA PD (Fig. 3A). In the Cox proportional hazards model, patients in the ctDNA DC group and ctDNA PD group had significantly increased risks of death compared with those in the ctDNA MR group, with HR = 1.55 (95% CI, 1.07–2.26; P = 0.021) and HR = 4.50 (95% CI, 1.74–11.6; P = 0.002), respectively. The same applied to the analysis of PFS according to early ctDNA response (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

In a depiction of log-transformed CA19-9 levels at time points A, B, and C according to early ctDNA response category, a consistent correlation between ctDNA response group and longitudinal changes in CA19-9 was not observed (Supplementary Fig. S7).

The prognostic value of late ctDNA response

A total of 128 patients had positive ctDNA prior to treatment and a blood sample from time point C and were classified into three groups based on the change in ctDNA from time point A to C: ctDNA MR 44 (34%), ctDNA DC 79 (62%), and ctDNA PD 5 (4%).

The median OS was significantly different across the three response categories of ctDNA MR, DC, and PD, with a median OS of 14.0 months (95% CI, 10.9–15.6) for ctDNA MR, 8.2 months (95% CI, 6.7–9.8) for ctDNA DC, and 4.1 months (3.3–NA, log-rank P < 0.001) for ctDNA PD (Fig. 3B). In the Cox proportional hazards model, patients in the ctDNA DC group and ctDNA PD group had significantly shorter OS compared with those in the ctDNA MR group, with HR = 1.80 (95% CI, 1.21–2.66; P = 0.003) and HR = 8.93 (95% CI, 3.32–24.01; P < 0.001), respectively, and the same applied to the analysis of PFS according to late ctDNA response, with HR = 1.53 (95% CI, 1.04–2.25; P = 0.029) and HR = 7.90 (95% CI, 2.97–21.06; P < 0.001) for patients in the ctDNA DC and ctDNA PD groups compared with patients in the ctDNA MR group, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Similarly, the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model confirmed that ctDNA response at the time of the first CT evaluation remained a predictor of OS. Patients in the ctDNA PD and the ctDNA DC groups had a significantly increased risk of death compared with those in the ctDNA MR group, with HR = 5.09 (95% CI, 1.46–17.7; P = 0.010) and HR = 1.88 (95% CI, 1.22–2.88; P = 0.004), respectively. Again, ECOG PS above 0 and the presence of liver metastases implied significantly shorter OS (Table 3, right). Individual analyses of OS according to late ctDNA response stratified by liver metastases showed consistent trends (Supplementary Fig. S4).

In the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, ctDNA response at the time of the first CT evaluation remained a predictor of PFS (Supplementary Table S2, right).

The multivariate Cox proportional hazards model of OS according to late ctDNA MR versus non-MR (aggregated DC and PD groups) resulted in a log-rank P value of 0.001, HR = 1.87 (95% CI, 1.27–2.75; P < 0.001) in univariate analysis and HR = 2.28 (95% CI, 1.61–4.13; P < 0.001) in multivariate analysis.

Similarly, prognostic value was observed when analyzing ctDNA changes from time point B to time point C, as demonstrated in Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3C. Among patients with both early (A and B) and late (A–C) ctDNA response evaluations, 91 had early ctDNA DC; five of these converted to ctDNA PD over time. None of the 31 patients with early ctDNA MR showed ctDNA PD during the first few months. Three patients with early ctDNA PD provided a blood sample at the time of the first CT evaluation, and all were categorized with ctDNA DC at that time point.

Discussion

In the present study, positive ctDNA prior to palliative chemotherapy was a strong negative prognostic biomarker in patients with metastatic PDAC, which is in accordance with earlier studies investigating the relation between positive ctDNA at baseline and clinical outcomes in patients with metastatic PDAC (25, 39). Also, early plasma ctDNA response, assessed prior to the second treatment cycle, was a strong predictor of longer OS. These results align with multiple smaller studies demonstrating a relation between ctDNA changes and clinical outcomes (18, 19, 40), some with ctDNA response evaluation as early as 2 weeks after the initiation of treatment (23, 24).

We used predefined thresholds for ctDNA response and progression, according to the ctDNA-RECIST (32), previously proposed by our research group, and based on ddPCR analysis. In the present study, the early response categories after the first cycle of chemotherapy were strong predictors of survival time: PD 3.6 months, DC 7.2 months, and MR 11.9 months, and the difference remained significant in multivariate analysis, underscoring the possibility of early response evaluation. The ctDNA changes between prior to the start of treatment and the first CT evaluation and from the second treatment cycle to the first CT evaluation were also related to OS and PFS, supporting the findings from the early ctDNA response.

The demonstrated associations between positive ctDNA or high ctDNA levels measured prior to therapy and OS raise the question of whether the ctDNA response merely reflects the prognostic value of pretreatment ctDNA levels. To address this, we explored the relationship between pretreatment ctDNA levels and ctDNA response categories using a Sankey plot. Our analysis revealed that the ctDNA response groups ctDNA MR and ctDNA DC included patients across all quartiles of pretreatment ctDNA levels, indicating that ctDNA response is not just a reflection of ctDNA levels prior to treatment but rather represents treatment-related changes. This has clinical potential. The ctDNA response evaluation may serve as a method for early prediction of treatment benefit. Besides these clinical advantages, the ctDNA evaluation may also be cost-effective compared with standard imaging-based evaluation, as demonstrated in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (41).

To date, the lack of validated cutoffs for ctDNA response and ctDNA progression has hampered progress toward clinical implementation. The current literature is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in terms of blood sampling time points, ctDNA quantification methods, ctDNA markers, and thresholds for ctDNA detection, ctDNA response, or ctDNA progression (18, 19, 23, 40, 42). Several studies use data-dependent cutoffs that lack external validation and adequate reporting of the robustness of the chosen threshold (23, 42). Additionally, these binary classification approaches limit the detection of patients with early PD. Robust standardization is needed to proceed toward clinical implementation.

To provide further insight into the performance of ctDNA-RECIST, we conducted survival analyses stratified by tertiles of early percentage change in ctDNA and additionally by applying a previously published cutoff defined as a reduction in ctDNA levels to ≤57.9% of the baseline value (23). Both approaches result in a loss of significant prognostic information. These findings support the potential of using ctDNA-RECIST.

In unresectable PDAC, ddPCR and next-generation sequencing (NGS) are the commonly used methods for ctDNA detection and quantification, with the KRAS mutation being the most used ctDNA marker (18, 20, 23, 42). Aberrant expression of HOXA9 is found in several solid tumors (26, 27), including ovarian cancer, lung cancer, breast cancer, and biliary tract cancer (28–31), but has until now only been sparsely investigated in PDAC. In this study, we demonstrated the relevance of HOXA9 methylation status based on ddPCR technology.

For longitudinal ctDNA response evaluation, ddPCR has the advantage of a faster turnaround time and lower cost compared with NGS (43). In published and ongoing studies, prospective ctDNA analysis was feasible and associated with a turnaround time of 2.7 (range: 2–6) and 3 (range: 2–4) working days (44, 45). In addition, NGS might face more challenges on a continuous scale relying on read depth and coverage.

Despite this potential, the pretreatment ctDNA detection rate was not complete, likely due to both biological and methodologic factors. Our predefined cutoff of five positive droplets was based on plasma from healthy donors, as previously described. With this threshold, ctDNA was positive in 71% of the patients with metastatic PDAC, a positivity rate that aligns with findings from other studies using ddPCR-based technologies for ctDNA analysis, ranging from 57% to 64% in patients with metastatic PDAC (23, 46–48). In our study, similar limitations were present with plasma CA19-9, the only established biomarker in this disease. For CA19-9, the clinical utility has some limitations due to interference from nonmalignant factors and individual differences in Lewis antigen expression. In our study, 12% of the patients with metastatic PDAC had CA19-9 within the reference range, whereas 29% had negative ctDNA prior to treatment. The ctDNA level prior to treatment was associated with the presence of liver metastases and a CA19-9 level above the median, suggesting that both ctDNA and CA19-9 reflect tumor burden. However, our analysis did not show a consistent correlation between the biomarkers. In multivariate analysis, including ctDNA, CA19-9, and the presence of liver metastases, ctDNA remained a consistent strong prognostic biomarker, whereas a less pronounced value was revealed with CA19-9. This suggests that the prognostic value of ctDNA extends beyond its association with liver involvement. Exploring this further, the prognostic value of early and late ctDNA response seemed consistent, independent of liver metastases. Given the limited subgroup sizes, these findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger cohorts.

The incomplete detection rate of both biomarkers, despite metastatic disease, demonstrates a certain drawback. The association with liver metastases may suggest low shedding as a contributor to ctDNA negativity. Methylated HOXA9 was detectable in 92% of patients at baseline, indicating that some of the 29% ctDNA-negative patients were below the predefined threshold rather than technically negative. Along with observed conversions during treatment, these findings support HOXA9 as a broadly applicable ctDNA marker. Currently, the clinical potential of measuring ctDNA longitudinally in patients with a positive baseline level seems strong, but increasing ctDNA sensitivity and specificity may be necessary for broader application. The clinical validity should be refined in future studies, in which studies in nonmetastatic but unresectable PDAC are equally relevant.

In the present sample size, we only detected five patients with ctDNA PD at the early ctDNA response evaluation. At the time of the first CT evaluation, another five ctDNA PD cases were identified, supporting the approach with serial sampling. The small number of patients in the ctDNA PD group limited the statistical robustness in the multivariate analyses, leading to uncertain HR estimates. To address this, we repeated the multivariate analyses by aggregating the ctDNA PD group with the ctDNA DC group.

In this study of real-world data, the evaluation of PD was based on either clinical or radiologic assessment, and detailed imaging data were unavailable. This limited the ability to compare ctDNA-based and radiologic response categories for concordance but will be investigated in future studies.

We selected eligible patients with the most complete sample sets for this retrospective analysis of prospectively collected blood samples. Consequently, there may be a group of patients who were not included in this study because they experienced PD or died before providing multiple blood samples. This exclusion of patients with more aggressive disease could explain the relatively small group of patients with observed ctDNA PD in our cohort. Alongside the inherent selection bias, the analysis of the late ctDNA response introduces an element of immortal time bias. Including only patients who survived to the first CT excludes those with early progression or death, overestimating survival and potentially underestimating the prognostic impact of ctDNA response. Although starting survival time at the CT evaluation could reduce immortal time, it would not address the underlying selection bias. Survival was calculated from treatment start to ensure comparability with prior studies (23, 24) and to provide a clinically meaningful context. The standardized sampling intervals and prioritization of complete sets likely helped limit the influence of survival bias in the analysis (23, 24).

The prospective collection of blood samples from a large cohort renders the study cohort unique as it was designed to systematically evaluate biomarkers at clinically relevant time points, including ctDNA. Storing samples over a longer period could potentially lower the detection rate (49, 50), but this was not the case in the present study, in which EDTA plasma samples were stored at −80°C, and mature real-world data strengthen the applicability of the results.

Ongoing parallel prospective studies aim to address the yet unestablished components of ctDNA-RECIST validation, including its correlation with radiologic response, validation against alternative cut-offs, and a randomized comparison with standard imaging-based evaluation.

The ctDNA-RECIST approach based on ddPCR analysis of ctDNA in plasma offers a clinically relevant, timely, and patient-centered alternative to imaging-based approaches.

Conclusion

Our findings support the clinical validity of ctDNA-RECIST as a future potential tool to guide treatment decisions in patients with metastatic PDAC, enabling clinicians to identify patients who benefit from therapy or may need an early treatment shift or discontinuation. Future studies should seek to prospectively validate ctDNA-RECIST and assess its added value in terms of prolonged survival and/or improved quality of life, comparing it with imaging-based response evaluations. This is the focus of the ctDNA-RECIST trial (NCT06562348).
