Factors influencing the fertility intentions of unmarried female nurses in South Korea: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Chung Hee Woo, Jiyeon Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnmarried female nurses may be easily affected by socially prescribed perfectionism or social comparison tendencies in a work environment that requires accurate performance within a limited time and in a social environment compared to the general population. The subjective sense of success in their careers is an important factor for understanding the various employment behaviors of the younger generation. However, limited research evidence exists on the relationship between these variables and fertility intention in the younger generation. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the possibility that socially prescribed perfectionism and social comparison orientation negatively affect unmarried female nurses’ fertility intentions, with work and generational characteristics and subjective career success as positive variables for their fertility intentions beyond work burden.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 190 unmarried female nurses aged 20–50 years who had worked for more than 6 months in general hospitals in South Korea. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling through an online community from June 20 to 30, 2023. For data analysis, the independent t-test, one-way analysis of variance, Pearson’s coefficient correlation, and stepwise multiple regression were used.ResultsThe factors influencing the fertility intention of unmarried female nurses were social comparison orientation (β = −0.22, p = 0.002), positive perception of child value (β = 0.19, p = 0.006), and subjective career success (β = 0.18, p = 0.014), in that order. Socially prescribed perfectionism was negatively correlated with fertility intention (r = −0.27, p < 0.001) but did not influence it.ConclusionsThis study showed that social comparison orientation and subjective career success were predictors of fertility intention among unmarried female nurses. To promote young unmarried nurses’ fertility intentions, creative systems should be developed where their social comparison orientation can positively affect fertility intentions, and nurses should be helped achieve subjective success and satisfaction with their careers, even in high-burden nursing settings.Keywords: Fertility intention, Socially prescribed perfectionism, Subjective career success, Social comparison orientation, Nurse

Background

The total fertility rate in South Korea in 2022 was projected at 0.78 [1]. The Korean total fertility rate, which refers to the average number of children a woman is expected to give birth to during her childbearing years, decreased drastically from 4.53 in 1970 to 1.48 in 30 years [2]. To achieve a stable population structure without changes in population growth or population aging, a long-term maintenance total fertility rate of 2.1 is required [1]. Although the phenomenon of the declining total fertility rate since 2000 has been experienced by countries in Eastern Europe, the Mediterranean, and East Asia, Korea’s fertility rate has been the lowest worldwide, excluding Hong Kong and Macau. Despite the average age of first childbirth in the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries being 29.3 years recently, this age has remained the highest in South Korea (32.3 years) and further increased to 33.0 years in 2022 [2]. As the fertility rate of women with high levels of education or professional occupation tends to be low, female labor force participation and fertility rates are highly related [3]. This might be because women may forgo parenthood instead of accepting career breaks for childcare when the work–family balance is difficult. Contrary to the tendency of the increase in the female employment rate from age 20 to 40 years and a decrease after age 50 in G5 countries, in South Korea, the employment rate, which increased until age 20, decreased sharply at age 30, recovered in the late 40s, and decreased again after age 50 [4]. Therefore, marriage and childbirth may have caused a decline in the employment rate of women in their 30s. Overall, the female labor force participation rate in South Korea is the lowest among the OECD countries [5]. When comparing the labor force participation rates between ages 25 and 44 years by sex, unmarried women were almost on par with men, whereas the rate halved among married women. Therefore, the characteristics of South Korean women’s economic activities and childbirth-related factors should be identified from various perspectives, considering their sociocultural uniqueness. However, in the case of unmarried women, this aspect cannot be ascertained through the actual fertility rate but rather from the desire or intention to give birth [6], as the intention predicts the execution of behavior in an extremely significant manner [7]. A previous study [8] that applied the Theory of Planned Behavior to identify factors affecting the childbirth intention of unmarried men and women stated that since the intention to have children is formed before the act, it is necessary to identify factors influencing this intention for those who are likely to have children in the next 5 to 15 years and establish policies to increase the birth rate of the younger generation. Additionally, marriage and childbirth have traditionally been equated but have recently changed rapidly in South Korea. Many younger generations aged < 40 years consider marriage and childbirth as distinct concepts and do not need children, even if married. This inference is supported by a study that showed that only 52.1% of married men and women have childbirth intentions [9].

In a study [10] that compared fertility rates at the peak birth point of 28 years by classifying the fertility rates of economically active women into professional and nonprofessional management positions, although no intergroup difference was detected, the fertility rate of public officials and teachers was relatively high. Therefore, the type of job or work environment can influence fertility intentions [4]. Clinical nurses have a high workload and undertake long-term shift work; particularly, the number of patients that a South Korean nurse should handle is significantly higher than the average for OECD member countries [3]. Clinical nurses, especially in South Korea, have a high workload and undertake long-term shift work, and the number of patients each nurse should care for is significantly higher than the OECD average [3]. Therefore, the factors that affect nurses’ childbirth intentions may differ based on their job characteristics. Studies related to fertility intentions among South Korean nurses have applied the theory of planned behavior [3] and ascertained the additional childbirth intentions of married nurses. These studies focused on age, number of children, values for children [11], attitudes toward childbirth, perceived behavioral control, and subjective norms [3] as factors that influence fertility intention. According to the Hospital Nurses Association [12], marriage, childbirth, and childcare are the third most important reasons for nurse turnover, which suggests that childbirth-related factors pose a major career-related problem for female nurses.

Even small mistakes in nursing services are frequently fatal; therefore, perfectionism may be instilled by encouraging nurses to perform their duties perfectly [13, 14]. According to a study [15], perfectionism is one of the factors that makes women voluntarily choose to stay unmarried. People with socially prescribed perfectionism fear not meeting the high standards set by others and are overly obsessed with the approval of others and society [13]. Accordingly, nurses with strong socially imposed perfectionism tend to feel significant pressure to perform their jobs accurately and thus perceive marriage and childbirth as additional burdens rather than as fulfilling factors in their lives.

Unmarried women mainly undertake an office-based or professional employment type. Professionals delay marriage due to work overload or lack of time and avoid marriage when the work–family balance is difficult [16]. Career success, rather than marriage or childbirth, is important for those with a strong desire to grow within the workplace [17]. In modern times, where values for work and occupation are diversifying, interest in one’s subjective perception of career success, rather than objective measures of career success, such as promotion and salary, has increased importance [18]. In a study examining factors affecting the intention to give birth among nurses at general hospitals, it was found that average monthly income had no significant effect on the intention to give birth [3]. Hotel workers’ subjective career success differed according to work–family and work–growth balances [19]. Thus, the relationship between subjective career success perceived by nurses not only with the work–family or work–growth balance but also with fertility intention can be predicted; however, little is known about this relationship.

In current society, it is common to share personal opinions and daily life experiences with others using Social Networking Services (SNS). The tendency to compare oneself with others and relatively evaluate one’s own value is called the social comparison orientation [20]. Even in a survey where people above their peak marriage period answered that the main reason for being unmarried or single was that they had not met a partner with conditions that met their expectations [10], the social comparison orientation appeared to be hidden. However, a study on the relationship satisfaction and social comparison orientation of 103 married people or cohabitants who read the case of other married individuals found that the influence of others depends on their own goals, even for those with high social comparison orientation [21]. Therefore, it is necessary to directly confirm whether social comparison orientation can predict the relationship between unmarried nurses’ childbirth intentions; however, a few studies have elucidated these associations.

To provide useful information for developing childbirth promotion policies tailored to nurses’ characteristics, this study aimed to explore the possibility that socially imposed perfectionism and social comparison orientation can negatively affect unmarried female nurses’ fertility intentions. In this study, beyond work burden, we evaluated socially prescribed perfectionism and subjective career success as explanatory variables for predicting the fertility intention of unmarried female nurses and included social comparison orientation as a universal social factor.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study investigated the fertility intentions of unmarried female South Korean nurses (N = 190).

Participants

The target population of this study was unmarried female nurses. The specific selection criteria were as follows: (1) nurses who had worked for more than 6 months in general or higher-level hospitals, (2) nurses aged 20–50 years (reproductive age group), and (3) willingness to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria included individuals with less than 6 months’ nursing experience, those younger than 20 years, those older than 50 years, male nurses, and married individuals. The appropriate number of samples was 184 (significance level of multiple regression analysis using G-Power 3.1.1 program α = 0.05, power (1 − β) 0.95, medium effect size (r) 0.15 and 12 predictors). With an expected dropout rate of 10%, 204 participants were recruited. After excluding respondents with insufficient data, 190 participants were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling through online hospital nurse communities to ensure maximum autonomy of participation. Additionally, the questionnaire was linked after verifying whether the selection criteria were met before the start of the questionnaire. Data collection was conducted from June 20 to 30, 2023, by linking structured questionnaires to an online platform.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board of Konyang University approved all study procedures (IRB-KY-2023-05-017). After confirming the overall contents of the study, including the purpose of the study presented in the recruitment letter, an online survey link was provided to nurses who wished to participate in the study. Before the study, the study description informed the following: (1) study purpose, procedures, and methods; (2) ensuring autonomy of participation and discontinuation; (3) no penalty for discontinuance; and (4) through anonymization and coding of data, it is planned to store as a file with a password set in a state where personal information cannot be identified and then completely destroyed. The participants checked the study description, clicked “agree” to start the survey, and clicked “exit survey” to stop. This study utilized an online data collection method that sufficiently guaranteed the autonomy of all participants and was exempt from requiring written consent from the institutional review board.

Study measures

Demographic variables

The general characteristics of the participants were age, education level, work type (fixed or shift), work experience (< 3, 3–5, 6–9, and ≥ 10 years), work-environment satisfaction (satisfied or dissatisfied), work–life balance (satisfied or dissatisfied), the health of the original family (healthy or unhealthy), child value (valuable or not valuable), and childbirth without marriage (disagree, conditionally agree, or disagree).

Fertility intention

Fertility intention was investigated using a Childbearing Intention Scale developed by Jeong [22]. The 20-item scale has the following four sub-factors: individual (2 items, including “It is much better spend time on social success than having and raising children”), family (2 items, including “I have to have children if my parents want”), economic (4 items, including “I am hesitant to have children because of the cost and burden of raising them”), and policy (3 items, including “If the government supports more childbirth and child care fees, I intend to have a child”), and uses a 4-point Likert scale. For the inverse calculation items, a higher total score after the inverse calculation indicated a higher fertility intention. Cronbach’s α for the reliability of the scale was 0.66 (0.65 to 0.85 for sub-factors) in the previous study [22] and 0.78 (0.70 to 0.90 for sub-factors) in this study.

Socially prescribed perfectionism

Socially prescribed perfectionism was explained using the Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale, developed by Hewitt and Flett [23], a Korean version that was validated by Han [24]. Among the subdomains of the perfectionism scale, self-oriented perfectionism, other-oriented perfectionism, and socially prescribed perfectionism, 15 items (including “People expect me to be perfect”) of socially prescribed perfectionism necessary for the research topic were used. On a Likert 7-point scale, 1 point (completely disagree) to 7 points (strongly agree) were rated, and a higher score indicated higher socially prescribed perfectionism. Cronbach’s α of the tool was 0.78 in a study investigating marriage intentions [25] and 0.71 in this study.

Subjective career success

The subjective career success of working women was calculated using a 7-item scale developed by Kim [26]. It is a 5-point Likert scale composed of the following two subdomains: career satisfaction (4 questions, including “I am satisfied with my work experience so far when it comes to achieving my career goal”) and employability (3 questions, including “I think I can easily find a new job in my field if I want to”). A higher total score indicates a higher subjective career success rate. Cronbach’s α of the instrument was 0.82 in the preceding study [26] and 0.77 (0.72 to 0.83 for subdomains) in this study.

Social comparison orientation

Social comparison orientation was measured using a Korean version of the Iowa–Netherlands Comparison Orientation Measure developed by Gibbons and Bunnk [20] and verified by Choi [27]. The 11-item scale is a 5-point Likert scale with the following two subdomains: abilities (6 questions, including “I always like to compare my behavior to that of others”) and opinions (5 questions, including “When you try to learn something, you try to know what others think about it”). The higher the total score, the higher the social comparison orientation score. Cronbach’s α of the tool was 0.83 in the previous study [27] and 0.70 (subdomains 0.68 or 70) in this study.

Data analysis

Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). The normality of continuous variables was confirmed through a normality evaluation. An independent t-test, one-way analysis of variance, and Scheffé’s post hoc test were applied if a post hoc analysis was required for differences in childbearing intentions according to the general characteristics of the participants. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to evaluate the relationships between major variables. Finally, factors that influenced fertility intention were analyzed using stepwise multiple regression.

Factors that influence fertility intention

To confirm the influencing factors on the participants’ fertility intentions, first, among the general characteristics, age, work-environment satisfaction, and child value, which had statistically significant differences, were converted into dummy variables. Second, the factors influencing participants’ fertility intention were identified using stepwise multiple regression. The independent variables included socially prescribed perfectionism, subjective career success, and social comparison orientation, which were significantly related to the participant’s fertility intention and previously generated dummy variables.

After testing the assumptions of regression analysis, the possibility of multicollinearity between independent variables (especially including work-environment satisfaction and subjective career success) was low. Specifically, the margin tolerance was 0.81 to 0.97, > 0.1, and the variance inflation coefficient was 1.13–1.36, < 10. It was confirmed that the condition index of the independent variables was 12 (criteria: ≤ 30), and the range of the variance ratio was 2%–37% (criteria: < 90%).

In the final result, social comparison orientation (β = −0.22, p = 0.002), subjective career success (β = 0.18, p = 0.014), and child value (β = 0.19, p = 0.006) were significant factors for fertility intention, and the explanatory power of these factors for childbirth intention was found to be 18% (Table 4).

Discussion

From 2010 to 2022, the pregnancy intentions of 18,376 female nurses aged 19–44 years in North America were strongly correlated with the incidence of pregnancy [28]. Therefore, the intention of childbirth of the participants in this study was used as a variable to indirectly understand the incidence of fertility among nurses. Furthermore, this study is significant because it attempts to determine the influence of fertility intentions among unmarried female nurses by considering factors, including socially prescribed perfectionism, subjective career success, and social comparison orientation, which have rarely been noted as contributors to fertility intentions.

The fertility intention of the participants was 2.15 points (total score: 4 points), which was lower than the fertility intentions of employed unmarried women [29] and university students in South Korea [30]. Particularly, in Han and Jung’s study [3], which had a married population ratio of 44.2%, the fertility intention was low compared with the nurses’ fertility intentions (3.69 of the 5 points). However, because these previous studies used a single question, it was difficult to directly compare them with this study. Nevertheless, it should be noted that unmarried female nurses have lower fertility intentions.

Social comparison orientation, child values, and subjective career success affected influential factors on unmarried female nurses’ fertility intentions. Among the study participants, the fertility intention in their 20s was significantly higher than that in their 40s, and the fertility intention in groups that were satisfied with the work environment or had positive child values was higher. However, age and work-environment satisfaction, which had a statistically significant difference in the average comparison, were not factors that ultimately influenced fertility intentions. This result is partially consistent with previous studies [3, 9, 29]. Child value is an influencing factor of childbirth intention in employed unmarried women [29], hospital workers [31], and male and female college students [32]. Thus, child value has been continuously reported as an important variable for more than a decade in predicting the fertility intentions of the younger generation.

In contrast, socially prescribed perfectionism had a statistically significant negative relationship with the dependent variable – fertility intention – in the correlation analysis. This result differs from that of another study [25], which found that perfectionism was not related to the marital intentions of unmarried men and women. Perfectionists tend to have dichotomous standards, and even in small tasks, they struggle to meet high standards [33]. Accordingly, nurses with high levels of socially prescribed perfectionism tended to perceive career management, marriage, and childbirth negatively. However, this was not an influencing factor when compared with the accompanying variables. Socially prescribed perfectionism is generally negatively related to positive emotional factors, such as self-acceptance, self-esteem, and subjective well-being [34]. Socially prescribed perfectionism experienced at work may be overcome or offset by positive emotional factors, such as career satisfaction or subjective career success. Nurses’ fertility intentions may involve diverse and complex pathways or mechanisms. Therefore, in future research on nurses’ fertility intentions, it is necessary to identify incentives, including work environment or organizational characteristics, that can offset the negative perception of childbirth.

Social comparison orientation was found to negatively affect unmarried female nurses’ fertility intentions. From the perspective of modern society, where materialism prevails, raising children has a cost structure, such as time, labor, and economy. If the younger generation does not assess the value of children regarding quality when evaluating the cost of childbirth, they prefer things that increase life satisfaction with less cost input than raising children, and the tendency to give birth is lowered [1]. Social comparison orientation is known to have a negative relationship with life satisfaction and self-esteem in the younger generation and a positive relationship with fear of negative evaluation [35, 36]. An individual’s propensity for social comparison, if it emerges from a materialistic context, undermines a sense of well-being [35]. This is difficult to conclude because social comparison orientation alone may not create the motivation to identify with others [21], and a few studies have identified the relationship between fertility intention and social comparison orientation. However, if marriage or childbirth still targets comparison with others, there is a possibility of staying unmarried [10]. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a new paradigm of SNS content or a spreading culture that can positively affect fertility intentions.

Until recently, career success variables have received attention as the final outcome of career development activities [37], and there have been a few attempts to understand their relationship with marriage or fertility intentions. In a study conducted mainly on office workers [18], subjective career success was highly correlated with their growth orientation and quality of life of office workers. In this study, in the context of unmarried female nurses’ fertility intentions, subjective career success was an important influencing factor. This suggests that the childbirth problem experienced by nurses with the conditions of being single and female was intrinsically linked to their growth orientation or quality of life. Particularly, the employability subdomain had a positive relationship with the fertility intentions of employed unmarried nurses. Nurses are recognized as having jobs with relatively high prospects for employability, even if they experience high-intensity work stress, and can play a positive role in their fertility intention. Therefore, it is expected that similar studies will be repeated in various settings to conduct an in-depth analysis to ensure that the mechanism of subjective career success in nurses’ intentions to give birth is elaborately identified.

The explanatory power of these variables for intention to give birth was 18.0%. A significant difference is found in influencing factors and explanatory power from a study that explained 36.1% of nurses’ intentions to give birth only with the attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control of planned action theory [3]. Given the long gap between Han’s study [3] and the recently reported total fertility rate in South Korea, careful interpretation of the implications is recommended. The identification of childbirth intention is considered more complicated because of the influence of the social phenomenon where the fertility rate is severely declining as the value of marriage or children changes significantly [1, 10]. In a study based on the Nurses’ Health Study 3 Cohort, work schedules did not affect the fecundity of 26,693 North American nurses, whereas physical factors, including working more than 40 h per week, had an effect [38]. Furthermore, although the childbirth-related fears of women of Chinese childbearing age negatively affect fertility intentions, childbirth preparedness can overcome childbirth-related fears and increase fertility intentions [39]. Therefore, further studies are required to explore the various roles of fertility-related factors.

This study had some limitations. First, applying a cross-sectional survey method may not be sufficient to provide an integrated view of fertility rates that fluctuate over time. In-depth data can be collected if the longitudinal survey method or the survey frequency is increased. Second, as this study was conducted only on nurses interested in the influence of the work environment, it did not provide a universal perspective on working women. However, in understanding the fertility intention of working women, the uniqueness of each occupation is more important than a universal perspective; therefore, in-depth research on various occupations should be undertaken in future studies.

Conclusion

This study selected socially prescribed perfectionism, subjective career success, and social comparison orientation as explanatory variables that have rarely been used as related variables in fertility intention research. We confirmed that these variables are related to or affect the fertility intentions of younger generations working in nursing environments that require precise performance under time pressure. Based on the results, it is hoped that measures will be implemented to reduce negative factors and strengthen positive factors in the policy of encouraging marriage and childbirth for nurses with a high proportion of unmarried women of childbearing age. Additionally, as social comparison orientation is a concept that reflects the social condition for comparing daily life with an unspecified number of people through SNS, it is hoped that repeated studies will be conducted to evaluate whether fertility intention can be predicted for various individuals.
