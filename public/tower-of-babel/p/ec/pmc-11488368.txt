Hemorrhagic intramedullary spinal cord metastasis from renal cell carcinoma: a rare case 15 years after cured renal cell carcinoma. Illustrative case
Authors: Mohammad Khalil Al-Barbarawi, Amr Badary, Jörg Silbermann, Oday Atallah, Mohamad Kinan Sultan, Adel Osman, Abdulaziz Albardan, Salah Maksoud, Wahab Moustafa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDRenal cell carcinoma (RCC), the most common kidney cancer, often metastasizes to bones, lungs, liver, and the central nervous system. Intramedullary spinal cord metastases (ISCMs) are rare, primarily originating from lung and breast cancer, while ISCMs originating from melanoma, malignant lymphoma, colon cancer, ovarian cancer, and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) are even more rare. ISCMs represent only 0.1%–2% of tumors of the spine. The incidence of RCC is low, accounting for only 4%–9% of such tumors.1 At the time of diagnosis, it is estimated that RCC metastasis will occur in approximately 23% of patients; however, RCC metastasis to the spine is extremely rare, occurring in only 3%–4% of patients.2, 3 Metastatic RCC is associated with poor overall survival, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 12% in patients with advanced disease.4 However, RCC exhibits a unique pattern of metastatic spread, including a tendency for late recurrence, even decades after removal of the primary tumor.5 Furthermore, RCC can spread to specialized sites, such as the pancreas, adrenals, peripheral nerves, and the central nervous system, including the brain and spinal cord.

ISCM has unique characteristics, usually requiring early treatment due to rapid neurological progression. A presentation with bleeding metastasis is extremely rare, with only one case reported in the literature to date.6 Understanding the clinical presentation of RCC metastasis, diagnostic challenges, and multidisciplinary therapeutic strategies will enhance our knowledge and improve outcomes for patients affected by this serious disease. Treatment response and disease progression were closely followed with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Illustrative Case

An 86-year-old man was referred to our institution with a 3-week history of progressive right lower-limb weakness and reduced sensation. His past medical history included right nephrectomy for RCC 15 years earlier and conservatively managed L4–S1 spondylosis for degenerative lumbar scoliosis 23 years earlier. No relevant comorbidities (e.g., vascular disorders) were reported.

Upon initial evaluation, the patient had Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 1–2 both distally and proximally in the right lower limb, and MRC grade 4 in the left lower limb affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups. Distal hypoesthesia was evident in the right lower limb and right knee. Tendon reflexes were absent. The L3 and L4 sensory levels were affected.

Computed tomography (CT) revealed stability of the previous spondylosis, and an incidental noncystic mass was found at the left upper pole of the kidney. Subsequent MRI of the spine with and without contrast raised suspicion for a bleeding lesion measuring approximately 30 mm in the conus medullaris at the level of the T11–12 vertebrae, as seen on T2-weighted imaging (Fig. 1) without contrast enhancement. Differential diagnoses included trauma, tumor, and other vascular abnormality.

Axial (left) and sagittal (right) T2-weighted MRI showing a bleeding mass at the level of T11–12.

After discussion with the patient, urgent exploration was recommended. Using sonography-guided microsurgical techniques, a T12 laminectomy was performed, and complete en bloc removal of the intramedullary bleeding lesion was achieved (Fig. 2). As a final step, we intraoperatively confirmed complete removal of the tumor using sonography. Postoperatively, positron emission tomography (PET)–CT scans showed no remaining tumor. The patient received deep venous thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis 1 day postoperatively.

A: Intraoperative image obtained after dorsal durotomy, showing the posterior spinal arteries. B: Intraoperative image obtained after dorsal midline myelotomy. C: Intraoperative image obtained after removing the bleeding lesion.

Histopathological evaluation revealed a hemorrhagic focus with a sparse presence of atypical cells displaying an altered nucleus-plasma relation. Immunohistochemical analysis using specific endothelial markers (ERG, CK8-18, and PAX8) confirmed the presence of endothelial cells, consistent with a metastatic lesion. The findings concurred with the diagnosis of intramedullary metastasis of RCC, morphologically confirming a late manifestation of the previously diagnosed, moderately differentiated clear cell RCC originating from the right kidney, demonstrating a notably increased proliferation rate.

The case was thoroughly reviewed by an interdisciplinary neuro-oncological board. Further diagnostic investigations, together with postoperative PET-CT scanning, including brain MRI, were diligently performed, which showed multiple small metastases in the brain.

After declining appropriate management for the brain metastasis, the patient underwent focused radiotherapy on the vertebral column, receiving a total of 12 cycles, 7 of which were administered during his hospital admission. In addition, he had intravenous immunotherapy with pembrolizumab 200 mg, in conjunction with oral axitinib 6 mg daily.

The patient showed a noticeable improvement in motor and sensory deficits. He was discharged with motor and sensory function restored to normal (grade 5) in the left lower limb. The MRC grade improved in the right proximal lower limb (grade 4) and right distal lower limb (grade 2), while the lost tendon reflexes remained unchanged.

Regular outpatient follow-ups were scheduled to comprehensively monitor treatment response and disease evolution. The patient continued to be followed up regularly in our outpatient clinic without new neurological manifestations. Three months later, the patient died at home, likely due to pulmonary artery thrombosis, following his decision to discontinue DVT prophylaxis without medical consultation.

Discussion

The presentation of ISCM from RCC is extremely rare, especially considering 15 years of remission after nephrectomy. This case represents a silent RCC metastasis manifesting long after initial treatment of the RCC and its presumed cure. The literature indicates that late-stage metastasis of RCC can occur. This highlights the critical need for long-term monitoring and vigilance in patients with a history of RCC. A study by Hadgaonkar et al. reported similar findings, emphasizing that RCC can metastasize many years after initial diagnosis and treatment, thus requiring long-term follow-up.7

ISCM presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. The differential diagnosis of such lesions typically includes primary spinal cord tumors, muscle dysfunction, and malignant lesions.8 The absence of contrast enhancement in this case complicated the initial diagnostic process, which reflected variation in ISCM imaging characteristics. In comparison, primary spinal tumors often show a different enhancement pattern on MRI and help distinguish them from metastatic lesions. Kim et al. described the difficulty in distinguishing between primary and secondary spinal lesions, especially in the absence of specific reflex characteristics.9

Surgical management of hemorrhagic ISCM requires a balance between maximization of resection and preservation of neurological function. In this case, sonography-guided microsurgical techniques facilitated complete resection. A recent systematic review showed that the use of intraoperative ultrasound offers significant benefits, including precise localization of spinal masses, identification of vascular structures, and assessment for any remnants.10 Prior studies that have detailed similar surgical techniques, such as the work of Nguyen et al., also support the effectiveness of careful surgical intervention in the treatment of solid spinal metastases.11

Postoperative therapeutic strategies, including radiotherapy and immunotherapy, focused on the control of residual disease and further neurological deterioration, played an important role in this case. The use of pembrolizumab and axitinib exhibits a similar approach to RCC, reflecting the advantages of targeted therapy and immunomodulation. Many published studies have demonstrated the efficacy of combination therapy in the management of metastatic RCC,12, 13 supporting the favorable clinical outcomes observed in our patient.

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) have shown promising results. A review of nine patients with ISCMs supports SRS as a safe and effective treatment.14 Although limited, the literature indicates that SBRT is both safe and effective for local control of intramedullary lesions, with patients showing complete responses and no neurological deficits from radiosurgery. Thus, SBRT or SRS is becoming the preferred treatment for intramedullary metastases, offering good control without causing myelopathy.15

The patient’s subsequent multiple brain metastases highlight the aggressive nature of metastatic RCC and the need for comprehensive systemic treatment. In this case, the involvement of neuro-oncological teams and intensive postoperative imaging exemplifies the collaborative effort needed to achieve optimal patient outcomes.16, 17 The event of fatal pulmonary arterial thrombosis highlights the complexity of managing metastatic disease and the need for continued research into effective systemic treatment and strategies to prevent thromboembolic events occurring in cancer patients.

Observations

This study illustrates the rare occurrence of a hemorrhagic ISCM from RCC 15 years postnephrectomy. The patient’s presentation with progressive neurological deficits was effectively managed through sonography-guided microsurgical resection and targeted postoperative therapies, resulting in significant motor and sensory improvement. The appearance of multiple brain metastases and the subsequent death underline the aggressive nature of metastatic RCC and the challenges in its management.

Lessons

The most important lesson of this case is the critical role of integrating surgery, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy in managing late-stage ISCM from RCC, which can result in temporary disease control and significant neurological improvement. However, the case also highlights the aggressive nature of metastatic RCC and the importance of ongoing surveillance and comprehensive care for patients with a history of RCC.
