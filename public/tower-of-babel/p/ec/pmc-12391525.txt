Acoustic Emissions from Grey Glacier, Chilean Patagonia: dataset of infrasound measurements
Authors: Claudia Sánchez, Duccio Gheri, Edgardo Casanova, Luciano Zuccarello, Silvio De Angelis, Emanuele Marchetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalving is a critical process contributing to ice mass loss in tidewater and lake-terminating glaciers, with significant implications for freshwater input, glacier dynamics, and climate change. Infrasound emissions generated by calving events represent a powerful tool for monitoring, quantifying, and understanding the mechanisms that drive glacier retreat. In this study, we present the first acoustic dataset collected over a six-day period during the austral summer at Grey Glacier, located in Torres del Paine National Park, Chile. We provide a detailed description of the instrumentation setup, data acquisition procedures, and signal processing methodologies. By applying high-sensitivity infrasound array processing techniques, we demonstrate that coherent signals generated by calving events can be reliably extracted from the acoustic data, despite environmental conditions, such as strong winds that affect infrasound propagation. This dataset represents a valuable resource for advancing research on cryosphere dynamics and offers practical applications for the glaciological and geophysical communities.Subject terms: Geophysics; Cryospheric science; Design, synthesis and processing

Background & Summary

The Patagonian Icefields are among the most rapidly retreating glaciated regions on Earth, with mass loss occurring primarily from ocean- and lake-terminating glaciers. Previous studies have shown that both the Northern and Southern Patagonian Icefields are experiencing rapid and substantial volume loss, at rates currently exceeding 20 km3 per year1. The contribution to sea-level rise per unit area of the Patagonian Icefields exceeds that of glaciers in Alaska2.

Despite the importance of calving in the loss of glacier mass, the physical mechanisms driving these events3, such as ice fracturing, basal motion and subaqueous break-off remain poorly understood. Some research4 highlights the inherent complexity and variability of calving processes, which are influenced by both internal glacier dynamics and external environmental factors. Calving glaciers generate distinct seismic and acoustic signals5–7, making them well-suited for the application of geophysical monitoring techniques that can help detect the onset, location, and type of ice loss events.

However, the observation of calving events in the field remains challenging owing to the remote location, and often hazardous nature, of glacial termini. Direct observations are limited, yet they are essential for understanding glacier-lake interactions and their influence on the local hydrology and regional climate. Geodetic and satellite data provide insight into long-term patterns in glacier behavior8, while in-situ geophysical techniques, such as seismic and infrasound, offer the potential for real-time observations of glacier dynamics under all weather conditions, including cloud cover.

Infrasound, that is, acoustic atmospheric waves with frequencies below 20 Hz, has emerged as a powerful tool to remotely detect and characterize mass movement events (e.g.,refs.9–11). Infrasound data can reveal signals from sub-aerial ice fractures, iceberg impacts, and detachment processes7,12. Long-term monitoring in Greenland13 revealed over 700,000 infrasonic detections related to glacier activity over an 18-year period, highlighting strong diurnal and inter-annual variability linked to atmospheric and oceanic temperature changes. Experimental studies have demonstrated that array-based infrasound analyses enable the detection of glacier collapse events with frequencies above 1 Hz, and allow tracking the gravity-driven movement of the source12. Yet, a high temporal resolutions infrasound observations from actively calving lake-terminating glaciers, especially in the Patagonian region, are lacking.

In February 2025, we deployed a temporary network of infrasound sensors near the calving front of Grey Glacier, a major outlet of the South Patagonian Ice Field. The Grey Glacier is a key site to investigate glacier dynamics in temperate environments. Its behavior is influenced by the complex interplay of ice flow, topography14, frontal ice geometry15, and seasonal variations in pro-glacial lake temperature16. These characteristics make the Grey Glacier an ideal natural laboratory for assessing the impacts of ongoing climate change17,18.

The acquired dataset captures a unique acoustic signature of glacier collapse processes, such as ice detachment events, contributing to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving glacier retreat within the lake.

In this manuscript we present a curated dataset of infrasound records that can be used to investigate glacier calving dynamics and, potentially, estimate ice loss volumes through acoustic observations. We describe instrumentation, sensor deployment strategies, and data recovery. Finally, the paper provides representative examples of recorded calving events to facilitate the use of the dataset by the wider scientific community in studies of glacier stability, lake-glacier interactions, and climate-driven ice loss.

Grey Glacier, Chile

The Grey Glacier located in the Southern Patagonian ice field (50.97∘S, 73.25∘W), is one of the largest and most dynamic glaciers in Chile. It extends approximately 28 km from its accumulation zone in the Andes to its calving front in Grey Lake, within the Torres del Paine National Park. The glacier is a temperate, lake-terminating system influenced by strong westerly winds16, and a maritime climate with high annual precipitation and regular seasonal temperature variations. Several authors, e.g.,refs.2,8,19–21, observed that the Grey Glacier has undergone significant retreat and thinning over the last 80 years, attributed to rising temperatures and increased calving activity. Its frontal position is highly sensitive to external forcing, including lake level fluctuations, subaqueous melting and atmospheric conditions16. The glacier’s calving activity contribute to freshwater input into Lake Grey, influencing local hydrology and controlling the local ecosystem balance8. As a key feature of the Patagonian cryosphere, the Grey Glacier is an important site for studying ice loss, climate change effects, and glacial-lake interactions in one of the most rapidly warming regions on Earth.

Methods

A 4-element infrasound array was deployed at Grey Glacier between February 04-11, 2025 by a team of researchers from the University of Bologna (IT), Magallanes University (CH) and the INGV-PISA (IT) (Fig. 1). The array was installed on moraine deposits along the western flank of the valley, near a glacial river. Table 1 presents the coordinates of the four sensors, with the digital data acquisition system co-located with sensor 4. The GREY array consisted of four Item differential pressure transducers (see https://www.item-geophysics.it/, last access 27 March, 2025) with a sensitivity of 400 mV/Pa in the frequency band 0.01-200 Hz and full-scale pressure range of +/−25 Pa with and a frequency response of -3dB at 0.1 Hz. Each sensor is connected via a 30 m electrical cable to a 24-bit Minimus Güralp digitizer (https://www.guralp.com/, last access 27 March, 2025), with data sampled at 100 Hz and GPS time-stamped.

The total power consumption of the four sensors and the digitizer is approximately 0.25 Ah; power was supplied by two 12V/40Ah Gel rechargeable batteries (Ultracell UHR40-12), which powered the station for the entire 6-day analysis period without the need for solar panels. To reduce wind noise, each sensor is deployed inside a non-sealed (to ensure coupling between the microphone and the atmosphere) plastic box buried 5 cm in the ground and covered with small rocks. The array was arranged with a L-shaped geometry (see Fig. 1). This layout was optimized to facilitate effective data acquisition while ensuring a compact and efficient deployment. The array was positioned according to (i) ensure site accessibility, (ii) minimize elevation discrepancies among the sensors within the array to maintain measurement uniformity, and (iii) optimize its capability to detect and discriminate activity across the entire Front 3 and part of Front 2 of the glacier.

The system operated continuously throughout the recording period in February 2025, without any data dropout. The distance from the array’s geometric center to Front 3 varied from 415 ± 6 m to 1389 ± 6 m over a back-azimuth range of 34°- 115°N (Fig. 1), while for Front 2 the distance ranged from 2297 ± 6 m to 3128 ± 6 m over a narrower back-azimuth range (100°- 115°N). Figure 1 suggests that a site installed further to the South, facing both fronts of the Grey Glacier, would likely have offered better azimuthal discrimination. However, due to the morphology of the U-shaped glacial valleys, that location lacked flat surfaces and featured steep walls, resulting in logistical challenges for access.

Data Records

The complete dataset described in this manuscript, including raw waveform data and station metadata is available from the repository in ref. 22. The dataset is composed by four Mseed files, the file GREY_detections. mat and the script detection_per_hour_polar_plot. m. The Mseed files are related to raw data in the time period of analysis for the four elements of the array and are named as GREY. FI. 0(1–4). HDF. The GREY_detections.mat file contains infrasound detections derived from array processing, characterizing the wavefield in terms of pressure amplitude, back-azimuth, and apparent velocity. It includes two structures: data, contains the time series of the detections, including key features such as pressure amplitude, back-azimuth, coherence, apparent velocity, and peak frequency. The info field provides the associated processing parameters used to compute these features—for example, the time window of analysis, frequency band, and time shift applied during array processing. For a comprehensive description of the processing approach and parameter definitions, please refer to ref. 22. The final file is used to visualize and reproduce the results (i.e., detections) shown in the figures presented in the Technical Validation section.

Here, we present the data recorded by sensor CH1, showing the Root-Mean-Square Amplitude (RMSA) calculated over a 10-minute moving window in the 1-30 Hz frequency band (Fig.1). Figure 2a displays RMSA values on the order of few Pascals, marked by a series of short-duration peaks that coincide with high-wind gust events (red line). In low-wind conditions, the RMSA remains consistently low showing weak variations. Figure 2b, shows the spectrogram of the Power Spectral Density (PSD), showing that the wind-induced peaks are accompanied by transient increases in energy predominantly at lower frequencies (below 10 Hz), with occasional bursts at higher frequencies. The strong temporal correlation between wind and infrasound amplitudes suggests that meteorological conditions can significantly increase noise level, and modulate the infrasound energy distribution across the frequency band.

Technical Validation

Array processing analysis enhances the signal-to-noise ratio, allowing for better discrimination between coherent signals and background noise (e.g., wind) while providing more precise estimates of wave parameters23. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, the Southern hemisphere Westerly Winds (SWW) in the Patagonia region are particularly strong and persistent during the summer months24. Given these challenging conditions, deploying an array of sensors equipped with wind noise reduction systems (WNRs) is essential for improving the detection and characterization of coherent infrasound signals.

Analogous to other beamforming techniques25–27 widely employed in infrasound analysis, our array processing method exploit a multi-channel cross-correlation method based on ref. 9. The method assumes that coherent signals are correlated across array sensors, while noise is not. Data were band-pass filtered between 2–30 Hz and analyzed using a 5-second sliding window (1-second step) over all sensor triplets in the four-element array, with an aperture ~30m. where Δtij is the observed time delay between sensors i and j and Δt^ij the expected or theoretical time delay9. Time residual ΔTn (~0.2 s) quantifies the effective time delay of a signal across a sensor triplet: values near zero indicate high correlation, while larger residuals reflect uncorrelated or noisy signals. Analogously, coherence is the degree of similarity between signals based on the cross-correlation function in the time window of analysis. It ranges from 0 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates perfectly correlated signals, and values close to 0 indicate low correlation (noise).

Under planar wave assumption, we estimate the Back-azimuth (in ∘) of the signal by analyzing the time delays between sensors in each triplet. Using the known distances between sensors we solve the two equations system9, that relate the wave direction assuming a constant propagation velocity across the array (c = 340 m/s). Specifically, the back-azimuth defined as the clockwise angle from North to the direction of the incoming wave indicates the horizontal direction from which the infrasound originates.

Array processing allows to estimate the infrasonic source direction and to differentiate the signal of interest from other sources. Note that, due to time-domain discretizations, signals lasting longer than the chosen time shift may result in multiple detections. For example, a 10-second signal, coherent across the array, results in 20 detections considering the time shift of 0.5 s.

Infrasound produced by glacier break-off events

Figures 4 and 5 show two examples of calving events recorded during our field experiment, also confirmed by photographic and video evidence. The infrasound waveform in Fig. 4a associated with the calving event at 09:58 UTC on 5 February 2025 is characterized by an emergent signal (i.e., lacking a sharp impulsive onset), reaching a peak amplitude of 2 Pa at sensor CH1, located approximately 400 meters from the source, about 2 seconds after the signal onset. Although the signal does not exhibit a clear or distinct waveform pattern, its energy is concentrated in two frequency bands-2-5 Hz and 10–11 Hz—across all four sensors of the GREY array (Fig. 4b). Array processing enables the identification of this signal from the background (Fig. 4c) with a coherence values > 0.8. The back-azimuth of the event is estimated at 73°N, which aligns well with visual observations (Fig. 4d).

The second event presented here (Fig. 5a) corresponds to a minor collapse at 17:53 UTC on 5 February. The recorded amplitude was approximately one order of magnitude lower than the event in Fig. 4, with a maximum of about 0.2 Pa at sensor CH1. The waveform is characterized by two distinct peaks of similar amplitude. The spectral analysis from all four sensors shows a clear peak around 6 Hz (Fig. 5b). This was a smaller collapse, with associated low-amplitude pressure, only slightly exceeding the background noise; the signal is identified by coherence values of around 0.7. The estimated back-azimuth is 90°N, which is consistent with visual observations (see Fig. 5d,e and the supplementary video materials).
