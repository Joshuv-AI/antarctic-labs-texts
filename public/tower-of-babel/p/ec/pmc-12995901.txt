Loss of factor XIII decreases immune cell infiltration and enhances vessel wall endothelialization after venous thrombosis∗
Authors: Mattea Pellerito, Catherine Luke, Kadri Kangro, Qing Cai, Abigail Dowling, Andrea Obi, Alisa S. Wolberg, Peter Henke
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Factor XIII (FXIII) is a protransglutaminase present in plasma and cells, including platelets and leukocytes.1 Following its activation, FXIIIa introduces covalent crosslinks within fibrin chains and in other plasma and cellular proteins. Fibrin crosslinking enhances the clot’s mechanical stability (stiffness) and resistance to biochemical dissolution (fibrinolysis). During venous thrombus (VT) formation, FXIII-dependent fibrin crosslinking also promotes red blood cell retention in contracted thrombi and increases thrombus mass2,3; this effect has been specifically attributed to the plasma FXIII compartment.4 Consequently, plasma FXIII may be a therapeutic target to reduce VT burden.5,6

VT resolution involves extensive structural remodeling of the vein wall, including collagen deposition and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transformation that increases vessel wall mechanical stiffness and decreases contractility.7 Persistent vein wall injury is associated with valvular reflex and recurrent venous thrombosis, contributing to the incidence and severity of postthrombotic syndrome.8 The VT fibrin matrix contributes to neovascularization by providing a scaffold for endothelial cells to adhere and form channels.9, 10, 11 Cells trapped within the thrombus also contribute to VT resolution by secreting proangiogenic growth factors and matrix metalloproteinases that promote neovascular channel formation and thrombus recanalization, and enable leukocyte influx into the thrombus.12 We and others have shown that the local monocyte phenotype—proinflammatory or prohealing—dictates the fate of the adjacent vein wall, wherein a lack of prohealing monocytes worsens post-VT injury (Kimball et al13, Gallagher et al,14 and P. Henke, M. Pellerito, and C. Luke, unpublished observation, December 2025). Given the profoundly reduced mass seen in FXIII-deficient VT at 24 hours,2, 3, 4 herein we determined the effect of genetic FXIII deficiency on subsequent VT evolution.

We subjected adult (8- to 18-week old) male, F13a1+/+, F13a1+/−, and F13a1−/− mice15 to an experimental model of VT induced by inferior vena cava ligation, as described.16 Male mice were used due to reduce the overall number of mice, variable gonadal vein anatomy in females, and higher incidence of VT in males.17,18 We measured VT mass and length, and characterized cellular and structural composition via hematoxylin and eosin staining, immunohistochemistry (Masson’s trichrome, CD31 staining), and immunoblotting thrombus lysates. We assessed vein wall injury by histology and immunohistochemistry (CD31 staining), as described.19 Detailed methods are provided in the supplemental Materials.

Eight days after thrombus induction, F13a1+/+ and F13a1+/− mice had similar thrombus mass (P = .9, Figure 1A) and mass-to-length ratios (P = .7, Figure 1B), even when corrected for mouse weight (supplemental Figure 1A). However, compared to F13a1+/+ or F13a1+/− mice, F13a1−/− mice had significantly smaller (58%) thrombi (Figure 1A-B), consistent with previous observations 24 hours after ligation.2,4 Given the similarity in thrombus mass in F13a1+/+ and F13a1+/− mice (Figure 1A-B), we focused subsequent analyses on the F13a1+/+ and F13a−/− genotypes. We first assessed VT structural and cellular components to determine the effect of FXIII on matrix composition and recruitment of cells to the maturing thrombus. Hematoxylin and eosin staining of VT harvested in situ (with the vessel) showed that compared to F13a1+/+ mice, VT from F13a1−/− mice occupied substantially less vessel lumen (Figure 1C-D). Analysis of isolated VT showed that compared to F13a1+/+ mice, VT from F13a1−/− mice had significantly reduced total fibrin(ogen) (P < .01, supplemental Figure 1B-D), fibrin (P = .004, Figure 1E), and fibronectin (P = .002, Figure 1F). Despite a similar number of intrathrombus Ly6G+ neutrophils (P = .2, Figure 1G), VT from F13a1−/− mice had significantly reduced citrullinated histone 3, a marker of neutrophil extracellular traps (P = .004, Figure 1H) and histone H3 (P = .05; supplemental Figure 1E), indicating reduced NETosis. A recent report detected FXIII-A in neutrophils and showed that upon externalization, FXIII-A is colocalized with NET markers during NETosis.20 Whether FXIII-A is required for NETosis or modifies the phenotype of local neutrophils (eg, procoagulant vs prothrombolytic state21) is unclear. Compared to F13a1+/+ mice, F13a1−/− mice had significantly reduced numbers of both prohealing (CD206+) monocytes (P = .01, Figure 1I; supplemental Figure 2A-B) and proinflammatory (CCR2+) monocytes (P = .03, Figure 1J; supplemental Figure 2C-D). These data implicate FXIII in generalized mechanisms mediating the response of monocytes/macrophages to VT. To determine the effect of FXIII on thrombus neovascularization, we measured markers of early (laminin)22 and late (CD31+ cells)23 neovascular channel formation. Compared to F13a1+/+ mice, VT from F13a1−/− mice had significantly reduced laminin (P = .002, Figure 1K) and fewer neovascular channels (P < .0001; Figure 1L; supplemental Figure 2E-F). Together, these findings suggest FXIII-dependent changes in VT formation and/or the cellular response to VT may promote neovascularization within the resolving thrombus.24,25

We also determined the impact of reduced thrombus burden and intraluminal inflammatory cells on postthrombotic vein wall injury. Compared to F13a1+/+ mice, the vein media of thrombosed inferior vena cava from F13a1−/− mice had significantly reduced numbers of prohealing (CD206+) monocytes (P = .0003; Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 2A-B), but similar numbers of CCR2+ cells (P = .4; Figure 2B) and fibroblasts (DDR2+ cells, P = .4; Figure 2C), and few Ly6G+ cells in the vein wall (not shown). Together with increased luminal endothelium staining in F13a1−/− mice (P = .003; Figure 2D-F), these findings suggest the endothelial vein intima is preserved with FXIII deficiency. We also used Masson’s trichrome staining to define vein wall matrix23,26; compared to F13a1+/+ mice, F13a1−/− mice had 2.5-fold increased vein wall thickness (P = .01; Figure 2G-I). It is currently unclear if increased wall thickness in this setting is pathologic. Given interest in FXIII as a potential therapeutic target,27 our study to define the downstream consequences of FXIII deficiency on VT evolution fills important knowledge gaps. First, the data show that decreased thrombus burden seen in F13a1−/− mice at early time points2,4 persists during VT resolution. Second, the data reveal events that occur following VT formation that are dependent on FXIII(s) activity, advancing understanding of these mechanisms and their consequences. In particular, the data expose a relationship between FXIII and vascular inflammatory processes, wherein loss of FXIII reduces thrombus neovascularization and recruitment of proinflammatory CCR2+ monocytes to the VT. Third, the findings associate loss of FXIII with improved endothelization of the perithrombus vessel lumen. Collectively, these findings suggest FXIII inhibition may blunt the innate immune response to VT formation, with complex implications for vein wall injury. It is currently unclear whether the vascular changes are solely due to the loss of plasma FXIII which reduces fibrin crosslinking and red blood cell retention in VT,4 or are (also) due to the loss of cellular FXIII. Since the active subunits of both plasma and platelet FXIII (FXIII-A2) are synthesized in hematopoietic cells,28 attempts to individually alter these FXIII compartments through genetic manipulation are challenging, especially for long-term studies.4,29 Studies with F13b−/− mice which would deplete plasma FXIII-A2B2 but preserve cellular FXIII-A2 may help define the operant compartment; efforts to develop and characterize F13b−/−mice are ongoing (J. Luyendyk, personal communication, December 2025). In the absence of FXIII, we lacked the established crosslinked fibrin cleavage product (eg, D-dimer) to characterize thrombus degradation. Although we attempted to characterize longer time points (14 days), we observed significant mortality (7 of 12 F13a1−/−mice) secondary to bleeding and did not pursue studies at this time point. This bleeding is consistent with clinical observations in humans,4 and suggests complete FXIII inhibition is unwise, particularly in settings where FXIII deficiency has been reported to be associated with bleeding (eg, postpartum hemorrhage).30 Moreover, complete FXIII deficiency has been associated with pulmonary embolism in mice.31,32 However, the data herein provide intriguing evidence that FXIII not only mediates initial thrombus mass and composition,2,3 but also modulates thrombus matrix and the immune response, with implications for resolution and vein wall injury. These findings motivate further studies to define the operant mechanisms.
