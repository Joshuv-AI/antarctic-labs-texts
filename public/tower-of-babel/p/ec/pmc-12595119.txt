MoringaLeafNet: A multi-class leaf disease dataset for precision agriculture and deep learning research
Authors: Sabit Ahamed Preanto, Tapon Paul, Abid Khan, Md Hasan Imam Bijoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoringa Oleifera, which has outstanding nutritional and health benefits, is prized around the world because its leaves are rich in essential vitamins, antioxidants, and minerals that support digestion, help the immune system, and fight inflammation. Still, growing Moringa can be difficult because diseases such as Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot are hard to detect early and spread fast, leading to a lot of damage. These illnesses cause plants to make less yield, so farmers depend on pesticides and spend more, which also damages the environment and their crops. Here, we make available the MoringaLeafNet dataset, including high-quality images of leaves from the Moringa tree affected by different diseases. The images in the dataset, gathered from March to April and August to September 2025, are divided into four classes: Healthy Leaf, Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot. We collected images from Sumi Nursery in Madhupur, Tangail, Bangladesh, and Rafin Nursery in Birulia, Savar, Bangladesh, under various weather conditions. To facilitate better use in deep learning, random rotation, flipping, and brightness/contrast adjustments were performed on the data. The dataset will help develop new disease detection systems in agriculture that allow the early recognition of Moringa leaf diseases. It can also support the development of real-time diagnostic systems that provide farmers with timely insights for decision-making.Keywords: Moringa leaf disease, Computer vision in agriculture, Leaf classification, Detecting leaf disease, Smart Agriculture, Sustainable Agriculture

Background

Moringa Oleifera, also known as the drumstick tree, is an indispensable crop due to its high medicinal and nutritional value. The leaves contain high amounts of essential nutrients in the form of vitamins A, C, and E, and minerals such as calcium, potassium, and iron. The leaves of Moringa are also packed with good antioxidants, anti-inflammatory compounds, and antimicrobial activities, thus emerging as a pivotal agent for preventing malnutrition and immune function enhancement [1]. Due to its high nutritional value and adaptability to various climates, Moringa is currently cultivated in major parts of the globe, especially in developing countries like Bangladesh [2].

However, the productivity of Moringa farming is threatened by various leaf diseases such as Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot. These tend to develop very fast, particularly in hot and humid climates and can cause extensive yield and leaf quality reduction. Such types of diseases need to be identified at the initial stages and controlled to ensure sustainable production and minimize excessive use of pesticides, which are harmful to the environment and human health.

To solve this problem, the MoringaLeafNet dataset was developed to help researchers and developers construct computer vision-based smart plant disease detection systems. The dataset is constituted of high-resolution images of the Moringa leaves taken in real-world agricultural farms in Bangladesh. The dataset contains healthy as well as diseased leaves at multiple stages of growth under varying light conditions and environmental conditions, thus making the dataset more robust and usable.

This dataset is a great resource to train and validate machine learning and deep learning models for image categorization, feature detection, and precision farming. MoringaLeafNet enables the overall objectives of food security, sustainable agriculture, and resource-friendly management of diseases with early detection of disease and smart farming solutions.

Data Description

Moringa oleifera, its leaves are eaten as a vegetable, utilized in traditional medicine, and are becoming more commercialized due to their medicinal values. Moringa plants, nevertheless, have numerous leaf diseases that lower leaf quality and marketability. Diseases are not easily noticed during their early stages, and thus, early intervention is not easy, and thereby, pesticide application is heavy [3]. Therefore, the MoringaLeafNet dataset was created to facilitate the development of computer vision-supported intelligent systems for monitoring plant health.

The dataset contains 2817 sharp images of Moringa leaves taken at Sumi Nursery, Madhupur, Tangail, Bangladesh and Rafin Nursery, Birulia, Savar, Bangladesh. Photos were taken under natural light and varying environmental conditions—including weather, light levels, and leaf development stages to capture diversity and ensure field usefulness. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Experimental design

The MoringaLeafNet dataset has been developed to support research on the diagnosis of Moringa oleifera plant diseases using computer vision techniques. The image collection took place from March 2025 to April 2025 at Sumi Nursery, located in Madhupur, Tangail, Bangladesh and August 2025 to September 2025 at Rafin Nursery, located in Birulia, Savar, Bangladesh. Photographs were captured under natural lighting and varying environmental conditions to reflect typical fluctuations in a farm setting.

Images were captured using two mobile phones—iQOO Z9 Turbo and OnePlus 8T—to ensure high resolution and suitability for field conditions. The photographs enable detailed analysis of leaf texture, structure, and discoloration patterns. The dataset contains four classes: Healthy Leaf, Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot.

Leaf classification with image pre-processing & data augmentation

We began building the dataset by consulting domain experts to identify the major disease types affecting Moringa oleifera leaves. Field visits were conducted at a nursery site in Madhupur, Tangail and Birulia, Savar, where leaves were harvested directly from trees and sorted into four categories based on visible symptoms. High-resolution images were then captured using smartphones (iQOO Z9 Turbo and OnePlus 8T) to ensure sharp and detailed visuals.

After acquisition, the dataset was processed in multiple stages. The raw images with natural field backgrounds were retained to preserve realism. For standardization, we also generated a pre-processed version in which leaf backgrounds were removed and replaced with a uniform white background, minimizing background variability and allowing models to focus on disease-related features. To further enhance class balance and variability, a third version was created using data augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, and brightness adjustment. Additionally, a fourth version was created by including metadata in a CSV file, which contains details such as Image Name, Class Name, Date, Location, Temperature, Weather, Device for each image. This metadata enhances the dataset’s utility by providing context for the images, which can be valuable for research and model evaluation.

Fig. 4 presents representative examples of the preprocessing and augmentation pipeline applied to the four Moringa leaf classes (Healthy, Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot). The preprocessing stage involved resizing images to 3000 × 3000 pixels, removing the background, and placing the leaves on a uniform white background to ensure consistency. To address class imbalance and enrich dataset diversity, augmentation techniques were applied using the Python Imaging Library (PIL). These included random rotations (–30° to +30°) with bicubic resampling, horizontal and vertical flipping (50 % probability each), and random adjustments of brightness and contrast within the range of 0.8–1.2. After each transformation, images were cropped back to 3000 × 3000 pixels to maintain uniformity. For each raw image, multiple augmented variants were generated, increasing dataset variability while preserving visible disease symptoms and natural leaf characteristics.

Background removal method

The preprocessing of images in the MoringaLeafNet dataset involved several steps to ensure that the dataset was standardized and suitable for machine learning tasks, particularly in terms of handling leaf disease detection. These steps include image resizing, background removal, and the addition of a white background.

1.Resizing: Each image was resized to a consistent dimension of 3000 × 3000 pixels to ensure uniformity across the dataset. This step allows the dataset to be used in deep learning models that require fixed-size inputs, ensuring that all images have the same resolution and aspect ratio.

Resizing: Each image was resized to a consistent dimension of 3000 × 3000 pixels to ensure uniformity across the dataset. This step allows the dataset to be used in deep learning models that require fixed-size inputs, ensuring that all images have the same resolution and aspect ratio.

2.Background Removal: The background removal process was accomplished using an AI-based tool called rembg. This tool utilizes machine learning techniques to detect and remove the background from images, leaving only the object of interest—in this case, the Moringa leaves. The removal process was applied to all images to isolate the leaves from their natural backgrounds, making the dataset more relevant for model training related to leaf disease detection.

3.Adding White Background: After removing the original background, a new white background was added to each image to maintain a consistent visual environment for the leaves. This step was performed using Python's Pillow library, where the background was replaced with a solid white background, ensuring uniformity across all images in the dataset. Software and libraries used

All preprocessing and augmentation tasks were carried out in Python 3.9. Duplicate image detection and removal were implemented using the ImageHash (v4.3) library, which applies an average hashing algorithm for identifying visually identical files. Directory and file operations were managed with the built-in os and pathlib modules. Data augmentation — including random rotation, horizontal and vertical flipping, brightness adjustment, contrast adjustment was performed using the Pillow (PIL, v9.2) library. These tools ensure transparency and reproducibility of the preprocessing pipeline.

Dataset structure

The MoringaLeafNet Dataset is organized into three primary folders: Original Dataset, Pre-processed Dataset, and Augmented Dataset. The Original Dataset consists of 2817 raw images gathered from Sumi Nursery, Tangail, Bangladesh and Rafin Nursery, Birulia, Savar, Bangladesh, and they are distributed into four primary disease classes: Healthy Leaf, Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot. The Original Images folder contains raw images of leaf conditions, categorized into classes such as Healthy, Yellow, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot. The Preprocessed Images folder holds images that have been processed to remove natural backgrounds and replace them with a uniform white background, standardizing the dataset for machine learning tasks. The Augmented Images folder includes images generated from the original dataset through data augmentation techniques like rotation, flipping, and brightness or contrast adjustments. The structured organization and consistent naming conventions across the dataset make it well-suited for machine learning tasks like training, validation, and model evaluation.

Data annotation protocol

The data annotation and labeling process was carried out by an expert agronomist, Professor Dr. M. A. Rahim, Head of the Department of Agricultural Science at Daffodil International University (DIU), Dhaka, Bangladesh, who possesses extensive expertise in plant disease diagnosis and classification. The process involved three key steps:

1.Initial Screening – Each image was carefully reviewed to ensure sharpness, clarity, and adequate visibility of disease symptoms. Images affected by motion blur, poor focus, excessive glare, or very low contrast were excluded from the dataset.

To further assess consistency, the expert agronomist re-annotated a subset of 300 images (≈10.6 % of the dataset). Agreement across repeated labeling was 92 % (Cohen’s κ = 0.87), demonstrating strong reproducibility and reliability of the labeling process. This rigorous approach ensures the creation of a high-quality dataset that is well-suited for machine learning-based plant disease detection and classification tasks.

Limitations

While the MoringaLeafNet dataset provides a valuable benchmark for computer vision in agriculture, it has several important limitations.

Device Variability: All images were collected using two smartphones (iQOO Z9 Turbo and OnePlus 8T). Although these devices provided high-resolution outputs, their consumer-grade sensors may introduce variability in sharpness, colour balance, and exposure compared to professional imaging equipment. This could influence how well-trained models generalize when applied to images captured with different devices.

Synthetic Augmentation: The dataset relies partly on synthetic augmentation techniques (such as flipping, rotation, and brightness adjustment) to increase its size and reduce class imbalance. While these techniques improve class distribution and support model training, they cannot fully replicate natural variability in field conditions. Augmentation may introduce artificial artifacts or emphasize features not truly representative of disease symptoms, which could bias downstream machine learning models.

Limited Disease Categories: The dataset includes only four leaf categories: Healthy Leaf, Yellow Leaf, Bacterial Leaf Spot, and Cercospora Leaf Spot. Other potential stressors, such as nutrient deficiencies, additional fungal or viral diseases, and abiotic damage, are not represented. This limits the scope of diagnostic models trained solely on this dataset.

Generalizability Beyond Preprocessed Images: Users are advised to validate models on the raw (non-preprocessed) dataset as well, to ensure the generalizability of the model beyond the preprocessed images and to better account for natural variability in field conditions.

These limitations emphasize the need for continued expansion of the MoringaLeafNet dataset, incorporating additional diseases, regions, seasons, and imaging conditions. Future work should aim to integrate naturally diverse samples alongside augmentation to provide a more robust and generalizable benchmark for precision agriculture research.
