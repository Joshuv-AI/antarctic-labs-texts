Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women worldwide, accounting for approximately one in four (24.5%) of all new cancer cases in females. In 2022, an estimated 2.3 million women were newly diagnosed with breast cancer globally, making it the leading cause of cancer incidence among women. According to recent data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), breast cancer also contributed to nearly 670,000 deaths in the same year, highlighting a significant global burden. Incidence rates continue to rise in both high- and low-income countries, with the sharpest increases observed in regions undergoing rapid socioeconomic transitions. Notably, women in less developed regions, with a total of 883,000 cases, show slightly higher incidence compared to more developed regions, which account for 794,000 cases. In India, despite a lower age-adjusted incidence rate of breast cancer (25.8 per 100,000) compared to the United Kingdom (95 per 100,000), the mortality rates are relatively comparable (12.7 vs. 17.1 per 100,000) [1–11].

Immune responses play a significant role in determining the prognosis and treatment efficacy of breast cancer. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are particularly important, with subsets like CD8-positive T cells exhibiting anti-tumor effects, while regulatory T cells (Tregs), marked by forkhead box protein 3 (FOXP3) expression, suppress immune activity. The host immune response to breast cancer is thought to be influenced by the interplay between these cell types. Thus, prognosis cannot be predicted by TIL levels alone; the relative balance between CD8+ and FOXP3+ T cells is also critical [12, 13].

Regulatory T cells (Tregs), a subset of CD4+ T lymphocytes, maintain immune tolerance by suppressing self-reactive T cells, thereby presenting a challenge to anti-tumor immunity. FOXP3 is a key transcription factor essential for Treg development and function. These cells exert immunosuppressive effects on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells, B cells, and macrophages through various mechanisms, including the secretion of cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β1), IL-2 consumption, and direct cytotoxicity via perforin and granzyme. Elevated infiltration of FOXP3+ Tregs is associated with poor cancer outcomes, underscoring their role in immune evasion and tumor progression [14].

The expression and prognostic role of CD8+ and FOXP3+ T cells in breast carcinoma, particularly within the Indian population, remain underexplored. Hence, this study was undertaken.

Immunohistochemistry procedure and reagents

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks of invasive ductal carcinoma were selected, and 3–4 µm thick sections were cut using a rotary microtome. The sections were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated glass slides and incubated at 60 °C for one hour to ensure proper adhesion. Slides were then deparaffinized in two changes of xylene for 10 min each, followed by rehydration through a graded series of ethanol (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%) for 5 min each and rinsed in distilled water.

Antigen retrieval was performed by placing the slides in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heating them in a pressure cooker at full pressure for 15 min to unmask the epitopes. The slides were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 5 min.

To block endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min. After washing in PBS, nonspecific background staining was reduced by incubating the sections in normal serum (5% goat serum) for 20 min at room temperature.

Primary antibody incubation

FOXP3: Sections were incubated with a monoclonal antibody against FOXP3 (Clone: 236A/E7, Isotype: IgG1, from Biocare Medical) at a dilution of 1:50. The target localization was nuclear, and colon cancer and melanoma tissues were used as positive controls.

FOXP3: Sections were incubated with a monoclonal antibody against FOXP3 (Clone: 236A/E7, Isotype: IgG1, from Biocare Medical) at a dilution of 1:50. The target localization was nuclear, and colon cancer and melanoma tissues were used as positive controls.

CD8: Adjacent sections were incubated with a monoclonal antibody against CD8 (Clone: SP16, Isotype: IgG, from Biocare Medical) at a dilution of 1:100. CD8 showed cell surface (membranous) localization, and tonsil tissue served as the positive control.

CD8: Adjacent sections were incubated with a monoclonal antibody against CD8 (Clone: SP16, Isotype: IgG, from Biocare Medical) at a dilution of 1:100. CD8 showed cell surface (membranous) localization, and tonsil tissue served as the positive control.

Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber to ensure optimal antigen–antibody binding.

After washing with PBS, the slides were incubated with secondary HRP-conjugated anti-mouse/rabbit IgG antibody (Biocare Medical) for 30 min at room temperature. After PBS washes, immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate for 5–10 min until brown chromogenic precipitate developed.

Sections were then counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 1 min, rinsed in tap water, dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted using DPX mounting medium.

Microscopic evaluation

The stained slides were examined under an Olympus CX23 bright-field microscope using 100 × and 400 × magnification. Evaluation of immunohistochemical expression was independently performed by two experienced pathologists blinded to the clinical data to avoid bias. Discrepant cases were reviewed jointly and resolved by consensus.

Assessment of immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining for CD8 was considered positive when lymphocytes showed evident membranous immunoreactions. Immunohistochemical staining for Foxp3 was considered positive when lymphocytes showed evident nuclear immunostaining.

In our study, the general assessment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) was initially performed on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections, following the recommendations of the International TILs Working Group. This included evaluation of stromal TILs (sTILs), which represent the lymphocytic infiltrate present in the tumor stroma rather than within tumor nests. Only stromal TILs were scored, as they are considered more reproducible and clinically relevant.Subsequently, to further characterize the immune infiltrate, we performed immunohistochemical staining for CD8 (cytotoxic T cells) and FOXP3 (regulatory T cells). These were specifically used to identify and quantify subsets of T cells within the TIL population, allowing us to evaluate their density and distribution in relation to clinicopathological parameters.

The Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI) was calculated using the standard formula: NPI = [0.2 × tumor size (cm)] + lymph node stage (1–3) + MBR grade (1–3). Based on the score, patients were grouped as follows:

Good prognosis: NPI ≤ 3.4

Moderate prognosis: NPI 3.41–5.4

Poor prognosis: NPI > 5.4

Poor prognosis: NPI > 5.4

Data regarding estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), HER2neu, and Ki-67 status were obtained through a combination of retrospective and prospective methods. In a subset of cases, the results were retrieved from existing patient pathology records. For the remaining cases where this information was not previously available, immunohistochemical staining was performed as part of the current study using standard protocols.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using Microsoft Excel and SPSS version 22. Categorical data were shown as frequencies and proportions, with chi-square or Fischer’s exact test for significance. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, using t-tests and ANOVA for comparisons. Pearson Correlation coefficient was used for correlations. Graphs were created with MS Excel and Word. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The ratio also differed significantly across Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI) groups (p = 0.016). Poor-prognosis cases displayed a markedly higher mean ratio (0.708 ± 0.793) than both moderate (0.1503 ± 0.1934) and good (0.1711 ± 0.2152) prognosis groups, indicating that a strongly immunosuppressive micro-environment accompanies more adverse NPI scores.

For Modified Bloom–Richardson (MBR) grade, an overall difference was observed (p = 0.019); however, the distribution did not follow a simple monotonic trend. Grade I tumours showed the highest mean ratio (0.1436 ± 0.1727), while Grades II and III were lower and virtually identical (0.1114 ± 0.1017 and 0.1128 ± 0.1386, respectively). This finding highlights biological heterogeneity within histological grading: although immunosuppression increases with clinical stage and NPI, it does not necessarily rise in parallel with morphological grade, underscoring the complexity of host–tumour immune interactions.

Discussion

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), and CD8+ T cells are pivotal in the immune response against breast cancer, each playing distinct roles within the tumor microenvironment. TILs, including CD8+ T cells, migrate to the tumor and are associated with a favorable prognosis by actively targeting and suppressing cancer cells, thereby potentially slowing tumor growth. Conversely, elevated levels of FOXP3+ Tregs often correlate with poorer outcomes due to their role in immune suppression, which may promote tumor progression and metastasis by dampening anti-tumor immune responses [16–18].

Our study extensively investigated the immunohistochemical expression of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (TILs) and FOXP3+ regulatory T cells within the tumor microenvironment of invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) of the breast. By analyzing these TIL subsets in a cohort of 96 patients, we aimed to unravel their roles and correlations with various clinicopathological parameters, offering deeper insights into their impact on breast cancer progression and immune response.

CD8 + TILs and overall TIL levels

Our study also revealed a significant direct correlation between CD8+ TILs and overall TIL levels, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.419 (p < 0.001). This positive correlation underscores the role of CD8+ TILs in contributing to a robust immune presence within the tumor. Higher levels of CD8+ TILs are associated with increased overall TIL counts, suggesting that these cytotoxic cells play a key role in promoting a more active immune environment. This observation aligns with the findings of Peng et al., who reported that elevated CD8+ TIL levels correlate with a more effective immune response and potentially better clinical outcomes in hormone receptor-positive breast cancers. Similarly, Reiko Fuku et al. noted that increased CD8+ TILs are indicative of a more favorable immune environment across various breast cancer subtypes [19, 20].

Correlation of CD8 + and FOXP3 + TILs

A noteworthy finding from our analysis was the significant inverse correlation between CD8+ TILs and FOXP3+ TILs, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of − 0.508 (p = 0.002). This inverse relationship suggests that higher levels of CD8+ TILs are often accompanied by lower levels of FOXP3+ TILs. This dynamic interplay indicates that an increase in cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which are crucial for targeting and destroying cancer cells, may correspond with a reduction in regulatory T cells, which are known to suppress immune responses. This finding highlights the potential for enhanced anti-tumor immunity when CD8+ TILs are predominant. This observation aligns with Peng et al., who reported that there is an inverse correlation between CD8 and FOXP3. [14].

Clinical relevance and comparative insights

The significant correlations observed in our study underscore the critical roles that CD8+ and FOXP3+ TILs play in shaping the immune landscape of IDC. Elevated levels of CD8+ TILs, associated with increased overall TILs, suggest a favorable immune response, corroborating findings from Peng et al. and Reiko Fuku et al. Conversely, the strong direct correlation between FOXP3+ TILs and the FOXP3/CD8 ratio highlights the immunosuppressive impact of regulatory T cells. This aligns with the observations by Reiko Fuku et al. and others, emphasizing the adverse prognostic implications of increased FOXP3+ TIL infiltration in breast cancer [13, 14].

This study, conducted in an Indian population, reveals significant correlations between CD8 and FOXP3 levels and various clinicopathological parameters, emphasizing their potential as biomarkers for refining prognostic models and guiding personalized immunotherapeutic approaches. Such insights enhance our understanding of breast cancer biology and support the development of targeted therapies that leverage the immune system’s capabilities to combat cancer.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), particularly the CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), in the immune microenvironment of invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) of the breast. The evaluation of these immune cell subsets and their ratios provides meaningful prognostic information and enhances our understanding of tumor-immune dynamics.

Our findings revealed a significant inverse correlation between CD8+ and FOXP3+ TILs, suggesting that a cytotoxic-dominant immune response (higher CD8, lower FOXP3) may favor anti-tumor immunity. Additionally, CD8+ TILs showed a strong positive correlation with overall TIL levels, indicating their central contribution to a robust immune presence in the tumor microenvironment.

Importantly, the FOXP3/CD8 ratio—a composite marker reflecting the balance between immunosuppression and cytotoxic immunity—was found to be significantly associated with tumor stage (p = 0.034), Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI) (p = 0.012), and MBR Grade (p = 0.048). These correlations suggest that a higher FOXP3/CD8 ratio, indicative of an immunosuppressive profile, tends to be associated with more aggressive tumor features and poorer prognosis.

A point of clarification: while the text suggests that higher-grade tumors exhibit a more immunosuppressive TIL profile (i.e., higher FOXP3/CD8 ratio), Table 4 shows a slightly higher ratio in MBR Grade II than in Grade III. This inconsistency may reflect biological variability or sample-specific differences, and warrants further investigation in larger cohorts to establish a consistent trend.

Lastly, we acknowledge that TILs are not a homogeneous population—CD8+ cells generally exert tumor-suppressive effects, whereas FOXP3+ cells promote immune tolerance and tumor progression. This nuanced view is essential when interpreting immune infiltration in breast cancer and designing future immunomodulatory strategies.

In conclusion, assessment of CD8+ and FOXP3+ TILs, along with their ratio, offers valuable insights into the immunobiology of IDC and holds promise for enhancing prognostic precision and informing immunotherapy decisions in breast cancer patients.
