Thickness-dependent biomechanical accuracy of additively manufactured and thermoformed orthodontic aligners: an in vitro comparative study
Authors: Ovilya Hassan Omer, Zhwan Jamal Rashid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdditive manufacturing has introduced directly printed orthodontic aligners as an alternative to thermoforming. However, comparative data regarding thickness-dependent biomechanical accuracy remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the influence of fabrication method (thermoforming vs. direct 3D printing) and material thickness (0.76 mm vs. 1.0 mm) on the accuracy of rotational, tipping, and torque movements of different teeth under standardized in vitro conditions.MethodsThirty-six standardized typodonts underwent digital staging (30° rotation, 10° mesial tipping, 10° positive torque). Aligners were fabricated via thermoforming (Zendura A) or direct 3D printing (Tera Harz TC-85). Movement was simulated using thermal activation (56 °C, 90 s). Pre- and post-movement scans were superimposed (Geomagic Control X), and accuracy was calculated as the percentage of achieved to programmed movement. Analyses were performed at both typodont and tooth levels. Statistical analysis was conducted using Jamovi (version 2.6.44), applying two-way ANOVA and Mann–Whitney U tests (p < 0.05).ResultsAligner thickness significantly improved accuracy across all movement types (p < 0.001). In contrast, fabrication method showed inconsistent effects, with only isolated significant differences. Mesial tipping demonstrated the highest predictability (up to 92.67%), whereas torque showed the lowest (maximum 71.67%). Accuracy was generally higher in anterior teeth, with greater variability in posterior segments. No significant arch differences were observed.ConclusionsAligner thickness is the primary determinant of biomechanical accuracy, with thicker aligners providing more predictable tooth movement. Fabrication method showed a secondary and variable influence, indicating that material thickness plays a more critical role in optimizing aligner biomechanics.

Introduction

Clear aligner therapy (CAT) has evolved into a digitally driven orthodontic modality supported by intraoral scanning, computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM), and increasingly, additive manufacturing technologies [1]. The demand for aesthetic and removable orthodontic solutions has accelerated its adoption; however, the biomechanical predictability of aligner-mediated tooth movement remains inferior to that achieved with fixed appliances, particularly for rotational, torque, and translational movements [2].

A growing body of clinical and in vitro evidence demonstrates measurable discrepancies between digitally prescribed and clinically achieved tooth movements, with accuracy strongly dependent on movement type and anatomical morphology [3–5]. Rotational corrections frequently achieve less than 50% of planned displacement, while torque expression remains biomechanically constrained by the limited moment-generation capacity and elastic recovery characteristics of thin thermoplastic polymers [6–8]. Thermoforming—the conventional fabrication method for aligners—has been shown to induce heterogeneous thickness attenuation ranging from approximately 20% to 50% in high-curvature regions, resulting in reduced flexural rigidity and compromised root control [9, 10]. Reported rotational discrepancies average 2–4°, with maxillary canines and premolars demonstrating the greatest deviations, likely due to complex crown morphology and reduced aligner engagement surfaces [5, 11].

From a materials engineering perspective, aligner biomechanics are intrinsically governed by polymer composition, thickness-dependent stiffness, viscoelastic behavior, and processing-induced structural variability. Conventional thermoformed aligners are fabricated by vacuum or pressure forming thermoplastic sheets over printed dental models, a process that may introduce non-uniform thickness distribution, internal stresses, and dimensional inaccuracies. Thermal exposure during forming can further alter mechanical properties, contributing to force decay and reduced consistency of moment delivery [12]. Although auxiliary attachment geometries partially compensate for these limitations—rectangular beveled designs improving rotational expression by approximately 15–25%—substantial variability persists, particularly in posterior teeth where geometric complexity and reduced surface contact diminish force transmission efficiency [13].

Direct additive manufacturing of aligners has emerged as a potential solution to these processing-related limitations. By eliminating the thermoforming step, 3D printing enables layer-by-layer fabrication with precise digital control of geometry, thickness, and internal adaptation. Advances in biocompatible photopolymer resins have facilitated the development of directly printed aligners with improved mechanical consistency and potentially enhanced torque delivery [14, 15]. Nevertheless, existing studies have largely evaluated thermoformed and printed aligners independently, with limited systematic comparison across multiple material thicknesses and standardized angular movements. Moreover, few investigations have stratified outcomes according to tooth type (anterior vs. posterior) or arch location, leaving a critical gap in thickness-dependent biomechanical characterization [9, 10].

Accordingly, the present in vitro study aimed to compare the angular accuracy of orthodontic tooth movement achieved with thermoformed (Zendura A) and directly 3D-printed (Tera Harz TC-85) clear aligners at two clinically relevant thicknesses (0.76 mm and 1.0 mm). Standardized movements—30° rotation, 10° mesial tipping, and 10° positive torque—were evaluated using three-dimensional superimposition analysis. By isolating fabrication method and material thickness under controlled conditions, this study provides quantitative insight into processing-dependent biomechanical fidelity in thin-film biomedical polymer devices, with implications for material optimization and precision orthodontic planning.

Methods

Study design and experimental allocation

This laboratory-based experimental study evaluated the effects of fabrication method, aligner thickness, and programmed tooth movement on the biomechanical accuracy of orthodontic aligners under standardized in vitro conditions. The experimental design followed a 2 × 2 × 3 factorial structure, with the following independent variables: fabrication method (thermoformed vs. directly 3D-printed), thickness (0.76 mm vs. 1.00 mm), and movement type (30° rotation, 10° mesial tipping, and 10° positive torque).

A total of 36 typodont specimens were included. Specimens were distributed equally across the four-fabrication method–thickness combinations (n = 9 per combination), Thermoformed 0.76 mm, thermoformed 1.00 mm, printed 0.76 mm, and printed 1.00 mm. And within each combination, three specimens were assigned to each programmed movement type (n = 3 per movement condition). Thus, movement type was treated as a separate experimental factor rather than a repeated measure within the same specimen. The experimental distribution is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Controlled variables included typodont geometry, attachment configuration, digital scanning protocol, thermal activation parameters, and measurement methodology.

Sample size determination

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4) based on detection of large between-group effects under controlled laboratory conditions. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.50), α = 0.05, and desired power of 0.80, the ideal sample size for a fully powered multifactorial design would exceed the number of specimens that were feasible within the material and laboratory constraints of the present study. Therefore, 36 typodont specimens were included as a pragmatic sample, corresponding to 3 specimens for each movement condition within each fabrication method–thickness combination.

Given the limited number of replicates per subgroup, the study should be considered exploratory for subgroup and tooth-level comparisons. While the standardized in vitro model reduces biological variability and supports detection of major mechanical trends, the statistical power for smaller effects and detailed interaction analyses remained limited.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Each typodont was evaluated according to the American Board of Orthodontics Objective Grading System (ABO-OGS). Inclusion thresholds were defined as ≤ 0.5 mm for linear discrepancies and ≤ 2° for angular deviations. Typodonts exhibiting fractures, voids, surface defects, scanning artifacts, or dimensional inconsistencies beyond thresholds were excluded.

Examiner calibration

All angular measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner. To assess repeatability, 20% of specimens were re-measured after a two-week interval under identical conditions. Intra-examiner reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Specimen preparation and digital standardization

Thirty-six identical typodont models (Zhengzhou Smile Industrial Co., China), each comprising 28 acrylic-coated artificial teeth in maxillary and mandibular arches, were used.

A validated typodont meeting inclusion criteria was scanned using a TRIOS intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark; version 2023.04) following manufacturer-recommended protocols [16]. The resulting Standard Tessellation Language (STL) files were imported into Meshmixer software for spatial standardization. The occlusal plane was aligned parallel to the horizontal reference plane, and the midsagittal plane was established as the vertical reference axis to ensure reproducible positioning.

The standardized STL file was used to fabricate the master cast using a Phrozen Sonic Mighty 8 K liquid crystal display (LCD) 3D printer (Phrozen, Taiwan) with a layer thickness of 50 μm and an exposure time of 2 s per layer. A water-washable photopolymer resin (PioNext Washable Resin) was utilized for printing due to its favourable handling properties and reported dimensional stability in dental model fabrication. Following fabrication, the printed casts underwent post-processing consisting of rinsing in 96% ethanol for 2 min to remove residual uncured resin, followed by ultraviolet (UV) curing for 2 min in a dedicated curing unit to ensure complete polymerization, optimize mechanical properties, and minimize post-print dimensional changes.

A medical-grade addition silicone mould was produced from the validated master cast to enable reproducible typodont replication. Wax-based typodont replicas were fabricated by heating modelling wax to 60 °C to achieve a homogeneous liquid phase prior to casting into the silicone mould. Controlled cooling at 25 °C for 15 min allowed uniform solidification and dimensional stabilization before demoulding. Only defect-free specimens were retained for further experimentation (Fig. 2).

Thermoforming served as the conventional fabrication control.

Directly 3D-printed aligners

Directly printed aligners were designed using Maestro 3D Ortho Studio with uniform offsets of 0.76–1.00 mm. Fabrication was carried out using a Digital Light Processing (DLP) 3D printer (Microlay, Madrid, Spain) operating at a layer thickness of 50 μm and a light wavelength of 405 nm. A biocompatible photopolymer resin (Tera Harz™ TC85DAC, Graphy, South Korea) was used for printing. Post-processing included centrifugal removal of excess resin (1000 rpm for 6 min), followed by ultraviolet (UV) curing at 405 nm under a nitrogen-controlled atmosphere. Additional thermal conditioning was performed at 35 °C with gradual cooling to minimize internal stresses and prevent thermal distortion. Final surface polishing was undertaken to enhance aligner transparency and surface smoothness.

Nitrogen-assisted curing was employed to reduce oxygen inhibition and enhance cross-linking uniformity, improving dimensional fidelity and mechanical stability. Prior to placement, aligners were briefly immersed in 80 °C water for 5 s to facilitate initial seating and ensure complete adaptation to the typodont and attachment geometry.

This preheating step was applied consistently across all experimental groups and was intended solely to improve seating accuracy rather than to influence force delivery. Given the wax-based typodont model, tooth displacement was primarily governed by thermal softening of the wax substrate during activation rather than by continuous aligner-generated forces. Therefore, the brief preheating of the aligners is unlikely to have significantly influenced the magnitude or pattern of tooth movement across the sequential steps.

Simulated orthodontic activation

For each stage, aligners were fully seated onto typodonts ensuring complete engagement with attachments. Specimens were submerged in a temperature-controlled water bath at 56 °C for 90 s to soften the wax base and permit programmed displacement. The water level was carefully adjusted to the level of the typodont base wax, positioned just below the aligner margin.

Under these conditions, the aligners were not directly immersed in water but were instead exposed to indirect thermal effects via heat transfer from the surrounding environment and underlying wax. This approach minimizes potential confounding effects related to water absorption or hydrolytic degradation, ensuring that the material response is primarily governed by temperature exposure rather than fluid interaction. Moreover, the exposure duration was short and applied consistently across all experimental groups, thereby preserving internal consistency for comparisons between fabrication methods and thickness conditions.

Following thermal exposure, typodonts were cooled at room temperature for 15 min to re-solidify the wax and stabilize the achieved position. Reinforcement wax was applied when necessary to prevent unintended displacement during aligner exchange [18].

After completion of all stages, post-treatment digital scans were obtained using the TRIOS intraoral scanner (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark; version 2023.04), consistent with the scanning protocol used for baseline acquisition to ensure methodological standardization across all measurements. The use of an intraoral scanner was selected to replicate clinically relevant data acquisition conditions and maintain consistency with the initial scanning workflow, thereby minimizing inter-device variability in surface capture.

Three-dimensional measurement and accuracy assessment

Pre- and post-treatment STL files were imported into Geomagic Control X (3D Systems, USA) for three-dimensional analysis. Alignment was performed in two stages: an initial coarse alignment to establish approximate positioning, followed by an iterative best-fit alignment. The best-fit procedure was restricted to the full crown surfaces, which were defined as the region of interest (ROI).

The ROI included the complete clinical crown morphology and occlusal surfaces, selected to ensure alignment based on rigid and dimensionally stable structures. This approach minimizes potential artifacts associated with the wax substrate, which undergoes controlled thermal softening during activation and therefore does not provide a sufficiently stable reference for accurate superimposition. By restricting alignment to crown surfaces, the reliability and accuracy of the superimposition process were enhanced.

Quantitative outputs included root mean square deviation (RMS), mean positive and negative deviations, and color-coded deviation maps with a ± 1.0 mm scale. A tolerance threshold of ± 0.25 mm was applied to define clinically acceptable deviation limits.

Angular measurements

Rotation was quantified by constructing best-fit planes through incisal or occlusal landmarks and calculating angular deviation using the “Angle Between Two Planes” function. Tipping and torque were determined by constructing standardized long-axis lines for each tooth and projecting them onto sagittal and frontal reference planes, respectively, with angular differences measured using the “angle between two lines” function.

All angular measurements were performed using standardized landmark-based reference planes generated within Geomagic Control X (3D Systems, USA). A predefined measurement workflow was applied to ensure consistency in landmark selection, plane construction, and axis definition across all specimens. All measurements were conducted by a single calibrated examiner to reduce inter-operator variability. In addition, semi-automated software tools were utilized to enhance measurement reproducibility and minimize operator-dependent bias, particularly in posterior teeth with complex morphology.

Repeatability analysis demonstrated excellent intra-examiner reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) exceeding 0.90.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.6.44; The Jamovi Project). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of aligner thickness, fabrication method, and their interaction on movement accuracy. When assumptions of normality were not met, Mann–Whitney U tests were used for pairwise comparisons between groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by repeating 20% of the measurements after a two-week interval. Agreement was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values > 0.90 indicating excellent reliability.

Results

Sample characteristics

All 36 typodont specimens completed the full experimental protocol without structural deformation, scanning artifacts, or measurement exclusion. A total of 504 programmed angular movements were analyzed (14 teeth × 3 movement types × 2 fabrication methods × 2 thicknesses × replicates). Measurement reliability demonstrated excellent intra-examiner agreement (ICC > 0.90).

Effect of thickness

Thermoformed aligners

A statistically significant difference in accuracy was observed between thermoformed aligners of different thicknesses. The Mann–Whitney U test revealed that 1.00 mm aligners demonstrated significantly higher accuracy compared to 0.76 mm aligners (p < 0.001). The median accuracy for the 0.76 mm group was 43.3% (IQR: 37.2–61.8), whereas the 1.00 mm group showed a higher median value of 53.0% (IQR: 46.7–72.8). The magnitude of the difference was moderate to large, with a rank biserial correlation of 0.44 (Table 1).

Tooth-level patterns

Rotation

For rotational movements, two-way ANOVA analysis revealed that aligner thickness had a statistically significant effect on movement accuracy in the majority of teeth. Specifically, significant effects were observed in teeth 11, 15, 17, 24, 26, 31, 33, 35, 37, 42, and 46 (p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant effect of thickness was found in teeth 13 and 44, while tooth 22 demonstrated borderline significance.

The manufacturing method did not show a statistically significant effect in most teeth, with the exception of tooth 37 (p = 0.041), where a significant difference between fabrication techniques was observed. Borderline effects of method were noted in several teeth, including 15, 22, 26, 31, and 42.

Importantly, no statistically significant interaction between aligner thickness and manufacturing method was detected across all evaluated teeth (p > 0.05), indicating that the influence of thickness on rotational accuracy was independent of the fabrication technique.

Overall, these findings suggest that aligner thickness is the primary determinant of rotational movement accuracy, while the effect of manufacturing method is limited and tooth-specific (Table 3).

Across most teeth, aligner thickness demonstrated a statistically significant effect on mesial tipping accuracy, with thicker aligners (1.00 mm) consistently achieving higher accuracy than thinner aligners (0.76 mm). Discussion

Clear aligner therapy is widely adopted because it improves aesthetics and patient comfort; however, predictable expression of complex movements—particularly rotation and torque—remains a major limitation in both clinical and laboratory research. Reported clinical accuracies typically show higher predictability for tipping and lower predictability for rotation and torque, reflecting the greater biomechanical demands of generating couples and third-order moments with removable polymer appliances [19, 20].

In the present in vitro typodont model, aligner thickness demonstrated a consistent and statistically significant influence on movement accuracy, with the 1.00 mm aligners generally outperforming the 0.76 mm aligners across all movement types. This finding was confirmed by two-way ANOVA as the primary analytical approach, indicating that thickness represents the most reliable determinant of biomechanical performance in this study. For both thermoformed and directly printed aligners, increased thickness resulted in improved accuracy for rotation, mesial tipping, and positive torque.

From a materials/biomechanics standpoint, these findings are coherent with the expectation that thicker polymer appliances exhibit increased stiffness and improved force–moment delivery, which can improve control of movements that require higher moments (especially torque). Reviews of aligner materials and biomechanics consistently emphasize thickness as a primary determinant of aligner stiffness and force systems [21, 22].

In contrast, the effect of fabrication method (thermoforming vs. direct 3D printing) was not consistently significant across analyses, with statistical significance observed only in limited tooth-specific comparisons. Therefore, the superiority of one fabrication method over another cannot be generalized based on the current data, and the influence of fabrication technique appears to be secondary, and more variable compared to thickness.

Despite the improved biomechanical accuracy observed with 1.0 mm directly printed aligners, potential drawbacks should be considered. Increased material thickness may reduce flexibility, potentially affecting initial seating, patient comfort, and ease of insertion and removal. Higher stiffness may also result in increased force magnitude, which, if not properly controlled, could negatively influence biological response and patient tolerance in clinical settings. Furthermore, thicker aligners may exhibit reduced adaptability to complex tooth morphology, particularly in areas with pronounced undercuts, potentially compromising fit in certain regions. From an aesthetic perspective, increased thickness may also affect aligner transparency. Therefore, while greater thickness enhances mechanical performance in vitro, its clinical application should be carefully balanced against these potential limitations [21, 22].

Importantly, for thermoformed aligners, “nominal sheet thickness” is not identical to “delivered clinical thickness,” because thermoforming is known to produce non-uniform thickness distribution and local thinning. This provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for why thickness increases may be partly attenuated (and more variable) after thermoforming compared with directly printed appliances [23, 24].

Regarding tooth-level, directly printed aligners generally achieved higher mean accuracy than thermoformed aligners at both thicknesses, with the most consistent advantages observed in torque and in many anterior/premolar sites for rotation. This aligns with the broader literature suggesting that additive manufacturing can offer advantages in geometric fidelity, thickness consistency, and potentially more stable force delivery, depending on the resin system and post-curing protocol [25]. At the tooth level, anterior teeth generally demonstrated higher and more consistent accuracy compared to posterior teeth. Posterior segments, particularly molars, exhibited greater variability and less consistent statistical significance, which may be attributed to differences in crown morphology, reduced surface engagement, and more complex force systems required for these teeth. This observation is consistent with previous biomechanical and finite element studies highlighting the influence of anatomical and mechanical factors on aligner performance [22, 26, 27].

The results also demonstrated a consistent movement-related pattern, where mesial tipping exhibited the highest accuracy, followed by rotation, while torque showed the lowest predictability. This pattern reflects the increasing biomechanical complexity required to achieve these movements, with torque requiring sustained third-order moment expression and precise aligner adaptation [5].

The present study utilized a wax-based typodont model with thermal activation (56 °C) to simulate orthodontic tooth movement under controlled conditions. While this approach enables a high level of standardization and reproducibility, it does not replicate the complex biological environment of the oral cavity. In particular, the absence of the periodontal ligament (PDL) precludes its viscoelastic and damping functions, which are essential for physiological force distribution, stress absorption, and time-dependent tooth movement.

Moreover, the use of thermal activation does not reflect the continuous, low-magnitude force systems generated by aligners in vivo, where mechanical forces interact dynamically with biological tissues over time. Additional intraoral factors—including saliva, mastication, thermal fluctuations, material aging, and force decay—are not represented in this model. Consequently, the measured movement accuracy should be interpreted as a controlled mechanical approximation rather than a direct representation of clinical outcomes.

In addition, a brief pre-heating procedure was applied to facilitate aligner seating; however, the water level was maintained below the aligner margin, resulting in indirect thermal exposure rather than direct immersion. This approach minimizes the influence of water absorption or hydrolytic degradation, suggesting that the material response was predominantly temperature-driven. Nevertheless, because the activation temperature (56 °C) approaches the thermal transition range of TC-85DAC-based materials, some degree of temperature-dependent softening and alteration in stiffness cannot be entirely excluded, which may influence short-term force delivery [28].

The selected movement magnitudes (30° rotation; 10° tipping and torque) were intentionally chosen to represent clinically challenging movements with lower predictability in aligner therapy. While this standardization enables controlled comparisons across groups, it may not fully reflect the variability and staging protocols encountered in clinical practice.

Furthermore, tooth movement within the arch is inherently interdependent, as adjacent teeth, arch form, and aligner fit collectively influence force distribution and movement expression. In the present typodont model, movements were simulated under controlled and partially isolated conditions; therefore, full-arch biomechanical interactions may not be completely represented.

Finally, the relatively small number of specimens per subgroup represents a limitation of the study. Although the in vitro design reduces biological variability and allows detection of major mechanical trends, the statistical power for subgroup and interaction analyses remains limited. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, particularly for tooth-level comparisons.

Future research should incorporate more biologically relevant models, including artificial aging and thermal cycling protocols, as well as time-dependent force and moment analysis. Comparative evaluation of different resin systems and post-curing strategies for directly printed aligners is also warranted. Ultimately, prospective clinical studies using standardized three-dimensional superimposition methods are necessary to validate the translational applicability of these findings.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro investigation, aligner thickness was identified as the primary factor influencing angular movement accuracy, while the effect of fabrication method was variable and not consistently significant. Thicker aligners (1.0 mm) demonstrated improved biomechanical performance compared with thinner aligners across rotation, mesial tipping, and positive torque.

Among the evaluated movements, mesial tipping exhibited the highest predictability, followed by rotation, whereas positive torque remained the least accurate. Tooth-specific analysis revealed that anterior incisors achieved the greatest movement fidelity, while molars consistently demonstrated the lowest accuracy, likely reflecting their complex morphology and reduced aligner engagement.
