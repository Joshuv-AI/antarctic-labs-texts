Modeling grain selection for single-crystal thin-wall inconel 718 tubes by laser powder bed fusion
Authors: Hussein Zein, Waleed H El-Garaihy, Abdulrahman I Alateyah, Majed Alinizzi, Eman M Zayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdditive manufacturing using Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) offers significant potential for fabricating high-performance nickel-based superalloy components; however, achieving single-crystal architectures remains limited by competitive grain growth. This study proposes a geometry-assisted grain selection strategy inspired by directional solidification casting for thin-wall Inconel 718 tubes. A thermo-mechanical simulation framework based on Simufact Additive is employed to evaluate a parametric design space of spiral grain selectors comprising a starter block and a helical section.Seventy geometric configurations are analyzed using macro-scale thermal indicators, including axial thermal gradient (G), temperature rate (|dT/dt|), a solidification-rate proxy (R), the G/R ratio, and residual stress distributions. The predicted thermal gradients and stress levels are consistent with experimentally validated LPBF literature, confirming the reliability of the simulation framework. The results show that four-turn spiral selectors with intermediate take-off angles (15°-20°) and small wire diameters provide optimal thermo-mechanical conditions, characterized by stable thermal gradients, favorable G/R ratios, and moderate residual stresses. In contrast, two-turn configurations fail to achieve sufficient thermal confinement. This study establishes a simulation-driven methodology for identifying geometries that promote single-grain survival in LPBF thin-wall superalloy components.Keywords: Thermo-mechanical modeling, Grain-selection preconditions, LPBF, Inconel 718, Spiral selector design, G/R ratioSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a transformative technology for producing complex metallic components, offering unprecedented design freedom, near-net-shape fabrication, and performance optimization beyond conventional processing routes. Among AM technologies, LPBF has become one of the most extensively investigated techniques for high-value alloys due to its ability to achieve fine geometric resolution and near-full density in intricate parts. Nickel-based Superalloys such as Inconel 718 are of particular industrial relevance owing to their superior high-temperature mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and widespread use in aerospace and energy sectors. Despite these advantages, the transition of LPBF from prototyping to safety–critical components remains constrained by limited confidence in microstructural reproducibility, defect formation, and residual stress accumulation during processing1. The microstructure that develops during LPBF is governed by the complex interplay between rapid thermal cycling, steep temperature gradients (G), and solidification rate (R) within the melt pool. Unlike conventional casting or directional solidification routes, LPBF operates at extremely high temperature rates strongly influence grain morphology, texture evolution, and solute redistribution2.

Importantly, grain morphology in conventional LPBF is not inherently equiaxed. Rather, it depends on local solidification conditions characterized by the G/R ratio, alloy chemistry, and melt pool dynamics. Under steep thermal gradients and directional heat extraction, epitaxial columnar grains commonly grow along the build direction. Conversely, reduced thermal gradients or enhanced heterogeneous nucleation at melt pool boundaries may promote equiaxed or mixed grain structures. Therefore, LPBF inherently provides conditions favorable for columnar growth; however, persistent competitive grain growth and stray nucleation events prevent the continuous survival of a single crystallographic orientation throughout the build3. The realization of single-crystal architectures in LPBF thus requires suppression of competitive grain growth rather than merely promoting columnar structures. This challenge is particularly critical for thin-wall tubular geometries, such as turbine cooling channels and advanced heat exchangers, where directional mechanical performance and creep resistance are essential. Achieving stable single-grain continuity demands not only control of thermal gradients but also strategies capable of progressively eliminating misoriented grains.

Figure 1 illustrates the conceptual evolution of grain control strategies in LPBF toward single-crystal thin-wall tube fabrication. Early efforts focused on scan-strategy optimization and thermal-gradient management to enhance columnar growth and texture alignment. While these approaches improved epitaxial continuity, grain competition persisted. The present study introduces a geometry-assisted grain selection strategy inspired by directional solidification casting, employing a starter block and spiral selector concept to progressively eliminate misoriented grains and promote single-grain survival within thin-wall LPBF structures4.

Grain control in LPBF progression.

Building on these foundations, the present study investigates the application of SIMUFACT Additive for simulating grain selection mechanisms in LPBF of Inconel 718 thin-wall tubes. By parametrically analyzing the influence of process parameters on thermal gradients and solidification conditions, this work seeks to establish computational strategies to favor epitaxial growth and minimize competitive grain selection, thereby moving closer to the realization of single-crystal AM components. Through comparison with prior high-fidelity models and experimental observations in the literature, this study contributes to bridging the gap between academic modeling of LPBF and practical, simulation-driven microstructure control for critical components5. The simulation of LPBF processes has been approached through a hierarchy of models ranging from continuum-scale finite element methods (FEM) to mesoscale cellular automaton and phase-field simulations. Continuum FEM tools have been widely employed to predict melt pool dimensions, thermal histories, and residual stresses under varying laser parameters. Roberts et al. (2009) demonstrated the utility of FEM for tracking transient temperature fields in three-dimensional powder beds, emphasizing that mesh refinement down to the melt pool radius is essential for convergence5. Heigel et al. (2015) extended this methodology to large-scale thermo-mechanical simulations, capturing residual stress accumulation and distortion in titanium and nickel alloys6.

More advanced phase-field and cellular automaton models have been developed to simulate dendritic growth kinetics and the columnar-to-equiaxed transition (CET) under LPBF conditions7. For Ni-based alloys, these models reveal that steep thermal gradients favor epitaxial columnar growth aligned with the build direction, whereas higher cooling rates combined with reduced gradients promote equiaxed nucleation8. These findings reinforce that achieving single-crystal continuity requires not simply enhancing columnar growth, but suppressing competitive grain survival during solidification9. Despite the capabilities of high-fidelity multiphysics platforms such as DIABLO and ALE3D, their computational cost limits broader industrial adoption10. In contrast, commercial tools such as SIMUFACT Additive provide a practical balance between computational efficiency and physical fidelity. Using an inherent strain or thermo-mechanical FEM framework, SIMUFACT enables prediction of layer-wise thermal histories, distortion, and residual stress accumulation across complex geometries11. Although microstructural evolution is not explicitly modeled, accurate estimation of thermal gradients and cooling rates allows meaningful linkage to grain growth mechanisms and competitive solidification dynamics12.

The aspiration to produce single-crystal Ni-based components originates from directional solidification casting, where columnar grains are deliberately aligned along the < 001 > crystallographic axis to maximize creep resistance in turbine blades. In conventional casting, grain selection is achieved using a starter block and spiral grain selector. The starter block promotes competitive growth aligned with heat flux, while the spiral geometrically enforces progressive elimination of misoriented grains until only one grain survives13. Crucially, the spiral ensures grain survival rather than crystallographic refinement. Although LPBF differs fundamentally from directional solidification casting, analogous grain selection principles can be envisioned. In LPBF, steep thermal gradients naturally promote epitaxial columnar growth, yet grain competition at melt pool boundaries disrupts single-grain continuity. By integrating geometric confinement strategies inspired by casting selectors and optimizing thermal conditions through simulation, LPBF processing may suppress competing grains and enable progressive grain filtering within thin-wall tubular geometries.

Through this integration of thermal modeling, geometric design, and directional solidification principles, the present study advances a simulation-guided pathway toward single-crystal additive manufacturing of critical Inconel 718 components.

Methodology

The concept of the grain selector, widely employed in directional solidification casting to achieve single-crystal growth, was adopted as the design inspiration for this study. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the selector is composed of two parts: a starter block, which facilitates competitive grain growth and orientation selection, and a spiral section, which promotes dendrite branching and the eventual elimination of misaligned grains, leaving a single surviving grain at the exit of the spiral13,14. In this work, spiral geometries were prepared in Unigraphic NX12 CAD software as shown in Fig. 2 and parametrically varied in terms of their take-off angle (θ), pitch length (Lp), spiral diameter (dS), and wire diameter (dw), in order to investigate their influence on grain selection efficiency under LPBF conditions.

Geometry parameters of 3D model for spiral selector 3D model (a) with elbow start, (b) with non-elbow start.

The design matrix comprised a total of seventy spiral selector configurations developed for two proposed spiral design models, namely elbow-start and non-elbow-start geometries, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The configurations were systematically categorized into groups according to spiral diameter, wire diameter, and take-off angle. For all cases, the starter block diameter (dB) was fixed at 20 mm with a thickness of 2 mm, consistent with industrial casting-inspired selector designs13.

The geometric parameters comprising the spiral design matrix were established based on a synthesis of classical directional solidification mechanics and additive manufacturing constraints. The spiral take-off angles were constrained between 5° and 40°. According to the geometrical blocking mechanisms identified by15 and16, smaller take-off angles significantly improve single-crystal yield in conventional casting by restricting the vertical growth space. However, in LPBF, take-off angles below 5° represent extreme overhangs that induce severe powder bed collapse and melt pool instability17. Furthermore, spiral diameters of 9, 12, and 15 mm were selected to accommodate the spatial footprint of the targeted thin-wall tubular component while providing sufficient thermal mass to prevent catastrophic heat accumulation at the spiral core.

While the full-factorial design matrix yielded 70 theoretical geometric combinations, physical viability constraints reduced the final testing pool. Specifically, 14 geometric combinations, typically those pairing the largest wire diameters with the smallest spiral diameters and lowest take-off angles resulted in a vertical pitch that was smaller than the wire thickness. This geometric conflict caused the consecutive turns of the spiral to vertically overlap and fuse into a continuous solid cylinder, thereby destroying the helical channel necessary for competitive grain selection. These 14 self-intersecting geometries were designated as Not Applicable (NA) and were excluded from the study. Consequently, exactly 56 spiral geometries were successfully discretized and simulated using the thermo-mechanical solver.

The simulations used Inconel 718 powder and a production-scale LPBF platform. Nominal process settings were: laser power 220 W, scan speed 1180 mm/s, effective spot size 60 µm, layer thickness 30 µm, and nominal laser efficiency 0.60. Temperature-dependent material properties from the Simufact database were adopted across the relevant thermal range. Where indicated, orthotropic thermal expansion factors (X, Y, Z) were retained from calibration to reflect process-induced anisotropy in the thermomechanical response. Each simulation case followed a four-stage process chain comprising layerwise part fabrication with thermal-cycle loading governed by the prescribed hatch strategy, chamber cooling to ambient temperature, release of the component from the baseplate by cutting along a predefined plane, and subsequent support removal. Baseplate boundary conditions were defined using standard clamping assumptions. A sensitivity analysis employing a deformable baseplate demonstrated negligible coupling between baseplate flexure and the thermo-mechanical response of the thin-walled tube geometries; consequently, a rigid baseplate representation was adopted for all production simulations to reduce computational cost without compromising accuracy. To manage the computational expense of evaluating 70 distinct spiral geometries, a continuum-level voxel mesh with a resolution of 0.8 mm was employed alongside a lumped-layer heat source approach. It is critical to note that this macro-scale discretization does not resolve the 30 µm layer thickness, the 60 µm laser spot size, or the transient fluid dynamics within the local melt pool. Instead, as demonstrated by established multiscale LPBF modeling frameworks11,18, this part-scale voxel resolution is designed to capture bulk heat accumulation, macroscopic heat flux vectors, and part-scale residual stresses. The thermal gradients (G) and temperature rates (|dT/dt|) extracted from this model therefore represent the bulk thermodynamic environment dictated by the macro-geometry of the spiral selector, acting as a geometric filter to identify configurations that prevent massive heat build-up and structural distortion.

A structured voxel-based mesh with a uniform element size of 0.8 mm, consisting of conformal hexahedral elements, was generated for the parts, support structures, and the baseplate where applicable, as illustrated in Fig. 4. This mesh resolution was selected to adequately capture the thin-wall thickness and local geometric curvature, ensure compatibility between voxel dimensions and surface triangulation for reliable result mapping, and minimize numerical artifacts associated with near-zero volume fractions in boundary elements within slender regions. All simulations were executed in parallel using eight computational domains unless stated otherwise.

A structured voxel mesh for sample 3A with (a) elbow start, and (b) non-elbow start.

Prior to the geometry sweep, a representative IN718 coupon was simulated to calibrate the thermal-cycle inputs (effective energy deposition and thermal expansion parameters) against the nominal exposure conditions. Calibration targets included peak-temperature levels, cooling-rate magnitudes consistent with the parameter set, and realistic distortion amplitudes on a simple geometry. The resulting parameter set comprising effective energy input and anisotropic expansion coefficients was held constant across all spiral-selector variants to isolate purely geometric effects.

The starter block serves as the first stage of grain selection by promoting competitive growth among nucleated grains. In classical directional solidification, models such as the VeriCast module of ProCAST have been validated against experimental casting furnaces to capture this mechanism14 In this study, SIMUFACT Additive was used to preserve a layerwise thermal history tied directly to the build strategy while maintaining computational tractability for full-part analyses. The solver advances a coupled finite-element formulation that maps time-resolved thermal loads to temperature-dependent elastoplastic behavior, thereby providing the temperature (T), strain (ε), and stress (σ) fields required to derive solidification-relevant metrics (thermal gradient G, interface velocity proxy R, and G/ R). Following the starter block, the spiral geometry functions as a second filter to enforce grain elimination through physical constraint and dendrite branching. Using the simulated thermal profiles from SIMUFACT, the evolution of grain numbers was tracked across the spiral pitch lengths. Grain number selection was found to be highly sensitive to the spiral take-off angle (θ). Lower angles corresponded to smaller pitch lengths, which shortened the growth path and increased dendrite wall interactions, thereby accelerating the elimination of misaligned grains. However, excessively small take-off angles are impractical to manufacture and may compromise structural integrity; therefore, industrial practice typically favors angles between (25)° and (30)°13,16.

The influence of spiral diameter (dS) and wire diameter (dw) was also investigated, with larger diameters and thicker wire diameter spirals reducing selector efficiency by increasing the likelihood of multiple grains surviving longer distances. This observation was consistent with earlier findings from casting studies. Ultimately, each of the 56 cases was simulated under identical initial conditions .The thermal stabilization height, defined as the minimum vertical distance required for the axial thermal gradient (G) and the G/R ratio to reach a steady state maximum, was extracted for each spiral configuration. This metric evaluates how rapidly a specific macro-geometry establishes the optimal thermodynamic preconditions necessary to sustain columnar growth.

In addition to grain number reduction, the spiral selector’s effect on crystallographic orientation was also studied. For each case, the orientation deviations of the surviving grains relative to the build axis (< 001 >) were recorded. The simulations confirmed that, while the starter block provided significant orientation optimization by filtering out grains misaligned with the heat flux, the spiral selector itself did not directly optimize orientation. Instead, its role was limited to reducing the grain population to a single survivor. This finding is consistent with experimental observations in directional solidification, where the final orientation of the surviving single crystal is determined randomly from among the grains selected at the starter block13. To simulate the LPBF process, SIMUFACT Additive was employed owing to its robust thermo-mechanical finite element framework, which enables the prediction of temperature evolution, heat transfer, and stress development during the build. The software discretizes the geometry into finite elements and solves the coupled energy and momentum equations to calculate macroscopic heat transfer, fluid flow, and solid mechanics within the build. A Gaussian volumetric heat source was applied to represent the laser, with temperature-dependent material properties calibrated for Inconel 718. Boundary conditions included conductive losses to the substrate, radiative losses from the free surface, and convective exchange with the chamber atmosphere.

The output of the thermal simulation comprised detailed transient temperature histories and temperature rates across the spiral and starter block geometries. These thermal profiles provided the basis for predicting solidification conditions, including thermal gradients (G) and interface velocity proxy (R), which are key parameters controlling competitive grain growth. By analyzing the G/R ratio as well as the residual stresses, insights into columnar-to-equiaxed transition behavior were derived. The simulated results therefore serve as a computational analogue to the competitive grain growth observed in traditional casting selectors.

To ensure that the localized thermo‑mechanical variations across the different spiral‑selector geometries could be clearly visualized and accurately interpreted, the color scales used in the thermal gradient, temperature‑rate, and residual stress contour plots were adjusted on a case‑specific basis. For each simulation case, the actual minimum and maximum field values were extracted directly from the numerical output, and the corresponding color bar was calibrated to these extrema. Given the substantial variation in magnitude among configurations with different wire diameters, spiral diameters, take‑off angles, and numbers of turns, applying a uniform default scale would have compressed the data range for many cases and obscured physically meaningful intra‑case differences. The adopted approach preserves the interpretive fidelity of the numerical fields by enhancing the visibility of subtle but important spatial patterns that would otherwise remain indistinguishable under a standardized global scale.

Within the continuum thermal model, the solidification front velocity (R) is approximated using the localized temperature rate (|dT/dt|) and the axial thermal gradient (G) via the standard kinematic relationship: R =|dT/dt| / G. While this relationship is fundamentally derived from steady state directional solidification casting19, it is widely utilized in additive manufacturing simulations as a necessary first order proxy to evaluate localized solidification kinetics20. By extracting these variables at the solidus–liquidus temperature interval, the model allows for the spatial mapping of the G/R ratio, which remains the primary recognized driver for predicting the columnar to equiaxed transition (CET) across complex geometries.

The use of the G/R ratio to evaluate the thermodynamic favorability for single-crystal survival is grounded in classical solidification theory, specifically the Hunt criterion21. This foundational model establishes that the Columnar-to-Equiaxed Transition (CET) is governed by the ratio of the thermal gradient to the solidification front velocity; stable columnar growth requires a high G/R ratio to prevent constitutional undercooling and suppress equiaxed grain nucleation. For the additive manufacturing of Inconel 718, these classical CET thresholds have been successfully adapted and experimentally validated. Studies by22 and23correlated numerically derived G and R metrics with physical Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD) maps, confirming that maintaining a high G/R ratio reliably predicts columnar structural integrity even under extreme cooling rates. Consequently, this study utilizes these established IN718 CET process boundaries as the comparative baseline to evaluate the geometric efficiency of the spiral selectors.

Because evaluating the microstructural evolution of 70 distinct spiral geometries using coupled meso-scale models (such as Cellular Automata or Phase-Field methods) is computationally prohibitive, this study utilizes macroscopic continuum metrics as predictive proxies. In the laser powder bed fusion of Superalloys, the thermal gradient (G) and solidification rate (R) control the solidification morphology. Specifically, the ratio G/R governs the Columnar to Equiaxed Transition (CET)23,24. For a single crystal to survive the spiral selector, the local thermal environment must strictly maintain a high G/R ratio to promote continuous columnar dendritic growth and suppress the nucleation of equiaxed stray grains. By mapping these localized thermal histories, the macro-scale model acts as a rigorous filter, identifying which macro-geometries provide the necessary thermodynamic preconditions for single-crystal survival before advancing to targeted, computationally intensive microstructural simulations.

By incorporating SIMUFACT Additive in this study, the analysis highlights the capability of commercial finite element software to reproduce key thermal and competitive grain growth phenomena previously modeled by research codes. The simulation framework therefore demonstrates how insights from grain selector design in casting can be adapted into LPBF geometries, paving the way for single-crystal thin-wall structures fabricated through additive manufacturing.

Results and discussion

The numerical results obtained with SIMUFACT Additive 2024.3 for the two- and four-turn spiral grain selectors elucidate the coupled thermal–mechanical conditions that control grain-selection efficiency under additive-manufacturing thermal cycling. The observed correlations among the axial thermal gradient G, the temperature-rate field dT/dt, and the residual-stress distribution delineate a geometric- thermophysical operating regime in which the spiral architecture promotes directional solidification while minimizing mechanical distortion conditions conducive to the formation of single-crystal or near single crystalmicrostructures. The parametric sweep covered two geometrical families (spiral with elbow start vs. without elbow) and two lengths (four‑turn vs. two‑turn spirals) while varying the take‑off angle θ and spiral dimensions across Groups A-D.

Across all four‑turn configurations, thermal gradient (G) exhibits a nonlinear dependence on take-off angle (θ). The results show a marked increase in G from 10° to 25°, followed by saturation or mild decline beyond 30°. The strongest gradients cluster around θ = 15°- 25° , particularly for small wire diameters, consistent with enhanced axial heat‑flow confinement along the helical path and more effective elimination of misaligned grains.

This trend mirrors the geometrical blocking principle reported for spiral selectors in directional solidification: smaller take‑off angles intensify wall interactions and confine growth paths, boosting selection efficiency, while excessively large angles relax the constraint and reduce filtering. Although the directional solidification literature often recommends moderate angles for manufacturability and mechanical robustness, the efficiency increase at lower θ is a robust, repeatedly observed phenomenon25. Beyond 30°, the decline in G indicates the onset of a thermal-control regime23, where the spiral curvature becomes insufficient to impose geometrical filtering, leading to a broader, less constrained melt-pool envelope. Hence, within additive thermal cycles, 20° ± 5° emerges as the most effective take-off range for maintaining a steep and stable axial gradient conducive to epitaxial grain propagation.

The results in Fig. 5 demonstrate a pronounced dependence of the maximum axial thermal gradient on the spiral take-off angle for four-turn configurations. Thermal gradients increase rapidly as θ rises from 10° to approximately 20°, beyond which the gradient either plateaus or marginally decreases. This non-linear response reflects the competing effects of geometric confinement and heat-flow redirection imposed by the spiral curvature. At intermediate angles (15°-25°), the helical geometry effectively constrains lateral heat dissipation, forcing heat extraction along the spiral axis and thereby maximizing G. In contrast, larger angles reduce curvature-induced confinement, allowing the thermal field to relax toward a quasi-vertical distribution similar to straight thin-wall LPBF builds. These thermal gradient magnitudes perfectly align with established, high fidelity LPBF thermal models in the literature26,27.

Figure 6 further illustrates the spatial localization of these gradients, revealing pronounced gradient intensification along the inner radii of the helix and near curvature transitions. These regions act as effective grain-filtering zones, where misoriented dendrites are suppressed by locally steep thermal gradients. Elbow-start geometries show more continuous gradient pathways, indicating smoother thermal steering compared with non-elbow designs, which exhibit localized gradient discontinuities at curvature junctions.

The temperature rate (dT/dt) results, shown in Fig. 7, quantify the local solidification front velocity (R). Moderate |dT/dt| values approximately 0.008 to 0.015 K s⁻1 are obtained for take-off angles of 20° ± 5°, particularly in Groups C and D, corresponding to high G/R ratios (> 104 K·s mm⁻2). Such ratios are widely recognized13,28 as indicative of columnar-to-single-crystal transitions.

Maximum temperature rate for four turns spiral selectors.

Conversely, low rates below 0.003 K s⁻1 at θ ≤ 10° suggest over-melting and slow interface recovery, while sharp peaks (> 0.018 K s⁻1) at θ ≥ 30° reflect rapid cooling that destabilizes the solidification front and encourages stray-grain nucleation. This confirms that intermediate solidification velocities under high thermal gradients a condition achieved at a take of angle of 20° ± 5° with a small wire diameter are thermodynamically optimal for stable columnar dendritic growth in LPBF spiral geometries.

It should be noted that applying the R =|dT/dt| / G approximation to LPBF processes carries inherent thermodynamic limitations. Unlike conventional investment casting, LPBF is characterized by highly transient thermal histories and rapidly moving, non-planar melt pool boundaries2. Consequently, using |dT/dt| as a direct proxy for R assumes that the dendritic growth velocity perfectly matches the velocity of the moving isotherms. While this steady state assumption represents a mathematical approximation of the true non equilibrium solidification dynamics, it remains a robust and well established comparative metric for evaluating how macro-scale geometric variations such as the spiral selector curvature influence the thermal environment and the resulting microstructural selection.

Figure 8 maps the simulated temperature-rate fields (dT/dt) for four-turn spiral grain selectors at θ = 15°, comparing elbow start and non-elbow start designs across Groups A-D. In all cases, cooling dominates and ∣dT/dt∣ is largest near the baseplate and inner radii of the early turns, then attenuates along the helix; however, elbow start geometries produce smoother, more uniform dT/dt distributions with fewer hot spots, indicating a steadier interface velocity R, whereas non-elbow variants exhibit heterogeneous bands of elevated ∣dT/dt∣ curvature transitions that imply oscillatory front kinetics. Increasing spiral and wire diameters tends to homogenize the field via higher thermal inertia, although non-elbow Group D still shows localized high ∣dT/dt∣ regions near the entrance. Taken together with the strong axial gradients G reported elsewhere, the elbow start layouts favor a higher G/R and thus more stable columnar to equiaxed transition. This distinction underscores the importance of entrance geometry in stabilizing solidification dynamics, while the localized ∣dT/dt∣ peaks in non-elbow designs may depress G/R locally and promote front destabilization/CET, reducing selector efficiency.

Figure 9 depicts the evolution of maximum residual stresses as a function of take-off angle. Notably, Group A with elbow start and group B with non-elbow start exhibit the lowest residual stresses (≈ 98–114 MPa) when θ = 15° for Group A with elbow start and non-elbow start, while both smaller and larger angles yield either excessive accumulation (> 600 MPa) or insufficient confinement (< 150 MPa). This trend demonstrates that the same geometry promoting efficient heat-flow alignment also stabilizes mechanical deformation by reducing stress concentration at the junction between spiral turns.

Maximum residual stresses for four turns spiral selectors.

In agreement with29, such moderate residual-stress states are desirable because they preserve the thermal gradient without exceeding the plastic-strain limit that could trigger recrystallization or crack initiation. Within the LPBF context, minimizing thermal strain energy further mitigates local remelting disturbances, ensuring continuity of dendritic growth across successive layers. Thus, the 20° ± 5° four-turn spiral not only maximizes G/R but also achieves a low-stress equilibrium, which is crucial for single crystal integrity.

The interplay between thermal gradients and mechanical relaxation is a known requirement in AM: sustained high gradients with manageable stress reduce the risk of local recrystallization, cracking, or distortion that would otherwise disrupt the growth front. The capability of SIMUFACT Additive to approximate layer‑wise residual stress fields has been validated across diverse LPBF geometries, reinforcing confidence in these trends1. When the three dominant parameters G, |dT/dt|, and maximum residual are considered collectively, the four-turn spiral model with a 15°-20° take-off angle and a small wire diameter emerges as the configuration offering optimal thermal directionality with mechanical relaxation capacity. This confirms that low residual stress and high gradient confinement are co-dependent outcomes of optimized spiral geometry. In particular, the four-turn structure enhances thermal path length, enabling gradual stress relaxation while maintaining sufficient curvature for geometrical blocking.

While the continuum level thermo-mechanical model provides a robust comparative framework for evaluating macro-scale selector geometries, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of neglecting melt pool fluid dynamics. In LPBF, steep thermal gradients across the melt pool surface drive vigorous Marangoni convection, which, alongside evaporation-induced recoil pressure, significantly alters the melt pool morphology and internal heat flux 1. Because competitive grain growth is strictly governed by the alignment of the primary dendrites with the local thermal gradient which remains strictly normal to the solid–liquid interface fluid-driven changes to the melt pool curvature can directly influence crystallographic texture and grain competition. Recent high-fidelity mesoscale studies utilizing coupled Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and phase-field modeling have demonstrated that these convective flows can induce local thermal fluctuations and alter interface stability, occasionally promoting the nucleation of misoriented stray grains that conduction dominated models may underpredict30,31. Therefore, while the predicted thermal gradients (G) and residual stresses herein successfully identify optimal macro-geometries for single-crystal yield, future work integrating mesoscale fluid dynamics will be essential to fully capture the microstructural complexities of the spiral selection process.

The model thus achieves the functional equivalence of traditional casting selectors13, yet under LPBF conditions where rapid solidification and cyclic reheating typically challenge grain continuity. These findings extend the fundamental principles of casting-based grain selection into the LPBF domain. They demonstrate that the geometry of the selector especially the number of turns and take-off angle directly modulates the thermal–mechanical environment, determining whether the growth front remains columnar or transitions to equiaxed.

While the optimized 15°–20° take-off angles present an ideal theoretical geometry for competitive grain selection, the physical fabrication of these acute overhangs in LPBF introduces complex manufacturing challenges that should be accounted for. In powder bed systems, the loose powder supporting the lower downward-facing (down-skin) surfaces possesses a drastically lower thermal conductivity than the solid metal supporting the upper (up-skin) surfaces32. This thermal asymmetry causes the loose powder to act as an insulator, leading to localized heat accumulation and an enlarged melt pool on the down-skin side. As demonstrated by33,34, this restricted heat dissipation significantly lowers the local thermal gradient (G) and alters the solidification rate (R) along the overhang boundary. In the context of single-crystal selection, a sudden drop in the G/R ratio on the down-skin surface could destabilize the columnar solidification front, increasing the risk of misoriented equiaxed stray grain nucleation and inducing asymmetric residual stresses due to differential temperature rates. Therefore, successfully deploying these spiral geometries in physical LPBF environments will necessitate the application of specialized down-skin contour scanning parameters; such as reduced laser power and adjusted scan speeds to minimize over-melting, stabilize the thermal gradients, and maintain the microstructural integrity of the selected grain.

By identifying the 15°-20° four-turn spiral as the configuration producing both the highest G/R ratio and lowest residual stress, the present study provides a quantitative guideline for single-crystal optimization in AM. It is critical to acknowledge the limitations of a purely macroscopic approach. The current model maps the thermodynamic preconditions favorable for columnar growth, but it does not explicitly simulate the competitive grain evolution, dendritic branching, or crystallographic selection mechanisms that physically occur within the spiral35. The actual selection efficiency specifically how misoriented grains are geometrically blocked by the spiral walls under these optimal thermal conditions should be verified. Therefore, the macroscopic thermal maps and down-selected geometries established in this study provide the required foundational boundary conditions for future meso-scale investigations, such as Cellular Automata Finite Element (CAFE) modeling, to explicitly track grain trajectory and validate the selection efficiency in LPBF.

Model benchmarking and validation

Because evaluating the microstructural evolution of 70 distinct spiral geometries through physical fabrication and EBSD mapping is experimentally prohibitive, this study employs continuum thermo-mechanical simulations to down-select the design space. To establish the credibility of this numerical approach, the baseline thermal outputs of the Simufact Additive model were benchmarked against experimentally validated LPBF literature. The maximum unscaled thermal gradients (G) computed at the solid–liquid interface ranged from 0.259 × 104 to 2.636 × 106 K/m, as well as the cooling rates (|dT/dt|). These magnitudes are in strong agreement with the experimentally calibrated numerical bounds reported by20,23,36 for LPBF processing of Inconel 718. Additionally, the baseline longitudinal residual stresses predicted within the stable columnar regions (approximately 100–150 MPa) are consistent with neutron-diffraction-based measurements of stress-relieved LPBF superalloy components37. By accurately reproducing these fundamental thermo-mechanical conditions, the macro-scale simulation framework is validated as a rigorous comparative tool for assessing the geometric viability of the spiral selectors.

By projecting the extracted thermal gradients (G) and solidification velocities (R) of the optimized 15°–20° four-turn spirals onto established additive manufacturing CET maps for Inconel 71838, it is evident that these specific macro-geometries successfully maintain the local thermal environment strictly within the columnar growth regime. Unlike the rejected geometries (e.g., shallow angles or two-turn spirals), which suffered from localized heat accumulation that collapsed the G/R ratio into the equiaxed zone, the 15°–20° configurations established a stable, maximized G/R thermodynamic precondition. While true microstructural tracking is still required, the alignment of these extracted metrics with experimentally validated CET thresholds22 provides high confidence that these geometries are optimally designed to support single-crystal survival in physical LPBF fabrication.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that geometry-assisted grain selection, inspired by directional solidification casting, can be effectively translated into the LPBF process for thin-wall Inconel 718 tubes through optimized spiral-selector design. A comprehensive thermo-mechanical simulation framework using Simufact Additive was developed to evaluate a wide parametric design space comprising seventy configurations, of which fifty-six physically viable geometries were successfully analyzed. The results reveal that grain-selection efficiency in LPBF is strongly governed by the interplay between spiral geometry and the resulting thermo-mechanical fields. Four-turn spiral selectors consistently produce significantly higher axial thermal gradients (up to ~ 10⁶ K/m), stable temperature rates, and elevated G/R ratios compared to two-turn configurations, which exhibit weak thermal confinement and limited grain-filtering capability. The optimal design is identified as a four-turn spiral with an intermediate take-off angle of 15°–20° and small wire diameter, which establishes a favorable balance between thermal gradient stability, solidification kinetics, and moderate residual stress levels (~ 100–150 MPa). Additionally, elbow-start geometries demonstrate smoother thermal-gradient continuity and more stable temperature-rate distributions compared to non-elbow designs, highlighting the importance of entrance geometry in stabilizing solidification dynamics. The results further confirm that spiral length and curvature are critical parameters controlling heat-flow directionality, dendrite branching, and competitive grain elimination. While the continuum-scale model does not explicitly resolve melt pool fluid dynamics or microstructural evolution, the predicted thermal gradients and stress fields are consistent with experimentally validated LPBF literature, confirming the reliability of the framework as a first-stage computational filter. This work establishes a simulation-driven methodology for identifying optimal geometries that promote single-grain survival in LPBF. The findings provide quantitative design guidelines for geometry-assisted microstructure control and lay the foundation for future meso-scale modeling and experimental validation toward achieving single-crystal additive manufacturing of critical superalloy components.
