The influence of continuous renal replacement therapy on 1,3-β-d-glucan levels in critically ill patients: a single-center retrospective propensity score study
Authors: M. Gabriela Cabanilla, Matthew J. Briski, Zachary Bruss, Lisa Saa, Pamela C. Vasquez, Chelsea N. Rodriguez, Jessica A. Mitchell, Michael L. Bernauer, Christos P. Argyropoulos, Cameron S. Crandall, J. Pedro Teixeira
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Abstract1,3-β-d-Glucan (BDG) is commonly used for diagnosing invasive fungal infections (IFIs). While exposure to cellulose-based hemodialyzers is known to cause false-positive BDG results, the impact of modern hemofilters used in continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) remains unclear. This retrospective, single-center cohort study aimed to evaluate the effect of CRRT on BDG levels in critically ill patients. We included adult intensive care unit (ICU) patients with ≥1 BDG measurement between December 2019 and December 2020. The primary outcome was the rate of false-positive BDG results in patients exposed to CRRT compared to unexposed patients. Propensity score analysis was performed to control for confounding factors. A total of 103 ICU patients with ≥1 BDG level were identified. Most (72.8%) were medical ICU patients. Forty patients underwent CRRT using hemofilter membranes composed of sodium methallyl sulfonate copolymer (AN 69 HF) (82.5%) and of polyarylethersulfone (PAES) (17.5%). Among the 91 patients without proven IFI, 31 (34.1%) had false-positive BDG results. Univariable analysis showed an association between CRRT exposure and false-positive BDG results. However, the association between CRRT exposure and false-positive BDG results was no longer significant across three propensity score models employed: 1:1 match (n = 32) (odds ratio (OR) 1.65, p = .48), model-adjusted (n = 91) (OR 1.75, p = .38), quintile-adjusted (n = 91) (OR 1.78, p = .36). In this single-center retrospective analysis, exposure to synthetic CRRT membranes did not independently increase the risk of false-positive BDG results. Larger prospective studies are needed to further evaluate the association between CRRT exposure and false-positive BDG results in critically ill patients with suspected IFI.

Introduction

Invasive fungal infections (IFIs) are associated with high mortality, especially among critically ill patients, in part due to delayed or missed diagnoses owing to the lack of sensitive noninvasive diagnostic tests [1–8]. In addition, delays in the initiation of antifungal therapy have been associated with an increased risk of death in patients with proven IFI, particularly candidemia [9,10]. As a result, empiric antifungal therapy is often used in the intensive care unit (ICU) in patients who are found not to have IFI [7,11], exposing these patients to unnecessary antimicrobial therapy and potentially promoting the development of antifungal resistance.

The Fungitell assay (Associates of Cape Cod, East Falmouth, MA), which detects the fungal polysaccharide 1,3-β-d-glucan (BDG) in serum and other biospecimens, has been developed as a noninvasive adjunct for the diagnosis of IFI. It is commonly used in the hospital setting to diagnose IFI caused by Aspergillus, Candida species, and Pneumocystis jirovecii. Fungitell is the only currently available FDA-cleared BDG assay and has the advantage that BDG can be measured in serum, which is readily available in hospitalized patients. However, the sensitivity and specificity of BDG for IFI are variable. In a 2011 meta-analysis, the sensitivity ranged from 38% to 100% and the specificity ranged from 45% to 99%, with similarly broad ranges observed for the positive predictive value (30–89%) and negative predictive value (73–97%) [12]. A study examining the performance of BDG (using a cutoff value of 80 pg/mL) specifically in critically ill, non-immunocompromised patients found that the sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value of serum BDG for invasive candidemia were 85%, 46%, and 50%, respectively, while the negative predictive value was high in their cohort at 88–96% [13]. Additionally, the ICU population is more prone to false-positive results [14–17], further complicating its interpretation. Given these limitations, the optimal use of BDG in the ICU has been evaluated in multiple recent randomized controlled trials, but these studies have produced mixed results [18–20].

The poor specificity of BDG in the ICU may relate to a series of factors commonly present in critically ill patients that are known to produce false-positive BDG levels. Such confounders include the use of certain antibacterial agents (specifically β lactam/β lactamase inhibitors (BL/BLIs)), bacteremia with Streptococcus pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (which both produce BDG), and blood transfusions [21–24]. Adding to this complexity, these same risk factors for false-positive BDG – broad spectrum antibiotic use, bacteremia, and blood transfusion – are also known risk factors for proven IFI [25].

Another specific potential limitation to BDG use in the ICU population is the effect of exposure to renal replacement therapy (RRT). Specifically, membranes containing unmodified cellulose found in older hemodialyzers can significantly increase serum BDG levels [26], whereas conflicting results have been observed for membranes with modified cellulose and synthetic materials such as polysulfone [27–33]. The effect on serum BDG levels of exposure to synthetic materials used in hemofilters employed specifically with modern continuous RRT (CRRT) devices has not been previously studied. Importantly, the potential spurious effect of CRRT exposure on BDG levels is potentially highly clinically relevant, as the need for RRT itself has been identified as a risk factor for IFI in both the ICU and non-ICU settings [4,25,34–36]. For example, when considering invasive candidiasis in critically ill patients, a recent large meta-analysis demonstrated that the need for RRT was more strongly associated with infection in multivariable analysis than other known risk factors for invasive candidiasis, such as broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, abdominal surgery, receipt of total parenteral nutrition (TPN), candidal colonization, or the presence of a central venous catheter (CVC) [25].

To better elucidate the effect of CRRT exposure on the diagnostic utility of BDG in the ICU, we compared the rate of false-positive BDG results between critically ill patients undergoing CRRT and those who did not require CRRT at our institution. Although no prior data exist on this relationship, our hypothesis was that the cellulose-free modern hemofilters used in CRRT would not produce false-positive BDG levels.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective, single-center, observational cohort study of adult patients admitted to an ICU at the University of New Mexico Hospital (UNMH) between 1 December 2019 and 21 December 2020. UNMH is a publicly funded, tertiary care center serving as the primary safety net hospital for the state of New Mexico, providing care to a largely medically underserved population [37]. The adult ICUs at UNMH have 72 beds (expanded up to approximately 100 beds during the COVID-19 pandemic) cared for by medical, trauma-surgical, neurosciences, and cardiothoracic-vascular ICU services. As of 2022, approximately 250 patients are treated with CRRT over approximately 30,000 therapy hours per year at UNMH. Our CRRT is delivered using Prismaflex or PrisMax devices and M100 or HF1000 hemofilters (all from Baxter International, Deerfield, IL). M100 hemofilters contain acrylonitrile and sodium methallyl sulfonate copolymer (AN 69 HF) hollow fibers, while HF1000 hemofilters contain polyarylethersulfone (PAES) hollow fibers. Additional manufacturer specifications for the M100 and HF1000 hemofilters are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Although we were unable to obtain data on the dose of CRRT in this cohort, our local practice is to target a delivered total effluent dose of 20–25 mL/kg/h in accordance with clinical practice guidelines for acute kidney injury (AKI) [38].

Institutional review board approval and waiver of consent were obtained from the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center Human Research Review Committee (protocol #22-201).

Study population

The population was defined as adult ICU patients admitted to our hospital for which at least one BDG (Fungitell) assay was performed during the study period. Patients with proven IFI were excluded from the analysis. The exposure group was considered those without proven IFI who were exposed to CRRT, as defined in further detail below. The control group included those without a proven IFI who had at least one BDG drawn during admission but not during or after CRRT exposure.

Definitions, data collection, and study outcome

The primary outcome of this study was to evaluate the rate of false-positive BDG test results in ICU patients undergoing CRRT compared to the rate in ICU patients not exposed to CRRT, controlling for as many potential confounding variables as statistically feasible given the limited sample size of our cohort. Data were collected through the end of each index admission.

The patients’ demographic data and extractable clinical information, including fungal serology results, were collected from the electronic health record via a PowerInsight query from the local Cerner database. Microbiological culture data, baseline kidney disease status, and indications for CRRT (i.e., AKI versus AKI on chronic kidney disease (CKD) versus end-stage kidney disease (ESKD)) were collected via chart review. Bacteremia was defined as at least one set of positive blood cultures with growth of either Streptococcus pneumoniae or Pseudomonas aeruginosa during index admission. A positive BDG (Fungitell) assay was defined per the manufacturer’s recommendations as ≥80 pg/mL. All results of <80 pg/mL (i.e., both ‘indeterminate’ and ‘negative’ BDG levels) were considered negative. Study investigators determined if a diagnosis of proven IFI was made during the index ICU admission using the definitions set forth by the 2020 European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group Education and Research Consortium (EORTC/MSG) (Supplementary Table 2) [39]. Authors MGC and JPT reviewed those final determinations.

Additional data collected included characteristics at the time of ICU admission, CRRT prescription details (duration of treatment, modality, and hemofilter used), and a variety of covariables. Any CRRT treatments interrupted by a full 24 h off of CRRT were defined as separate CRRT runs. Otherwise, data available on the duration of CRRT therapy only considered the start and stop times of CRRT runs and did not account for other filter downtime (e.g., shorter periods of time lost to circuit clotting, circuit changes, or diagnostic or therapeutic procedures requiring interruptions in CRRT). The covariables collected included factors known to either promote a proven IFI or false-positive BDG results, such as immunosuppression prior to admission, inpatient corticosteroid use, treatment with and duration of treatment with BL/BLI antibiotics, number of units of packed red blood cells (pRBC) transfused during the admission, CVC days, gastrointestinal (GI) surgery/procedure, and TPN administration. Immunosuppression prior to admission was defined as receipt of immunosuppressive therapy for an autoimmune disease, exposure to chemotherapeutic agents, immunocompromising conditions such as HIV/AIDS, solid organ, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and the use of chronic corticosteroids at a dose of prednisone ≥20 mg/day or equivalent prior to index hospitalization. Inpatient corticosteroid use was defined as any treatment with systemic (intravenous or oral) corticosteroids during index admission. CVC days were defined as the total number of inclusive calendar days (rounded up to full days) each patient had a CVC in place, excluding midlines, indwelling ports, or cannulae for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), with two CVCs present in a single day counting as two CVC days and so on. For the variables TPN, CVC days, GI surgery/procedure, and BL/BLI antibiotics, we collected exposure data for 7 and 30 days prior to BDG sample collection date. We also collected data on which patients in the cohort were treated with intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) during their admission, but we were unable to reliably determine the timing of these treatments relative to the BDG levels and therefore could not include IHD exposure as a covariable in our analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions. Continuous variables are presented as medians with ranges. The primary outcome of the study was first analyzed using logistic regression. As our sample size was not sufficient to allow for meaningful adjustment for all potential confounding variables collected, we started by performing univariable analysis between the rate of false-positive BDG results and exposure to the covariables to help identify the variables to be included in multivariable analyses. The variables evaluated by univariable analysis included exposure to CRRT over three different time intervals: CRRT exposure within 30 days of the first BDG level, CRRT exposure within seven days of the first BDG level, and ongoing CRRT at the time the first BDG level was obtained. The CRRT variable most strongly associated with false-positive BDG results in univariable analysis (i.e., the variable with the largest odds ratio (OR)) was selected as the primary exposure variable for subsequent multivariable analyses.

Multivariable analysis was conducted using propensity scores to quantify the propensity of the subjects to be exposed to CRRT. The variables included in the propensity score analysis were identified using two methods. First, we included basic demographic variables, namely, age and gender. Second, we identified confounders defined as those variables who result in ≥10% change in the 𝛽 coefficient for the exposure variable.

Three different analytical approaches were used to provide propensity score-adjusted estimates of the effect of CRRT on the risk of BDG false positivity: propensity score matching, model-based adjustment using propensity score, and model-based adjustment using propensity quintiles. For the first approach, we implemented a 1:1 nearest neighbors match in which CRRT-exposed patients were matched with unexposed controls. Logistic regression was performed on the matched dataset to estimate the effect of CRRT exposure on the risk of false-positive BDG results. For the second approach, we fitted a logistic regression model on the entire dataset, which included both the exposure variable (CRRT) and the estimated logit from the propensity score model. For the third approach, we used propensity scores to stratify patients into quintiles. We then confirmed that confounding covariates were balanced within each quintile, after which we fitted a logistic regression model that included a main effect for the exposure variable (CRRT) and four design variables to capture the quintile effects. Statistical significance was defined as a p value of ≤.05. Data compilation and statistical analyses were performed using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

In patients with multiple BDG results, only the initial BDG level was included in the primary analysis. However, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the highest BDG level instead of the first determination.

Results

A total of 103 patients who had at least one BDG level obtained during the study period were identified (Figure 1). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of our cohort are summarized in Table 1. Mean subject age was 55 years, most (66%) were male, 33% were American Indian/Alaska Native, and 29% were Hispanic/Latinx. Eleven subjects received ECMO, and 38% died during the admission. The majority of patients were admitted to a medical ICU service (72.8%, 75/103), with the most common (82.5%, 85/103) admission diagnosis being acute hypoxic respiratory failure, including 31.1% (32/103) with COVID-19 pneumonia.

Among the 103 patients in the entire cohort, 40 were treated with CRRT at some point during the admission. Most patients underwent a single CRRT run, with eight patients undergoing two separate CRRT runs and two patients undergoing three runs. The hemofilters used were M100 in 33 (82.5%) and HF1000 in seven (17.5%). The median duration of total CRRT treatment per patient was 6.7 days (IQR: 2.7–21.4 days; range: 0.7–76.6 days). Of these 40 patients, CRRT was initiated for AKI in 37, for pre-existing ESKD in two, and for refractory volume overload in the setting of ECMO requirement in one. Of the AKI patients, 27 had de novo AKI, 10 had AKI on CKD, and two were kidney transplant recipients (one each with normal baseline graft function and stage 3 CKD). Most patients with AKI were clinically diagnosed with acute tubular injury and/or a hemodynamic cause of AKI (e.g., cardiorenal or hepatorenal syndrome), while one was diagnosed with rapidly progressive antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) glomerulonephritis treated with immunosuppressive therapy. Of the 10 patients with CKD, including the transplant CKD patient, three patients had stage 4 CKD, four had stage 3 CKD, one had borderline stage 2–3 CKD, and two had stage 2 CKD. Of the 40 patients treated with CRRT, 22 were ultimately treated with IHD at some point during hospitalization.

Among the 103 patients in the cohort, 28 received CRRT within 30 days prior to the first BDG level, 25 received CRRT within seven days prior to the first BDG level, and 16 had the first BDG level drawn while on CRRT. Ten patients without IFI were exposed to CRRT after the first BDG level was obtained or >30 days before first BDG level; they were included as controls, along with 55 patients without IFI who did not receive CRRT. Among the 28 patients who received CRRT within 30 days of the first BDG level, the most common CRRT modality was continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF) alone (n = 26, 92.9%), with 7.1% (n = 2) of the patients receiving slow continuous ultrafiltration in addition to CVVHDF. Among these 28 patients, the most common hemofilter used was M100 (23/28, 82.1%), with HF1000 used in the rest (5/28, 17.9%).

According to the EORTC/MSG criteria, 12 (11.7%) patients were found to have a proven IFI, with three false-negative BDG results (Supplementary Table 3). Two patients with proven IFI required CRRT during their hospitalization. Among the 91 patients without IFI, a higher rate of false-positive BDG results was found among patients who received CRRT within 30 days prior to the first BDG level (13/26, 50%) than among the controls (18/65, 27.7%). When considering all BDG levels obtained in the entire cohort (including 24 repeated BDG levels in 18 patients), the median BDG level was 46 pg/mL (IQR: <31–167; range: <31 to >500). When considering only the 103 initial BDG levels included in the primary analysis, the distribution was similar (median: 40 pg/mL; IQR: <31–155; range: <31 to >500). After excluding patients with proven IFI, those with CRRT exposure within 30 days (n = 16) had a median BDG level of 114 pg/mL (IQR: 48.3–235.3) compared to a median of <31 pg/mL (IQR: <31–95) in the no CRRT exposure group (n = 75). Of the 91 patients without IFI, 31 (34.1%) had false-positive BDG results and were compared to 60 true-negative patients to determine which factors were associated with a false-positive BDG.

Univariable analysis

On univariable analysis (Table 2), CRRT exposure at all time points evaluated was associated with false-positive BDG results. Being on CRRT when the first BDG was drawn was the exposure most strongly associated with false-positive BDG (OR: 6.94, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.27–24.3, p = .001), and this variable was therefore selected as the primary exposure variable in the subsequent multivariable analyses. Other variables associated with a false-positive BDG on univariable analysis included GI procedure/surgery within 30 days before admission (OR: 4.08, 95% CI 1.13–16.8, p = .036), receipt of any pRBC transfusion during admission (OR: 3.81, 95% CI 1.55–9.72, p = .004), and exposure to CVC within seven days of BDG, either when defined as CVC days (OR: 1.15, 95% CI 1.04–1.27, p = .009) or as a yes/no exposure within seven days (OR: 4.50, 95% CI 1.52–16.7, p = .012). There were no statistically significant relationships between BL/BLI antibiotic exposure within 7 or 30 days of the first BDG (OR: 0.66, 95% CI 0.25–1.64, p = .4, and OR: 0.77, 95% CI 0.30–1.89, p = .6, respectively) or trauma (OR: 1.97, 95% CI 0.08–50.9, p = .6) and the outcome of false-positive BDG results in our cohort. Some variables were excluded from all analyses due to their rare occurrences. For example, our cohort included only three patients with S. pneumoniae or P. aeruginosa bacteremia, three patients total with trauma or burns, and one patient with necrotizing pancreatitis.

Propensity score analyses

The effect of CRRT exposure – defined as being on CRRT when the first BDG level was obtained – on the rate of false-positive BDG results was explored in three separate propensity score models (Table 3). The covariables included in the propensity score model included age and gender (added a priori) and four variables that were found to be confounders as defined above: CVC exposure (yes/no) within seven days of the first BDG level, receipt (yes/no) of any RBC transfusion during admission, inpatient corticosteroid steroid use (yes/no), and ECMO treatment (yes/no) during admission. Logistic regression was used as the propensity score model in which the exposure variable was regressed on the four confounding covariates as well as age and gender. The model equation achieved modest discrimination, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.69.

For the first approach, a 1:1 nearest neighbor match was implemented in which 16 CRRT-treated subjects were matched with 16 controls. However, we were unable to achieve balance for ECMO and gender (standardized mean difference >0.1). Logistic regression was performed on the matched dataset (n = 32) to estimate the effect of CRRT exposure on risk of false-positive BDG. For the second approach, we fitted a logistic regression model on the cohort of patients without IFI (n = 91), which included both the CRRT exposure variable and the estimated logit from the propensity score model. For the third approach, we used propensity scores to stratify patients into quintiles. We confirmed that confounding covariates were balanced within each quintile and fitted a logistic regression model, which included a main effect for the treatment variable and four design variables to capture quintile effects.

In all three models, the 1:1 match (n = 32), propensity score-adjusted model (n = 91), and quintile-adjusted model (n = 91), being on CRRT when the first BDG was obtained was not significantly associated with a false-positive BDG result (OR: 1.65, 95% CI 0.41–6.91, p = .48, OR: 1.75, 95% CI 0.49–6.27, p = .38, and OR: 1.78, 95% 0.51–6.34, p = .36, respectively).

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed in which, rather than the initial BDG level, we utilized the highest BDG level in the 18 patients with multiple BDG levels obtained. However, only two patients were reclassified from negative to positive BDG when analyzing the highest level rather than the first BDG level. In addition, these two patients had proven IFI (i.e., were reclassified from false-negative to true-positive BDG), which does not affect the analysis of false-positive BDG levels.

Discussion

In our study, CRRT exposure was not independently associated with an increased risk of false-positive BDG levels. This report represents the first study describing the influence of exposure to CRRT hemofilters specifically composed of AN 69 HF and PAES on BDG levels in critically ill patients. Prior studies have shown that exposure to unmodified cellulose dialyzers is associated with a substantial rate of false-positive BDG levels in patients without evidence of proven IFI, while conflicting results have been found in studies of different types of modified cellulose membrane filters [26–28]. It has been speculated that cellulose membranes, specifically the filling liquid found in these hollow fiber membranes, contain β-d-glucan, which may lead to blood contamination during hemodialysis [26,27]. Most previous studies analyzing the effect of exposure to synthetic membrane filters did not detect an influence on BDG levels. However, these studies were small, tested other hemofilters, and were specific to patients undergoing intermittent RRT in the outpatient setting [26–33]. This study – with 40 ICU patients undergoing CRRT and specifically 16 patients without proven IFI exposed to CRRT at the time of serum BDG measurement – represents one of the largest studies analyzing the effect of synthetic RRT membrane exposure on BDG levels in critically ill patients (Table 4). Our data suggest that, when adjusting for known risk factors for false-positive BDG or proven IFI, exposure to synthetic CRRT membranes composed of AN 69 HF or PAES is not independently associated with a higher risk of false-positive BDG results.

The kinetics of BDG in the human blood are poorly characterized. However, the biological half-life of BDG appears to be relatively short, on the order of hours, in both animal models [41–43] and human studies [26,44]. Although BDG clearance appears to be primarily hepatic (approximately 80%), a smaller component (approximately 10%) of clearance may be renal [43], but whether acute or chronic renal failure significantly affects BDG clearance in vivo remains unclear. Regardless, to the degree that exposure to synthetic hemodialysis membranes may induce false-positive BDG results, the mechanism of this effect remains unclear, as it would presumably not result from direct contamination of the bloodstream, as is postulated for the false-positive BDG elevation seen after exposure to cellulose-based hemodialyzers. Indeed, in at least one small human study, false-positive BDG was detected in the serum over a period of up to seven days after treatment with IHD using a synthetic (polysulfone) dialyzer [29]. Given the possibilities that exposure to synthetic membranes may somehow indirectly induce an elevation in serum BDG levels and that microparticles of such synthetic materials could plausibly persist in humans long after CRRT ends, we opted to analyze the effect of CRRT exposure over a series of different time frames. However, the effect of exposure to CRRT over 7 or 30 days prior to the first BDG appeared to be somewhat weaker (i.e., had lower OR) than the effect of being on CRRT at the time of BDG level. In addition, the effect on the risk of false-positive BDG with CRRT exposure was similar over all three time frames (i.e., statistically significant effect seen on univariable analysis that was no longer significant in multivariable analysis).

Critically ill patients requiring RRT are inherently at risk for IFI due to the need for a vascular catheter and immune dysfunction associated with acute or chronic kidney failure [45–47]. However, we observed a relatively low rate of proven IFI in our cohort (11.7% overall and 5.0% in patients treated with CRRT). Although the origin of false-positive BDG results in critically ill patients is not entirely known, false-positive BDG results have been reported in patients receiving BL/BLI antibiotics and in those with S. pneumoniae or P. aeruginosa bacteremia [21–24]. However, our study did not find an association between false-positive BDG results and treatment with BL/BLI antibiotics. Mennink-Kersten et al. found higher serum BDG levels in patients treated with amoxicillin–clavulanic acid but found no BDG reactivity in patients treated with piperacillin–tazobactam [48]. Notably, the intravenous amoxicillin–clavulanic acid formulation used in that study is not currently available in the U.S. and, therefore, was not evaluated in our study. Due to the low occurrence of bacteremia in our cohort, this variable was not included in our analyses; thus, we cannot draw any conclusions concerning this potential confounding factor. Additionally, any GI procedure or surgery that impairs the natural mucosal barrier and predisposes to translocation from the GI tract may predispose to IFI in patients who are colonized with C. albicans [25,49]. Our results were consistent with those previously reported findings.

Overall, this study reinforces the concept that, while a negative BDG has useful negative predictive value in patients with intermediate risk of IFI, a positive BDG test should not be the sole indicator to start antifungal therapy given the common presence of factors in the ICU that may lead to a false-positive result [49]. Our study demonstrates that a positive BDG test alone does not confirm the presence of a fungal infection with a low positive predictive value in unselected critically ill patients, and that, in such patients, further investigation is needed to confirm or exclude the presence of an IFI and the need for antifungal therapy. With the currently available data to date, the BDG assay is best reserved as an adjunctive test in patients with an intermediate-to-high risk of IFI, where potential risk factors for false-positive results, including exposure to RRT, have been considered, and in whom a negative BDG test has a useful negative predictive value. The interpretation of BDG levels in the context of CRRT should be performed cautiously, and clinical judgment should guide overall patient management. Although an incidental finding in our study, our sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated a lower false-negative rate when considering the highest rather than the first BDG level obtained, is consistent with data that serial BDG levels may be more useful than single BDG measurements for the detection of IFI [50,51].

The strengths of this study include its novelty in considering the effects of commonly used modern CRRT hemofilters, specifically in critically ill patients in whom BDG is commonly measured, and, compared to other similar studies, a larger sample size. However, our study has a series of important limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it may have been prone to selection bias and residual confounding. Second, although relatively large for a study of its kind, the sample size was nonetheless limited, and our inability to find an independent association between CRRT exposure and false-positive BDG results could be due to inadequate statistical power. Third, many of the assessed risk factors were highly correlated with each other (e.g., CVC and CRRT exposure). However, we conducted three separate propensity score analyses to attempt to control for the effect of these confounding variables. Fourth, although we performed a sensitivity analysis in which the highest BDG level was substituted for the first BDG level, as most of our cohort had only a single BDG level, we analyzed only one serum BDG sample for each patient. This is a limitation when considering the previous reports that BDG specificity increases with sequential sampling [50,51]. Fifth, given our limited overall sample size and the small number of patients specifically exposed to HF1000 hemofilters, we opted to pool all CRRT-exposed patients in our analysis to increase the statistical power of detecting any effect of CRRT membrane exposure. However, exposure to AN 69 HF and PAES membranes could result in different effects on BDG levels. Sixth, Fungitell is the only FDA-cleared BDG assay currently available; thus, the findings of this study may not be generalizable to other BDG assays available outside the U.S. Seventh, although several of our findings are consistent with those of previous studies, the generalizability of our results is inherently limited by the single-center nature of our study. Eighth, although we were able to collect data on the start and stop times of CRRT treatments with reasonable granularity, allowing for the detection of prolonged treatment interruptions, smaller interruptions in treatment time are not reliably charted in our electronic health records. As prior studies have consistently observed that CRRT downtime may account for up to 25–30% of prescribed CRRT time [52–54], our inability to capture all interruptions in CRRT with 100% fidelity represents a limitation in our certainty regarding which BDG levels were truly obtained while patients were on CRRT. However, this limitation is mitigated by the finding that the overall results of the study remain unchanged when considering CRRT exposure over a period of 7 or 30 days prior to the first BDG level. Finally, we were not able to include every potentially important covariable in our analysis, including notably IHD exposure prior to first BDG level.

Conclusions

In this single-center study of critically ill patients, although false-positive BDG levels were common in patients treated with CRRT, exposure to synthetic CRRT membranes composed of AN 69 HF or PAES was not independently associated with an increased risk of false-positive BDG levels. Pending more definitive data, the interpretation of BDG levels in the context of CRRT should be performed cautiously. Larger prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether CRRT exposure is associated with false-positive BDG levels in critically ill patients with suspected IFI.
