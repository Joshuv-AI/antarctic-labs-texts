Lipidomic dataset of optic nerves from the silicone oil–induced ocular hypertension mouse model
Authors: Zixuan Hao, Wenxuan Li, Isabella Moceri, Rui Ma, David Holden, Yuan Liu, Yu Hong, Sanjoy K. Bhattacharya, Richard K. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glaucoma is characterized by progressive degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and their optic-nerve axons, with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) as a major risk factor for retinal ganglion cell death. Lipids are essential to axonal structure and signalling, yet optic nerve lipidomic datasets under high IOP are limited. We present a stage-specific lipidomic dataset from a mouse model of silicone oil (SO)–induced ocular hypertension (high IOP) with contralateral balanced salt solution (BSS)–injected controls. Optic nerves from high-IOP eyes and matched contralateral controls were harvested at 2 weeks and 8 weeks post-injection to represent early and established optic nerve degeneration, with n = 6 per time point (3 males, 3 females). Lipids were extracted by a modified Bligh Dyer protocol and profiled by untargeted LC-MS/MS on a Vanquish Horizon ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) coupled to a Thermo Q Exactive Orbitrap. Raw files were processed in LipidSearch 5.1, and statistical analysis was conducted through MetaboAnalyst 6.0. This data is available through Metabolomics Workbench, Study ID: ST004446.

Background

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness, projected to affect around 112 million people worldwide by 2040 [1]. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) is the principal modifiable risk factor, and current therapies primarily lower IOP and indirectly protect or restore optic-nerve axons [2]. Lipids are fundamental to axonal structure and function: they define the plasma membrane and myelin, organize signalling microdomains, support energy metabolism, and serve as precursors of bioactive mediators that modulate central nervous system inflammation, degeneration, and repair [3]. However, lipidomic profiles of the optic nerve under high IOP are scarce. Here, we profile optic-nerve lipidomes in a mouse model of silicone-oil–induced ocular hypertension at 2 weeks (early stage) and 8 weeks (established stage). This dataset provides a stage-specific lipid reference for high-IOP–induced optic-nerve injury and enables exploratory nomination of lipid pathways and candidate therapeutic targets relevant to axonal degeneration.

Data Description

Lipid profiling of optic nerves from 5-month-old C57BL/6 J mice was performed at 2 and 8 weeks after silicone-oil injection, with BSS-injected controls. The experimental design and workflow are shown in Fig. 1. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured with a TonoLab rebound tonometer (Fig. 2A), and peripheral regions of flat-mounted retinas were imaged and quantified by two investigators masked to group assignment (Fig. 2B, C). Lipids were extracted using a modified Bligh and Dyer protocol with EquiSPLASH™ internal standards and analysed by reversed-phase HPLC coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer. Lipid identification and relative quantification were performed in LipidSearch 5.1, and data processing and statistical analyses were conducted in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Group separation was visualized with partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA, Fig. 3A, B), and variable-importance-in-projection (VIP) scores plot was used to rank discriminant lipids, highlighting the top 15 species (Fig. 3C, D). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Animals

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the University of Miami Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Twelve 5-month-old C57BL/6 J mice (6 males, 6 females) were used in this study. Mice were assigned to 2-week (n = 6) or 8-week (n = 6) post-injection endpoints. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) was induced by slowly injecting 2–3 µl of sterile silicone oil into the anterior chamber of the left eye until the chamber was filled, taking care to avoid damage to the lens and iris [4]. The contralateral eye received an equal volume (2–3 µl) of sterile balanced salt solution in the anterior chamber and served as the injection control. Following injection, ophthalmic ointment containing erythromycin was applied to prevent corneal and conjunctival wound infection. At indicated time-points, mice were euthanized, and optic nerves were immediately dissected out, and then flash frozen on dry ice and stored at −80 °C until lipid extraction.

IOP measurement

IOP was measured with a tonometer (Tonolab Colonial Medical Supply, Franconia, NH) [5]. The tonometer was fixed in a vertical position to a support stand with clamps. Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (15 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally. Pupils were dilated, and measurements were obtained approximately 15 min after dilation. The Tonolab tonometer takes five measurements, eliminates high and low readings and calculates an average intraocular pressure measurement.

Retinal ganglion cells quantification

At 2- and 8-week post-injection, eyeballs were enucleated and post fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 2 h. After fixation, retinas were dissected from the globes. Retinas were subjected to three cycles of freeze–thaw, then washed extensively in 1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, repeated three times. Retinas were blocked with Rodent Blocking Buffer (RBR962, Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature. The primary antibody, anti-RBPMS (1:400; ABN1362; Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), was diluted in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100. Floating retinas were incubated with primary antibodies for 7 days at 4 °C, followed by three 10-minute washes with PBS. A donkey anti-rabbit Cy3 secondary antibody (1:500; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) was applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by three 10-min PBS washes. Tissues were mounted in VectaShield (Vector Laboratories) fluorescent mounting medium containing DAPI (Vector Laboratories H-1200). Imaging was performed on a Leica TCS AOBS SP5 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Exton, PA, USA). Twelve fields of peripheral retina per retina were imaged with a 63 × objective. Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) were manually counted by two independent, masked observers, and counts were averaged.

Lipid extraction

Lipids were extracted from optic nerves using a modified Bligh and Dyer method [6]. The lower organic phase containing lipids was collected and dried in a Speed-Vac (Model 7810,014; Labconco, Kansas City, MO). To prevent lipid oxidation, the tubes were flushed with argon gas before storage. The upper aqueous phase was reserved for protein quantification using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (cat# ab102536, Abcam, USA). The 2-week and 8-week cohorts were extracted on different days (two extraction batches) using identical solvents, volumes, and timing; sample order was randomized within each batch, and all downstream comparisons were performed within timepoint to avoid cross-batch bias.

High performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry

Dried lipid samples were reconstituted in 49 µl of isopropanol–acetonitrile 1:1 (v/v) with 1 µl of EquiSPLASH™ LIPIDOMIX® Quantitative Mass Spec Internal Standard (330,731), followed by 15 min in an ultrasonic water bath. Samples were split into two separate vials containing 25 µl each, one for positive mode and one for negative mode, with each one containing 2 µg/ml internal standard. Reverse-phase chromatographic separation was performed using a Vanquish Horizon UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) equipped with an Accucore Vanquish C18+ column. The Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was operated under heated electrospray ionization (HESI). Data was collected in positive and negative modes for each sample. Mobile phase A was 50% acetonitrile, 50% water, 5 mM ammonium formate, and 0.1% formic acid. Mobile phase B was 88% isopropanol, 10% acetonitrile, 2% water, 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid.

Ionization and detection were performed with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Data was collected in both positive and negative modes for each sample. Spray voltage was 4.0 kV in positive mode and 2.50 kV in negative mode. For positive and negative mode, sheath gas flow rate was 35, auxiliary gas flow rate was 15, and sweep gas was 0. Capillary temperature was 325 and S-lens RF level was 70. The full scan range was 250 to 1200 m/z, resolution was 70,000 and microscans was 1. Automatic gain control (AGC) target was 1e6 and maximum inject time was 100 ms. In dd-MS2, the resolution was 17,500, AGC target was 1e5, loop count was 10, and isolation window was 1.0m/z. Normalized collision energy (NCE) was set to 20, 30, 40, intensity threshold was 2.0e4, and dynamic exclusion was 8.0 s.

Lipid identification and statistical analysis

The lipids were identified and quantified from the *.RAW scans using LipidSearch 5.1 software (Thermo) with M-score 2 and the predefined product search module for Q-Exactive. Quantification used class-specific deuterated internal standards (EquiSPLASH) with per-class IS scaling and protein concentration from upper phase of extraction. ESI+ and ESI− data were processed independently and merged at the lipid-species level for downstream analysis. Data matrices were log transformed (base 10) and autoscaled in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (mean-centered, divided by SD). Analyses were one-factor contrasts (SO vs BSS) performed within each timepoint (2 weeks or 8 weeks).

Limitations

LC–MS/MS acquisitions for the 2- and 8-week cohorts were performed on different days as separate batches, and pooled-QC samples were not acquired. Despite identical sample preparation, chromatography, and MS settings with class-specific internal-standard normalization, residual batch effects may persist.

Ethics Statement

This study utilized animals (mouse, encompassing both genders) only. All experiments were performed in compliance with the U.S. National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals adhering to ARVO Statement for use of animals in vision research. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Miami (Protocol ID: IPROTO202200000022). The sex of the mouse is not known to influence or have an association with optic nerve neuron degeneration.
