Immune checkpoint inhibitors in thymic epithelial tumors: current evidence, immune-related toxicity, and perioperative considerations—a narrative review
Authors: Qixuan Wang, Yuan Xu, Hongsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveThymic epithelial tumors (TETs) are rare mediastinal malignancies with unique immunobiological features related to thymic immune tolerance. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown clinical activity, especially in thymic carcinoma, their use is limited by frequent and sometimes severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs), particularly in thymoma. This review summarizes current evidence on the efficacy, toxicity, biomarkers, and perioperative implications of ICIs in TETs.MethodsA narrative review was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase for English-language studies on ICIs in TETs published from database inception to September 2025. Reference lists of relevant articles and guideline papers were also manually screened. Prospective trials, retrospective cohorts, guideline papers, and key mechanistic studies were prioritized, with emphasis on histology-specific efficacy, irAEs, biomarkers, and perioperative application.Key Content and FindingsCurrent evidence suggests that ICIs have more consistent antitumor activity in thymic carcinoma than in thymoma. In contrast, thymoma carries a substantially higher risk of severe early irAEs, particularly myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis overlap syndromes. Biomarkers such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and immune-related signatures remain promising but are not yet sufficiently validated for routine patient selection. Perioperative immunotherapy is an emerging strategy, but current evidence remains limited and safety concerns are significant.ConclusionsICIs represent a promising treatment option for selected patients with advanced TETs, particularly thymic carcinoma, but their clinical use remains restricted by toxicity and limited evidence. Future progress will depend on better risk stratification, biomarker-guided selection, and safer multidisciplinary integration.Keywords: Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), thymoma, thymic carcinoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), immune-related adverse events (irAEs)

Introduction

Clinical background of thymic epithelial tumors

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), including thymoma and thymic carcinoma, are rare and heterogeneous malignancies of the anterior mediastinum (1,2). Thymoma is more often associated with paraneoplastic autoimmunity, particularly myasthenia gravis, whereas thymic carcinoma usually shows more aggressive behavior, a higher likelihood of advanced-stage presentation, and a poorer prognosis (1). Complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for resectable disease and offers the best chance of long-term disease control (1,3). For locally advanced tumors, multimodality treatment with induction chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or postoperative therapy may be considered according to histology, stage, completeness of resection, and multidisciplinary assessment (4,5).

For unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease, systemic treatment options remain limited. Platinum-based chemotherapy is commonly used as first-line treatment, but durable disease control is not achieved in many patients (6,7). Later-line strategies, including anti-angiogenic agents and other targeted approaches, have shown activity in selected patients, particularly in thymic carcinoma, but their benefits are still limited by disease heterogeneity and the lack of robust predictive biomarkers (8,9). These therapeutic gaps have created a rationale for exploring immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), especially in thymic carcinoma, where programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and immune-cell infiltration are frequently observed (10,11). However, the same thymic immune biology that makes TETs immunologically distinctive also creates an unusually high risk of immune-related toxicity, making immunotherapy a promising but potentially hazardous treatment strategy (12).

Why immunotherapy matters in advanced and recurrent TETs

Beyond the therapeutic limitations of conventional systemic therapy, clinical studies have shown that approximately 15–25% of patients with previously treated advanced thymic carcinoma achieve objective responses to programmed death 1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy, with higher response rates reported in selected combination regimens (13,14). However, the thymic origin of these tumors makes immunotherapy fundamentally different from its use in most other thoracic malignancies (15). Because the thymus is central to T-cell development and immune tolerance, treatment benefit must be weighed against an unusually high risk of severe autoimmunity (16).

Objective of this review

In TETs, the relevance of immunotherapy extends beyond tumor response (17). Because surgery remains the main curative treatment and is still considered after induction therapy in selected locally advanced cases, the practical value of ICIs depends on whether they improve disease control without narrowing the window for safe resection (1,3). This is particularly important in thymoma, where myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis-like syndromes may abruptly alter perioperative risk (18). Against this background, this review summarizes the current evidence on efficacy, toxicity, biomarkers, and perioperative application of ICIs in TETs, with particular attention to the different clinical implications in thymoma and thymic carcinoma (19,20). We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0993/rc).

Methods

Literature search strategy

A narrative review was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase to identify English-language studies on ICIs in TETs published through September 2025. Reference lists of relevant original articles, reviews, and guideline papers were also manually screened. The search focused on terms related to TETs, PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, immune-related adverse events (irAEs), biomarkers, and perioperative immunotherapy.

Study selection and evidence focus

Because randomized evidence in TETs remains limited, this review was designed to summarize the most clinically informative literature rather than provide a formal quantitative synthesis. Priority was given to prospective clinical trials, multi-institutional retrospective cohorts, major guideline or consensus papers, and representative translational studies relevant to four themes: immunobiology, clinical efficacy, immune-related toxicity, and biomarkers or perioperative application. Case reports and small case series were also considered when they provided clinically relevant information on rare but severe irAEs, perioperative complications, or emerging perioperative use of ICIs in TETs. Particular attention was paid to differences between thymoma and thymic carcinoma, severe neuromuscular-cardiac toxicities, and issues likely to influence treatment sequencing or later surgical management. Why TETs are a unique challenge for thoracic surgeons: immunobiology and autoimmunity

Thymic origin and defective central tolerance

The thymus is central to T-cell development and immune tolerance (16). During normal thymic maturation, immature thymocytes undergo positive and negative selection within a highly organized epithelial microenvironment, and medullary thymic epithelial cells help eliminate autoreactive clones through the expression of tissue-restricted antigens under the control of autoimmune regulator (AIRE) and related regulators (16). This biological background helps explain why TETs differ from most other thoracic malignancies (2,21). In thymoma, disruption of normal thymic architecture, altered epithelial differentiation, reduced tolerance-related signaling, and persistence of immature thymocyte populations may impair negative selection and allow autoreactive T cells to escape into the periphery (12). This provides a plausible explanation for the close association between thymoma and autoimmune disorders such as myasthenia gravis, pure red cell aplasia, and hypogammaglobulinemia (12). Thymic carcinoma, although still arising from thymic epithelium, is less dominated by thymopoietic features and is less commonly associated with classic paraneoplastic autoimmunity (22).

Histology-specific immune contexture: thymoma versus thymic carcinoma

The immune microenvironment of TETs is strongly shaped by histological subtype (22). Thymoma often retains features of the native thymic milieu, including abundant immature T cells and a microenvironment influenced by disordered thymopoiesis (22). In lower-grade thymomas, the presence of immature CD4+CD8+ thymocytes and ectopic lymphoid elements may reflect persistence of thymus-like immune architecture rather than an effective antitumor response (22). By contrast, thymic carcinoma more often shows higher PD-L1 expression, a greater proportion of mature tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and a more conventional exhausted or suppressive immune phenotype (10,22). These differences help explain why checkpoint blockade appears biologically plausible in both entities, yet clinically more effective in thymic carcinoma and more hazardous in thymoma (Figure 1) (23,24).

Clinical implications: efficacy in thymic carcinoma, toxicity in thymoma

These biological differences have direct clinical consequences (22). In thymic carcinoma, PD-L1 expression, immune infiltration, and prospective trial data together support a meaningful, although still limited, role for ICIs (13,14). In thymoma, the same treatment may produce tumor regression in selected cases, but this potential benefit is counterbalanced by a much higher risk of severe early immune toxicity (25). The practical problem is therefore not simply whether ICIs can work in TETs, but in which subtype, in which clinical setting, and at what cost (26). This distinction becomes especially important when immunotherapy is considered before possible surgery, because treatment-related myocarditis, myositis, or myasthenic worsening may narrow or even eliminate the opportunity for safe resection (17).

irAEs in TETs: why they are different and why surgeons should care

The distinctive irAE spectrum in TETs

irAEs are seen across many solid tumors treated with checkpoint blockade, but the toxicity pattern in TETs is unusually distinctive in both type and severity (27,28). Common irAEs observed with ICIs include dermatologic toxicity, diarrhea or colitis, hepatitis or transaminitis, pneumonitis, and endocrine dysfunction. In TETs, however, special attention is required for severe neuromuscular and cardiac toxicities, particularly myositis, myocarditis, and myasthenia gravis-like syndromes. These events may occur early, evolve rapidly, and involve more than one organ system at the same time (18).

This pattern is closely linked to the thymic origin of these tumors (16). Because TETs arise in an organ that normally governs central immune tolerance, checkpoint blockade may unmask or amplify pre-existing immune dysregulation in a way that differs from most non-thymic malignancies (12). As a result, toxicity in TETs is not simply a treatment-related side effect; it is partly shaped by the underlying biology of the disease itself (12). Myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis overlap syndrome

Among irAEs reported in TETs, the combination of myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis-like manifestations is the most feared (18). These toxicities may occur together as an overlap syndrome, creating a particularly dangerous clinical scenario (18). Patients may present with myalgia, proximal weakness, ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, dyspnea, arrhythmia, chest discomfort, or unexplained biomarker elevation, and deterioration may occur over a short period of time (18). In some cases, these events have developed after only a single dose of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy (18).

This overlap syndrome is biologically consistent with the immune background of TETs (16). Defective central tolerance may allow autoreactive T cells to persist, while shared antigenic features between tumor tissue and striated muscle or cardiac tissue may promote immune cross-reactivity after checkpoint inhibition (18). Clinically, the relevance is obvious: myocarditis may compromise hemodynamic stability, myositis may impair respiratory muscle function, and myasthenic symptoms may lead to aspiration risk or prolonged ventilatory support (18,29). When these toxicities coexist, surgery often has to be delayed or reconsidered (17).

Timing, early warning signs, and high-risk populations

A major challenge of irAEs in TETs is their timing (18). Severe toxicities often occur early, sometimes within the first few weeks of treatment and occasionally after the initial dose (18). This is clinically important because the first treatment cycles often overlap with reassessment of response, operability, and further treatment planning (17).

Early warning signs may be subtle (18). Fatigue, myalgia, ptosis, dysphagia, new dyspnea, or modest elevations in creatine kinase or troponin should not be dismissed lightly in this setting, because they may represent the first signal of evolving myositis, myocarditis, or neuromuscular involvement (18,29). High-risk features include thymoma, pre-existing myasthenia gravis, other autoimmune manifestations, neuromuscular symptoms, and abnormal baseline cardiac or muscle biomarkers (12). In these patients, even apparently limited immunotherapy exposure may carry a narrow safety margin (25).

Practical implications for preoperative evaluation and postoperative safety

The perioperative implications of irAEs in TETs begin before surgery (17). Assessment should not be limited to staging and routine cardiopulmonary evaluation, but should also include focused questioning about ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, fluctuating weakness, exercise intolerance, and other symptoms suggestive of neuromuscular involvement (28). Baseline creatine kinase, high-sensitivity troponin, and electrocardiography may help identify patients who need further neurology or cardiology evaluation before either immunotherapy or surgery (30).

After ICI exposure, operative timing becomes a major issue (17). A patient may show stable or improved imaging findings and still be unsafe for surgery if myocarditis, myositis, or myasthenic worsening is evolving (17). Conversely, waiting too long may risk disease progression or increase technical difficulty because of treatment-related fibrosis and adhesions (31). Postoperatively, prior neuromuscular or cardiac toxicity may translate into slower recovery of ventilation, greater aspiration risk, arrhythmia, or increased ICU needs (28). These issues are often more relevant to decision-making than formal toxicity grade alone (30).

Multidisciplinary management of severe irAEs in surgical candidates

Once severe irAEs are suspected in a patient with TETs, management should be prompt and multidisciplinary (28). High-dose corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of initial treatment, but in cases of suspected myocarditis, myositis, or myasthenic overlap, escalation to intravenous immunoglobulin, plasmapheresis, or additional immunosuppressive therapy may be required without delay (30). Cardiology, neurology, intensive care, and anesthesia input should be obtained early, because deterioration can be rapid and single-organ management is often insufficient (30).

If surgery is still being considered after toxicity control, reassessment should focus on functional recovery rather than symptom improvement alone (17). The key question is whether neuromuscular and cardiac status have improved enough to allow anesthesia and major mediastinal surgery safely (28). In some patients, the safest approach will be to abandon perioperative immunotherapy and adopt another treatment sequence; in others, surgery may still be feasible after stabilization (31). These decisions require joint reassessment rather than parallel decision-making. Current role of ICIs in unresectable, recurrent, and metastatic TETs

PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy

PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy has the most mature prospective evidence in TETs, particularly in thymic carcinoma (13). Pembrolizumab remains the best-studied agent (13). In the phase II study of previously treated thymic carcinoma, pembrolizumab achieved an objective response rate of 22.5%, with durable benefit in a subset of patients, but severe autoimmune toxicity occurred in 15%, including myocarditis in 5% (13). In the later phase II trial that enrolled both thymic carcinoma and thymoma, responses were seen in both groups, but toxicity differed clearly by histology: thymoma patients had a substantially higher incidence of clinically significant irAEs (24).

Nivolumab has shown a more modest activity profile (32,33). In the PRIMER study, no Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) responses were observed in thymic carcinoma, although durable stable disease occurred in a subset (32). In NIVOTHYM, nivolumab produced an objective response rate of 12% and a median progression-free survival of about 6 months in pretreated B3 thymoma and thymic carcinoma (33). Taken together, these studies suggest that PD-1 blockade has a reproducible but limited role in advanced TETs, with the clearest signal in thymic carcinoma and a much narrower therapeutic window in thymoma (33). PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy

The clinical dataset for PD-L1 inhibitors is smaller, but still informative (25). Avelumab is particularly notable because it demonstrated both biological activity and the narrow therapeutic window of immunotherapy in TETs (25). In relapsed thymoma, confirmed partial responses were observed, but all responders developed irAEs requiring immunosuppressive treatment (25). These findings show that tumor regression in thymoma does not necessarily translate into a favorable clinical risk-benefit profile (25).

Atezolizumab has provided prospective evidence in thymic carcinoma. In a multicenter Chinese phase II study, atezolizumab achieved an objective response rate of 14.7% and a median progression-free survival of 3.2 months in previously treated advanced thymic carcinoma. Notably, the objective response rate was 40.0% among patients with PD-L1-positive tumors, supporting the biological relevance of PD-L1 blockade in this subtype. However, immune toxicity and treatment discontinuation remained important limitations (14).

Combination strategies with anti-angiogenic agents or chemotherapy

Combination regimens have produced the strongest efficacy signals reported so far in TETs (34,35). The rationale is biologically plausible: anti-angiogenic agents may reduce myeloid suppression and improve T-cell trafficking, whereas chemotherapy may enhance antigen release and immune priming (36). In practice, these approaches appear more active than monotherapy, especially in thymic carcinoma and selected B3 thymoma populations, although added efficacy is accompanied by added complexity in toxicity assessment (37).

In CAVEATT, avelumab plus axitinib achieved an objective response rate of 34% in platinum-refractory B3 thymoma and thymic carcinoma, with serious new-onset irAEs reported in 12% of patients (34). In PECATI, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab met its primary endpoint, with a 5-month progression-free survival of 88.4% in heavily pretreated metastatic B3 thymoma and thymic carcinoma, although grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events occurred in 37% (36). These studies suggest that the ceiling of ICI activity in TETs may be higher when rational combinations are used, but toxicity remains substantial (38).

Chemo-immunotherapy is another important direction (39). In MARBLE, first-line atezolizumab combined with carboplatin and paclitaxel produced an objective response rate of 56% in advanced or recurrent thymic carcinoma, with most grade 3 or higher events being hematologic and chemotherapy-related (35). These results support further exploration of combination regimens earlier in the disease course (39). At the same time, whether higher radiographic response rates will translate into better resectability, safer induction strategies, or improved perioperative outcomes remains uncertain (17).

Emerging perioperative evidence and its limitations

Most ICI data in TETs still come from unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic settings, but perioperative experience has started to emerge. Recent prospective evaluation of perioperative pembrolizumab-based treatment in locally advanced TETs suggested encouraging major pathologic response and R0 resection rates in selected patients (17). Real-world series have also reported relatively high complete resection rates after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, particularly in thymic carcinoma (31).

These findings suggest that ICI-based treatment may have potential as part of induction or perioperative strategies in carefully selected patients (17,31). However, the evidence remains early and should be interpreted cautiously. Available studies are small, heterogeneous, and vulnerable to selection bias, and patients at high autoimmune risk are often excluded before treatment. Therefore, current perioperative evidence supports feasibility in selected patients but does not justify routine adoption outside experienced multidisciplinary settings or clinical trials.

Key differences in efficacy between thymoma and thymic carcinoma

Current evidence supports a strongly subtype-dependent interpretation of immunotherapy in TETs (26). Thymic carcinoma has shown the clearest and most reproducible efficacy signal, with monotherapy response rates generally around 15% to 25% and higher activity emerging in selected combination regimens (13,14). Although these data are not yet sufficient to redefine standard treatment broadly, they support continued development of ICI-based strategies in this subtype (38).

Thymoma remains more difficult (25). Tumor responses have been documented, which indicates that checkpoint blockade can be biologically active, but the unusually high frequency of severe and early irAEs substantially limits its clinical usefulness (24). As a result, thymoma and thymic carcinoma should not be discussed as equivalent immunotherapy settings (38). In practical terms, the question is not only whether treatment can reduce tumor burden, but whether it can do so without narrowing the chance for safe subsequent management, including surgery when relevant (17). Histology, baseline autoimmune risk, and treatment sequencing therefore need to be considered together before ICIs are introduced (1).

Biomarkers for patient selection: from response prediction to surgical risk stratification

Biomarkers of response: PD-L1 and tumor immune context

Among currently available biomarkers, PD-L1 expression is the most accessible and the most consistently studied in TETs (10,40). In thymic carcinoma, higher PD-L1 expression appears to enrich for response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, although the association is not uniform and is insufficient for stand-alone treatment selection (10,40). Beyond PD-L1, the broader immune context may also matter (10). Thymic carcinoma more often shows a mature, inflamed, or exhausted immune phenotype that is interpretable within the framework used for other solid tumors, whereas thymoma frequently contains abundant lymphoid elements that reflect disordered thymopoiesis rather than an effective antitumor response (22,23). Genomic features may also contribute to heterogeneity, but their current value remains exploratory (41,42). In practice, these markers can help frame the probability of benefit, but none has yet reached the level required for routine decisive use (10,43).

Toxicity-risk assessment: clinical phenotype, baseline autoimmunity, and early monitoring

In TETs, biomarkers are clinically important not only for estimating the probability of response, but also for identifying patients at increased risk of severe toxicity (10,12). At present, the most useful risk indicators remain clinical rather than molecular. Thymoma histology, pre-existing myasthenia gravis, other autoimmune manifestations, fluctuating neuromuscular symptoms, bulbar symptoms, and a history suggestive of baseline immune dysregulation should all be regarded as warning signs before ICI treatment is considered (12,18).

A pragmatic pretreatment assessment should include histological subtype, autoimmune history, focused evaluation for ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, dyspnea, fluctuating weakness, and exercise intolerance, as well as baseline creatine kinase, high-sensitivity troponin, and electrocardiography. Neurology or cardiology consultation should be considered for patients with known myasthenia gravis, suspicious neuromuscular symptoms, abnormal cardiac or skeletal muscle biomarkers, or electrocardiographic abnormalities (28,30).

Risk assessment should continue during the first treatment cycles because many severe irAEs in TETs develop early. Serial clinical review together with repeat creatine kinase, troponin, and electrocardiography may be more useful than relying on pretreatment markers alone (18,28). More sophisticated approaches, including circulating tumor DNA, peripheral immune profiling, and tissue-based immune signatures, remain promising but investigational (10,44). Until such markers are validated, treatment decisions should integrate expected antitumor benefit with baseline autoimmune risk and early toxicity signals.

Perioperative considerations and surgical integration

Patient selection, timing, and operative concerns

At present, perioperative ICI-based treatment in TETs should be considered only in selected clinical settings rather than as a routine extension of systemic therapy (17,45). The most reasonable candidates are patients with unresectable, borderline-resectable, or locally advanced thymic carcinoma in whom induction treatment may improve the likelihood of complete resection or durable local control (17,31). By contrast, the threshold should remain much higher in clearly resectable disease, particularly in thymoma, where the potential perioperative cost of immune toxicity may outweigh uncertain oncologic benefit (24).

The clearest high-risk group includes patients with thymoma accompanied by active or poorly controlled myasthenia gravis, fluctuating neuromuscular symptoms, prior severe autoimmune manifestations, or baseline evidence of cardiac or skeletal muscle vulnerability. Patients with unexplained elevations in creatine kinase or troponin, conduction abnormalities on electrocardiography, or symptoms suspicious for occult myocarditis, myositis, or bulbar weakness should also be approached with extreme caution (12,18). In practice, the question is not only whether immunotherapy might improve radiographic response, but whether that potential gain justifies the possibility of narrowing the window for safe surgery.

Operative timing remains insufficiently standardized (17,31). In principle, surgery should proceed after meaningful oncologic benefit has been achieved but before immune toxicity, fibrosis, adhesions, or clinical deterioration begin to narrow the margin of safety. Unlike conventional induction chemotherapy, immunotherapy may be followed by delayed radiographic change, ongoing immune activation, or evolving myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenic symptoms even after treatment has stopped (17). In addition, induction treatment may alter tissue planes, increase adhesions, and complicate dissection around mediastinal structures, particularly when vascular, pericardial, or phrenic nerve involvement is present (31).

Practical multidisciplinary assessment before and after surgery

Assessment should begin before ICI treatment rather than after induction has already been completed. Histology, stage, and technical resectability need to be defined as precisely as possible at baseline, ideally with thoracic surgeon involvement when later resection remains a possibility (1,17). At the same time, immune-risk assessment should include autoimmune history, myasthenic or bulbar symptoms, exertional intolerance, creatine kinase, high-sensitivity troponin, and electrocardiography. Neurology consultation should be obtained for patients with known myasthenia gravis or suspected neuromuscular symptoms, and cardiology consultation should be considered when troponin, creatine kinase, or electrocardiographic findings are abnormal (28,30).

During treatment, response evaluation should not rely on imaging alone. Radiographic shrinkage is meaningful only if it is accompanied by preserved cardiomuscular stability and the absence of evolving severe irAEs. Before surgery, the key question is not only whether the tumor has become technically removable, but whether the patient has recovered sufficiently from treatment exposure to tolerate anesthesia and major mediastinal surgery. Recent ICI exposure should also prompt closer postoperative attention to respiratory insufficiency, aspiration risk, arrhythmia, delayed ventilatory recovery, and increased intensive care needs in patients with possible neuromuscular or cardiac toxicity (28,30). Unresolved questions and future directions

Several questions remain unresolved (46). It is still unclear which patients should receive ICI-based induction rather than conventional chemotherapy, how long treatment should continue before re-evaluation, whether postoperative continuation adds benefit after complete resection, and how pathologic response should be incorporated into decision-making (17,47). Future trials should address not only response, but also endpoints directly relevant to perioperative care, including conversion to resectability, R0 resection rate, treatment-related delay of surgery, severe irAE-related surgical cancellation, postoperative morbidity, and long-term disease control (47,48).

Histology-specific enrollment will be particularly important, because pooling thymoma and thymic carcinoma too broadly risks obscuring clinically meaningful differences. Future perioperative studies should also incorporate standardized baseline autoimmune assessment, early neuromuscular-cardiac monitoring, predefined surgical timing criteria, and multidisciplinary stopping rules for suspected severe irAEs. Until such evidence becomes available, perioperative ICI use in TETs should remain selective, histology-aware, and restricted to experienced multidisciplinary settings or clinical trials (46,47).

Strengths and limitations of this review

This review brings together current evidence on ICIs in TETs across immunobiology, clinical efficacy, toxicity, biomarkers, and perioperative use, while keeping thymoma and thymic carcinoma distinct throughout the discussion (49,50). Its main limitation is the evidence base itself: most available data come from small phase I/II studies, retrospective cohorts, and selected case reports, with substantial heterogeneity in histology, treatment setting, and toxicity reporting (26). Biomarker studies and perioperative data remain particularly immature (10). The conclusions should therefore be read as a clinically oriented summary of evolving evidence rather than as definitive practice guidance (49).

Conclusions

ICIs have introduced a meaningful treatment option for selected patients with advanced TETs, with the clearest efficacy signal in thymic carcinoma (13,14). Their role in thymoma remains much more limited because severe early immune toxicity is a major constraint (24,25). Current evidence supports a selective, histology-aware approach rather than broad empirical use (26). Future progress will depend on better risk stratification, more reliable biomarkers, and safer integration of immunotherapy into multimodality care, particularly when surgery is still part of the treatment plan (1,49).
