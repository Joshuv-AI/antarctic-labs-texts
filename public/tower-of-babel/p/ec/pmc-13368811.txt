Measuring bio-psycho-social frailty in older adults across multiple surveys and indicators: evidence from Italy for public health policy
Authors: Tommaso Aicardi, Elisabetta Listorti, Benedetta Pongiglione
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting and measuring frailty is essential to inform health policy. Numerous frailty tools exist, but their application to population data remains limited. This study focuses on Italy and adopts a policymaker’s perspective to identify practical, nationally applicable tools for bio-psycho-social frailty assessment using existing survey data. Three validated bio-psycho-social indicators—the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), the Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), and the Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS)—were operationalized across four population surveys (SHARE, EU-SILC, EHIS, and AVQ). The study proceeded in three steps: (i) reconstruction of each indicator within each dataset to assess items’ replicability; (ii) validation of reproducible indicators by examining their psychometric properties; (iii) comparison of prevalence and predictive validity across indicators to illustrate their relative strengths and limitations. The GFI was the most replicable across surveys, while the EFS showed the strongest predictive ability. Among datasets, EHIS emerged as the most suitable platform, offering higher indicators’ reproducibility and strong validity. Regional analyses revealed a clear North–South gradient, and regression models confirmed that frailty adds explanatory power for health outcomes beyond chronological age and other sociodemographic characteristics. The study demonstrates that bio-psycho-social frailty can be measured using existing national surveys, providing a low-cost and reproducible framework for national monitoring. By highlighting the added value of frailty over age in predicting health needs, it lays the groundwork for integrating frailty assessment into public health planning and supporting more equitable and effective policy responses to population ageing.

Introduction

The increasing number and proportion of people aged 60 years and older poses unprecedented, yet unavoidable, societal challenges. One of the greatest challenges is the increasing pressure on healthcare systems, particularly in primary care and long-term care services [1]. Frailty is widely recognized as one of the most critical and problematic manifestations of population ageing [2]. Frailer individuals have been documented to report worse health trajectories [3] characterized by cognitive impairment [4], limitations in basic activities of daily living and a higher use of healthcare services, such as emergency admissions [5] and hospitalizations [6].

The widespread awareness of the limited resources available to deal with increasing healthcare demand calls on policymakers to strengthen the adoption of evidence-informed policies. In this regard, evidence from frailty research has not yet been fully translated into health care policy [7], despite its potential to diminish the impact of ageing on health care systems and strengthen their sustainability. This makes frailty an urgent focal point for health policy development [8]. In fact, frailty represents a valuable opportunity to disentangle people that, albeit sharing the same chronological age, may differ in their ageing process due to their level of frailty, and consequent healthcare needs [9].

The main challenge in monitoring frailty lies in its non-observable nature: it stems from the interaction of multiple physical and psychosocial factors; and it often overlaps with related conditions such as disability and comorbidity [10]. There is not a universally agreed-upon definition. The first and most common interpretation of frailty is the medical or physical frailty model, which defines frailty primarily as a biological syndrome of decreased physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors and associated with ageing [11]. Over time, alongside the predominant adoption of the phenotypic model, the conceptual development of frailty has been shaped by the deficit accumulation model introduced by Rockwood and Mitnitski as well as by the multidimensional construct, encompassing physical, psychological, sensory, and social dimensions that collectively contribute to adverse health outcomes advanced by Gobbens [12, 13].

Detection and measurement of frailty are considered crucial both for providing appropriate, goal-directed care to frail older adults [2, 3] and for informing healthcare policy. Early identification allows policymakers to plan preventive actions and design targeted interventions to mitigate frail individuals’ disproportionate use of services [14]. Reflecting this importance, numerous literature reviews have mapped the wide range of frailty measurement tools [15–19]. Existing tools differ not only in the paradigm adopted, but also in the level of information required and the settings in which they are implemented (e.g. primary care, hospitals, or community). As a result, estimates of frailty prevalence in older adults vary widely across studies.

Our study focuses on the case of Italy, one of the countries with highest proportion of older people, which shows frailty prevalence estimates around 15%–16% [20]. We embrace the perspective of healthcare policy makers with the aim of identifying practical, nationally applicable tools to measure frailty using nationally representative surveys. Specifically, we aim to assess how existing data sources can be leveraged to support the monitoring of frailty.

We adopt a bio-psycho-social conceptualization of frailty. While similar frameworks have previously been proposed in the Italian context, existing studies have generally suffered from limited sample coverage, lack of representativeness at the national level, or the use of dated information [10]. This study seeks to address these gaps and assesses whether existing nationally representative survey data can be used to measure and monitor bio-psycho-social frailty in the community-dwelling older population. By operationalizing multiple validated frailty indicators across various national and international (covering Italy) surveys, we evaluate their respective strengths and limitations and develop a comparative framework that highlights how prevalence estimates vary depending on the measurement tool and survey used. This approach not only advances methodological clarity, but also provides policymakers with practical and low-cost instruments for surveillance and planning as recently advocated [21].

Methods

Data and samples

Data were extracted from four main sources: one international survey, two international surveys with national implementation, and one national survey. These sources were selected for their national coverage and because each encompasses, to varying extents, domains of physical and psychological health as well as social life. They also offer the potential to link frailty with other domains, such as service utilization, socioeconomic conditions, and living arrangements. Finally, all sources are designed to generate reliable evidence to inform policy development. For the purposes of this study, the analytic sample was restricted to respondents aged 65 years and older.

The Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) is a multidisciplinary and cross-national panel database of micro data on health, socioeconomic status, and social and family networks of individuals aged 50 or older [22]. We used the ninth wave, carried out in 2022, and selected respondents aged 65 and above and resident in Italy, corresponding to 2825 respondents.

The European Health Interview Survey (EHIS) is a harmonized survey conducted across EU member states that collects self-reported information on health status, health determinants, and healthcare use [23]. We drew data from the 2019 wave of EHIS Italy, which included 13 719 respondents aged 65 and older.

The European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC) is an annual survey providing harmonized microdata on income, poverty, social exclusion, and living conditions across EU member states [24]. We used the 2022 cross-sectional EU-SILC data for Italy, including ad hoc modules on health and quality of life. The dataset comprised 15 462 respondents aged 65 and older.

The Aspetti della Vita Quotidiana (AVQ, Aspects of Daily Life) survey is conducted annually by the Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT), to collect information on daily-life habits, health status, and access to services among the Italian population [25]. We used data from the 2022 AVQ survey, corresponding to 10 991 respondents aged 65 and older.

Frailty indicators

We selected three indicators of bio-psycho-social frailty from among the many measures available [15–19]. The selection was guided by four criteria: (i) the ability to capture the bio-psycho-social nature of frailty; (ii) evidence of wide validation and frequent application in general population studies; (iii) feasibility of implementation, without imposing excessive measurement burden; (iv) prior demonstration of measurability using survey data [26].

The bio-psycho-social frailty measures chosen were the Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI), Groningen Frailty Indicator (GFI), and Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS).

The TFI was developed by Gobbens et al. [27]. It consists of 15 items structured across three dimensions: eight questions cover the physical frailty domain, four questions cover psychological frailty domain, and three questions cover the social frailty domain. The indicator has been officially translated and validated in Italian [28], which facilitates its operationalization within Italian survey questionnaires.

The GFI was developed by Steverink et al. [29] and consists of 15 items: four cover aspects of daily-life mobility, six are about physical and cognitive functioning, and five cover psychosocial conditions. To our knowledge, the indicator has not been translated nor validated in the Italian context.

The EFS was developed by Rolfson et al. [30] and assesses nine aspects of frailty: cognition, general health status, functional independence, social support, medication use, nutrition, mood, continence, and functional performance. The indicator has been validated and applied within the Italian context on 366 hospitalized patients [31].

Operationalization of the frailty indicators

To operationalize each frailty indicator, we proceeded identifying, for each indicator and survey, the survey question(s) corresponding to each indicator item; subsequently we dichotomized/categorized the responses. In cases without a direct match, variables’ selection and response classification done in SHARE were replicated [26]. If these variables were not available, question selection and response classification were conducted independently in double-blind by two researchers, with inter-rater agreement assessed and disagreement resolved by consensus. When no suitable survey question was available, the item was left blank (see Supplementary Files S1 and S2 for detailed description of the matching procedure).

Participants were excluded from analysis of a specific index if they had missing values for more than 20% of that scale’s items. This choice was made due to the low level of missing data and in line with previous relevant literature, imputing missing values with zero [26].

To estimate frailty prevalence, we referred to the cut-off scores reported in the literature. Because some survey items were unavailable or not fully comparable, reconstructed TFI, GFI, and EFS indicators had narrower ranges than the originals. Therefore, thresholds were proportionally rescaled to each indicator’s maximum attainable score (e.g. TFI: 5/15 in the original, 4/12 in EHIS).

Analysis plan

Psychometric properties

For the indicators deemed sufficiently replicable in the selected surveys, we computed psychometric properties, assessing their reliability and validity across applicable datasets.

Reliability was evaluated in terms of internal consistency and parallel forms reliability. Internal consistency, which refers to the extent to which all items in a test measure the same concept [27, 28], was assessed using the Cronbach’s alpha (>0.70 acceptable). Additionally, corrected total item correlation was calculated to assess if the single items behaved consistently with the rest of the test. Parallel forms reliability, which examines the correlation between two versions of a measurement instrument, was assessed through the correlation among the three frailty indicators within each dataset via Pearson’s correlation [32].

Validity was assessed through criterion validity and known-group validity. Criterion validity was evaluated using correlation coefficients and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses, calculating the area under the curve (AUC) (<0.7 = poor; 0.7–0.8 = acceptable; 0.8–0.9 = excellent; ≥0.9 = outstanding). The choice of external criteria varied across datasets, depending on data availability, and included self-rated health (SRH), hospitalizations, chronic conditions, Global Activity Limitation Indicator (GALI), doctor visits, domestic accidents, life satisfaction (see Supplementary file S3).

Known-group validity, which evaluates an instrument’s ability to discriminate between groups known to differ on the variable of interest [33], was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and the one-way ANOVA. The groups considered were gender, age, and socioeconomic status.

Applied analysis of frailty indicators

Further analyses were run as illustrative example of the informative value that policy makers could derive from the use of these tools.

First, age-standardized prevalence was estimated for each indicator across surveys, and compared across regions (Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics—NUTS2 level), to evaluate how frailty prevalence varied depending on tools and data used. Age standardization was performed using the direct method, applying age-specific prevalence estimates to the Italian population structure [34]. Results were represented using geographic maps.

Then, logistic regression models were estimated to assess the predictive power of the frailty indicators on various health outcomes, while controlling for sociodemographic characteristics. The baseline model included age, gender, and education as covariates, while the frailty indicator was added in a second specification to assess its incremental contribution. Changes in explained variance and likelihood ratio tests between nested models were used to quantify the additional explanatory power of the frailty indicator. The article was prepared following the STROBE reporting guideline (details available upon request) [35].

Psychometric properties

The psychometric properties were assessed only for indicators and surveys with sufficient reproducibility. Therefore, this section focuses on SHARE, EHIS, and EU-SILC, excluding AVQ.

Internal consistency reliability

Overall, internal consistency was satisfactory for most indicators and across surveys, with TFI having all Cronbach’s alphas larger or equal than 0.7; and indicators measured in EHIS performing best (Cronbach’s alpha for TFI 0.80; GFI 0.72; EFS 0.80; see Supplementary file S6 for full results). For TFI, reliability of specific domains was also estimated. Reliability of physical component was satisfactory (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70, 0.76, and 0.69 in SHARE, EHIS, and EU-SILC, respectively), while psychological and especially social component had poor reliability (see Supplementary file S6). This was coherent with results from other studies [36]. Supplementary file S7 presents internal consistency for single items.

Parallel forms reliability

Reliability evaluated through correlation among indicators was high (Pearson’s r > 0.76, see Supplementary file S6). This pattern was especially pronounced in the EHIS dataset, with correlations of 0.89 between TFI and GFI, 0.88 between TFI and EFS, and 0.88 between GFI and EFS.

Criterion validity

Figure 2 reports the AUC values across surveys, external criteria, and indicators (see Supplementary file S8 for AUC values). In synthesis, (i) the highest AUC values were observed in EHIS (AUC for SRH criterion with EFS: 0.92, 95% CI 0.915–0.925) and EU-SILC (AUC for GALI criterion with EFS: 0.96, 95% CI = 0.954–0.961) surveys—notably, in EU-SILC, the criteria yielding the highest AUC were also part of the indicator’s item set; (ii) the EFS indicator emerged as best performing; (iii) among the criteria that allowed cross-survey comparisons, EHIS showed a better performance in both GALI and doctor visits; (iv) when the criterion was available in only one survey, comparable AUC values were observed across the three indicators.

Known-group validity

Frailty scores were higher among older respondents, women, and those with lower educational attainment, with significant differences (see Supplementary file S9). These findings provide evidence that the indicators effectively distinguish between the groups known to vary in frailty.

Analyses of frailty prevalence and predictive capability were conducted using EHIS data, based on its superior psychometric performance.

However, quartile assignments slightly vary depending on the indicator used (e.g. depending on the indicator, Umbria region shifted from Q2 to Q3).

Logistic regression models were run focusing on the outcomes doctor visits, GALI, and domestic accidents, using EHIS data as above and the EFS indicator (results presented in Supplementary file S10). We found that adding the frailty indicator to the regression models led to an increase in explained variance for all outcomes considered, particularly for the GALI outcome (R-squared increased from 0.104 to 0.541; likelihood-ratio test = 4676.6, P-value <.01; EFS OR = 1.82 with 95% CI 1.82–1.86).

Discussion

This study sought to examine how bio-psycho-social frailty can be effectively measured using existing population surveys and how these indicators can inform public health policy. Our findings demonstrate that the replication of bio-psycho-social frailty indicators across national surveys is both feasible and reliable, extending previous methodological work [10, 26]. Beyond methodological relevance, these indicators hold practical value for policymakers, as they can help identify frail subgroups and, as results show, explain healthcare demand more effectively than using chronological age alone.

The progressive ageing of the population poses a major challenge to public health systems, particularly in contexts of limited healthcare resources. In this perspective, frailty—rather than chronological age—represents a meaningful predictor of healthcare needs and service utilization [3]. Recognizing frailty as a dynamic and multidimensional process supports the shift from reactive to preventive health care, focusing on maintaining functional ability and independence. The ability to identify frail individuals within the population therefore becomes a strategic prerequisite for evidence-based planning and prevention. Achieving this goal requires tools that are not only valid and reliable, but also accessible, replicable, and compatible with existing data infrastructures.

Our study contributes to this discussion by adopting three widely used measures of bio-psycho-social frailty operationalized through four major European and national surveys. This approach widens the scope of previous research [26], extending the comparison both within and between surveys to better capture the variability linked to data source and context. While SHARE includes a broader range of variables, its sample size for respondents aged 65+ is smaller than in the other sources, and, due to privacy restrictions, information on place of residence is available only at the NUTS1 level. The use of additional surveys with national implementation addresses this limitation and creates opportunities for discussion with policy makers, about selecting the most appropriate data source based on specific needs and objectives (e.g. international surveys allow cross-country comparisons (EHIS and EU-SILC) or interest in correlation with life-course socioeconomic conditions (EU-SILC), etc.). Moreover, by relying on existing validated indicators rather than developing new ones, we avoided adding to the proliferation of frailty measures and enhanced the comparability of our findings with previous research.

Our comparative analyses revealed key insights in terms of replicability, usability, and applicability of the frailty indicators. The GFI showed slightly better replicability across surveys, enabling a more complete reconstruction of its components. By contrast, the EFS showed stronger predictive ability (i.e. highest AUC values) than the TFI and GFI, in line with previous evidence [26]. From an implementation standpoint, the EFS remains particularly attractive in clinical and operational settings due to its short administration time (3–5 min) and multidomain coverage [19]. At the same time, the TFI is the most extensively used indicator, with strong evidence of reliability and validity, particularly in community and research contexts [15]. Taken together, these patterns underscore that each indicator has a distinct strength—replicability across surveys for GFI, predictive performance and clinical practicality for EFS, and accumulated validation for TFI—thereby reinforcing the view that the selection of a frailty instrument should be context-driven.

SHARE was used as the gold standard and reference for measuring frailty, as it had been already investigated and provides complete reproducibility of indicators. Among the other surveys, EHIS emerged as the most suitable platform for frailty assessment overall, showing higher indicators’ reproducibility and strong predictive accuracy. Despite this potential, however, it lacks information on items such as weight loss and handgrip strength, which are elements coherent with the survey’s scope. Including these measures would add significant value, as it would enable the assessment of bio-psycho-social frailty as well as phenotypic frailty—a widely used operationalization of frailty.

Regional analyses based on EHIS data displayed a clear North–South gradient, with higher frailty prevalence in Southern regions. Several regions had scores that placed them in different quartiles depending on the frailty indicator used. This sensitivity has direct policy relevance: minor definitional differences can lead to substantial variations in interpreting territorial disparities and identifying priority areas.

The regression analyses further highlighted the explanatory value of frailty indicators. From a public health perspective, this means that frailty provides a complementary layer of information, predicting health service utilization and so supporting more efficient resource allocation.

Specifically from a policy perspective, we argue that survey-based bio-psycho-social frailty indicators can support policymakers in identifying and geographically mapping populations at the highest risk of frailty, thereby enabling the timely targeting of preventive and community-based interventions. Beyond early identification of at-risk groups [21], these indicators can more broadly be incorporated into a population risk stratification approach [37], segmenting populations according to frailty rather than age alone. Drawing on the conceptual logic underpinning Population Health Management (PHM) [38], and following the example of several recent Italian chronic care policies that have adopted risk stratification models to guide service organization and resource allocation [39], such stratification can inform the development of differentiated policy strategies across different levels of frailty, particularly in relation to resource allocation and service capacity planning. Monitoring differences across regions as well as over time (e.g. comparing results from repeated waves of surveys) may therefore provide actionable signals for anticipatory planning in ageing societies.

As with any secondary data analysis, this study has inherent limitations. The surveys were not explicitly designed to reproduce frailty indicators, and differences in item wording or availability may affect comparability. Likewise, the availability of endpoints for use as external criteria was limited, with key outcomes, such as mortality, not available. These drawbacks should be considered alongside the well-known limitations of survey data, which, despite being a key source of information on population status, are subject to issues related to coverage, non-response, and survey mode effects [40]. As for the risks of methodological constraints, variable selection and response categorization were conducted independently and in double-blind to mitigate them. Although this cannot fully remove imprecision, it strengthens the rigor of the operationalization process. An important aspect that emerged is the poor measurement properties of the social domain of the TFI. This limitation was already acknowledged and justified by Gobbens and colleagues [27], but it may warrant further exploration.

Frailty assessment should not remain a purely methodological exercise. By demonstrating that bio-psycho-social frailty can be measured using existing national surveys, this study lays the groundwork for future evaluations of public health interventions [21], and supports policies that promote more equitable and effective responses to the challenges of ageing societies.
