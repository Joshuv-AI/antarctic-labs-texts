Indoxyl sulfate induces retinal microvascular injury via COX-2/PGE2 activation in diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Lan Zhou, Hongyan Sun, Gongyi Chen, Cunzi Li, Dan Liu, Xurui Wang, Ting Meng, Zhenyou Jiang, Shu Yang, Ming-Ming Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic retinopathy (DR), the principal cause of acquired blindness among the working-age population, is the most frequent microvascular complication of diabetes. Although metabolic disorders are hypothesized to play a role in its pathogenesis, the underlying mechanism remains largely elusive.MethodsTo elucidate the mechanism, we initially compared metabolite profiles of vitreous fluid between 23 patients with DR and 12 non-diabetic controls using liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry, identifying the distinct metabolite indoxyl sulfate (IS). Subsequently, streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic and IS-injected rat models were established to examine the effects of IS on retinal microvasculature. RNA sequencing was conducted to identify potential regulatory mechanisms in IS-treated human retinal endothelial cells (HREC). Finally, target gene knockdown in HREC and treatment of IS-injected rats with inhibitors (targeting IS production or downstream regulators) were employed to elucidate the detailed mechanisms and identify therapeutic targets for DR.ResultsMetabolomics identified 172 significantly altered metabolites in the vitreous humor of diabetics, including the dysregulated tryptophan metabolite indoxyl sulfate (IS). IS was observed to breach the blood-retinal barrier and accumulate in the intraocular fluid of diabetic rats. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments indicated that elevated levels of IS induced endothelial apoptosis and disrupted cell junctions. RNA sequencing pinpointed prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthetase-cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2) as a potential target of IS. Validation experiments demonstrated that IS enhanced COX-2 expression, which subsequently increased PGE2 secretion by promoting transcription factor EGR1 binding to COX-2 DNA following entry into cells via organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATP2B1). Furthermore, inhibition of COX-2 in vivo or silencing EGR1/OATP2B1 in HREC mitigated IS-induced microcapillary damage and the activation of COX-2/PGE2.ConclusionOur study demonstrated that indoxyl sulfate (IS), a uremic toxin originating from the gut microbiota product indole, increased significantly and contributed to retinal microvascular damage in diabetic retinopathy (DR). Mechanistically, IS impaired retinal microvascular integrity by inducing the expression of COX-2 and the production of PGE2. Consequently, targeting the gut microbiota or the PGE2 pathway may offer effective therapeutic strategies for the treatment of DR.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05654-1.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a chronic retinal microvascular complication characterized by microaneurysms, hemorrhages, intraretinal chronic microvascular abnormalities and neovascularization in diabetic patients. It is one of the leading causes of blindness among the working-age population [1]. DR affects as much as 19.73–25.03% individuals with diabetes, and the number is estimated to reach 160.50 million by 2045 [2, 3]. Due to the complicated pathogenesis, currently, there is still a lack of effective therapeutic approaches for DR, leading to about one-third of patients irreversible visual impairment [4]. Clarifying the underlying mechanism of diabetic retinal vascular dysfunction will be beneficial for its treatment and prevention.

In histopathological terms, retinal endothelial cells (RECs) and pericytes are the primary susceptible cell types that initiate vascular leakage in the early stages of DR [5]. From a molecular standpoint, aberrant expression of vascular endothelial growth factor is identified as a key contributor [6]. Furthermore, other signaling pathways, particularly those associated with inflammatory responses such as TNF-α, complement factor, prostanoids etc., have also been implicated as contributory mechanisms [7–9]. While the identification of these factors enhances understanding of certain aspects of DR’s mechanisms, they remain inadequate to fully explain the natural progression of the disease.

The advancements in multi-omics techniques, particularly metabolomics, have enabled a comprehensive examination of complex biochemical processes and the relationships between metabolic networks in organisms with metabolic disorder diseases [10]. To date, various studies on the metabolomics of blood or vitreous in patients with DR have been conducted. Numerous metabolites, such as lactate, glutamine, galactitol, proline, sphingolipids, and creatine, have been identified as dysregulated in DR patients [11, 12]. In our study, we also conducted metabolomic analyses of the vitreous humor from DR patients and observed a tryptophan metabolic product indoxyl sulfate (IS) increased significantly. Indoxyl sulfate is produced jointly by the intestinal microbiota with the host, and displayed a strong affinity for albumin; Dietary tryptophan is initially transformed into indole by intestinal flora, and subsequently, indole is metabolized into IS by cytochrome P450 enzyme and sulfotransferase in the liver [13]. Primarily, indoxyl sulfate is excreted through the renal tubular organic anion transport system [14]. Patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) even those undergoing dialysis treatment have 20 times levels of IS than healthy individuals [15]. Increased IS would upregulate the level of inflammatory factors and promote oxidative stress in patients with DKD and eventually cause renal fibrosis and a variety of cardiovascular diseases through multiple pathways [16–18]. However, the interaction between IS accumulation and DR microvascular injuries remains unclear.

Therefore, by integrating metabolomics and transcriptomics, this study sought to explore the correlation between IS accumulation and microcapillary damage in a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rat model. Furthermore, we investigated whether IS exerts its effects through activation of the COX-2/PGE2 axis, ultimately aiding in elucidating the pathogenesis of DR and identifying novel intervention targets.

Materials and methods

Patients

23 patients with DR (diabetic macular edema or proliferative diabetic retinopathy) and 12 non-diabetic patients with idiopathic macular epiretinal membrane (age and sex matched) were recruited from 2020 to 2022 for this study. All measurements were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the hospital (LL-KT-2018338) and informed consent was obtained from all individual. All participants underwent visual examination, intraocular pressure measurement, slit-lamp microscopy, Fundus Fluorescein Angiography, Optical Coherence Tomography Angiography, and B-ultrasound scanning. DR patients meeting the following inclusion criteria were included: (1) age above 18 years; (2) type 1 or 2 diabetes mellitus; (3) DR grade based on the international clinical diabetic retinopathy disease severity scale and DME based on the international clinical diabetic macular edema disease severity scale proposed by the American Academy of Ophthalmology in 2019; (4) underwent first anti-VEGF or vitrectomy treatment. Individuals with the following conditions were excluded: (1) presence of any other retinal disorder such as retinal vein occlusion, glaucoma, or uveitis; (2) any systemic inflammatory diseases or history of thromboembolic disease; (3) previous treatment with intraocular surgery, laser photocoagulation, or intravitreal injection of anti-VEGF.

Vitreous samples preparation for metabolomics

Approximately 0.5 mL of vitreous humor was harvested prior to surgery and stored in a frozen tube. The samples were then placed in a liquid nitrogen tank and sent to the ophthalmic biobank at Shenzhen People’s Hospital, where they were stored at −80 °C. Before metabolomic analysis, the vitreous humor was thawed and dissolved at 4 °C. A 50-μL sample was extracted and added to a 200-μL volume of cold acetonitrile/methanol to precipitate proteins. The mixtures were vortexed for 30 s and then allowed to stand for 10 min at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The upper phase and lower organic phase were separately collected and evaporated to dry in a concentrator. Finally, the residue was resuspended in 100 μL of the mobile phase for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis.

LC–MS analysis

The metabolites were separated on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) liquid chromatography column using a Vanquish (Thermo Fisher Scientific) ultra-performance liquid chromatograph. The mobile A phase consisted of 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia in water, and mobile B phase was acetonitrile. The mobile phase flow rate was 0.5 mL/min with the following linear gradient: 0‒0.5 min, 95% B; 0.5‒7 min, 95%‒65% B; 7‒8 min, 65%‒40% B; 8‒9 min, 40% B; 9‒9.1 min, 40%‒95% B; 9.1‒12 min, 95% B. The column temperature was 25 °C, and the sample tray temperature was 4 °C. The Thermo Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer is capable of primary and secondary mass spectrometry data acquisition under the control of the control software (Xcalibur, Thermo). The detailed parameters are as follows: Sheath gas flow rate: 50 Arb, Aux gas flow rate: 10 Arb, Capillary temperature: 320 °C, Full MS resolution: 60,000, MS/MS resolution: 7500, Collision energy: 10/30/60 in NCE mode, Spray Voltage: 3.5 kV (positive), or 3.2 kV (negative).

The metabolomics raw data were converted to mzXML format using the ProteoWizard software and processed for peak identification, peak extraction, peak alignment, and integration using the R package (kernel XCMS). Then, it was matched to the secondary mass spectrometry database of BiotreeDB (V2.1) for substance annotation, with the algorithm scoring cutoff value set to 0.3. Using VIP > 1 as the criteria, the normalized data was submitted to principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal to partial least squares-discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA).

RNA sequencing analysis

RNA sequencing analysis was performed in the HREC treated with 2 mmol/L IS (n = 3 for IS group or control group). The total RNA was extracted by using Trizol Reagent (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA). RNA was sent to Wuhan Baiyi Huineng Biotechnology Co., Ltd for RNA sequencing. Sequenced raw reads were mapped to human reference genome using hisat2. After alignment, RSEM (V1.2.29) was used to generate FPKM values for known gene models. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (v1.22.2). Fold-changes were estimated according to each sample’s FPKM. Differentially expressed genes were selected using the following filter criteria: P value ≤0.05, FDR ≤ 0.1, |FoldChange| ≥ 2, mean FPKM ≥ 1. Gene set functional enrichment analysis was performed using cluster Profiler (v3.17.0) with gene set size set to 5–500, P value cutoff set to 0.05, and adjusted P value set to 0.25. Heatmap plots were generated using pheatmap (v1.0.12). Gene-function networks for differentially expressed chemokine genes and related biological processes were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.6.1). Protein Protein Interaction network (PPI) was generated by the public database String (https://string-db.org) and STRINGdb (v2.8.4).

DR rat model

Since the effect of estrogen on blood glucose metabolism [19], female rats injected with STZ demonstrated poor success rate of DR model and higher mortality (Supplement Table S1). Sixty male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (weighing 150–200 g, aged 8–10 weeks) were purchased from the laboratory animal center of Dean Gene Technology in Guangzhou, China. The rats were kept in 12-h light/12-h dark conditions. They were randomly divided into five groups: the control group (n = 12), the DR group (n = 12), DR + BB536 (n = 12), vehicle treatment group (n = 12) and the IS treatment group (n = 12).

The DR model was established as previously described [20]. Briefly, the rats were fed with high fat and sugar diets for 3 weeks (Dean Gene Technology, Guangzhou, China) and then fasted for 6 h before streptozotocin (STZ, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) injection, then they received an intraperitoneal injection of single dose of STZ (50 mg/kg) or vehicle (citrate buffer control). The fasting Blood glucose levels and weight were evaluated every week after STZ administration by an Accu-Chek active glucometer (Roche, Basel, BS, Switzerland). Only those with blood glucose levels higher than 15 mmol/L were considered as diabetic group and used for further study. For the DR plus BB536 group, BB536—a bifidobacterium longum strain that has been proved to reduce serum IS levels was used [21], and DR rats in this group were fed a diet containing 1% BB536 (Morinaga, Morinaga Milk, Tokyo, Japan) for 4 months after STZ injection. The IS treatment group were given IS (Alfa Aescar, Shanghai, China) at a single i.p. injection dose of 100 mg/kg daily for 1 months. The vehicle group were given an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) daily by i.p. injection. All rats had free access to food and water and were kept in an air-conditioned room with a 12-h light–dark cycle. Pentobarbital (20 mg/kg, i.p.) and topical anesthetics (0.5% tetracaine hydrochloride) were used to euthanize each animal. All animal experiments were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory animals approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of our hospital (No. LL-KY-20220108).

Measurements of IS

The aqueous humor (AH) was collected from the rats under anesthesia using a syringe with a 35-gauge needle (Hamilton, Beijing, China). A total of 15 µl of AH was collected from each eye and then was centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. The detections of IS in AH was performed using a rat IS ELISA Kit (Bioroyee Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Retinal trypsin digestion assay

The eyes balls were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. They were then equatorially bisected, and the retinas were removed. The retinas were incubated with 3% trypsin at 37 °C for 4 h, and then gently shaken to free vessel network, washed, and mounted on the glass slides to dry. Retinal vasculature was then stained with periodic acid-Schiff and hematoxylin. Finally, the vascular network was observed under microscopy, 10 fields from every retina were choose for next analysis.

Evans blue assay

To investigate the vascular permeability, Evans blue (EB, 20 mg/mL in saline; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was injected through the tail vein at a dose of 50 mg/kg. After the dye had been circulated for 2 h, the rats were sacrificed with high doses of pentobarbital, and the eyes were isolated and fixed in fresh 4% PFA for 1 h. Then, the retinal mounts were prepared, and EB leakage was examined by laser confocal microscopy (Leica, Wet-zlar, Germany). The proportion of evans blue leakage area were measured using Image J (NIH, USA).

Cell and transfection

The primary human retinal endothelial cells (HREC) were purchased from Angio-Proteomie (Boston, MA, USA) and cultured in endothelial cell medium (ScienCell Research, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS), 5% FBS, and penicillin/streptomycin. HREC within 8 passages were used for experiments.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) against OATP2B1, EGR1, and non-targeting scrambled negative control siRNA were used (Hanyi Biology, Guangzhou, China, siRNA sequence displayed in Supplement Table S2). HREC cells were transfected with scrambled or target siRNA (50 nmol/L) using ESCORT III (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Western blot

Protein was harvested from the rat retinal tissue or cell culture using RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors, and the concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Protein samples were separated on 8% or 10% SDS–PAGE and electroblotted onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking in 5% fresh dry fat-free milk, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Antibody sources and dilutions are shown in Supplement Table S2. The bound antibodies signal was visualized using an immobilon western chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore, Germany) and G-BOX (Syngene, USA) image capture system. Relative protein expression level was measured by calculating the band density with ImageJ software (NIH, USA), followed by normalization to β-actin or GAPDH loading control.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability or survival rate was determined using a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK8, Dojindo, Japan). Cells were seeded in 96-well culture plates (5000 cells/well), CCK-8 reagent was added into the cell culture after treatment with different concentration of IS and then the 450 nm absorbance values were recorded.

In vitro vascular permeability assay

The effect of IS on retinal endothelial cell permeability was assessed using a Millipore in Vitro Vascular Permeability Assay. Following the established procedures [22], 200,000 retinal endothelial cells per well (passage 5) were cultured on 1 µm transwells in growth medium for 72 h. Post-culture, cells were either untreated or exposed to various IS concentrations in growth medium for 18 h. Subsequently, FITC-conjugated dextran (2 µg) was introduced to the upper chamber. Over 30 min, fluorescence intensities in the lower chambers were measured at 485 nm excitation and 535 nm emission using a fluorescence plate reader.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence (IF) was performed on cell culture or retinal cryostat sections of the eye cups. The tissue samples were permeabilized in 0.5% Triton-X-100 for 15 min, blocked in 5% donkey serum for 1 h at RT, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody, which was followed by an incubation with an Alexa Fluor labeled secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA) for 1 h at RT after 3 washes with PBS. The sample was lastly incubated with 4′,6-Diamidino-2 phenylindol dihydroclorid (DAPI; 1:2000, Vienna, Austria) for 5 min at RT. Antibody sources are shown in Supplement Table S2.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNAs were extracted using Trizol reagent (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA). RNA was quantified using 260/280 UV spectrophotometry. Total RNA pellets were dissolved in an appropriate amount of RNase water, followed by removal of potentially contaminated DNA by treatment with gDNA eraser (Takara Bio Inc., Beijing, China). Next, 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was used for reverse transcription reaction with a PrimerScriptTM Reagent kit (Takara Bio Inc., Beijing, China) to produce first-strand cDNA in a 20 μL reaction mixture. qPCR was performed to measure mRNA expression using the SYBR® Green Real-time PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc., Beijing, China) with specific primers (Supplement Table S3). The specificity of the amplified products was determined by melting curve analysis. The relative mRNA levels of target genes were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Gene expression values were normalized against GAPDH mRNA.

Measurements of PGE2

PGE2 in the vitreous fluid of the above rats or supernatant of IS-treatment cell culture was measured using commercial ELISA kits (Bioroyee Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were carried out using a commercial kit from Beyotime Biotechnology according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, treated cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at RT for 10 min. Cross-linked chromatin was sonicated to reduce the DNA fragments to 200–800 bp. Soluble chromatin was centrifuged for 10 min at 14 000 rpm at 4 °C, and 1% of the supernatant was saved as input DNA. Chromatin samples were pre-cleared using protein G agarose, then immunoprecipitated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies against EGR1(Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK), Histone H3 (Positive Control, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) or IgG (Negative Control, ABclonal Technology, Wuhan, China). Immunocomplexes were recovered using the ssDNA/protein G agarose slurry, washed with TE buffer, then incubated in elution buffer for 15 min at 25 °C, followed by heating at 65 °C for 4 h. DNA fragments were obtained by equal volume phenol/chloroform extraction, then precipitated with ethanol. Immunoprecipitated DNA samples were analyzed by PCR using the following primer pair: forward, 5′-TCCACAGCATCGATGTCACC-3′ and reverse, 5′-ACGCTTTATGCTGAAGCCCT-3′. PCR products were separated by electrophoresis and a 150 bp product was detected.

Prediction of transcription factors

To predict the locations of the binding sites for the transcription factors EGR1 on the PTGS2 promoter region, JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/), an open-access database was used for analysis. The promoter region of the target gene was obtained from UCSC (http://genome.ucsc.edu/).

Molecular docking test

Molecular Docking test was executed with the software of AutoDock. The crystal structure of the published EGR1 (PDB code: 4R2A) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The structure of 3-indoxyl sulphate was got from PubChem database (CID cod: 10258). The solvent molecules within the protein structure were removed in the docking calculations, and the best ligand pose was chosen according to the dock score.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained in all experiments were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Kolmogorov–Smirnov testing and variance homogeneity test were performed on all data before analysis, to test whether data were normally distributed and equal variances. If the data was normally distributed and had equal variances, difference between more than two groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Dunnett’s test comparing all treatment groups to the control group. Otherwise, data was analyzed by Kruskal–Wallis analysis followed by Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc method to compare all treatment groups versus the control group. The two-tailed Student’s t test was used to compare the differences between two groups.

Results

Vitreous humor metabolomics revealed tryptophan metabolism disorder and its metabolites-IS accumulation in DR patients

To investigate the metabolite profiles in the vitreous humor of diabetic retinopathy (DR) patients, we recruited 23 individuals with DR and 12 non-diabetic controls with idiopathic macular epiretinal membrane. Demographic and clinic trait of these patients were presented in Supplement Table 4, showing age and sex were matched, while fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and HbA1c values were elevated in the DR group (P < 0.0001). In addition, a significant decrease in the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was observed in DR patients (P < 0.0001). A principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to discern whether metabolite profiles could differentiate DR patients from controls, revealing a distinct separation between the two groups (PC1, 37.9% of the variance, Fig. 1A). The robustness of the model was confirmed through a permutation test, ensuring no overfitting (Fig. 1B). Using P value ≤ 0.05 for screening, 172 metabolites with fold change >1.5 or <0.7 were identified for further study. Among these, 143 metabolites were elevated in DR samples, whereas 28 were increased in the control group. Figure 1C displays a heatmap of the top 44 metabolites that exhibited the greatest statistical differences, highlighting significant increases in amino acid metabolites such as 3-Indoxyl sulfate (IS, red arrows in Fig. 1C), glutamine, and phenyllactic acid. To better understand the correlation and metabolic pathways of these changes in DR, correlation analysis of differential metabolites and metabolite set enrichment analysis was further performed. Notably, amino acid metabolites, particularly tryptophan metabolic products such as IS and Kynurenic acid O-hexoside, showed strong correlations (Fig. 1D), with a significant upregulation of the tryptophan metabolism pathway (red arrows in Fig. 1E). Indoxyl sulfate penetrated into blood-retinal barrier and induced microcapillary damage in STZ-induced DR rats

Tryptophan metabolism in the gut involves its direct transformation by intestinal microorganisms into several molecules, such as indole and its derivatives [23]. Indoxyl sulfate (IS), one of these derivatives, is typically excreted by healthy kidneys. To investigate whether IS can penetrate the blood-retinal barrier and its role in diabetic retinopathy (DR), we established both a rat diabetic model and an IS treatment rat model. After 19 weeks, the IS concentration in the aqueous humor of DR rats nearly doubled (P < 0.01, Fig. 2A, B), however, when DR rats were intervened with BB536, a bifidobacterium longum strain which has been showed to effectively reduce serum IS in chronic kidney disease [24], the IS concentration reduced significantly compared with the DR group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, IS was injected daily into the peritoneal cavity of normal rats, and after 4 weeks, the IS levels in the aqueous humor significantly increased compared to the control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2A, C). To investigate the relationship between IS accumulation and retinal microcapillary damage in diabetic retinopathy, we conducted Evans blue leakage assays and retinal trypsin digestion assays across different groups. As depicted in Fig. 2D, there was a significant increase in the number of acellular capillaries within the diabetic retinal vascular network (red arrows in Fig. 2D, F); however, this increase was partially mitigated by BB536 (P < 0.0001, red arrows in Fig. 2D, F). Furthermore, intraperitoneal injection of IS led to a tripling of acellular capillary formation compared to the vehicle group (P < 0.0001, red arrows in Fig. 2E, G). The integrity of the blood-retinal barrier was assessed using the Evans blue leakage assay; which showed a substantially higher leakage area in the diabetic group compared to the normal group, and this effect was alleviated by BB536 (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2H, I). Additionally, the IS injection group exhibited pronounced Evans blue leakage relative to the vehicle group (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2J, K). Overall, our findings suggest that IS can breach the blood-retinal barrier and contribute to microcapillary damage in vivo.

High concentration of IS induced human retinal endothelium cells damage in vitro

To further investigate the impact of indoxyl sulfate (IS) on retinal endothelial cells, primary human retinal endothelial cell (HREC) cultures were exposed to varying IS concentrations. Considering previous findings where healthy individuals show serum IS levels below 0.01 mmol/L, and these levels rise above 0.2 mmol/L in chronic kidney disease [25], we tested IS effects starting from 0.02 mmol/L. Elevated IS concentrations (>0.5 mmol/L) notably downregulated tight junction-related genes like ZO-1 and Occludin; for instance, 2 mmol/L IS reduced ZO-1 expression by nearly twofold relative to controls (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A, B) and significantly diminished Occludin levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C). Immunofluorescence analyses further demonstrated significant disorganization in ZO-1 architecture upon treatment with 2 mmol/L IS (Fig. 3D). To consolidate the destructive effects of IS on retinal endothelial cells permeability, a Millipore in Vitro Vascular Permeability Assay was introduced, showing that the transport of fluorescent molecules through the endothelial layer increased significantly at IS concentrations starting from 0.05 mmol/L (P < 0.001, Fig. 3F). Additionally, apoptosis studies revealed increased cleaved-caspase 3 positive cells at IS levels reaching 0.2 mmol/L (Fig. 3E, G), and a CCK-8 assay confirmed significant reductions in HREC survival at IS concentrations as low as 0.1 mmol/L (P < 0.01, Fig. 3H). RNA sequencing profiles demonstrated signatures of the underlying regulators in IS-treated HREC: prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthetase

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, RNA sequencing was conducted on HREC treated with IS, and the results were integrated with clinical metabolomics data. Analysis of the transcriptional profiles revealed significant differences in gene expression within the IS treatment group. A total of 375 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with P value <0.05 and |FoldChange| ≥ 2, were identified for further investigation; among these, 198 genes were up-regulated, while 177 were down-regulated. The top 100 significantly differentially expressed genes were displayed in a heat map (Fig. 4A). Notably, genes including PTGS1, PTGER4, PTGS2 and PLA2G4A had garnered special interest (red arrows in Fig. 4A), since they are key components in PGE2 generation or activation [26] and the PGE2 signal pathway is believed to promote DR progression [8, 27]. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed to reveal potential signal pathways associated with IS treatment. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were predominantly involved in peroxidase activity, heme binding, and other processes (top 20 listed in Fig. 4B), while KEGG pathway analysis highlighted PI3K-AKT signal pathway, cellular senescence, apoptosis and others (top 20 listed in Fig. 4C). IS promoted COX-2 expression and then PGE2 secretion in vivo and in vitro

Based on RNA sequencing data, we next explored the potential mechanism in the IS-induced HREC cultures and IS treatment rats. We first examined the expression of potential target genes including PTGS1, PTGER4, PTGS2, and PLA2G4A in HRECs treated with 2 mmol/L IS through qPCR. Interestingly, except for PTGS1 and PLA2G4A, the expression of PTGS2, PTGER4 increased significantly compared to the control (Fig. 5A). The cyclooxygenase-2–prostaglandin E2 pathway involves cyclooxygenase, secretion prostanoids and binding of prostanoids to their receptors. Among these potential target genes, PTGS1 (COX-1) and PTGS2 (COX-2) are cyclooxygenases that provide basal and excessive prostanoids levels respectively, while PLA2G4A catalyzes the hydrolysis of membrane phospholipids to release arachidonic acid metabolized into prostanoids [28]. Consequently, the data suggest activation of the PTGS2-PGE2-PTGER4 axis [26]. Further investigation involving western blot analysis confirmed significant upregulation of PTGS2 and PTGER4 upon IS treatment at a high concentration above 0.5 mmol/L (Fig. 5B–D). Immunofluorescence staining, as depicted in Fig. 5E and F, showed PTGS2 accumulation in the HREC cytoplasm following IS induction, and PTGER4 aggregated in the cytoplasm or cytomembranes. The fluorescence intensity of both proteins, quantified using ImageJ, was significantly greater compared to the control group after IS treatment (P < 0.0001, Fig. 5G, H). Furthermore, intravitreal injection of IS in rats showed upregulated PTGS2 and PTGER4 expression (Fig. 5J–L). Additionally, PGE2 secretion in vivo and in vitro, measured using an ELISA kit, showed significantly increased levels in the supernatant of IS-induced HREC (Fig. 5I) and in the aqueous humor of treated rats (Fig. 5M). Celecoxib, a canonical selective COX-2 inhibitor [29], was chosen for this purpose. Consistent with this, there was a substantial decrease in the proportion of Evans blue leakage area in the celecoxib plus IS treatment group compared to the IS-only group (Fig. 6C, D). OATP2B1 mediates the cellular uptake of IS in HREC

However, the precise mechanism by which indoxyl sulfate enters endothelial cells and subsequently enhances COX-2 expression remains unclear. Previous studies suggest that IS is transported from blood to urine via organic anion transporters (OATs) and organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs) in tubular epithelial cells [30]. It has been demonstrated in macrophages, that OATP2B1 also facilitates the uptake of indoxyl sulfate [17]. Importantly, retinal endothelial cells predominantly express OATP2B1 [31]. We hypothesized that IS is transported into the HREC via OATP2B1. To validate this, we first evaluated OATP2B1 expression in vitro and in vivo experiments; in vitro results revealed a significant elevation of OATP2B1 at 2 mmol/L IS concentration (Fig. 7A, B); similarly, in vivo data showed increased OATP2B1 expression in rat retinas following a 4-week intraperitoneal IS injection (Fig. 7C, D). Further, using siRNA to knock down OATP2B1, we observed that the IS-induced upregulation of PTGS2 (COX-2) was abrogated upon OATP2B1 deficiency (Fig. 7E, F). Additionally, it is established from prior research that OATP inhibitors such as probenecid, rifampicin, and cyclosporine A diminish indoxyl sulfate uptake via OATP2B1 in macrophages [17]. Thereby, we selected cyclosporine A as an OATP2B1 inhibitor in IS-treated rats, resulting in a significant reduction of IS levels in the aqueous humor compared to the IS-only group (Fig. 7G). IS activated PTGS2 by promoting transcription factor EGR1 binding to its DNA

As a small molecule compound, IS would not directly bind to PTGS2 DNA to stimulate its expression akin to transcription factors. However, our analysis of RNA sequencing data from IS-treated HREC revealed an elevated transcription factor EGR1 is associated with PTGS2 (blue arrow in Fig. 4D), and EGR1 has been identified as a transcription factor of PTGS2 by previous study [32]. Prompted by these, we questioned whether IS would activate PTGS2 by interacting with EGR1. We first verified the increase of EGR1 in RNA sequencing using qPCR, which showed a fourfold increase after IS stimulation (P < 0.0001, Fig. 8A). Then, using the JASPAR and UCSC databases, we predicted the locations of the overlapping binding sites for EGR1 on the PTGS2 promoter region, the EGR1 Motif sequence logo was presented in Fig. 8B, and four binding sites with score ranging from 12.1 to 15.1 were identified (Fig. 8B, C). Additionally, molecular docking tests confirmed a substantial interaction between IS and EGR1, where IS oriented its benzene and pyrrole rings into the hydrophobic pocket of EGR1, forming a robust interaction depicted as hydrogen bonds visualized with black dashed lines (Fig. 8D). To further investigate whether this interaction enhances PTGS2 expression, we subsequently conducted immunofluorescence staining for EGR1 and PTGS2 following IS induction in HREC. Discussion

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a prevalent complication of diabetes, leads to significant alterations in the metabolic profiles of biological fluids such as serum and vitreous humor. While serum generally reflects systemic metabolic alterations, the vitreous humor, due to its close proximity to retinal tissues, offers valuable insights into the local biochemical milieu of the eye. Previous metabolomic studies have identified distinct disparities in the metabolic signatures of these fluids among individuals with DR [11, 33, 34]. However, a critical unresolved issue remains regarding how these metabolites traverse the blood-retinal barrier and contribute to the disease progression. In this study, we propose a novel mechanism, where the accumulation of tryptophan metabolites, specifically indoxyl sulfate (IS), interacts with microcapillary damage in DR by integrating metabolomics with transcriptomics data. The key findings include: (1) Tryptophan metabolic signal pathway is highly dysregulated in DR and their metabolites IS serve as a potential therapy target. (2) IS could penetrate into the blood-retinal barrier and accumulate in the intraocular fluid of diabetic models. (3) Increased levels of IS induces retinal endothelial cells damage in vivo and vitro. (4) OATP2B1 mediates uptake of IS by vascular endothelial cell in vivo and vitro. (5) After entry into vascular endothelial cell, IS would promote COX-2 expression and then PGE2 secretion through promoting transcription factor EGR1 binding to COX-2 DNA. (6) Reduction of IS generation with bifidobacterium longum strain mitigates DR progression. (7) The administration of a COX-2 inhibitor reduces IS-induced microcapillary damage.

By-products of tissue metabolism, or metabolites, reveal intrinsic alterations in tissue homeostasis and the extrinsic influences from the external environment such as diet, medications, and the host gut microbiome. In ophthalmology, chemical changes in the vitreous humor are viewed as indicators of retinal metabolism [35]. Here, using the samples of vitreous humor from DR patients, we conducted a metabolomic analysis and found that several amino acids metabolism including tyrosine, tryptophan and arginine etc. significantly dysregulated in diabetic patients. Among them, we picked out the tryptophan metabolism pathway as the intervention target, since one of their products indoxyl sulphate apparently increased but has not been identified in prior DR study. Tryptophan, an essential aromatic amino acid, which is a biosynthetic precursor of a large number of microbial and host metabolites [23]. The metabolism of tryptophan follows three major pathways in the gastrointestinal tract: (1) the direct transformation of tryptophan into several molecules by the gut microbiota, including Indole and its derivatives certified as ligands of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [36]; (2) the kynurenine pathway (KP) via indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) 1 [37]; and (3) the serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine [5-HT]) production pathway in enterochromaffin cells through tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TpH1) [38]. In the last few decades, accumulating evidences suggest the impaired tryptophan metabolism and their end products contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of many diseases. For instance, several seminal publications have highlighted alterations in gut tryptophan metabolism with potential links to inflammatory bowel diseases or intestinal immunity [18, 39, 40]. In the metabolomic studies of DR, some researchers demonstrate that the level of plasma kynurenine increases and plasma tryptophan decreases in patients [41]; however, other studies from diabetic animal models suggest that the level of tryptophan–kynurenine presents no change, so they infer that tryptophan metabolites may not participate in the pathogenesis of DR [42, 43]. Different from these, our study highlights the central role of tryptophan metabolism in DR and importantly, identifies indoxyl sulfate as a novel metabolic target.

Indoxyl sulphate, a product of indole metabolized by cytochrome P450 in the liver, is excreted through healthy kidneys [44]. During the kidney function failure, this metabolite accumulates and cannot be removed by dialysis, and its proinflammatory and oxidant effects are implicated in the pathogenesis of arteriosclerosis, congestive heart failure, and other cardiovascular complications that are particularly over-accelerated in patients with chronic kidney failure (CKD) [17, 45, 46]. Our current study demonstrates the detrimental effects of indoxyl sulphate on retinal endothelial cells in DR. Furthermore, we suggest that reducing indoxyl sulphate accumulation could attenuate microcapillary damage. These findings underscore the need to investigate why indoxyl sulphate levels increase in DR and how they damage retinal endothelial cells. The origin of indoxyl sulphate is influenced both by renal function and intestinal microflora. As common microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus, DR and CKD are tightly linked; renal function impairment often exacerbates retinal microvascular issues through gut microbiota, uremic toxins, and systemic inflammation [47, 48]. In our study, we observed reduced glomerular filtration rate in DR patients, leading us to conclude that the elevation of IS partially results from renal dysfunction. On the other hand, our study indicates that IS toxicity would be relieved by a bifidobacterium longum strain. In CKD contexts, researchers conducting 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing have noted dysbacteriosis contributing to IS accumulation [16]. Recently, an increasing number of studies have also revealed microbiota and dysbacteriosis highly influence the occurrence, progression, prevention, and treatment of DR, and the concept of microbiota-gut-retina axis is increasingly believed to help us understand the mechanisms underlying the effect of gut microbiota on triggering or promoting DR [49–51]. However, our study did not evaluate microbiota changes or their correlation with indoxyl sulphate damage; thus, further exploration is warranted to determine whether gut dysbiosis plays a critical role in indoxyl sulphate generation in DR.

One of the focal points of this article is to elucidate how indoxyl sulfate (IS) penetrates endothelial cells and causes damage. To maintain effective cytotoxicity, IS must traverse two barriers: the inner blood-retinal barrier (BRB), which selectively transports chemical substances through the endothelial cell membrane, and the outer BRB, comprising retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells linked by tight junctions. Our emphasis here is on the endothelial cells. According to previous studies, the excretion of indoxyl sulphate is mediated by organic anion transporters (OATs) and organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs) in the tubular epithelial cells [30]. Some investigators also demonstrate OATP2B1 mediates the uptake of indoxyl sulfate by human and mouse macrophages in CKD [17]. And OATP2B1 is considered as drug transports in the brain blood barrier [52]. Our current data indicate that OATP2B1 mediates the uptake of IS in retinal endothelial cells. Moreover, an inhibitor of OATP2B1 has been shown to mitigate IS-induced lesions. Thus, alongside clearly elucidating the mechanism of IS transport, we also identify a potential therapeutic target in DR.

To delve deeper into mechanisms, this study integrates transcriptome and metabolome analyses, focusing on the PGE2-PTGER signaling pathway, a well-established influencer of pathological retinal neovascularization and diabetic retinopathy (DR) progression [8, 27, 53]. The present study shows that IS would activate this pathway by promote prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthetase-cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2) expression, meanwhile, we show that PGE2 functions through biding to PTGER4. Nonetheless, the synthesis of prostanoids and their downstream signaling pathways are intricate; COX-2 products are not limited to PGE2 but also include PGD2, PGF2a, and prostacyclin (PGI2). Moreover, at least four PGE2 receptors-PTGER1, PTGER2, PTGER3, and PTGER4 are present in endothelial cells [54]. This study primarily reveals the role of the PGE2/PTGER4 interaction based on RNA sequencing data, leaving the involvement of other factors or receptors in this process unknown.

Moreover, we further report that IS enhance PTGS2 expression by promoting transcription factor EGR1 binding to its DNA. EGR1, an early response gene, is implicated in growth, differentiation, apoptosis, neurite outgrowth, and wound healing. As a transcription factor, EGR1 is known to be activated by cytokines, growth factors, and stress signals such as radiation, injury, or mechanical stress [55, 56]. In DR, previous studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemia or inflammation induces upregulation of EGR1, which in turn leads to endothelial dysfunction [57–59]. In our study, we discovered that EGR1 might interact with metabolites arising from metabolic dysfunction and, in turn, enhance the secretion of inflammatory factors. To the best of our knowledge, this presents a novel perspective on the research into the mechanism of DR.

There are several limitations to our study. Firstly, we did not perform any target gene knock-out experiments on rats treated with IS to verify the mechanistic pathways; for instance, using OATP2B1 knock-out animals could robustly confirm IS uptake. Moreover, to verify the impact of IS on PTGS2 expression, conducting a PTGS2 knockdown or knockout in retinal endothelial cells exposed to IS would be rational. Secondly, although we utilized molecular docking tests to validate IS binding to EGR1, this method might not fully simulate in vivo conditions. Ideally, demonstrating this binding in vitro by applying IS with a fluorescent signal directly to the EGR1 protein would be more convincing, yet this approach also presents significant challenges. Thirdly, establishing a correlation between IS concentrations in DR patients and clinical data, particularly regarding retinal vascular structure and function, is crucial for substantiating the link between increased IS levels and microcapillary damage. Unfortunately, this study does not successfully establish such a correlation. Fourthly, previous studies have indicated that the Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) serves as an endogenous effector for IS and that IS activation through AhR can lead to the activation of several inflammatory pathways in endothelial cells, including those involving E-selectin or monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 [60–62]. However, our study did not provide evidence of any interaction between AhR and COX-2/PGE2.

In summary, our results reveal a novel mechanism for DR by integrating metabolomics and transcriptomics. These data suggest that accumulation of IS in DR, a uremic toxin derived from gut microbiota, induced retinal microcapillary injuries by activating COX-2/PGE2 axis. Since anti-VEGF drug is limited by insufficient efficacy and/or drug resistance in clinic, this study shed light on a new potentially targeted method for DR treatment and prevention.
